Immediate effects of TENS intervention in bruxers with localized masticatory muscle hyperalgesia: a randomized, double-blinded, proof-of-concept study
Authors: Rongxuan Chen, Caiqun She, Bingjie Wang, Yu Zhang, Linfeng Yu, Yuanxiu Zhang, Hu Li, Chen Wang, Jinglu Zhang, Kelun Wang, Peter Svensson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to explore the immediate effects of transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) on localized masticatory muscle mechanical hyperalgesia, jaw-muscle activation, and mandibular movement measures in individuals with probable bruxism but without DC/TMD-defined painful TMD, compared with sham TENS.Materials and methodsForty participants (7 men, mean age: 25.6 years; 33 women, mean age: 23.6 years) classified as having probable bruxism and presenting with localized masticatory muscle mechanical hyperalgesia were randomly assigned to TENS/sham TENS (sTENS) treatment groups, using a randomized and double-blind design with 20 participants in each group. TENS or sham TENS was applied once. TENS was delivered using rectangular pulse current waveforms, with the output current set above the sensory threshold, for 30 min. The primary pain-sensitivity outcomes were the immediate changes in pressure pain threshold (PPT) at the masseter and temporalis sites from baseline to post-treatment. Thenar PPT was assessed as a remote reference site and was not considered a primary outcome. Secondary exploratory outcomes included mandibular movement measures and electromyography (EMG) activity of the masseter and temporalis muscles during MRP and MCE tasks. Because the study was exploratory, the secondary outcomes should be interpreted cautiously.ResultsBaseline data revealed no significant differences between the two groups before treatment. Within-group comparisons showed that, after intervention the TENS group showed increased PPT values (masseter, p = 0.003; temporalis, p = 0.002), decreased EMG activity during mandibular rest position (MRP) (masseter, p = 0.003; temporalis, p = 0.004), increased EMG activity during maximum clenching effort (MCE) on 10-mm cotton rolls (p = 0.003). In contrast, the sTENS group showed no significant pre-to-post changes in these outcomes (PPT: masseter, p = 0.422; temporalis, p = 0.266) (EMG at MRP: masseter, p = 0.553; temporalis, p = 0.539). Two-way mixed-design ANOVA revealed significant time × treatment interactions for masseter PPT (p = 0.007), masseter EMG activity during MRP (p = 0.011), and maximum opening distance (p = 0.004). Temporalis PPT showed a significant main effect of time but no significant time × treatment interaction. Post-hoc comparisons indicated greater increases in masseter PPT and greater reductions in masseter EMG activity during MRP in the TENS group than in the sham TENS group. Maximum opening distance was interpreted cautiously as a secondary exploratory finding.ConclusionIn this proof-of-concept study, a single session of TENS was associated with immediate increases in masseter PPT and changes in selected EMG and mandibular movement measures in individuals with probable bruxism and localized masticatory muscle mechanical hyperalgesia but without DC/TMD-defined painful TMD.Clinical relevanceThese results support further investigation of repeated TENS sessions and longitudinal follow-up in this specific subclinical phenotype. The present study does not establish whether TENS modifies bruxism activity or prevents the development of painful temporomandibular disorders.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry ChiCTR2400086817, registered on July 11, 2024. Introduction

Bruxism is currently defined as a repetitive jaw-muscle activity characterized by clenching or grinding of the teeth and/or bracing or thrusting of the mandible [1]. Bruxism has two circadian manifestations: sleep bruxism, which occurs during sleep, and awake bruxism, which occurs during wakefulness [2]. Reported prevalence rates range from 10% to 13% for sleep bruxism and from 22% to 31% for awake bruxism, with bruxism-related behaviors more commonly observed in younger individuals [3]. Contemporary international consensus emphasizes that bruxism should not automatically be regarded as a disorder or an inherently pathological condition. Depending on the clinical context, bruxism-related activity may act as a risk factor, a protective factor, or a neutral behavior. Therefore, clinical attention is generally directed toward individuals in whom bruxism-related activity is associated with negative consequences, such as tooth wear, restoration damage, muscle symptoms, or other patient-relevant complaints, rather than toward the elimination of bruxism itself. Bruxism can be influenced by psychosocial, physiological, and external factors [4]. In some individuals, bruxism-related activity may be associated with tooth wear, restoration damage, masticatory muscle symptoms, headache complaints, or other oral-health concerns; however, these associations are context-dependent and should not be interpreted as evidence that bruxism is invariably harmful [5]. Some individuals with bruxism-related concerns report morning headaches, tension, or fatigue in the masticatory muscles [6]. Studies indicate that women with bruxism may present with temporal headaches and masseter muscle pain during palpation [7, 8]. Systematic reviews have reported associations between sleep or awake bruxism and some temporomandibular disorder-related signs or symptoms, but the direction and causal nature of these relationships remain uncertain [9, 10].

Management strategies in individuals with bruxism-related concerns are usually directed at protecting oral structures or reducing associated symptoms, rather than eliminating the jaw-muscle activity itself. Oral appliances, for example, are generally considered protective approaches for limiting dental consequences, although they do not necessarily reduce bruxism activity [11, 12]. Other conservative interventions may be considered when bruxism-related activity is accompanied by patient-relevant symptoms, such as masticatory muscle discomfort or mechanical sensitivity, but their role should be interpreted according to the specific clinical context and measured outcomes [13–15].

Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) is a noninvasive pain management technique. Previous studies suggest that TENS may influence peripheral and central inhibitory processes and may reduce nociceptive input in some pain conditions [16, 17]. Moreover, TENS has also been proposed to act through muscle relaxation and improved peripheral metabolic exchange within contracted muscles, although these mechanisms remain hypothetical in the absence of direct mechanistic measurements [18]. Previous studies in patients with TMD have reported changes in pain-related outcomes and selected stomatognathic measures, such as bite force or mouth opening, after TENS [19, 20]. Another previous study found that short-term TENS treatment can increase maximum clenching effort (MCE) EMG values and decrease mandibular rest position (MRP) EMG values in the masticatory muscles of patients with TMD [21]. However, these findings cannot be directly extrapolated to individuals with probable bruxism but without DC/TMD-defined painful TMD. Little is known about the immediate effects of TENS on localized masticatory muscle mechanical hyperalgesia, jaw-muscle activation, and mandibular movement measures in this population.

Thus, the present proof-of-concept study aimed to evaluate the immediate effects of a single TENS session on localized masticatory muscle mechanical hyperalgesia, jaw-muscle EMG activation, and mandibular movement measures in participants with probable bruxism but without DC/TMD-defined painful TMD, compared with sham TENS. The primary pain-sensitivity outcomes were the immediate changes in PPT at the masseter and temporalis sites from baseline to post-treatment. Thenar PPT was assessed as a remote reference site and was not considered a primary outcome. Secondary exploratory outcomes included mandibular movement measures and EMG activity of the masseter and temporalis muscles during MRP and MCE tasks.

We hypothesized that active TENS would increase masseter PPT and produce immediate changes in selected EMG and mandibular movement measures compared with sham TENS.

Materials and methods

The Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University approved the study (approval No. PJ2022-138-001) and informed consent from each participant was obtained after a full explanation of the research purposes and procedures. The study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR; Registration number: ChiCTR2400086817; Registration date: July 11, 2024).

This study was reported in accordance with the CONSORT guidelines.

Participants

Participants classified as having probable bruxism and presenting with coexisting localized masticatory muscle mechanical hyperalgesia were recruited amongst patients referred to the clinic of the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, P. R. China, from January 2022 to July 2022. A total of 52 participants fulfilling this operational classification were initially enrolled in the experiment. After applying the exclusion criteria, 12 participants were excluded and 40 other participants were randomized into 2 groups. The inclusion criteria were as follows: participants were aged ≥ 18 years; classified as having probable bruxism according to the traditional consensus grading framework; and presented with localized masticatory muscle mechanical hyperalgesia. In line with contemporary assessment terminology, bruxism classification in the present study was based on a combination of subject-based self-report and clinically based signs, without device-based confirmation. Specifically, probable bruxism was operationally defined as the presence of at least one self-reported bruxism-related behavior or symptom, such as awareness of tooth grinding or clenching during sleep or wakefulness, or frequent jaw-closing muscle stiffness, fatigue, or discomfort upon awakening, together with at least one clinical sign, including masseter muscle hypertrophy during voluntary contraction or tooth wear and/or shiny spots on dental restorations. No polysomnography or ambulatory EMG-based sleep bruxism recordings were performed. Participants were not required to report ongoing spontaneous pain. Localized mechanical sensitivity was operationally defined as pain or tenderness elicited during standardized palpation at one or more masticatory muscle sites, without fulfilling DC/TMD criteria for myalgia or myofascial pain. The coexistence of these features was used only for eligibility and does not imply a causal relationship.

All participants underwent a standardized clinical examination according to the DC/TMD Protocol for Calibrated Examination. The E1a and E1b sections were applied to document the presence of spontaneous symptoms and headache location. Only 3 of 40 participants (7.5%) reported mild spontaneous muscle or temporomandibular joint discomfort at rest, and none fulfilled the DC/TMD diagnostic criteria for myalgia or myofascial pain. All enrolled participants reported pain on palpation in at least one masticatory muscle site. However, this pain was elicited only during palpation or experimental testing and was not accompanied by ongoing spontaneous pain as defined by the DC/TMD criteria. Therefore, the study population should not be classified as TMD pain patients. Instead, participants were characterized as individuals classified as having probable bruxism and presenting with localized mechanical hyperalgesia, manifested as pain elicited during palpation or experimental testing rather than as ongoing spontaneous pain.

The exclusion criteria were a history of neurologic or psychiatric disorders; the use of prescription medicine or drugs with possible sleep effects or alterations of motor behavior; currently being under medical or dental treatment; having a pacemaker or implanted defibrillator; the loss of more than two posterior teeth excluding third molars; or having removable partial or full dentures. All participants were informed of the procedures and purpose of this study, and informed written consent was obtained from all volunteers before initiation of the study.

The rationale for studying this subgroup was not to evaluate treatment of bruxism itself or treatment of established painful TMD, but to explore whether a single TENS session could modulate localized mechanical pain sensitivity and jaw-muscle activation in individuals who may represent a subclinical phenotype. Whether this subclinical phenotype represents a true at-risk state for future painful TMD requires longitudinal investigation. This distinction is important because the presence of localized hyperalgesia may be clinically relevant even in the absence of DC/TMD-defined painful TMD, while the need for intervention should be guided by symptoms, patient concerns, and clinical context.

Study protocol

Participants were randomly allocated to the TENS or sham TENS group using a computer-generated random sequence created by an independent researcher who was not involved in data collection or analysis. Allocation was concealed using sealed, opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes. The envelopes were opened only after baseline assessments had been completed. No formal a priori sample-size calculation was performed because this study was designed as a proof-of-concept study. However, to provide an exploratory sample-size rationale, the primary outcome was considered to be the immediate change in masseter and temporalis pressure pain threshold from baseline to post-treatment. Previous TENS analgesia literature has reported a large short-term standardized effect of TENS compared with placebo when pain was assessed during or immediately after treatment [22]. Therefore, assuming a conservative standardized between-group effect size of 0.90 for the difference in change scores, a two-sided α level of 0.05, and 80% power, 19.4 participants were estimated to be required per group. This number was rounded up to 20 participants per group, giving a total sample size of 40 participants. Because directly applicable effect-size estimates for masseter and temporalis pressure pain threshold in individuals with probable bruxism and localized masticatory muscle mechanical hyperalgesia were unavailable, this sample-size rationale should be interpreted as exploratory. All participants were randomly assigned to two groups, with equal numbers in each: TENS and sham TENS (sTENS) group. Three different measurements (Pressure pain threshold (PPT), mandibular movements, EMG) were recorded by different devices (Fig. 1). PPT was assessed using a hand-held pressure algometer (Medoc Ltd., Israel). Different measurements of mandibular motor function were conducted with the use of K7 (Myotronics K7, USA): (1) EMG of masticatory muscles at mandibular rest position (MRP). (2) EMG of maximum clenching effort (MCE) at ICP: including assessments with natural dentition or a 10 mm thick cotton roll. (3) Mandibular movements (including mandibular protrusion, horizontal movements, and maximum opening distance). EMG values for a duration of 15 s were processed and analyzed using the K7 evaluation system and its supporting analysis software to derive average and peak values of continuous EMG [23]. The treatment was administered by a dental TENS device (J5 Myo-monitor; Myotronics-noromed, INC., Seattle, USA). A 10-minute rest period was observed between each of the three measurements (see Fig. 1). Data from the measurements (e.g., mandibular movements, EMG and PPT values) were collected both before and after TENS/sTENS application for all participants. The primary pain-sensitivity outcomes were the immediate changes in PPT at the masseter and temporalis sites from baseline to post-treatment. Thenar PPT was assessed as a remote reference site and was not considered a primary outcome. Secondary exploratory outcomes included mandibular movement measures and EMG activity of the masseter and temporalis muscles during MRP and MCE tasks. Because the study was exploratory, the secondary outcomes were interpreted cautiously.

Pressure pain threshold (PPT)

The masticatory muscles, including masseter and temporalis muscles, and right thenar eminence, which was used as a reference were carefully palpated using a hand-held pressure algometer (Medoc Ltd., Israel). The diameter of the probe for the assessment was 5 mm. The probe of the algometer was applied perpendicularly to the surface of the target site on the masticatory muscles. The pressure was increased at a constant application rate of 30 kPa/s [24]. Visual feedback of the pressure rate guided the examiners during PPT measurements. The participants were instructed to concentrate their attention on the test stimulus and to press the switch button as soon as they felt the slightest pain from the pressure change. The amount of pressure (kPa) at this point was defined as the PPT value. Three measurements per site were made at 1-min intervals to calculate a mean value.

Masticatory muscle electromyography

Changes in masticatory muscle EMG were recorded using a commercial dental neuromuscular analysis system (Myotronics K7, USA) before and after TENS/sTENS treatment. In a quiet room with a temperature of 22–25 °C, the subjects relaxed and sat upright. Their skin was wiped with 75% alcohol for degreasing and drying, and bipolar electrodes were affixed to the middle of the participant’s left and right masseter muscle and the surface of the anterior bundle of the temporalis muscle, aligned with the direction of the muscle fibers. The reference electrode was attached to the skin. The participants were asked to complete the following mandibular movements and tasks: (1) mandibular rest position (MRP). (2) maximum clenching effort (MCE): including clenching in the maximal intercuspal position or on 10 mm thick cotton roll; EMG activity for 15 s was recorded. All tests were performed by the same operator. Each participant repeated the measurements at least 3 times, and stable waveform data were recorded to calculate the mean. The interval between each experiment was 2–3 min to prevent muscle fatigue. The EMG signal was processed and analyzed using K7 evaluation system and its supporting analysis software, yielding the average and peak value of continuous EMG (µV).

Mandibular movements

The same recording system (Myotronics K7, USA) was used to record and analyze changes of mandibular movement track of each participant before and after TENS/sTENS treatment. The participants were asked to relax the head and neck as much as possible, look straight ahead, and the mandibular plane to be parallel to the horizontal plane. The experimental instruments were connected and calibrated, and the participants were instructed to do mandibular opening, left, right and protrusive movements. The mandibular movement trajectory and data were recorded with test software, repeated twice each time.

TENS /sTENS Treatment

A dental TENS device (J5 Myo-monitor; Myotronics-noromed, INC., Seattle, USA) was used for electrical stimulation of the masticatory muscles. The TENS parameters were rectangular pulse current waveforms with a pulse duration 500 µs and an interval of 1.5 s, with the output current set above the sensory threshold. The intensity of the stimulation was adjusted by the researchers based on the participants’ sensitivity and tolerance thresholds, ensuring that the stimulation was not painful and that there were no side effects, such as eyelid twitching. Two self-adhesive carbon rubber electrodes were placed on the bilateral masseter muscles and on the back of the neck. The TENS treatment lasted for 30 min, with a green light flashing. For the sTENS group, the same equipment was used with the same green light flashing for 30 min, but without electrical stimulation output.

To maintain the double-blind design, participants and outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation. The examiner responsible for group allocation had no communication with the examiner conducting the outcome assessments, and allocation information was not disclosed until the completion of data collection. Researchers informed the participants that using the device would lead to a new form of treatment, stressing that this would not cause pain and that it was perfectly normal to not experience any uncomfortable sensation or irritation during the treatment. To confirm whether blinding was successful, all participants were asked the question: “Which treatment do you think you have received?” after TENS/sTENS treatment. Although the sham device used the same equipment and visual indicator without electrical output, active TENS was delivered above the sensory threshold; therefore, potential sensory differences between the active and sham conditions were specifically assessed through this blinding question and are considered in the interpretation of the findings.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 statistical software (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA) and Statistica 13.5 statistical software (Statistica, TIBCO Software Inc., USA). To compare gender ratios between groups, chi-square tests were used. Age was compared between groups by using independent samples t-tests. A two-way mixed design analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to analyze the effects of the within-subject factors of time (2 levels: Baseline and After TENS/sTENS) and the between-subject factor of treatment (2 levels: TENS group and sTENS group) on PPT, mandibular movement distance, and EMG values of masticatory muscles. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean, and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. In cases where significant main effects and/or interactions were found, Bonferroni-adjusted comparisons were used to further investigate the differences. Raw values at baseline and post-treatment were used for the two-way mixed-design ANOVA. In addition, percentage changes relative to baseline were calculated to facilitate between-group comparisons of treatment-related changes. Percentage changes were compared between groups using unpaired t-tests. For multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied separately within predefined outcome domains: PPT sites, mandibular movement measures, MRP EMG measures, MCE EMG on natural dentition, and MCE EMG on cotton rolls. All reported between-group p values for percentage changes are Bonferroni-adjusted.

Results

Participants

Forty participants were enrolled, including 7 men and 33 women. The TENS group included 4 men and 16 women with a mean age of 24.4 ± 2.2 years, whereas the sTENS group included 3 men and 17 women with a mean age of 23.4 ± 2.1 years. There were no significant between-group differences in sex distribution or age. There was no significant difference in sex ratio between the two groups (p = 0.68), and the mean age was not significantly different between the two groups (p = 0.72).

In the TENS group (n = 20), 11 participants guessed to have received TENS and 9 guessed to have received sTENS, whereas in the sTENS group (n = 20), 8 participants guessed TENS and 12 guessed sTENS. Statistical analysis using chi-square test showed no significant difference between perceived and actual treatment (χ² = 0.45, p = 0.50), indicating that blinding was maintained. These results suggest that participants could not reliably determine whether they received TENS or sTENS based on their experience, supporting the effectiveness of participant blinding in this study. Nevertheless, because active TENS involved sensory stimulation above threshold, potential sensory differences between conditions cannot be completely excluded.

Pressure pain threshold (PPT)

There was no significant main effect or significant interactions for the PPT of right thenar eminence, which was used as a reference.

At baseline, masseter PPT did not differ between the TENS and sham-TENS groups. Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant interaction between time and treatment for masseter PPT (F = 8.160, df = 38, p = 0.007), whereas the main effects of treatment (F = 1.341, df = 38, p = 0.254) and time (F = 2.920, df = 38, p = 0.096) were not significant. Post-hoc comparisons showed a significant increase in masseter PPT after TENS compared with pre-treatment values (p = 0.013), while the sham-TENS group showed no significant pre-to-post change (p > 0.05). Between-group comparisons of percentage change were performed using unpaired t-tests (see Fig. 2). PPT values indicated a significant difference favoring the TENS group (Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.027).

At baseline, temporalis PPT did not differ significantly between the TENS and sham-TENS groups. Two-way ANOVA revealed no significant interaction between time and treatment (F = 2.325, df = 38, p = 0.136) and no significant main effect of treatment (F = 1.923, df = 38, p = 0.174). However, there was a significant main effect of time (F = 9.744, df = 38, p = 0.003). Post-hoc comparisons showed a significant increase in temporalis PPT after TENS compared with pre-treatment values (p = 0.011), while the sham-TENS group showed no significant change (p > 0.05). Between-group comparisons of normalized changes were performed using unpaired t-tests (see Fig. 2). The PPT values revealed no significant difference between TENS and sham-TENS (Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.094).

Jaw movement distance

Maximum mouth opening distance

At baseline, maximum mouth opening distance did not differ between the TENS and sham-TENS groups. Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant interaction between time and treatment for maximum mouth opening distance (F = 9.454, df = 38, p = 0.004), whereas the main effects of time (F = 0.081, df = 38, p = 0.777) and treatment (F = 0.179, df = 38, p = 0.675) were not significant. Post-hoc comparisons showed no significant change in maximum mouth opening distance after active TENS compared with pre-treatment values (p = 0.335), while the sham-TENS group showed no significant pre-to-post change (p > 0.05). Between-group comparisons of percentage change were performed using unpaired t-tests (see Fig. 2). Between-group comparison of percentage change showed a significant difference favoring the TENS group (Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.022). However, because the within-group post hoc change after active TENS did not reach statistical significance, this finding should be interpreted cautiously as an exploratory between-group change.

Maximum protrusive movement

At baseline, maximum protrusive movement did not differ between the TENS and sham-TENS groups. Two-way ANOVA revealed no significant interaction between time and treatment for maximum protrusive movement (F = 2.125, df = 38, p = 0.153), and the main effects of time (F = 2.316, df = 38, p = 0.136) and treatment (F = 0.844, df = 38, p = 0.364) were also not significant. Post-hoc comparisons showed no significant change in maximum protrusive movement after active TENS compared with pre-treatment values (p = 0.198), while the sham-TENS group showed no significant pre-to-post change (p > 0.05). Between-group comparisons of percentage change were performed using unpaired t-tests. Maximum protrusive movement values revealed no significant difference between the TENS and sham-TENS groups (Bonferroni-adjusted p > 0.05).

Maximum left excursion

At baseline, maximum left excursion did not differ between the TENS and sham-TENS groups. Two-way ANOVA revealed no significant interaction between time and treatment for maximum left excursion (F = 0.968, df = 38, p = 0.331), and the main effect of time was not significant (F = 0.300, df = 38, p = 0.587), whereas the main effect of treatment reached statistical significance (F = 5.332, df = 38, p = 0.026). Post-hoc comparisons showed no significant change in maximum left excursion after active TENS compared with pre-treatment values (p = 0.858), while the sham-TENS group showed no significant change (p > 0.05). Between-group comparisons of percentage change were performed using unpaired t-tests. Maximum left excursion values revealed no significant difference between the TENS and sham-TENS groups (Bonferroni-adjusted p > 0.05).

Maximum right excursion

At baseline, maximum right lateral movement did not differ between the TENS and sham-TENS groups. Two-way ANOVA revealed no significant interaction between time and treatment for Maximum right excursion (F = 0.933, df = 38, p = 0.341), and the main effects of time (F = 0.598, df = 38, p = 0.444) and treatment (F = 1.258, df = 38, p = 0.269) were also not significant. Post-hoc comparisons showed no significant change in maximum right excursion after active TENS compared with pre-treatment values (p = 0.712), while the sham-TENS group showed no significant pre-to-post change (p > 0.05). Between-group comparisons of percentage change were performed using unpaired t-tests. Maximum right excursion values indicated no significant difference between the TENS and sham-TENS groups (Bonferroni-adjusted p > 0.05).

Electromyographic characteristics of masticatory muscle

EMG activity during mandibular rest position (MRP)

EMG activity of the masseter muscle during MRP

At baseline, EMG activity of the masseter muscle during MRP did not differ between the TENS and sham-TENS groups. Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant interaction between time and treatment for EMG activity of the masseter muscle during MRP (F = 7.132, df = 38, p = 0.011), whereas the main effects of time (F = 3.220, df = 38, p = 0.081) and treatment (F = 0.763, df = 38, p = 0.388) were not significant. Post hoc comparisons showed a significant decrease in masseter EMG activity during MRP after TENS compared with pre-treatment values (p = 0.019), while the sham-TENS group showed no significant pre-to-post change (p > 0.05). Between-group comparisons of percentage change were performed using unpaired t-tests (see Fig. 2). EMG activity values indicated a significant difference favoring the TENS group (Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.017).

EMG activity of the temporalis muscle during MRP

At baseline, EMG activity of the temporalis muscle during MRP did not differ between the TENS and sham-TENS groups. Two-way ANOVA revealed no significant interaction between time and treatment for temporalis muscle EMG activity during MRP (F = 3.016, df = 38, p = 0.091), whereas the main effect of time was significant (F = 6.667, df = 38, p = 0.014) and the main effect of treatment was not (F = 0.028, df = 38, p = 0.869). Post-hoc comparisons showed a significant decrease in temporalis muscle EMG activity after active TENS compared with pre-treatment values (p = 0.003), while the sham-TENS group showed no significant pre-to-post change (p > 0.05). Between-group comparisons of percentage change were performed using unpaired t-tests. EMG activity values indicated a significant difference favoring the TENS group (Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.031).

EMG activity during maximum clenching effort (MCE)

Peak EMG values clenching on a 10-mm cotton roll

At baseline, peak EMG activity of the masseter muscle during maximum clenching effort (MCE) on a 10-mm cotton roll did not differ between the TENS and sham-TENS groups. Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant interaction between time and treatment for masseter muscle peak EMG activity during MCE on a 10-mm cotton roll (F = 7.490, df = 38, p = 0.009), whereas the main effects of time (F = 3.029, df = 38, p = 0.090) and treatment (F = 0.117, df = 38, p = 0.735) were not significant. Post-hoc comparisons showed a significant increase in peak EMG activity after active TENS compared with pre-treatment values (p = 0.003), while the sham-TENS group showed no significant pre-to-post change (p = 0.485). Between-group comparisons of percentage change were performed using unpaired t-tests (see Fig. 2). Peak EMG values indicated a significant difference favoring the TENS group (Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.003).

Discussion

In this randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled proof-of-concept study, a single session of active TENS produced immediate changes in pressure pain sensitivity and selected jaw-muscle EMG measures in participants classified as having probable bruxism with localized masticatory muscle mechanical hyperalgesia but without DC/TMD-defined painful TMD. The main finding was an immediate increase in masseter PPT after active TENS compared with sham TENS. Changes in EMG amplitude during MRP and MCE tasks were also observed. These findings should be interpreted as short-term sensory and muscle-activation changes, rather than as evidence that TENS reduces bruxism activity, treats painful TMD, or improves masticatory function.

Therefore, the present study should not be interpreted as supporting routine treatment for individuals with probable bruxism without DC/TMD-defined painful TMD or ongoing spontaneous pain complaints. Rather, it was designed to explore whether localized mechanical hyperalgesia and jaw-muscle activation in this subclinical phenotype could be modulated immediately by a single TENS session.

Effects of TENS treatment

The immediate effects of TENS treatment on localized masticatory muscle mechanical hyperalgesia in participants classified as having probable bruxism could be associated with the alternate frequency protocol used in the study. Alternating low- and high-frequency stimulation has been suggested to engage peripheral and central inhibitory processes, including endogenous opioid-related modulation. Such mechanisms may partly explain the observed reduction in mechanical sensitivity [25]. However, the present study did not measure opioid activity, blood flow, central inhibition, neuromuscular adaptations, or other mechanistic biomarkers. Therefore, these mechanisms should be considered plausible explanations rather than demonstrated effects in the present sample.

TENS is an inexpensive, noninvasive and generally safe intervention compared with long-term medication [26, 27]. Although previous studies in painful TMD populations provide useful background, the present sample differed because participants did not fulfill DC/TMD criteria for myalgia or myofascial pain. Therefore, findings from TMD pain studies should be interpreted only as indirect contextual evidence and should not be directly extrapolated to the present subclinical phenotype. TENS has also been proposed to reduce pain through mechanisms related to rhythmic stimulation of motor nerves, gentle muscle contractions, and local metabolic changes. This repetitive depolarization of masticatory muscle as well as gentle, rhythmic muscle movements may increase local blood circulation, which could theoretically reduce interstitial edema and the accumulation of deleterious tissue metabolites [28, 29]. In this way, pain is thought to be reduced, and the reduction of fatigue contracture in masticatory muscles is associated with an adequate supply of high-energy phosphate [30–32]. In the present study, the effects of TENS on temporalis muscle outcomes appeared less consistent than those observed for the masseter muscle. One possible explanation is that the electrode placement and stimulation pathway may have preferentially affected the masseter region rather than all synergistic masticatory muscles [33]. Also, due to the proximity of the temporalis muscle to the hairline, hair may interfere with electrode adhesion, potentially affecting the effectiveness of TENS treatment. As a result, the changes observed in temporalis PPT and EMG activity were relatively limited. However, because the present study did not directly assess blood flow, tissue metabolites, muscle metabolism, or high-energy phosphate availability, these explanations remain speculative.

Pressure pain sensitivity

PPT is a highly sensitive test method for mechanical pain sensation, and the sensitivity changes of patients with masticatory pain are prominent in the tenderness threshold. The tenderness threshold measures the sensitivity of deep tissue pain by stimulating C and Aδ fibers [34]. A hand-held pressure algometry was used in the present study to standardize the stimulus, thus allowing objective measurement and comparison of the pain experienced following the application of pressure. No significant differences in pain sensitivity pre-treatment were found upon the palpation of the masseter muscles and temporalis muscles [35]. The present results showed that active TENS was associated with an immediate increase in masseter PPT, indicating reduced mechanical pain sensitivity at this site after stimulation. Although temporalis PPT was defined as a primary pain-sensitivity outcome, the time × treatment interaction for temporalis PPT did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, the temporalis PPT finding should be interpreted more cautiously than the masseter PPT finding and should not be considered strong evidence of a treatment-specific effect. The observed PPT change may reflect short-term modulation of peripheral or central nociceptive processing, but this interpretation remains hypothetical because mechanistic measures were not included. All these findings are consistent with what has been found in a previous study [25]. In addition, to facilitate comparison across participants and time points, PPT values were normalized by defining the baseline as 100%, and subsequent changes after treatment were expressed as a percentage relative to baseline. This normalization approach allowed clearer interpretation of treatment-induced effects.

Electromyographic activity

Another notable finding was that active TENS was associated with immediate changes in surface EMG amplitude during both mandibular rest and clenching tasks. Reduced EMG activity during MRP may indicate lower resting jaw-muscle activation, whereas increased EMG amplitude during MCE on a 10-mm cotton roll indicates greater muscle activation during this specific task. However, higher EMG amplitude should not be interpreted as improved masticatory function in the absence of direct functional outcomes such as bite-force performance, chewing efficiency, endurance, or patient-reported functional improvement.

EMG activity recorded by surface electrodes can be used to study the electrical activity of superficial muscle fibers close to the electrodes. EMG tracks electrical signals coming from muscles; these signals are manifestations of excitatory processes generated by action potentials propagating along the membrane of muscle fibers. Therefore, EMG activity can quantify the intensity of muscle fiber excitation. Thus, lower EMG activity during mandibular rest may be consistent with reduced resting muscle activation, although surface EMG alone cannot fully characterize muscle relaxation, fatigue, or functional performance [36, 37].

The present findings demonstrated a muscle-specific effect of TENS on EMG activity, with a significant reduction in masseter EMG activity at the mandibular resting position, whereas the effects on the temporalis muscle were less consistent. This pattern may suggest a muscle-specific response to TENS, possibly related to differences in baseline activation, electrode placement, tissue characteristics, or sensory input. However, because the present study did not directly measure motor neuron excitability, afferent input, or muscle blood flow, this interpretation remains speculative. In line with this interpretation, previous studies have reported reductions in EMG activity of masticatory and cervical muscles following TENS stimulation [38]. Together, these findings are consistent with a possible neuromodulatory influence of TENS on muscle activation, although the present study did not directly measure the underlying neurophysiological mechanisms.

During voluntary maximal contraction after TENS treatment, participants showed a significant increase in both average and peak EMG values of the masticatory muscle, clenching on a 10 mm cotton roll. This finding indicates greater jaw-muscle activation during this standardized clenching task, but it should not be equated with enhanced masticatory function. The task-dependent increase may be related to altered sensory input, changes in vertical dimension during cotton-roll clenching, or improved tolerance of maximal contraction after reduced mechanical pain sensitivity; these explanations remain hypothetical. However, in the temporalis muscles, only average EMG values clenching on 10 mm cotton roll showed statistically significant difference. The EMG values clenching on natural dentition in the temporalis muscles showed no significant difference. Rather than reflecting occlusal factors, this finding may be related to task-dependent neuromuscular mechanisms. Clenching on a cotton roll slightly increases the vertical dimension, which may place the masticatory muscles at a more favorable length on the length–tension curve, thereby allowing stronger voluntary contraction. In addition, cotton roll clenching may reduce the influence of occlusal sensory feedback and fine motor control, facilitating a more consistent maximal contraction, particularly in the temporalis muscle [39].

Mandibular movement

Bruxism is characterized by repetitive jaw-muscle activity, but its clinical consequences are context-dependent. In the present study, mandibular movement measures were included as secondary exploratory outcomes to evaluate whether immediate sensory or muscle-activation changes after TENS were accompanied by measurable changes in jaw movement capacity. Mandibular movement is produced by the contraction of the jaw muscle [40]. Although previous studies in painful TMD populations have reported changes in mouth opening after TENS [41], the present study found that maximum opening distance showed a significant time × treatment interaction and a between-group difference in percentage change. In contrast, lateral and protrusive movements did not show consistent changes. This differential effect may be explained by the distinct neuromuscular control and muscle recruitment patterns underlying different mandibular movements. Maximum mouth opening may be influenced by changes in jaw-elevator muscle activity and resting muscle tone, which may be more susceptible to the short-term neuromodulatory effects of TENS. In contrast, lateral and protrusive movements require more complex and asymmetric coordination among multiple muscle groups and temporomandibular joint structures, which may be less responsive to a single, short-term TENS treatment [42, 43]. Because no chewing performance, bite-force endurance, or patient-reported functional measures were included, these movement findings should not be interpreted as broad functional improvement.

Importantly, the present study did not include direct measurements of sleep or awake bruxism activity, such as polysomnography, ambulatory EMG monitoring, or ecological momentary assessment. Therefore, the observed changes in PPT and EMG activation should not be interpreted as evidence that TENS reduces bruxism frequency, intensity, or behavior. Future studies should combine symptom-related outcomes with contemporary multidimensional bruxism assessment tools and objective recordings when possible. The distinction between localized masticatory muscle mechanical hyperalgesia and clinically diagnosed painful TMD should be emphasized. Although all participants reported pain during palpation or pressure testing at one or more masticatory muscle sites, they did not fulfill DC/TMD criteria for myalgia or myofascial pain. Accordingly, the present findings should not be generalized to patients with chronic painful TMD or ongoing spontaneous masticatory muscle pain.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although an exploratory sample-size rationale was provided, the study remained a proof-of-concept trial with a modest sample size; therefore, the findings should be considered preliminary and require confirmation in larger, prospectively powered studies. Second, the intervention consisted of a single 30-minute TENS session, and no longitudinal follow-up was performed; consequently, no conclusions can be drawn regarding cumulative effects, durability of response, or prevention of painful TMD. Third, bruxism was assessed using the traditional probable bruxism framework, based on subject-based self-report and clinically based signs, without device-based confirmation. Contemporary tools such as the Standardised Tool for the Assessment of Bruxism (STAB) [44] or BruxScreen [45] were not used, and no polysomnography or ambulatory EMG-based monitoring was performed. Thus, the study did not objectively quantify sleep or awake bruxism activity. Therefore, the present study cannot determine whether localized mechanical hyperalgesia was caused by bruxism-related activity. Fourth, the sample consisted of participants with probable bruxism and localized masticatory muscle mechanical hyperalgesia but without DC/TMD-defined painful TMD; therefore, the findings cannot be generalized to patients with chronic painful TMD, myalgia, myofascial pain, or spontaneous ongoing masticatory muscle pain. Fifth, although blinding assessment suggested that participants could not reliably identify their group allocation, active TENS was delivered above the sensory threshold, and sensory differences between active and sham stimulation cannot be completely excluded. Sixth, the study did not include mechanistic measures such as blood flow, neurophysiological testing, endogenous opioid markers, muscle metabolism, or central pain modulation assessments; therefore, mechanistic explanations remain hypothetical. Seventh, menstrual cycle phase was not controlled in female participants, which may have contributed to inter-individual variability in pain sensitivity.

Finally, the etiology or phenotype of bruxism was not classified in detail. Bruxism is a heterogeneous condition with multifactorial underlying mechanisms, and different bruxism-related profiles may respond differently to TENS [43, 46]. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be extrapolated to specific bruxism subtypes or broader painful TMD populations without further studies.

Conclusions

The present proof-of-concept study showed that a single session of active TENS was associated with immediate increases in masseter PPT and selected changes in jaw-muscle EMG activation in individuals with probable bruxism and localized masticatory muscle mechanical hyperalgesia but without DC/TMD-defined painful TMD. The immediate effects on mandibular movement measures were limited and exploratory. These findings should be interpreted as short-term changes in mechanical pain sensitivity and jaw-muscle activation, rather than evidence for modification of bruxism activity or treatment of painful TMD. Larger studies using objective bruxism recordings, contemporary bruxism assessment tools, repeated TENS protocols, and longitudinal follow-up are needed.
