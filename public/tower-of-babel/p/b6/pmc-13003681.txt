The impact of school-based disaster education on psychological resilience in high-risk urban areas of Türkiye: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Fevziye Dolunay Cuğ
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined the impact of a school-based disaster education program aimed at enhancing psychological resilience among teachers, administrators, and students in high-risk urban areas of Istanbul, Türkiye. Unlike conventional programs that focus solely on physical preparedness, this intervention emphasizes cognitive, emotional, and social components to strengthen psychological readiness. Using a quasi-experimental pretest–posttest-control group design, data were collected from six schools across three high-risk districts. The findings indicate that the intervention led to a significantly greater improvement in psychological resilience levels in the experimental group compared to the control group, with these gains sustained over a three-month follow-up period. While promising, these findings should be interpreted within the context of the study's limitations, including its sample size and relatively short follow-up duration. This study contributes to limited research on integrated, psychologically focused disaster education in urban contexts. The results highlight the critical importance of integrating psychological resilience into disaster preparedness policies and provide a practical, evidence-based model for enhancing school and community readiness in earthquake-prone urban areas in Türkiye.Keywords: Administrators, Educational leaders, Teachers, Students, School improvement

Introduction

Disasters, particularly earthquakes, are among the most devastating events that profoundly impact individuals and communities, especially in urban areas with a high risk. In addition to physical destruction, disasters cause significant psychological consequences, including posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, anxiety, and suicidal tendencies, particularly in heavily affected regions [7, 31, 53]. For example, following the 2023 Syrian earthquakes, the prevalence of PTSD and anxiety was reported to be considerably higher in severely damaged areas [49]. Similarly, in the aftermath of the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes in Türkiye, communities in high-risk zones experienced extensive psychological distress [12]. These findings highlight the urgent need for proactive, community-based, and culturally responsive interventions that address the psychological dimensions of disaster preparedness [38].

Psychological resilience as a community process

Adversity. First, Carver [8] defines resilience as the ability to return to the previous level of functioning or even move to a higher level of functioning after a stressful event. Initially seen as the opposite of vulnerability, the concept of resilience has evolved to encompass post-disaster development, transformation, adaptation, foresight, innovation, and proactivity [24]. This evolution marks a critical shift from viewing resilience merely as an individual trait to understanding it as a dynamic, multilevel process cultivated within social systems and communities. Furthermore, preparedness must be understood as a process that unfolds over the long term, as the mental health of affected individuals is often shaped more by the enduring psychological and material life changes catalyzed by the disaster than by the immediate event [27].

This community-oriented perspective is central to the theory of community resilience, which posits that a community's ability to adapt and achieve wellness after a disturbance emerges from a network of adaptive capacities [40]. These capacities—encompassing Economic Development, Social Capital, Information & Communication, and Community Competence—provide a concrete strategy for disaster readiness. Schools, as foundational community institutions, are critical nodes for developing these capacities. They are not just physical infrastructures but are embedded social ecosystems where trust, social support, collective efficacy, and competent action can be nurtured to buffer against the long-term psychological distress documented in post-disaster populations [27].

In post-disaster recovery, resilience is important because it mediates the relationship between exposure to disaster and mental health outcomes. Research has shown that disaster resilience serves as a mediating factor in the relationship between disaster exposure and the development of depression and posttraumatic stress symptoms [22]. This finding suggests that resilience may act as a protective factor and help individuals cope with the psychological effects of disasters. The concept of resilience is of particular interest in this respect, as it goes beyond individual coping mechanisms to include community-level factors. Community resilience and social capital are often recognized as advantageous for facilitating emergency responses and recovery [26]. These factors can enable collective action after a disaster by providing self-organizing, social, psychological, financial, and material resources [43]. Resilience has been identified as a crucial characteristic that enables individuals to cope with challenges and maintain their mental well-being. Increasing psychological resilience through interventions, especially during critical periods such as adolescence, has been shown to have significant positive effects on mental health and overall well-being [57].

As various studies have highlighted, effective disaster management and recovery are contingent on disaster preparedness and psychological resilience. Disaster preparedness is essential for building resilience and reducing risks at the individual and community levels. Household preparedness, including material, behavioral, and awareness aspects, is imperative for disaster risk reduction [52]. It plays a crucial role in the preparedness, response, and recovery of urban areas in the event of a disaster. The effectiveness of pre-disaster preparedness in enhancing the resilience of urban systems has been well documented [59]. Psychological resilience is particularly important during and after a disaster. Research has demonstrated that the presence of robust social group connections before a disaster is associated with a reduction in psychological distress and an enhancement in resilience 12–18 months after the event [11]. This finding indicates that disaster preparedness training should encompass both professional competencies and resilience-building strategies to ensure effective disaster response. While preparedness has been shown to reduce risks and improve recovery capabilities, resilience has been shown to support better mental health outcomes and community recovery. Investment in both directions has been demonstrated to engender more resilient individuals, communities, and health systems, which are better equipped to confront and recover from disasters (e.g., [2, 10, 52]).

Individual and institutional resilience are crucial for disaster preparedness, significantly contributing to community resilience and effective disaster management. Personal disaster preparedness activities, including awareness-related preparedness and public participation, are positively associated with community resilience [46]. Furthermore, disaster education mediates the relationship between school and student preparedness, emphasizing the importance of integrating effective school preparedness and disaster education initiatives [54]. The present study demonstrates that, institutionally, community resilience factors such as community connection maintenance, resource endowment, potential for change, disaster management, and information communication significantly affect residents' disaster preparedness [35]. Consequently, both individual and institutional resilience are pivotal for effective disaster preparedness. These measures contribute to enhancing community resilience, reducing vulnerability, and enhancing the capacity to cope with and recover from disasters. To enhance resilience, strategies should be developed that focus on building social capital, enhancing disaster education, improving institutional preparedness, and promoting collaboration among individuals, communities, and organizations [19].

Finally, psychological preparedness, defined as cognitive and emotional readiness to deal with disaster-related stress, is gaining recognition as an essential component of disaster readiness [1]. Although some studies suggest that this aspect is often neglected in training programs, others highlight its growing integration, particularly in the health and education sectors. Building on the community resilience framework, psychological preparedness in schools can thus be seen as an active process of strengthening the community's collective adaptive capacities before a crisis. This proactive, preventative approach is crucial for mitigating the adverse mental health outcomes associated with the significant life transitions that disasters impose [27]. For instance, research in Türkiye and China has established strong links between psychological resilience and reduced mental health burden among professionals following disasters [15, 53].

The present study: bridging the gap

Despite the recognized importance of psychological preparedness within community resilience frameworks, a significant gap persists in its operationalization in school-based disaster education. Existing programs, particularly in high-risk regions such as Türkiye, predominantly focus on physical safety and procedural drills, often neglecting the cognitive, emotional, and social dimensions of resilience [15]. This leaves school communities vulnerable to the long-term psychological distress that follows disasters [27]. To address this gap, the present study positions itself as a direct test of a preventive, psychologically informed intervention embedded in the school as a community hub. By applying the principles of community resilience [40] —specifically fostering social capital, communication, and collective competence—the program was designed to move beyond knowledge transfer to actively build adaptive capacities among all school stakeholders. Consequently, this study was guided by the following research question: How effective is this integrated disaster education program in increasing the psychological resilience of the school community? Specifically, does it lead to significant and sustained improvements in resilience among (1) teachers, (2) school administrators, and (3) students? The findings aim to provide empirical evidence for integrating psychological resilience as a core, standardized component of disaster preparedness policies in schools.

Problem and purpose

The literature suggests a growing recognition of the importance of psychological factors in disaster preparedness [19, 39, 44]. However, there is a need for more comprehensive approaches that integrate both the physical and psychological aspects of disaster readiness, as highlighted by the call for "long-term systematic planning for promoting relevant skills and safety culture" [19]. In the literature, school-based disaster preparedness programs generally focus on evacuation drills, physical safety precautions, and basic disaster awareness [25]. However, these programs often ignore the emotional and psychological reactions of individuals [55]. Addressing the psychological trauma that disasters may cause in advance is crucial for individuals to maintain their ability to function after disasters. Practical, reflective, long-term, and supportive interventions aimed at developing psychological resilience are needed.

Urban areas are increasingly vulnerable to disasters owing to climate change, complex infrastructure, and health inequalities [48]. Large cities, such as Istanbul, are particularly important in terms of disaster management because of past earthquakes and future risks. In this context, schools are not only educational spaces but also social centers where post-disaster intervention and social recovery processes can take place. Therefore, the psychological preparation of school communities for disasters plays a key role in increasing individual and community resilience. While most studies conducted in the field of disaster education in Türkiye focus on physical preparedness, the dimensions of psychological preparedness and resilience have been neglected [15]. The number of psychological intervention programs that enable teachers and administrators to remain functional after a disaster is limited. In addition, studies on holistic education programs developed for educators and measuring long-term effects are quite rare. With the motivation of filling this gap, this study was designed to examine the impact of a training program with an innovative and comprehensive structure, which aims to increase the psychological resilience levels of teachers, administrators, and students working in schools in high-risk districts in Istanbul. The program is not only based on the transfer of knowledge but also considers individual needs, focusing on the psychological dimension. The research questions are as follows:

Methods

Design

This study followed a quasi-experimental design with a pretest–posttest-control group design, selected for its high degree of scientific control over internal validity threats, qualifying it as a "true experimental design" [28]. The sample size was determined based on practical accessibility and voluntary participation from the available school clusters. Although a formal power analysis was not conducted, the attained sample was considered adequate for detecting meaningful effects in this exploratory quasi-experimental design.

The school-based disaster education program was a structured, multi-component intervention program developed by a research team of expert psychological counselors and disaster education specialists. It was planned to be implemented over 23 weeks in sessions, each of which lasted 90 min. The program was conducted according to a standardized guide to ensure the accuracy of implementation across all three intervention schools. Sessions directly related to psychological resilience were administered by a doctoral-level counselor. The intervention groups, who underwent a 23-week disaster resilience training program, were compared with a control group that maintained standard daily school routines. Pretest data were collected before starting the training program from six schools in both groups. The training program’s aim was twofold: first, to include administrators, teachers, and students in the disaster education process, and second, to contribute to the quality of life in society by increasing psychological resilience. The first step was to determine the needs of the target individuals in schools. Based on the results, research and problem-solving processes, cooperation, effective communication and teamwork, instructional leadership, innovative learning approaches based on cooperation in disasters, and constructive activity development topics that would increase students' high-level thinking and decision-making skills were addressed for a period of three weeks. Activities related to psychological resilience were also conducted. In this context, teachers were presented with a training program that included the definition of psychological resilience, concepts that support it, and ways to increase it. In addition, this program was designed and implemented for students based on an activity-based approach. In this context, the concepts of hope and cognitive flexibility, which are the most important elements of psychological resilience, have been emphasized. In addition, the teachers were informed about the “Psychological First Aid” intervention approach that can be applied to children and adults facing a serious crisis, and they were provided with the opportunity to gain experience by role-playing with the sample cases. Posttests were conducted after the program ended, and follow-up tests were administered three months later for both the experimental and control groups.

Research setting: population and sample

The population of this study consisted of administrators, teachers, and students at public schools in Istanbul, which is situated in a high-risk seismic zone, with scientific projections indicating a significant probability of a major earthquake occurring soon [16]. The city's dense population, complex urban infrastructure, and historical vulnerability to natural disasters heighten the urgency of preparedness efforts. Moreover, studies have shown that psychological readiness plays a crucial role in individuals’ ability to cope with and recover from disasters [18]. Focusing on Istanbul not only addresses a region of immediate risk but also provides insights that can inform disaster resilience strategies in other urban centers facing similar threats. Therefore, Istanbul provides a critical and relevant setting for testing the effectiveness of interventions aimed at enhancing psychological resilience in the face of earthquakes.

In response to this critical need, the study focused on schools situated in Istanbul. The selection process was informed by the Istanbul Metropolitan Municipality report (IBB [30]), which highlighted eight districts in the city as being at the highest risk for earthquakes. Schools from these districts were invited to join the study, and among those that volunteered, the six largest were chosen through purposive sampling to ensure both diversity and feasibility in the sample size. Ultimately, the study included three intervention schools and three control schools from the high-risk districts of Zeytinburnu, Güngören, and Esenler.

While deciding on the grade of students, the national curricula issued by the Ministry of National Education were first reviewed to identify the grade level most likely to be familiar with disaster education content and concepts. An analysis of primary and elementary school curricula showed that many relevant learning outcomes are taught in the 3rd, 4th, and 5th grades. Accordingly, it was decided to select 6th-grade students, those who had already completed these learning objectives— and their teachers as the target groups. Given the holistic nature of the training program, the administrators (principals and vice principals) of each school were also included in the sample to ensure data triangulation. Additionally, when selecting the schools to serve as experimental and control groups, careful attention was paid to ensuring similarity across variables such as school size, educational level, and physical infrastructure. A total of 1,613 students participated voluntarily in the study, comprising 823 boys (51.1%) and 790 girls (48.9%). In addition, 99 school personnel (84 teachers and 15 administrators) were involved in the intervention and control schools (Table 1). Each group (intervention and control) included 42 teachers and 15 administrators. Most participants were between 21 and 30 years of age, with a balanced gender distribution in the control group and a higher proportion of male participants in the intervention group. The majority had 1–10 years of professional experience and held bachelor’s degrees, while a few had postgraduate qualifications. Notably, earthquake exposure differed: only five participants in the intervention group had experienced a major earthquake, compared to 18 in the control group. None of the participants had prior experience in disaster education.

Data collection and analysis

To explore the effectiveness of the training program, the Brief Resilience Scale, developed by Smith et al. [47] and adapted to Turkish by Doğan [14], was used to test the psychological resilience of the administrators and teachers. The goodness of fit values as a result of confirmatory factor analysis of the adaptation data of the scale by Doğan [14] are as follows: χ2/df = 1.83,RMSEA = 0.05; GFI = 0.99; CFI = 0.99; and IFI = 0.99. The Cronbach's alpha value of the scale was 0.83.

Furthermore, to test the effectiveness of the training program for students, the Child and Youth Resilience Measure (CYRM-12) was used, developed by Liebenberg et al. [34]. The Turkish adaptation study of the CYRM-12 by Arslan [3] confirmed a unidimensional factor structure for this population, reporting excellent fit indices for a single-factor model (χ2/df = 2.03,RMSEA = 0.06; GFI = 0.94; CFI = 0.97; IFI = 0.97). The Cronbach's alpha value of the scale was 0.91. While the original theoretical model proposed three dimensions, the adapted version validated for Turkish contexts supports the calculation and use of a total resilience score. Therefore, in line with this validation study, our primary analysis of students utilized the total CYRM-12 score. All analyses for both adult scales (BRS) and the student scale (CYRM-12) were conducted and reported at the total score level, ensuring methodological consistency with the specific adapted instruments used in this study.

The tested hypotheses are:

H₀. The training program has no significant difference in administrators’, teachers’, and students' psychological resilience scores.

H₁. The training program has a significant difference in administrators’, teachers’, and students' psychological resilience scores.

Descriptive statistics were used to calculate the mean and standard deviation of each dimension and item of the survey before the intervention. The interval width was calculated using the formula 'range width/number of groups to be formed to determine the levels of occurrence based on the responses given by participants to the scale [51]. Accordingly, the interval width for all scales used was calculated as (5–1)/5 = 0.80. For evaluation, the arithmetic mean intervals were defined as follows: 1.00–1.79: “none”, 1.80–2.59: “low”, 2.60–3.39: “medium”, 3.40–4.19: “high”, and 4.20–5.00: “full”.

Before conducting inferential tests, the necessary statistical assumptions were rigorously evaluated. The normality of the data distributions for each group (teachers, administrators, and students) was assessed using both visual inspections (histograms and Q-Q plots) and the Shapiro–Wilk test, as recommended for the sample sizes in this study [42]. The results indicated that the pretest and posttest difference scores for teachers (W = 0.97, p = 0.15) and students (W = 0.99, p = 0.12) did not significantly deviate from normal distribution. However, the data for school administrators violated the normality assumption (W = 0.86, p = 0.02). Additionally, the data were examined for outliers using box plots, and no extreme values were detected.

Based on these assessments, parametric tests were selected for groups meeting the normality assumption, and non-parametric equivalents were used when the assumption was violated. Specifically, for teachers and students, paired-samples t-tests were employed to compare pretest and posttest scores, as this test is powerful and appropriate for related samples with normally distributed difference scores [21]. For school administrators, the non-parametric Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test was applied, as it is suitable for related samples that do not meet parametric assumptions [21, 50]. This approach ensured the appropriateness and robustness of the statistical conclusions for each distinct participant group.

The analytical strategy prioritized tests that directly addressed our primary research questions regarding changes within the intervention group over time. Although a mixed-design ANOVA could have been used to analyze the interaction between time (pretest, posttest) and group (intervention, control) simultaneously, the violation of normality in the administrator data and the focused aim of assessing the within-group efficacy of the intervention led to the selection of paired-sample tests. This approach provides a clear and robust evaluation of the pre-post changes for each key participant group. The parallel analysis of the control group with identical tests serves as a critical reference to differentiate program effects from general maturation or testing effects."

To examine the effectiveness of the training program on psychological resilience, paired-samples t-tests were conducted for teachers and students to compare pretest and posttest scores. This test is appropriate for within-subject designs when comparing two related means [21]. Cohen’s d was used to interpret the effect sizes, which were categorized as small (0.2), medium (0.5), and large (above 0.8) [9]. Furthermore, for school administrators, the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test—a non-parametric alternative suitable for related samples that do not meet the normality assumption—was applied [21, 50]. The Wilcoxon test is a non-parametric alternative to the paired-samples t-test and is suitable for assessing differences in related measurements [56]. Effect sizes for this test were calculated using the r.

For the follow-up tests, the effectiveness of the training provided to teachers and students was tested using a one-way ANOVA for repeated measures, as the data were normally distributed. Multiple comparisons between the three tests were conducted using the Bonferroni correction. As the data for school administrators did not follow a normal distribution, the effectiveness of the training provided to them was analyzed using the Friedman Test. For multiple comparisons, the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test was used. In this context, the follow-up test data, collected three months after the posttest, were compared with the pretest and posttest data.

Validity, reliability, and ethical considerations

Fraenkel et al. [23] emphasized that researchers should consider several factors that may threaten internal validity, such as subject characteristics, attrition, setting, measurement tools, testing effects, historical influences, maturation, participant attitudes, statistical regression, and the implementation process. In this study, due to the regulations governing the Turkish educational system, randomly assigning or matching participants across groups was not feasible. To minimize the risks related to participant characteristics, the researchers carefully selected schools that were comparable in key aspects, such as socioeconomic background, student population size, and earthquake risk level. In addition, the study utilized multiple pairs of experimental and control groups to evaluate the training program’s impact across diverse school settings in three different districts.

All intervention groups received training from the same instructor under similar classroom conditions using consistent instructional materials. Both the pretest and posttests were administered by the same researcher using the same measurement tools, thereby reducing potential threats related to setting, instrumentation, testing procedures, maturation, attitudes, implementation fidelity.

To further address threats such as historical influences, maturation effects, and regression to the mean, the sample included schools from districts with comparable disaster risk profiles, and the participants had no prior exposure to any disaster education training other than regular school curricula. Finally, the assumptions required for conducting the t-test were checked before statistical analysis. The reliability of the data was also tested using Cronbach's alpha values.

To ensure the participants’ confidentiality, only district names were disclosed, while the school names were replaced by codes; E1, E2, and E3 were used for the experimental group, and C1, C2, and C3 were used for the control group schools.

Results

This section presents the findings of the study on the effectiveness of the disaster education program in enhancing the psychological resilience of school communities. The results are organized according to the sub-questions of this study.

In summary, the disaster education program led to statistically significant and sustained improvements in psychological resilience in all participant groups in the intervention schools. Teachers and students demonstrated significant pretest-to-posttest gains, with moderate to large effect sizes, as measured by paired samples t-tests. School administrators also showed significant improvement, analyzed using the non-parametric Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test due to the non-normal distribution of their data. Crucially, a three-month follow-up assessment confirmed that these gains were maintained across all groups, indicating the program's lasting impact. In contrast, the control groups exhibited only minimal changes. The detailed findings for each group are presented in the following sections.

Discussion and conclusion

Within the scope of this study, it is anticipated that the psychological resilience of administrators, teachers, and students in schools where the education program is implemented will increase, thereby contributing to the quality of their social and cultural lives. To this end, an education program was conducted to promote a cultural transformation aimed at increasing resilience to disasters by making disaster preparedness a way of life for the target audience. The results revealed that the school-based disaster education program significantly increased the psychological resilience of teachers, school administrators, and students in the high-risk urban areas of Istanbul. The findings showed that the effects persisted not only immediately after the intervention but also after a three-month follow-up period. These findings contribute to a growing evidence base that highlights the importance of proactive school-embedded psychosocial support systems in crisis preparedness. While our program utilized structured psychoeducation and skill-building modules, alternative approaches, such as participatory arts, have also shown significant efficacy in fostering psychosocial adjustment and well-being during health emergencies [37]. This suggests that the active, experiential component—whether through role-play in psychological first aid or creative expression in the arts—is crucial in moving from passive knowledge to embodied resilience.

The results showed a significant increase in the psychological resilience levels of both the experimental and control groups of teachers. However, the more pronounced improvement in the experimental group indicates that the program had a greater impact on increasing teachers' psychological resilience than in the control group. A significant increase in teachers' psychological resilience reflects improvements in their ability to manage stressful events. This finding is also supported by Yalçın and Saygı [58], who emphasized that an increase in teachers' psychological resilience also strengthens their professional commitment. Furthermore, equipping teachers with these skills is crucial for fulfilling their leadership roles in the social functioning of the school following a crisis [13].

Findings related to school administrators revealed a significant increase in psychological resilience levels from the pretest to posttest in both the experimental and control groups [32]. This increase in psychological resilience may enable school administrators to be more effective in their managerial decision-making. Baytiyeh [6] and Ma et al. [35] emphasized that managerial capacity and organizational readiness are crucial for strengthening social resilience. In this context, psychological preparedness programs for school administrators appear to contribute to both individual and organizational resilience.

The results for teachers and administrators highlight the multiplier effect of investing in school staff. Equipping educators with resilience and psychological first-aid skills does more than enhance personal coping; it activates their latent role as community crisis responders. This transforms schools from passive recipients of external disaster aid to active nodes of psychosocial support. This aligns with the concept of schools as hubs for community resilience, a role that can be fulfilled through diverse methodologies, from the structured training presented here to more open-ended, arts-based participatory processes that facilitate communal expression and coping [36].

The observed increase in students' psychological resilience may be linked to factors such as social support, a sense of belonging, coping skills, and future expectations. The support students receive from their families and schools may improve their capacity for emotional regulation. Bachman et al. [4] emphasized the importance of maintaining consistency within the teacher-family school triangle to increase psychological resilience. Similarly, Xing et al. [57] demonstrated that interventions focused on psychological resilience are effective for adolescents when shaped around concepts such as hope and flexibility.

Overall, significant improvements in psychological resilience levels were reported for all the participant groups in both the experimental and control schools. However, the experimental group showed a more pronounced increase in pretest and posttest scores than the control group. The larger difference in scores between the pretest and posttests in the experimental group than in the control group highlights the program's effectiveness. The “moderate to large” effect size further supports the practical importance of this intervention [32]. Nevertheless, the improvement observed in both groups raises the possibility of a Hawthorne effect, in which participants’ awareness of being part of a research study may have influenced their responses or behaviors [33]. Additionally, natural maturation processes, such as personal development, emotional growth, and increased self-awareness during the study period, may have contributed to resilience gains in both groups [29]. Although the researchers carefully monitored school processes to ensure that the control groups did not receive any formal disaster education during the study period, the unique context of Istanbul as a high-risk earthquake zone presented additional challenges. In these areas, local municipalities and district-level Disaster and Emergency Management Authority (AFAD) offices regularly conduct public training sessions and awareness activities targeting adults. It is likely that students, their parents, and school personnel in the control schools were indirectly exposed to these community-based initiatives, which may have influenced their psychological resilience. Studies should consider these uncontrollable external factors and adopt strategies such as longer follow-up periods, mixed-method approaches, and qualitative interviews to better distinguish the specific effects of interventions. The results of this study support the notion that psychological resilience is a skill that can be developed and enhanced through structured interventions. This aligns with Carver’s [8] perspective, which characterizes psychological resilience as both an individual resistance mechanism and developmental capacity. The program’s focus on topics such as hope, cognitive flexibility, and psychological first aid for teachers and students is consistent with the suggestions in the literature on increasing resilience at both the individual and community levels [17, 21]. Furthermore, the study's findings align with the existing literature, which emphasizes the importance of integrating physical and psychological preparedness, as highlighted by Paton [44] and Baytiyeh, H. [5]. While traditional disaster training usually focuses on evacuation drills and physical safety, the program developed in this study offers a holistic approach with cognitive, emotional, and social dimensions.

These findings align with and extend the international literature on psychosocial support in disaster-prone regions. Similar to school-based interventions in Japan and New Zealand that prioritize long-term coping skills over mere drills, our program's success underscores the value of proactive, capacity-building approaches [10, 41]. The critical role of teachers and administrators as resilience multipliers mirrors evidence from post-earthquake initiatives in Italy, where empowering educators proved fundamental to community recovery [45]. Our results strengthen this paradigm by showing that targeted training can activate school staff's latent role as frontline psychosocial responders, transforming schools from passive infrastructures into active hubs of community resilience.

Implications and recommendations

The findings revealed that the intervention effectively enhanced psychological resilience among students, teachers, and school administrators. Significant differences in pretest, posttest, and follow-up test scores suggest that the program had a positive and lasting impact on participants' resilience levels. In conclusion, these results highlight the potential of targeted psychological interventions to strengthen resilience in educational settings in the future. The sustained improvement in resilience levels among various stakeholders—students, teachers, and administrators—demonstrates the broad applicability and effectiveness of this intervention. Future research should identify the specific components that contribute to the intervention's success and examine its long-term effects on other aspects of school life, such as academic performance, teacher retention rates, and the overall school environment. These findings imply that disaster education programs should extend beyond physical preparedness to include psychological components that foster community resilience.

This study is also particularly significant for policymakers in earthquake-prone countries such as Türkiye, as it shows that school-based interventions can serve as both preventive and recovery mechanisms for posttraumatic stress. Based on the results, several recommendations can be made for counselors: Guidance and psychological counseling education programs should be updated to incorporate disaster-focused psychological resilience studies, and school guidance teachers should receive professional training in this area.

Despite these promising results, this study has several important limitations. First, the quasi-experimental, non-randomized design may introduce selection bias and limit strong causal inference. Although a control group and pretests were employed, the influence of unmeasured confounding variables cannot be ruled out in this study. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted with caution and are primarily generalizable to educational contexts similar to the one studied. Second, the sample was confined to schools in high-risk urban districts of Istanbul, meaning that the findings may not be directly applicable to rural or lower-risk areas. Third, follow-up measurements were limited to three months, while indicating short-term sustainability, they are insufficient to assess the long-term integration of skills; resilience literature suggests that effects may require booster sessions or curricular embedding over years to maintain [20]. Fourth, the particularly large effect sizes observed for certain subgroups, such as school administrators, must be considered in the context of their limited sample sizes, which can inflate the magnitude of these estimates.

Future research should address these limitations by employing randomized designs where feasible, incorporating larger and more diverse samples, and conducting longer-term follow-up assessments (e.g., six or twelve months). Mixed method designs that integrate qualitative insights can further illuminate the mechanisms of change. Investigations exploring the role of teacher-student-family dynamics in resilience development are also warranted.

Ultimately, the findings underscore the fact that schools are crucial venues for enhancing disaster preparedness. In high-risk regions such as Istanbul, integrating psychological resilience and psychological first aid training into continuous professional development for educators within a broader social ecosystem approach that includes family participation is a critical step forward.
