Patterns of suicidal ideation disclosure among acute psychiatric inpatients
Authors: Erica R Garagiola, Yukti Bhatt, Hannah Lawrence, Victoria Witerska, Talia Rosen, Igor Galynker, Sara Bloch-Elkouby
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrior literature has explored disclosure of suicidality to formal and informal sources, identifying reasons for nondisclosure (e.g., fear of rejection, shame, and hospitalization); however, existing research is limited to retrospective data, often collected months or years after suicide attempts have occurred. The present study seeks to address this gap by identifying patterns of nondisclosure among recent suicide attempters within a one-week period following the attempt, shortening the temporal distance from event to recall. Adult psychiatric inpatients (N = 39, 38.5% female, 61.5% White) who had attempted suicide within the past seven days were recruited from a large urban hospital system in New York. At intake, patients completed a self-report questionnaire, which included two questions on suicidal ideation (SI) disclosure. Consensual Qualitative Research (CQR) methodology was used to extract and categorize reasons for nondisclosure. Nearly half of the patients (46.2%) reported not disclosing their suicidal intentions prior to their suicide attempt. Four key themes emerged as reasons for nondisclosure: fear of stigma (27.8%), fear of consequences such as involuntary hospitalization (22.3%), social isolation and lack of confidants (22.3%), and impulsivity (16.7%). Future research should examine targeted interventions to improve disclosure and explore how different disclosure patterns influence treatment outcomes.Keywords: Suicidal ideation, Suicide attempt, Nondisclosure, Psychiatric inpatients, Impulsivity

Introduction

Suicide remains a leading cause of death in the United States, with over 49,000 lives lost in 2023, equating to nearly one death every eleven minutes (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, [7]). Despite ongoing public health efforts, suicide rates continue to rise, particularly among psychiatric populations.

Suicidal ideation (SI) refers to preoccupation with thoughts of ending one’s life and is generally considered to exist on a spectrum of intensity from passive to active, determined by intent to put thoughts into action [24]. Assessment of SI has been a cornerstone of suicide risk assessment and intervention [1, 22]; however, the effectiveness of SI-based risk assessment is fundamentally dependent on patient disclosure.

Research has increasingly sought to understand SI disclosure by examining to whom individuals disclose their thoughts, including informal sources (e.g., friends and family) and formal sources (e.g., clinicians) [3, 6, 26]. However, much less research has focused on the equally crucial issue of nondisclosure, specifically among acute psychiatric inpatients, a population at particularly high risk [9, 18]. Specifically, a meta-analysis of 100 studies found that the post-discharge suicide rate of psychiatric inpatients within the first three months after discharge was nearly 200 times the global rate [8], highlighting the critical need to better understand the barriers to disclosure among this population.

Further, most existing nondisclosure research relies on retrospective data collected months or even years after the suicide attempt [3, 5, 23], introducing potential recall bias and limiting understanding of nondisclosure in the acute, high-risk period immediately following an attempt.

Understanding why patients choose not to disclose suicidal thoughts has significant implications for clinical practice and suicide prevention. The present study seeks to address critical gaps in nondisclosure literature by identifying patterns of nondisclosure among recently hospitalized psychiatric inpatients within a time-limited window of seven days post-suicide attempt.

Literature review

Factors influencing SI disclosure

Research examining whether patients disclose SI prior to attempting suicide has yielded mixed findings. Hallford et al., [10] conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis examining disclosure prevalence and found that only 45.9% of people disclosed suicidal ideation or behavior. Several studies have similarly shown evidence that nearly half of individuals do not disclose SI [15, 16, 21]. For instance, Høyen et al., [15] found that 51.5% of adult psychiatric inpatients withheld SI information during intake procedures. Further, in a large-scale Dutch study assessing 719 suicidal adults, Mérelle and colleagues [21] found 48% did not disclose SI. Social loneliness was significantly associated with nondisclosure, and age, poor health status, frequent SI, and severe psychological distress were inversely associated with SI nondisclosure.

Calear and Batterham, [6] provided additional insight into disclosure patterns by examining disclosure sources among individuals with SI. Their study revealed that 39% of participants did not disclose to anyone, highlighting a substantial gap in help-seeking behavior among at-risk individuals. Importantly, their study identified several factors associated with greater likelihood of disclosure, including severity of suicidal thoughts, younger age, and a suicide attempt in the past 12 months. These findings underscore why understanding nondisclosure patterns is critical for clinical suicide risk assessment, as traditional approaches that rely solely on patient self-report may miss individuals at highest risk who intentionally or unintentionally withhold this information. In fact, individuals who died by suicide often denied experiencing suicidal thoughts or behavior to a mental health professional shortly before their attempt, as seen in a study conducted by Berman [2], which found that 67% of individuals who died by suicide denied SI during assessments within two days of death.

Another study reported higher rates of disclosure, finding that over 75% reported disclosing their suicidal thoughts [25]. However, this finding still indicates that nearly one in four individuals (24.4%) did not disclose their SI to anyone. The study also revealed important demographic disparities in disclosure patterns, with Black and Hispanic participants less likely to disclose than White participants.

A large-scale population study in France revealed higher rates of nondisclosure to be associated with male gender, older age, lower education level, and poor social connectedness [16].

Differential disclosure patterns: formal vs. informal sources

Bloch-Elkouby and colleagues [4] examined SI nondisclosure and concealment among psychiatric outpatients to elucidate patterns of differential nondisclosure. Specifically, they looked at differential disclosure based on whether the disclosure was made in the context of treatment (e.g., clinicians) or in the context of research (e.g., non-clinicians). Results revealed that patients were significantly more likely to disclose SI to research assistants than clinicians, indicating that disclosure may be influenced by situational factors, such as the power of the confidante to delay discharge.

Rogers et al., [26] assessed the relationship between SI nondisclosure and Suicide Crisis Syndrome (SCS) as well as other suicidal outcomes. Results revealed that patients who disclosed suicidal intent to research assistants (but not necessarily to their clinicians) reported more severe suicidal ideation and higher rates of suicide attempts at one month follow-up, suggesting that disclosure to researchers, who have no impact on treatment decisions, may be a stronger predictor of future suicide risk than disclosure to clinicians. Additionally, patients who disclosed suicidal intent to anyone showed higher concurrent symptoms of Suicide Crisis Syndrome compared to those who denied suicidal intent entirely, but there were no differences in these symptoms between those who disclosed only to clinicians versus only to researchers.

Husky and colleagues [16] found that among a sample of 4,156 individuals with lifetime SI, half did not disclose to anyone, 6.3% did so with health professionals only, 20% with friends or family only, and 20% with both healthcare professionals and close others.

In a study by McGillivray et al., [20], researchers assessed reasons for nondisclosure to mental health professionals among a sample of Australian youth (ages 16–25), finding that 39% of participants did not disclose SI, most frequently citing concern that it would not remain confidential.

Motivations for nondisclosure

While identifying patterns of disclosure is important, understanding the underlying reasons why individuals choose not to disclose suicidal thoughts is equally critical to developing targeted interventions. Several studies have begun to illuminate the complex factors that contribute to SI nondisclosure.

Blanchard and Farber, [3] conducted research examining patients’ reasons for concealing SI in psychotherapy contexts. Their qualitative investigation revealed multiple interconnected factors that contribute to nondisclosure, including concerns about therapists’ reactions, fear of hospitalization, shame and embarrassment, and concerns about burdening others. Importantly, this research was conducted in outpatient settings, leaving gaps in understanding as to whether similar patterns exist among acute populations.

Fulginiti and Frey [9] similarly examined disclosure motivation, finding that the most commonly endorsed reasons for nondisclosure included fear of rejection, shame, and concerns about involuntary hospitalization. In their research, Fulginiti and Frey identified what they termed “avoidance-motivations”, indicating psychological and practical concerns that actively discourage individuals from seeking help or revealing suicidal thoughts. Further, Bloch-Elkouby et al., [4] looked at patients’ demographic characteristics and found that extraversion and trait anxiety predicted less disclosure.

Litschi and colleagues assessed perspectives on nondisclosure by interviewing clinicians who primarily treat military-affiliated patients, a group at increased risk of suicide, and found that fear of negative consequences and perceived stigma were commonly cited as barriers to disclosure among [19]. Other commonly cited reasons for SI nondisclosure among the existing body of literature include concerns about loss of personal autonomy or involuntary hospitalization; feelings of shame, embarrassment, rejection, and stigma; perceived social isolation; and the belief that they will not act on their thoughts [3, 5, 9, 14, 23, 27].

Despite the growing body of research on SI disclosure and nondisclosure, several gaps remain to be addressed. First, the majority of existing literature relies on retrospective data collection, often months or years after suicide attempts have occurred. This temporal distance introduces recall bias and memory constraints, failing to capture the immediate psychological states and circumstances that influence disclosure decisions at the time of the crisis.

Second, much of the existing literature focuses on outpatients or community samples, with limited research focusing on psychiatric inpatient populations. This represents a critical gap, as psychiatric inpatients represent a high-risk population for completed suicides. Further, the inpatient setting provides a unique opportunity to examine disclosure patterns in real-time, within days of suicide attempts, potentially yielding more accurate clinical insights.

Third, there is a need for qualitative studies to fully capture participants lived experience and the complex decision-making processes underlying nondisclosure. While quantitative research has identified broad categories of reasons for SI nondisclosure, qualitative approaches can explore the nuanced interplay between these factors to further the field’s understanding of the reasons for nondisclosure.

Finally, existing research has predominantly focused on disclosure patterns among individuals experiencing suicidal ideation, with limited attention to disclosure behaviors among recent suicide attempters. To our knowledge, no studies have specifically examined SI nondisclosure patterns and reasons for nondisclosure among individuals immediately following a suicide attempt, making this study a valuable contribution to understanding reasons for disclosure.

Present study

Accordingly, the present study addresses this critical gap by examining both the prevalence and underlying reasons for SI nondisclosure among acute psychiatric inpatients within seven days of suicide attempts. More specifically, the first study aim was to assess the prevalence of SI nondisclosure in the days preceding a suicidal attempt. The second aim was to explore the reasons that motivated individuals not to disclose their suicidal thoughts prior to making a suicide attempt.

Methods

Participants

Thirty-nine adult psychiatric inpatients (N = 39) were initially recruited from acute psychiatric inpatient units within a large hospital system in New York City. Eligibility criteria required participants to be at least 18 years old, and for recruitment to occur within four days of admission and one week of the suicide attempt. Exclusion criteria included being undomiciled, exhibiting aggressive behavior, lacking internet access or a personal email, not having a Social Security number, or having limited English proficiency that would hinder informed consent and study participation. These criteria were set to ensure safe researcher-patient interactions, enable proper compensation, and support necessary follow-up communication.

Measures

SI nondisclosure

Participants completed a self-report questionnaire at intake that included two ad-hoc questions on SI nondisclosure. Specifically, patients were asked yes/no, “Did you ever disclose your suicidal thoughts or intent to anyone before the attempt?”. If the patient selected “No”, the following open-ended question was asked: “If you did not disclose, what were the reasons you didn’t?”

Demographic measure

Participants completed a demographic self-report questionnaire that asked for information regarding age, biological sex assigned at birth, gender assigned at birth, sexual identity, gender identity, ethnicity, and race(s).

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed for patient demographics and rates of nondisclosure using IBM SPSS Statistics v. 27 (IBM Corp., [17]).

Qualitative responses regarding reasons for nondisclosure of SI were analyzed using CQR methodology, which emphasizes consensus among multiple coders and seeks to minimize individual bias [11, 12].

Procedure

Qualitative data analysis followed CQR procedures, which involves a systematic three-step process of organizing data into domains, core ideas, and thematic categories. This structured consensus process allowed for both depth and reliability in capturing the primary reasons participants gave for not disclosing suicidal intent prior to their attempt.

First, three trained researchers independently reviewed and coded the open-ended responses, organizing the data into broad domains based on frequency of occurrence and conceptual similarity. Second, within each domain, coders then determined the core idea of each response by developing a concise summary to capture the essence of the response. Third, coders met together to reach consensus on all domains and core ideas and then conducted a cross-analysis to establish thematic categories that reflected patterns across responses. After each of these steps, a fourth individual— a PhD-level clinical supervisor with expertise in qualitative methods—served as an external auditor and reviewed the final coding structure to ensure coherence, completeness, and reliability.

Throughout this process, coders worked collaboratively to reconcile any discrepancies, iteratively refining the coding through group consensus and referring back to the raw data as needed to ensure accuracy. Team members openly acknowledged their own biases and actively worked to reduce the impacts of these biases on data interpretation.

Representative quotes were selected to illustrate each category, and frequency labels were applied to each category to indicate the prevalence of responses according to CQR methodology: “general” (all or all but one case), “typical” (more than half of the cases), and “variant” (at least two cases but less than half) [12]. In this study, all categories were designated as “variant”, meaning each theme was endorsed by at least two but fewer than half of the participants. Because two participants did not provide answers regarding their reasons for nondisclosure, frequency labels have been calculated based on the 16 respondents who provided data.

Results

Descriptive statistics

A total of 39 participants were recruited for this study, ranging in age from 18 to 70 years (M = 30.85, SD = 13.24). The sample consisted of 38.5% female, 33.3% male, 2.6% non-binary, and 2.6% transgender participants, with gender data missing for 23.1% of participants.

Regarding race, 61.5% identified as White, 7.7% as Black, 7.7% as Asian, 10.3% as two or more races, 5.1% as another race, and 7.7% did not report their race. In terms of ethnicity, 28.2% identified as Hispanic/Latinx.

With respect to disclosure of suicidal ideation, 18 participants (46.2%) reported not disclosing their suicidal thoughts before their attempt, while 21 participants (53.8%) did disclose.

Of the 18 participants who reported not disclosing suicidal ideation, 16 provided qualitative data regarding their reasons for nondisclosure. Among these 16 participants included in the qualitative analysis, the gender distribution was 6 males (37.5%), 6 females (37.5%), and 1 non-binary participant (6.25%), with gender data missing for 3 participants (18.75%). Participants’ racial backgrounds were: 15 White (93.75%) and 1 Black (6.25%).

Reasons for nondisclosure

A cross-analysis was conducted using Hill et al.’s [13] Consensual Qualitative Research (CQR) methodology to explore the reasons that contributed to SI nondisclosure. Two primary domains were identified underlying the reasons for SI nondisclosure: (1) Motivational Factors and (2) Situational Factors. From these two domains, four thematic categories were identified: (1) Fear of Consequence, (2) Stigma, (3) Isolation, and (4) Impulsivity. Detailed distributions are reported in Table 1.

Motivational factors

Within Motivational Factors, two categories emerged: Fear of Consequences and Stigma

Fear of stigma

Fear of stigma was a variant reason and the most frequently cited factor for SI nondisclosure, with 27.8% of participants expressing concerns about being judged or receiving negative reactions. For example, one participant reported that they “felt too ashamed that they had become intoxicated to reach out to anyone.” Another participant stated, “I did not think anyone would believe me.”

Fear of consequences

Fear of consequences, such as involuntary hospitalization, was also a variant reason, reported by 22.3% of participants. One participant stated, “I didn’t want to be sent to the hospital,” while another shared, “I did not want to be stopped.”

Situational factors

The Situational Factors domain included two categories: Isolation and Impulsivity

Isolation

Isolation and the absence of a trusted confidant, another variant reason, reportedly prevented disclosure for another 22.3% of participants, with one participant stating that they had “No one to talk to,” and another sharing, “I wanted to deal with it on my own.”

Impulsivity

Impulsivity was another variant reason, as 16.7% of participants attributed their nondisclosure to impulsivity, describing their attempt as sudden and unplanned. One participant stated, “It was impulsive in the moment; I overdosed as soon as the thought came into my head.” Another participant reported, “It was an impulsive decision.” The remaining 11.2% of participants did not specify a reason for nondisclosure.

Discussion

The present study contributes to the growing body of research on SI disclosure by identifying key nondisclosure patterns among acute psychiatric inpatients. Findings indicate that nearly half of the participants did not disclose their SI prior to a suicide attempt. Fear of stigma emerged as the most commonly cited reason for nondisclosure. Other reasons included fear of consequences such as hospitalization, social isolation, and impulsivity.

The finding that 46.2% of participants did not disclose SI prior to their attempt is consistent with prior research demonstrating that a high number of individuals experiencing SI do not seek help or disclose to others [6]. The fear of stigma, reported by 27.8% of participants, aligns with existing literature indicating that concerns about judgment and negative societal perceptions contribute to nondisclosure [9]. Social isolation, cited by 22.3% of participants, reinforces the important role of social support in suicide prevention. This finding highlights the need for interventions that strengthen social connections and help individuals identify trusted confidants.

Fear of consequences was another barrier to disclosure, reported by 22.3% of participants. Concerns about involuntary hospitalization or interventions that could be perceived as punitive suggest that certain crisis response protocols may inadvertently discourage people from seeking help. This finding supports previous studies showing that those who anticipate negative consequences from disclosure are less likely to engage with healthcare professionals [3]. These results suggest that healthcare systems should examine their crisis response protocols to ensure a balance between safety, psychoeducation, and accessibility to care.

Impulsivity was identified as a reason for nondisclosure by 16.7% of participants, suggesting that some suicide attempts may occur without much forethought, limiting opportunities for disclosure. This finding is particularly relevant for crisis intervention strategies as it highlights the need for proactive approaches to suicide prevention, such as means restriction or readily available crisis resources.

Limitations

Although the study provides valuable insights into SI disclosure patterns, several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, participants may have underreported or misattributed their reasons for nondisclosure due to subject bias or social desirability effects. Second, the study was conducted within a single hospital system in an urban setting, with an inpatientonly sample, potentially limiting the generalizability of findings to individuals in other geographic regions, healthcare environments, or outpatient settings. Cultural factors and healthcare accessibility may influence SI disclosure patterns, necessitating further research in diverse settings.

Finally, because this study was conducted as a secondary analysis from an existing data set, the items on SI nondisclosure were not as specific as they could have been. For instance, in asking patients, “Did you ever disclose your suicidal thoughts or intent to anyone before the attempt?” there is no differentiation between formal and informal sources, which prior literature has demonstrated differences in disclosure patterns. Additionally, the single open-ended question about nondisclosure reasons, without follow-up probes, yielded limited narrative data for deeper analysis.

Future directions

Areas for future research include assessing SI nondisclosure variations (1) by clinical diagnoses (e.g., psychosis, mood disorders) to understand diagnostic-specific barriers to disclosure, (2) among culturally diverse populations (e.g., non-English speakers) to understand cultural factors influencing disclosure, and (3) by perceived social support to better understand the role of social isolation in SI nondisclosure. Exploration of online disclosure and nondisclosure will also be important as technology continues to evolve.

By addressing disclosure barriers through anti-stigma efforts, strengthening social support networks, refining crisis response protocols, and implementing proactive screening, clinicians can work toward creating more supportive environments that ultimately reduce suicide rates and encourage help-seeking behavior. Continued research into disclosure patterns, particularly among diverse populations and online platforms, is essential for improving suicide prevention and treatment outcomes.

Conclusion

The current study highlights SI disclosure patterns among acute psychiatric inpatients within seven days of a suicide attempt. Fear of stigma, fear of consequences, isolation, and impulsivity emerged as key factors influencing individuals’ decisions not to disclose suicidal thoughts before an attempt. These findings highlight the critical need for targeted interventions to encourage disclosure and improve early identification of individuals at risk for suicide.
