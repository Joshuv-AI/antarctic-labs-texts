The genetic insights of sporadic male infertility: a systematic review of WES and WGS studies (2014–2024)
Authors: Hoda Zahi, Fatima Sfifou, Meriem Slaoui, Lamyae Chentoufi, Redouane Abouqal, Houyam Hardizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMale infertility is a complex and heterogeneous disorder. It has a significant genetic component, although some cases remain idiopathic. Next-generation sequencing, particularly whole-exome (WES) and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) has become a powerful tool for uncovering new genetic causes. This systematic review aimed to identify and synthesize genes linked to male infertility reported in WES or WGS studies. The review was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024597301). A bibliographic search was conducted on PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science (2014–2024). We included human studies that used WES or WGS to investigate the genetics of male infertility. Two reviewers independently performed screening, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment with JBI checklists. Out of 8018 identified records, 23 studies met the inclusion criteria. In total, 169 unique genes were reported; after removing duplicates, 143 genes remained. The most frequently implicated phenotypes were multiple morphological abnormalities of the flagella (MMAF) and non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA). Genes were stratified by recurrence across independent cohorts and by functional validation status. Of the 143 genes, five were replicated with functional validation, 22 demonstrated either replication or functional validation, and 116 were reported in single studies with only in silico support. In studies applying the criteria of American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG), the diagnostic yield was 48% for MMAF and 12–23% for NOA, with an average variant-of-uncertain-significance (VUS) burden of 47%. ACMG classification was inconsistently applied across studies. MMAF-associated genes were predominantly autosomal recessive (95%), whereas NOA-associated genes exhibited greater diversity: 60% autosomal recessive, 25% X-linked, and 15% autosomal dominant. Only 34% of genes had undergone functional validation. In summary, among WES and WGS studies of predominantly sporadic cohorts, most identified genes were reported in single studies without functional validation, and standardized variant classification was implemented in only a minority of studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-026-00991-2.Keywords: Male infertility, Whole-exome sequencing, Whole-genome sequencing, Genes, Genetic variants, Systematic review

Introduction

Infertility is recognized a major global health problem, affecting 17.5% of adults worldwide (one in six individuals) according to the 2023 WHO report [1]. Male factors contribute to nearly half of all cases, with wide-ranging psychological, social, and economic consequences for affected individuals and couples [2, 3]. This highlights the pressing need to improve both access to and quality of sexual and reproductive health care.

Given the significant role played by male factors, it is essential to acknowledge that male infertility is a complex and increasingly frequent multifactorial condition [4], with highly heterogeneous phenotypic presentations ranging from azoospermia (complete absence of spermatozoa in the ejaculate) to various quantitative and qualitative sperm abnormalities. These include oligozoospermia (low sperm count), asthenozoospermia (reduced sperm motility), teratozoospermia (abnormal sperm morphology), and oligo-asthenoteratozoospermia (OAT), which represents a combination of these defects [5, 6]. Non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA) accounts for 15% of male infertility and encompasses distinct histological subtypes, including Sertoli cell-only syndrome, maturation arrest, and hypospermatogenesis [7, 8].

Causes of male infertility are also varied and include genetic mutations, lifestyle factors, metabolic conditions, genital trauma, varicocele, infections, cryptorchidism, and iatrogenic factors [9, 10]. Genetic factors are frequently involved. Up to 10% of cases result from genetic defects, including chromosomal aberrations, Y-chromosome microdeletions, copy number variations (CNVs), single-gene mutations, or CFTR (Cystic Fibrosis Transmembrane Conductance Regulator) gene variants [4, 11]. Notably, AZF (Azoospermia Factor) microdeletions account for about 10–15% of NOA cases and 5–10% of severe oligozoospermia cases [12]. However, 40–60% of impaired spermatogenesis remains unexplained. This proportion rises to 80% in moderate oligozoospermia [13]. Among the 40–60% of cases that remain idiopathic, an estimated 15–30% may have a monogenic cause, while the remainder are likely due to polygenic or multifactorial factors [14, 15].

Current diagnostic approaches rely on semen analysis, which provides fundamental information on sperm count, motility, and morphology, but fails to identify underlying molecular abnormalities [16]. Routine diagnostic work-up for genetic causes of male infertility includes karyotyping and AZF deletion screening for patients with extreme oligozoospermia or azoospermia, and screening for variants in the CFTR gene in case of (assumed) congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens (CBAVD) [17].

However, the genetics of male infertility is highly complex, given the involvement of up to 2,300 genes in spermatogenesis, as well as the large heterogeneity of semen and testicular histological phenotypes [18].

Whole exome sequencing (WES) and whole genome sequencing (WGS) has enabled the comprehensive detection of genes potentially implicated in male infertility. However, most identified genes have not undergone replication studies, functional validation, or standardized variant classification. Diagnostic rates differ among phenotypes, and a high proportion of variants of uncertain significance (VUS) continues to limit clinical utility.

This systematic review aimed to identify genes reported in WES and WGS studies of sporadic male infertility, describe the available supporting evidence regarding replication and functional validation, and place these findings within the broader literature on the genetics of sporadic male infertility.

Methods

We conducted a systematic review in accordance with the updated Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020). The protocol of this study is registered at PROSPERO (Registration number: CRD42024597301).

Search strategy

We conducted an in-depth literature search across the online databases PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus. Grey literature was excluded. The search strategy included terms related to “Male infertility” in combination with “Genes”, “Panel”, “Genetics”, “Genetic techniques”, and “Mutation” to identify relevant studies. The specific Boolean query was male infertility AND gene OR panel of gene AND genetics OR genetic profile AND genetic techniques AND genetic mutations. We also limited searches to human studies published in English or French within the last decade (2014–2024).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) included patients with syndromic or non-syndromic male infertility, who had already undergone genetic testing: Karyotyping, Y chromosome microdeletion analysis, and /or CFTR gene mutation testing. (2) Used Next Generation Sequencing (NGS), including WES/WGS technologies for genetic analysis to identify variants associated with male infertility in humans. (3) Used one of the following study designs: Analytical cross-sectional, cohort, case–control, or case series. (4) Included at least five participants to minimize overinterpretation of variants identified in small cohort.

Exclusion criteria

We excluded studies that met any of the following criteria: (1) focused on male infertility due to acquired causes such as gonadotoxic treatment, varicocele, orchitis, or history of cryptorchidism. (2) Mixed causes of infertility, including an identified cause in the female partner. (3) Use of animal models. (4) Insufficient data, or studies published as conference abstracts, commentaries, or duplicate publications. (5) Non-WES/WGS technologies: Used panel of genes, Multiplex Ligation-dependent Probe Amplification (MLPA), or Genome Wide Association Studies (GWAS). (6) Purely family-based or consanguineous cohort studies were excluded. Only mixed cohorts with a predominance of sporadic cases were retained.

Data extraction process

We compiled all identified studies into an Excel file and removed duplicates. Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts according to the inclusion criteria, with a third reviewer resolving any discrepancies. Studies that did not meet the criteria were excluded. Full texts of eligible articles were then reviewed, and those meeting the inclusion criteria were retained for analysis.

For each study, we extracted the following data: first author, publication year, gene, phenotype, inheritance pattern, functional validation status, and, where available, ACMG classification (Table 1). Quality assessment and bias

The risk of bias in the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) evaluation grids for cross-sectional studies, case series, and case–control studies. Two reviewers independently conducted the bias assessments.

Results

A total of 8018 articles were identified from PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. After removing 122 duplicates, 7896 records underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 7540 irrelevant articles were excluded. A total of 356 were evaluated based on the abstract. Following abstract screening, 218 articles were selected for full-text review. Subsequently, 195 articles were excluded according to predefined criteria. This left 23 studies for inclusion (Fig. 1).

Preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) flowchart of review methodology

The main reasons for exclusion were: (1) Irrelevant topic. (2) Studies that had results from animal models. (3) Articles without full-text. (4) Studies that did not use NGS technologies (GWAS, MLPA, Polymerase chain reaction (PCR), Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms Genotyping (SNP Genotyping) and Ribonucleic acid transcriptome (RNA transcriptome). (6) Studies addressing male infertility due to chromosomal abnormalities. (7) Systematic reviews, literature reviews, and meta-analyses. (8) Purely family-based and consanguineous cohort studies (9) Studies with fewer than five participants. (10) Studies using a custom gene panel for genetic analysis.

Of the 23 included studies, most were case series (74%, n = 17). Case–control studies accounted for 13% (n = 3), cross-sectional studies for 9% (n = 2), and case reports for 4% (n = 1). The studies appeared in 16 journals, most frequently in Human Reproduction (n = 5), The American Journal of Human Genetics (n = 3), and Asian Journal of Andrology (n = 3). Regarding geography, China accounted for 57% of the studies. France contributed 9%, Tunisia 8%, and Iran 7%.

MMAF represented the most frequently reported phenotype, identified in 14 studies, followed by NOA in seven studies (Table 1). Additional phenotypes included asthenozoospermia, OAT and ASS.

Of the 143 unique genes identified in the included studies, 19 were reported in at least two independent cohorts. Discussion

This systematic review aimed to identify and summarize candidate genes, both novel and previously identified, involved in various phenotypes of sporadic male infertility. The review exclusively focused on studies that used WES and WGS for genetic analysis over the past decade. Twenty-three studies met our inclusion and relevance criteria and identified 143 genes. The most frequently reported phenotypes were MMAF and NOA, indicating a significant genetic contribution. Among the identified genes, only five were consistently reported across independent studies and supported by experimental functional evidence. An additional 22 genes exhibited either independent replication or some level of functional support. In contrast, most genes were reported in single studies and relied primarily on computational predictions without direct experimental validation.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the genetic basis of sporadic male infertility remains insufficiently characterized. This underscores the necessity for replication studies and standardized functional validation to establish robust gene–disease relationships.

These genes are involved in multiple aspects of spermatogenesis, such as flagellar assembly, meiotic recombination, head–tail junction formation, chromatin condensation, and DNA repair. The findings of this review suggest that, in the analyzed WES and WGS studies primarily involving sporadic cohorts, the majority of reported genes are supported by limited evidence, with most lacking independent replication and direct functional validation.

Nineteen genes were reported in at least two independent studies, including FSIP2, CFAP251 (WDR66), FANCM, and TEX14 appeared in three studies, while DNAH1, DNAH2, DNAH12, CFAP58, SUN5, ACTRT1, DMC1, MEI1, MCMDC2, and MSH5 were reported in two studies each. This replicability lowers the risk that a discovery is due to chance or error. It supports their potential involvement in male infertility. However, of the 19 genes identified in at least two studies, only six have been functionally validated. This indicates that even among recurrently identified genes, experimental support remains limited.

The diagnostic yield varied by phenotype: 48% for MMAF, 40% for ASS, 12–23% for NOA, and 0.56% for OAT. These differences likely reflect the distinct genetic architecture of each phenotype. MMAF, which affects the sperm flagellum, has been associated with over 40 genes [42], thus, given the limited number of implicated genes, a high diagnostic yield is achievable. Similarly, ASS affects the head tail-junction and involves a very limited number of genes. Mutations in SUN5 and PMFBP1 are well known to be implicated in ASS [43]. In contrast, NOA results from genetic disruptions at any stage of spermatogenesis, from early cell development to final sperm maturation. Since nearly 2000 genes regulate spermatogenesis, diagnosing NOA is much harder, which explains its lower diagnostic yield [44]. For OAT, the low diagnostic yield suggests a more multifaceted phenotype, with its genetic etiology remaining unknown in most cases, and various factors contributing to its pathogenesis [45]. Additionally, this may result from patients with OAT often proceeding directly to In-Vitro Fertilization (IVF)/Intracytoplasmic Sperm Injection (ICSI) without extensive genetic testing.

Transitioning from gene identification to clinical application requires conclusive evidence of pathogenicity. Studies were reviewed that classified variants according to the ACMG guidelines. Only eight of the 23 studies applied ACMG criteria. Among the four studies that reported both P/LP and VUS classifications, P/LP variants were found in 10/21 patients (48%), 64/521 patients (12.3%), 4/10 patients (40%), and 12/149 patients (8%) in the studies by Oud et al. [30], Lillepea et al. [35], Zhang et al. [27], and Li et al. [29], respectively. The average burden of VUS was 47%, ranging from 33 to 69%. This indicates that there is insufficient or conflicting evidence regarding the role of these molecular alterations in male infertility. Therefore, VUS should not be used in clinical medical decisions [46, 47]. According to Gaikwad et al. [48], a multi-layered approach integrating clinical phenotyping, in silico prediction, and functional assays can reclassify up to 60% of VUS. This highlights the importance of combining clinical and experimental data to reliably assess pathogenicity. Therefore, VUS should have experimental data to assess its pathogenicity and enable reliable classification [48].

To move beyond gene cataloging, while avoiding overinterpretation of gene–disease validity, the identified genes were organized using a descriptive evidence stratification framework. In this framework, the two parameters used for stratification are: (1) recurrence of a gene across independent cohorts, and (2) the presence of experimental functional evidence supporting gene–disease association.

In this framework, five genes (FSIP2, CFAP251, DNAH2, CFAP58, and SUN5) showed both independent replication and functional support. Another 22 genes showed either recurrence across studies or some degree of functional evidence, but not both. Most of the identified genes (n = 116), however, were reported in single studies and relied mainly on in silico predictions without direct experimental validation.

These findings show that most candidate genes identified through WES and WGS studies in sporadic male infertility have limited supporting evidence. This underscores the need for independent replication and standardized functional validation before robust gene–disease relationships can be established.

In alignment with Oud et al. [49], who recommended restricting clinical application to genes with sufficient supporting evidence, These five genes may be the most promising candidates for inclusion in future clinical diagnostic panels. However, further validation and standardized gene-disease curation are required prior to routine clinical implementation.

Furthermore, as Zhao et al. [14] highlighted, genes with limited evidence should not be used clinically until they are validated.

Previous systematic reviews have reported varying numbers of genes associated with male infertility, which likely reflects significant methodological heterogeneity among studies, especially regarding inclusion criteria, cohort selection, and gene evidence assessment methods.

Oud et al. [49] applied a standardized clinical validity scoring method and identified 78 genes associated with male infertility, based on 1337 publications that included family-based and consanguineous study designs. Houston et al. [50] subsequently increased this number to 120 genes. Additionally, Robay et al. [51] identified 28 genes across 23 NGS studies.

A 2025 systematic review by Zhao et al. [14] identified 191 monogenic causes of male infertility from 229 publications, with 41 genes classified as having definitive evidence.

The relatively limited number of genes demonstrating both independent replication and experimental functional support in this review likely reflects important methodological differences compared with previous systematic reviews. Earlier studies [49, 50] frequently incorporated family-based and consanguineous cohorts, where segregation analyses across multigenerational pedigrees provided strong genetic evidence for gene–disease associations. In several cases, these findings were further reinforced by functional investigations in animal or cellular models, contributing to higher levels of gene validity assessment.

In contrast, the WES and WGS studies included in the present review predominantly involved sporadic patient cohorts and generally lacked consistent access to segregation data and other forms of high-level genetic evidence. Given the substantial heterogeneity of the included studies and the inconsistent reporting of key parameters required for formal gene validity frameworks, a comprehensive scoring approach such as ClinGen could not be uniformly applied.

Therefore, genes were descriptively stratified according to two evidence dimensions that were consistently available across studies: recurrence across independent cohorts and the presence of experimental functional evidence.

Collectively, these findings highlight the current limitations of the available evidence derived from WES and WGS studies in sporadic male infertility and underscore the need for large-scale replication studies, standardized functional validation, and systematic evaluation of previously reported familial infertility genes in genetically diverse sporadic populations before robust gene–disease relationships can be established.

Analysis of clinical implications and inheritance patterns indicates that MMAF genes are predominantly autosomal recessive (95%), while NOA genes exhibit greater diversity: 60% are autosomal recessive, 25% are X-linked (TEX11, USP26), and 15% are autosomal dominant (SYCP2, NR5A1). These inheritance patterns have significant clinical relevance. For X-linked genes, the recurrence risk for male offspring is 50% if the mother is a carrier of the mutation. This information is relevant, especially for genetic counseling.

Another clinical implication is the ability to predict outcomes of Testicular Sperm Extraction (TESE). Indeed, for patients with meiotic arrest genes (TEX11, TEX14, STAG3, SYCP2), TESE is ineffective; identifying these genes prior to surgery prevents patients from undergoing an unnecessary invasive procedure with no chance of sperm retrieval [52]. In contrast, for men with MMAF genes (DNAH1, CFAP43, FSIP2), TESE is often successful because spermatogenesis is preserved [42].

Another clinical implication is that, once a monogenic cause of male infertility is identified, preimplantation genetic testing for monogenic disorders (PGT-M) can be used to select embryos free of the mutation. This is particularly relevant for X-linked, or for autosomal recessive genes.

Limitations

Several important limitations must be acknowledged in this review. Family-based and consanguineous studies were excluded to focus specifically on genetic evidence derived from WES and WGS investigations in predominantly sporadic male infertility cohorts, which more closely reflect routine clinical populations. However, because familial and consanguineous studies have historically contributed substantially to male infertility gene discovery through segregation-based evidence, their exclusion likely resulted in an underrepresentation of genes primarily supported by inherited familial patterns and may partly explain the limited number of genes with strong supporting evidence identified in this review.

In addition, several key evidence components required for comprehensive gene validity assessment—including detailed segregation data, large independent replication cohorts, and standardized functional investigations in animal or cellular models—were inconsistently reported or unavailable across the included WES and WGS studies. Consequently, formal evidence-based frameworks such as those applied by Oud et al. [49], Houston et al. [50], and Zhao et al. [14] could not be uniformly implemented in the present analysis.

To avoid overinterpretation while enabling structured comparison across studies, the identified genes were evaluated using a descriptive evidence stratification approach based on two parameters consistently available across all included studies: recurrence across independent cohorts and the presence of experimental functional validation. This framework is not a formal assessment of gene-disease validity. It reflects the current evidence landscape in sporadic WES and WGS cohorts and is intended to facilitate research prioritization while complementing prior comprehensive assessments that incorporated familial and consanguineous cohorts.

The predominance of the Chinese cohort (57%) reflects both substantial scientific investment and potential publication bias, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to other countries, particularly regarding genetic data. However, certain genes, including FSIP2 and CFAP251, have been validated across diverse populations. FSIP2 has been reported in Tunisia, Iran, France, China, Poland, Norway, and the USA, while CFAP251 has been identified in China, Tunisia, Iran, France, Poland, Norway, and the USA. These genes have been validated across diverse populations and may be relevant across ethnic groups. In contrast, SUN5 c.381delA has only been reported in Chinese studies and appears to be a founder allele in the Fujian population [28]. Therefore, multi-ethnic studies are required to differentiate universal from population-specific genetic associations.

Although several studies included substantial participant numbers [34, 35, 41], the sample sizes were 314, 521, and 513, respectively. Larger cohorts are necessary to confirm and more precisely estimate the roles of genes or variants previously or newly associated with male infertility.

A further limitation is that only 34% of genes (49 genes) underwent functional validation, while the remaining 66% (94 genes) lacked experimental validation and were assessed solely through in silico prediction. Indeed, many variants were classified using bioinformatics tools and population frequency databases such as the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) and the Exome Aggregation Consortium (ExAC). Variants absent from gnomAD, ExAC, or other population databases were presumed rare. This rarity was used as supporting evidence of their potential pathogenicity.

Given the rapid advancements in this field, it is possible that new studies were published during the revision process. This review summarizes a decade (2014–2024) of research on WES and WGS highlighting the need for regular updates.

Conclusion

This systematic review identified 143 genes across 23 WES and WGS studies predominantly involving sporadic male infertility cohorts, highlighting both the rapid expansion and the current limitations of genetic evidence in this field. Despite the large number of reported genes, only five demonstrated both independent replication and experimental functional support, whereas most genes were identified in isolated studies and lacked robust biological validation. These findings indicate that the current genetic landscape of sporadic male infertility remains largely preliminary, with relatively few candidate genes supported by convergent evidence across independent investigations.

Diagnostic yield varied substantially according to phenotype, ranging from 0.56% in oligoasthenoteratozoospermia to 48% in MMAF, underscoring the marked genetic heterogeneity of male infertility and the greater diagnostic utility of WES and WGS in severe and phenotypically homogeneous conditions. In addition, the limited application of ACMG criteria across the included studies and the high proportion of VUS further emphasize the ongoing challenges related to variant interpretation and clinical implementation.

Collectively, these findings underscore the need for large-scale collaborative studies integrating genetically diverse populations, standardized variant interpretation frameworks, independent replication, segregation analyses when feasible, and robust functional validation to establish reliable gene–disease relationships and support future translation into clinical practice.
