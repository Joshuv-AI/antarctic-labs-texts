Capturing the multimodal dynamics of acute stress responses: Evidence from the Montreal Imaging Stress Task (MIST)
Authors: Wei Zhao, Pengrui Li, Dezhong Yao, Yun Qin, Tiejun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCapturing multimodal acute stress responses is important for understanding how stress is expressed across psychophysiological systems. Pupil diameter and electroencephalogram (EEG) are important indices for assessing acute stress responses. However, the relationships among behavioral, pupillary, and EEG responses during sustained stress exposure remain less well understood.MethodsWe collected multimodal data, including self-reported stress, salivary cortisol, electrocardiogram (ECG), pupil diameter, behavioral performance, and EEG from 33 healthy young adults during the Montreal Imaging Stress Task (MIST). Differences in responses between the training and testing conditions across difficulty levels were analyzed to characterize changes in the multimodal dynamics of acute stress responses across MIST blocks, and exploratory correlation analyses were conducted to examine associations between pupil diameter changes and relative EEG power changesResultsThe testing condition successfully elicited an acute stress response profile, reflected by increased self-reported stress, salivary cortisol, heart rate, and pupil diameter, alongside decreased behavioral performance and a shift in relative EEG power from lower to higher frequency bands. During the testing condition, behavioral performance improved and pupil diameter decreased from the medium to the hard difficulty block, accompanied by changes in relative alpha, beta (beta1, beta2, beta3), and gamma power. These findings may reflect adaptive-like changes over the course of the testing condition. Exploratory analyses showed limited associations between pupil diameter changes and relative EEG power changes, restricted to changes from the medium to the hard difficulty block in the testing condition and mainly involving frontal relative beta2 power.ConclusionOur findings suggest that acute stress involves coordinated but partly dissociable multimodal dynamics across MIST blocks and highlight the value of integrating behavioral, pupillary, and EEG measures to characterize acute stress responses.Keywords: Acute stress, Montreal Imaging Stress Task (MIST), Electroencephalogram (EEG), Pupil diameter, Multimodal dynamics

Introduction

In today’s fast-paced and highly competitive society, individuals are frequently exposed to acute stressors. Acute stress involves multiple systemic interactions, including the activation of the sympathetic-adrenal-medullary (SAM) system and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis (Herman et al., 2016; McEwen, 2007; Turner et al., 2020), which regulate central and peripheral physiological pathways and are accompanied by emotional, cognitive, and behavioral changes related to stress coping (Arnsten, 2009; McEwen, 2013). To characterize the dynamics of acute stress responses, previous studies have commonly adopted an anticipatory-reactive-recovery phase model (Vanhollebeke et al., 2022). However, acute stress responses are not determined solely by stress phase, but also influenced by task-related demands and individual regulatory processes, such as cognitive demands, time pressure, social-evaluative threat, continued task engagement, and adaptation or habituation (Angioletti, Rovelli & Balconi, 2025; Ehrhardt, Fietz, Kopf-Beck, Kappelmann & Brem, 2022; Hermans, Henckens, Joels & Fernandez, 2014; Kohn, Hermans & Fernández, 2017; Sinha, Lacadie, Constable & Seo, 2016; R. Wang, Zhen, Zhou & Yu, 2022).

Pupil diameter and electroencephalogram (EEG) are two useful indices for assessing acute stress responses. Pupil diameter has been widely used as an indirect index of activity in the locus coeruleus-norepinephrine (LC—NE) system (Grueschow et al., 2021; Joshi, Li, Kalwani & Gold, 2016) and is sensitive to changes in arousal, cognitive effort, and task difficulty (Faller, Cummings, Saproo & Sajda, 2019; Joshi & Gold, 2020). EEG provides a temporally precise measure of cortical activity, and acute stress has been associated with changes across several EEG frequency bands (Angioletti et al., 2025; Roy et al., 2025; Vanhollebeke et al., 2022). Stress-related multimodal research has reported pupil dilation alongside increased frontal delta and theta power during cold pressor stress exposure (Bullock et al., 2023), as well as pupil dilation alongside increased occipital theta power during fear images stimulation (Ebato et al., 2025). However, these studies have mainly characterized multimodal stress responses rather than directly testing the association between pupil diameter and EEG.

Previous studies have examined associations between pupil diameter and EEG activity in resting, fixation, perceptual, and cognitive task. During inactive wakefulness or fixation, spontaneous pupil fluctuations covary with alpha power, with alpha amplitude peaking shortly before pupil dilation peaks (Montefusco-Siegmund et al., 2022). In a near-threshold detection task, larger prestimulus pupil diameter was positively correlated with alpha and beta power, but not with theta power (Ruuskanen, Boehler & Mathôt, 2025). Under cognitive demand, pupil diameter increases with task difficulty, whereas alpha power shows modality-dependent changes, suggesting that pupil diameter and alpha power may not always track task demands in the same way (Kraus, Tune, Obleser & Herrmann, 2023). Together, these findings suggest that the association between pupil diameter and EEG may vary across task contexts and frequency bands. However, it remains unclear whether pupil diameter changes are associated with relative EEG power changes during acute stress.

The Montreal Imaging Stress Task (MIST) is a well-established computerized stress paradigm that combines arithmetic challenges, time pressure, and social-evaluative threat (Dedovic et al., 2005). It has been widely used to examine stress responses in healthy young adults, and prior work suggests that responses to the MIST may vary with task design, repeated exposure, and physiological system (De Calheiros Velozo et al., 2021; Dedovic et al., 2005). Previous EEG and EEG-fNIRS studies using MIST have mainly focused on stress assessment, stress classification, or prediction of perceived stress levels from neural features (Al-Shargie et al., 2016; Hag et al., 2021; Minguillon, Lopez-Gordo, Renedo-Criado, Sanchez-Carrion & Pelayo, 2017; Perez-Valero, Vaquero-Blasco, Lopez-Gordo & Morillas, 2021). However, fewer studies have used the MIST to characterize multimodal psychophysiological response patterns during sustained exposure to the MIST. It also remains unclear whether pupil diameter changes covary with relative EEG power changes during acute stress. To address this gap, the present study employed an adapted version of the MIST to characterize changes in the multimodal dynamics of acute stress responses across MIST blocks in healthy young adults. Specifically, we aimed to: (1) characterize changes in multimodal psychophysiological responses during the MIST and (2) examine exploratory associations between pupil diameter changes and relative EEG power changes.

Materials and methods

Participants

Thirty-three healthy young adults (7 females, age: 24.14 ± 2.03 years, all right-handed, non-smokers) were recruited via online platforms at the University of Electronic Science and Technology of China (UESTC). Female participants were required to report regular menstrual cycles and to be in the follicular or early luteal phase, to reduce potential effects of sex hormone fluctuations on stress responses. All participants were undergraduate or graduate students, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, no history of psychiatric or neurological disorders (e.g., traumatic brain injury, epilepsy, depression or anxiety disorders), no history of substance abuse (e.g., alcohol or drug dependence). They provided written informed consent and were instructed to obtain adequate rest and abstain from psychoactive substances (including alcohol and caffeine) during the 24 h preceding the experiment. At the end of the experiment, participants received appropriate monetary compensation. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee (Approval No 1061,423,031,025,334) and followed the Declaration of Helsinki.

Stress induction protocol

Stress was induced using a modified version of the Montreal Imaging Stress Task (MIST) (Dedovic et al., 2005). The protocol comprised two sequential conditions: a non-time-pressured and non-evaluative training condition (denoted as N) lasting approximately 12 min, and an acute stress-inducing testing condition (denoted as S) lasting approximately 8 min. Each condition included 3 sequential mental arithmetic blocks with increasing difficulty levels, labeled as easy, medium and hard (N1-N3 for the training condition; S1-S3 for the testing condition), and each block contained 60 trials. The easy, medium, and hard blocks were defined according to the objective complexity of the arithmetic problems, including the number of operands, the numerical range, and the permitted arithmetic operations. In the easy block, mental arithmetic problems consisted of two or three one-digit integers, and the operations were limited to addition and subtraction (example: 2 + 3 or 4 + 5–3). In the medium block, problems consisted of up to four integers, with up to two integers in the two-digit range, and multiplication was allowed (example: 4 + 76–80 or 10–45+9 × 4). In the hard block, problems consisted of four integers, multiplication and division were allowed, and all integers could be in the two-digit range (example: 69/46×18–20 or 84×99/84–94). All arithmetic problems were randomly generated by the program, and their answers were integers ranging from 0 to 9. Participants responded by selecting a single digit. During the training condition, participants completed the mental arithmetic tasks via keypress without time limit and social-evaluative threat. Participants were instructed to respond to the mental arithmetic tasks as quickly and accurately as possible. They were also informed that these tasks were solely for assessing their baseline arithmetic ability. In the testing condition, participants were required to solve mental arithmetic tasks under limited time and were told that their performance would be compared with others. The time limit was visually indicated by an orange bar. The initial time was set as the average reaction time corresponding to the difficulty level under the training conditions and after every three consecutive correct answers, the limited time was automatically reduced to 90% of the current limited time. Conversely, for three consecutive incorrect answers, the limited time was increased to 110% of the current limited time. This adaptation aimed to maintain a range of about 20%∼45% accuracy across testing condition (Dedovic et al., 2005). A red-yellow-green bar at the top of the screen displayed two performance metrics: the average performance of all participants (down-arrow, randomly positioned in the green area, labeled "excellent performance") and the participant’s real-time performance (up-arrow, initially randomly positioned in the yellow area, labeled "normal performance"). After each response, the result (correct, incorrect, or timeout) and performance metrics were displayed. Furthermore, participants were randomly given standardized verbal negative feedback (e.g., "Too slow, please speed up"). The MIST protocol was programmed in MATLAB (R2023b) using Psychtoolbox (3.0.19), allowing synchronization with pupillary and electrophysiological data collection. A black background of MIST was used throughout to reduce luminance-induced pupil fluctuations. The visual layout was optimized to minimize visual search for task-relevant information and response options, thereby reducing non-arithmetic task demands and eye-movement artifacts. Furthermore, participants responded by using the arrow keys to navigate a cursor and the "0" key on the numeric keypad to confirm their selection. The response interface allowed wrap-around cursor movement, enabling faster response selection while minimizing unnecessary key presses and muscle artifacts.

Procedure

The experimental procedure was conducted sequentially as illustrated in Fig. 1A. All participants arrived at the EEG laboratory between 13:00 and 16:00 to reduce potential diurnal cortisol fluctuation. The experiment was conducted in a dark, electrically shielded Faraday cage room, with the computer screen serving as the only light source. Upon arrival, participants received information about the experiment and signed the informed consent. EEG, ECG and eye tracker devices were then prepared. Participants were subsequently asked to rest for 5 min before completing the MIST. After each block, participants completed the 9-point Likert stress rating (ranging from "not at all" to "very strongly") displayed on the screen (Fig. 1B). Saliva samples were collected after the training condition and after the testing condition. Throughout the experiment, the true purpose of the study and the manipulated nature of the performance feedback were concealed from the participants. A full debriefing was provided after completion of the entire experiment.

Measurements of physiological and psychological stress response

Behavioral performance and self-reported stress measures

For mental arithmetic behavioral performance, the reaction time and response type were recorded for each arithmetic problem. The mean accuracy (ACC), reaction time (RT), and performance efficiency (PE) were calculated for each block. PE was defined as the ratio of ACC to RT. Self-reported stress was calculated as the average 9-point Likert stress rating within each block.

Salivary cortisol measures

Saliva samples were collected after the training condition and after the testing condition using Salivette® Cortisol (Art. No 51.1534.500), and were stored at −80 °C until analysis. Salivary cortisol concentration was determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and all samples were analyzed in a single assay using a microplate reader (Rayto RT-6100). Salivary cortisol concentration was expressed in nmol/L. Two participants’ saliva samples were excluded because of contamination, leaving thirty-one participants in the analysis (25 males and 6 females).

EEG, ECG and pupillary data recording

EEG signals were acquired using a 64-channel actiCHamp Plus amplifier (Brain Products GmbH, Germany), placed following the international 10–20 system, with FCz as the reference and Fz as the ground. The electrode impedances were maintained below 5 kΩ during recording. ECG signals were synchronously recorded with EEG through the amplifier’s adapter (BIP2AUX, Brain Products GmbH, Germany). The adapter used three electrodes, one placed on the left rib (+) and one on the right clavicle (-), with a ground electrode on the right rib. EEG and ECG were recorded using BrainVision Recorder software (Brain Products GmbH, Germany) at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz.

Pupillary data were collected with a Tobii Pro Fusion eye tracker (Tobii AB, Sweden). Online control of the eye tracker was implemented via Tobii Pro SDK (MATLAB_1.11.0.1), integrated into the MIST protocol developed in PsychToolbox (3.0.19). At the start of each MIST condition, the eye tracker was calibrated using a 5-point calibration. Pupil diameters from both eyes were recorded at a sampling rate of 250 Hz.

EEG processing and analysis

EEG signals were preprocessed using EEGLAB toolbox (v2023.1) (Delorme & Makeig, 2004) and custom MATLAB scripts including 0.5∼45 Hz bandpass filtering, 50 Hz notch filtering, resampling to 250 Hz, removing eye-movement and blink artifacts based on the independent component analysis (ICA), referencing to “infinity” zero using reference electrode standardization technique (REST) (Dong et al., 2017), segmenting data into 5-second epochs with 50% overlap from each block (discarding epochs <5 s), rejecting bad epochs by extreme value method (±60 μV threshold).

Relative EEG power indices were calculated using WeBrain EEGPipe (Dong et al., 2021). Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) was first applied to the preprocessed 5-second EEG epochs to calculate the absolute power at each electrode within each frequency band. EEG frequency bands were defined as follows: delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), beta1 (12–15 Hz), beta2 (15–20 Hz), beta3 (20–30 Hz), gamma (30–40 Hz), and full band (1–40 Hz). Relative EEG power indices were calculated as the ratio of band-specific power to full-band power. For each block, relative power indices were first averaged across epochs at each electrode and then averaged across electrodes within 12 predefined scalp regions according to the international 10–20 system (Table 1).

ECG processing and analysis

ECG signals were preprocessed using custom MATLAB scripts adapted from HRVAS toolbox (Ramshur Jr, 2010), including 1∼40 Hz bandpass filtering and R-peaks detection using the Pan-Tompkins algorithm (Pan & Tompkins, 1985). Detected R-peaks were then manually corrected by visual inspection. Mean heart rate for each block was calculated from the corrected R-peak series and expressed in beats per minute (bpm).

Pupillary data processing and analysis

Pupillary data of the left eye were preprocessed using custom MATLAB scripts adapted from an open-source pupil size preprocessing pipeline (https://github.com/ElioS-S/pupil-size). Blink-contaminated and invalid samples affected by noise or artifacts were removed from the pupil diameter time series. Cubic spline interpolation, implemented using the MATLAB spline function, was used to estimate pupil diameter during these removed periods. The signal was then smoothed using a bandpass Butterworth filter between 0.025 Hz and 4 Hz. Outliers, defined as values more than three standard deviations above the mean for pupil diameter changes, were labeled. Preprocessed pupil diameter time series were segmented into 5-second epochs with 50% overlap from each block. Epochs shorter than 5 s or with >50% missing data due to blinks or outliers were excluded, and mean pupil diameter, expressed in millimeters (mm), was then averaged across valid epochs within each block (Rojas-Thomas et al., 2023).

Statistical analysis

Salivary cortisol concentrations measured after the training and testing condition were compared using a paired t-test. Sex differences in cortisol change, calculated as testing minus training, were examined using a Mann-Whitney U test. For self-reported stress, mental arithmetic behavioral performance (including RT, ACC, and PE), heart rate, and pupil diameter, 2 (condition: training, testing) × 3 (difficulty: easy, medium, hard) within-subject repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) were conducted, with condition and difficulty as within-subject factors. Significant main effects or interaction effects involving factors with more than two levels were further examined using post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction. Sensitivity analyses additionally included sex as a between-subject factor in these ANOVA models. For the limited time during the testing condition, a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted across three testing blocks (S1-S3), followed by post hoc comparisons with Bonferroni correction. In addition, linear fitting was performed separately within each testing block to characterize trial level changes in the limited time and actual reaction time.

For relative EEG power, 2 (condition: training, testing) × 3 (difficulty: easy, medium, hard) × 12 (scalp region) repeated-measures ANOVAs were conducted for each predefined frequency band, with condition, difficulty, and scalp region as within-subject factors. The predefined frequency bands were treated as separate hypotheses, and no correction was applied across frequency bands. The main effect of condition was used to assess overall testing versus training differences in relative EEG power within each frequency band. When a significant condition × difficulty × scalp region interaction was observed within a given frequency band, follow-up 2 (condition: training, testing) × 3 (difficulty: easy, medium, hard) repeated-measures ANOVAs were conducted separately within each scalp region. For these follow-up analyses, P values for each effect were corrected across the 12 scalp regions using the Bonferroni correction. Scalp regions showing significant corrected condition × difficulty interactions were further examined using post hoc simple effects comparisons, including testing versus training comparisons at each difficulty level and pairwise comparisons among difficulty levels within each condition. For scalp regions showing a significant corrected main effect of condition, testing minus training contrasts were also examined. When the condition × difficulty × scalp region interaction was not significant but a significant condition × difficulty interaction was observed within a given frequency band, post hoc comparisons were performed after averaging across scalp regions with Bonferroni correction.

Exploratory Spearman’s correlation analyses were conducted to investigate associations between changes in pupil diameter and changes in relative EEG power for each pairwise difficulty contrast within each condition. The Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) was applied separately within each frequency band across the 12 scalp regions for each within-condition difficulty contrast.

For all ANOVA models, sphericity was tested using Mauchly's test, and Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied when the assumption of sphericity was violated. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27, GraphPad Prism 10, Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox (MATLAB R2023b), and R 4.5.2. All tests were two-tailed, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Self-reported stress, salivary cortisol, ECG and pupillary responses during the MIST

For self-reported stress, significant main effect of condition (F(1,32) = 130.077, P < 0.001, η² = 0.803) indicated higher stressfulness in the testing condition than in the training condition (N: 4.631±0.153, S: 7.040±0.170, P < 0.001). A significant condition × difficulty interaction effect (F(2,64) = 5.161, P = 0.008, η² = 0.139) was also observed. Post hoc analyses revealed that self-reported stress increased across difficulty levels during the training condition. In the testing condition, self-reported stress remained elevated relative to the training condition but did not differ significantly across difficulty levels (Fig. 2A). Paired t-test showed a significant increase in salivary cortisol concentration from the training to the testing condition (N: 7.254±1.670 nmol/L, S: 11.42±2.442 nmol/L, t(30) = 6.890, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). The ANOVA of heart rate showed a significant main effect of condition (F(1,32) = 10.722, P = 0.003, η² = 0.251), confirming a higher heart rate during testing condition (N: 75.061±1.705 bpm, S: 77.237±1.771 bpm, P = 0.003) (Fig. 2C). For pupillary response, significant main effects of condition (F(1,32) = 18.433, P < 0.001, η² = 0.365), difficulty (F(1.515,48.482) = 33.998, P < 0.001, η² = 0.515), and condition × difficulty interaction (F(1.505,48.170) = 5.501, P = 0.012, η² = 0.147) (Fig. 2D) were observed. Post hoc comparisons showed that pupil diameters increased with difficulty levels during the training condition (N1pupil: 3.744±0.134 mm, N2pupil: 3.876±0.150 mm, N3pupil: 3.951±0.152 mm). In the testing condition, pupil diameter showed an inverted U-shaped pattern, with larger pupil diameter at S2 than at S1 and S3 (S1pupil: 3.928±0.140 mm, S2pupil: 4.093±0.146 mm, S3pupil: 4.037±0.141 mm). Mixed-design ANOVAs including sex as a between-subject factor revealed no significant sex-related effects for self-reported stress, heart rate, and pupil diameter, detailed results are provided in Supplementary Table S1 to S3. Similarly, no significant sex-related difference was observed in cortisol change (Mann-Whitney U = 42, P = 0.105) (Fig. S1).

Behavioral performance during the MIST

For behavioral performance, the ANOVA results for mean ACC, mean RT, and mean PE were summarized in Table 2. Post hoc analyses of the interaction effects for mean ACC (Fig. 3A), mean RT (Fig. 3B), and mean PE (Fig. 3C), revealed distinct behavioral patterns between the training and testing conditions. During the training condition, as difficulty levels increased, ACC showed a significant linear decline (N1ACC: 0.794±0.002, N2ACC: 0.757±0.006, N3ACC: 0.718±0.011), RT showed a progressive increase (N1RT: 1.727±0.056, N2RT: 4.164±0.150, N3RT: 5.766±0.221), and PE showed a continuous decrease (N1PE: 0.475±0.015, N2PE: 0.190±0.007, N3PE: 0.131±0.007). In contrast, the testing condition induced a non-linear behavioral pattern. From S1 to S2, RT increased (S1RT: 1.230±0.030, S2RT: 2.766±0.088), and PE decreased (S1PE: 0.425±0.012, S2PE: 0.195±0.006), whereas ACC did not differ significantly between these two blocks (S1ACC: 0.514±0.011; S2ACC: 0.522±0.007). From S2 to S3, ACC and PE increased (S3ACC: 0.618±0.011; S3PE: 0.235±0.009), whereas RT (S3RT: 2.692±0.071) did not differ significantly from S2. For the limited time during the testing condition, the one-way repeated-measures ANOVA showed a significant effect of testing block, limited time was significantly longer in S2 and S3 than in S1, whereas no significant difference was observed between S2 and S3 (Fig. 3D and E). Linear fitting within each testing block revealed generally negative slopes for both limited time and actual reaction time (Table 3), indicating that the limited time and actual reaction time tended to decrease across trials within each testing block. Mixed-design ANOVAs including sex as a between-subject factor showed no significant sex-related effects for ACC, RT and PE, detailed results are provided in Supplementary Table S4 to S6.

Relative EEG power responses during the MIST

The results of the three-way repeated measures ANOVAs for seven frequency bands are provided in Supplementary Table S7-S13. Significant main effects of condition were observed in the delta, theta, beta1, beta2, beta3, and gamma bands, with lower relative delta and theta power and higher relative beta1, beta2, beta3, and gamma power in the testing condition than in the training condition after averaging across difficulty levels and scalp regions. Significant condition × difficulty × scalp region interactions were observed in the delta, theta, alpha, beta3, and gamma bands, but not in the beta1 and beta2 bands.

For frequency bands with significant three-way interactions, follow-up two-way ANOVAs were conducted separately within each scalp region to identify significant condition main effects (Supplementary Table S14) and condition × difficulty interactions (Supplementary Table S15). Significant testing versus training differences were mainly observed in the overall S-N, S1-N1, and S2-N2 contrasts, with no significant differences in the S3-N3 contrast. In the overall S-N contrast, relative delta power was lower during the testing condition in the left temporal region, and relative theta power was lower in right central, left parietal, left temporal, and medial central regions. Relative beta3 power was higher during the testing condition in left central, left parietal, left temporal, and medial parietal regions, whereas relative gamma power was higher in the left temporal region. For S1-N1 and S2-N2 contrasts, lower relative delta and theta power during the testing condition was confined to temporal regions, and lower relative alpha power was observed in the medial parietal region. In contrast, higher relative beta3 and gamma power during the testing condition showed broader distributions, with beta3 effects involving central, parietal, temporal, and midline regions and gamma effects involving right frontal, right parietal, bilateral temporal, and medial parietal regions (Fig. 4).

Within-condition comparisons across difficulty levels showed distinct patterns in the training and testing conditions (Fig. 5). During the training condition, relative delta and theta power decreased at higher difficulty levels mainly in temporal regions. Relative alpha power peaked at N2 in the left temporal region and decreased at N3 in occipital and medial parietal regions. Relative beta3 and gamma power increased at higher difficulty levels during the training condition, with beta3 effects distributed across central, parietal, temporal, and midline regions and gamma effects mainly involving right frontal, right parietal, bilateral temporal, and medial parietal regions. During the testing condition, relative delta power increased from S2 to S3 in the right temporal region, and relative alpha power increased from S1 to later difficulty levels in temporal, occipital, and medial parietal regions. Relative beta3 and gamma power decreased from earlier testing levels to S3, with these decreases restricted to bilateral temporal regions.

Spearman correlation analyses were conducted between pupil diameter changes and relative EEG power changes during within-condition difficulty contrasts. After FDR correction across the 12 scalp regions within each frequency band and contrast, significant positive correlations were observed only for the S3-S2 contrast in the beta2 band (Fig. 6). The S3-S2 correlation results are provided in Supplementary Table S18. No other within-condition contrast survived FDR correction. Discussion

This study investigated multimodal psychophysiological responses during an adapted MIST in healthy young adults. The testing condition of the MIST elicited an acute stress response profile, characterized by increased self-reported stress, salivary cortisol, heart rate, and pupil diameter, alongside decreased behavioral performance and a shift in relative EEG power from lower to higher frequency bands. Within the testing condition, improved behavioral performance from S2 to S3 was accompanied by decreased pupil diameter and changes in relative alpha, beta (beta1, beta2, beta3), and gamma power, which may reflect adaptive-like changes during the testing condition. Exploratory analyses provided limited evidence of associations between pupil diameter changes and relative EEG power changes, restricted to the comparison between S2 and S3.

In the training condition, participants exhibited decreased ACC, reduced PE, prolonged RT, and increased pupil diameter from N1 to N3. This pattern is consistent with Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller, 1988), and may suggest increased cognitive demand in response to escalating cognitive load, although the fixed easy to hard order prevents a complete separation of difficulty effects from time on task. In contrast, behavioral performance and pupil diameter displayed a nonlinear pattern in the testing condition. At S1, participants showed the shortest RT and the lowest ACC, suggesting a speed over accuracy response tendency. At S2, despite longer RT, ACC remained low while pupil diameter increased to its peak. At S3, RT did not differ significantly from S2, whereas ACC and PE increased and pupil diameter decreased from its peak. This transition from S2 to S3 may reflect adaptive-like changes in behavioral and pupillary responses during the testing condition. Additional trial level analyses of the testing condition showed that both the limited time and actual RT had negative slopes within S2 and S3, and the overall limited time did not differ significantly between these two blocks. Thus, the improved ACC at S3 and the concurrent decrease in pupil diameter are unlikely to be explained solely by a longer limited time. Instead, these findings may indicate more efficient task performance or a reduction in excessive arousal at S3, a pattern broadly compatible with the Yerkes-Dodson law (Faller et al., 2019; Yerkes & Dodson, 1908).

Analysis of relative EEG power showed that the MIST elicited distinct EEG responses across frequency bands and scalp regions. Considering both the ANOVA results and the topographical patterns, the testing condition showed a general tendency toward lower relative delta, theta, and alpha power, but higher relative power in the beta (beta1, beta2, beta3), and gamma bands than the training condition. This tendency suggests that the testing condition was associated with a shift in relative EEG power from lower to higher frequency bands and is broadly consistent with previous EEG studies. In an acute stress study using an aversive video paradigm, reduced low frequency power in the 4–15 Hz range, and increased gamma power were observed mainly over frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital regions (Roy et al., 2025). Increases in beta and gamma power during stress performance relative to preparation has also been observed in a modified Trier Social Stress Test (Angioletti et al., 2025). The two conditions showed different changes across task blocks. During the training condition, relative beta3 and gamma power generally increased from N1 to N3. Beta3 power has been shown to contribute to cognitive load classification during arithmetic tasks (Plechawska-Wójcik, Tokovarov, Kaczorowska & Zapała, 2019), and increased gamma power has been shown to differentiate working memory load levels and to involve the left temporal region during an n-back task (S. Wang, Gwizdka & Chaovalitwongse, 2015). The findings suggest that the increases in relative beta3 and gamma power during the training condition may be related to greater cognitive demand. Given the fixed easy to hard order of task difficulty, these increases cannot be attributed exclusively to difficulty and may also include effects of time on task or block order. In contrast, relative beta3 and gamma power did not continue to increase from S1 to S3, but decreased from earlier testing levels to S3, with these decreases mainly observed in temporal regions. Together with the absence of significant S3-N3 contrasts in all frequency bands, this pattern contrasts with the increase observed from N1 to N3. Thus, the testing condition did not show a simple increase in relative beta3 and gamma power with higher difficulty, suggesting that the later testing responses were not driven primarily by increasing arithmetic difficulty. A similar but scalp averaged pattern was observed in the beta1 and beta2. During the testing condition, relative beta1 and beta2 power were higher at S2 than at S3. Considering that broader beta activity has been linked to ongoing cognitive processing in stress EEG research (Vanhollebeke et al., 2022), the decrease in relative beta (beta1, beta2, and beta3) power from S2 to S3 may be related to reduced task control demand during this transition. This interpretation is consistent with the reduced beta and gamma effects observed as stress exposure progressed, possibly reflecting a plateau effect, habituation mechanisms, or coping strategies (Angioletti et al., 2025). Relative alpha power increased after S1 in temporal, occipital, and medial parietal regions. As alpha power has been associated with attentional filtering and inhibition of irrelevant task information (Van Diepen, Foxe & Mazaheri, 2019; Zhozhikashvili et al., 2022), this increase may therefore reflect greater filtering of irrelevant information during the testing condition. Together, these findings may reflect adaptive-like changes from S2 to S3, including improved behavioral performance, decreased pupil diameter, a general reduction in relative beta (beta1, beta2, and beta3) and gamma power, and increase in relative alpha power.

The associations between pupil diameter changes and relative EEG power changes were limited and were observed only for the S3-S2 contrast, in which pupil diameter changes were positively associated with frontal relative beta2 power changes. Because both variables were calculated as S3 minus S2, the positive association indicates that more negative pupil diameter changes were associated with more negative frontal relative beta2 power changes. A previous study reported that higher beta2 power in parietal regions was associated with slower processing speed, whereas increased frontal beta2 power was associated with improved processing speed after training (Escolano et al., 2014), suggesting that the functional relevance of the beta2 activity may depend on scalp region. Given that behavioral performance improved from S2 to S3 in the present study, the concurrent decreases in pupil diameter and frontal relative beta2 power are not readily interpreted as reduced task performance. Rather, these concurrent decreases may reflect reduced arousal or reduced task control demand during the testing condition.

Limitation

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, all participants were healthy young adults with high mental arithmetic ability, broader sociodemographic characteristics, long-term lifestyle factors, and stress coping styles were not systematically assessed. This may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader populations. In addition, the sample had an imbalanced sex distribution. Although no significant sex-related differences were observed in the analyses, the small number of female participants limits conclusions regarding sex-specific response patterns. Future studies should recruit larger and more balanced samples and collect more detailed sociodemographic, lifestyle, and stress coping information. Second, this study used a fixed easy-to-hard difficulty sequence within each MIST condition. Although this design allowed systematic manipulation of arithmetic load, it may limit the separation of difficulty effects from task progression. Future studies could counterbalance or randomize difficulty levels within conditions, independently manipulate task difficulty and time pressure, and include additional stress paradigms to validate the robustness of these findings. Third, cortisol was collected only twice, after the training and testing conditions. No baseline sample was collected, and the interval between samples was insufficient to capture the expected cortisol peak. Therefore, the cortisol results can only provide a rough indication of the cortisol response trend. Future studies should include baseline and repeated samples to examine the temporal relationship between cortisol responses and other modal responses. Finally, the present analyses characterized the multimodal dynamics of acute stress responses across MIST blocks, rather than continuous temporal dynamics. The pupil-EEG correlations were exploratory and cannot establish causal relationship between pupil diameter and EEG. Future studies should use trial level or time resolved analyses to examine individual adaptation trajectories more directly.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study characterized multimodal psychophysiological responses to acute stress induced by an adapted MIST in healthy young adults. The testing condition elicited an acute stress response profile, including increased self-reported stress, salivary cortisol, heart rate, and pupil diameter, alongside decreased behavioral performance and a shift in relative EEG power from lower to higher frequency bands. Within the testing condition, behavioral performance improved and pupil diameter decreased from S2 to S3, accompanied by changes in relative alpha, beta (beta1, beta2, beta3), and gamma power. These findings may reflect adaptive-like changes over the course of the testing condition. Exploratory correlation analyses showed limited associations between pupil diameter changes and relative EEG power changes, restricted to the S3-S2 contrast and mainly involving frontal relative beta2 power. Overall, these findings suggest that acute stress involves coordinated but partly dissociable multimodal dynamics across MIST blocks and highlight the value of integrating behavioral, pupillary, and EEG measures to characterize acute stress responses.
