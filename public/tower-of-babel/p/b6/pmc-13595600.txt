Indigenous knowledge of medicinal plants used in the treatment of prostate diseases in southern Benin
Authors: Mahouwena Germaine Avohou, Relique Ignace Agbo, Dewanou Casimir Akpovi, Augustin Gaetan Julien Segbo, Antoine Abel Missihoun, Mahoudo Fidèle Assogba, Clément Agbangla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ContextMedicinal plants play an important role in the prevention and treatment of several diseases in Africa. They are the first resort for patients with Prostate Diseases (PD) as soon as the first symptoms appear in Benin; PD, particularly prostate cancer, are the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide and in Benin. This study aims to document the medicinal plants used by populations in southern Benin in the prevention and treatment of prostate diseases and to carry out chemical screening and larval toxicity testing on the most commonly used plants.MethodsUsing a structured questionnaire, 41 herbalists and 27 traditional healers who agreed to participate in the study were interviewed on plants they use in the prevention and treatment of PD. The parts of these plants, their methods of use, and their route of administration were recorded. A triangulation was conducted on medicinal plants cited by herbalists and traditional healers in order to identify their pharmacological properties.Qualitative phytochemical screening based on colour and/or precipitation reactions and the larval toxicity test evaluating the survival of brine shrimp larvae in the presence of different extracts were carried out on the eight most commonly used plants.ResultA total of 28 medicinal plants species belonging to 28 families and 29 genus were collected and identified. All plants were administered orally. The dominant family was Annonaceae (11%). The main species according to frequency of citation (FC = percentage of informants who cited the plant) were Caesalpinia bonduc (FC = 36%), Bambusa vulgaris (FC = 29%), and Annona muricata (FC = 21%). Decoctions were the preferred method of preparation, and roots were the most commonly used part of the plant. The plants, rich in flavonoids, tannins, and coumarins, were non-toxic. Data analysis showed that Caesalpinia bonduc was the most widely used plant in southern Benin for treating prostate diseases.ConclusionThis study revealed a significant diversity of medicinal plants traditionally used to treat PD in southern Benin. The predominance of Annonaceae families, the preferential use of roots in decoctions, and the richness in non-toxic bioactive metabolites support these traditional practices. Further pharmacological studies are needed to validate their therapeutic efficacy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-026-05453-9.

Introduction

Traditional medicine, according to the World Health Organisation (WHO), is all codified or non-codified systems for healthcare and well-being, comprising practices, skills, knowledge and philosophies originating in different historical and cultural contexts, which are distinct from and pre-date biomedicine, evolving with science for current use from an experience-based origin [1].

It plays an important role in healthcare provision both in developing countries and in some developed countries where certain populations do not have access to modern medicine due to a lack of healthcare funding [2]. These populations rely mainly on medicinal plants for treatment. Even in some developed countries, more than half of the population uses traditional medicine as their primary or complementary treatment [3].

Medicinal plants were once used in the treatment of several chronic conditions such as diabetes and hypertension, but also in acute pathologies. The same observations are made today in serious pathologies such as prostate diseases (PD). In Benin, these disorders have undergone a significant evolution in recent years. In 1990, the prevalence of prostate cancer, one of the most serious prostate diseases, was 6.54% [4]. Today, it has become the leading urological cancer with a prevalence of 12.8% [5]. Its manifestation is often non-specific at the onset of the disease. Specific symptoms appear late, and the manifestation of clinical signs unfortunately reflects an advanced stage of the disease. It begins with urinary disorders, persistent bone pain, weight loss and even unexplained asthenia, sometimes accompanied by a deterioration in general health [6]. In 2008, the WHO estimated that there were 12.7 million new cases of cancer worldwide, resulting in 7.6 million deaths, making this cancer a real public health problem among other prostate diseases [5].

The treatment of prostate cancer, and especially its complications when not diagnosed early, has a huge economic and health impact on the patient and their family. Despite advances in modern medicine, medical treatments often leave significant side effects such as cardiovascular complications, metabolic alterations, etc. and sometimes even induce fatigue and peripheral neuropathies [7]. Some anti-cancer drugs cause serious bone alterations, sometimes leading to bone marrow suppression these effects make patients vulnerable to infections and other diseases [8].

The aim of mitigating these post-treatment consequences has led to intensified research in ethnomedicine for well-packaged herbal medicines with clearly defined dosages for patients. In current medical practice, various plant-based anti-cancer products such as taxol, vincristine and vinblastine have been introduced [9].

Traditional medicine plays an important role in the discovery and development of new medicines, hence the need to highlight the knowledge of our indigenous populations on medicinal plants. In Benin, medicinal plants play an important role in the well-being of patients with prostate diseases for their prevention and treatment.

The preservation of traditional medical knowledge can only be guaranteed through documentation for the conservation of this endogenous knowledge. Despite the prominent place occupied by traditional medicine with the increasing use of medicinal plants, very few studies have focused on documenting endogenous knowledge for its preservation.

This study was initiated to fill this information gap, with the overall objective of determining the chemical composition of medicinal plants used in the prevention and treatment of PD in southern Benin. Specifically, the study aimed to (i) document the different plant species used in the prevention and treatment of prostate disorders in southern Benin; (ii) determine the most commonly used parts of the plants according to the methods used to prepare them; (iii) perform chemical screening and larval toxicity tests on the most commonly used plants.

Methodology

Study area

The information gathering phase of this study was conducted in the southern departments of Benin where seven departments were surveyed (Atlantic, Littoral, Ouémé, Plateau, Mono, Couffo, and Zou) (Fig. 1). Southern Benin is located between 6°20 and 7°30 North Latitude and between 1°35 and 2°45 East Longitude [10]. It covers an area of 17,920 km [10] and is characterised by a sub-equatorial climate (two rainy seasons and two dry seasons) with small temperature variations [11]. The average annual temperature is 28 °C. The terrain consists of a plateau marked by depressions, low valleys, and a coastal area. It has several types of soils, including ferralitic soils, vertisols, and hydromorphic soils. Rainfall varies between 950 and 1,400 mm per year and the growing season lasts around 240 days [12]. Southern Benin accounts for 58.89% of Benin's population, with a density of 5,894,168 inhabitants [13]. Ethnobotanical surveys

The ethnobotanical survey was conducted over a period of seven (07) months using a questionnaire developed and pre-tested in the study area. The pre-test covered all traditional healers recognised by the National Programme for the Promotion of Traditional Medicine who treat PD. Only those who confirmed that they had received and treated patients suffering from these conditions were enrolled in the study.

The survey was conducted using a semi-structured study method [14]. The questionnaire was used to collect specific information about the respondents: sociodemographic information (age, gender, level of education) respondents' knowledge of prostate diseases (the origin of the disease, symptoms,…), and plants used in treatment (plants used, method of administration of plants, parts of the plants used).

In order to encourage the participation of respondents, the purpose of the study was explained to them, emphasising the value of their endogenous knowledge of medicinal plants and their importance as stakeholders. Additionally, the contribution of medicinal plants to cancer diseases prevention and management was highlighted to them.

The survey was conducted in two phases: the first phase consisted of collecting information on the plants sold by herbalists for the treatment and prevention of diseases. To this end, forty-one (41) herbalists were interviewed in areas where medicinal plants are sold in markets in the Littoral and Atlantic departments. Sampling was carried out in markets in the main towns of the five southern departments of Benin reserved exclusively for the sale of medicinal plants. Herbalists were selected on the basis of the richness of their shelves and their knowledge of disease. Interviews were conducted in local languages (Fon, Aïzo, Mina and Goun) and ended with the purchase of plants. The second phase consisted of interviewing traditional healers. Of the 32 traditional healers listed in the National Programme for Pharmacopoeia and Traditional Medicine, 27 traditional healers were interviewed in their local language with the help of guides fluent in the local language. Traditional healers treating prostate diseases were selected at random and according to their availability to participate in the study. Same information collected from 41 herbalists were also collected from 27 traditional healers.

Some of the plants mentioned were purchased from herbalists, while others were collected from their natural habitat in southern Benin. The identification of the plant material used in this study was carried out by the conservator of the National Herbarium of Benin, Professor Hounnankpon YEDOMONHAN. Reference specimens of the various samples have been deposited at the National Herbarium. The sample identification numbers are specified in the identification certificate (cf certificate of authenticity in Appendix 2).

Eight of the listed plants were selected for chemical screening and larval toxicity testing based on citation frequency (high FC) and also on the relevance of the information provided by respondents. These are:

1 = Caesalpinia bonduc; 2 = Bambusa vulgaris; 3 = Carissa spinarum; 4 = Clausena anisata; 5 = Gardenia ternifolia; 6 = Rhaphiostylis beniniensis; 7 = Garcinia kola; 8 = Annona muricata.

Preparation of extracts

The samples were collected from herbalists and on private estates with the owners’ permission. Plant samples were shade-dried at room temperature and ground using an electric grinder. 50 g of the plant part (seeds, bark, root, and fruit) of the plants studied were macerated in 500 mL of distilled water and then heated for 30 min. The resulting decoctions were filtered using 3 mm thick Wattman filter paper. Raw powder extracts were obtained after evaporating the aqueous filtrates in an oven at 50 °C until a dry extract of constant weight was obtained. These powder samples were stored in a freezer for chemical screening and larval toxicity testing.

Larval toxicity assessment

This is a preliminary test used by Houmènou and al. [16] and used year 2023 by Opwoko and al. [17]. This test assesses the survival of brine shrimp larvae in the presence of the plant extract.

Stock solutions were prepared by heating 1 g of extract powder from each sample in 20 mL of distilled water at 50 °C for 20 min to obtain a concentration of 50 mg/mL of decoction. A series of 10 successive 1/2 dilutions was carried out with seawater from the decoctions in ten test tubes. The first tube contained the raw extract and the other nine tubes contained successive dilutions of the extract (25 mg/mL; 12.5 mg/mL; 6.25 mg/mL; 3.125 mg/L; 1.582 mg/mL; 0.781 mg/L; 0.391 mg/mL; 0.195 mg/mL; 0.098 mg/mL; 0.049 mg/mL). To assess larval toxicity, Artemia salina Leach (Artemiidae) eggs were incubated to hatch in seawater for 24 h to obtain larvae. The eggs used in this study were JBL Artemio Mix eggs that we had purchased from a shop. Sixteen live larvae were then placed in the dilution series of the ten test tubes for culture. These media and the controls (tubes containing only the larval solutions) were left under agitation and the live larvae were counted 24 h after incubation. To ensure that the mortality observed during the tests was solely attributable to the extracts and not to starvation, A comparison of the tests was carried out with control tubes. Artemia salina shrimp larvae can live up to 48 h without food, but living off their yolk sac [17, 18].

Data analysis

The data collected was entered and analysed using SPSS for Windows version 11.5. Descriptive statistics (mean, frequency) were calculated to assess the demographic characteristics of the respondents. To assess the importance of the plants used, the frequency of mention of each plant was calculated using the following formula [19]:Frequency of Citation = (number of citations for the plant * 100) / total number of citations

The Lethal concentration for half of the shrimp larvae (LC50), in each sample, was determined. The lethal concentration that caused the death of 50% of the larvae (LC50) was obtained from the best-fit curve giving the concentration equivalent to the death of half of the larvae. Results

Description of respondents

This study involved 41 herbalists and 27 traditional healers. Most practitioners were over 50 years old. The average age was 53 for herbalists and 51 for traditional healers. The oldest traditional healer was sixty-five (65) years old and the oldest herbalist was seventy-five (75) years old (Table 3). It should be noted that all traditional healers were men, unlike herbalists, who were of both sexes (Table 3). The level of education varied in both groups. Among traditional healers, 70% were illiterate, 4% had completed primary school, 19% had completed secondary school, and 7% had completed higher education (Table 3). In the herbalist group, more than 83% were illiterate, 14% had completed primary school, and 3% had completed secondary school (Table 3). For more than half of those surveyed (71%), believe that prostate diseases (PD) were caused by sexual promiscuity, while (29%), suspect this disease was caused by a lack of sexual intercourse (Table 3). Phytochemical study and larval toxicity

Among the plants listed, eight were selected based on high frequency of mention and relevance of information for chemical screening and larval toxicity testing. The phytochemical study focused on the plants commonly used in the prevention and treatment of PD. The results of the chemical study are presented in Table 8. The presence of a group of chemical compounds is indicated by a (+) sign and its absence by a (-) sign. Analysis of this table shows that eight chemical groups were sought. The most prevalent chemical groups were alkaloids, tannins, terpenes, and coumarins, followed by triterpenes and steroids. There was a complete absence of cyanogenic and anthracene derivatives. Quinone derivatives and saponosides were only present in the seeds of Caesalpinia bonduc and the fruit of Annona muricata, respectively. Discussion

This study aims to document the medicinal plants used by populations in southern Benin in the prevention and treatment of prostate diseases. A reluctance to participate in the survey on the part of traditional medicine practitioners was observed. Some believed that this survey was an attempt to exploit their knowledge and experience in the prevention and treatment of prostate diseases for free in order to become their potential competitors, which made it difficult to obtain information from them. Nevertheless, other practitioners agreed to participate in the study and shared their knowledge. It should be noted that the vast majority of herbalists were women who sold medicinal plants in markets, unlike traditional healers, who were males (Table 3). Indeed, the sale of medicinal plants in markets is an income-generating activity that is generally practiced by women. Apart from some women who voluntarily seek training from herbalists in exchange for remuneration, this activity is passed down from mother to daughter [28]. The practice of this activity is widespread in cities where proximity to medicinal plants is no longer possible due to urbanisation. Furthermore, it should be noted that in our cities, most people are urban dwellers who are not familiar with medicinal plants and take advantage of the knowledge of these traders to treat themselves at a lower cost. The cost of these plants varied depending on the condition and remained less expensive than medical treatment. The treatment of various ailments with medicinal plants sometimes involved occultism, i.e., deities who have their own taboos, which explains the scarcity of women. Although the majority of those surveyed were illiterate (70% of traditional healers and 83% of herbalists), it was nevertheless noticeable that the level of education among traditional healers had higher than that of herbalists (7% of traditional healers have higher education compared to 0% of herbalists, and 19% had secondary education compared to 3% of herbalists). Although there are incentives for girls' education in our country, Benin, the results are still unsatisfactory [29].

For the majority of our respondents (71%), prostate diseases is a disease caused by promiscuity. This view is widespread among the Beninese population and makes this condition a “disease of shame.” The same findings have been made in Burkina Faso, where promiscuity is considered by the population to be a risk factor for prostate diseases [30]. Patients therefore hide their illness from their loved ones and only begin treatment at an advanced stage [31]. Only increased awareness and screening campaigns could improve the situation. This would undoubtedly reduce mortality from prostate diseases in Benin. The respondents stated that they diagnosed prostate diseases in their patients based on the presence of certain symptoms. These include frequent or urgent need to urinate, particularly at night, feelings of incomplete emptying or sometimes urine retention. The lack of biological tests to confirm or refute the diagnosis is a limitation in the diagnosis by our respondents. Prostate diseases include prostate adenoma, prostatitis and prostate cancer. Only a definitive diagnosis can ensure appropriate patient care.

Various plants have been cited in the prevention and treatment of this disease in southern Benin. These plants belong to various botanical families, the most represented of which was Annonaceae; the most commonly used plants are Annona muricata, Caesalpinia bonduc, and Bambusa vulgaris. It should be noted that these plants are used in combination. Preparation methods vary from one recipe to another. They are used in various ways, predominantly as decoctions (69%) and macerations (12%). This is because decoctions allow the most active ingredients to be extracted. These results corroborate those of Houmenou and al. [32], who showed that decoction and maceration are the most commonly used methods of preparation in traditional medicine. To this end, different parts of the plants are used, with the roots being the most important. These results do not corroborate those of Amadou and al. [33] who showed that leaves are the parts of plants most commonly used in the treatment of ailments. This difference can be explained by the type of ailment treated and the plants used. However, it is through the roots that the plant draws the nutrients it needs for growth and survival. The roots therefore play an important role in the plant's development. Their increased use in the treatment of diseases is therefore detrimental to the survival of the species [34, 35]. The plants most commonly cited in the treatment of PD in southern Benin are Caesalpinia bonduc, Annona muricata, and Bambusa vulgaris. These results are consistent with those of Nnanga Nga and al., [36] who showed that Annona muricata and others plants have anticancer properties as mentioned in the last table. Respondents were hesitant about the dosage and duration of treatment. Some respondents were vague about the question and others avoided it. The dosage given by those who answered the question is one coffee cup in the morning and evening or three times a day until symptoms disappear completely. With regard to prevention, respondents mentioned seasonal use of the fruits of the plants and the use of plant extracts every month or every quarter.

Phytochemical analysis of leaf and root extracts revealed the presence of several phytochemical compounds. These include alkaloids, polyphenolic compounds, tannins (catechinic and gallic), flavonoids, coumarins, anthocyanins, and leucoanthocyanins. Some species are richer than others. Extracts from Annona muricata plants is richer in secondary metabolites (92%). They are followed by extracts from Clausena anisata and Caesalpinia bonduc, which contain 81% and 77% secondary metabolites, respectively. Extracts from Carissa spinarum and Gardenia ternifolia are moderately rich in these metabolites (57%) and are also the extracts containing the lowest numbers of secondary metabolites. The presence of these metabolites justifies their use in the treatment of PD. According to Sulaiman an al. [37] and Dhyani and al. [38] alkaloids have several biological properties and are highly sought after for their broad spectrum of biological activities, including antibiotic, antiparasitic, anesthetic, analgesic, antitumor, and anticancer properties. This compound is found in all of the plants used. Similarly, with the exception of extracts from Caesalpinia bonduc and Bambusa vulgaris, flavonoids are present in all these medicinal plants and have anticancer and antiproliferative activities. They are also powerful antioxidants [39].

Cumarins and mucilages are present in Caesalpinia bonduc, Bambusa vulgaris, Clausena anisata, Rhaphiostylis beniniensis, Garcinia kola, Annona muricata, and Caesalpinia bonduc, Carissa spinarum, Garcinia kola, and Annona muricata, respectively. Similar results were observed by Houmènou and al. [16].

Catechinic and gallic tannins are present in extracts of Caesalpinia bonduc, Bambusa vulgaris, Clausena anisata, Raphiostylis benineensis, Garcinia kola, and Annona muricata. They are known for their antioxidant and antiviral properties. They are secondary metabolites that exhibit antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and antitumor activities [40].

These plants contain various secondary metabolites known for their pharmacological properties. This justifies their use in the prevention and treatment of prostate diseases.

Toxicity of different extracts

The lethal cytotoxicity value (LC50) of the different samples varies from one species to another. The highest LC50 value was observed with Annona muricata (7.8 mg/mL) and the lowest with Clausena anisata (0.15). Some species, such as Garcinia kola, Clausena anisata, and Caesalpinia bonduc, have a toxicity index of less than 0.1 mg/mL (0.8, 0.15, and 0.7, respectively). The other species have a toxicity index greater than 0.1 mg/mL. All the plants tested have a toxicity index greater than 1 (LC 50 > 1 mg/mL). This shows that these plants are non-toxic and their use poses no risk to consumers. Similar results were observed by Houmènou and al. [16].

Conclusion

This study focused on various medicinal plants used by traditional medicine practitioners (traditional healers and herbalists) in the prevention and treatment of prostate diseases in southern Benin. Chemical screening of these plants revealed the presence of several secondary metabolites with antioxidant and antiproliferative properties. A triangulation of the various plants cited in this study revealed that some of these plants are used in other countries to treat prostate diseases and have been shown to have antiproliferative or anticarcinogenic properties. The exploration of these plants can serve as a model for the discovery and development of new cancer drugs. Also, these plants, taken individually, do not exhibit any cytotoxicity. But are the recipes made from several of these plants by traditional healers cytotoxic? Further pharmacological studies are needed to validate their therapeutic efficacy.
