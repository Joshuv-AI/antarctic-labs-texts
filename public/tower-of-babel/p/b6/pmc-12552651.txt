From response to cure: Unaddressed clinical challenges in the Tali-cel™ CD19 CAR-T trial for relapsed/refractory pediatric B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia
Authors: Zhao Yin, Qing Zhang, Yangmin Zhu, Shuang Liu
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the recent report by Narula et al. [1] on talicabtagene autoleucel (Tali-cel™), a novel humanized CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy, in pediatric relapsed/refractory B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL). The authors achieved an impressive 30-day overall response rate of 91.7% (66.7% complete remission (CR) and 25% partial remission (PR)) in 12 heavily pretreated patients, demonstrating the feasibility of indigenous CAR T-cell manufacturing in a resource-limited setting. We applaud this important step toward broader CAR T accessibility. However, several clinical and translational gaps merit discussion to contextualize these promising early-phase results.

A central concern is the durability of Tali-cel responses beyond the short follow-up. The report notes that CRs at the higher dose levels were sustained to 12 months, but long-term remissions in pediatric CAR T trials often require multi-year observation. For instance, in the pivotal tisagenlecleucel study, only about half of responding pediatric patients remained relapse-free at 3 years [2]. Similarly, a meta-analysis estimated 2-year event-free survival rates of only ~40–50% after CD19 CAR T-cell therapy [3]. The Narula et al. study provides limited data on B-cell aplasia, an important surrogate for CAR T persistence and durable remission. Loss of B-cell aplasia within 6 months post-infusion is a known harbinger of impending relapse [4]. Indeed, two patients in their cohort who received lower CAR T doses lost B-cell aplasia early and subsequently relapsed or required salvage therapy. We encourage further reporting of sustained B-cell aplasia rates and longer follow-up in ongoing trials. Early reemergence of normal B cells should prompt interventions (e.g., reinfusion of CAR-T or bridging allografts) to prevent frank relapse. Notably, recent strategies such as maintenance therapy after early B-cell recovery have shown potential to prolong remission [5]. A deeper discussion of how Tali-cel persistence will be ensured or monitored in Phase 2 (beyond the 12-month window) would strengthen confidence that the remissions are indeed “durable”.

The trial’s high response rate is laudable, yet 3 of 12 patients achieved only a partial response (residual disease) and “had early loss of response or rapid progression”. The report provides minimal guidance on managing these suboptimal responders. In aggressive pediatric ALL, a morphologic PR (or measurable residual disease (MRD)-positive CR) after CAR-T is typically not sufficient for lasting disease control. Prior experiences with CD19 CAR T-cells have shown that patients who do not achieve MRD-negative CR by the first assessment are at extremely high risk of relapse [6]. Unfortunately, outcomes after CAR-T failure remain poor, with one study reporting a 12-month post-CAR-T relapse incidence of ~37% (21% CD19-positive and 16% CD19-negative relapses) [7]. It would therefore be prudent to outline a clear plan for partial responders. Options include prompt consolidation with allogeneic stem cell transplant (Allo-SCT) or enrollment in trials of alternative immunotherapies. Indeed, several groups have used Allo-SCT as a consolidation strategy following CAR T in high-risk or PR patients, with encouraging survival in those who can bridge to transplant [8, 9]. Narula et al. note that one PR patient received oral maintenance and another underwent Allo-SCT, but a broader discussion is warranted. For instance, should all PRs proceed to transplant if feasible, given the likely short-lived nature of PRs? Could re-treatment with CAR T cells (at a higher dose or with a manufacturing tweak) rescue partial responders? Clarifying the management algorithm for less-than-CR outcomes would enhance the clinical applicability of their findings.

Another gap is the paucity of correlative biomarkers to explain why certain patients fared better. The study focuses on clinical outcomes and toxicity, but does not report in-depth analyses of CAR T-cell expansion, persistence, or patient immune reconstitution beyond noting IVIG replacement for B-cell aplasia. While the authors measured serum cytokines and observed low levels overall, they did not correlate these or other biomarkers with response durability. In future reports, it would be valuable to include data on CAR T-cell kinetic profiles (peak expansion, persistence by PCR or flow cytometry) and their relationship to outcomes. Other pediatric CAR trials have identified baseline tumor burden and marrow disease status as critical modulators of CAR T efficacy. In fact, achieving a low disease burden or remission prior to CAR-T infusion is associated with improved long-term survival [6]. Narula et al. briefly note that their highest disease burden patients had less durable remissions, aligning with this consensus, but no specific risk-stratification or biomarker-driven approach is described. Moreover, given the humanized scFv of Tali-cel, one might expect reduced immunogenicity; yet no data on anti-CAR immune responses or T-cell phenotypes were presented. We suggest implementing correlative studies (e.g., profiling of memory/effector T-cell subsets in the apheresis product, CAR transgene levels over time, and immune checkpoint marker expression) to identify predictors of response or relapse. Such analyses have yielded insights in other trials. For example, differentiating T-cell phenotypes in the infused product that correlate with durable remissions versus early relapses [10]. Enriching the translational data around Tali-cel will help optimize patient selection and potentially guide adjunctive therapies (e.g., tumor burden reduction or immunomodulation pre/post CAR-T) to maximize benefit.

Finally, the report touches only briefly on mechanisms of resistance. CD19 antigen loss is a well-documented cause of relapse after CD19 CAR-T in ALL, occurring in roughly 10-20% of cases across studies [11]. Narula et al. observed one CD19-negative relapse (8.3% of their cohort), which emerged as early as month 1. They postulate that a pre-existing CD19-negative leukemic clone may have been present, although none was evident by pre-infusion flow cytometry. This finding raises important questions. Was the CD19-negative relapse verified by immunophenotyping at recurrence, and were any molecular analyses (e.g., CD19 gene sequencing or lineage tracking) performed to confirm an antigen-escape mechanism? A single CD19-negative relapse in 12 patients may seem anecdotal, but it aligns with larger series where antigen escape accounts for a significant subset of failures [7]. The authors did not discuss how they plan to address antigen-negative relapse moving forward. In the field at large, investigational approaches include sequential or concurrent use of alternate CAR T targets (such as CD22 or CD20) and dual-targeted CAR T cells [11, 12]. Indeed, a recent pediatric trial of dual CD19/CD22 CAR T therapy reported mitigation of CD19-negative relapse, but still encountered relapses due to limited CAR persistence. This underscores that antigen escape and poor CAR T-cell persistence are intertwined challenges. Tali-cel’s humanized CAR design may reduce immunogenic clearance, but we wonder if any data on its long-term persistence were obtained (perhaps via serial qPCR for the CAR transgene). The letter would benefit from the authors’ perspective on tackling resistance - e.g., will patients who relapse with CD19-negative disease be eligible for a Tali-cel “repeat” at a higher dose, or for novel combinatorial approaches? A deeper exploration of resistance mechanisms, even with limited patient numbers, would be valuable for guiding next steps in product development (for example, incorporating a backup target or enhancing CAR T durability).

In summary, Narula et al. have delivered an encouraging proof-of-concept for CAR T therapy in pediatric B-ALL, demonstrating impressive early response rates and manageable toxicity. To fully realize the translational impact of Tali-cel, we urge further attention to long-term remission durability, management plans for suboptimal responders, incorporation of correlative science, and strategies to preempt or overcome resistance. Addressing these gaps will help ensure that this promising therapy can achieve sustained cures in a broader patient population and not merely short-lived remissions. We look forward to additional data from the ongoing Phase 2 trial and a continued dialog on how best to integrate Tali-cel into the evolving treatment landscape of ALL.
