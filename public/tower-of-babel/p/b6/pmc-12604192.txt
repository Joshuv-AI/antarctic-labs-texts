Accuracy of Raman spectroscopy in laryngeal carcinoma diagnosis: a meta-analysis
Authors: Yahui Zhao, Zhaofeng Qiu, Jianzhou Guo, Wei Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study explores the accuracy of Raman spectroscopy in diagnosing laryngeal carcinoma.MethodsA comprehensive search was performed in PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase (retrieval deadline: March 2025) to locate studies that presented data on true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN), and false negatives (FN) regarding the use of Raman spectroscopy for diagnosing laryngeal carcinoma. Using either random or fixed-effects models, we computed pooled sensitivity, specificity, diagnostic odds ratio (DOR), and the area under the summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve. The methodological quality was evaluated with QUADAS-2, and Deek’s test was employed to assess publication bias.ResultsSeven studies involving 1076 samples in total were analyzed. The overall sensitivity and specificity were determined to be 0.88 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.86–0.90) and 0.91 (95% CI 0.88–0.93), respectively. Positive likelihood ratios (PLR) and negative likelihood ratios (NLR) were calculated at 9.70 (95% CI 7.13–13.21) and 0.13 (95% CI 0.11–0.16), respectively, resulting in a diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) of 74.07 (95% CI 39.0–140.9). The summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve demonstrated an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.95 (95% CI 0.94–0.98). Furthermore, subgroup analysis revealed significant improvements in specificity over time (p = 0.02), and no significant regional or algorithmic heterogeneity was found.ConclusionRaman spectroscopy exhibits remarkable diagnostic accuracy in detecting laryngeal carcinoma, with a pooled sensitivity of 88% and a specificity of 91%. This highlights its promise as a non-invasive supplement for diagnosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03309-5.

Introduction

Laryngeal carcinoma ranks among the most common types of malignant tumors found in the head and neck area. According to estimates from the American Cancer Society, roughly 13,000 new instances of laryngeal carcinoma are diagnosed annually, leading to about 3700 deaths each year. Early detection and diagnosis, coupled with effective treatment, can significantly enhance the 5-year survival rate [1]. Currently, histological examination serves as the gold standard for tissue diagnosis. The typical approach for intraoperative diagnosis and margin evaluation is frozen section analysis; nonetheless, this method is expensive and requires considerable time, as postoperative pathology outcomes frequently take several hours or even days to obtain. Furthermore, only a limited number of critical margins are extracted for testing, which increases the risk of missed diagnoses [2]. Consequently, there is a pressing need for more convenient diagnostic tools that offer higher specificity and sensitivity in the diagnosis of laryngeal carcinoma.

Raman spectroscopy, a non-invasive analytical technique based on molecular vibrational fingerprints, can accurately elucidate the structural differences in biomolecular structures by capturing characteristic frequency shifts resulting from the interaction between photons and molecules. This method is particularly effective in tumor diagnosis, as it identifies specific protein conformational changes and lipid metabolic abnormalities in cancerous tissues, achieving high specificity in differentiating between breast carcinoma, gastric carcinoma, and normal tissues [3]. Compared to traditional biopsy, this technology offers three core advantages: non-destructive detection, real-time feedback, and molecular resolution, making it particularly suitable for rapid intraoperative diagnostic scenarios. Recent advancements in surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS), tip-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (TERS), and shell-separated nanoparticle enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SHINERS) have improved detection sensitivity to the single-molecule level, significantly enhancing the early detection rate of microscopic lesions [4]. Moreover, this technology exhibits excellent sample adaptability, ranging from in situ detection of live cells to the analysis of complex biological fluids. Its spectral imaging capability allows for the simultaneous acquisition of information on tissue spatial heterogeneity, providing crucial molecular imaging evidence for clinical precision diagnosis and treatment [5]. Although single-center studies have reported on the diagnostic capabilities of Raman spectroscopy for laryngeal carcinoma, differences in findings could stem from inconsistencies in experimental designs, methods of sample collection, and the choice of Raman spectroscopy techniques. For the first time, we strictly followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [4]. We performed a systematic review combined with meta-analysis to assess the diagnostic effectiveness of Raman spectroscopy in the context of laryngeal carcinoma, with the goal of elucidating its efficacy in confirming the presence of this type of carcinoma.

Method

Literature review

We invited two attending physicians specializing in otolaryngology to conduct a comprehensive search of the database content, with a deadline extending to March 2025. The databases utilized for this search included PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science. We employed medical subject headings such as “larynx”, “cancer”, and various iterations of “Raman spectroscopy” to facilitate our search. Furthermore, we performed a supplementary review of the references from the retrieved articles to ensure the thoroughness of the literature extracted. In the event of any disputes regarding inclusion, the deputy chief physician would render the final decision.

Include inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

We systematically and meticulously screened all relevant articles based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) Raman spectroscopy utilized as an independent diagnostic tool for distinguishing cancerous tissue from normal tissue; (2) the selected literature must consist of human tissues or specimens; (3) relevant data must be extractable; (4) all articles must be published in English. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) trials that do not involve human specimens were excluded; (2) articles that do not conform to the specified types, such as conference abstracts, letters, reviews, and case reports, were excluded; (3) articles from which relevant data cannot be accurately extracted were excluded; (4) non-English articles were excluded; (5) articles that do not meet the requirements after a final review by associate chief physicians were excluded.

Data fetch

In this study, we included essential information such as the title, year, country, sample type, type of Raman spectrum, true positive (TP), false positive (FP), true negative (TN), false negative (FN), and other relevant data. Ultimately, we compiled these findings into a comprehensive table. Notably, the two tables concerning attending physicians exhibited a high degree of consistency.

Quality assessment and publication bias

We conducted a quality assessment of the seven included studies using R for Windows, based on the Diagnostic Accuracy Research Quality Assessment (QUADAS-2) checklist. The Stata software version 14.0 (STATA Corp, TX, USA) was utilized, along with the Deeks funnel plot asymmetry test; an asymmetrical funnel shape or a p-value of less than 0.05 indicates the presence of publication bias.

Statistical analysis

To begin with, it is essential to extract the relevant data pertaining to true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN), and false negatives (FN). Once this data is gathered, we can proceed to calculate several important statistical metrics that inform our understanding of the diagnostic tool’s performance. These metrics include summary sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive and negative likelihood ratios (LRs), as well as the diagnostic odds ratio (DOR). Additionally, we can assess the summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve, the area under the curve (AUC), and the 95% confidence intervals (CI). For the statistical analysis, we employ the software Stata version 14.0, which provides robust capabilities for such tasks. The interpretation of the AUC value is particularly significant in evaluating the diagnostic tool. When the AUC exceeds 0.9, it signifies that the diagnostic tool is operating at an excellent level of accuracy. Conversely, if the AUC falls between 0.8 and 0.9, the diagnostic performance is deemed good. An AUC in the range of 0.7–0.8 represents a moderate level of diagnostic effectiveness, while a value below 0.7 is regarded as poor, indicating a need for improvement in the diagnostic method or tool.

Results

Literature search and study selection

A comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, EMBASE and Web of Science databases, and a total of 517 studies were identified. The specific search strategy is detailed in supplementary document 1. Following this initial identification, we proceeded to eliminate any duplicate entries and conducted a thorough screening of the titles and abstracts to assess their relevance. From this rigorous evaluation process, we ultimately selected 10 studies that fulfilled the criteria necessary for a full-text review. After a detailed examination of these texts, we determined that 7 of the studies qualified for inclusion in the subsequent meta-analysis. The diagnostic potential of Raman spectroscopy in the context of laryngeal carcinoma has been evaluated through a review of seven studies, revealing a sensitivity range between 0.69 and 0.93. When these results were combined, the overall pooled sensitivity was determined to be 0.88, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.86 to 0.90. Such a level of sensitivity indicates that Raman spectroscopy is proficient in decreasing the likelihood of missed diagnoses, enhancing its role as a reliable tool in the detection of laryngeal carcinoma. Furthermore, the specificity outcomes across these studies varied from 0.82 to 0.93, leading to a pooled specificity of 0.91, accompanied by a 95% confidence interval of 0.88–0.93(Fig. 4). This high level of specificity suggests that Raman spectroscopy is effective in minimizing false positive results, thereby increasing the accuracy of diagnoses in clinical settings. We conducted a meta-regression analysis to examine the potential heterogeneity among studies. The covariates considered included publication year (2000–2013 or 2014–2025), region (Asia or other), and diagnostic algorithm (PCA-LDA or others). The results indicated that the P-values for both region and diagnostic algorithm exceeded 0.05, as illustrated in Fig. 9, suggesting no significant heterogeneity between regions and diagnostic algorithms. This finding was further corroborated through subgroup analysis, as presented in Table 2. Discussion

Laryngeal carcinoma continues to pose a significant threat to individual’s quality of life, primarily due to insufficient early screening rates and limitations in diagnostic techniques. Traditional diagnostic methods often rely on invasive procedures such as endoscopic biopsies, which not only demand a high level of technical skill from the operator but also face challenges in detecting early microlesions [13]. Additionally, pathological diagnoses frequently carry a risk of false negatives, stemming from sampling errors or low patient cooperation. To address these challenges, researchers have developed novel clinical testing tools and biomarkers. Among these, Raman spectroscopy technology has gained prominence due to its advantages of non-invasive sampling, real-time analysis, and the capability to detect molecular vibration fingerprints. This technology identifies early lesions by capturing characteristic spectral changes in cellular metabolic products, demonstrating significant clinical potential in minimally invasive contexts [8, 14]. Consequently, it opens new avenues for the precise diagnosis and treatment of laryngeal carcinoma. This study aims to systematically evaluate the clinical diagnostic efficacy of Raman spectroscopy technology for laryngeal carcinoma, rigorously verifying its diagnostic accuracy through statistical methods. In terms of methodology, to ensure the robustness of the meta-analysis results, the research team employed a random effects model for data with low heterogeneity and a fixed effects model for studies exhibiting potential heterogeneity [10, 15]. This approach effectively mitigates the impact of diagnostic threshold effects and inter-study heterogeneity on the results. The primary diagnostic performance indicators reveal that the combined sensitivity reaches 88% (95% CI 0.86–0.90), indicating the technology’s ability to accurately identify 88% of laryngeal carcinoma patients. Additionally, the combined specificity is 91% (95% CI 0.88–0.93), meaning that 91% of healthy individuals can be correctly excluded. The analysis of the receiver operating characteristic curve reveals an area under the curve of 0.95, with a 95% confidence interval ranging from 0.94 to 0.98. This high AUC value suggests a significant level of reliability for the diagnostic test, as an AUC that approaches 1 indicates excellent diagnostic performance. Additionally, the combined DOR is calculated to be 4.13, accompanied by a 95% confidence interval of 3.90–4.72. This DOR not only encapsulates the effectiveness of the test in terms of both sensitivity and specificity but also implies that a higher DOR value corresponds to superior diagnostic discrimination, affirming the test’s capability to accurately differentiate between the presence and absence of the condition in question.

The combined sensitivity of 88% (95% CI 0.86–0.90) and specificity of 91% (95% CI 0.88–0.93), along with an area under the SROC curve of 0.95 (95% CI 0.94–0.98), indicates a robust diagnostic performance that exceeds the sensitivity threshold of traditional endoscopic biopsy, which typically is ≤75%. All seven utilized employed spontaneous Raman spectroscopy, though variations existed in laser wavelengths, tissue types (ex vivo/in vivo), and pretreatment procedures. Most utilized near-infrared lasers (785nm or 830 nm), with Lin2012 and Lin2016 being the only studies alongside in vivo measurements alongside ex vivo tissue analysis. To improve spectral quality, all research universally implemented smoothing filters and fluorescence background removal steps as standard pretreatment protocols.

It is recommended to integrate RS technology into the tertiary prevention system for laryngeal carcinoma. This integration should include the use of RS as an opportunistic screening tool in primary healthcare institutions for non-invasive screening of high-risk populations, such as those with a smoking index greater than 400, HPV positivity, or a history of occupational exposure [8, 16]. Additionally, it is advisable to establish a dual-track diagnostic pathway of ‘RS initial screening + pathological confirmation’ in secondary hospitals to guide stratified referrals based on positive likelihood ratios [11, 17]. Furthermore, in the realm of digital innovation in surgical paradigms, intraoperative RS real-time imaging systems can achieve molecular-level definitions of tumor boundaries, with a spatial resolution of 5 µm, thereby reducing diagnostic time compared to traditional frozen section diagnosis by 75%. It is suggested to develop an intelligent surgical navigation system guided by RS, which involves constructing a three-dimensional tumor metabolic model through multimodal RS images prior to surgery. Intraoperatively, dynamic margin assessment demonstrated a predictive value of RS for negative margins reaching 98% [18]. Postoperatively, the RS spectral database can be utilized for intelligent early warning of residual lesions. Notably, in laryngeal function-preserving surgery, RS technology quantitatively evaluates the biomechanical properties of the vocal membrane, significantly enhancing postoperative voice quality. The platformization of translational medicine research has allowed RS technology to evolve beyond a mere diagnostic tool, becoming an innovative engine for tumor biology research. It is recommended to establish an RS-driven laryngeal carcinoma research platform, develop machine learning-based metabolic pathway analysis algorithms, and achieve a closed-loop study from molecular phenotypes to clinical translation [9, 19]. A limitation of this study is the inclusion of literature solely in English, which may introduce language bias. Additionally, all included literature pertains to tissue specimens, with no relevant research on blood or other specimen types. Only seven high-quality studies were incorporated, indicating a need for more relevant literature to enhance the accuracy of this article. This study was conducted across subgroups. In the future, It is called to establish a standardized operating procedure (SOP) for Raman spectroscopy diagnosis of laryngeal carcinoma, including quality control indicators such as spatial resolution and laser power. More multi-center and large-sample studies are needed to verify the universality of the conclusions. Future studies will be expanded to non-English databases (such as CNKI, Wanfang, etc.) and non-invasive samples such as blood and saliva to enhance the clinical translational value.

Conclusion

In conclusion, RS is a tool with significant application prospects and value, which can be utilized for the screening and diagnosis of laryngeal carcinoma in the future. It will assist otolaryngologists in accurately locating tumors and making informed decisions, thereby effectively reducing the incidence laryngeal carcinoma and improving survival rates.
