Mechanical Strength: An Unrecognised Target in the Genetic Improvement of Crops
Authors: Qingbiao Shi, Qibin Wang, Guodong Wang, Yiduo An, Hengjia Yang, Qing Tao, Ying Xia, Zihao Jiao, Naiqian Li, Ran Gao, Junfen Li, Fanying Kong, Haisen Zhang, Pinghua Li, Mingyue Gou, Haiyang Wang, Bosheng Li, Gang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLeaf angle (LA) is a crucial agronomic trait influencing planting density and crop yield. Previous research highlighted the importance of cellular variations in the ligular region for determining LA, but the underlying regulatory mechanisms remain unclear. Here, we demonstrate LA is not a static trait, but rather represents a dynamic equilibrium between mechanical forces maintaining leaf erectness and those promoting blade drooping. To quantify the drooping tendency, we introduce gravitational moments, which show positive correlations with LA, blade length (BL) and blade weight (BWt). Notably, the mechanical forces are tightly regulated by the sheath layers surrounding the stalk and by the thickness and lignin deposition on the ligular region of sclerenchyma (SC) cells. Furthermore, we applied single‐nucleus transcriptome analyses (snRNA‐seq) to construct a comprehensive transcriptional atlas spanning the ligular regions of compact‐type (Z58), intermediate‐type (B73) and expanded‐type (W22) inbred lines. Through the comparative analysis of snRNA‐seq and RNA‐seq of three inbred lines, we identified the adaxial hypodermis (HP) cells as pivotal sites where lignin biogenesis and metabolism genes were specifically expressed in compact‐type Z58, consistent with the lignin deposition pattern. Notably, we discovered that the NAM, ATAF and CUC (NAC) transcription factor‐encoding genes NAC secondary wall thickening promoting factor 2 (NST2) and NST3, which mediate lignin biogenesis in the ligular region, especially on the adaxial side, play key roles in reinforcing mechanical support and reducing LA. Collectively, this study advances our understanding of ligular development and LA regulatory mechanisms and provides strategic insights for breeding crops with improved agricultural productivity.Keywords: gravitational moments, leaf angle, lignin deposition, maize, NSTs

Introduction

Plant architecture is one of the most critical factors influencing both morphology and crop yield, and is shaped by the complex interplay of multiple traits, including plant height, tiller numbers and angles, branching patterns, leaf morphology and panicle structure (Barthélémy and Caraglio 2007; Reinhardt and Kuhlemeier 2002). Among these traits, leaf morphology—particularly its dimensional attributes of blade length (BL), blade width (BW), blade weight (BWt), leaf angle (LA) and orientation—plays a pivotal role in optimising plant architecture (Du et al. 2018; Xu et al. 2021). LA, defined as the inclination angle between the blade midrib and the stem axis, is a major determinant of architecture that significantly affects compactness, planting density, canopy structure, photosynthetic efficiency, biomass and yield. Over the past few decades, the global increase in maize yield has largely been attributed to the rising density of hybrid varieties featuring reduced LA and erect leaves (Duvick 2005; Elli et al. 2023; Jafari et al. 2023). Therefore, deciphering the mechanisms governing LA formation has become an important focus in plant biology and crop science.

Previous studies suggested that LA formation is primarily determined by the developmental and cellular variations of the ligular region (also known as lamina joint), a hinge structure that links the blade and sheath, and is composed of the ligule, auricle, and connecting median region (Cao et al. 2022; Kong et al. 2017). Key milestones in this field include the identification of major developmental regulators of LA like Liguleless1 (LG1) and LG2 in maize during the 1990s (Moreno et al. 1997; Walsh et al. 1998). Subsequent studies characterised orthologs of these genes in other crops, including rice and wheat, revealing their conserved roles in regulating LA and panicle branching angles. Advance in biotechnology have enabled the identification of multiple conserved LA regulators in maize, rice, and wheat through the integration of traditional genetic approaches (QTL, GWAS), with modern multi‐omics techniques (RNA‐seq, snRNA‐seq) (Duan et al. 2022; Dzievit et al. 2019; Kong et al. 2017; Tian et al. 2011, 2024; Wang, Guo, et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2017; Zhu et al. 2023).

Despite these significant advances, fundamental questions about LA regulation remain unresolved, particularly concerning: (1) the evolutionary conservation and divergence of LA regulation across species; (2) the developmental and cellular processes involved in LA formation; and (3) the coordination between internal developmental factors (e.g., hormones) and external environmental signals (e.g., light, temperature, wind, gravity) in regulating LA. In this study, we highlight the significant role of mechanical strength in regulating crop architecture, and thoroughly analyse the conservation and variation of mechanical support systems across different species in maintaining leaf morphology. We innovatively demonstrate that LA is dynamically maintained through an equilibrium between the gravitational forces that promote drooping and the mechanical support that sustains erectness. By comparing leaf angles (LAs), blade weights (BWts), mechanical support capacity, and the internal development of ligular region across various maize inbred lines, we found that lignin deposition at the ligular region—particularly the presence or absence of lignin on the adaxial side—plays a critical role in determining the mechanical support capacity of the leaf, thereby influencing LA. Through multi‐omics analysis, we identified the adaxial hypodermal (HP) cells as key sites for the specific expression of genes involved in lignin biosynthesis and metabolism in compact‐type cultivars such as Z58, correlating with significant lignin accumulation. Furthermore, this study indicates that transcription factors NST2 and NST3 specifically regulate lignin biosynthesis in the adaxial side of the leaf ligular, reinforcing mechanical support and contributing to a reduction in leaf angle. Overall, this study advances our knowledge of ligular development and its regulatory mechanisms. These findings provide a valuable foundation for breeding crop varieties with optimised architecture and enhanced yield.

Results

Comparative Histological Analyses of the Ligular Region in Grass Species

Given that leaves of grass plants exhibit a similar architecture, consisting of a sheath, blade, and a connecting hinge region, it suggests that species within the Poaceae family may share evolutionarily conserved regulatory mechanisms governing LA formation. To identify key factors influencing LA, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of leaf traits (BL, BW and BWt) across phylogenetically diverse grass species at two growth stages (young and adult). Six grass species representing distinct lineages within Poaceae were selected for histological and morphological analysis: maize ( Zea mays ) from the tribe Andropogoneae, rice ( Oryza sativa ) from Oryzoideae, wheat ( Triticum aestivum ) from Triticeae, and three Paniceae species—foxtail millet ( Setaria italica ), green foxtail ( Setaria viridis ) and crabgrass ( Digitaria sanguinalis ). These species encompass both major subfamilies (Panicoideae, Pooideae and Oryzoideae) and tribes within Poaceae, thereby covering a broad phylogenetic spectrum. Phylogenetic studies have demonstrated that Setaria italica and Setaria viridis are closely related species within Paniceae, serving as models for C4 photosynthesis and bioenergy grasses (GPWG III 2025). The inclusion of these species, along with wheat, rice and maize, captures important evolutionary diversity and variation in leaf morphology and developmental programmes. This diversity facilitates understanding of conserved versus lineage‐specific regulatory mechanisms controlling LA formation, contributing to insights into evolutionary adaptation of grass leaf architecture. We found that cultivated crops (wheat, foxtail millet, rice and maize) generally exhibited LAs below 40°, significantly narrower than those of the weeds (green foxtail and crabgrass), which display loose and lazy architecture with LAs of 50°–60° (Figure 1A,B). Interestingly, blade lengths in staple crops typically exceed 20 cm, with maize plants reaching impressive lengths of 60 ~ 80 cm, while weeds and wheat typically have shorter blades (~10–20 cm). Notably, maize plants develop substantially broader (8 ~ 10 cm, 7 ~ 9 times wider) and heavier blades (10 ~ 20 g, 70 ~ 80 times heavier) than the other grass species, while still maintaining narrower LA. This combination of extreme blade size and weight with a narrow LAs suggests that maize likely possesses distinct biomechanical properties supporting its leaf architecture (Figures 1A,B and S1A).

To further explore the regulatory mechanisms underlying LA, we performed sectioning and lignin staining of the ligular median region across species (Figure 1C). Our histological investigations reveal that species with short blades, including the weeds (green foxtail and crabgrass), wheat, and foxtail millet, possess ligular regions generally less than 1.5 mm thick. In contrast, species with longer blades (e.g., rice and maize) exhibit ligular regions exceeding 2 mm thick (Figure 1D). Moreover, the density and distribution patterns of vascular bundles (Vb), as well as the thickness and layering of sclerenchyma (SC) cells, vary significantly among species (Figure 1C–F and S1B). For instance, in crops such as wheat, foxtail millet, and maize, Vb are predominantly restricted to the abaxial side, with abundant parenchyma cells occupying the central area. Conversely, rice displays a more intricate arrangement, with Vb orderly distributed on both the adaxial and abaxial sides, and lignin deposition specifically localised to the abaxial SC cells. Notably, multiple large aerenchyma cavities are uniquely observed in the lamina joint of rice, highlighting one of its remarkable evolutionary adaptations for thriving in aquatic environments.

More strikingly, maize plants exhibit a pronounced increase in ligular region thickness, the number of Vb, thickness, and layers of SC cells, and lignin deposition (Figures 1C–F and S1B). These rigid structures likely provide enough mechanical strength and reinforcement to support the erectness of their exceptionally large and heavy blade. Collectively, our cytological results revealed that the distinct patterns and compositions of the ligular region are intricately linked to evolutionary adaptation and domestication of plants and play a critical role in regulating LA formation.

Mechanical Analysis of Forces and Moments Affecting LA

Given that the blade is an outstretched, thin, flexible‐elastic structure extending from the sheath, the ligular region functions as a hinge‐like flexible structure and mechanical interaction point that maintains the blade in an upright inclination position and keeps it closer to the stem by counteracting gravitationally induced drooping forces. To quantify the forces responsible for downward curvature of the blade, we introduce the concept of gravitational moment (M), which is the physical quantity that changes the rotational state of an object and is defined as M = d × F. Here, d is the moment arm, representing the perpendicular distance from the line of action of the force to the axis of rotation; in plant leaves, this corresponds to the horizontal component of the distance from the leaf's centre of gravity (or actual centroid) to the ligular region. F is the force, representing the vertical component of the blade's weight.

Plant leaves are heterogeneous and curved. Specifically, the part near the stem is wider and mechanically stiffer, whereas the distal part near the leaf tip is narrower and more flexible (Figure S2A). Moreover, different maize plant types exhibit variable leaf curvature, with more compact types showing smaller curvature and intermediate or flat types exhibiting larger curvature. These factors make it difficult to precisely determine the position of the leaf's centre of gravity or actual centroid (Figure S2B). Assuming the blade behaves as a simple rigid body with uniform mass distribution, the centre of gravity can be approximated at the midpoint of the blade length (1/2BL). Under this assumption, the gravitational moment can be simply defined as Mgrav = (1/2BL) × BWt. × g × sin(LA), where BWt is the blade weight, g is the acceleration due to gravity, and LA is the leaf angle relative to the stem (Figure 2A, left). The moment arm length corresponds to the horizontal distance from the ligular region to the leaf centroid, calculated as 1/2BL × sin(LA). It should be noted that the heterogeneity and curvature of the leaf blade generally cause the effective moment arm length to be less than the simplified value 1/2BL × sin(LA), resulting in the actual gravitational moment often being lower than the value calculated by this formula (Figure S2B). Therefore, this formula serves as an ideal model with defined applicability and limitations, but can effectively capture the overall variation trend of the leaf's support force.

After applying this formula to calculate the gravitational moments among various grass plants, we found that species with longer blades (e.g., foxtail millet, rice and maize) exhibit significantly larger gravitational moments compared to species with shorter blades (Figure 2A, right). Notably, maize plants exhibited gravitational moments approximately 100–300 times greater than those of short‐bladed species, primarily due to their elongated, wider, and heavier blades, which enhance torque through an extended level arm of the leaf. To verify that the gravitational moment is a reasonable and significant factor representing the drooping forces affecting LA, we calculated its values for 147 maize inbred lines and performed a correlation analysis between gravitational moment and various leaf traits (Figure 2B; Table S1). The results showed that gravitational moment is positively correlated with LA (R 2, 0.7694), BWt (R 2, 0.7557) and BL (R 2, 0.5815). These findings indicate that gravitational moment is a reasonable and reliable parameter for representing the drooping forces acting on the blade and for elucidating the regulation of LA.

Mechanical Forces Maintain the Erectness of the Leaf

To maintain a precise inclination angle of the leaf and effectively withstand environmental stresses like wind and rain, the collective supporting and resisting forces generated by stems and sheaths, as well as the tensile and compressive strengths of the blade itself, must substantially exceed the effects of drooping forces. However, accurately quantifying these biomechanical properties remains challenging due to technical constraints. To address this, we refined our analytical approach by decomposing these support and resistance forces into horizontal (Fx) and vertical (Fy) two components, enabling a more detailed and systematic investigation.

We conducted cross‐sections analyses of leaf collar regions and stem junctions in maize and rice to investigate the support and resistance forces in these directions (Figure 2C). In maize, more than 40 large Vb were observed in the sheath and ligular region, while these numbers significantly decreased in rice (Figure 2C). In the vertical direction, the gravitational force from the blade is transmitted to the ground through the direct sheath‐stalk connection. Concurrently, the stem and sheath generate an upward reactive force supporting the ligular region and blade, contributing to erectness. Across grass species, this vertical support strength positively correlates with Vb density, thickness, and layering of SC, and lignin content in the sheath and ligular regions (Figure 1C–F). Species with reduced numbers of Vb or lignin contents exhibit large LA, especially in weeds (Figures 1C and 2C). Moreover, the mechanical support strength provided by the midrib, specifically its tensile and compressive strengths, further enhances the erectness of the blade. This effect is particularly evident in the maize drooping leaf1 (drl1) mutant, which has a midribless blade (Strable et al. 2017). The resulting reduction in mechanical support increases LA and causes drooping, underscoring the crucial role of the composition of leaf, particularly the number of Vb and lignin content in the sheath and blade, in resisting drooping forces.

To analyse horizontal resistance forces (Fx), we focused on surface friction and structural resistance within the leaf collar (Figure 2C). Maize sheaths form tightly wrapped layers around the stalk, enhancing friction between the stalk and sheath layers, thereby reducing LA. In contrast, rice sheaths encase the stalk less effectively and exhibit fewer overlaps, reducing surface friction and internal resistance. This likely explains the larger LA observed in rice, particularly in its lower leaves. In addition, the LA of maize gradually increased from top to bottom. The analysis of the degree of sheath wrapping at different leaf positions showed that the degree of sheath wrapping was negatively correlated with the leaf angle (Figure 2D). To further verify the contribution of sheaths to LA formation, we analysed the maize narrow sheath and drooping leaf 1 (nd1) mutant (Figure 2D). Compared to wild‐type (WT) plants, the nd1 mutant seedling and mature plants displayed a significantly reduced sheath width and disrupted wrapping around the stem, resulting in markedly increased LA, leaf drooping, and an architecture resembling rice (Figure 2E,F). These findings confirm that stem‐to‐sheath resistance and friction forces are critical for preventing leaf drooping.

Disrupt the Equilibrium Between Supporting and Drooping Forces Alter LA

To validate the relationship between mechanical strength, plant architecture, and lignin deposition in the leaf ligular region of maize, we first categorised the inbred lines based on LAs measurements from different leaf positions. Specifically, using the LAs of the second leaf above the ear (upper leaf) and the leaf below the ear (lower leaf), the maize inbred lines were classified into three distinct architectural types. These include the compact type, where both the upper and lower LAs are less than 20°, the expanded type, where both LAs exceed 30°, and the intermediate type, which is defined by a lower leaf angle under 20° and a difference of more than 10° between the upper and lower LAs. Based on these criteria, a total of 30 inbred lines representing the three architectural types were selected for subsequent analyses (Figure 3A,B). The results showed that for different leaf positions, the gravitational moments for upper leaves with small LA were significantly lower than those for lower leaves with larger LA across all inbred lines. This indicates that lower leaves require greater supporting forces to maintain their existing LA (Figure 3C). Furthermore, we observed that for different inbred lines, the gravitational moments of compact inbred lines (e.g., Z58) were smaller compared to those of expanded‐type inbred lines (e.g., W22). This suggests that expanded inbred lines require greater support to maintain their existing LA compared to compact inbred lines (Figure 3C).

To validate the effect of drooping forces on LA, various maize inbred lines were subjected to long‐term external load treatment. The statistical results show that after hanging a 1 g external load on leaf 3 (L3) of maize seedlings grown until the V3 stage (the leaf ligular has just broken through the sheath) for one week, the LAs of B73, W22, Mo17 and KN5585 increased by 70%, 31%, 66% and 64%, respectively, compared to the control. The result showed that long‐term loading significantly increased the LA of maize leaves, and this effect was stable and irreversible (Figure S3). To further analyse the supporting capacity among various maize inbred lines and leaf positions, we applied incremental external downward force (by increasing loads) to measure the maximum threshold at which leaf deformation occurred (Figure 3A). Results showed upper leaves sustained higher loads than lower leaves within each line, confirming their stronger support capacity. Furthermore, leaves of compact‐type inbred lines exhibited significantly greater support capacity than those of expanded‐type lines (Figures 3D–F and S4). Among the inbred lines tested, the lower leaf of expanded‐type inbred lines showed the lowest load‐carrying ability (average 3.50 N), while the upper leaf of compact‐type inbred lines showed the highest (average 4.94 N). This suggests that the supporting ability provided by compact type maize significantly exceeds the requirements to balance drooping force, resulting in a more stable plant architecture.

Importantly, lignin staining revealed significant lignin deposition in the ligular adaxial region of leaves with small LA, whereas leaves with large LA lacked this adaxial lignin deposition (Figure 3G,H). This finding verified the essential role of adaxial lignin in preventing leaf drooping, consistent with our previous hypothesis (Wang, Guo, et al. 2024). Remarkably, lignin abundance and the number of adaxial SC layers were positively correlated with support capacity. For instance, Sup1 exhibited ~5 layers of adaxial SC, Z58 and B73 exhibited ~15 layers, while W22 lacked adaxial SC entirely (Figure 3H). This likely explains the greater tolerance of compact‐type inbred lines to external load treatment.

Construction of a Comprehensive Ligular Regions Cell Atlas of Three Inbred Lines

To investigate the distinct cell types in the ligular region of various maize inbred lines at a single‐nucleus resolution, we performed an snRNA‐seq analysis. The ligular region (second leaf, V3) in W22, B73 and Z58 plants was collected and used for snRNA‐seq (Figure 4A). We used a rigorous criterion with > 200 genes per nucleus to obtain high‐quality data for subsequent analyses. As a result, a total of 18 427 nuclei passed our quality control and were then used for the construction of a comprehensive integrative cell atlas (Table S2). We used Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) for dimensionality reduction and visualisation, and ultimately identified 34 putative cell clusters (C0‐C33) in the ligular region (Figure 4B, Tables S3 and S4).

To annotate these cell clusters, multiple previously established markers of the ligular region were used (Figures 4C and S5, Table S3). For example, the bundle sheath (BS) abaxial cells markers sucrose transport protein Sugars Will Eventually be Exported Transporters 13a/b/c (SWEET13a/b/c), the phloem marker Sugar Transporter Protein (STP1/7) genes, the epidermal marker Glossy1 and 3‐Ketoacyl‐CoA synthase (KCS3/13) genes, the mesophyll marker gene Rubisco small subunit (SSU) and Rubisco activase 1 (RCA1) (Figures 4C and S5). Consistent with the actual cellular structure of the ligular region, multiple cell types were annotated, including the phloem (C25, C27 and C31), xylem (C25 and C29), BS‐ad (C30 and C33), BS‐ab (C19, C22, C24), mesophyll cells (C0, C4, C14 and C15), epidermal cells (C10, C13 and C21) and hypodermis cells (Figure 4B). Among these hypodermis cells, the cell expansion‐related gene, including alpha expansin 1 (EXPA1), EXPA5 and EXPB8, were highly expressed in cluster C2, C6, C8, C9, C12 and C18, indicating that these cells might represent hypodermis (HP1 and HP5) cells. Multiple hormone‐related genes, such as BRD1, were enriched in C7, C16 and C20, indicating that these (HP2) cells might occupy the nearby of HP to regulate the hormone response. Taken together, we construct a comprehensive ligular regions cell atlas and revealed 13 major cell types, which will help to facilitate the exploration of functional cell types and regulators in the ligular region.

Comparative Analysis of the Ligular Region Cell Atlas of Three Inbred Lines Identified Lignified Adaxial Hypodermal Cells

To analyse the differences in cell types among the leaf ligular regions of three inbred lines in detail, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the snRNA‐seq results from these lines (Figure 5A,B). Comparative analysis of the mapping results for the three inbred lines revealed that most cell clusters were conserved, with differences primarily in cell numbers. Through a comparative analysis of cell numbers across different cell clusters, we found that the HP3 cell clusters (C11, C26) were exclusively present in the tissues of Z58 and B73, while the HP4 cell cluster (C23) was unique to the W22 tissue (Figure 5C, Table S4). We believe that the differences between HP3 and HP4 cells are closely related to the lignin deposition in the adaxial hypodermis cells of the three inbred lines.

To investigate the functions of the enriched genes in C11, C23 and C26, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) analysis on the specific genes. The results indicated that the functions of the enriched genes in C11, C23 and C26 mainly focused on phosphate metabolism, cellular protein metabolism and phenylpropanoid metabolism (Figure 5D,E). Furthermore, our analysis revealed that a few lignin biosynthesis‐related genes were specifically enriched in HP3, such as Peroxidases 26 (PRX26), 4‐coumarate CoA ligase 2 (4CL2) and Hydroxycinnamoyl‐CoA shikimates/quinate hydroxycinnamoyl transferase 7 (HCT7), which can be attributed to the fact that lignin synthesis in the leaf ligular occurs across various cell types, such as abaxial SC cells and xylem cells (Figures 5F and S6). Additionally, we observed that the expression levels of transcription factors related to lignin biosynthesis were relatively low, potentially due to the young tissues collected, as lignin synthesis in the adaxial region predominantly occurs during the seedling stage (Table S4).

NST2 And NST3 Regulate Adaxial Lignin Deposition to Influence Mechanical Strength and LA

To further analyse the genes that regulate lignin synthesis at the adaxial and abaxial sides of leaf ligular in inbred lines such as Z58, B73 and W22, we re‐evaluated previously published transcriptomic data from mature plants of these lines and focused specifically on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) at the adaxial and abaxial ends in W22 (Wang, Guo, et al. 2024). Our analysis identified 1244 genes that were specifically downregulated and 1228 genes that were upregulated at the adaxial end of W22 (Figure 6A, Tables S5 and S6). GO analysis indicated that the functions of the downregulated genes at the adaxial side were primarily enriched in lignin metabolism, glucan metabolism, stress response, signal transduction and biological process regulation (Figure 6B). We paid attention to transcription factors involved in these biological processes, including NAC transcription factors (such as NST1, NST2, NST3 and NST4), Myeloblastosis (MYB) transcription factors (such as MYB18 and MYB27), and basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) transcription factors (such as bHLH9 and bHLH135). NAC and MYB transcription factors are well‐known regulators of lignin biosynthesis, for example, NST1, NST2, NST3, NST4, which are reported to directly control lignin biosynthesis in various plants (Figure 6C).

RT‐qPCR assays revealed that NAC transcription factors NST1, NST2, NST3, NAC16, NAC65 and NAC47, as well as MYB transcription factors MYB70, MYB77 and MYB79, exhibit adaxial and abaxial specific differential expression in the ligular region of the W22 inbred line, with significantly higher expression levels in the abaxial compared to the adaxial side. In contrast, no significant differential expression was observed between these regions in the B73 and Z58 inbred lines (Figure 6D and S7). These findings are consistent with previous transcriptome analyses and cytological observations across the three inbred lines (Figures 3G,H and 6C), suggesting that NST1, NST2, NST3, NAC16, NAC65, MYB70, MYB77, and related factors may be involved in regulating lignin accumulation in the ligular region, thereby influencing mechanical strength and leaf angle. Furthermore, we selected NST2 and NST3 for RNA in situ hybridization to validate their expression pattern in the ligule of W22 and Z58. The results showed that NST2 and NST3 transcripts predominantly accumulate in the abaxial vascular bundle sheath, sclerenchyma cells, as well as in the adaxial ligule, epidermis and hypodermis cells—regions associated with lignin deposition (Figure 6E and S8). This spatial expression pattern strongly supports our hypothesis that NST transcription factors regulate lignin deposition in the ligular region, ultimately affecting mechanical properties and leaf angle modulation.

To validate the roles of NSTs in LA regulation and mechanical strength, we obtained loss‐of‐function mutants for NST2 and NST3 (Figure S9A). Phenotypic observations revealed that the LAs of nst2 and nst3 mutants were significantly increased compared to those of the wild type, indicating that NST2 and NST3 negatively regulate LA (Figure 6F,G). Mechanical testing results indicated that while the gravitational moment in NSTs mutants did not show significant differences compared to the wild type, the maximum downward force tolerated by nst2 and nst3 mutants was significantly reduced, suggesting that NST2 and NST3 positively regulate the mechanical support capacity of maize (Figure 6H,I). Consistent with these findings, tissue section analyses showed that lignin deposition in the leaf ligular of nst2 and nst3 mutants was significantly reduced, with a complete absence of lignin deposition at the adaxial side (Figures 6G and S9B,C). This indicates that NST2 and NST3 negatively regulate leaf angle and positively regulate mechanical support capacity by modulating lignin biosynthesis, particularly in the adaxial side of the leaf ligular region. These results further confirm that the level of lignin deposition in the leaf ligular, especially in the adaxial region, is positively correlated with mechanical support capacity and negatively correlated with leaf angle, which is consistent with our previous results (Figure 3G,H).

Discussion

Plant architecture, defined by the three‐dimensional organisation of branches, leaves, and reproductive structures, is a complex phenotype shaped by evolutionary adaptation and agricultural selection. Deciphering the underlying regulatory mechanisms is critical for the genetic improvement of crops, enabling targeted architectural modifications to enhance plant performance and grain yield. This study investigates the developmental and cellular mechanisms, along with the genetic networks governing this morphological diversity across grasses, with a focus on how dynamic mechanical strength regulates leaf angle, revealing both conserved principles and lineage‐specific innovations.

Mechanical support, essential for plant growth and development, relies on the coordinated actions of structures like cell walls, xylem, roots and stems. This system enables plants to maintain stable, upright forms under variable environmental conditions (Coen and Cosgrove 2023; Shah et al. 2017). The primary cell wall, composed mainly of cellulose, hemicellulose and pectin, provides basic rigidity and strength for the structure. The secondary cell wall, characterised by increased lignin deposition, significantly enhances mechanical strength, particularly in SC and xylem cells (Hill Jr. et al. 2018; Kumar et al. 2016). Our findings demonstrate that lignification within the adaxial SC cell layers of the ligular region, coupled with increased Vb (xylem) distribution and density, is crucial for mechanical reinforcement in the leaves, especially in maize. Although the outward extension of the blade improves light capture efficiency, it concurrently increases structural instability to environmental stimuli, like wind. During evolution and natural selection, various grasses have evolved distinct strategies to resolve this trade‐off (Byrne et al. 2001). Species with shorter blades (e.g., foxtail, ryegrass, wheat) minimise leaf weight to mitigate the adverse effects of environmental stimuli. Conversely, maize domestication selected for expanded blade areas to maximise photosynthetic capacity and grain yield, driving the evolution of robust mechanical support systems to ensure structural stability (Figures 1 and S1).

Previous researches on LA predominantly focus on ligular region morphology (e.g., auricle areas, emergence of ligule and auricle, asymmetric division/elongation) (Cao et al. 2022; Kong et al. 2017), our findings establish that LA is negatively correlated with support capacity and positively correlated with the downward bending force generated by the blade weight (Figures 1, 2, 3). We demonstrate that the balance between support capacity and downward bending force is critical for maintaining leaf posture. In species like foxtail and wheat, a weaker support system combined with relatively heavy leaves leads to larger LA or even drooping leaves. In contrast, maize and rice, which possess robust support systems, demonstrate that the support provided by the ligular region significantly exceeds the mechanical demands of the leaves, facilitating upward growth (Figure 2A). Support capacity primarily depends on mechanical strength, including sheath, leaf midrib, and minor veins toughness, SC cell layers, and cell wall thickness in the ligular region (Figures 1, 2, 3). Furthermore, the degree and layer of sheath wrapping around the stem significantly influence both support capacity and LA (Figure 2C–F).

Through innovative mechanical support capability assessment and tissue section analysis of different leaf positions in various maize inbred lines, we discovered a close relationship between the lignification at the adaxial side of the leaf ligular and leaf angle, as well as mechanical strength. Leaves with a high degree of lignification at the adaxial side typically exhibit stronger mechanical support and smaller LAs. This finding elucidates the interesting phenomenon observed in the “tight topper and loose lower” type of maize, where significant lignin deposition is evident at the adaxial end of the upper leaves, while the lower leaves show no lignin deposition at the same location (Figure 4).

snRNA‐seq analysis of the leaf ligular in three representative maize inbred lines (Z58, B73 and W22) revealed significant differences in the hypodermis cells at the adaxial end (Figures 4 and 5). Specifically, HP3 was found in B73 and Z58 inbred lines, while HP4 was present in the W22 inbred line. Detailed analysis of genes specifically enriched in HP3 and HP4 indicated that lignin biosynthesis‐related genes (e.g., PRX26, 4CL2 and HCT7) were significantly enriched in HP3 but were nearly undetectable in HP4 (Figure 5). This suggests that HP3 and HP4 represent distinct regions of lignin deposition at the adaxial side. Taken together, whether lignification occurs in the hypodermis cells at the ligular adaxial side is a crucial factor determining the mechanical support capacity and leaf angle in maize. NAC transcription factors, particularly NST1/NST2/NST3/NST4 have long been recognised as key regulators of lignin synthesis (Liu et al. 2021; Mitsuda et al. 2007; Wang, Xing, et al. 2024). Our study, through phenotypic and tissue section analyses of nst2 and nst3 mutants, demonstrates that NST2 and NST3 regulate lignin biosynthesis in the leaf ligular, especially on the adaxial side, thereby enhancing the mechanical support capacity of the leaves and contributing to reduced LAs (Figure 6).

In summary, our study elucidates the dynamic regulation of LA through the interplay of drooping forces and supporting forces. Crucially, inherent supporting forces, derived from mechanical strength contributed by sheath layers, sclerenchyma thickness and lignin deposition, typically surpass those required to counteract the drooping effect. Leaf angle is inversely proportional to the difference between mechanical support capacity and leaf weight, reflecting a dynamic equilibrium that varies among leaf types. In compact‐type leaves, supporting forces greatly exceed drooping forces; in intermediate‐type, supporting forces exceed drooping forces; and in expanded‐type, supporting forces moderately exceed or approximate drooping forces. Disrupting this balance, as observed in nst2 and nst3 mutants with reduced lignin deposition in the hypodermal cells on the adaxial side of the leaf ligular region, weakens mechanical support and consequently increases LA. This highlights lignin biosynthesis regulation by key factors such as NST2 and NST3 as critical for modulating mechanical support capacity and leaf angle in maize (Figure 6K). Overall, our findings identify mechanical reinforcement as a prime, previously underappreciated target for genetic improvement of crops, providing novel avenues for advancing agricultural productivity through precise modulation of leaf architecture.

Material and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

In this study, maize (inbred lines B73 and Z58), rice (cultivars ZH11 and Nipponbare), wheat (JM22 and Fielder), Setaria italica (cultivars Zhonggu 6 and Qinggu), Setaria viridis (accession ME034V) and Digitaria sanguinalis were grown under well‐controlled growth chamber or natural outdoor field conditions and used for physiological and histological comparative analyses. For further analyses, as well as molecular studies, wild‐type and mutant varieties from maize were utilised, including 148 maize inbred lines (e.g., Z58, B73 and W22), the nst2 and nst3 mutant plants. The EMS mutants of NST2 (Zm00001d035084) and NST3 (Zm00001d036050) in the B73 background. The nst2 mutant (EMS4‐134f89) was described in previous studies (Wang, Xing, et al. 2024), and the nst3 mutant (EMS4‐1ae85e) was ordered from the maize EMS database (http://maizeems.qlnu.edu.cn/).

The culture conditions in the growth chamber and greenhouse were as follows: 12 h light/12 h dark, with a day temperature of 28°C, night temperature of 22°C, humidity at 50%, and light intensity of 400 μmol m−2 s−1. The field experiments were conducted in the field of Shandong Agricultural University in Tai'an, Shandong Province, China (36.1980° N, 117.1297° E) for three years (2022–2025).

Sections Staining and Observation

For histological analyses, fresh ligular regions were collected from multiple plants and sectioned freehand using a sharp razor blade. At the mature stage, cross and longitudinal sections of the ligular median region of functional leaves from different plants were prepared and immediately used for histological staining and analysis. For safranine O staining assays, the ligular medial region of various plants was sliced into approximately 50 μm‐thick sections and immersed in safranine O staining solution (Sigma‐Aldrich) for 15 min. The samples were then washed three times with 75% ethanol solution for 10–30 min each, followed by two washes with sterile water before imaging with a microscope. For propidium iodide (PI) staining, the ligular median region of various plants was sliced into 50 μm‐thick sections and incubated in a 1 μg/mL PI solution (Sigma‐Aldrich) for 1 min. The RFP fluorescence was excited at 561 nm and detected in the range of 560–640 nm using a Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (LSM880, Zeiss). To observe lignin autofluorescence, similar 50 μm‐thick sections of the lamina joint (ligular median) region were prepared and imaged using the same Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (LSM880, Zeiss).

Calculation Formula of Gravitational Moment of Plant Leaves

The foundational calculation formula of gravitational moment is expressed as: M = (1/2 × BL) × BWt × g × sin(LA). The key parameters in the formula are defined as follows: BWt refers to the weight of a single blade. Gravitational acceleration (g) is the constant acceleration due to Earth's gravity, approximated as 9.8 m/s2. BL is the distance from the connection point between the sheath and the leaf tip, measured in metres (m). LA is the angle between the leaf and the stem, measured in degrees (°) using angle‐measuring devices. The derivation of the key intermediate variable, “the distance from the petiole region to the horizontal centre position of the blade (d),” is based on the simplification of the leaf as a rigid structure with uniform thickness and consistent density. Under this assumption, the mass is evenly distributed, placing the centre of mass at the midpoint of the BL, specifically at a straight‐line distance of 1/2 × BL from the petiole region. When there is an angle between the leaf and the horizontal direction, the effective distance (d) influencing the moment is the projection of the centre of mass distance onto the horizontal direction, calculated as: d = (1/2 × BL) × sin(LA). Finally, integrating the above parameters and derivation process yields the complete moment calculation formula.

RNA‐Seq, SnRNA‐Seq and Data Analysis

For RNA‐seq analyses, the adaxial and abaxial sides of the leaf ligular region of the leaf above the primary ear from Z58, B73 and W22 were equally divided and used for the bulk RNA‐seq assay were used for RNA‐seq analysis, respectively. Three biological replicates were used for various plants, and the leaf ligular region of 10 plants was pooled together as one replicate. RNA‐seq was performed by Biomaker Tech (Beijing, China). Z58, B73 and W22 reads were mapped to the B73 reference genome (GRAMENE_4.0) using HISAT2 (Kim et al. 2015). StringTie was used to perform transcript assembly and quantification (Pertea et al. 2015). The differential expression analysis was performed in R using the DESeq2 package (v1.40.2). The raw read count matrix generated by featureCounts was used as input to construct a DESeqDataSet object, with mock‐treated samples set as the reference group. Differential expression testing was conducted using the DESeq function under default settings, employing the Wald test. p‐values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. DEG was identified based on the Fold Change (log2FC ≥ 1 or ≤ −1), p‐value (p‐value ≤ 0.05), and FPKM (≥ 1, an average of FPKM in different samples). The gene expression heat map was created using the R package ComplexHeatmap (Gu et al. 2016). Venn analysis was done on the Venny 2.1 website (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html).

For snRNA‐seq analyses, the ligular region of the second leaf was harvested from 3‐week‐old Z58, B73 and W22 plants grown in a plant growth chamber. The procedures of snRNA‐seq analyses were performed following previous studies (Wang, Guo, et al. 2024). The DEGs among various cell clusters were identified using the FindAllMarkers function from the Seurat package (v5.1.0) in R. The Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was applied with the following parameters: only.pos = TRUE, log2FC. threshold = 0.25 and min.pct = 0.25. The DEGs were used for cell type annotation. The raw RNA‐seq and snRNA‐seq data from this study have been deposited at the China National Center for Bioinformation under accession number PRJCA020760.

RNA In Situ Hybridization Assays

The ligular median regions from L3 and L4 of Z58 and W22 seedlings at the V3 stage were used for longitudinal sectioning and subsequent in situ hybridization assays. The in situ hybridization analyses were performed using DIG‐labelled specific probes for NST2 and NST3 (Table S7), designed and synthesised by SweAMI (GDP1087; Servicebio). After washing with PBS, the ligular tissues were fixed in a plant‐specific in situ hybridization fixative solution (G1113; Servicebio) for over 12 h under vacuum using a vacuum pump, and then stored at 4°C. The fixed tissues were sectioned into 6–8 mm pieces, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin sections of 6 μm thickness were cut, baked at 62°C for 2 h, deparaffinised, and rehydrated through graded ethanol to water. The sections were then subjected to repair with appropriate solutions, digested with proteinase K, washed, and pre‐hybridised. Hybridization was performed with the probe solutions, followed by washing and subsequent hybridization with branched and signal probes. Finally, the sections were incubated with anti‐DIG‐AP antibody (11093274910; Roche), developed with BCIP/NBT substrate (G1623; Servicebio), mounted and analysed for positive signals under a microscope (Nikon DS‐U3).
