Scaling evidence: national causal evaluation of perinatal quality collaboratives needs better data
Authors: Rachel Nesbit, Rachel K Landis, Kandice A Kapinos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerinatal quality collaboratives (PQCs) have been widely adopted across US states to improve maternal–infant health care delivery and outcomes, yet rigorous evidence of their national impact on maternal mortality and severe maternal morbidity remains limited. This commentary identifies 2 fundamental challenges constraining causal inference in PQC evaluations. First, inconsistent measurement of key outcomes, stemming from staggered state adoption of revised vital statistics certificates and coding changes, confounds attempts to distinguish genuine health improvements from data artifacts. Second, the causal pathway from state-level PQC adoption to health outcomes involves multiple intermediate changes in clinical practices and care delivery that remain unobserved in administrative data. This imprecision in treatment measurement, combined with rare outcomes, severely limits statistical power, especially for analysis of subpopulations at increased risk of adverse maternal–infant health outcomes (e.g., Black women, those in low-income communities). We recommend establishing a national registry to track quality improvement initiatives systematically, investing in harmonization of vital statistics data across states and time periods, and exploring alternative evaluation designs (e.g., synthetic control methods, stepped-wedge trials) to credibly estimate PQC impacts on maternal health outcomes.

The US maternal health crisis continues and in 2022, the maternal mortality rate was more than 50% higher than the closest peer country.1 Severe maternal morbidity (SMM)—life-threatening conditions during the labor and delivery hospitalization—is much more common, affecting up to 60, 000 women annually.2 In response, many federal and state initiatives have been implemented, including systems-level interventions designed to improve the quality and safety of maternity care across hospitals, health systems, and clinical settings. Among the most prominent of these programs are perinatal quality collaboratives (PQCs), which aim to improve maternal–infant healthcare delivery and outcomes, particularly preventable deaths.3

All states have implemented PQCs, though the timing and degree of involvement vary. PQCs facilitate data-driven quality improvement (QI) models, typically focusing on provider-level processes adapted to local needs. Despite widespread adoption and some evidence of improvements in specific outcomes,3 little is known about their national, population-level impact on maternal mortality and SMM. Most studies have focused on descriptive pre–post comparisons of a specific initiative or state and suggested improvements. However, because many initiatives target local maternal health challenges, naïve comparisons might reflect elevated baseline risk driven by environmental, sociopolitical, or broader healthcare system factors, and without a strong design to address confounding, may not reflect true impacts. Two challenges complicate expanding the evidence base. First, measurement of key outcomes (e.g., maternal death or SMM metrics) has changed considerably over time and across states.4,5 Second, the causal pathway between initiative implementation and improved outcomes is complex, multilevel, includes unobservable elements, and varies across states. In this commentary, we discuss each of these issues and their interaction, concluding with recommendations for moving this literature forward. Addressing these challenges requires consistently measured maternal-infant data and precise information about the scope and timing of PQC adoption and implementation. As Congress reauthorized the Preventing Maternal Deaths Act with renewed investments in Maternal Mortality Review Committee (MMRC) data6—the only national data on preventable maternal deaths—this is a timely opportunity to consider improvements in related data infrastructure.

Although the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) National Vital Statistics System (NVSS) provides restricted-access data on birth complications and maternal and infant deaths, these data have important limitations. In 2003, NCHS revised both the standard birth and death certificates, adding a pregnancy checkbox to the death certificate and morbidity checkboxes to the birth certificate, but state adoption was slow and uneven.4 Moreover, changes in cause-of-death coding and variation in certificate completion compound inconsistencies in the measurement of maternal deaths and SMM. As others have documented, attempts to create time-consistent measures either underestimate maternal deaths5 (making a rare event rarer) or reduce the number of state-years available for analysis. Consequently, observed differences in maternal health may reflect changes in data collection and measurement rather than changes in care. The Pregnancy-Related Mortality Surveillance System and state MMRCs have their own comparability challenges. State MMRCs vary in how they determine pregnancy-related deaths and classify underlying causes of death, though the CDC is working to standardize this process.6 Because mortality case reviews are complex, MMRC reports are often years out of date at the time of public release, constraining their utility for timely PQC evaluation.7 Birth-certificate-based SMM measures have also been shown to be poorly correlated with hospital billing data, raising validity concerns.8 State Inpatient Databases offer promising measures of SMM but do not contain comprehensive information about deaths, and not all states make their data available to researchers.

The standard SMM measure has important structural limitations. It excludes mental health conditions, a critical omission as they are the leading cause of pregnancy-related death, and events related to pregnancy outcomes other than live births.7 Traditional measures of maternal mortality and SMM also ignore the 60% or more of “pregnancy-related deaths” that occur outside the labor and delivery inpatient stay,7 including those related to overdose and suicide, events often targeted by PQC initiatives. Without better data, PQC effectiveness in these areas cannot be empirically demonstrated, even if initiatives are working. Similarly, PQCs are increasingly expanding their focus from in-hospital birth care to also engage with emergency departments and ambulatory clinics, but without operationalizable quality metrics, any care improvements in those spaces cannot be assessed.

Beyond measurement challenges, treating PQC adoption as a single intervention obscures meaningful heterogeneity across and within PQCs. As Figure 1 shows, the causal chain between state-level PQC adoption and maternal mortality and SMM rates has many intermediate points: states adopt PQCs, hospitals and clinicians choose whether to participate, and PQCs choose specific initiatives. All of these choices jointly affect care processes and outcomes, but because they are often unobservable in administrative data, national assessments can only estimate state-level adoption effects, not the mechanisms underpinning implementation or the effects of actual participation. Resulting estimates are therefore best understood as intent-to-treat rather than treatment-on-the-treated effects of collaboration. This distinction matters because PQCs vary in structure, initiatives implemented, hospital participation, and data-reporting capabilities—differences that may themselves explain variation in outcomes across states.

These issues compound the problem of low statistical power. Outcomes such as maternal mortality and SMM are relatively rare, and the level of available variation is constrained by reductions in sample size due to changes in vital statistics data, as well as imprecision in the measurement of the timing of treatment and the identification of mothers who were exposed to PQC initiatives. A difference-in-differences study using state-level death certificates would be underpowered to detect an impact as small as 1 death per 100 000 births. For a minimum detectible effect of this size, researchers would need a sample of almost 200 states. In reality, in analyses using NVSS birth data, only 25 states could be analyzed due to the timing of adoption and certificate updates.9 Even in individual-level analyses with many observations, the observable variation in PQC adoption comes from the state level, constraining statistical power to detect causal effects.

These challenges intensify when examining causal effects of PQCs for subpopulations with disproportionately high rates of SMM and maternal mortality, especially Black women, those in lower-income communities, and those with Medicaid insurance.10 Restricting the sample to these subpopulations further reduces statistical power and undermines our ability to evaluate efforts to reduce maternal health disparities.

The path forward requires action at multiple levels. With sufficient funding, PQCs and their hospital partners can act immediately. Establishing a systematic registry of quality improvement initiatives akin to clinical trial registries or to the Department of Education's What Works Clearinghouse would improve both measurement and cross-hospital learning and sharing. While longer-term data infrastructure is being developed, PQCs should prioritize measuring process and proximal outcomes, like protocol adoption rates and intermediate clinical outcomes, that are more directly observable, more proximate to PQC activities, and more statistically tractable. PQCs should also partner with researchers to build prospective evaluations such as step-wedge trials into implementation from the start, provided funders support these efforts. Federal action is equally essential. Congress's reauthorization of the Preventing Maternal Deaths Act is a timely opportunity to fund harmonization of NCHS birth certificate data across states and over time and expand standardized MMRC capacity—investments that would strengthen the evidence base for PQCs and maternal health policy broadly. These steps are essential to support the rigorous national analyses that PQCs merit and mothers deserve.
