Persistent Ompahalomesenteric Duct in a Newborn With 16p11.2 Deletion: Report of New Findings
Authors: Surasak Puvabanditsin, Sathyaprasad Burjonrappa, Natasha Cordero, Su Young Park, Jenny Choi, Amrryn Halari, Dawn Arumemi, Rajeev Mehta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOmphalomesenteric duct (OMD) is an embryologic communication between the extraembryonic yolk sac and the primitive midgut. The OMD malformations are relatively rare diseases associated with developmental defects related to the closure of the omphalomesenteric duct. These malformations can present in various forms, including cysts, fistulas, or sinuses, and may be either asymptomatic or symptomatic. Meckel's diverticulum is the most common outcome of failed OMD resorption. We report a case of a newborn with a symptomatic OMD malformation associated with a 16p11.2 microdeletion. This is the first case report of this association, and we present a literature review.

Introduction

Omphalomesenteric (vitelline) duct malformations comprise a wide spectrum of anatomic structures and associated symptoms (or no symptoms). The duct may persist after birth and manifestations may range from a completely patent omphalomesenteric duct (POMD) at the umbilicus to a variety of lesser remnants including omphalomesenteric duct cysts, fibrous cords connecting the umbilicus to the distal ileum, granulation tissue at the umbilicus, umbilical hernia, umbilical polyp, and the famous diverticulum of Meckel's diverticulum (frequency 2% to 3% of the population) [1, 2, 3]. 16p11.2 microdeletion prevalence has been estimated at about 3 in 10,000 [4]. Most affected individuals experience developmental delays, autistic characteristics, and intellectual disabilities. Some may have recurrent seizures, while others experience no health or behavioral problems. We report a new case of a neonate with symptomatic omphalomesenteric duct malformation with a 16p11.2 microdeletion and review the literature.

Case History/Examination

This is a small for gestational age preterm male neonate who was born at 35 5/7 weeks of gestation to a 31‐year‐old primigravida mother by cesarean section because of fetal distress. Apgar scores were 4 and 9 at 1 and 5 min, respectively. He was apneic and floppy; IPPV and ET intubation were administered in the labor and delivery room. Prenatal care was complicated by abnormal fetal ultrasound. Prenatal sonogram revealed a small stomach in the 1st trimester. The family history was unremarkable, and there is no consanguinity. The non‐invasive prenatal test (NIPT) performed at 12 weeks of gestation revealed a normal 46 XY. At delivery, a beefy red structure with intestinal luminal mucosa and meconium at its end protruded from the umbilicus (Figures 1, 2, 3). Normal saline gauze was applied to the umbilical content before he was transferred to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU). Birth weight was 2155 g (8th centile), length 46 cm (45th centile), and head circumference was 31 cm (11th centile). The physical findings at birth showed a small for gestational age appearing neonate, dysmorphic facies, low‐set ears, retrognathia, cleft soft palate, and umbilical cord abnormalities (Figure 4). Pulmonary hypertension and bilateral superior vena cava were detected by echocardiography.

Methods

He underwent exploratory laparotomy on the 2nd day of life. The operative findings were a patent OMD prolapsing through the umbilicus. There appeared to be an intestinal obstruction at the proximal end of the patent omphalomesenteric duct (OMD) at the level of the umbilical ring with significant dilation of the proximal small bowel and collapsed distal small bowel and microcolon (Figure 5). There were some adhesions of the patent OMD to the umbilical stalk. The patent OMD and segmental bowel resections were resected, and the inverting anastomosis was performed between the proximal and distal ends of the small bowel. The appendix was removed. There was no urachal patency.

Photograph showing OMD site (arrow) with marked dilated proximal bowel and small distal bowel.

Conclusion and Results

The pathologic finding of the patent OMD showed small bowel morphology without ectopic gastric mucosa (Figure 6). The post‐operative course was complicated by moderately persistent pulmonary hypertension. The infant developed supraventricular tachycardia on the 8th day of life (DOL), which was treated with Digoxin. An upper airway obstruction was noted due to micrognathia and posterior displacement of the tongue. The patient required surgery by a Pediatric Ear, Nose, and Throat surgeon. He was discharged home at 4 months of age. The infant is on room air and partially feeding orally, supplemented by tube feeding. Cleft palate is scheduled to be repaired at 15–18 months of age.

Cytogenetic and Molecular Studies

Whole genome SNP (Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms) microarray analysis was performed using the SNP oligonucleotide microarray analysis (SOMA) CytoScan HD Accel platform, which uses over 29,441 nonpolymorphic copy number (NPCN) probes and 742,130 SNP probes for LOH/AOH analysis and relationship assessment. The array has an average intragenic spacing of 0.818 kilobase (kb) and an average intergenic spacing of 1.51 kb. Total genomic DNA was extracted from the patient's blood sample and digested with Xcel, and then ligated to Xcel adaptors. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products were purified and quantified. Purified DNA was fragmented, biotin labeled, and hybridized to the Cytoscan HD Accel Gene Chip. The data was analyzed using Chromosome Analysis Suite, which is based on the GRCh37/hg19 assembly. There was a 587 kilobase (KB) deletion of the short arm of chromosome 16: arr[hg19] 16p11.2(29,591,327–30,177,999)x1. This interval includes numerous OMIM genes (start: SPN to end MAPK3).

Discussion

Our patient is a dysmorphic newborn with persistent OMD, which has not previously been reported in association with 16p11.2 microdeletion. During the 3rd week of intrauterine life, a communication between the intraembryonic gut and the yolk sac was observed. As the development proceeded, this communication narrowed into a tube known as the omphalomesenteric duct (OMD), which usually obliterated by the end of the 7th week. During normal development, the OMD closes its lumen to become a thin fibrous band that is eventually absorbed. By the 10th week, the midgut returns to the abdominal cavity. If the OMD fails to atrophy and disintegrate completely, the omphalomesenteric duct remnant will persist as a malformation. The anatomical anomalies of the omphalomesenteric duct remnant/malformation comprise a broad spectrum that can be classified into four types: (1) Meckel's diverticulum (MD), the most common malformation, occurs in about 2% of the population. Ectopic gastric mucosa can often be found lining the MD, which may lead to bleeding. Other complications include intussusception and diverticulitis. (2) Umbilical polyp, a remnant of gastric or intestinal mucosa at the umbilicus, appears as bright red polypoid tissue with persistent discharge. The umbilical polyp may appear similar to an umbilical granuloma. While umbilical granuloma should respond to simple cauterization, the umbilical polyp would not. (3) Omphalomesenteric duct cyst occurs when one or more cysts develop within the remnants of the obliterated omphalomesenteric duct, which can potentially lead to small bowel volvulus. (4) Persistent omphalomesenteric duct presents with gastrointestinal drainage from the umbilicus in the neonatal period [1].

In the case of our patient, the malformation manifests as a completely patent omphalomesenteric duct at the umbilicus. POMD may be patent or obliterated. Incomplete obliteration of the omphalomesenteric duct (OMD) can result in a fibrous connective cord attached to the umbilicus. A fully patent OMD would result in an omphaloileal fistula, as in this case. The fistula may contain ectopic gastric, colonic, or pancreatic tissue. Symptoms of a patent OMD include fistulas at the umbilicus, intussusception/prolapse of ileum at the umbilicus, intestinal obstruction from various causes, melena, anemia, abdominal pain, inflammation, etc. Although symptoms occur most frequently during childhood (especially within the first 2 years of life), they may persist into adulthood. OMD malformations are found with equal frequency among the sexes, but a significantly greater incidence of symptoms occurs in males [1, 2, 5].

POMD being a cause of small bowel obstruction at birth, as observed in our patient, is unique. Only a few cases have been reported [2, 6]. There are many mechanisms for small‐bowel obstruction from a persistent omphalomesenteric duct. These include intussusception in the case of a patent omphalomesenteric duct, volvulus, an internal hernia (closed‐loop obstruction) from a patent omphalomesenteric duct, or a fibrous connection between the umbilicus and the ileum. Congenital omphalomesenteric ducts are clinically significant due to their potential to result in intestinal obstruction, as observed in our patient. The POMD image in this case showed eviscerated mucosa of ileum through OMD at the umbilicus, causing the intestinal obstruction.

Our case report should remind and alert obstetricians, pediatricians, and nurses who routinely clamp, manipulate, and shorten the umbilical cord that careful inspection of the cord of every newborn at the time of delivery is essential. Any unusual thickening of the base of the umbilical cord should caution healthcare providers to look twice before clamping the cord. If this is misdiagnosed, clamping of the umbilical cord after birth may cause trauma to the OMD or intestinal loop [7, 8].

16p11.2 deletion syndrome is a disorder caused by the deletion of a small piece of chromosome 16. The deletion occurs near the middle of the chromosome at a location designated p11.2. The frequency has been estimated to be approximately 3 in 10,000 [9]. Most people tested for the 16p11.2 deletion have needed medical attention as a result of developmental delay or autistic characteristics. Common clinical features include motor speech disorder, language disorder, motor coordination difficulties, psychiatric conditions, and autistic features [9, 10]. Other medical issues reported in 16p11.2 microdeletions include vertebral anomalies (e.g., Scoliosis), craniosynostosis, cardiac malformations, neuroblastoma, and hearing loss [4, 9]. Notably, we have reported new clinical findings, including micrognathia, cleft soft palate, and POMD in a patient with 16p11.2 microdeletion.

In conclusion, POMD as a cause of small bowel obstruction in newborns is an extremely rare and exceptional finding. The malformations observed in our patient with chromosomal anomaly 16p11.2 microdeletion have not been previously reported. We report a rare association of POMD with small bowel obstruction, micrognathia, cleft palate, and 16p11.2 microdeletion syndrome. Recognizing the diversity of POMD malformations is crucial for effective and optimal management.
