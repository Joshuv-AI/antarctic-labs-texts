A new era for heavy menstrual bleeding
Authors: Veronica H Flood
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Langer and O’Brien1 present a compelling case for the necessity of awareness of appropriate diagnostic and management strategies for bleeding across the life span in persons with von Willebrand disease (VWD). They outline issues in younger adolescents, those of childbearing age, and those dealing with menopause and aging. Clear discussion of the differing needs in these different eras helps clinicians and patients alike to deal with bleeding in VWD. Special attention is given to the problem of iron deficiency, particularly prevalent in VWD and other bleeding disorders.

Bleeding remains a perennial problem in people who menstruate. Multiple factors contribute to the challenges in diagnosis and treatment. Medical science has certainly progressed since 1900 BC, when hysteria in women was attributed to a wandering uterus.2 Interestingly, some ancient cultures viewed menstruation as a sacred gift from the gods, whereas many world religions place it in the category of sin or impurity.3 The 20th century brought medical insight to women’s issues, including moving childbirth into a hospital setting. This change, at least at first, did not actually improve maternal mortality and may have temporarily increased it before the adoption of sterile techniques and antibiotic use.4

Information on these issues is sorely needed. The preguideline survey conducted to inform the American Society of Hematology/International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis/National Bleeding Disorders Foundation/World Federation of Hemophilia VWD guidelines, heavy menstrual bleeding, postpartum bleeding, and treatment surrounding pregnancy and delivery were all ranked highly as items of interest to both medical professionals and patients and families.5 This suggests a growing need for better information, better research, and better funding to address these issues.

We are fortunate that more attention has come in recent years. Several reviews over the last few years highlight abnormal uterine bleeding in persons with bleeding disorders,6,7 bleeding issues for persons who menstruate on anticoagulation,8 and pregnancy in patients with bleeding disorders.9,10 Improvements in therapies are needed, as well as improvement in our understanding of the underlying reasons for uterine bleeding, so that targeted therapies can evolve.

Figure 3 in the article by Langer and O’Brien1 highlight several areas where data deficits exist. Despite the fact that VWD has been officially around for 100 years,11 we have much to learn on both the diagnosis and management side. Gaps also exist for other bleeding disorders, including bleeding disorders of unknown cause.

We also need to normalize discussion around periods, childbirth, and menopause. Multiple groups are hard at work on this task, including the National Bleeding Disorders Foundation (www.bleeding.org), the Foundation for Women and Girls with Bleeding Disorders (www.fwgbd.org), the World Federation of Hemophilia (www.wfh.org), and Let’s Talk Period (letstalkperiod.ca). Hopefully, the study by Langer and O'Brien1 motivates the next generation to do better than their predecessors. Here’s to an era with better management of heavy menstrual bleeding and childbirth.
