ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe incidence of acute pancreatitis is increasing in the Western world. About 10% of cases are caused by hypertriglyceridemia. Plasmapheresis was shown to reduce serum triglyceride (TG) levels, and current apheresis guidelines recommend its use in severe acute hypertriglyceridemia‐induced pancreatitis (HIP). However, data on safety and efficacy are lacking. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical efficacy of plasmapheresis in hypertriglyceridemia‐induced pancreatitis.MethodsThis is a retrospective multicenter cohort study of patients hospitalized for an episode of hypertriglyceridemia‐induced pancreatitis from January 1, 2012 to December 31, 2022. The predefined composite primary endpoint was in‐hospital mortality and organ failure. To reduce allocation bias, we performed propensity score matching.Results245 episodes of hypertriglyceridemia‐induced pancreatitis from 13 German centers were included. Of those, 95 episodes were treated with plasmapheresis. After propensity score matching, the final cohort consisted of 60 well‐balanced pairs. Plasmapheresis was not associated with a difference in the primary composite outcome, in‐hospital mortality, and organ failure (8/60 vs. 5/60; χ 2 (1) = 0.776; p = 0.378), nor was there any difference in the severity of pancreatitis episodes. It showed only a moderate reduction of serum triglyceride compared to the non‐plasmapheresis group, but a significantly longer hospital stay in the plasmapheresis group (12 days; IQR 14 vs. 9 days; IQR 11; U = 1356; Z = −2.46; p = 0.014).ConclusionsPlasmapheresis in patients with hypertriglyceridemia‐induced pancreatitis was not associated with a better clinical outcome compared with conservative treatment in this propensity score‐matched retrospective cohort study. Outside clinical studies, this costly and potentially complicative treatment should be considered with caution.

Introduction

The incidence of acute pancreatitis is steadily increasing throughout North America and Europe [1]. Despite most episodes being mild and self‐limiting, severe acute pancreatitis, which occurs in 10%–20% of cases [2], is correlated with significant mortality and long‐term morbidity [3]. Acute pancreatitis significantly affects individuals' health and imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden, as it remains one of the most common reasons for hospitalization in gastroenterology [1]. While the most common etiologies remain biliary and alcoholic [4], around 10% of cases of acute pancreatitis are caused by hypertriglyceridemia [5], making it the third most common single‐agent cause.

The exact mechanism by which increased serum triglycerides (TG) lead to acute pancreatitis (AP) has not been fully clarified. Both the direct toxicity of free fatty acids on the acinar cells and local ischemia leading to inflammation of the pancreas are discussed in the literature [6]. Epidemiological data show a correlation between serum TG and an increased risk of AP [7].

In clinical practice, different treatment approaches to lower serum TG are currently used in addition to supportive therapy with analgesia and hydration therapy [6] in episodes of acute hypertriglyceridemia‐induced pancreatitis (HIP). In secondary hypertriglyceridemia, treatment of the primary cause, such as alcohol cessation, if possible, is useful to reduce TG levels. Insulin accelerates chylomicron degradation through activation of lipoprotein lipase [8], and a meta‐analysis of three studies reported a shorter length of hospitalization in patients receiving intensive insulin therapy [9]. Apart from that, heparin infusion also reduced triglyceride levels, probably by facilitating the release of stored lipoprotein lipase from endothelial cells [10].

Additionally, plasmapheresis—with either albumin or plasma replacement—has been propagated to be used in severe cases of HIP as it is supposed to rapidly lower TG serum levels [11]. Although evidence on the clinical benefit of plasmapheresis in HIP is sparse and conflicting, it is used in daily clinical routine. A single controlled trial comparing plasmapheresis to the standard of care in Chinese patients showed a faster TG clearance in the plasmapheresis group and a lower rate of local complications and recurrence of HIP [12]. However, a recent meta‐analysis including 15 observational studies reported a higher economic burden and increased mortality in the plasmapheresis group [13]. Since the underlying data are derived from historical cohort studies and do not account for clinical and paraclinical differences between groups, the observed effect on mortality cannot be conclusively linked to the treatment, and relevant bias should be suspected. Three current Chinese single‐center retrospective studies comparing plasmapheresis to the standard of care showed a higher TG‐lowering effect than the control group but no changes in persistent organ failure, local complications, or mortality [14, 15, 16]. A multicenter propensity score analysis from China did not show a clinical benefit from plasmapheresis therapy in patients with HIP but a higher need for intensive care admission [17]. Data from European centers, potentially reflecting a different standard of care or conceivable differences due to ethnicity, are lacking.

Plasmapheresis is not discussed in current American [18] or German pancreatitis guidelines [19]. The American Society of Apheresis guidelines strongly recommend plasmapheresis in acute severe HIP [20], albeit with limited data quality on clinical efficacy underlying this recommendation, as outlined above.

Given the current lack of data supporting the clinical efficacy of plasmapheresis in HIP, we conducted a German multicenter retrospective propensity‐score matched study to investigate the effect of plasmapheresis on the clinical course in patients with HIP with a predefined composite endpoint of organ failure or in‐hospital mortality.

Methods

Ethics Approval

The study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki and all relevant regulations after ethics approval (Ethics vote number 22‐2996‐104).

Inclusion or Exclusion Criteria

In this retrospective multicenter cohort study, we included episodes of HIP in adult patients (≥ 18 years) hospitalized from January 1, 2012, to December 31, 2022, at one of the 13 cooperating centers. AP was defined following the revised Atlanta criteria with two of three features necessary to confirm the diagnosis: (1) characteristic abdominal pain; (2) serum lipase activity at least three times greater than the upper limit of normal; and (3) characteristic findings in radiological imaging [21]. For the diagnosis of HIP, patients had to exhibit serum TG levels of at least 1000 mg/dL (11.3 mmol/L) and HIP had to be diagnosed by the treating physician.

Screening and Data Security

Initial screening was performed decentralizedly based on local ICD‐10 discharge codes (K85.x) and serum TG levels, utilizing a cut‐off value of 1000 mg/dL. All potential episodes were screened by local investigators for inclusion criteria and internal consistency. Data from admission were defined as values from the first blood draw. Regarding TG levels at different time points, a time frame of 8 h around the specified time point (24 h, 48 h, 72 h) was defined. All episodes were included in the final analysis if 90% of all values were extractable. Final statistical analysis was done on the combined pseudonymized data centrally.

Group Allocation and Prespecified Endpoints

Patients with at least one plasmapheresis session were included in the plasmapheresis group, and all other patients were grouped in the non‐plasmapheresis group. Before data extraction, a composite primary endpoint consisting of in‐hospital mortality and organ failure operationalized as a need for vasopressor therapy, intubation, or dialysis was defined. This pragmatic definition was chosen to reduce the risk of misclassification bias. Prespecified secondary endpoints consisted of in‐hospital‐mortality, reduction of TG in the first 24 h, and absolute TG levels at 24, 48, and 72 hours respectively, severity, as defined by the revised Atlanta classification [21], intensive care admission, length of intensive care unit (ICU) stay, and length of hospital stay.

Propensity Score Matching and Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with IBM SPSS for Windows Version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Released 2020, Armonk, NY). Analyses were performed in the whole cohort and after propensity score matching to account for potential confounders, including initial TG, ionized calcium, hematocrit, age, gender, SOFA score [22], and Charlson Comorbidity Index [23]. These variables were selected as they are well‐established prognostic indicators of severe pancreatitis (TG, calcium, hematocrit, age, SOFA score), while balanced demographic data regarding age and gender, as well as comorbidities (Charlson Comorbidity Index), were ensured to exclude them as potential influencing factors. Matching was performed using logistic regression, where the propensity score for each patient was calculated based on the aforementioned variables. The nearest neighbor matching method without replacement, utilizing a caliper of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score, was employed following Austin et al. [24] to pair individuals from the treatment and control groups. Episodes with single missing values in one matched parameter were censored (n = 54). In the matched cohort, TG levels at 24, 48, and 72 h were available in 86, 83, and 79 episodes, respectively. Episodes with missing values were censored for the corresponding time point. Additionally, we performed analysis in patients where data on all three time points were available (n = 62). Continuous variables are described as medians and interquartile ranges throughout the text and the tables. Categorical data in the two groups were compared utilizing Fisher's exact test or the Chi‐squared test, and independent continuous variables with the Mann–Whitney U test, as we did not assume a normal distribution of data. For repeat measures, we utilized the Friedman Test as we did not assume normal distribution or sphericity. All statistical testing was done two‐sided. The statistical significance level was set a priori at p = 0.05.

Results

Two Hundred and Forty‐Five episodes from 13 centers were included in our analysis, including 92 episodes treated with plasmapheresis and 153 treated without. The median number of plasmapheresis procedures performed was 2 (25th and 75th percentile 1–2), and the median time until the first plasmapheresis was 9 h (25th and 75th percentile 2–24 h). No Significant Difference in the Incidence of the Composite Primary Endpoint

The compound primary endpoint, encompassing in‐hospital mortality or organ failure operationalized through a need for vasopressor therapy, dialysis, or intubation, occurred in 13 out of 120 patients (10.8%). No statistically significant difference was seen in patients treated with plasmapheresis compared to our control group (8/60 (13.3%) vs. 5/60 (8.3%); p = 0.56; see Figure 1a). This result did not change when we only compared patients with a SOFA score ≥ 2 (4/13 vs. 4/16; p = 1.00).

Primary endpoint and severity of pancreatitis episode by revised Atlanta classification. (a) No difference was observed in the frequency of the composite primary endpoint of in‐hospital mortality or organ failure in episodes treated without or with plasmapheresis (8.3% vs. 13.3%; p = 0.56). (b) No difference was observed in the severity of episodes as defined by the revised Atlanta classification (no plasmapheresis: mild: 63.3%, moderate: 21.7%; severe: 15.0%; plasmapheresis: mild: 50.0%; moderate: 25.0%; severe: 25.0%; χ 2 (2) = 2.584; p = 0.275); p > 0.05 = n.s.

There Were No Significant Differences in the Components of the Primary Endpoint, Irrespective of Early or Late Initiation of Plasmapheresis or the Severity of Pancreatitis Following the Revised Atlanta Classification

No in‐hospital mortality was observed in our propensity‐matched cohort. Need for intubation occurred in 5/60 patients in the plasmapheresis group and 5/60 patients in the standard of care therapy (χ 2 (1) = 0.000; p = 1.000), hemodialysis therapy was necessary in 1/60 episodes treated with plasmapheresis and 2/60 episodes in the non‐plasmapheresis group, (p = 0.998), and vasopressor support was necessary in 7/60 episodes in the plasmapheresis group and 4/60 in the standard of care group (p = 0.53). When comparing episodes receiving early initiation plasmapheresis with patients without plasmapheresis, there was no statistically significant difference in our primary composite endpoint (5/33 vs. 5/60; p = 0.48) or need for intubation (3/33 vs. 5/60; p = 1.00), dialysis (2/33 vs. 1/60; p = 0.56) or vasopressors (4/33 vs. 4/60, p = 0.45). No difference was observed in the distribution of severity following the revised Atlanta classification (plasmapheresis: mild: 30/60 (50.0%), moderate: 15/60 (25.0%), severe: 15/60 (25.0%); no plasmapheresis: mild: 38/60 (63.3%), moderate: 13/60 (21.7%), severe: 9/60 (15.0%); χ 2 (2) = 2.584, p = 0.275; Figure 1b), albeit a trend toward higher severity in the plasmapheresis group was observed.

Significantly Lower Triglyceride Levels Up to 72 Hours After Admission Were Observed in the Plasmapheresis Group

Next, we investigated the reduction of TG in the first 24 h post‐admission and absolute TG levels at 24, 48, and 72 h. The reduction of TG in the first 24 h in the plasmapheresis group was numerically higher compared to the non‐plasmapheresis group (median 2187 mg/dL, IQR 2476 mg/dL vs. 1643 mg/dL; IQR 2849 mg/dL; U = 640, Z = −1.439, p = 0.150, Cohen's r = −0.159) but this effect did not reach statistical significance.

The absolute TG levels at 24 h (U = 409, Z = −4.502, p < 0.001, Cohen's r = −0.477), 48 h (U = 293, Z = −4,847, p < 0.001, Cohen's r = 0.538) and 72 h (U = 503, Z = −3.083, p = 0.002, Cohen's r = −0.339) post‐admission were lower in the plasmapheresis group with moderate effect sizes exhibited (Figure 2, Table 2). When only comparing patients with data at all three time points (n = 62), the effect did not change substantially (24 h: 1840 mg/dL, IQR 2213 mg/dL vs. 1124 mg/dL, IQR 956 mg/dL; 48 h: 1080 mg/dL, IQR 1061 mg/dL vs. 566.5 mg/dL, IQR 339 mg/dL; 72 h: 817 mg/dL, IQR 801 mg/dL vs. 563 mg/dL, IQR 229 mg/dL; p = 0.001).

Plasmapheresis is correlated with significantly lower serum triglycerides (TG) compared with standard of care. Serum TGs were lower in the plasmapheresis group at 24 h post‐admission (median 818 mg/dL vs. 1840 mg/dL), 48 h (median 539 vs. 1092 mg/dL) post‐admission, and 72 h post‐admission (median 558 mg/dL vs. 826 mg/dL). The magnitude of the effect was moderate (**p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001).

The proportion of patients admitted to the ICU was significantly higher in the plasmapheresis group compared to standard of care (41/60 (60.0%) versus 24/60 (43.4%), χ 2 (1) = 9.701, p = 0.002; Figure 3a). Discussion

The PHIP‐JuGa‐Study presents data from a large, German, multicenter, retrospective, propensity‐score‐matched cohort study investigating the impact of plasmapheresis on episodes of HIP. Our study reports on the hitherto largest and only European propensity score‐matched cohort investigating this significant clinical question. It includes data from 13 centers in Germany, enhancing the generalizability of the findings across diverse healthcare settings and patient populations. With 245 episodes analyzed, this study offers robust data on a relatively rare condition, allowing for meaningful analysis of treatment outcomes.

Our study minimizes allocation bias by employing propensity score matching, leading to a well‐balanced cohort for comparing plasmapheresis with standard care. In our model, we included patient characteristics of age and gender as well as pertinent laboratory variables, specifically initial TG, ionized calcium, hematocrit, and CRP, known risk factors for a severe episode [25]. As a measure of clinical severity at admission, we included the SOFA score [22], and a validated measure of prior medical conditions, the Charlson Comorbidity Index [23]. This approach strengthens the reliability of the observed associations, but due to the stringent matching and episodes with single not extractable values in one of the parameters, a number of episodes were not included in the matched cohort.

We chose a robust and prespecified clinical primary endpoint, namely a composite of death or organ failure operationalized as a need for intubation, vasopressors, or dialysis. Both plasmapheresis and standard of care showed comparable results regarding the composite endpoint and its components. There was a trend toward a higher frequency of non‐mild episodes according to the revised Atlanta classification; however, this difference was not statistically significant. Accordingly, our study did not demonstrate a clinical benefit of plasmapheresis in these clinical endpoints, which aligns with previously published retrospective Chinese studies [14, 15, 16].

ICU admission was significantly more frequent in the plasmapheresis group, likely reflecting the common practice in Germany to perform plasmapheresis in ICU settings rather than indicating a more severe clinical course ‐ particularly as the need for organ replacement therapy did not differ between groups. Similar findings were reported by Cao et al. [17]. The overall length of hospital stay was longer in the plasmapheresis group, whereas the length of ICU stay did not differ significantly. These secondary endpoints point toward a significant economic impact of the use of plasmapheresis, albeit we did not explicitly investigate total treatment costs in this analysis. Each session of plasmapheresis typically costs €2000–4000 in European centers, excluding additional costs from prolonged hospitalization. In contrast, insulin‐plus‐heparin protocols incur minimal added expense. Given the lack of clear clinical benefit, the incremental cost per triglyceride reduction or per quality‐adjusted life‐year gained appears unjustified. These observations highlight the need for formal cost‐effectiveness analyses in future randomized trials to guide both clinical and policy decisions.

Our study found a numerically stronger decrease in serum TG in the first 24 h post‐admission in the plasmapheresis group and a statistically significantly lower TG level after 24 h. These findings persisted at 48 and 72 h. Of note, this treatment modality only demonstrated a moderate overall impact on TG levels. These findings align with a small randomized trial comparing insulin and glucose with plasmapheresis without demonstrating a difference in the approaches [26] and a Japanese study even demonstrating no difference in TG levels after plasmapheresis [27]. Unfortunately, most comparative studies do not report on effect sizes regarding TG lowering, so the true effect size of the utilization of plasmapheresis remains unclear. Despite the pathophysiological rationale suggesting that rapidly lowering TG during an episode of HIP may confer a clinical benefit, there is currently no convincing evidence to support this hypothesis [28]. Lowering of TG levels alone through plasmapheresis or other methods should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of clinical efficacy. Our study and other published results question the assumption that lowering TG is an adequate surrogate marker for treatment success in HIP. Further research is warranted to investigate this question specifically and the clinical impact of TG lowering in HIP in general.

Several limitations should be kept in mind when interpreting results from this study. Firstly, despite a good balance after propensity score matching, imbalances in parameters not extracted or not extractable can never be entirely ruled out in a retrospective study. Therefore, some degree of residual allocation bias is plausible. Secondly, different technical differences in plasmapheresis might impact efficacy, including duration, timing, type (albumin or plasma), the amount of fluid exchanged, and the anticoagulant used. We did not address these questions in our study, as data on most of these parameters are inconsistent [29]. With regard to early initiation, no difference in our predefined primary endpoint or its components was observed when we only compared episodes initiated in the first 24 h with episodes without the use of plasmapheresis. Investigating these factors has considerable merit in the eyes of the authors, but was not feasible as part of this study, giving statistical power in subgroup analysis with a relatively low incidence of our primary endpoint. Further studies are warranted to highlight these aspects. Thirdly, the endpoint chosen in this study cannot rule out other meaningful clinical benefits, such as time to pain cessation or organ failure‐free days. Lastly, temporal trajectories are very important in acute pancreatitis. As we assumed significant misclassification bias, we decided against the extraction of time from symptom onset in our analysis.

In summary, our data indicate that plasmapheresis offers only modest additional triglyceride reduction without improving clinical outcomes in hypertriglyceridemia‐induced pancreatitis, while being associated with a significantly longer hospital stay. Given the absence of demonstrated clinical benefit, its use should be limited to clinical trials. Two large randomized controlled trials are currently addressing this question. The international ELEFANT trial, comparing plasmapheresis, insulin + heparin, and aggressive fluid resuscitation, is expected to complete enrollment by December 2025 [30]. Similarly, the PERFORM‐R trial compares plasmapheresis versus insulin + low‐molecular‐weight heparin [31]. Results from these studies will determine whether the theoretical advantage of rapid triglyceride clearance with plasmapheresis translates into tangible clinical improvements. Until then, the use of plasmapheresis should be approached with considerable caution, given its high cost and substantial implications for resource utilization and length of stay.
