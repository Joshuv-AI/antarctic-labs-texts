High‐Throughput Screening of Cyanobacterial PHB Production Using Fluorescence‐Based Detection
Authors: Artai Lage Julià, Beatriz Altamira‐Algarra, Joan Garcia, Eva Gonzalez‐Flo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThree different cultures of cyanobacteria: Synechocystis sp., Leptolyngbya sp., and a co‐culture of both Synechocystis sp. and Synechococcus sp. were tested for their polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) production potential under diverse conditions. High‐throughput screening in a multi‐well plate, using Nile blue fluorescence to estimate PHB accumulation, enabled simultaneous testing of multiple parameters without complex setups and faster PHB estimation compared to conventional gas chromatography. Fluorescence results indicated that the best conditions for PHB accumulation were nitrogen depletion under darkness at 30°C with acetate supplementation after 4 days of glycogen accumulation. Finally, these optimal conditions were validated in single‐batch proof‐of‐concept photobioreactors (1 L working volume). The scale‐up proved successful, yielding 7% PHB dry cell weight (dcw) for Synechocystis sp. and a promising 13% PHB dcw for Leptolyngbya sp. In addition, scale‐up experiments in 1‐L photobioreactors demonstrated that a dedicated glycogen pre‐accumulation step is unnecessary, as PHB can be efficiently synthesized directly from acetate.

Introduction

The development of new methods to quickly measure intracellular polymers is of utmost relevance, as it could allow responsive adjustments of bioprocesses driven by real‐time data. This is particularly compelling in industrial biotechnology, where monitoring intracellular metabolites can enhance the production of bioplastics, biofuels, and other value‐added materials from microbial cultures. Conventional analyses of intracellular storage compounds usually involve complex and laborious procedures. These often require extraction, centrifugation, and, in many cases, specific storage conditions such as freezing, lyophilisation, or the addition of chemical reagents to enable subsequent processing. Such protocols are frequently time‐consuming, requiring more than 1 day from sample preparation to final results (Villar Sola et al. 2025). Among intracellular polymers, polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) has attracted considerable attention due to its complete biodegradability and physicochemical properties comparable to those of petroleum‐based plastics (Mandragutti et al. 2024). PHB is naturally synthesized by a wide range of prokaryotic microorganisms and represents a promising alternative to conventional plastics in the context of a circular bioeconomy (Radadiya and Pandya 2024). Despite its potential, the industrial deployment of PHB remains limited by production costs and the efficiency of microbial production systems.

Cyanobacteria are particularly attractive candidates for sustainable PHB production because of their photoautotrophic metabolism, which allows them to convert carbon dioxide into biomass and storage polymers using light as an energy source (Yashavanth et al. 2021). This capability offers a clear advantage over heterotrophic bacteria, which require organic carbon sources, complex nutrient media, and costly, intensive aeration to meet high oxygen demands (Ouyang et al. 2018). Unlike heterotrophs, oxygenic cyanobacteria generate their own oxygen, reducing their gas exchange needs primarily to CO2 supply and culture mixing (Yashavanth et al. 2021). However, PHB accumulation in cyanobacteria is generally lower than in heterotrophic systems, with reported stable PHB content typically not exceeding 15% dcw (Yashavanth et al. 2021), compared to values above 80% dcw achieved in some heterotrophic bacteria (Khetkorn et al. 2016). In addition to PHB, cyanobacteria accumulate glycogen as a major intracellular carbon reserve (Ortega‐Martínez et al. 2025). Under specific environmental or metabolic conditions, carbon stored in glycogen can be redirected toward PHB biosynthesis (Cantrell et al. 2023; Koch et al. 2019), although this conversion is often inefficient due to competition between glycogen and PHB synthesis for shared carbon precursors (Forchhammer et al. 2022).

PHB accumulation in cyanobacteria is strongly influenced by environmental and physiological factors, including nutrient availability (Afreen et al. 2021), light–dark regimes, temperature (Kamravamanesh et al. 2017), and the supplementation of metabolic precursors such as acetate (Rueda et al. 2020). Consequently, identifying optimal cultivation conditions is essential to maximize PHB productivity. Nevertheless, comprehensive optimization across multiple strains and environmental conditions remains limited (Singh et al. 2019). This limitation is largely attributable to the constraints imposed by conventional PHB quantification methods, which are laborious, require substantial amounts of biomass, and rely on hazardous solvents such as chloroform or ethylene carbonate for polymer extraction (Aramvash et al. 2017). As a result, experiments are typically conducted at relatively large culture volumes, restricting the number of strains and conditions that can be tested simultaneously (Sánchez‐Bayo et al. 2020).

Fluorescence‐based methods, particularly Nile blue staining, have emerged as promising alternatives for the rapid estimation of PHB content. Nile blue selectively binds to PHB granules and emits fluorescence that correlates with polymer accumulation, enabling both qualitative assessment and microscopic visualization of intracellular PHB (Cao et al. 2022; Rodríguez et al. 2023). While this approach offers clear advantages in terms of speed and simplicity, its quantitative accuracy can be affected by factors such as biomass concentration, low PHB content, and dye accessibility to intracellular granules (Zuriani et al. 2013). Despite these limitations, Nile blue staining provides a valuable foundation for rapid screening approaches. However, this technique has not been widely used in cyanobacteria compared to the many studies performed with heterotrophic bacteria‐based systems (Kettner and Griehl 2020).

To overcome the constraints of conventional analytical workflows and enable systematic exploration of PHB production, high‐throughput screening strategies coupled with fluorescence‐based detection could represent a powerful solution. Such approaches would allow the simultaneous evaluation of a large number of strains and cultivation conditions using minimal culture volumes and reduced resource consumption. This capability is particularly relevant for screening environmentally derived cyanobacterial isolates, which remain underexplored compared to strains obtained from established culture collections (Ansari and Fatma 2016; Thajuddin et al. 2025). In this context, there is growing interest in harnessing cyanobacterial microbiomes to improve process stability. Whether operating as naturally enriched environmental consortia or as artificially assembled co‐cultures, these multi‐population systems often display enhanced robustness against operational and environmental stress (Altamira‐Algarra, Lage, García, and Gonzalez‐Flo 2024). Within these microbiomes, distinct microbial populations can synergistically complement each other's metabolic deficiencies, ultimately leading to more resilient and competent bioprocesses than strict monocultures (Sánchez‐Bayo et al. 2020).

In this study, we developed and applied a high‐throughput fluorescence‐based screening method using Nile blue staining to efficiently evaluate PHB production in cyanobacteria under a wide range of environmental and nutritional conditions. To gain deeper insight into the physiological response of the cultures, selected conditions were transferred to bench‐scale photobioreactors. This allowed for accurate monitoring of the metabolic interplay between carbon sources and PHB synthesis, providing data on glycogen usage and acetate assimilation that extend beyond the capabilities of microplate screening.

Materials and Methods

Strains and Culture Maintenance

The strains tested in this study were previously isolated from environmental samples and were specifically selected based on their demonstrated potential for PHB production during preliminary screenings (Lage et al. 2025). Initially, the experimental design aimed to evaluate three independent pure strains. However, due to the poor growth and operational fragility of Synechococcus sp. AP as a pure monoculture (Lage et al. 2025), we opted to leverage the biological advantages of co‐cultivation, a strategy supported by literature indicating that consortia comprising both Synechocystis sp. and Synechococcus sp. are highly productive and robust (Altamira‐Algarra, Lage, García, and Gonzalez‐Flo 2024). Consequently, three distinct cultivation systems were established: one containing pure Synechocystis sp. AP, another containing pure Leptolyngbya sp. R, and a third defined co‐culture containing both Synechocystis sp. AP and Synechococcus sp. AP in similar proportions (approximately 50% ± 10% for each strain throughout the study) (AP stands for Agricultural Pond and R for River, which indicate the sites from which the original samples were collected).

Cultures were maintained in 1 L Erlenmeyer flasks inside an incubator (Memmert, BioLab) at 30°C, under light: dark cycles of 15:9 h of white light at 185 μmol m−2 s−1, and agitated with a conventional magnetic stirrer at 250 rpm in BG‐11 liquid media (Rueda et al. 2020). Once per week, 300 mL of each culture was removed and replaced by fresh BG‐11 medium. Monitoring of the strains was carried out through microscopic observations (Figure S1).

Photobioreactor Set Up and Operation

The three cultures were scaled up to 3‐L photobioreactors for growth to obtain biomass for subsequent PHB accumulation experiments using the high‐throughput approach (Figure 1A). In selected experiments (Figure 1B), after nutrient depletion, cultures were subjected to an intermediate step aimed at enhancing glycogen synthesis.

Schematic representation of the photobioreactors operation and PHB accumulation. (A) Photobioreactors were operated in batch mode under alternating light–dark cycles. Cultures were grown until nitrogen and phosphorus were depleted (typically after 4 days), after which the culture was purged and replaced with fresh BG‐11 medium to initiate a new growth cycle. Independent photobioreactors were used for each strain, and PHB accumulation assays were performed in multi‐well plates incubated in a plate reader, enabling systematic testing of multiple experimental variables. (B) In selected experiments aimed at enhancing glycogen accumulation, cultures were first grown in photobioreactors under batch conditions until nitrogen and phosphorus depletion. Glycogen accumulation was then induced by supplying inorganic carbon. Following this induction phase, cultures were purged and transferred to multi‐well plates for subsequent PHB accumulation assays.

Photobioreactors consisted of cylindrical vertical columns agitated with a conventional magnetic stirrer. Photobioreactors were placed at 15 cm from 150‐W cool‐white LED lights allowing an illumination of the reactors of 300 μmol m−2 s−1, under light: dark cycles of 15:9 h. pH inside the photobioreactors was maintained between 8 and 8.3 using automated pH control, with CO2 injection when the medium exceeded pH 8.3 (Altamira‐Algarra, Lage, García, and Gonzalez‐Flo 2024). The BG‐11 medium used for growth was modified by adjusting NaNO3, K2HPO4 and NaHCO3 to have the following concentrations: N‐NO3 − = 25 mg L−1, P‐PO4 3− = 3.46 mg L−1 and C‐HCO3 − = 50 mg L−1. Phosphorous concentration was adjusted to attain a N:P ratio of 7.2:1 (molar ratio 16:1) (Zhao et al. 2023).

Photobioreactors operated in semi‐continuous mode; after nitrogen depletion and when biomass reached 800–1200 mg VSS L−1 (Volatile Suspended Solids), which was obtained every 4 days, 1 L of culture was replaced with fresh BG‐11 medium. The purged volume was used for PHB accumulation tests (Figure 1A). In the experiments for glycogen synthesis enhancement (Figure 1B), after nitrogen and phosphorus depletion during the growth phase, photobioreactors were supplemented with inorganic carbon to achieve a concentration of at least 50 mg L−1 C as HCO3 − in the culture medium. The reactors were then operated under the same conditions as previously described for a period of 7 days, after which the biomass was harvested and subjected to PHB accumulation experiments.

Biomass concentration was analysed as VSS following the methodology 2540 E (American Public Health Association 2012). N‐NO3 − was measured from filtered liquid fraction following the methodology in 4500‐N (American Public Health Association 2012).

PHB Accumulation

PHB accumulation tests were performed in a 96‐multiwell plate. Each well was filled with 350 μL of photobioreactors' purge and covered with the plate lid to minimize oxygen exchange with the air, as oxygen presence is detrimental for PHB synthesis (Rueda et al. 2020; Altamira‐Algarra, Lage, García, and Gonzalez‐Flo 2024). Plates were agitated at an orbital frequency of 280 rpm and maintained at 30°C in the dark to prevent oxygen generation via photosynthesis (Yashavanth et al. 2021) for different days using a microplate reader (Synergy HTX, BioTek, Agilent). For the first two experiments, the incubation period was set to 7–8 days. This timeframe was chosen based on previous studies (Altamira‐Algarra, Lage, García, and Gonzalez‐Flo 2024), which demonstrated that extending the accumulation phase beyond this period results in a decrease in PHB production. For the third experiment, the incubation duration was modified based on the kinetic observations from these initial trials. The absence of cross‐contamination between wells during agitation was previously confirmed using dye solutions. The biomass for all tests was adjusted to a 1000 mg VSS L−1 (performing the appropriate dilutions or centrifugations based on the photobioreactors' biomass purge) as a reference biomass concentration used along the whole study. Nutrient Concentration Assay

The purpose of this experiment was to investigate the effect of the presence of nutrients at different concentrations during the accumulation phase in order to assess the necessity of their depletion. The tested conditions included: (1) nitrogen concentration ranging from 100 to 2 mg N‐NO3 − L−1, with no phosphorus; (2) phosphorus concentration ranging from 12.5 to 0.25 mg P‐PO4 3− L−1, with no nitrogen; (3) a combination of varied concentrations of both nitrogen and phosphorus; (4) and a control lacking both nitrogen and phosphorus.

To eliminate any remaining nutrients from the 3‐L photobioreactors, the culture samples were centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. The resulting pellet was resuspended, and nitrogen and/or phosphorus were added in the appropriate amounts from stock solutions of a known concentration. Each condition was tested in triplicate. All plate wells were supplemented with acetate to achieve a concentration of 0.6 g L−1 to enhance PHB production (Altamira‐Algarra, Lage, Meléndez, et al. 2024). On this test, we only evaluated the culture dominated by Synechocystis sp. AP, under the assumption that the fundamental effect of nutrient availability on PHB accumulation is a universal physiological response across cyanobacteria (Afreen et al. 2021). This representative approach allowed us to optimize the high‐throughput screening and avoid unnecessary experimental redundancy. After an incubation period of 7 days, Nile blue fluorescence was measured.

Enhanced Glycogen Accumulation Assay

The purpose of this experiment was to investigate the effect of an intermediate step promoting glycogen accumulation on subsequent PHB production. First, to assess the dynamics of glycogen accumulation, photobioreactors were supplemented with inorganic carbon (to achieve at least 50 mg C‐HCO3 − L−1) following nutrient depletion during the growth phase (Figure 1B), and glycogen concentration was quantified daily.

After establishing the glycogen accumulation profile for the three different cultures, a second experiment was performed only using the Synechocystis sp. AP culture. This culture was maintained under the same glycogen‐accumulating conditions, but in this case the aim was to assess the potential PHB production using the previously stored glycogen.

For PHB accumulation assessment, 350 μL of the daily samples from the Synechocystis sp. AP photobioreactor were incubated with 0.6 g L−1 acetate for 8 days in the multi‐well plate. PHB was quantified every 2 days using the Nile blue fluorescence. For each sampling day, three replicate incubations were prepared, resulting in four incubation periods in total (Figure 2).

Temperature and Acetate Concentration Assay

This test was designed to evaluate the effects of acetate concentration, and temperature. We tested two acetate concentrations (0.6 and 3 g L−1, plus a no‐acetate control) and two temperature conditions (30°C and 40°C). The 0.6 g L−1 acetate level was selected as it approximates the maximum concentration tolerated by certain strains like Synechocystis sp. (Altamira‐Algarra, Lage, García, and Gonzalez‐Flo 2024), while 3 g L−1 was included to test the cultures' capacity to handle higher acetate levels. This experiment was tested on the three different cultures (Synechocystis sp. AP, Leptolyngbya sp. R, and the co‐culture of Synechocystis sp. AP and Synechococcus sp. AP). Biomass used for this test was collected after a previous four‐day glycogen accumulation in the photobioreactors from Figure 1B. The harvested biomass was centrifuged and resuspended in modified BG‐11 medium without nitrogen, phosphorus, or inorganic carbon, supplemented with the corresponding acetate concentration. PHB accumulation was monitored over time by measuring Nile blue fluorescence on day 0, 2, 3 and 4. Three independent replicates were prepared for each combination of culture, temperature, and acetate concentration at each of the four incubation time points.

PHB Fluorescence Quantification

PHB was assessed by measuring the fluorescence emitted upon Nile blue staining, based on the optimized methodology described by (Altamira‐Algarra, Lage, García, and Gonzalez‐Flo 2024). Staining conditions, including dye concentration and incubation time, were previously optimized in that study and adopted here. Briefly, 100 μL from the incubation multi‐well plate where PHB accumulation was taking place were transferred into a new multi‐well plate for its punctual fluorescence evaluation. Those transferred 100 μL were incubated with 25 μL 0.01 (w/v) Nile blue solution dissolved in Milli‐Q water, resulting in a final dye concentration of 20 μg mL−1 in the assay wells. The plate was incubated in the dark for 45 min. Fluorescence (excitation: 485 nm; emission: 590 nm) and optical density (OD) at 750 nm were measured using the Gen5 3.14 Microplate Reader and Image Software. To isolate the PHB‐specific fluorescence, measurements were performed both before (F0) to account for the intrinsic fluorescence of the sample's pigments, and after Nile blue incubation (FNB). A parallel blank control consisting of 100 μL of Milli‐Q water was also measured before (B0) and after (BNB) the addition of Nile blue to eliminate the background noise of the liquid and the dye itself (which was typically undetectable). The corrected PHB‐specific fluorescence and biomass‐normalized FPHB was calculated according to Equation (1), where OD750 is the optical density of the respective well: (1) FPHB=FNB−BNB−F0−B0OD750

PHB Production in Bioreactors

The optimal conditions for each culture, identified at the previously described high‐throughput screening assays, were tested at a larger scale in 3‐L photobioreactors (1 per strain) for growth and glycogen accumulation, and two 1‐L reactors per culture for PHB accumulation. Cultures were first grown in BG‐11 as described in Section 2.2. Following nitrogen depletion, an intermediate glycogen accumulation period was implemented for 4 days (as in Figure 1B). For PHB accumulation, reactors were operated over 1 week under dark conditions, with one reactor receiving acetate supplementation (Synechocystis sp. AP = 0.6 g L−1, Leptolyngbya sp. R = 3 g L−1 and co‐culture of Synechocystis sp. AP and Synechococcus sp. AP = 3 g L−1) and the other maintained without acetate. Samples were taken daily from all reactors to measure biomass concentration (as VSS), glycogen and PHB content, while acetate consumption was additionally analysed only in the acetate‐supplemented reactors. PHB analysis was performed using both Nile blue staining and conventional quantification methods (Section 2.6.1), for the later correlation of both methodologies.

Polymer Quantification With Conventional Methods

For PHB and glycogen analysis, 50‐mL samples were taken and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 4.5 min. The cell pellet was frozen first at −20°C for 24 h and 1 h to −80°C. The frozen samples were then freeze‐dried for 24 h (−110°C, 0.05 hPa).

PHB Quantification

For PHB extraction, the methodology described by (Lanham et al. 2013) was followed. Briefly, approximately 3.5 mg of lyophilized biomass was mixed with 1‐mL CH3OH with H2SO4 (20% v/v) and 1imL CHCl3 containing 0.05% w/w benzoic acid. The samples were then digested for 5 h at 100°C in a dry‐heat thermo‐block (Selecta). After digestion, samples were cooled on ice for 30 min. Thereafter, 1 mL of distilled water was added and mixed for 1 min until separating solvents by density. The CHCl3 phase was recovered and introduced into a chromatography vial with molecular sieves. Gas chromatography analysis (7820 A, Agilent Technologies) was performed using a DB‐WAX 125–7062 column. Helium served as the gas carrier (4.5 mL min−1), with an injector split ratio of 5:1 and a temperature of 230°C. The flame ionization detector (FID) temperature was set to 300°C. A standard curve of the co‐polymer poly(3‐hydroxybutyrate‐co‐3‐hydroxyvalerate) (P[3HB‐co‐3HV], 88:12 wt, Sigma Aldrich) was used for PHB quantification.

Glycogen Quantification

Glycogen analysis was done following the method described by (Altamira‐Algarra et al. 2025). In brief, glycogen was extracted from 2 mg of lyophilized biomass by hydrolysis with 2 mL of 0.9 M HCl during 3 h at 100°C. After digestion, samples were filtered through 0.45‐μm membrane filters. The filtrate was diluted to 10 mL with Milli‐Q water and analysed by ion chromatography (IC; Metrohm 930 Flex). Glycogen content was quantified based on the released glucose monomers resulting from acid hydrolysis.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the JMP software. Results from the ‘Nutrients concentration’ test were compared using the post hoc Tukey HSD. For the multivariable test of acetate and temperature effect, the experimental data were analysed using a comprehensive multifactorial analysis of variance (ANOVA) approach to account for the complex experimental design and to test both main effects and interaction terms. The practical significance of significant effects was quantified using partial eta‐squared (η 2) for ANOVA effects and Cohen's d for pairwise comparisons. Effects were interpreted as small (η 2 < 0.01, d < 0.2), medium (η 2 = 0.01–0.06, d = 0.2–0.8), or large (η 2 > 0.06, d > 0.8) based on conventional benchmarks.

Results and Discussion

We investigated the potential of three cyanobacterial cultures isolated from environmental samples for PHB production through a two‐phase process consisting of a biomass growth phase followed by a biopolymer accumulation phase. PHB accumulation was induced by cultivating the cultures under nutrient‐depleted, dark, and anoxic conditions with acetate supplementation for 7 days, conditions previously reported to enhance PHB production in cyanobacteria (Altamira‐Algarra, Lage, García, and Gonzalez‐Flo 2024). The use of Nile blue staining combined with fluorescence measurements allows for rapid, high‐throughput screening of PHB accumulation dynamics.

Biomass Growth and Nutrient Consumption Optimization

The growth phase was optimized in 3‐L photobioreactors to achieve complete nutrient consumption with a minimum cultivation time, reaching a target biomass of 800–1000 mgVSS L−1 for the subsequent PHB accumulation. This initial optimization was performed with the co‐culture dominated by Synechocystis sp. AP and Synechococcus sp. AP, starting at 30 mg L−1 nitrogen and 4.15 mg L−1 phosphorus (Figure S2a). Later, the culture conditions were adjusted as described in Section 2.2. These nutrient conditions were subsequently applied to the cultures of Synechocystis sp. AP (Figure S2b) and Leptolyngbya sp. R (Figure S2c). Table 2 shows the values of the kinetic parameters of the three cultures as well as data from other previous studies for comparison. Synechocystis sp. AP exhibited higher growth rate, biomass production and nitrogen consumption rate. All three cultures achieved values of kinetic parameters that surpassed those reported for analogous systems in the literature (Rueda et al. 2020).

PHB Accumulation Tests

Biomass collected from the growth phase was used to evaluate multiple conditions for PHB induction (Table 1). The initial sections of this study present high‐throughput microplate screenings, where PHB accumulation was estimated indirectly via Nile blue fluorescence to rapidly identify preliminary trends and optimal parameters. The definitive validation of these selected conditions was subsequently addressed in larger‐scale bioreactor experiments, where robust and direct chromatographic quantification of both glycogen and PHB was performed.

Nutrient Concentration Effect

The effect of nutrient concentration on PHB accumulation was evaluated in the Synechocystis sp. AP culture, assuming that nutrient responses are broadly comparable among cyanobacteria and that nutrient depletion universally enhances PHB‐related gene expression (Afreen et al. 2021; Santin et al. 2024). This test was done to check the effect of nitrogen alone, phosphorus alone, and the combination of nitrogen and phosphorus. The results were obtained after 1‐week incubation (Figure 3). Among the nitrogen concentrations tested, only low nitrogen concentrations, such as 2 mg N L−1, showed no significant difference in PHB accumulation in contrast to the control with no nitrogen addition (p = 0.7499, Post hoc Tukey HSD test), while the rest of the conditions differ significantly. Phosphorous concentration had no detectable effect on PHB accumulation, with no significant differences compared to the condition of depletion. These results stand in contrast with previous reports indicating that phosphorus depletion triggers PHB synthesis (Afreen et al. 2021). In our study, the treatments supplemented with both nitrogen and phosphorus were not significantly different from the results obtained using nitrogen alone. This similarity might be attributed to the experimental duration. Krasaesueb et al. (2021) demonstrated that the effect of phosphorus in the presence of nitrogen only becomes distinguishable after 8 days of incubation. Since our experiment was limited to 1 week, it is suggested that a longer incubation period would be required to observe the specific impact of phosphorus on PHB synthesis. Overall, the results of this assay indicate that in the short term, nitrogen is the primary nutrient driving PHB accumulation (Troschl et al. 2017).

Glycogen Accumulation Effect

In this assay, after nitrogen depletion during the growth phase, favourable glycogen accumulation conditions were established by supplementing inorganic carbon to at least have 50 mg C‐HCO3 − L−1 in the reactor, following the same day and night light regime as in the growth period (15:9 h). These glycogen accumulation conditions were applied to all three different cultures tested in this study.

The three cultures exhibited a comparable profile, characterized by an increase during the early glycogen accumulation phase and a maximum on day four, followed by a slight decline (Figure 4). While these initial glycogen measurements provide valuable insights, they were based on a single replicate (n = 1) to allow for rapid screening, and therefore represent preliminary trends. This pattern is consistent with observations in literature, where the glycogen peak has been linked to the exhaustion of available inorganic carbon (Kettner et al. 2022; Rueda et al. 2022). Considering that the initial inorganic carbon concentration in this study (50 mg C‐HCO3 − L−1) was lower than in the cited works (Kettner et al. 2022; Rueda et al. 2022) (227 and 2000 mg C L−1, respectively), the observed timing of the peak suggests a likely earlier limitation of carbon. Consistent with previous reports (Troschl et al. 2017; Kettner et al. 2022), Synechocystis sp. accumulated greater glycogen contents than Leptolyngbya sp. contents; however, the maximum value reached in this study (21% of dry cell weight [dcw]) fell considerably short of ~60% dcw achieved under nutrient limitation combined with high inorganic carbon (2000 mg C‐HCO3 − L−1) (De Porcellinis et al. 2017). The lower carbon concentration used here likely accounts for this difference, implying higher inorganic carbon inputs may be required to maximize glycogen accumulation (Rueda et al. 2022).

Evolution of glycogen content for the three cultures during 1 week after the supplementation of inorganic carbon. Results expressed in percentage of glycogen respect to dry cell weight (n = 1).

To evaluate the conversion of glycogen into PHB, daily samples were taken from the glycogen‐accumulating reactors and incubated for PHB accumulation. Since previous analyses demonstrated that the glycogen accumulation profile was highly similar across the three evaluated cultures, this subsequent conversion test was exclusively performed with Synechocystis sp. AP to avoid experimental redundancy. Figure 5 shows that, regardless of the initial glycogen content, determined by the day on which samples were extracted from the photobioreactor, a PHB fluorescence peak occurred always between the fourth and sixth day of accumulation (Figure 5A–E). Notably, fluorescence intensity increased progressively with each sampling day, reaching maximum values in cultures subjected to longer glycogen accumulation periods (Figure 5E). This result suggests that part of this PHB production may come from the internal glycogen storage, as previously reported by (Rueda Hernández and García 2021). Nevertheless, as these microplate assays (n = 1 for glycogen, indirect fluorescence for PHB) serve as a preliminary screening, definitive conclusions regarding the link between glycogen and PHB are derived from the subsequent bioreactor‐scale validation.

The sample with 7 days of glycogen accumulation showed lower PHB fluorescence than expected (Figure 5F), despite maintaining a glycogen content of 15% dcw. This value was not significantly different from the 17% dcw peak observed on day 4 (Figure 4). However, the samples from day 4 performed distinguishably better in terms of PHB accumulation. This reduced fluorescence may reflect the cumulative impact of extended nutrient stress followed by an extra week for PHB accumulation, compared to other cultures that were exposed to such conditions for less time. Prolonged stress under these conditions may have led to reduced fluorescence due to cellular death or PHB degradation (Martínez et al. 2023).

Acetate and Temperature Combination Effect

Two acetate concentrations (0.6 and 3 g L−1), as well as a control without acetate addition, were tested in each culture to evaluate strain‐dependent effects on PHB accumulation. The different acetate concentrations were studied at both 30°C and 40°C. Furthermore, based on the kinetic profiles observed in the previous experiments, where the maximum PHB accumulation peak was consistently reached around the fourth day across all conditions, the incubation timeframe for this assay was reduced to exactly 4 days.

Fluorescence detection profile varied among cultures (Figure 6). In most cases (for the accumulations performed in Leptolyngbya sp. R and the co‐culture), higher acetate concentrations clearly corresponded to increased fluorescence (representative fluorescence images illustrating these patterns are shown in Figure S3). On the other hand, Synechocystis sp. AP culture had maximum fluorescent values when 0.6 g L−1 of acetate was used (Figure 6A,B). This strain‐specific response to acetate is supported by the highly significant Strain × Acetate interaction observed in the statistical analysis, indicating that acetate concentration affects each strain differently. Notably, the absence of acetate resulted in lower fluorescence in all cultures, confirming that no external carbon substrate was available to stimulate PHB synthesis (Troschl et al. 2017).

Fluorescence was measured under three acetate concentrations (0.6, 3 g L−1 and a control without acetate supplementation) and two temperatures (30°C and 40°C). Fluorescence at 40°C was consistently lower than at 30°C for all strains, indicating that elevated temperatures may hinder PHB synthesis (Kamravamanesh et al. 2017). These trends align with previous reports showing PHB production peaks around 30°C and declines at higher temperatures (Ansari and Fatma 2016). However, the statistical analysis revealed a significant Strain × Temperature interaction, indicating that the impact of temperature is strain‐dependent, with each strain displaying different levels of sensitivity and response intensity.

Regarding Synechocystis sp. AP, it exhibited its maximum fluorescence at 0.6 g L−1 acetate and 30°C, although remaining much lower than in other cultures. Increasing acetate concentration to 3 g L−1 did not enhance PHB accumulation (Figure 6A,B), suggesting that high acetate concentrations may inhibit PHB synthesis in this strain. This phenomenon was also documented in our previous work (Lage et al. 2025), where Synechocystis sp. monocultures showed a PHB production decrease after adding an acetate supplementation higher than 0.6 g L−1. Statistical analysis showed moderate temperature preference (p = 0.046, 32.9% improvement at 30°C than at 40°C). Note that the lower PHB fluorescence observed in this acetate/temperature experiment compared to the other experiments (Section 3.2.1 Nutrient concentration effect and Section 3.2.2 Glycogen accumulation effect) was likely related to the shorter incubation period (4 days versus 7–8 days).

The co‐culture of Synechocystis sp. AP and Synechococcus sp. AP showed a distinct behaviour (Figure 6C,D). At 30°C and with 0.6 g L−1 acetate, fluorescence reached its maximum on day 2 and declined thereafter, whereas with 3 g L−1 acetate, PHB fluorescence increased over time and reached significantly higher values than in any other condition (Figure 6C). The co‐culture demonstrated the highest temperature sensitivity among all strains (p = 0.002, Cohen's d = 0.892) with 70% improvement at 30°C compared to 40°C, explaining the significant Strain × Temperature interaction observed in the overall analysis. At 40°C, the trend was similar, but fluorescence values were markedly lower (Figure 6D), in line with the observation that elevated temperatures hinder PHB synthesis.

Finally, Leptolyngbya sp. R showed a gradual increase in PHB fluorescence at 30°C from the initial day to the 4th day with 0.6 g L−1 acetate (Figure 6E), and slightly higher values at 3 g L−1. At 40°C, while fluorescence was observed in samples from both 0.6 and 3 g L−1 acetate, only the latter reached high intensities similar to the levels obtained at 30°C (Figure 6F). Strain analysis indicated moderate temperature sensitivity (p = 0.039, Cohen's d = 0.401), with 37% higher performance at 30°C than at 40°C.

Regarding the specific performance of Synechocystis sp. AP, despite its presence in both the monoculture and the co‐culture, the fluorescence profiles were clearly distinct. Such differences may result from a synergistic interaction between Synechococcus sp. AP and Synechocystis sp. AP, leading to enhanced PHB accumulation under co‐culture conditions (Mariotto et al. 2023). Crucially, these results must be attributed to the interaction between the two strains rather than the sole contribution of Synechococcus sp. AP, as previous evaluations have demonstrated that this strain is highly unproductive when cultivated in strict isolation (Lage et al. 2025). Furthermore, while the current results suggest that the Synechocystis sp. AP monoculture is also relatively unproductive on its own, prior studies have shown that the synergy between Synechocystis sp. and Synechococcus sp. within resilient microbiomes generates exceptionally high productivities (Altamira‐Algarra, Lage, García, and Gonzalez‐Flo 2024). This powerful synergistic dynamic was clearly reflected in the elevated fluorescence detected for the co‐culture in Figure 6C. This interpretation was also supported by the significant Strain × Temperature × Acetate interaction, showing that each strain responds differently depending on the specific combination of temperature and acetate concentration. Overall, statistical analysis revealed significant main effects for all four factors: strain type, acetate concentration, temperature and accumulation time.

PHB Production in Bioreactors

Optimal PHB accumulation conditions identified for each culture were scaled up in 3‐L bioreactors (1 per strain). Cultures underwent a first 4‐day growth phase followed by a 4‐day glycogen accumulation phase under nutrient deprivation supplemented with 50 mg L−1 inorganic carbon. Subsequently, PHB accumulation was induced in two separate 1‐L photobioreactors per strain: one receiving acetate to stimulate PHB synthesis, and a second maintained without acetate to evaluate baseline PHB levels just from glycogen storage. In this experiment, PHB values were estimated using Nile blue fluorescence and verified by gas chromatography. Results indicate that in the bioreactors deprived of acetate, glycogen content slightly increased during the 4‐day glycogen accumulation period (Figure 7C,E), except for the Synechocystis sp. AP culture (Figure 7A). However, during the subsequent PHB accumulation phase, PHB production remained low and did not increase over time. This observation was consistent with previous reports showing that glycogen can be mobilized in the dark to support cellular maintenance metabolism in cyanobacteria, rather than serving as a direct precursor for PHB accumulation under acetate‐limited conditions (Doello et al. 2018). Given that the conversion of glycogen into PHB can be inefficient since both polymers compete for the same carbon precursors (Forchhammer et al. 2022), external carbon sources such as acetate are often supplemented to boost biopolymer yields (Rueda et al. 2020). Although relying on a single biological replicate (n = 1) limits the statistical robustness of individual curves, the lack of glycogen contribution to PHB synthesis is reinforced by the consistent observation of this trend across all six experimental scenarios (three cultures, with and without acetate). Furthermore, mobilizing glycogen toward PHB typically requires much higher intracellular pools than those achieved here (Koch et al. 2019). However, our dark‐anoxic setup may influence glycogen metabolism differently than conventional photoautotrophic systems (Ortega‐Martínez et al. 2025), which could explain why we do not observe a substantial contribution under our conditions.

For Synechocystis sp. AP supplemented with acetate (0.6 g L−1), biomass concentration and glycogen content remained relatively stable (Figure 7B), while acetate was being consumed. PHB accumulation clearly responded to acetate addition, reaching a maximum content of 8% dcw PHB on the fourth day of accumulation.

Regarding Leptolyngbya sp. R supplemented with 3 g L−1 acetate (Figure 7D), results were consistent with the high‐throughput analysis and reached a maximum PHB content of 13% dcw on day seven. Although this yield is higher than the typical basal production (~2% dcw) reported for Leptolyngbya sp. (Rueda et al. 2020), it remains lower than other high‐yield productions (~20% dcw) obtained when supplementing acetate after nitrogen depletion (Kettner et al. 2022). However, acetate levels in our culture only decreased moderately, reaching a final concentration of 1.71 g L−1. This persistence of residual substrate indicates the culture had not yet reached its production ceiling. This contrasts with the Synechocystis sp. AP culture (Figure 7B), which fully depleted the substrate within a week, resulting in a distinct production peak. The importance of total substrate consumption is further highlighted by Kettner et al. (2022), who obtained significantly higher PHB yields (~30% dcw) specifically upon the complete consumption of 2 g L−1 acetate. Since our culture only metabolized 1.29 g L−1 by day seven, prolonging the cultivation period could likely narrow this yield gap.

Furthermore, a distinct metabolic behaviour was observed regarding carbon reserves in Leptolyngbya sp. R. Unlike Kettner et al. (2022), where glycogen consumption fueled PHB production, our results show stable glycogen levels, implying that synthesis was driven exclusively by exogenous acetate (Figure 7D). Intriguingly, this strain exhibited notable glycogen consumption, yet this catabolic activity appeared futile, translating into neither PHB synthesis nor biomass growth (Figure 7C). This general inefficiency in mobilizing glycogen aligns with previous observations in Lenployngbia sp. (Lage et al. 2025), which appears unable to utilize carbon storage for heterotrophic growth or PHB production, a trait that contrasts with other strains capable of rapid glycogen turnover via the LBWT_21500 gene (Hida et al. 2025).

The co‐culture of Synechocystis sp. AP and Synechococcus sp. AP, supplemented with 3 g L−1 of acetate, exhibited the lowest PHB (1% dcw, Figure 7F) and a correspondingly low fluorescence signal, despite exhibiting the highest PHB fluorescence levels in the uncontaminated acetate and temperature effect tests (Figure 6). These low PHB and fluorescence values likely resulted from eukaryotic microalgae contamination during the bioreactor run, which are unable to produce PHB (Figure S4) (Altamira‐Algarra, Lage, García, and Gonzalez‐Flo 2024), thereby substantially reducing the PHB percentage relative to total biomass. Although PHB productivity was low, acetate was consumed, suggesting that cyanobacteria likely used some for PHB synthesis while eukaryotic algae diverted a substantial portion toward other metabolic pathways, such as lipid production (Gong et al. 2024).

Overall, despite the optimization strategies employed, the maximum PHB accumulation obtained for a culture containing Synechocystis sp. AP in this study (8% dcw) represents a modest improvement over values typically reported for similar systems under simple nutrient depletion (~6% dcw; Kovalcik et al. 2017; Saratale et al. 2024). While higher yields (up to 20% dcw) have been achieved in this genus through genetic engineering of the Entner–Doudoroff pathway (Koch et al. 2019). Comparable concentrations (~22% dcw) have also been reported in non‐engineered microbiomes formed by Synechocystis sp. and Synechococcus sp. (Altamira‐Algarra, Lage, Meléndez, et al. 2024). However, a critical distinction lies in the culture history: whereas the high yields reported by Altamira‐Algarra, Lage, Meléndez, et al. (2024) were achieved after subjecting the community to multiple rounds of selection, the results presented here reflect the baseline performance of a non‐adapted culture. Therefore, our findings represent a preliminary stage, suggesting that applying similar adaptive evolutionary pressure to these optimized conditions could significantly enhance production toward those higher theoretical limits.

Validation of Nile Blue Fluorescence for PHB Quantification

During the 3 L bioreactor experiments, PHB accumulation was quantified by both chromatographic analysis and Nile blue fluorescence, enabling direct comparison between fluorescence intensity and PHB content. Initial analysis of 30 samples showed a weak correlation (R 2 = 0.64), primarily due to the inherent biological variability of the cultures and the optical nature of the rapid assay, which can impact the correlation in smaller datasets. However, extending the analysis to include parallel studies with the same cultures under similar conditions (see Table S1) yielded a robust correlation (R 2 = 0.82; Figure 8). This demonstrates that Nile blue provides a reasonably accurate estimate when the correlation model is built using a sufficiently large dataset of independent samples (total sample size n > 80 data points). Furthermore, as observed in Figure 8, the assay demonstrates higher accuracy at lower PHB concentrations. As intracellular PHB increases above a threshold of approximately 10% dcw, the dispersion of fluorescence values also increases. Consequently, the method acts as a reliable quantitative estimator at lower concentrations, but transitions into a valuable qualitative indicator at higher accumulation levels. Its primary utility lies in rapidly discriminating between low‐ and high‐accumulating candidates, rather than determining exact high‐end values. This reliability contrasts with the performance of Nile red, a fluorophore widely employed in cyanobacterial research (Hirai et al. 2019), which often suffers from spectral limitations. For instance, Kettner et al. (2022) failed to establish a valid correlation (R 2 = 0.34) using Nile red due to the significant overlap between its emission peak (610 nm) and phycocyanin autofluorescence (~640 nm). In contrast, the Nile blue emission monitored in our study (590 nm) ensures greater spectral separation, mirroring the success of LipidGreen2 (R 2 > 0.94; Kettner et al. 2022). To definitively rule out any residual interference, our protocol further incorporated a specific background subtraction step (see Section 2.4 PHB fluorescence quantification); a rigorous control often omitted in other studies.

Correlation between PHB values measured with Gas Chromatography with respect to fluorescence due to Nile blue PHB staining. Data collected from the test described in Section 3.3 and from parallel tests (See Table S1) performed with the same cultures. R 2 = 0.823.

Beyond its analytical accuracy, the implementation of Nile blue proved essential for the experimental design. Its non‐destructive, solvent‐free nature (Villar Sola et al. 2025; Aramvash et al. 2017) allowed for the continuous assessment of 124 experimental conditions using only 140 mL of culture. Such high‐throughput screening would have been unfeasible with conventional gas chromatography, which requires toxic solvents and biomass amounts exceeding those available in these micro‐volume setups. Collectively, these findings position Nile blue as a robust, superior alternative to the spectrally problematic Nile red for future high‐throughput PHB monitoring in cyanobacteria.

Conclusions

This study successfully identified nitrogen depletion, darkness, a temperature of 30°C, and acetate supplementation as the optimal conditions for PHB accumulation in cyanobacteria. While the high‐throughput fluorescence method proved to be a robust tool for screening these conditions, the subsequent scale‐up to reactor level provided key metabolic insights. Notably, scale‐up experiments demonstrated that a specific glycogen pre‐accumulation step is unnecessary. This is attributed to the observation that the metabolic turnover from intracellular glycogen to PHB is not a direct or efficient pathway in these strains, with production being driven primarily by exogenous acetate uptake. This finding significantly simplifies the operational strategy, proving that exposing biomass to nitrogen depletion and darkness at 30°C with acetate feeding is sufficient for efficient PHB production. Furthermore, this work validates Nile blue staining as a superior, non‐destructive tool for high‐throughput polymer estimation. Rather than providing exact absolute quantification at high intracellular levels, its true potential lies in rapidly discriminating between low‐ and high‐accumulating candidates. Its ability to mitigate spectral interference makes it a reliable alternative to conventional dyes like Nile red, paving the way for more accurate and efficient monitoring in future cyanobacterial bioprocesses.
