Loneliness in older adults: an assessment on social network usage, social support, and active aging
Authors: Mükerrem Kabataş Yıldız, Ayşe Çal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to examine the relationship between social network usage, active aging, perceived social support, and loneliness among community-dwelling older adults aged 60 years and above who live in a city center in Türkiye and use social networks.MethodsThe data for this descriptive study were collected between November 2022 and May 2023. The sample consisted of 319 individuals aged 60 in Türkiye. Data were gathered through face-to-face interviews using the Demographic Information Form, Loneliness Scale for the Elderly, Scale for the Social Network Usage Purposes of Older Adults, Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support, and Active Aging Scale. Descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation analysis, and multiple linear regression analysis were used to analyze the data.ResultsThe perception of loneliness in older adults was 7.85 ± 4.94; the mean score for active aging-activity dimension was 33.65 ± 12.56; social network usage was 75.46 ± 20.03, and the perceived social support was 24.06 ± 4.42. A moderate relationship was found between loneliness in old age and perceived social support (r=-0.501), and a weak negative relationship was found between loneliness in old age and the active aging-activity dimension (r = 0.155). Additionally, the communication and collaboration sub-dimensions of social network usage were found to be associated with loneliness. Social support and social network usage predict loneliness (26%). Social support and social network usage explain 50% and 11% of the variance in loneliness, respectively.ConclusionsPerceived social support and social network usage play a determinant role in the perception of loneliness in older adults.

Introduction

In the digitizing world, the internet is gradually becoming integrated into every aspect of the daily life of older adults and is expected to play an increasingly important role [1]. Recent studies highlight the prevalence of social network and media usage in addition to traditional behavioral patterns associated with aging [2]. According to Turkish Statistical Institute (TURKSTAT) data, the percentage of individuals aged 65–74 using the internet increased from 11.3% in 2017 to 36.6% in 2022 [3].

In the old age phase of life, factors such as the loss of spouse and friends, deteriorating health, restricted mobility, and the reduction of economic income with retirement tend to increase older adults’ susceptibility to social isolation and loneliness [4]. It has been observed that as older adults experience a more intense feeling of loneliness with age, their perceived level of social support decreases. The need for friendship and social support in older adults can also contribute to increased rates of depression, cognitive decline, morbidity, and mortality [2]. Loneliness can lead to a decline in social and cognitive functions and, consequently, a decrease in life satisfaction [4, 5]. The impact of loneliness on older adults’ mental health and its strong association with depression have been emphasized [6–8]. The absence of social connections and meaningful relationships in older adults can lead to feelings of emptiness, anxiety, and sadness [9]. Loneliness adversely affects the physical health of older adults and is associated with an increased risk of mortality [10, 11]. Recognizing and addressing loneliness in older adults is crucial for preserving and improving older adults health due to all these negative effects of loneliness.

In the literature, it is noted that the use of social networks can assist older adults in maintaining communication with their existing friends and increase interpersonal communication frequency [1, 12]. The use of social networks can enable them to actively participate in life. Furthermore, it is anticipated that social network usage can play a significant role in increasing the life satisfaction of older adults by mitigating the decline associated with aging. Learning and using new technologies are crucial for their integration into society, which is a factor related to active aging. For older adults who experience loneliness, have limited social support, and find it challenging to engage in activities, social network usage can serve as an alternative pastime. Social network usage provides opportunities for both socialization and leading a more productive, quality, and enjoyable life [13]. In a limited number of studies examining the relationship between social network usage and loneliness in older adults, it is expressed that loneliness adversely affects older adults health [2, 5, 14].

In the study, social network usage refers to older adults’ engagement with online social networking platforms to communicate, share content, and collaborate with others [12]. Perceived social support is defined as the extent to which individuals believe that they receive emotional, informational, and practical support from their social environment [2]. Active aging is conceptualized as continued participation in social, physical, and productive activities that support well-being and functional independence in later life [13]. Loneliness is defined as a subjective and distressing experience arising from a perceived discrepancy between desired and actual social relationships [10].

In parallel with the advancement of technology, understanding how older adults use social networks has become increasingly important in the context of aging and well-being. Although loneliness in older adults has been widely studied in relation to social network use, perceived social support, and active aging, these factors have largely been examined separately. Moreover, prior research has predominantly focused on general or frequency-based measures of social network use, providing limited insight into the role of purpose-specific digital engagement [2, 12]. To address this gap, the present study adopts an integrative approach by examining social network usage purposes, perceived social support, and the activity dimension of active aging simultaneously in relation to loneliness among older adults. In this study, loneliness was conceptualized as the primary outcome variable, as it represents a key psychosocial indicator of well-being in later life and is strongly associated with adverse mental, physical, and social health outcomes among older adults. Accordingly, social network usage, perceived social support, and active aging were examined as potential antecedent and modifiable factors influencing loneliness. The purpose of this research is to determine the impact of social network usage, active aging, and social support on loneliness in individuals aged 60 and above.

Methods

Study design

The research with a descriptive cross-sectional design was conducted between November 2022 and May 2023.

Population and sample of the study

The target population of this study consisted of community-dwelling older adults aged 60 years and above who use social networks and reside in a specific city center in the northern region of Türkiye. The sample size for the research was determined using the G*Power (v3.1.9.4) program. Based on the correlation coefficient (r = 0.287) between loneliness and social networks reported in the study by Kim et al., (2021) the power analysis indicated that a minimum of 147 volunteers would be required at a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method from individuals aged 60 years and older who applied to family health centers. Individuals who met the inclusion criteria were informed about the study, and those who agreed to participate were included in the research. The sample of the study consisted of 319 individuals aged 60 and above, resided in the city center where the research was conducted, used social networks, were not diagnosed with a severe neurological or psychiatric illness, and volunteered to participate in the research. This study was conducted with a sample of community-dwelling older adults living in a specific city center in Türkiye and attending family health centers. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted in relation to older adults who live in the community and use social networks, rather than being generalized to the entire older population in Türkiye.

Data collection tools

The research utilized the Demographic Information Form, Loneliness Scale for the Older Adults, Scale for the Social Network Usage Purposes of Older Adults, Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support, and Active Aging Scale - Activity Dimension to collect data.

Demographic information form

The form was developed by the researchers drawing upon the existing literature [1, 2]. This questionnaire consists of 11 questions and is structured to gather sociodemographic information from participants, encompassing details such as age, gender, marital status, etc. Additionally, it delves into specific aspects of their social network usage, encompassing the devices employed to access these networks, the purpose behind their engagement, duration, frequency, and related details.

Loneliness scale for the older adults

The scale was developed and revised by van Tilburg and de Jong Gierveld (1999) to assess the level of loneliness in older adults. The Turkish adaptation of the scale was conducted by Akgül and Yeşilyaprak (2015) [15, 16]. The scale consists of a total of 11 items under two subscales: “emotional loneliness” and “social loneliness.” Six items of the scale (2, 3, 5, 6, 9, 10) measure emotional loneliness and are phrased negatively, while five items (1, 4, 7, 8, 11) measure social loneliness and are phrased positively. The lowest score that can be obtained from the scale is 0, and the highest is 22, with higher scores indicating a higher level of loneliness. The internal consistency coefficient of the scale is reported as 0.97, and in this study, the Cronbach’s alpha value was determined to be 0.84 [16].

Scale for the social network usage purposes of older adults

The scale was developed by Kalınkara and Sarı (2019) to identify the purposes of social network usage among individuals aged 60 and above.2 It comprises a total of 26 items, utilizing a five-point Likert scale, and is organized into three subscales: “content sharing,” “communication,” and “collaboration.” Scores on the scale can range from a minimum of 26 to a maximum of 130. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale is determined as 0.88 [2], and in the current study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was found to be 0.94. In the present study, social network usage specifically refers to engagement with online social networking platforms (e.g., WhatsApp, Facebook, YouTube) and focuses on the purposes of digital engagement, including content sharing, communication, and collaboration, as measured by the Scale for the Social Network Usage Purposes of Older Adults.

Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS)

The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) was developed by Zimet et al. (1988), and the Turkish adaptation of the scale was conducted by Eker et al. (2001) [15, 17]. The scale comprises a total of 12 items and evaluates the perceived support received from three sources: family, friends, and a significant other. A higher score on the scale indicates a higher perceived level of social support [17]. The total scale and its subscales exhibit reliability coefficients ranging from 0.80 to 0.95. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha values for the scale ranged between 0.89 and 0.94. In the MSPSS, the “significant other” subscale refers to a special person perceived as an important source of emotional support, such as a spouse, partner, or another person with whom the individual has a close emotional relationship, and is conceptually distinct from family and friends. Participants were instructed to respond to these items in accordance with the original scale instructions, considering the person they perceive as their closest source of personal emotional support.

The active aging scale-activity dimension

The scale was developed by Rantanen et al. (2018) and adapted to Turkish by Demir Erbil and Hazer (2019) [18, 19]. It consists of 17 activity questions designed to measure individuals’ active aging. These questions are divided into four subscales: assessing purposes, functional capacity, opportunities, and activities. In this study, the activity dimension of the scale was used to evaluate the frequency of activities performed by older adults. Each statement in the questionnaire is rated on a five-point Likert scale from 0 (lowest, for example, least active) to 4 (highest, for example, most active). The minimum and maximum scores for all subscales are 0 and 68, respectively. The Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient for the scale is reported to be above 0.90. In the current study, the Cronbach’s alpha value is 0.88.

Data collection process

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by the researchers. Before commencing the study, the data collection form including all the scales was administered to a group of 15 individuals, and necessary revisions were made to finalize the form. The data obtained in the pilot study were not included in the analysis. The data collection process took approximately 20–25 min per participant.

Data analysis

In the research, analyses were conducted using SPSS 22.0 software. Descriptive statistics such as frequency, percentage, mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum were employed. Reliability analysis was performed using the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, and a significance level of 0.05 was adopted. The normal distribution of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Pearson correlation and multiple linear regression analyses were employed to determine the relationships between the scales.

Before conducting the regression analysis, the dataset was examined for missing data, and no missing values requiring imputation were identified. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using tolerance and Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values, and no multicollinearity problem was identified (tolerance > 0.10, VIF < 10). The regression analysis included only the main study variables in accordance with the research questions and theoretical framework of the study. In the regression model, loneliness score was treated as a continuous dependent variable, while the subscales of social network usage, perceived social support, and active aging were treated as continuous independent variables. No categorical variables or covariates were included in the regression model, as the analysis focused on examining the relationships among the primary study variables.

Ethical considerations

To conduct the research, approval was obtained from the Ondokuz Mayıs University Social and Human Sciences Ethics Committee (date: October 28, 2022; no: 2022 − 852). Permission was also obtained from the developers of the scales used in the study. Informed consent was acquired from participants by adding an explanation to the data collection form, clarifying the research team and purpose, emphasizing the voluntary nature of participation, stating that participants could withdraw from the study at any time, ensuring the confidentiality of their information, and specifying that the data would be used solely for scientific purposes. Throughout all stages of the study, ethical principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration were adhered to.

Trial registration

Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Results

Among them, 51.4% are male, 86.2% are married, and 77.7% belong to the middle-income level. The frequency of social network use among individuals is 42.3%, with 2–3 times a day, and the usage duration is 39.5%, approximately one hour. The most commonly used social networks are observed to be WhatsApp (37.8%) and Facebook (32.7%). Additionally, the primary purpose of social network use is messaging, accounting for 27.6% (Table 1).

Discussion

The study aimed to examine the relationships between social network usage, perceived social support, active aging, and loneliness among older adults. Social network usage was common among participants, with communication being the primary purpose of use. While perceived social support and active aging were associated with lower levels of loneliness, an unexpected finding emerged indicating that communication-oriented social network usage was positively associated with loneliness. These findings are discussed below in relation to the existing literature.

In the present study, the mean total loneliness score among older adults was 7.85 ± 4.94, with the emotional loneliness score (5.19 ± 3.17) exceeding the social loneliness score (2.66 ± 4.94), suggesting that emotional loneliness may be more prominent. This finding is consistent with a qualitative study where the total loneliness score for older adults was found to be 11.55 ± 0.19, the emotional loneliness subscale score was 3.29 ± 0.22, and the social loneliness subscale score was 8.26 ± 0.14 [2]. Güner et al. [20] determined the average loneliness score in older adults to be 11.39 ± 5.31, which is relatively low. Loneliness in old age, with its emotional and social dimensions, is a significant concept that negatively affects individuals physically, socially, and psychologically. In this regard, preventing or reducing loneliness in old age is essential to help older adults lead healthier and more satisfying lives.

The findings showed that nearly half of the older adults used social networks two to three times per day, with an average daily usage duration of approximately one hour. WhatsApp and Facebook were identified as the most commonly used platforms, and communication with friends and acquaintances emerged as the primary purpose of use. These findings are consistent with prior studies indicating that older adults primarily use social networks to maintain contact with family members and friends and typically spend around one hour per day on these platforms [2, 21–24]. Although social network usage was widespread, the overall purpose-specific usage level observed in the present study remained limited, which is consistent with earlier findings obtained using the same measurement tool [2]. The widespread adoption of social networks among older adults highlights their potential importance for mental health, social connectedness, emotional support, and information sharing, while also underscoring the need for guidance on safe and effective use.

Despite the generally positive role attributed to social network use in the literature, the present study yielded an unexpected finding: higher levels of communication-oriented social network usage were associated with increased loneliness among older adults. Although social network platforms are often assumed to reduce loneliness by facilitating interpersonal communication, this relationship appears to be more complex in later life. One possible explanation is that increased online communication may reflect compensatory behavior rather than a protective factor. Older adults experiencing higher levels of loneliness may engage more frequently in online communication to cope with reduced face-to-face interactions, as suggested by previous studies on compensatory internet use [25, 26]. In this context, frequent communication does not necessarily indicate satisfying or meaningful social relationships but may instead signal unmet social needs. Moreover, exposure to others’ social activities and family interactions through digital platforms may heighten awareness of social exclusion, thereby intensifying feelings of loneliness [27]. The limited emotional depth, physical presence, and reciprocity inherent in online interactions may further constrain their capacity to provide meaningful social support for older adults who value in-person contact. Importantly, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence that increased social network use causes loneliness. Rather, it is more consistent with an association in which loneliness may motivate increased online communication, rather than increased communication leading to loneliness.

The study found that older adults reported high levels of perceived social support, a finding consistent with previous research [28, 29]. Furthermore, perceived social support from family and friends was associated with lower levels of loneliness. This finding aligns with earlier studies demonstrating that reduced social support is linked to increased loneliness among older adults [2, 30]. Social support may alleviate loneliness by fostering feelings of being valued, cared for, and emotionally connected, thereby enhancing psychological well-being in later life.

Regarding active aging, the findings indicated that older adults engaged in activities to a certain extent, and higher engagement was associated with increased perceived social support and reduced loneliness. This result aligns with existing literature suggesting that older adults who are more physically and socially active tend to experience higher levels of social support and lower levels of loneliness [31–35]. Participation in activities not only fulfills physical and cognitive needs but also facilitates social interaction and the maintenance of supportive relationships. These findings highlight the importance of promoting active aging strategies to strengthen social networks and reduce loneliness among older adults.

Although social network usage, perceived social support, active aging, and loneliness have been examined separately in previous research, the present study contributes to the literature by addressing these constructs simultaneously within a single analytical framework among older adults. This integrative perspective provides a more nuanced understanding of how digital communication, social resources, and activity engagement jointly shape loneliness in later life. By focusing on purpose-specific social network usage rather than overall usage frequency, the study offers novel insight into the differentiated effects of digital engagement. Notably, the finding that communication-oriented social network use was associated with higher loneliness underscores the complex and non-linear nature of digital social interaction in older age and challenges the assumption that increased communication uniformly alleviates loneliness. Taken together, these findings emphasize that the quality, meaning, and contextual characteristics of social interactions—rather than mere access or frequency—are central to understanding loneliness among older adults [12].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It provides comprehensive data by simultaneously examining social network usage, perceived social support, and active aging in relation to loneliness among older adults. The use of validated measurement tools and face-to-face data collection is another strength that enhanced data quality and reliability. Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, due to the cross-sectional design of the research, causal inferences cannot be drawn regarding the relationships between social network usage, perceived social support, active aging, and loneliness. Accordingly, the findings reflect associations between variables and should not be interpreted as evidence of directionality or causation. The study sample was limited to individuals aged 60 years and older who actively use social networks and reside in a single city center in the northern region of Türkiye. This restricts the generalizability of the results to older adults living in rural areas, those from different sociocultural contexts, or individuals with limited digital literacy or no access to social networking platforms. In addition, the sample was not selected using a probability sampling method and included only individuals living in a specific city center who use social networks and attend family health centers. Therefore, the findings cannot be generalized to the entire older population in Türkiye and may only be applicable to community-dwelling older adults with similar characteristics. Furthermore, as all data were collected using self-report measures, the findings may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Although multiple regression analysis was conducted, certain potential confounding variables—such as physical health status, cognitive functioning, personality traits, and the quality of face-to-face social relationships—were not included in the analytical model and may have influenced levels of loneliness. Finally, as only the activity dimension of the Active Aging Scale was assessed, the multidimensional nature of active aging may not have been fully captured. Future research employing longitudinal designs, larger and more diverse samples, and comprehensive assessments of active aging is recommended. In addition, the study did not include a direct comparison between online and face-to-face communication, which should be considered when interpreting the findings related to social network usage and loneliness.

Implications for practice

Supporting the technology usage skills of older adults can contribute to reducing loneliness risks. Ensuring safe use of social networks is essential to promote active aging and provide social support. Nurses should identify individual risk factors related to social network usage, active aging, and social support while integrating interventions to reduce perceived loneliness in older adults into their care practices.

Research recommendations

Based on the findings of this study, future research may employ longitudinal designs to clarify the directionality of the relationships between social network usage and loneliness in older adults. In addition, further studies should differentiate between various types and qualities of online and offline social interactions to better understand their distinct effects on loneliness. Interventional research focusing on enhancing meaningful social engagement and promoting balanced and purposeful use of digital platforms may also contribute to reducing loneliness in later life.

Conclusion

The study revealed that perceived social support and social network usage play a determinant role in the loneliness perception of older adults. Based on the findings, it is recommended to structure interventions that support social support, social network usage, and activity frequency in older adults. Thus, it is believed that reducing the perceived level of loneliness in older adults can mitigate the psychological and physiological challenges associated with loneliness. The balanced and conscious use of social networks by older adults should be supervised and supported. The effective and secure use of online social networks may be a valid strategy for preventing loneliness in old age.
