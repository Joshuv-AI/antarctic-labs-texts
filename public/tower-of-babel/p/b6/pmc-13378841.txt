Multiple thyroid cysts: a benign and stable clinical entity based on longitudinal data
Authors: Yuji Nagayama, Seigo Tachibana, Takashi Fukuda, Daisuke Tatsushima, Yusuke Mori, Hisakazu Shindo, Hiroshi Takahashi, Shinya Sato, Hiroyuki Yamashita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multiple thyroid cysts are commonly detected by ultrasonography, and are considered clinically insignificant. On the other hand, polycystic thyroid disease (PCTD) has been proposed as a cause of antibody-negative hypothyroidism in iodine-replete regions; however, the clinical characteristics and natural history of multiple thyroid cysts as a broader entity remain unclear. To elucidate these issues, we retrospectively reviewed 7,727 consecutive new outpatients at a high-volume thyroid center between 2013 and 2016. Multiple thyroid cysts were defined as ≥4 cysts (≥3 mm in diameter) in the absence of thyroid autoantibodies. Among all patients, 164 (2.1%) met the criteria. The mean age was 58.3 years, and 91.0% were female. At presentation, 86.6% were euthyroid, 6.1% and 6.7% had subclinical hypothyroidism and subclinical hyperthyroidism, respectively, and one patient had Graves’ disease. Thus, approximately 6% of multiple thyroid cysts may be PCTD. Forty-six patients were followed up for ≥5 years (mean 9.8 years) without iodine restriction. Thyroid function remained stable in most cases, with no significant progression to overt dysfunction and no requirement for surgical or interventional treatment. Ultrasonographic findings ranged from numerous diffuse cysts throughout the gland to several scattered or localized cysts. Some cysts remained stable, some increased, and others decreased in size in the follow-up periods. The cyst distribution patterns (diffuse versus localized) were not associated with thyroid function. Therefore, multiple thyroid cysts may generally represent benign and stable clinical entities, supporting conservative management with periodic monitoring in most patients.

Introduction

Simple thyroid cysts are often detected incidentally during ultrasonography (US) and have received little attention, because they are generally considered clinically insignificant abnormalities that rarely affect thyroid function. However, Kubota et al. have previously reported that polycystic thyroid disease (PCTD)—a distinct disease entity characterized by multiple thyroid cysts detected by US (≥4 cysts), the absence of thyroid autoantibodies, and the susceptibility to hypothyroidism due to high iodine intake—may represent a previously overlooked cause of hypothyroidism in iodine-replete Japan [1, 2]. In their study of six adult patients with hypothyroidism and PCTD, all of whom had negative thyroglobulin (Tg) antibodies and thyroid peroxidase (TPO) antibodies and high iodine intake, dietary iodine restriction led to the resolution of hypothyroidism [1], suggesting a causal relationship between multiple thyroid cysts and thyroid dysfunction.

In a larger retrospective analysis of the same group, 24 (7.8%) of 307 patients with overt hypothyroidism and 42 (7.7%) of 546 patients with subclinical hypothyroidism met the criteria for PCTD, highlighting its nonnegligible role in antibody-negative hypothyroidism [2]. PCTD generally occurs more frequently in older adults, and as with most thyroid disorders, a higher prevalence among females is suspected. Furthermore, histopathological examinations of three patients with PCTD who underwent thyroidectomy revealed multiple enlarged follicles lined with normal cuboidal epithelium, distinct from those of multinodular goiters, supporting the concept of PCTD as a unique pathological entity [3].

Subsequently, multiple thyroid cysts associated with thyroid dysfunction have also been reported in pediatric populations, including two antibody-negative hypothyroid cases [4] and one hyperthyroid case [5]; the latter did not fulfill the diagnostic criteria of PCTD proposed by Kubota et al. [2].

Assuming that PCTD constitutes a subset of multiple thyroid cysts, this study aimed to elucidate the clinical characteristics and natural history of multiple thyroid cysts.

Materials and Methods

Patients

We reviewed the thyroid US findings of 7,727 patients who visited Yamashita Thyroid Hospital, a high-volume thyroid-specialty hospital in Fukuoka Japan, for the first time between April 2013 and March 2016. Using the diagnostic criteria for PCTD proposed by Kubota et al. [2], we defined the definition of multiple thyroid cysts as having ≥4 thyroid cysts and negative thyroid autoantibodies [2]. Cystic lesions measuring ≥3 mm in diameter were considered significant [6]. Demographic and biochemical data were extracted from the patients’ medical records.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines, and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yamashita Thyroid Hospital (approval number: 2025-8). Because this was an observational study based on routine clinical practice without any additional interventions, the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

Laboratory tests

Serum levels of free thyroxine (FT4; the normal reference range: 0.9–1.7 ng/dL), TSH (0.5–5.0 μIU/mL), anti-TSH receptor antibodies (TRAb; <2.0 IU/L), anti-Tg antibodies (<33.7 IU/mL), and anti-TPO antibodies (<16.0 IU/mL) were measured using an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay kit (COBAS 8000 e801 analyzer, Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA). Experienced laboratory technicians performed the thyroid US examinations using high-resolution instruments. A thyroid cyst in multiple thyroid cysts was differentiated from cystic degeneration in an adenomatous nodule, with the former appearing on US as an anechoic area with a smooth, well-defined round or oval border, no solid internal components, and occasionally accompanied by posterior acoustic enhancement. By contrast, an adenomatous nodule with cystic degeneration appears as a mixed solid-and-cystic lesion rather than a purely fluid cyst and may exhibit an irregular shape or internal partitions.

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as means ± standard deviations (SDs). For values below the lower limit of detection, the detection limit was used in the calculations. Continuous variables in the demographic and thyroid function data were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Statistical analyses were conducted using EZR version 1.68 [7], which provides a graphical user interface for R version 4.0.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of the 7,727 new outpatients, 171 had ≥4 cysts. After excluding seven patients with positive or unmeasured thyroid autoantibodies, 164 met the diagnostic criteria for multiple thyroid cysts. Thus, the prevalence of multiple thyroid cysts among the new outpatients was 2.1%. The demographic characteristics and thyroid function test results of these patients are shown in Table 1. The mean age was 58.3 ± 16.4 years (mean ± SD), with 12 patients in their 20s and 17 in their 30s; 91.0% were females. Most patients (86.6%) had normal thyroid function at presentation, whereas 10 of 164 (6.1%) had subclinical hypothyroidism (TSH 5–10 μIU/mL with normal FT4), 11 (6.7%) had subclinical hyperthyroidism (TSH 0.1–0.5 μIU/mL with normal FT4), and one (0.6%) had overt hyperthyroidism due to Graves’ disease (positive TRAb >40 IU/L). Of these 11 patients with subclinical hyperthyroidism, five tested negative for TRAb, and none showed nodular lesions indicating toxic nodules. No statistically significant differences in age or sex ratio were observed among the patients with euthyroidism, hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism (data not shown).

Forty-six patients were followed up for ≥5 years (9.8 ± 3.8). Iodine restriction was not recommended for these patients. At baseline, two patients (4.3%) had subclinical hypothyroidism and four (8.7%) had subclinical hyperthyroidism; at the end of the follow-up, one patient (2.1%) had subclinical hypothyroidism and five (10.9%) had subclinical hyperthyroidism (Table 1). Thus, thyroid function remained stable throughout the observation period. Only one patient improved from subclinical hypothyroidism to normal function, whereas other progressed from normal thyroid function to subclinical hyperthyroidism. No statistically significant differences in age, sex ratio, the rates of thyroid dysfunction, and narrowly defined multiple thyroid cysts (see below) were observed between patients with and without long-term follow-up (data not shown).

US findings showed that, in approximately one-third of patients (33.5%, 55/164), numerous cysts (usually ≥10) of varying sizes were diffusely distributed throughout the thyroid gland, whereas in the remaining two-thirds (66.5%, 109/164), only several cysts (<10, mostly 4–6) were scattered or localized. Representative US images of each pattern are shown in Fig. 1. Diffuse, numerous cyst patterns can be narrowly defined multiple thyroid cysts. No significant differences in the demographic or thyroid function data were observed between the two patterns (data not shown). Changes in cyst size during the follow-up varied: some cysts remained stable, others increased in size, and the remainder decreased (Supplementary Fig. 1). Discussion

This retrospective study evaluated the prevalence and clinical course of multiple thyroid cysts in a large cohort of new outpatients at a thyroid-specialty center. Among the 7,727 new outpatients, 2.1% (164/7,727) met the diagnostic criteria for multiple thyroid cysts. Among 46 of 164 patients (28%) who could be followed up for ≥5 years, all patients followed a benign, non-progressive course. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to describe long-term thyroid function stability in a relatively large cohort of patients with multiple thyroid cysts, and raises the possibility that many patients with multiple thyroid cysts might not require immediate active intervention and could potentially be managed with periodic monitoring of thyroid function.

Moreover, the number and distribution of cysts—whether diffuse (numerous cysts throughout the gland) or localized (fewer, confined cysts)—did not significantly correlate with thyroid function or demographic parameters. Contrary to previous hypotheses that the presence of multiple enlarged follicles reduces the total number of functioning follicular cells, thereby contributing to decreased thyroid hormone production [2], our findings suggest that the cyst burden alone could not predict clinical outcome.

Kubota et al. have reported that the prevalence of PCTD among patients with thyroid autoantibody-negative hypothyroidism was approximately 8%, and the prevalence among all new outpatients was 0.46% (66/14,365) [2]. Thus, PCTD may likely be a disease that constitutes part of multiple thyroid cysts. In our study, 10 patients with multiple thyroid cysts and subclinical hypothyroidism can be considered to have PCTD because they met the diagnostic criteria (6.1% (10/164) in multiple thyroid cysts, and 0.13% (10/7,727) in all out patients), whereas the remaining patients with normal thyroid function may represent a group that could develop hypothyroidism due to excessive iodine intake or aging (see below) (can be called a ‘pre-PCTD’ group) or a group unrelated to PCTD. The difference in age distributions (a mean age of 58.3 years with a substantial number of younger individuals among multiple thyroid cysts in our study versus 66.6 years among patients with PCTD and subclinical hypothyroidism and 71.5 years among patients with PCTD and overt hypothyroidism in the studies by Kubota et al. [1, 2]) supports the lower prevalence of patients with PCTD in our study and an idea that our study may include many patients at the earlier stage of PCTD. However, their study on US revealed that ‘the tissue between the cysts was hypoechoic and had sponge-like appearance in all the patients’ [1], a feature absent in our patients. Hence, it cannot be ruled out that their study and ours may have examined different disease groups.

Of interest, Kubota et al. have reported that four hypothyroid PCTD patients with a history of excess iodine intake became euthyroid after iodine restriction (patients 1, 3–5), but one of these four patients (patient 4) later developed hypothyroidism. In addition, patient 6 gradually developed subclinical hypothyroidism over a 12-year-course [1]. Although the daily iodine intake of these two patients was not described, hypothyroidism may have developed regardless of the amount of iodine intake. Similarly, the same group examined the frequency of PCTD among patients with hypothyroidism, and identified that seven of 24 patients with PCTD and overt hypothyroidism and 23 of 42 patients with PCTD and subclinical hypothyroidism had no history of an iodine excess diet [2], suggesting that the onset of hypothyroidism in patients with PCTD may not be solely attributable to iodine excess, but also to other factors such as aging.

Moreover, no marked difference in iodine intake was noted between the areas where the previous [1, 2] and current studies were conducted [8].

This study has some limitations. This study was conducted at a single high-volume thyroid center, which may have limited the generalizability of the findings. Patients presenting to such a specialized facility may differ from the general population due to referral bias. The retrospective design and reliance on routine clinical data also introduce certain limitations. For example, iodine intake was not quantified, preventing a direct assessment of its association with thyroid function. Because TRAb measurements were performed in only 5 of 11 patients with subclinical hyperthyroidism, the possibility of early-stage Graves’ disease, which may be attributed to the cystic phenotype, cannot be ruled out. In addition, the definition of a colloid cyst (≥3 mm in diameter) was somewhat arbitrary, and small cysts or borderline cases may have been overlooked. Finally, long-term follow-up data were available only for a subset of patients, and their outcomes may not fully represent those of the entire cohort.

Nevertheless, our study provides valuable new insights into the epidemiology and clinical behavior of multiple thyroid cysts (Graphical Abstract). Multiple thyroid cysts are not uncommon disorders with a prevalence of approximately 2% in new outpatients, and follow a benign, non-progressive clinical course. Only a fraction of patients (~6%) with multiple thyroid cysts may have PCTD. Therefore, conservative follow-up is appropriate for this disorder. Future prospective studies incorporating systematic iodine assessments and standardized US protocols are warranted to refine the diagnostic criteria and elucidate the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying multiple thyroid cysts and PCTD.
