Spatiotemporal trends, demographic disparities, and predictions to 2040 in gastrointestinal cancer mortality in the United States
Authors: Maolang He, Jingyi Zhang, Shangqi Wang, Kainan Xing, Yanxin Zhao, Jianzhen Chen, Xi Zhang, Yong Zheng, Shuxin Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastrointestinal cancers represent a major public health challenge in the United States, yet the latest mortality trends and population disparities remain unclear. This study aimed to systematically analyze the demographic disparities and spatiotemporal trends in age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMR) of six gastrointestinal cancers in the United States from 1999 to 2023, and to project their mortality trends through 2040.MethodsData were extracted from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide-ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC WONDER) database and the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2021. Joinpoint regression analysis were used to evaluate the annual percent change (APC) and average annual percent change (AAPC) for gastrointestinal cancers, stratified by sex, race/ethnicity, age group, census region, and state. The autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model was used to forecast mortality rates by 2040.ResultsOverall, except for liver cancer (LC) and pancreatic cancer (PC), the AAMR of the remaining gastrointestinal cancers showed downward trend. LC had the highest AAPC (1.58, 95% CI: 1.51 to 1.64), while gastric cancer (GC) had the lowest AAPC (-2.48, 95% CI: -2.59 to -2.36). By sex, males had higher AAMR than females for gastrointestinal cancers except biliary tract cancer, with esophageal cancer showing the most significant disparity. By race/ethnicity, Non-Hispanic Black individuals had the highest colorectal cancer mortality, whereas non-Hispanic White individuals exhibited the fastest increase in LC mortality. By age, crude mortality rates peaked in adults ≥ 85 years. Projections to 2040 indicated that GC would experience the largest decline in mortality rates, while the mortality rates of LC and PC will continue to rise.ConclusionWhile overall gastrointestinal cancer mortality in the United States has declined, LC and PC burdens continue to rise, with significant disparities by race, region, and age, highlighting the need for targeted prevention and control measures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27254-y.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal cancers originate from organs including the esophagus, stomach, liver, biliary tract, pancreas, and colorectum, accounting for approximately one-third of all global cancer deaths [1]. In 2021, the number of global deaths caused by these types of cancers reached 3.7 million, among which colorectal cancer (CRC) accounted for the highest proportion, followed by gastric cancer (GC) [2]. In the United States, these cancers also present substantial public health challenges and contribute significantly to the healthcare burden. According to projections by the American Cancer Society, approximately 362,200 newly diagnosed gastrointestinal cancer cases and 174,520 deaths are expected to occur in 2025 in the United States, with CRC accounting for the highest proportion of the burden, followed by pancreatic cancer (PC) [3]. In the United States, it is projected that by 2030, annual spending on gastrointestinal cancer management will exceed $21 billion, which is primarily driven by hospitalizations for CRC and imposes a considerable and growing burden on healthcare utilization and costs [4, 5]. An in-depth understanding of the mortality patterns and their temporal trends of gastrointestinal cancers is of critical importance for evaluating the effectiveness of preventive strategies implemented in recent decades, formulating future public health policies, and rationally allocating healthcare resources.

Although previous studies have yielded significant findings on the epidemiological patterns of individual gastrointestinal cancers in the United States, there remains a notable gap in the comprehensive and systematic assessment of these cancers nationwide [6–8]. Furthermore, there is currently no research available on the latest epidemiological data of gastrointestinal cancers in the United States for 2023. Therefore, this study evaluated the demographic disparities and spatiotemporal trends in age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMR) of gastrointestinal cancers in the United States from 1999 to 2023, and projected the future trends of gastrointestinal cancers through 2040, thereby providing new insights for the prevention of gastrointestinal cancers.

Materials and methods

Data sources

Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide-ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC WONDER) database is a web-based system developed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention that provides public health professionals and the general public with streamlined access to a wide range of public health data for analysis, research, and decision-making. The death certificate data derived from the CDC WONDER database were analyzed in this retrospective study. We extracted mortality and corresponding population data for six types of gastrointestinal cancers from 1999 to 2023. The International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision code (ICD-10) codes for esophageal cancer (EC) were C15; for GC, C16; for liver cancer (LC), C22; for biliary tract cancer (BTC), C23 and C24; for PC, C25; and for CRC, C18, C19, and C20. The CDC WONDER database has been employed in prior studies on gastrointestinal cancers, and these ICD-10 codes have been previously validated for identifying mortality trends related to specific digestive malignancies [9–11]. This study was exempt from institutional review board approval, as it relied exclusively on de-identified, publicly available data from governmental sources and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines to ensure methodological rigor.

The Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 (GBD 2021) constitutes a systematic scientific effort to produce robust estimates of disease burden for 371 diseases and injuries across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021 [2]. As a result, it provides a publicly accessible, detailed dataset for the comprehensive analysis of disease patterns. To forecast changes in mortality rates by 2040, we extracted data on six gastrointestinal cancers in the United States for the period 1990 to 2021 from the GBD 2021 database. The ICD-10 codes for the respective gastrointestinal cancers in the GBD 2021 database were as follows: C15-C15.9 for EC; C16-C16.9 for GC; C22-C22.4 and C22.7-C22.8 for LC; C23-C24.9 for BTC; C25-C25.9 for PC; and C18-C19.0, C20, and C21-C21.8 for CRC. The Global Population Forecasts 2017–2100 provided the population forecast data used in this study. As the study utilized publicly available GBD 2021 data, it was exempt from institutional review board approval.

Data management

The following variables were extracted for analysis: sex, racial and ethnic groups, age groups, census region, and state. Sex was categorized as male or female. Race and ethnicity were classified as follows: Hispanic or Latino, non-Hispanic (NH) White, NH Black or African American, NH Asian or Pacific Islander, and NH American Indian or Alaska Native [12]. Age was stratified into 7 ten-year intervals: 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74, 75–84, and 85 years and above. According to the United States Census Bureau classifications, the geographic region was divided into four areas: Northeast, Midwest, South, and West [13].

Statistical analysis

To analyze the demographic disparities and spatiotemporal trends in mortality rates associated with the six gastrointestinal cancers in the United States, we retrieved both the crude mortality rate (CMR) and AAMR per 100,000 population for the period from 1999 to 2023. These rates were stratified by year, sex, race and ethnicity, age group, census region, and state, with 95% confidence intervals (CI). CMR was derived by dividing the number of deaths attributed to a specific gastrointestinal cancer by the corresponding United States population of that year. The AAMR was standardized using the 2000 United States standard population to eliminate the influence of age structure [14]. To identify and quantify temporal changes in mortality trends for gastrointestinal cancers, we utilized the Joinpoint Regression Program (Version 5.1.0, National Cancer Institute). This software employs log-linear regression models and uses the Monte Carlo permutation method for model selection. With the number of joinpoints constrained to a range of 0 to 5, the program identifies the optimal model by testing for statistically significant trend changes, and further estimates the annual percent change (APC) in AAMR along with its 95% CI for each trend segment [15, 16]. The weighted average of the APCs across all trend segments over the entire study period was defined as the average annual percent change (AAPC), with its corresponding 95% CI also reported. The AAPC functions as a key metric to evaluate the average temporal trend in gastrointestinal cancer mortality rates in the United States from 1999 to 2023, thereby offering a comprehensive representation of the overall mortality trajectory over this period [17]. A two-tailed t-test was used to assess the statistical significance of the slope describing mortality trends. If the slope differed significantly from zero, the corresponding APC or AAPC was interpreted as indicating a statistically significant increasing or decreasing trend. The significance level was set at P < 0.05. In compliance with the privacy protection protocols of the CDC WONDER database, death counts with fewer than 10 cases are labeled as “Suppressed”. Due to the unavailability of data for the 25–34 age group with BTC, this specific age group was excluded from the Joinpoint regression analysis.

For time series predictive analysis, an autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model was used to forecast the age-standardized mortality rates of gastrointestinal cancers in the United States. We conducted the primary forecasting to 2040 using the CDC WONDER database and subsequently employed the GBD 2021 database to perform an independent external validation. In addition to point estimates, the ARIMA models were used to generate 95% prediction intervals to quantify the uncertainty associated with the future mortality projections. The ARIMA model integrates three components: autoregression, integration, and moving average, and is formally denoted as ARIMA (p, d, q). The optimal values of p and q were identified via the autocorrelation function and partial autocorrelation function, respectively, while the value of d was determined by conducting a stationarity test. The model was selected for its ability to handle non-stationary time series and capture underlying trends without requiring complex assumptions about causal mechanisms, making it suitable for long-term forecasting [18]. This model has been widely applied in predicting cancer mortality rates [19, 20]. We used the auto ARIMA function to select the optimal ARIMA model based on the lowest Akaike Information Criterion, ensuring a good balance between model fit and complexity. The Ljung-Box test was applied to assess whether the residuals followed a white noise pattern. P > 0.05 indicates that no significant autocorrelation exists in the residuals, confirming the model has captured all interpretable temporal trends [21]. The constructed optimal model was then used for forecasting. Data were analyzed using the R software package (version 4.5.1) and JD_GBDR (version 2.7.4, Jingding Medical Technology Co., Ltd.).

Result

Overall

The number of deaths from gastrointestinal cancers increased from 126,212 in 1999 to 164,063 in 2023. Overall, except for LC and PC, the AAMR of the remaining gastrointestinal cancers showed downward trend. In terms of the peak in temporal trends, LC had an AAMR of 6.95 (95% CI: 6.83 to 7.07) in 1999, which increased to a peak of 10.34 (95% CI: 10.22 to 10.47) in 2016 and then declined minimally to 10.29 (95% CI: 10.17 to 10.41) in 2023. In contrast, PC exhibited a modest upward trend in AAMR, with values rising from 16.41 (95% CI: 16.22 to 16.60) in 1999 to 17.33 (95% CI: 17.17 to 17.48) in 2023. Additionally, LC had the highest AAPC in AAMR at 1.58 (95% CI: 1.51 to 1.64), which was approximately 6.58 times higher than that of PC, whose AAPC was 0.24 (95% CI: 0.19 to 0.30). Conversely, GC showed the most significant downward trend in AAMR, with values decreasing from 7.16 (95% CI: 7.03 to 7.28) in 1999 to 4.01 (95% CI: 3.93 to 4.08) in 2023 and the lowest AAPC of -2.48 (95% CI: -2.59 to -2.36) (Table 1). Specifically, GC AAMR declined markedly from 1999 to 2007 (APC = -3.19*, 95% CI: -4.52 to -2.75), and the rate of decline decelerated after 2007 (APC = -2.13*, 95% CI: -2.29 to -1.80) (Fig. 1). CRC ranked first in terms of the number of deaths both 1999 and 2023, with the number of deaths in 2023 was approximately 12.36 times higher than that of BTC. The AAMR of CRC decreased from 32.06 (95% CI: 31.80 to 32.33) in 1999 to 19.57 (95% CI: 19.40 to 19.74) in 2023. Its AAMR declined continuously (AAPC = -2.08, 95% CI: -2.22 to -1.99), but the downward trend attenuated gradually after 2020. It is noteworthy that BTC was the only gastrointestinal malignancy in this study with a higher number of deaths among females than males. Furthermore, BTC had the lowest AAMR of 1.53 (1.48 to 1.57) in 2023 (Table 1).

Sex stratified

Except for BTC, males had a higher AAMR for gastrointestinal cancers than females. The AAMR in LC among men increased from 9.96 (95% CI: 9.74 to 10.19) in 1999 to 14.41 (95% CI: 14.20 to 14.62) in 2023, with the highest AAPC of 1.47 (95% CI: 1.39 to 1.57) (Table 1). For AAMR of LC among men, the APC was 2.77* (95% CI: 2.60 to 3.01) from 1999 to 2013, then showed no significant change to 0.55 (95% CI: -0.23 to 2.54) from 2013 to 2017, and finally declined consistently to -0.89* (95% CI: -1.58 to -0.53) from 2017 to 2023. In contrast, the AAMR of GC among men decreased from 10.18 (95% CI: 9.94 to 10.41) in 1999 to 5.23 (95% CI: 5.10 to 5.36) in 2023, showing the lowest AAPC of -2.82 (95% CI: -2.91 to -2.73). Correspondingly, females showed an increase in AAMR of LC from 4.56 (95% CI: 4.43 to 4.70) in 1999 to 6.78 (95% CI: 6.65 to 6.91) in 2023, with the highest AAPC of 1.64 (95% CI: 1.53 to 1.74). For LC among females, the APC for AAMR was 1.38* (95% CI: 0.52 to 1.73) from 1999 to 2008, then rose more steeply to 3.15* (95% CI: 2.39 to 4.51) from 2008 to 2013, and finally decreased again to 1.13* (95% CI: 0.82 to 1.37) from 2013 to 2023 (Fig. 1 and Fig. S1-S6). For women in GC, the AAMR decreased from 5.04 (95% CI: 4.90 to 5.18) in 1999 to 3.03 (95% CI: 2.93 to 3.12) in 2023, with the lowest AAPC of -2.26 (95% CI: -2.38 to -2.14). In addition, the difference in AAMR between men and women is the most significant for EC. In 2023, the EC-related AAMR in men was 3.85 times higher than that in women (Table 1).

Race/ethnicity stratified

For gastrointestinal cancers, NH Black or African American consistently had the highest AAMR in CRC, with a significant decrease from 44.00 (95% CI: 42.93 to 45.07) in 1999 to 24.90 (95% CI: 24.30 to 25.50) in 2023 and an AAPC of -2.49 (95% CI: -2.69 to -2.35). In NH Black or African American, the APC for AAMR in CRC was − 2.72* (95% CI: -3.46 to -2.55) from 1999 to 2019, before changing to -1.28 (95% CI: -2.59 to 1.11) from 2019 to 2023. Among NH Black or African American, the AAMR in GC decreased from 14.35 (95% CI: 13.74 to 14.96) in 1999 to 6.45 (95% CI: 6.15 to 6.76) in 2023, with an AAPC of -3.18 (95% CI: -3.32 to -3.04). In contrast, NH White consistently had lower AAMR in LC, with an increase from 6.02 (95% CI: 5.89 to 6.15) in 1999 to 9.54 (95% CI: 9.41 to 9.68) in 2023 and an AAPC of 1.89 (95% CI: 1.80 to 1.99). For NH White, the APC of AAMR in LC was 2.28* (95% CI: 0.99 to 2.71) from 1999 to 2007, then increased to 3.36* (95% CI: 2.73 to 4.44) from 2007 to 2013, and finally slowed markedly to 0.71* (95% CI: 0.48 to 0.95) from 2013 to 2023. In addition, The change in AAMR for PC was not significant across all racial groups. Among NH Asian or Pacific Islander, the AAMR in PC increased slowly from 11.76 (95% CI: 10.70 to 12.83) in 1999 to 11.55 (95% CI: 11.01 to 12.10) in 2023, with an AAPC of 0.04 (95% CI: -0.17 to 0.29). Notably, due to incomplete BTC mortality data for NH American Indian or Alaska Native individuals from 1999 to 2002, this time segment was excluded from the Joinpoint regression analysis of BTC trends in this racial/ethnic group. (Fig. 2 and Fig. S7-S12).

Age group stratified

The CMR for six types of gastrointestinal cancers increased with advancing age, with the highest CMR observed in patients aged 85 years or older. Among these, the CMR of CRC was highest among individuals aged 85 years or older. The CMR of CRC decreased significantly from 270.27 (95% CI: 265.27 to 275.27) in 1999 to 158.07 (95% CI: 155.03 to 161.12) in 2018, and subsequently declined to 157.66 (95% CI: 154.53 to 160.79) in 2023. The APC for CRC from 1999 to 2018 was − 2.96* (95% CI: -3.16 to -2.79), while from 2018 to 2023 it was − 0.02 (95% CI: -1.23 to 2.20. In contrast, BTC exhibited the lowest CMR among those aged 85 or above, with CMR decreasing from 16.23 (95% CI: 15.00 to 17.45) in 1999 to 11.80 (95% CI: 10.94 to 12.66) in 2023. With an AAPC of -1.56 (95% CI: -1.95 to -1.20), the APC for BTC was − 2.43* (95% CI: -4.68 to -1.84) between 1999 and 2013, and − 0.33 (95% CI: -1.33 to 3.00) from 2013 to 2023. Due to data suppression for BTC in the 25–34 age group, this age group was excluded from the Joinpoint regression analysis of BTC mortality trends. (Fig. 3 and Fig. S13-S18).

Census region-based differences

For the same type of gastrointestinal cancers, the trends of mortality rate change across different census regions in the United States showed overall consistency from 1999 to 2023. In 2023, the AAMR of CRC was the highest in the South at 21.13 (95% CI: 20.84 to 21.41), followed by the Midwest at 20.13 (95% CI: 19.75 to 20.50) and the West at 18.31 (95% CI: 17.97 to 18.65). The Northeast had the lowest AAMR of CRC, at 17.31 (95% CI: 16.94 to 17.68). Compared with other regions, the Northeast exhibited the lowest AAPC of CRC, at -3.08 (95% CI: -3.18 to -2.98). From 1999 to 2009, the APC of CRC in the Northeast was − 3.75 (95% CI: -4.18 to -3.48), and the APC from 2009 to 2023 was − 2.60 (95% CI: -2.79 to -2.34). Notably, the AAMR of LC in the South increased significantly from 7.18 (95% CI: 6.97 to 7.39) in 1999 to 11.08 (95% CI: 10.88 to 11.28) in 2023, with an AAPC of 1.82 (95% CI: 1.71 to 1.95). In contrast, the AAMR of GC in the Midwest decreased from 6.37 (95% CI: 6.12 to 6.61) in 1999 to 3.41 (95% CI: 3.25 to 3.56) in 2023, with an AAPC of -2.59 (95% CI: -2.76 to -2.40) (Fig. 4 and Fig. S19-S24). CMR for the six gastrointestinal cancers increased with advancing age across all four census regions, with the most substantial rise observed in the Midwest. Notably, adults aged 85 years and older in the Midwest had the highest CRC mortality rate, with a CMR of 179.43 (95% CI: 172.24 to 186.63) (Fig. 5; Table S1).

States stratified

From 1999 to 2023, there were significant and diverse differences in AAMR across different states. Overall, CRC is a type of cancer that has remained stable in a state of high mortality for a long period. In 2023, the states with CRC-related AAMR in the ≤ 10th percentile were Massachusetts (AAMR: 14.88, 95% CI: 13.87 to 15.88), Connecticut (AAMR: 15.51, 95% CI: 14.10 to 16.92), New York (AAMR: 16.35, 95% CI: 15.73 to 16.97), New Hampshire (AAMR: 16.42, 95% CI: 14.11 to 18.73), and Colorado (AAMR: 16.63, 95% CI: 15.40 to 17.86). Additionally, the states with CRC-related AAMR in the ≥ 90th percentile were Louisiana (AAMR: 24.43, 95% CI: 22.78 to 26.08), West Virginia (AAMR: 25.40, 95% CI: 22.88 to 27.92), Oklahoma (AAMR: 25.45, 95% CI: 23.64 to 27.26), Kentucky (AAMR: 26.91, 95% CI: 25.20 to 28.62), and Mississippi (AAMR: 27.34, 95% CI: 25.20 to 29.47). From 1999 to 2023, the AAMR in all states of LC showed a significant upward trend. Among them, Oregon had the highest AAPC of 3.20 (95% CI: 2.70 to 3.92), while New York had the lowest AAPC of 0.36 (95% CI: 0.20 to 0.53). Conversely, the AAMR among all states of GC showed a significant downward trend from 1999 to 2023, with the highest AAPC of -1.37 (95% CI: -2.09 to -0.67) in Oklahoma and the lowest of -4.62 (95% CI: -5.24 to -4.07) in Hawaii, with significant differences in interstate trends (Fig. 6; Table S2-S7).

Future projection

By 2040, based on the CDC WONDER database, the AAMR for EC, GC, BTC, and CRC in the United States is projected to continue declining, with GC showing the largest decrease (63.57%), followed by CRC (47.71%). The AAMR for GC and CRC decreased from 7.16 to 32.06 per 100,000 population in 1999 to 2.61 and 16.77 per 100,000 population in 2040, respectively. In contrast, the AAMR for LC and PC increased from 6.95 to 16.41 per 100,000 population in 1999 to 11.05 and 17.29 per 100,000 population in 2040, respectively. Additionally, the AAMR for both EC and BTC is anticipated to decrease slightly from 2024 to 2040. Notably, except for BTC, the AAMR for the five major gastrointestinal cancers will consistently be higher in male than in female from 2024 to 2040. The AAMR for EC in male is projected to be 8.21 per 100,000 population by 2040, while the AAMR for female is projected to be 1.64 per 100,000 population, representing a difference of approximately 5.0 times (Fig. 7; Table S8-S13). Furthermore, we utilized data from the 2021 GBD database to project mortality rates for gastrointestinal-related cancers, and the observed trends were largely consistent with those derived from the CDC database (Fig. S25; Table S14-S19).

Discussion

In this study, we systematically analyzed the disparities and trends in mortality rates of six gastrointestinal cancers in the United States and projected the trends of these cancers over the next 18 years. Our results indicate that from 1999 to 2023, with the exception of LC and PC, the AAMR of the other four gastrointestinal cancers have shown a declining trend. Overall, among gastrointestinal cancers in the United States, CRC accounted for the highest number of deaths, while BTC accounted for the fewest. Furthermore, mortality rates of all gastrointestinal cancers were significantly associated with race and ethnicity, age, region, and state. Concerning sex differences, AAMR were higher in males than in females for all gastrointestinal cancers except BTC. These findings provide accurate and comprehensive information for optimizing surveillance and prevention strategies for gastrointestinal cancers.

Esophageal cancer

Our study found that in the United States, the AAMR for EC in men was approximately 4 times higher than that in women in 2023, which may be closely associated with high-risk factors for EC. A global study indicate that smoking and alcohol consumption are the primary risk factors for EC: the proportion of disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) attributable to smoking was 50.1% in men versus only 11.3% in women, while the proportion of DALYs attributable to alcohol consumption was 29.6% in men and 5.1% in women [22]. Another study on alcohol-attributable cancer burden revealed that the number of EC deaths attributable to alcohol in the United States was roughly 3.56 times higher in men than in women [23]. Data from the GBD Study 2021 showed that smoking accounted for the highest proportion of EC deaths, at approximately 48.98% [24]. It is hypothesized that the higher susceptibility to cancers of common anatomical sites in men stems from interconnected biological differences between the sexes [25]. Multiple studies have confirmed that estrogen may reduce the risk of EC in women [26, 27]. Notably, our study also found that the AAMR for EC showed a consistent downward trend in Black populations, while EC mortality remained relatively stable in White populations. We hypothesize that these disparities are driven by changes in EC histological subtypes and risk factors. One study observed that the incidence of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) declined most significantly in Black men, with an AAPC of -7.6 [28]. Meanwhile, the incidence of esophageal adenocarcinoma is rising and has become the more prevalent EC subtype in the United States, particularly among White populations [29]. Additionally, ESCC is highly associated with heavy smoking and alcohol consumption, but effective public health initiatives and improved educational attainment have reduced exposure to these risk factors [30, 31]. This complexity underscores the need for more effective approaches to address gender and racial disparities.

Gastric cancer

The mortality rate of GC in the United States showed a significant downward trend from 1999 to 2023. Notably, the mortality rate among White individuals was significantly lower than that among other racial groups, which is mainly attributed to disparities in Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection and unequal access to healthcare resources. From 2000 to 2019, all ethnic groups in the United States exhibited an overall decline in GC mortality rate, with White individuals having the lowest AAMR [32]. Helicobacter pylori is globally recognized as the most prominent cause of GC [33]. Studies have shown that the positive detection rate of H. pylori among NH Black individuals was the highest, reaching 40.2%, while that among NH White individuals was only 20.1% [34]. A nationwide study observed that NH White individuals had the lowest seropositivity rate, at merely 21% [35]. Another retrospective cohort study involving 18,984 GC patients found that the overall survival of Black individuals was 13% lower compared with that of NH White individuals [36]. Such disparities may also be ascribed to unequal healthcare resources across different racial groups, particularly in communities with socioeconomic disadvantages and a high proportion of Black residents, where healthcare resources are relatively scarce [37].

Liver cancer

In recent years, LC has exhibited the fastest upward trend in mortality rate among gastrointestinal cancers in the United States, primarily driven by risk factors such as non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), alcohol consumption, and viral hepatitis infections. From 1999 to 2020, the mortality of hepatocellular carcinoma in the United States increased rapidly, with an AAPC of 1.8% [38]. Within the global spectrum of chronic liver diseases, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) has become the most common type of chronic liver disease, posing a significant public health burden [39]. NASH is the fastest-growing cause of LC, followed by excessive alcohol consumption [24]. A prospective study of asymptomatic middle-aged Americans showed that the prevalence of NAFLD among middle-aged individuals was 38%, and the carcinogenic risk of NASH is particularly prominent in populations with a high prevalence of metabolic syndrome [40]. Between 2000 and 2021, the mortality rate of alcoholic liver disease in the United States increased by 79%, with 21,860 deaths recorded in 2021—indicating that alcoholic liver disease remains one of the major etiologies of LC [41]. Additionally, hepatitis C virus is another key cause of LC, primarily transmitted through high-risk behaviors such as intravenous drug use [24]. Notably, alcohol intake also increases the all-cause mortality rate in patients with chronic viral hepatitis [42]. Meanwhile, we observed that the southern United States and Oregon exhibit the steepest increase in LC mortality. We recommend conducting targeted screening for NAFLD, covering middle-aged populations and high-risk groups such as those with obesity or diabetes; strengthening health education on alcohol-related harms; and expanding the coverage of hepatitis C virus testing.

Biliary tract cancer

Among the gastrointestinal cancers, BTC represented the lowest AAMR and number of deaths in the United States. From 1999 to 2023, mortality due to BTC in the United States showed a declining trend, which is consistent with global trends [43]. Gallstones and gallbladder polyps are well-established risk factors for gallbladder cancer. The broader adoption of cholecystectomy in the United States, procedures commonly performed for these conditions, has likely contributed to the decline in gallbladder cancer mortality [44, 45]. Interestingly, our study found that, unlike other gastrointestinal cancers, BTC exhibited a higher mortality rate in women than in men. A recent study further highlighted this gender disparity in gallbladder cancer, reporting an AAMR in 2023 of 0.57 per 100,000 population in men compared to 1.00 per 100,000 population in women in the United States [46]. This disparity may be mainly attributed to estrogen and obesity as identified in recent comprehensive meta-analyses [47, 48]. Specifically, estrogen has been shown to facilitate gallstone formation through various pathways, thereby increasing the risk of BTC in women [49, 50]. A study in the United States reported that in 2021, the age-standardized prevalence of obesity among women aged 25 and older was 45.6%, compared to 41.5% among men, further reinforcing this sex difference [51]. These findings collectively underscore the necessity for targeted preventive strategies against BTC in the female population.

Pancreatic cancer

Mortality of PC in the United States demonstrated a slow but persistent upward trend, ranking second only to CRC. The high mortality rate of PC may be attributed to the lack of early symptoms and reliable biomarkers, resulting in the majority of patients being diagnosed at advanced stages and experiencing delays in treatment [52]. Due to the highly malignant and aggressive nature of PC, the median survival period for patients is approximately 4 months, with a 5-year survival rate of only 13%, indicating an extremely high mortality rate [53]. Furthermore, studies indicate that population aging, along with the rising prevalence of obesity, diabetes, and smoking, may lead to an increase in mortality of PC [54]. Our findings also indicate that mortality from PC is significantly higher among Black individuals compared to other racial groups. The reasons for these racial disparities may be associated with healthcare accessibility. Specifically, due to low income, low educational attainment, and residential segregation leading to inadequate healthcare access, Black patients are more frequently diagnosed with PC at an advanced stage [55, 56]. Disparities also exist in PC care, with Black individuals having lower rates of chemotherapy and likelihood of surgery compared to White individuals [57–59]. Furthermore, another study found that between 2005 and 2020, White patients accounted for 84.7% of clinical trial participants, while Black patients only made up 8.2% of the participants [60, 61]. These findings underscore the imperative to expand healthcare coverage in order to mitigate racial disparities. Furthermore, smoking cessation, limiting alcohol consumption, reducing intake of processed meats and sugary beverages, and maintaining regular physical activity are core measures to effectively reduce the risk of pancreatic cancer incidence. For high-risk groups, targeted screening using endoscopic ultrasound or magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography is recommended to facilitate early detection and intervention.

Colorectal cancer

Our study revealed a consistent declining trend in the AAMR of CRC in the United States from 1999 to 2023. This trend is consistent with findings from multiple prior epidemiological studies [7, 62]. Specifically, CRC mortality in the United States has continued to decrease since the early 2000s, primarily attributed to the widespread adoption of screening programs and advancements in therapeutic strategies [63, 64]. Research has confirmed that the screening rate for CRC increased from 41.5% in 1999 to 76.3% in 2023, and this significant improvement has effectively reduced CRC mortality [65]. Given the proven value of screening in mitigating CRC burden, the United States Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) recommends CRC screening for adults aged 45 to 49 years [66]. To further improve screening adherence and expand coverage, non-invasive detection methods such as multi-target fecal DNA testing have significantly increased the overall screening rate for CRC by reducing invasive procedures [67]. Our study further revealed that the mortality rate of CRC in the southern United States ranks the highest nationwide, with Mississippi having the highest AAMR of 27.34 (95% CI: 25.20 to 29.47). This geographic clustering pattern is closely associated with a high proportion of the Black population and low health insurance coverage in the region [68, 69]. We recommend expanding Medicaid coverage to fully include CRC screening services, so as to improve screening participation rates among high-risk populations.

Limitations

First, the CDC WONDER data are entirely dependent on death certificate coding. However, when recording cancer-specific causes of death, death certificates may have coding errors or omissions, particularly among elderly patients with comorbidities affecting multiple systems. This could lead to inaccuracies in the attribution of causes of death. Second, the CDC WONDER database provides only basic demographic variables such as sex, race/ethnicity, age, and region, lacking data on clinical and socioeconomic factors that significantly influence the mortality rate of gastrointestinal cancer. This makes it difficult to explain the underlying reasons for disparities in mortality rates across different populations or regions. Third, the absence of data on key lifestyle risk factors, including smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and dietary patterns, also limits further attribution analysis of the driving factors behind mortality rate trends. Additionally, to protect privacy and ensure data quality, the database marks subdivided populations with fewer than 10 deaths as “Suppressed”, as seen in specific subgroups like patients aged 25 to 34 with BTC. This exclusion of these groups from Joinpoint regression analysis may result in the omission of potential trend characteristics of BTC in younger populations.

Conclusion

This study systematically analyzed the spatiotemporal trends and demographic disparities in mortality for six major gastrointestinal cancers in the United States from 1999 to 2023, with forecasts extending to 2040. From 1999 to 2023, EC, GC, BTC, and CRC showed declining mortality rates, with GC having the steepest drop. In contrast, the burden on LC and PC continues to rise. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions, including prioritizing LC and PC prevention, expanding equitable screening initiatives, and addressing regional and racial disparities to reduce the gastrointestinal cancer burden in the United States. Our study provides critical insights to guide evidence-based policies and optimize healthcare resource allocation.
