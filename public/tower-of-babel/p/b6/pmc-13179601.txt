The safety of radiofrequency surgical sponges in orthopedic surgery: a validation study in 2,251 arthroplasty procedures
Authors: Jack A Will, Lachlan F Anderson, Steven Ogden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRetained foreign objects (RFOs), particularly surgical sponges, remain a serious yet preventable source of morbidity and litigation. Although radiofrequency (RF)-tagged sponges have shown 100% sensitivity and specificity in general surgery, their performance in orthopedic procedures involving metallic implants remains underexplored. This study evaluates the reliability of RF-tagged sponges in total joint arthroplasty and identifies unique limitations associated with their use in orthopedic surgery.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted of 2,251 total knee and total hip arthroplasty cases performed at a high-volume orthopedic center from April 30, 2024 to February 28, 2025. All cases employed both standard manual sponge counts and RF detection technology. The primary outcome was the discrepancy rate between manual and RF counts, with secondary analysis of false-positive and false-negative events and contributing intraoperative factors.ResultsThree discrepancies (0.13%) were identified. One case represented a false-negative RF scan in which the tag became undetectable due to cement polymerization and proximity to a metallic femoral stem. Two cases involved false-positive detections that resolved on repeat scanning without evidence of retained items.ConclusionsRF-tagged surgical sponges may serve as a useful adjunct for RFO prevention in orthopedic surgery but may be affected by metallic interference and tag damage during implant cementation. The technology should supplement, not replace, manual counts and intraoperative imaging. Further research is warranted to optimize RF performance in orthopedic applications.Keywords: Radiofrequency detection, Orthopaedic surgery, Arthroplasty, Patient safety, Surgical technology

Introduction

Retained foreign objects (RFOs) are among the most serious yet preventable adverse events in modern surgery, representing a persistent threat to patient safety and a source of substantial clinical and financial burden [1–5]. RFOs are defined as any surgical object unintentionally left in a patient’s body after the completion of a procedure [2, 6, 7]. Of these, surgical sponges, which are used ubiquitously across all surgical disciplines, are the most frequently retained [2, 8]. Their soft texture, pliability, and small size make them especially difficult to detect once inadvertently retained in deep or obscured anatomical spaces, potentially leading to adverse outcomes such as local inflammation, abscess formation, and sepsis [6, 9]. These outcomes often necessitate further surgical interventions, resulting in increased patient morbidity, prolonged hospitalization, and a significant decline in postoperative quality of life [10–12]. Prior studies estimate a retained item occurs in approximately 1 out of every 5,500 surgical cases, though actual rates are suspected to be underreported due to medicolegal concerns and variations in surveillance practices [8, 13, 14].

Traditionally, prevention of RFOs relies on manual counting protocols performed by circulating nurses and surgical technologists before, during, and after operative procedures [4, 15]. Despite being mandated and standardized, manual sponge counts remain prone to error, particularly during high stress or emergent cases, prolonged procedures with staff turnover, and situations involving frequent sponge exchanges [13, 16–18]. These limitations have prompted the development of technological adjuncts designed to enhance sponge-tracking accuracy and reduce reliance on human counting alone.

One commonly used method to prevent RFOs is radiofrequency (RF) detection systems [19, 20]. RF-tagged surgical sponges are embedded with a small, passive radiofrequency chip that emits a signal detectable by a handheld wand [20, 21]. Before wound closure, the surgical team can scan the operative field and surrounding areas to identify any retained RF-tagged sponges [20, 21]. This process is rapid, non-invasive, and independent of manual counting accuracy. Prior evaluations of RF technology have demonstrated strong performance characteristics. In one example of a prospective study of 210 general surgery cases, RF sponge detection achieved 100% sensitivity and specificity, supporting its reliability even in patients with morbid obesity [22].

Despite this data, the adoption of RF-tagged sponges remains variable across institutions and surgical specialties. Most existing research has focused on general surgery, where anatomical exposure differs significantly from that encountered in orthopedic surgery. Orthopedic operations, particularly total joint arthroplasty, provide a unique environment for the use of RF technology, frequently involving deep and narrow surgical fields, bony retraction, and the use of metallic implants and tools [23].

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) and total hip arthroplasty (THA) specifically represent an ideal context for evaluating RF detection systems in orthopedic surgery. These high-volume procedures, with more than 1.3 million performed annually in the United States, involve extensive tissue handling and implantation of prosthetic components, which may increase the risk of retained surgical items [6, 24]. Despite this potential risk, limited data exist evaluating RF tagged sponge performance in orthopedic procedures, particularly in the presence of metal implants and deep joint spaces. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to assess the feasibility and reliability of RF technology in arthroplasty by evaluating the performance and discrepancy rate of RF detection systems during TKA and THA at a high-volume orthopedic center. A secondary objective was to analyze discrepancies between traditional manual sponge counts and RF detection results.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study included patients who underwent TKA or THA procedures at Texas Health Resources Clearfork, a high-volume orthopedic center in Fort Worth, Texas. The study period spanned from April 30, 2024 to February 28, 2025. All included cases employed RF-tagged surgical sponge detection technology alongside standard manual sponge counts.

Eligible patients were identified through EHR using ICD-10 procedural codes for TKA, THA, revision TKA, and revision THA. Inclusion criteria included patients with complete documentation of initial, intraoperative, and final manual sponge counts, as well as documented use of RF detection systems. Cases with missing count data or undocumented RF scanning were excluded.

Per institutional protocol, the sponge count process followed the Texas Health Resources (THR) system-wide clinical specifications for prevention of retained surgical items. This included:

Initial count: Performed before the patient entered the operating room

Initial count: Performed before the patient entered the operating room

First closing: Performed during closure of deep surgical cavities

First closing: Performed during closure of deep surgical cavities

Final/skin closure count: Performed prior to final skin suture being placed

Final/skin closure count: Performed prior to final skin suture being placed

Manual counts were performed audibly and visually by two trained staff members, including at least one registered nurse, and were recorded on a whiteboard visible to the OR team. A “no interruption zone” was maintained during counts to minimize distractions.

In addition to manual counts, RF-tagged sponges were used in all cases. RF scanning was performed using a handheld wand after completion of the final manual count but before skin closure. The RF console and wand provided real-time confirmation of sponge clearance. If the RF wand detected a positive signal, the surgical team halted the procedure to investigate and reconcile the discrepancy. If the item could not be located through manual and RF technology, intraoperative radiography was obtained, per THR policy. Documentation included:

RF scan results

Any count discrepancies or positive RF detections

Any count discrepancies or positive RF detections

Need for intraoperative radiographs

Need for intraoperative radiographs

Primary outcomes included the rate and nature of discrepancies of the RF detection system in comparison to manual counts. All data was extracted from the EPIC electronic health record (EHR) system and reviewed retrospectively. Statistical analysis was performed focusing on concordance between manual and RF counts.

Results

A total of 2,251 cases utilizing combined manual surgical counts and RF detection were reviewed, comprising 1,509 TKA and revision TKA and 742 THA and revision THA, performed between April 30, 2024, and February 28, 2025. Among these, three cases demonstrated discrepancies, yielding an overall discrepancy rate of 0.13%.

In the first case, during a left total knee arthroplasty revision, manual sponge counts were performed during the initial closure but found to be incorrect. A repeat manual count was also inaccurate. The RF detection wand was then passed over the surgical field but failed to detect any RFO signal. Subsequently, the wand was passed over the trash receptacle, where it produced a positive signal, leading the team to believe the missing sponge had been discarded. Closure continued based on this assumption while the trash was examined. The sponge was not produced. Thus, prior to leaving the operative suite, a radiograph was obtained revealing the retained item within the femoral canal, cemented at the base of the femoral implant. The RF-tagged sponge had inadvertently been deactivated by the impaction of the stem or the heat of the cement during polymerization which likely interfered with accurate detection. At that time, a conscious decision was made to leave the sponge in place and return later for its removal.

The second and third cases involved correct manual counts with initial positive RF detections. In both cases, after re-verification of counts, repeat RF scanning produced negative results despite no changes in the number or type of sponges on the field. No retained items were ultimately identified, and no intraoperative imaging was required.

Discussion

This retrospective study is among the first to evaluate the performance of RF-tagged surgical sponges specifically within the setting of orthopedic surgery, focusing on TKA and THA procedures. Our findings provide important insights into the feasibility, performance, and limitations of this emerging technology when used alongside standard manual counting protocols.

Notably, across 2,251 cases, three discrepancies were observed. In the first, a retained surgical sponge was missed despite two incorrect manual counts and a false-negative RF scan. This case illustrates important limitations of RF detection technology under specific intraoperative conditions. It is likely that the orientation of the RF tag relative to the detection wand can significantly affect sensitivity, and proximity to objects such as surgical implants, bone cement, and operating tables can interfere with signal transmission or generate misleading results [25]. In the instance noted in this study, the sponge’s RF device was embedded within the femoral canal near a metallic implant during the cementation process. The exothermic reaction of the cement and the pressure of the femoral stem against the RF tag are both factors that likely contributed to the RF scan’s inability to detect the tag. This also emphasizes the importance of only utilizing the scanning device to scan the operative field and not the trash receptacle which could lead to a false sense of security.

The second and third cases involved initial positive RF detections that resolved on subsequent scans without any changes to the surgical field or count, and no retained sponges were ultimately identified. These episodes suggest the occurrence of false-positive RF signals, which may have been due to transient signal interference or interactions with metallic implants commonly used in orthopedic procedures [25, 26].

While these false positives did not result in unnecessary interventions, they did require additional time for manual count verification and repeat scanning, potentially prolonging operative time and increasing intraoperative workflow complexity. The RF device used was serviced following these two events, and no further false signals have been documented since. However, these episodes highlight a potential pitfall in that malfunctions of the scanning device itself can also occur and should be considered when interpreting unexpected RF detections.

Prior literature has similarly described rare but clinically relevant retained items, including fragmentation of radio opaque gauze requiring removal [27], a retained cotton ball in an orthopedic procedure discovered during revision for infection and delayed wound healing [28], and retained sponges in abdominal surgery associated with chronic fistula formation, attributed to breakdowns in safety protocols [29]. Collectively, these reports emphasize that while RF technology may enhance detection, it should be used as an adjunct to, rather than a replacement for, established counting and safety practices.

Taken together, findings from the present study highlight both the strengths and the limitations of RF technology in the orthopedic setting. However, the presence of false-positive and negative signals emphasizes the importance of continued reliance on manual counting protocols and clinical judgment, rather than sole dependence on technological adjuncts. The findings reinforce the importance of layered safety strategies in the operating room, where technological adjuncts function best when integrated with standardized counting protocols and intraoperative imaging.

Notably, these observations differ from prior studies focusing on general surgery, where RF detection has been shown to achieve 100% sensitivity and specificity, with a 0% discrepancy rate [22]. This highlights how orthopedic surgery potentially introduces unique challenges that are not present in general surgery procedures, including the presence of metallic implants and retractors, deep joint spaces, and hard surfaces such as bone and metal where RF-tagged sponges may be crushed. Our findings suggest that while RF-tagged sponge systems perform well in this environment, surgeons and operating room staff should remain aware of the potential for false-positive or negative alerts and be prepared to use additional diagnostic tools, such as intraoperative radiography, when needed.

This study is limited by its retrospective design and single-center setting, which may affect generalizability. Additionally, the low event rate limited formal calculation of diagnostic accuracy metrics. Intraoperative RF detection outcomes were also dependent on operator technique and equipment calibration. Future prospective studies across multiple orthopedic centers are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusion

This study highlights the both the strengths and limitations of RF-tagged surgical sponges in orthopedic surgery. This technology should be considered an adjunct to manual counting protocols in orthopedic procedures, particularly TKA and THA. Our findings demonstrate that RF technology can fail to detect retained surgical sponges due to potential damage or interference of the sensor from metallic implants or equipment. The occurrence of both false-positive and false-negative signals underscores the importance of using this technology alongside, rather than as a replacement for, established surgical counting protocols and radiographs. As orthopedic surgery presents unique intraoperative challenges, specifically metallic implants, further research is warranted to optimize RF system performance.
