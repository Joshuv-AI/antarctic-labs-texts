Levels of heavy metals distribution near an urban wastewater in Southeastern Michigan
Authors: Matt Chames, Eva N Nyutu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract The Detroit River watershed has been utilized for industrial and recreational activities. Over the past century, the river has undergone significant urban, industrial, and agricultural development. The Detroit River has a long history of environmental quality monitoring. However, due to changes in sampling methods and assessment techniques over the years, it is challenging to determine whether the river’s condition has improved. The absence of baseline data to assess the levels of dissolved heavy metals near the Detroit wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) is evident in the literature. This study aimed to evaluate the concentrations of dissolved heavy metals (Cr, Fe, Cu, Zn, As, Cd, and Pb) near the upstream and downstream areas of the Detroit WWTP, allowing for an assessment of heavy metal mobility. The study found that all the sites had higher concentrations of heavy metals in the upstream sites (1, 3, and 4) compared to the downstream sites (5 and 6). However, the mean concentrations of all detected heavy metals at both sites were below the limits set by the US EPA. This information is crucial for identifying at-risk watersheds and can serve as a benchmark for future contaminant reduction strategies and remediation efforts.Subject terms: Freshwater ecology, Environmental impact

Introduction

The Detroit River is a 51 km-long connecting channel that links Lake St. Clair to the western basin of Lake Erie. The Detroit River watershed is home to 4.4-million people and has long been used for industrial and recreational purposes1–3. Over the past 100 years, this river has experienced a large amount of urban, industrial, and agricultural development. The Detroit River and Rouge River stretches more than 126 miles in length before emptying into the lower 20 miles of Detroit River at Zug Island4–7. The Detroit and Rouge River have been designated as binational Great Lakes Area of Concern (AOC) through the Great Lakes Water Quality Board of the International Joint Commission in 1985 and is an economic and social resource1–3,8,9. As industries have become more intensive, and the watershed more developed, water pollution, loss of habitat, and point and non-point source pollution have become a concerning issue in the Detroit River. The lower portion Rouge River watershed has numerous petrochemical plants, steel mills, petroleum refineries, and producers of automotive parts, rubbers, and plastics. The upper portion is urbanized and borders industrial and municipal facilities. Backyard chemical applications, improper disposal of batteries, paint, and storm water runoff from a variety of impermeable surfaces, are additional sources of pollution in urban environments1–3,8–14,.

Heavy metals have been a persistent and significant pollutant in communities with a history of industrialization; like the Detroit and Rouge River Watershed of Southeastern Michigan. Heavy metals are of particular concern because they are less visible, their effects on the ecosystem are intensive, they have toxic effects and bioaccumulate in aquatic ecosystems15–20. Previous studies have shown that heavy metals such as chromium (Cr), copper (Cu), iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), cadmium (Cd), and lead (Pb) are present in the Detroit and River Rouge watersheds. These metals are associated with both point and nonpoint source pollution and are often found at concentrations that exceed established toxicity guidelines. Additionally, these heavy metals have been detected in the gills, liver, and muscle tissues of various species of fish and aquatic organisms living in contaminated water ecosystems6,7,12,15–17,20. Heavy metals easily adsorb to suspended particles in water (soil, silt, organic debris), settling down in the riverbed, and are later released into the water column, where they become a potential secondary source of contamination, threatening ecosystems7,12,15,16,21,22. These metals may have entered the river through runoff, atmospheric deposition, and the influx of groundwater.

Although the Detroit River has one of the longest records of environmental quality monitoring in the Great Lakes, the state of the river and whether the river is improving cannot be answered owing to changes in sampling designs and assessment techniques across surveys conducted in different years making it difficult to compare results across studies14. Moreover, most studies have assessed the sediment contaminants found in the river6,7,12,21,22. Our review of the literature reveals an absence of baseline data to examine the levels of dissolved heavy metals in the Detroit wastewater treatment plant located in Southeast Michigan6,7,11,12,16,21–23. The Detroit WWTP treats an average of 660 million gallons per day (MGD) (∼2.5 billion L per day) of wastewater. As a combined sewer treatment facility, the influent includes both raw wastewater as well as stormwater. The Detroit WWTP effluent is released into the Detroit River5,7,12,21. Detroit River is the influent to Lake Erie, one of the five Laurentian Great Lakes. All the Great lakes together comprise the largest freshwater system24–26. The nearby Rouge River, which feeds the Detroit River and has an average annual flow rate of 0.5–10 m3 s7,12,21,22.

Therefore, this study evaluated dissolved heavy metal concentrations in the upstream and downstream of the Detroit WTTP thus enabling an evaluation of heavy metal mobility. Such information helps identify at-risk watersheds and serves as a benchmark for future contaminant reduction strategies and remediation efforts. To determine the amount of contamination represented by the measured concentrations of heavy metals (Cr, Fe, Cu, Zn, As, Cd and Pb) in sites near the upstream and downstream of the Detroit WTTP; and to assess the potential public health and ecosystem impacts from any dissolved heavy metal contamination found.

Pb is associated with mining, fossil fuel burning and various manufacturing industries (e.g., batteries, ammunition, glass, paints, coatings, and metal products), pesticides, and plumbing fixtures. As is usually found in ground and surface water, mine drainage, pesticide application, wood products, industrial waste, and naturally occurring geothermal discharges arsenical pesticides, natural mineral deposits or inappropriate disposal of arsenical chemicals, smelting, sedimentary and meta-sedimentary bed rocks. Most of the paints, dyes, soaps, metals, semi-conductors and drugs contain arsenic. Certain pesticides, fertilizers and animal feeding operations also release arsenic to the environment7,12,21,22,27,28. Cd naturally exists as a cadmium sulfide ore and is used mainly for electroplating of steel, found in batteries, plastic stabilizer smelting pigments, plastic stabilizers, coatings, pesticides, biosolids, and phosphate fertilizers. Soils and rocks, including coal and mineral fertilizers, contain some amount of cadmium27,28.

Cr is commonly found in nature as a result of the combustion of oil and coal. It is also present in petroleum obtained from ferrochrome refractory material. Cr is used for various applications like pigment oxidants, catalysts, a component in chromium steel, oil well drilling, and metal plating in tanneries. Moreover, human activities, such as sewage disposal and application of fertilizer landfill leachate, wood treatment, smelting, textile manufacture, fungicides can contribute to Cr. Cr is extensively used in industries such as metallurgy, electroplating, production of paints and pigments, tanning, wood preservation, chemical production and pulp and paper production27,28. Zn is associated with car tires, anti-rust chemicals, building sidings, smelting agricultural fertilizers, biosolid waste, and nonpoint source pollution from urban environments. Cu is associated with mining operations, smelting, and structural piping in urban areas27,28.

Heavy metals above allowable limits will often lead to detrimental effects in humans, and other organisms. The harmful effects of heavy metals in humans depends on their dosage, rate of emission, and period of exposure15–17,20. In order to understand the level at which these metals are toxic they should be compared to the allowable concentrations of the specified US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)15,16.

Methods

Sampling sites

Samples were collected in Summer near the Detroit WWTP from the upstream and downstream areas in five different sampling sites that were coded 1 to 5. Site 1: Clinton River mouth (42° 35′ 40.7′′ N, 82° 46′ 01.6′′ W); Site 3: Northeast Belle Isle (42° 35′ 17.1′′ N, 82° 95′ 94.7′′ W); Site 4: Southwest Belle Isle (N 42° 33′ 35′′ N, 82° 98′ 64.2′′ W); Site 5: Rouge River mouth (42° 27′ 52.1′′ N, 83° 11′ 07.6′′ W); and Site 6: Detroit River International Wildlife Refuge (DRIWR)/Trenton Channel (N42° 09′ 97.8′′ N, 83° 18′ 26.0′′ W). The locations were chosen based on proximity to water management facilities (e.g. Detroit WWTPs), as well as importance for recreation and/or wildlife habitat (Fig. 1).

Sites 1–6 upstream and downstream sampling locations.

Collection of water samples

The sampling kits contained one-liter polyethylene bottles that were used to collect samples for heavy metal analysis which were obtained from commercial laboratories. Water samples were collected from Sites 4–6 using a 2-meter polyethylene dipper from a boat, as these sites were deep and could only be accessed with a boat. In contrast, samples from Sites 1 and 3 were collected from the shore of the Detroit River, as these locations were shallower and accessible without a boat. Gloves were changed between each site to avoid contamination and all sampling devices were disinfected between sites using a commercial detergent (Alconox) and rinsed with distilled water. All samples were kept at 4 degrees Celsius for a maximum of 1 h prior to arrival at commercial laboratories for analysis.

Physical–chemical parameters of water samples

Analysis of physicochemical variables at each site, water temperature (Celsius) total dissolves solids (TDS) (ppm), pH, turbidity (turb), and electrical conductivity (EC) (uS/cm), were recorded using a digital water quality flow meter which was lowered 6/10 of the water column.

Rainfall for the month preceding each sampling effort was obtained from a weather station located at Detroit Metro Airport (latitude: 42.2313; longitude: _83.3308) via the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration’s National Centers for Environmental Information.

Analysis of heavy metals in water samples

The analysis of heavy metals was performed by a commercial laboratory (Lumigen Instrument Center, Michigan) using Agilent 7700x ICP-MS. The laboratory analyses were carried out using the ELAN DRC-e model inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) device.

The samples were introduced into an Agilent 7700x ICP-MS by a glass concentric nebulizer (Micro Mist, U-series). All data were recorded and processed with the Agilent MassHunter Workstation software (version 5.1). The calibration solutions were 0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 5.0, 10, 50, 100, and 200 ppb. Calibrators were prepared by serial dilution of the standard 10 ppm solution into 2% nitric acid and 0.5% HCl (v/v). The internal standard was prepared to a concentration of 10 ppb in 2% nitric acid and 0.5% HCl (v/v). The internal standard was diluted online to all blanks, standards, and samples. Water samples received were spiked with concentrated nitric acid and hydrochloric acid to a final concentration of 2% HNO3 and 0.5% HCl and were digested overnight before analysis. The samples were prepared following the procedure defined in U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) method 3005 A32.

This study evaluated the impact of heavy metals near the upstream and downstream of the Detroit WWTP. In the current study, conductivity was highest for samples obtained from site 3 (upstream) 929 µS/cm (Table 1). This value was higher than the range for conductivity of river water which is between 400 and 600 µS/cm. The conductivity values for the water samples from the remaining sites (Table 1) had conductivity levels that were within the WHO standards33.The source of the conductive ions may have been introduced in the river from dissolved ions which came from the dissolved substances including pollutants from nearby industries hence increasing conductivity. Previous studies have shown that domestic effluents discharge into the river which can increase conductivity16,34. Moreover site 3 also had high turbidity levels which might be due to the road construction activities near this site, nearby industries may have contributed to increased particles and ions in the waste water channels hence accounting for the raised turbidity and conductivity levels. It was noted that increased turbidity corresponded to increased conductivity and vice versa (Table 1). During the wet season, urban run-off can introduce high load of suspended matter into the rivers35–38. In this study we had to postpone our sampling days due to heavy rains that occurred 2 days before we went out to collect our water samples. The temperature of water samples ranged from 17.90 to 29.00 °C (Table 1), which was within the recommended range of 20 to 32 °C33. The pH values showed that the water was alkaline (7.80–9.00). The water pH was within the safe limit USEPA recommends that municipal drinking water suppliers keep their water supply at a pH of 6.5 to 8.531,39The concentrations levels of Cr, Fe, Cu, Zn, As, Cd, and Pb were below the limits set by US EPA31. Copper and Cadmium were not detected in all sites.

Previous studies have shown that heavy metal concentration values were higher during the wet season as compared to the dry season. This can be attributed to anthropogenic runoff from land into the rivers during the wet season35–38. We collected our samples during the hot dry summer month end of June and July and rainfall did occur two days before we collected our water samples. This might have been the reason why all of the heavy metals that were detected in both sites had mean concentrations below the limits set by US EPA31. Several other studies conducted in the heavily urbanized region in the Detroit river, by Refs5,7., reported occurrence of heavy metals in near surface and subsurface soil within the Rouge River watershed and found strong elevated concentrations of Cd, Cr, Cu, Pb, Ni, and Zn in surface soil at industrial sites compared with commercial and residential properties and very high concentrations of Pb in surface soil at industrial and residential sites. Soils contaminated with metals have pathways to the river. Storm runoff can sweep surface soils adsorbed with metals into the river40,41. Surface and near-surface soils throughout the Detroit and Rouge River watershed contain various metals; however, their mobility in soil and groundwater is limited due to processes such as cation exchange and sorption onto mineral grains7,12. The concentrations of both naturally occurring and anthropogenically introduced metals in near-surface soils can migrate to shallow groundwater and can vary significantly due to the physical and chemical processes that occur across different geographic regions7,12. The Rouge River acts as an effluent stream, with nearly 100% of its base flow derived from groundwater. Most of the sediment transported and deposited by the river consists of fine to medium-grained sand. Contaminated groundwater, which serves as a nonpoint source of pollution within the Rouge River Watershed, plays a significant role in the transportation and deposition of heavy metals in the stream sediments of urban river systems7,12. In this study all the sites had higher concentrations in the upstream sites1,40, and24 compared to the downstream sites (5 and 6). Location 5 lies just downstream of the Detroit and Rouge Rivers’ convergence, while location 6 lies just upstream.

The origin of element concentration in these sites can be attributed to high concentration of the trace metals in Rouge River. The 202-km Rouge River is a major tributary of the Detroit River. As industries expanded along the shores of these rivers and as these watersheds grew into major metropolitan areas, the health of these rivers rapidly declined and both were identified as Great Lakes Areas of Concern by the International Joint Commission in 198529. The Rouge River drains in Detroit River at Zug Island, a privately-owned industrial zone which includes high coal piles, smokestacks, and burning gas flares. Numerous large industries include steel mills, petroleum refineries, electrical power generating plants, manufacturers of chemical, automotive parts, rubber products, salts and plastics are located along the Michigan shore of the Detroit River close by the Detroit WWTP28,42. During the 1960 s, the Rouge River was considered one of the most polluted streams in Michigan and the Detroit River was considered one of the most polluted aquatic ecosystems in the United States28,42. Due to oil spills; the Rouge River catching fire from unabated oil discharges; discharges from industries and municipalities that were not adequately regulated; wastewater treatment plants only providing primary treatment with disinfection; and untreated wastewater from combined sewer overflows5,25,28,43. Overall, more than 50% of the land use in the basin is residential, commercial, or industrial. The remaining land use is primarily farmland or is undeveloped. The eastern portion of the catchment is intensely urbanized and includes the city of Detroit. Previous studies have reported that six heavy metals, Cr, Cu, Fe, Ni, Pb, and Zn occur in sediments near the areas where the Rouge River and Detroit river at concentrations which frequently exceed toxicity-based guidelines5–7,21,44.

Our results demonstrate that even though the water near the upstream had higher concentration levels than the downstream of the Detroit WWTP. All of the heavy metals that were detected in both sites had mean concentrations below the limits set by US EPA31. Several factors could account for this notably there has been extensive sediment clean-up activities that have been ongoing in the upstream and downstream of various locations of the Detroit River14,45. Another factor could be that these lower levels of heavy metals might be attributed to the city of Detroit’s industrial pre-treatment program and development of the remedial action plan. The general decline of industrial activity in the Detroit area, the concentrated efforts of industries to reduce discharges through pollution prevention, and a dry rain year46. Moreover, previous studies have shown that some metals like Cu and Cd may have existed in form of metal complexes that did not dissociate on the column and these are more likely to be associated with colloidal materials and therefore not easily detected38,47,48. Heavy metals tend to be less soluble, immobile and less bioavailable when the pH is basic40. These results suggest that the overall quality of the water near the upstream and downstream of the Detroit WWTP has improved since the early eighties. The decreases in mean metal concentrations, tend to suggest improvement, but it should be noted that maximum concentrations from this study are still confined to the sites near the Detroit WWTP.

Conclusion

This study represents the initial effort to characterize dissolved heavy metal concentrations and their distribution near the upstream and downstream of the Detroit WWTP in an urban watershed in southeastern Michigan. Urbanization and its accompanying industrial activities have contributed to heavy metal impacts across all land use categories (industrial, commercial, and residential). All of the heavy metal mean level concentrations detected at all sites were below the detection limits recommended by USEPA. The quality and safety of the final effluents discharged into the receiving surface water is critical due to the potential exposure to heavy metals by aquatic and human lives. Our sampling methods provide an instantaneous measurement of contaminants at a specific time and location. Our study underrepresents the variability, and the number of heavy metals present in the Detroit River watershed. In the current study sampling included a shorter sampling period that was conducted only during the warm season which might completely have missed some heavy metals, leading to the underestimation of the heavy metal concentrations. Therefore, a minimum of one year of sampling is needed to understand, the heavy metal variation in regard to season, location, downstream distance, and depth.
