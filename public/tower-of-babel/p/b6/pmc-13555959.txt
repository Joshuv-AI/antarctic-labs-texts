Prevalence of myopia among 26,697 school-aged children and adolescents in Taiyuan
Authors: Xiaoxia Zhang, Chunfang Wang, Qiang Su, Zheyi Yan, Rongping Ma, Chunhui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeInvestigation and analysis of the current status and influencing factors of vision screening-detected myopia among school-aged children and adolescents in primary, junior high, and senior high schools across various urban districts of Taiyuan, the capital city of Shanxi Province, to provide directions and recommendations for myopia prevention and control in the region.MethodsA cross-sectional survey study was conducted in March 2025, involving a total of 26,697 primary, junior high, and senior high school students in Taiyuan. Data were collected through questionnaires and non-cycloplegic autorefraction.ResultsThe overall screening prevalence of myopia among school-aged children and adolescents in Taiyuan City was 81.8%. The prevalence of myopia was significantly lower in males than in females (χ² = 45.57, P < 0.001).The screening myopia prevalence rates were 71.0% in primary school, 90.4% in junior high school, and 94.4% in senior high school, while the high myopia prevalence rates were 1.7%, 9.6%, and 17.2%, respectively. In primary and junior high schools, the detection rate of myopia was significantly higher in females than in males (χ² = 15.32 and 17.41; P < 0.001). However, in senior high school, the detection rate of myopia was similar between females and males (χ² = 3.57; P > 0.05).At the primary, junior high, and senior high school levels, female students exhibited a significantly higher degree of SER than male students (P < 0.05).The higher prevalence of both myopia and high myopia was significantly associated with the following factors: prolonged near-work duration (≥ 12 h per day, OR = 1.43), insufficient near-work distance, and parental myopia (at least one parent with myopia, OR = 4.02).ConclusionsIn 2025, the screening-detected prevalence of myopia and high myopia among school-aged children and adolescents in Taiyuan City, Shanxi Province, was notably high. This phenomenon was associated with factors such as near work activities and parental myopia. Furthermore, the detection rate of myopia exhibited an increasing trend with advancing educational stages, and the overall prevalence of myopia was higher in females than in males.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28322-z.Keywords: myopia, high myopia༛children and adolescents

Introduction

Myopia is one of the most prevalent eye diseases that cause visual impairment and a serious public health burden worldwide [1, 2].Currently, the global prevalence of myopia is approximately 28.3%. The prevalence is estimated to increase to 4.76 billion individuals (49.8% of the global population) for myopia and almost 940 million individuals (9.8% of the global population) for high myopia by 2050 [3, 4]. The prevalence of myopia among children and adolescents varies across different regions worldwide and is influenced by age, gender, geography and ethnicity [5].One of the most striking features of myopia is the high prevalence of both myopia and high myopia in some locations in East and Southeast Asia (47.0%), which is much higher than that in Central Europe (27.1%), Central Asia (17.0%) and Central Africa (7.0%) [6].

In 2020, the myopia rate among children and adolescents in China was 52.7%, and it may rise to as high as 84% by 2050 [7]. The myopia epidemic among children and adolescents shows a worrisome trend of high incidence, younger onset age, and accelerated progression. In response, China has implemented a series of national strategic guidelines for myopia prevention and control. Although numerous epidemiological studies have been conducted in China to investigate the prevalence, influencing factors and prevention strategies of myopia among children and adolescents, large-sample and high-quality local data focusing on age and gender-specific myopia profiles as well as associated risk factors remain scarce in Taiyuan, Shanxi Province. In particular, there is a lack of regional evidence obtained via standardized protocols, uniform equipment and consistent refractive criteria. Against this backdrop, the present study collected myopia-related data of school-aged children and adolescents aged 6 to 17 years in Taiyuan in March 2025. We analyzed myopia prevalence across different genders and school stages, as well as the relevant influencing factors. The findings are expected to provide empirical evidence for myopia prevention and control among school-aged children and adolescents.

Methods

Study design and sample

Taiyuan, the capital city of Shanxi Province, has six urban districts. A multi-stage stratified random sampling method was used in this study. In March 2025, 6 primary schools, 4 junior high schools, and 3 senior high schools were randomly selected from each of the six urban districts in Taiyuan. Students were then randomly selected from each grade within each sampled school. This study took myopia prevalence as the primary outcome indicator. Referring to previous national survey data, the expected prevalence was set at 53.6%, with a type I error (α) of 0.05, a statistical power of 90% and an allowable error of 0.05. The minimum sample size required for simple random sampling was calculated to be 383 cases. Given the adoption of a multistage stratified random sampling method in this study, we incorporated a design effect (Deff) of 1.8 for sample size adjustment. In addition, multiple subgroup analyses including gender, school stage, visual behavior and parental myopia were pre-specified at the research design stage. Potential sample loss during the field investigation was also taken into account. All relevant corrections and overall sample planning were completed prior to subject recruitment. A total of 26,697 children and adolescents were finally enrolled, including 13,376 males and 13,321 females, with a mean age of 12.07 ± 3.86 years.

Inclusion criteria: 1.Currently enrolled school students; 2.Aged 6–17 years; 3.Participants voluntarily underwent examinations and completed the questionnaire.

Exclusion criteria: 1.Presence of significant visual impairments (e.g. amblyopia, congenital cataracts, or prior ocular surgery); 2.Current use of orthokeratology lenses. 3.Individuals who declined to participate in the examinations and/or questionnaire survey. (FIGURE 1).

Flowchart of Study Participants

This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, with all participants or their legal guardians providing written informed consent prior to participation. All participating students completed personal information forms including name, gender, age, grade, and glasses-wearing status, while ophthalmologists and optometrists recorded examination results, with all data subsequently collected and archived systematically. For primary school students, written informed consent was obtained from legal guardians along with verbal assent from the students themselves, while both junior and senior high school students and their guardians were required to provide dual signed informed consent. Ethics approval was obtained from the Ethics Committees of the First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University (Protocol KYLL-2025-290). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Procedures

All students underwent visual acuity testing by two certified physicians from our department using LED visual acuity charts compliant with China’s national standards (GB11533 Standard Logarithmic Visual Acuity Chart), with examinations performed sequentially starting with the right eye followed by the left eye. Optometrists performed measurements using an autorefractor (RM-8800, Topcon, Japan) under non-cycloplegic conditions, with each eye tested at least three times and the average refractive value recorded. Statistical analyses were consistently performed using right-eye measurements. Prior to the survey, all participating ophthalmologists, optometrists, and field staff underwent standardized training, with dual data entry performed by two independent technicians to ensure accuracy. All students and their parents jointly completed a detailed questionnaire covering factors such as near work patterns (including duration and working distance), television viewing distance, number of parents with myopia, time spent on outdoor activities, and household income status.

Diagnostic criteria

SER was defined as the sum of spherical power and half of the cylinder. Myopia was defined as a SER of -0.5 D or less. Myopia severity classification: low myopia (-3.00 D ≤ SER ≤ -0.50 D), moderate myopia (-6.00 D ≤ SER< -3.25 D), and high myopia (SER< -6.00 D).

Statistical methods

Original data collection and organization were performed using Microsoft Excel 2019. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 27.0; SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL). Normally distributed continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical data were presented as rates (%). T-tests and chi-square tests were employed to compare myopia differences across gender groups and educational stages. Multivariable logistic regression models were employed to explore factors associated with the prevalence of myopia. All P values were two-sided and were considered statistically significant when the P values were < 0.05.

Overall myopia prevalence among children and adolescents

Among the 26,697 enrolled children and adolescents, 21,827 were screened as myopic (10,723 boys and 11,104 girls). The overall myopia prevalence among children and adolescents was 81.8% (boy: 80.2%, girl: 83.4%). Male students exhibited a significantly lower overall myopia prevalence compared to females (χ²=45.57, P < 0.001). Among the examined students, myopia was detected in 9,285 of 13,072 primary school students (71.0%), 7,085 of 7,842 junior high students (90.3%), and 5,457 of 5,783 senior high students (94.4%). The myopia detection rate was relatively lower among younger school-aged children, demonstrating a statistically significant upward trend with advancing educational stages (χ2trend = 85.53, P < 0.05). (Table 1).

The prevalence of myopia increased from 42.8% in grade 1 to 94.5% in grade 12. The prevalence of myopia among boys and girls increased from 43.9% to 41.6%, respectively, in grade 1, to 92.4% and 96.4%, in grade 12 (Table 2). Besides, Table 2 also shows mean SERs for each educational stages. The mean SER decreased substantially from − 0.92 ± 0.75 D in grade 1 to -4.15 ± 2.17 D in grade 12. Mean SER decreased from − 0.93 ± 0.76 D to -3.91 ± 2.11 D in boys, and from − 0.90 ± 0.75 D to -4.35 ± 2.21 D in girls. (Fig. 2).

Myopia distribution in primary school students

The prevalence of myopia increased progressively from 42.8% in Grade 1 to 87.1% in Grade 6 among primary school students. Female students demonstrated significantly higher myopia detection rates than males (χ²=15.32, P < 0.001), with greater mean spherical equivalent refraction (SER:-2.04 ± 1.54D vs.-1.97 ± 1.52D; t = 2.35, P = 0.019). In grades 1–4, no significant gender differences in myopia prevalence were observed (all P > 0.05). However, girls in grades 5 (χ²=8.74, P = 0.003) and 6 (χ²=32.54, P < 0.001) showed significantly higher detection rates than boys. No significant gender differences in SER were observed at any grade level (all P > 0.05).

The differences in myopia prevalence rates between grades 1–2, 2–3, and 3–4 (16.6%, 11.4%, and 9.7%, respectively) were significantly higher than those observed in other grade transitions. Notably, the differences in myopia prevalence between grades 1–2 and 2–3 were greater in girls (19.2% and 11.7%, respectively) compared to boys (14.3% and 10.9%, respectively).The differences in SER between grades 3–4, 4–5, and 5–6 (0.5 D, 0.4 D, and 0.37 D, respectively) were significantly greater than those observed in other grade transitions. The differences in SER between grades 3–4 and 4–5 were larger in girls (0.54 D and 0.42 D, respectively) compared to boys (0.45 D and 0.38 D, respectively). (Figures 3 and 4).

Myopia distribution in junior high school students

The myopia prevalence among junior high school students increased from 87.8% in Grade 7 to 91.9% in Grade 9. Female students demonstrated significantly higher myopia prevalence than males (χ²=17.41, P < 0.001), with greater mean spherical equivalent refraction (SER:-3.45 ± 1.92D vs.-3.37 ± 2.01D; t = 2.28, P = 0.023). Girls exhibited significantly higher myopia detection rates than boys at every grade level (all P < 0.05). However, no gender differences in SER were observed across all grades (all P > 0.05).

Myopia distribution in senior high school students

Among senior high school students (Grades 10–12), myopia prevalence increased from 93.8% in Grade 10 to 94.5% in Grade 12. No significant gender difference was observed in overall myopia prevalence (χ²=3.57, P > 0.05). However, female students demonstrated greater mean spherical equivalent refraction than males (SER: -4.12 ± 2.14 D vs. -3.97 ± 2.17 D; t = 3.35, P = 0.001). No significant gender differences in myopia prevalence were observed in Grade 10 (χ²=0.002, P = 0.964) or Grade 11 (χ²=0.842, P = 0.359). However, Grade 12 females showed significantly higher prevalence than males (χ²=8.42, P = 0.004). No significant gender difference in SER was found in Grade 10 (t = 0.65, P = 0.517). However, female students demonstrated significantly higher SER than males in both Grade 11 (t = 2.21, P = 0.028) and Grade 12 (t = 3.24, P = 0.001).

Distribution of low/moderate/high myopia

Among the 9,285 myopic cases in primary schools, low myopia accounted for 7,301 cases (78.6%), followed by moderate myopia (1,829 cases, 19.7%) and high myopia (155 cases, 1.7%). Among 7,085 myopic cases in junior high schools, low myopia accounted for 3,398 cases (48.0%), moderate myopia for 3,010 cases (42.5%), and high myopia for 677 cases (9.6%).Among 5,457 myopic cases in senior high schools, low myopia accounted for 1,975 cases (36.2%), moderate myopia for 2,545 cases (46.6%), and high myopia for 937 cases (17.2%).A significant age-related increase in high myopia prevalence was observed among students. Analysis of the prevalence of myopia and high myopia in relation to associated factors

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed with the prevalence of myopia and high myopia as the dependent variables, and their associated factors as independent variables. The results demonstrated that a higher prevalence of myopia was statistically associated with the following factors: female sex (OR = 1.11), higher household income (≥ 5,000 RMB, OR = 1.69), longer near-work duration (8–12 h group: OR = 1.37; ≥12 h group: OR = 1.49), shorter near-work distance, longer time spent on outdoor activities, and parental myopia (at least one parent with myopia, OR = 2.23).

A higher prevalence of high myopia was significantly associated with the following factors: longer near-work duration (≥ 12 h group, OR = 1.43), shorter near-work distance, and parental myopia (at least one parent with myopia, OR = 4.02). (Table 4).

Discussion

Myopia, one of the most prevalent ocular disorders causing visual impairment worldwide, poses a particularly serious public health challenge. High myopia, in particular, can lead to irreversible vision damage through complications including presenile cataract, glaucoma, retinal detachment, choroidal neovascularization, myopic macular degeneration and macular haemorrhage [8, 9]. This growing epidemic has become an urgent global public health issue requiring immediate intervention to protect the ocular health of children and adolescents. According to the 2018 national myopia survey conducted by China’s National Health Commission, the overall myopia rate among children and adolescents reached 53.6%. The screening-detected myopia prevalence rates were 36.0% for primary school students, 71.6% for junior high school students, and 81.0% for senior high school students. Notably, the myopia prevalence among primary school students showed a dramatic increase from 15.7% in first grade to 59.1% by sixth grade. Our research reported a screening-detected myopia prevalence of 81.8% among children and adolescents in March 2025. The myopia rates showed significant progression across educational stages: 71.0% in primary school students, 90.3% in junior high school students, and 94.4% in senior high school students. This study’s screening results, conducted seven years later, reveal significantly higher myopia rates compared to the 2018 national survey data, with primary school students demonstrating the most pronounced increase in myopia prevalence.

Over recent decades, there has been a marked global increase in myopia prevalence, with Asian countries demonstrating significantly higher rates compared to Western nations [10, 11]. Myopia prevalence exhibits significant variations across different geographic regions and ethnic groups. In the United States, the latest estimates show myopia’s prevalence to be 41.6% [12]. In the United States or the United Kingdom with a high proportion of residents whose ancestors immigrated from East or Southeast Asia, the proportion of myopes among people with Asian roots is significantly higher than that among the descendants of people with Caucasians. Australian children aged 11 to 15 years whose ancestors came from East Asia are eight times more likely to develop myopia than children of Caucasian ancestors [13]. China represents a critical area for myopia prevalence, with this study revealing significantly higher rates of childhood and adolescent myopia compared to Western research findings. In Portugal, the prevalence of myopia among adolescents aged 10–18 years (mean age: 12.9 years) was 21.5% [14]. And for 5–7 year-old children, it was 20% in Spain [15]. The prevalence of myopia among children aged 6–10 years in Bulgaria was 14.2% [16]. A UK study reported that the prevalence of high myopia in adults was only 4% [17]. Notably, this study revealed an alarmingly high myopia prevalence of 42.8% among first-grade students (age 6), with even more severe rates observed in higher grades.

Studies have indicated that the high prevalence of myopia is associated with both genetic factors and environmental influences. Parental myopia significantly increases the risk of myopia in children. Furthermore, the prevalence of myopia rises with age and educational stage [18–20], accompanied by a corresponding increase in the prevalence of high myopia. Consistent with our findings, children with at least one myopic parent had a significantly higher prevalence of both myopia and high myopia than children whose parents were both non‑myopic. Age stands as the most critical parameter in global myopia epidemiological analyses. In this study, the prevalence of myopia increased from 42.8% in grade 1 to 94.5% in grade 12. High myopia prevalence demonstrated a progressive escalation across educational stages: 1.7% in primary schools, 9.6% in junior high schools, and 17.2% in senior high schools. Environmental factors associated with myopia, such as prolonged screen time [21], increased near-work intensity, and reduced outdoor activity [22], can elevate the prevalence of myopia among children and adolescents, especially for moderate and high myopia [23–27]. (Table 4).

Household income has also been identified as a factor associated with myopia. Individuals with better living conditions tend to have higher educational attainment, which in turn relates to a higher prevalence of myopia. Our study similarly demonstrated this association: adolescents from households with higher income had a 1.64-fold higher risk of myopia compared with those from lower-income households.

The study findings align with existing literature [28–31] demonstrating significant gender differences in myopia distribution during primary school years, with girls showing higher prevalence rates than boys in higher grades. Specifically, our results revealed markedly higher myopia detection rates among female students in both grade 5 (χ²=15.32, P < 0.001) and grade 6 (χ²=17.41, P < 0.001). However, no statistically significant difference was observed between genders in grades 1–4 (P > 0.05). The underlying reasons may be that girls are more engaged in study and spend less time in outdoor activities, leading to a higher prevalence of myopia in girls than in boys. This difference may also be related to gender-related developmental disparities: girls experience faster pubertal growth and ocular development, and their eyes are more susceptible to environmental influences. Thus, girls are more prone to developing myopia when exposed to increased visual load [32].

Li’s research [17] suggests that 8–9 years of age is a critical period for the onset and progression of myopia. Moreover, the earlier the onset of myopia, the faster its progression, and the higher the risk of developing high myopia or pathological myopia [33, 34]. This inverse relationship between age and progression rate suggests early primary school years may represent a critical window for intervention. Although our study adopted a cross-sectional design rather than a longitudinal analysis, our findings revealed that the differences in SER between grades 3–4, 4–5, and 5–6 (0.5 D, 0.4 D, and 0.37 D, respectively) were greater than those in other grade transitions. Similarly, the differences in myopia prevalence between grades 1–2, 2–3, and 3–4 (16.6%, 11.4%, and 9.7%, respectively) were also higher than in other grade transitions. These observations indirectly support the findings reported by Li et al. With the advancement of technology and the widespread use of digital devices in China in recent years, the onset of myopia has been occurring at increasingly younger ages. Even preschool children are exhibiting premature emmetropization, contributing to a growing trend of early-onset myopia. Young children are in a critical period susceptible to myopia onset and progression. Therefore, it is essential to capitalize on this key phase for myopia prevention and control, thereby reducing the prevalence of myopia among children and adolescents and ultimately minimizing the risk of high myopia or pathological myopia development.

This study has several limitations. First, information regarding daily near-work time, outdoor activity time, and working distance was collected via questionnaire, representing self-reported data that may be subject to recall bias. Participants might not accurately recall their daily visual behaviors, potentially introducing systematic or random measurement errors. To minimize recall bias, all investigators received standardized training and used uniform instructions before the survey; questionnaire items were concise and clear, requiring participants only to report visual behaviors and outdoor activities during the most recent week to shorten the recall period; and obviously illogical data were checked and corrected on-site during the investigation. Despite these measures, residual recall errors may still exist, which could slightly weaken or overestimate the strength of the association between visual behaviors and myopia. Nevertheless, the large sample size and stable trends of the results ensure the reliability of the core conclusions. Second, the key limitation of this study is that all refractive measurements were performed without cycloplegia. School-aged children and adolescents have active ocular accommodation, and physiological accommodative tension, accommodative lead and even accommodative spasm are prevalent in this population. Under such circumstances, automated refraction inevitably causes a myopic shift in the measured values. Additionally, this study lacked auxiliary indicators including axial length and corneal curvature for verification. These issues directly lead to overestimation of the overall myopia prevalence and spherical equivalent refraction, and may result in misclassification of low myopia as moderate or high myopia. Such measurement biases are particularly prominent among younger children, individuals with heavy near-work loads and those with unstable accommodation. Consequently, the accuracy of myopia severity stratification is compromised, and the odds ratios generated in the risk factor analyses for myopia and high myopia are also affected accordingly. Although the myopia grading in this study fails to meet the standards for clinical diagnosis, the overall trend of myopia prevalence, as well as its distribution across genders and school stages, and the general association trends of relevant risk factors still possess important epidemiological reference value. This is a cross-sectional observational study without long-term follow-up or myopia intervention measures; therefore, longitudinal analyses could not be performed. In future research, we will conduct small-sample, high-precision investigations, supplement ocular biometric indicators including axial length and corneal curvature, and continuously monitor the myopia status of primary and secondary school students in this city.

Conclusions

In 2025, the screening-detected prevalence of myopia and high myopia among school-aged children and adolescents in urban Taiyuan, Shanxi Province, was notably high, with a higher prevalence observed in females than in males. The burden of myopia worsened with advancing educational stages. Therefore, it is recommended to reduce time spent on near work (both academic and recreational), increase time outdoors, and maintain adequate working distances during near activities to decrease the incidence of myopia in children and adolescents, thereby reducing the risk of developing high myopia or pathological myopia.
