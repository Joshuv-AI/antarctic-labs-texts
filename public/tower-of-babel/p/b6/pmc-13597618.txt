ORMDL3 as Key Regulator of Endothelial Dysfunction in Atherosclerosis via Sphingolipid Biosynthesis
Authors: Liyuan Wang, Yan Yun, Yu Song, Diming Zhao, Jinzhang Li, Shouji Zhang, Zhengjun Wang, Xiaochun Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ORMDL3 (ORMDL sphingolipid biosynthesis regulator 3) gene has been genetically and functionally linked to inflammatory conditions and atherosclerosis (AS). The aim of this study is to elucidate the regulatory role of ORMDL3 in endothelial inflammation, lipid homeostasis, and apoptosis within the context of AS. A recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) encoding the D377Y mutant of human proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (rAAV/D377Y-mPCSK9) was used to induce atherosclerosis in mice and to assess the role of ORMDL3 in atherogenesis and vascular inflammation. Mechanistic studies were conducted using human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) to evaluate the effects of ORMDL3 on endothelial inflammation, lipid metabolism, and apoptotic signaling. ORMDL3 expression was found to be elevated in aortic atherosclerotic lesions and in HUVECs exposed to pro-atherogenic stimuli. Genetic deletion of Ormdl3 in mice reduced plaque formation and vascular inflammation. In vitro, Ormdl3 knockdown suppressed the expression of adhesion molecules and proinflammatory cytokines, whereas its overexpression enhanced these responses, potentially through activation of the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway. ORMDL3 deficiency attenuated lipid accumulation, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and apoptosis through upregulation of ABCA1. Lipidomic profiling indicated increased ceramide and dihydroceramide levels in ORMDL3-deficient cells. Pharmacological inhibition of ceramide biosynthesis partially reversed ABCA1 upregulation, suggesting a lipid-mediated regulatory mechanism. Additionally, miR-34a-5p binds directly to ORMDL3 mRNA, downregulates its expression, and suppresses endothelial inflammation and apoptosis. Inflammatory stimulation led to downregulation of miR-34a-5p, indicating a feedback loop in ORMDL3 regulation. ORMDL3 contributed to endothelial dysfunction and plaque instability by modulating ABCA1-mediated lipid efflux, ER stress, inflammation, and apoptotic pathways. The regulatory involvement of ceramide biosynthesis and the miR-34a-5p/ORMDL3 axis provides additional insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying ORMDL3-mediated atherogenesis. Therapeutic strategies targeting ORMDL3 may enhance endothelial function and promote plaque stabilization in patients with AS. Introduction

Coronary artery disease and cerebrovascular atherosclerotic diseases currently represent the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. Atherosclerosis (AS) serves as the common pathological foundation for coronary artery disease, ischemic stroke, aortic aneurysm, and peripheral arterial disease, making it a major public health concern [2]. Insights into the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying AS have been partially obtained through studies in murine models, involving diverse cell types and molecular pathways [3, 4]. Genetic association studies have played a key role in identifying susceptibility genes and loci, thereby enhancing the understanding of inherited predisposition to AS [5]. A growing body of evidence underscores the central role of chronic lipid-induced inflammation in the initiation and progression of atherosclerotic plaque development [6, 7]. Over the past few decades, experimental research has elucidated molecular and cellular inflammatory pathways that modulate various aspects of plaque biology and the thrombotic complications associated with AS [8]. The translation of these mechanistic insights into clinical practice has significantly advanced both diagnostic tools and therapeutic approaches for AS [9].

Since its initial identification in association with asthma in 2007, the ORMDL3 (ORMDL sphingolipid biosynthesis regulator 3) gene has been reported to be genetically linked to a range of immune-mediated chronic inflammatory diseases, including systemic lupus erythematosus, ulcerative colitis, type 1 diabetes mellitus, AS, and Crohn’s disease (CD), across multiple ethnic populations [10–12]. These findings indicate the presence of shared genetic susceptibility among chronic inflammatory conditions. Recent evidence has demonstrated a significant genetic association between two single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) located within the chromosome 17q21 region encompassing the ORMDL3 gene and increased susceptibility to coronary AS, along with elevated ORMDL3 expression in two independent Chinese cohorts [13]. The ORMDL3 gene, a member of the ORMDL gene family (ORMDL1–3), comprises five exons and encodes a conserved endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-anchored protein. The resulting ORMDL3 protein consists of 153 amino acids and is ubiquitously expressed in both adult and fetal tissues [10]. In yeast, Orm proteins act as negative regulators of serine palmitoyltransferase (SPT), the rate-limiting enzyme in the initial step of sphingolipid biosynthesis [14]. Functional studies have recently highlighted the regulatory role of ORMDL3 in modulating inflammatory responses and ER stress [11, 12, 15]. However most studies exploring the relationship between ORMDL3 and inflammation have predominantly focused on its role in asthma.

ORMDL3 has been identified as a key contributor to airway inflammation and remodeling, exerting modulatory effects on inflammatory responses in airway epithelial cells, smooth muscle cells, mast cells, and eosinophils [16–18]. The potential mechanisms through which ORMDL3 influences airway inflammation may involve disruption of ER calcium homeostasis, activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR), and induction of cellular stress pathways [11, 19]. Previous research has reported elevated ORMDL3 expression in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of patients with coronary AS [13]. In addition, oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) increases ORMDL3 expression in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) in both a dose- and time-dependent manner [13]. Silencing ORMDL3 in HUVECs suppresses ox-LDL-induced secretion of interleukin-17 A (IL-17 A) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα) [13]. Although novel loci within the ORMDL3 gene have been identified in genetic association studies of AS, the primary regulatory mechanisms underlying its involvement remain insufficiently defined.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small non-coding RNA molecules that serve critical functions in the post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression. Increasing evidence has highlighted their key role in the pathogenesis and progression of AS [20]. MiRNAs are involved in multiple stages of AS development, including endothelial dysfunction, foam cell formation, smooth muscle cell proliferation, and plaque destabilization. These regulatory effects are mediated through the modulation of key genes associated with lipid metabolism, inflammatory pathways, oxidative stress, and vascular remodeling [20]. Altered miRNA expression in AS may be influenced by factors such as dyslipidemia, oxidative stress, and shear stress. A comprehensive understanding of the complex miRNA regulatory network and its contribution to AS may offer valuable insights into disease mechanisms and assist in the identification of novel therapeutic targets [21].

In both in vitro and in vivo models, the present findings confirmed that ORMDL3 plays a regulatory role in endothelial inflammation, lipid metabolism, and apoptosis in the context of AS. The influence of ORMDL3 on ceramide biosynthesis, along with the involvement of the miR-34a-5p/ORMDL3 regulatory axis, contributes to endothelial dysfunction. These results support the potential of ORMDL3 as a therapeutic target in atherosclerotic disease.

Materials and Methods

Mice and Diet

Ormdl3⁺/⁻ mice on a C57BL/6 (B6) background were generated as previously described, and homozygous knockout mice (Ormdl3⁻/⁻) were subsequently produced through interbreeding [22]. Genotyping and verification of gene deletion efficiency were conducted according to previously established methods [22]. Male Ormdl3⁻/⁻ mice and their wild-type (WT) littermates (8 weeks old) on a C57BL/6 background were used in all experiments in order to minimize the influence of hormonal fluctuations on lipid metabolism and atherosclerosis progression. Wild-type (WT) littermates served as controls. All mice were housed at a controlled temperature of 21 °C under a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with unrestricted access to food and water. Mice were provided either with a Western-type high-fat diet (HFD; Research Diets, D12492) or a standard chow diet. This study was approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong University (Approval No. (NSFC: NO. 2018-027)). All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines from Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes or the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The method of euthanasia for terminal tissue collection was carbon dioxide (CO₂) inhalation, performed in accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. Death was confirmed by cervical dislocation.

Cell Culture and Treatment

HUVECs were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection and cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂, using Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 100 U/ml penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/cm² in 6-well plates and grown to approximately 80–90% confluence before stimulation. HUVECs were stimulated with oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL; 100 µg/mL; Yiyuan Biotechnologies, YB-002), palmitic acid (PA; 200 µmol/L) (Sigma, P5585-10G), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα; 100 ng/ml) (MCE, HY-P70426A) for various time intervals (0, 6, 12, and 24 h). Myriocin is a well-established serine palmitoyltransferase (SPT) inhibitor, which blocks the rate-limiting step in ceramide biosynthesis. It was used in this study to verify whether the effects of ORMDL3 on ABCA1 expression and lipid metabolism were mediated through the ceramide synthetic pathway. For pharmacological inhibition of ceramide biosynthesis, HUVECs were pretreated with myriocin (10 µM; MCE, HY-N6798) for 2 h prior to ox-LDL stimulation and subsequent analyses. This treatment concentration and duration were based on previous studies demonstrating effective inhibition of SPT activity without cytotoxicity [23].

rAAV/D377Y-mPCSK9 Treatment

To rapidly induce hypercholesterolemia and atherosclerosis on the Ormdl3 knockout background, a recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) encoding the D377Y mutant form of human proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (rAAV/D377Y-mPCSK9) (Penn Vector, University of Pennsylvania) was utilized to induce an LDL receptor-deficient phenotype in both WT and Ormdl3⁻/⁻ mice. The use of this construct to induce efficient LDL receptor deficiency and accelerate atherosclerosis follows the protocol described by Martin Maeng Bjørklund et al. and has been widely adopted in recent lipid-related studies [24]. This model is particularly advantageous when studying genetically modified mice generated on a C57BL/6 background. The rAAV/D377Y-mPCSK9 vector was diluted to a concentration of 2 × 1011 particles in 100 µL of sterile saline and administered via intravenous injection through the tail vein in mice maintained on either a high-fat diet or a normal chow diet.

Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Sigma) and stored in diethyl pyrocarbonate-treated water at − 80 °C. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from total RNA using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, R212). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using 1 µg of cDNA. The expression levels of multiple messenger RNAs (mRNAs), as indicated in the corresponding figures, were analyzed by qPCR with primers listed in Supplemental Table 1. The relative abundance of each mRNA transcript was calculated following normalization to GAPDH mRNA, and results were expressed as fold change relative to the control group.

Western Blot Analysis

HUVECs were lysed in RIPA buffer containing 0.5% NP-40, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-Cl (pH 7.5), protease inhibitor cocktail (MCE), and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride. For western blot analysis of aortic tissues, entire thoracic aortas were dissected, rinsed in ice-cold PBS to remove blood, and homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, No. P0013B) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (MCE, HY-K0013). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected for protein quantification using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, No. 23227). Protein concentrations were determined using a detergent-compatible protein assay kit (Bio-Rad). Cell lysates were separated on Bis-Tris gels and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010). Membranes were then probed with primary antibodies. The following antibodies were used: rabbit anti-ORMDL3 (Abcam, ab211522), NF-κB Pathway Antibody Sampler Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, 9936), rabbit anti-PERK (Cell Signaling Technology, 5683), rabbit anti-phospho-PERK (p-PERK) (Absin, abs137056), rabbit anti-ABCA1 (Cell Signaling Technology, E7 × 5G), rabbit anti-ABCG1 (Abcam, ab52617), rabbit anti-SRA1 (Immunoway, YT7810), rabbit anti-CD36 (Cell Signaling Technology, E8B7S), rabbit anti-LXR (Abcam, ab216691), rabbit anti-IκBα (Cell Signaling Technology, 44D4), rabbit anti-p-IκBα (Cell Signaling Technology, 14D4), rabbit anti-P65 (Cell Signaling Technology, D14E12), rabbit anti-p-P65 (Cell Signaling Technology, 93H1), rabbit anti-CHOP (Cell Signaling Technology, 2895), rabbit anti-ICAM-1 (Abcam, ab222736), rabbit anti-VCAM-1 (Abcam, ab134047), rabbit anti-β-actin (Abcam, ab213262), and rabbit anti-GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, 5174). A goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (Jackson ImmunoResearch) was used as the secondary antibody.

ELISA

The concentrations of TNFα, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and Monocyte Chemoattractant Protein-1 (MCP-1) in the cell culture medium were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Thermo, 88-7346-88, BMS213HS, 88-7399-88), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Flow Cytometry and CCK8 Assay

Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, CK04) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, HUVECs were seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 10³ cells/well), treated as indicated, and incubated with 10 µL of CCK-8 solution for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek). The control group was set as 100% survival.

For flow cytometry, adherent HUVECs were gently detached using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, 25200-056) without phenol red, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in binding buffer prior to Annexin V–FITC/PI staining (Vazyme, A211-01). Single-cell suspensions were treated with Fc receptor blocking solution (BioLegend, 101319) for 15 min and subsequently incubated with the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Vazyme, A211-01) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Flow cytometry was conducted using a Gallios instrument (Beckman Coulter), and the data were analyzed using FlowJo software. Cells positive for Annexin V alone (early apoptosis) or for both Annexin V and PI (late apoptosis) were classified as apoptotic, whereas Annexin V⁻/PI⁺ cells were considered necrotic.

Vector Construction and Transfection

The vector for ORMDL3 gene overexpression was constructed as previously described [22]. Transient transfection with either the ORMDL3 overexpression vector or small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) was performed following established protocols [13].

Dual-Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay

The 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) of the ORMDL3 gene was cloned into the pmir-GLO dual-luciferase expression vector. Both the WT and mutant versions of the ORMDL3 3′UTR, containing the predicted miR-34a-5p binding site, were generated by site-directed mutagenesis using overlap extension PCR. Subsequently, the luciferase reporter vector or control vector was co-transfected into HUVECs along with miR-34a-5p mimics or inhibitors. The luciferase assay, along with the transfection of small interfering RNA (siRNA), miR-34a-5p mimics, and inhibitors, was conducted as previously described [25, 26].

Histology and Immunofluorescence Assay

Mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation, followed by dissection of tissues, which were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Liver tissues were collected for subsequent western blot analysis. Aortic atherosclerotic lesions were quantified using both en face analysis and cross-sectional analysis of the aortic root.

For en face analysis, entire aortas were placed in ice-cold normal saline. Surrounding adipose tissue was removed, and the aortas were longitudinally incised in the thoracic and abdominal regions, as well as the aortic arch. The specimens were stained with 0.5% oil red O (ORO; Yiyuan Biotechnology) for 30 min, and images were acquired using a digital camera (Canon 70D). Lesion areas were quantified as the percentage of the total ORO-positive area using SigmaScan Pro 5 software (Systat Software, Inc.).

For aortic root cross-sectional analysis, hearts containing the aortic root were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound and serially sectioned at 4 μm. Slides were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with ORO and hematoxylin-eosin (H&E; Solarbio, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology) according to standard protocols.

For immunohistochemical staining, frozen cross-sections were treated with 3% H₂O₂ at room temperature for 10 min, followed by blocking with 5% normal goat serum for 1 h. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: rabbit anti-CD68 (Abcam, ab283654), rabbit anti-α-smooth muscle actin (Abcam, ab124964) and rabbit anti-ORMDL3 antibody (Abcam, ab211522). Subsequently, sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ZSJB-BIO, PV-9001) at room temperature for 30 min and developed using the AEC Peroxidase Substrate Kit (#A2010, Solarbio). The proportion of positively stained areas was quantified using Image-Pro Plus software (Media Cybernetics, Inc.). Masson’s trichrome staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Solarbio, G1340).

For immunofluorescence staining, frozen cross-sections were treated with 3% H₂O₂ for 10 min and permeabilized with 0.4% Triton X-100 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 15 min at room temperature. Blocking was performed with 5% normal donkey or goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were then incubated with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-ICAM-1 (Abcam, ab222736), rabbit anti-VCAM-1 (Abcam, ab134047), and rat anti-CD31 (Abcam, ab7388). Secondary antibodies included goat anti-rat IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 647; Abcam, ab150167) and donkey anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488; Abcam, ab150061), applied for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were counterstained with undiluted Fluoroshield Mounting Medium containing DAPI (Abcam, ab104139) and imaged using an upright fluorescence microscope.

Cell Staining for Immunofluorescence Microscopy

HUVECs were prepared for immunofluorescence microscopy as previously described [22]. BODIPY-cholesterol staining was conducted according to previously established methods [27]. Both BODIPY-cholesterol (HY-125746) and probucol (HY-B0388) were obtained from MedChemExpress (MCE).

Statistical Analysis

All results were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student’s t-test with SPSS software (Chicago, IL). Image processing was performed using GraphPad Prism 5 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla). For experiments involving three or more groups, one-way ANOVA was performed followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons where appropriate. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

Results

ORMDL3 Expression in Endothelial Cells is Upregulated in Response to Hyperlipidemia

Endothelial cells play a key role in the initiation and progression of AS by responding to metabolic and inflammatory stimuli. To evaluate the involvement of ORMDL3 in AS, its expression was examined in the aortas of atherosclerotic mice. Western blot analysis demonstrated a significant upregulation of ORMDL3 in atherosclerotic aortic tissue compared to controls (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 1A). Immunohistochemical staining further confirmed elevated ORMDL3 expression within aortic plaques, indicating a potential role in plaque development (Fig. 1B).

To assess whether ORMDL3 upregulation is induced by atherogenic stimuli, HUVECs were treated with ox-LDL and PA. A time-dependent increase in ORMDL3 expression was observed following exposure to both metabolic stressors (Fig. 1C and D, Supplementary Fig. 1B, C). In addition, stimulation with the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α led to increased ORMDL3 expression (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Fig. 1D), suggesting that both metabolic and inflammatory stimuli contribute to its regulation.

These findings indicate that ORMDL3 is upregulated in atherosclerotic lesions and is responsive to key atherogenic factors, supporting its functional involvement in endothelial dysfunction and plaque development.

Deletion of Ormdl3 Attenuated Atherogenesis in Mice

Having observed elevated ORMDL3 expression in atherosclerotic lesions and in response to atherogenic stimuli in endothelial cells (Sect. 3.1), we next sought to determine the functional role of ORMDL3 in atherogenesis in vivo. To this end, we employed the rAAV/D377Y-mPCSK9 model to induce hypercholesterolemia and atherosclerosis in both wild-type and Ormdl3−/− mice. This model enables rapid and efficient atherosclerosis development on the C57BL/6 background without the need for complex crossbreeding, allowing direct evaluation of Ormdl3 deficiency in an established atherosclerotic setting. The efficacy of AAV-mPCSK9 transduction was verified by the marked reduction of hepatic LDLR protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 2). Ormdl3−/− mice infected with rAAV/D377Y-mPCSK9 and fed a HFD exhibited reduced plaque areas in the aorta compared to WT mice receiving the same treatment (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 3), as assessed by Oil Red O staining (Fig. 2B). A similar reduction in plaque burden was observed in the aortic root, as demonstrated by both Oil Red O and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Fig. 2C and D). In addition, significantly smaller plaque areas were detected in the abdominal aorta of Ormdl3⁻/⁻ mice compared with WT controls (Fig. 2C and D).

Several pathological features, such as lipid erosion and local macrophage infiltration, are known to compromise plaque stability and contribute to the occurrence of cardiovascular disease events. Further analysis of plaques in the aortic root indicated increased collagen content in Ormdl3⁻/⁻ mice, as indicated by Masson’s trichrome staining (Fig. 2E). Smooth muscle cell content, an important determinant of plaque stability, was elevated in Ormdl3⁻/⁻ mice, as evidenced by increased expression of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) compared to WT mice (Fig. 2E). Moreover, immunohistochemical evaluation demonstrated decreased infiltration of CD68⁺ macrophages in Ormdl3⁻/⁻ mice (Fig. 2F), indicating reduced inflammatory cell recruitment within plaques.

Pro-inflammatory stimuli and oxidized lipids have been implicated in promoting endothelial inflammation and dysfunction. Activated endothelial cells express adhesion molecules, such as vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) and intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), which facilitate monocyte and lymphocyte adhesion — processes that are critical in the early stages of AS. Atherosclerotic stimuli promote the secretion of MCP-1 by endothelial cells, macrophages, fibroblasts, B cells, and smooth muscle cells, thereby regulating leukocyte rolling and transendothelial migration.

To investigate the involvement of Ormdl3 in endothelial inflammation, immunofluorescence co-staining for adhesion molecules and CD31 was performed in HFD-fed WT and Ormdl3⁻/⁻ mice following AAV-PCSK9 infection. The co-localization of VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 with CD31 was reduced in Ormdl3⁻/⁻ mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 2G and H), suggesting reduced endothelial inflammatory activation in the absence of Ormdl3.

ORMDL3 Deficiency Limits Endothelial Lipid Accumulation and ERS Through ABCA1 Upregulation

Although previous studies have demonstrated that ORMDL3 deficiency significantly reduces lipid deposition and inflammatory infiltration in aortic plaques of mice, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. To further examine these mechanisms, an in vitro model using oxLDL-stimulated HUVECs was used. Genetic manipulation experiments indicated that among the lipid metabolism–associated receptors evaluated—ABCA1, ABCG1, SRA1, CD36, and LXR, only ABCA1 protein expression was significantly upregulated following ORMDL3 knockdown (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 4A). In contrast, overexpression of ORMDL3 led to a selective downregulation of ABCA1 (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig. 4B).

These observations were further supported by BODIPY-cholesterol staining, which indicated a substantial decrease in lipid accumulation in ORMDL3-deficient cells (Fig. 3C), consistent with the phenotypic findings observed in vivo. Importantly, the cholesterol-lowering effect resulting from ORMDL3 knockdown was reversed by treatment with probucol, an ABCA1 inhibitor (Fig. 3C). These data indicate that ORMDL3 deficiency may reduce intracellular lipid deposition by specifically enhancing ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux.

Given the association between excessive intracellular lipid accumulation and the induction of ER stress, this pathway was further examined. Results demonstrated that ORMDL3 knockdown significantly attenuated ER stress in HUVECs (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Fig. 4C), whereas ORMDL3 overexpression exacerbated ER stress responses (Fig. 3E, Supplementary Fig. 4D). Flow cytometry analysis confirmed that ORMDL3-deficient cells exhibited significantly lower rates of apoptosis compared to control cells (Fig. 3F). Collectively, these findings indicate that ORMDL3 deficiency promotes ABCA1 expression, thereby reducing lipid accumulation, alleviating ER stress, and suppressing apoptosis.

Building on the observation that ORMDL3 knockdown in endothelial cells enhances ABCA1-mediated lipid efflux and mitigates lipotoxic stress under hyperlipidemic conditions, further investigation was conducted to determine if ORMDL3 depletion could attenuate endothelial inflammation at the cellular level. To explore the role of ORMDL3 in endothelial inflammation, the expression levels of adhesion molecules VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 were assessed in ORMDL3-deficient HUVECs. Notably, knockdown of ORMDL3 significantly reduced both protein and mRNA levels of VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 (Fig. 4A and B and Supplementary Fig. 5A). In contrast, overexpression of ORMDL3 under basal conditions led to a marked upregulation of VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 at both transcriptional and protein levels (Fig. 4C and D and Supplementary Fig. 5B).

These findings indicate that ORMDL3 may serve as a regulatory factor in the inflammatory response of endothelial cells, although the precise molecular mechanisms remain to be elucidated. To further clarify the underlying pathways, the effects of ORMDL3 modulation on the NF-κB signaling pathway a central regulator of inflammation were investigated. Under ox-LDL stimulation, ORMDL3 knockdown effectively suppressed the phosphorylation of IκBα and P65 (Fig. 4E, Supplementary Fig. 5C). Conversely, overexpression of ORMDL3 significantly increased phosphorylation of both IκBα and P65, as reflected by elevated p-IκBα/IκBα and p-P65/P65 ratios (Fig. 4F, Supplementary Fig. 5D). These results indicate that the regulatory role of ORMDL3 in endothelial inflammation may be mediated through the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway.

To evaluate the impact of altered ORMDL3 expression on inflammatory cytokine production, endothelial cells were subjected to ox-LDL stimulation following ORMDL3 overexpression or knockdown. The mRNA expression and secretion levels of TNFα, IL-6, and MCP1 were quantified using qPCR and ELISA, respectively. Knockdown of ORMDL3 resulted in a significant reduction in both mRNA expression and secretion of TNFα, IL-6, and MCP1 compared to control cells (Fig. 4G and H). In contrast, overexpression of ORMDL3 led to significant increases in both the transcription and protein secretion of these cytokines (Fig. 4I and J).

Collectively, these findings indicate that ORMDL3 plays a modulatory role in the inflammatory response of endothelial cells, potentially via activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, although further mechanistic studies are warranted.

Regulation of ABCA1 Expression by ORMDL3 Through the Ceramide Biosynthetic Pathway

To elucidate the mechanism by which ORMDL3 regulates ABCA1 expression in endothelial cells, comparative lipidomic analysis was conducted between ORMDL3-deficient and control cells. A significant elevation in cellular levels of ceramides and dihydroceramides (DHCer) was observed following ORMDL3 knockout (Fig. 5A). This finding is consistent with existing reports indicating that ORMDL3 negatively regulates the expression of SPT, the rate-limiting enzyme in ceramide biosynthesis. Loss of ORMDL3 function relieved this inhibition, leading to increased ceramide production. No significant changes were detected in other lipid species (Fig. 5B). Further characterization demonstrated a general increase in various ceramide and DHCer species across different carbon chain lengths and saturation states in ORMDL3-deficient cells (Fig. 5C and D).

Given that previous studies have indicated a role for ceramides in promoting ABCA1 expression, pharmacological inhibition of SPT using myriocin was used to assess whether ceramide accumulation mediates the upregulation of ABCA1 in response to ORMDL3 knockdown [28]. miRNAs are recognized as key regulators in various biological processes, including development, cell differentiation, immune responses, and metabolism. Among these, miR-34a has emerged as a key modulator of inflammaging and vascular disease. Atherogenic and hypoxic stimuli have been reported to reduce miR-34a levels and promote the proliferation and migration of vascular smooth muscle cells during AS progression [29]. In this study, miR-34a-5p was found to regulate ORMDL3 expression in endothelial cells (ECs). Specifically, transfection of miR-34a-5p mimics into ECs resulted in downregulated ORMDL3 protein expression, whereas transfection of miR-34a-5p inhibitors led to an increase in ORMDL3 protein expression (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Fig. 7).

Mature miRNAs guide the RNA-induced silencing complex to target mRNAs, typically through binding to complementary sequences within the 3′UTR of the mRNA. TargetScan software was used to predict a binding site for miR-34a-5p within the 3′UTR of the ORMDL3 transcript. To validate the specificity and regulatory effect of this interaction, luciferase reporter assays were performed. The 3′UTR of ORMDL3, containing either the predicted miR-34a-5p binding site (wild type) or a mutated version, was cloned downstream of the luciferase gene in a reporter plasmid. These plasmids, along with miR-34a-5p mimics or inhibitors, were co-transfected into ECs (Fig. 6B). In cells transfected with the WT reporter plasmid, co-transfection with miR-34a-5p mimics significantly reduced luciferase activity, while co-transfection with miR-34a-5p inhibitors increased luciferase activity (Fig. 6C). No significant changes in luciferase activity were observed when mutant reporter plasmids were used, regardless of the presence of mimics or inhibitors.

To examine the relationship between inflammatory stimuli and miR-34a-5p expression, ECs were stimulated with ox-LDL, TNFα, and PA. Quantitative PCR analysis indicated that each of these inflammatory stimuli suppressed miR-34a-5p expression (Fig. 6D and F). These findings suggest that inflammation-mediated downregulation of miR-34a-5p may contribute to the upregulation of ORMDL3 expression and subsequent enhancement of inflammatory responses in ECs.

Previous studies have reported that downregulation of miR-34a-5p promotes apoptosis in osteoarthritis [30]. To examine whether miR-34a-5p influences oxLDL-induced endothelial inflammation and apoptosis, transfection experiments were performed in ECs using miR-34a-5p mimics and inhibitors. Introduction of miR-34a-5p mimics resulted in significant suppression of TNFα, IL-6, and MCP1 mRNA expression, whereas transfection with miR-34a-5p inhibitors led to an upregulation of these genes (Fig. 7A). Consistent with these transcriptional findings, ELISA confirmed that secretion levels of TNFα, IL-6, and MCP1 in the cell culture medium mirrored the changes observed at the mRNA level (Fig. 7B and C).

To determine whether miR-34a-5p modulates endothelial apoptosis through regulation of ORMDL3, further transfection experiments were conducted. Discussion

Inflammation is involved in all stages of atherosclerotic plaque progression and may act in concert with both traditional and emerging risk factors in AS [9]. Endothelial activation and inflammation occur during the early stages of atheroma development, during which ECs interact with accumulated modified lipoproteins and facilitate the recruitment of leukocytes into the arterial intima through the release of chemokines [2]. Endothelial inflammation also promotes coagulation and thrombosis, ultimately contributing to endothelial erosion and the clinical complications associated with atherosclerotic plaques [31]. These inflammatory processes within the endothelium, therefore, represent promising therapeutic targets for disease mitigation.

The ORMDL3 gene has previously been identified and validated as a susceptibility gene for AS in two independent Chinese cohorts [13]. In a northern Chinese cohort, increased ORMDL3 mRNA expression in PBMCs was observed among individuals carrying the risk alleles of SNPs rs7216389 and rs9303277, compared to individuals homozygous for the reference alleles [13]. However, the association between ORMDL3 and coronary heart disease in other populations has not yet been confirmed. Prior findings indicated elevated ORMDL3 mRNA and protein expression in PBMCs of individuals with coronary AS compared to healthy controls [13]. Moreover, in this study, stimulation of ECs with inflammatory agents such as ox-LDL, TNFα, and PA resulted in a time-dependent upregulation of ORMDL3 at both the mRNA and protein levels. Despite these findings, aberrant ORMDL3 expression in arterial plaques has not yet been verified in murine models or human aortic tissue.

Abnormal Ormdl3 expression in ECs may be closely associated with endothelial inflammation and the development of atherosclerotic plaques. A previous study reported that ORMDL3 knockdown enhanced ox-LDL-induced secretion of IL-17 A and TNFα in HUVECs [13]. In this study, overexpression of ORMDL3 in ECs led to increased mRNA expression and secretion of TNFα, IL-6, and MCP-1, whereas ORMDL3 knockdown reduced both the transcriptional and protein levels of these inflammatory cytokines. Additionally, overexpression of ORMDL3 induced the upregulation of VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 in ECs.

Although the role of ORMDL3 in atherosclerotic plaque formation has not been previously investigated in vivo, numerous studies have utilized genetically modified mice to evaluate the function of Ormdl3 in airway inflammation and remodeling. In Ormdl3 transgenic mice, airway inflammation and remodeling were significantly increased compared to WT mice, whereas Ormdl3 knockdown in the lungs attenuated these responses in models of asthma [32, 33]. Similarities in pathological features between vascular and airway remodeling suggest a possible shared underlying mechanism. In Ormdl3 transgenic mice, structural changes in the airways such as airway wall thickening, inflammatory cell infiltration, myofibroblast activation, epithelial injury, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition were reminiscent of atherosclerotic plaque development [34].

Ha et al. reported that ORMDL3 overexpression in eosinophils led to phosphorylation of extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 and nuclear translocation of NF-κB in the context of allergic inflammation [18]. In this study, reduced infiltration of CD68⁺ macrophages was observed in Ormdl3⁻/⁻ mice. Furthermore, co-expression of VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 with CD31 was decreased in Ormdl3⁻/⁻ mice, indicating attenuated endothelial inflammation. These results are consistent with previous findings and indicate that ORMDL3 may regulate endothelial inflammatory responses, potentially via the NF-κB signaling pathway. Further studies, including the use of endothelial-specific Ormdl3 knockout mouse models, are warranted to validate the role of ORMDL3 in endothelial inflammation.

Endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) plays a key role in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases and is mediated through three primary branches: protein kinase RNA-like ER kinase (PERK), inositol-requiring protein 1α (IRE1α), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) [35]. Maladaptive ERS and an unresolved UPR have been associated with increased autophagy, apoptosis, and inflammation in AS [36]. Mechanistically, ORMDL3 may regulate ER stress and apoptosis in ECs, potentially through the PERK/CHOP pathway.

As early as 2012, Miller et al. reported that overexpression of ORMDL3 in lung epithelial cells activated ATF6α [37]. In subsequent work, the same group demonstrated that airway remodeling in Ormdl3 transgenic mice was associated with selective activation of ATF6, without involvement of IRE1α or PERK [32]. Additional in vitro and in vivo studies from the present group confirmed that ORMDL3 mediates B lymphocyte autophagy via the ATF6-Beclin1 autophagy pathway [22]. Similarly, Yang et al. indicated that ORMDL3 overexpression in early diabetes enhanced beta cell proliferation through transcriptional activation of ATF6 [38]. More recently, Li et al. reported that ORMDL3 may negatively regulate antigen-mediated mast cell activation through an ATF6-dependent mechanism [39]. Collectively, these studies support the hypothesis that ORMDL3 modulates ER stress primarily via the ATF6 signaling pathway. Further investigation is warranted to determine if ORMDL3 regulates ER stress in ECs through a similar ATF6-mediated mechanism.

MiR-34a has emerged as a key regulatory molecule in metabolic diseases and exerts its effects through various molecular pathways. Dysregulation of miR-34a has been documented in multiple metabolic disorders, including obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases [40–42]. It has been implicated in the regulation of adipogenesis and lipid metabolism by targeting genes involved in adipocyte differentiation and inflammation [40, 43]. Additionally, miR-34a contributes to the control of glucose homeostasis and insulin resistance through modulation of insulin signaling pathways [44–46]. Its role in hepatic lipid metabolism and the pathogenesis of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease has also been described [42, 47]. In the cardiovascular system, miR-34a influences vascular inflammation and the progression of AS [29, 48, 49]. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of miR-34a as a diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target in metabolic and cardiovascular conditions.

Recent findings have demonstrated that miR-34a binds directly to the UTR of ORMDL3 mRNA, resulting in suppression of its protein expression. Inflammatory stimuli promote ORMDL3 expression, at least in part, by downregulating miR-34a. These observations further elucidate an upstream regulatory mechanism governing ORMDL3 expression in endothelial inflammatory responses.

Future research is needed to explore the molecular regulatory network surrounding ORMDL3 in the context of endothelial inflammation and plaque progression. A deeper understanding of upstream regulators, downstream effects, and molecular interactions involving ORMDL3 may provide new insights into its functional role in atherogenesis. In addition, the potential involvement of other microRNAs and transcription factors in regulating ORMDL3 expression warrants investigation. Elucidation of these regulatory pathways could enhance the understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying endothelial inflammation and plaque development, and may offer new targets and strategies for therapeutic intervention in related vascular diseases.

The present findings suggest a complex compensatory mechanism by which miR-34a-5p differentially regulates endothelial homeostasis through distinct, context-dependent pathways. Although previous studies have identified the role of miR-34a in promoting endothelial dysfunction via suppression of Silent Information Regulator 2 Homolog 1 (SIRT1) and activation of p53, the current results demonstrate that, under specific metabolic conditions, miR-34a-5p concurrently activates a protective axis involving ORMDL3-mediated ceramide biosynthesis and subsequent upregulation of ABCA1 [50, 51]. This dual functionality provides a mechanistic resolution to a previously noted paradox in vascular biology, in which a single microRNA exerts both deleterious and beneficial effects depending on the cellular context.

The temporal dynamics of these regulatory pathways appear to be critical. Acute stressors favor activation of the pro-senescence SIRT1/p53 axis to facilitate clearance of damaged cells, whereas chronic metabolic stress engages the ceramide–ABCA1 lipid homeostatic pathway to support endothelial cell viability. The identification of this ORMDL3-dependent mechanism expands the current understanding of the pleiotropic effects of miR-34a, which previously included regulation of SIRT1 and MTA2 (metastasis-associated protein 2)-mediated vascular aging [50–52]. The current study additionally implicates miR-34a-5p in the crosstalk between sphingolipid and cholesterol metabolism.

This newly identified compensatory pathway provides a molecular basis for endothelial adaptation under conditions of prolonged metabolic stress. These findings suggest that therapeutic strategies targeting miR-34a in vascular disease may require careful consideration of both temporal factors and the specific downstream signaling pathways involved to optimize clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

This study elucidates the key role of ORMDL3 in the pathogenesis of AS, particularly in the regulation of endothelial inflammation, lipid metabolism, and apoptosis. ORMDL3 was upregulated in response to atherogenic stimuli and contributed to endothelial dysfunction through activation of NF-κB signaling, induction of endoplasmic reticulum stress, and modulation of ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux. Additionally, the miR-34a-5p/ORMDL3 axis was identified as a key regulatory mechanism involved in endothelial cell pathology in the context of AS. These findings position ORMDL3 as a potential therapeutic target for mitigating endothelial dysfunction and plaque instability in atherosclerotic disease. Further investigation into the molecular pathways involving ORMDL3 may support the development of novel therapeutic strategies.
