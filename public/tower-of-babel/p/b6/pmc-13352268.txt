Understanding Surface-Grafted Trihalo Alkylsilane Particles for Hydrophobic Cellulosic Fibers
Authors: Naji Majoudi, Dhanush U. Jamadgni, Lianett A. Pineda, El-Houssaine Ablouh, Martin M. Thuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Surface engineering of cellulose and fibrous materials in general underpins many applications of these materials, yet the mechanism by which trifunctionalized silanes hydrophobize the surfaces remains unresolved. Competing models invoke either self-assembled monolayer formation or water mediated deposition of oligomeric species. Herein, we demonstrate that vacuum-assisted chemical vapor deposition of trichloro alkylsilanes on cellulose fibers does not yield the classical self-assembled monolayers. Surface modification with trihalosilanes proceeds via surface water-driven polymerization, with physisorbed water acting as a comonomer to generate oligomeric siloxane clusters. Using tri­chloro­(per­fluoro­octyl)­silane as a model system, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy depth profiling reveals a fluorine-rich surface layer (∼2.5 nm) exhibiting asymmetric, nonlinear concentration gradients that extend only a few nanometers into the substrate. Scanning electron microscopy and wetting measurements corroborate the formation of ultrahydrophobic surfaces with water contact angles exceeding 130°. Variation in silane concentration modulates the oligomer size and penetration depth. These results establish that silane modification of rough, multiscale cellulose substrates is governed by the oligomerization and clustering of the trihalosilane rather than molecular self-assembly, enabling scalable and robust hydrophobization strategies. The stochastic nature of surface grafting on fibers implies that the modified fibers are complex particles given the multiscale asymmetry in order (cellulose in the fibers) and disorder (grafted particles and microfibril organization).

Introduction

The advent of covalently attached self-assembled monolayers as surface modifiers − emerged on the back of trihydroxy alkyl silanol based grafting on solid substrates. This seminal work stimulated a well-established field of technology based on self-assembled monolayers primarily on coinage metals and a variety of anchoring groups. − , Given the state of technology and know-how at the time, it has become necessary to revisit some of these early works with aspects like the role of surface roughness, ,, adsorbed water, ,, and coupled interdependence of substrate–molecule attributes − in predicting the eventual properties of the modified surface. Early controversies like the odd–even parity effect ,,− were shown to be driven by a poor understanding of the role of surface roughness. ,, Critical challenges with trifunctionalized silane monolayers, however, emanate from chemistry, topology, and reaction kinetics. The seminal work by Sagiv on covalently attached self-assembled monolayers for surface modification relied on the hydrolysis of octyl trichlorosilane to the silanol. Considering Flory–Stockmayer theory, , the hydrolysis of trihalosilane reaches the gel point at relatively low concentrations (∼54 μL of water/mmol of silane). Analysis of wetting properties of the resulting surfaces indicates that surface modification with silanes goes beyond the effect of surface chemistry given that contact angles are often higher than those of comparable surfaces like Teflon (for perfluorinated molecules). The propensity of trihalosilanes to polymerize in the presence of water implies that polymeric adducts are generated, and hence, well-ordered, unimolecular monolayers are unlikely to form. , We , and others , have demonstrated that oligomeric adducts with complex morphologies result from these molecules. Multiple transmission–reflection infrared (MTR-IR) spectroscopy revealed that depending on the functional groups on the silane, trifunctionalized alkylsilanes give monolayers of different surface coverage (density) and orientation. The readily hydrolyzable trihalo alkylsilanes form denser oligomerized coatings whose orientation was closest to the expected monolayer, albeit with incomplete surface attachment and buckled orientation. , Trialkoxysilanes, however, give surface coatings with slanted molecular orientation and lower molecule density compared to trihalo analogues. , Analogous or complementary findings have been observed to further suggest that silanes do form complex coatings on the surface of a material. − Despite evidence of oligo/polymerization, no study has been able to ascertain the depth profile or applied surface sensitive X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). We infer that the depth profile likely relates to reaction kinetics, and hence, the stoichiometry and surface topology of the surface being modified should significantly affect the morphology of the coating. Applying silanes to hierarchically assembled cellulosic materials (from cellulose to microfibrils to fibers and then paper), we anticipate that (i) physisorbed water will significantly affect the gel formation in trihalo alkylsilanes and (ii) the multiscale hierarchical assembly of cellulose into paper will present a hierarchical texture onto which rather complex surface coatings can be deposited. For molecules that form single monolayers (∼1 nm thick), a majority of this texture would be irrelevant. For alkylsilanes, however, we anticipate that the oligomeric/polymerized surface coating will be highly affected by the roughness. Adopting the structure proposed by Spencer , and assuming a uniform surface, we anticipate partial attachment of the oligomerized silane (Figure a–c). The tri­chloro­(per­fluoro­octyl)­silane (TCPOS) first contacts the physisorbed water (Figure a) and oligomerizes (Figure b); it attaches to the surface only upon depletion of the capillary and nonfreezing water layers (Figure c). Given that the short Si–O–Si bond distance cannot support well-ordered alkyl chains, , we infer that preoligomerization drives the partial attachment. By partially pinning unreacted Si–Cl away from the surface due to the necessary curvature of the −(O–(alkyl)­Si–O−) n backbone, unreacted moieties can be hydrolyzed only by the water farther away from the fiber surface. Herein, we use XPS to delineate the nature of the attachment and support our inference using wetting properties and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The TCPOS treated papers show an asymmetric distribution of fluorine (Figure d) and ultrahydrophobicity after treatments of ≥10 min (Figure e). Depth profiling reveals that the distribution of fluorine shows a high surface concentration with a slight jump at ∼2.5 nm (Figure f), further indicating that the distribution of silane is asymmetric across the hierarchically organized fiber and existing multiscale roughness. Despite the slight increase in fluorine at 2.7 nm, we observe a continuous decline in the moieties beyond that point, suggesting that these modifiers cannot readily penetrate the multiscale roughness. This inference is further supported by the realization that polyalkylsilane surfaces will not be permeable to the analogous surfactant-like monomer. Changing the amount of silane alters the ability of the oligomerized adducts to penetrate the multilevel roughness of cellulosic paper. At 100 and 750 μL, we see a rapid jump in the F signal at ca. 2.4 and 0.6 nm, respectively. We infer that the low concentration forms smaller oligomeric adducts that penetrate deeper into the paper, as opposed to the latter. We can therefore infer that the nonlinear trend in the F signal with depth is inconsistent with a self-assembled monolayer, and hence, there is a need to further evaluate the associated evolution in wetting to conclusively established the underlying mechanism.

Results and Discussion

Alfa-derived cellulose paper sheets were used in this study (see Materials and Methods). The pristine paper (UNPr) was subsequently treated with trihalo alkylsilanes at varying concentrations and exposure times to investigate the pathway of surface modification. UNPr is inherently hydrophilic due to the abundance of surface hydroxyl groups, resulting in strong polarity and rapid water absorption. Consequently, the initial water contact angle (CA) of UNPr was measured as 0°, reflecting instantaneous wetting. The surface wetting properties of cellulose fibers can be altered by reducing the surface energy, either by introducing surface roughness, hydrophobic molecules, or a combination of both. Figure a shows the evolution of CA with increasing treatment time (0–60 min) for various amounts of silane in a 1 L desiccator. The CA gradually increased with exposure time and began to asymptote after ∼10–30 min, confirming the concentration-dependent reaction kinetics. Interestingly, we observe a two-stage evolution in the contact angles that changes as the concentration increases. A rapid but observable drop in contract angle occurs at 25, 20, and 15 min for 100, 250, and 500 μL of silane, respectively. This linear shift in the CA minimum implies that an almost instantaneous (∼5 min) shift should occur for 750 μL (Figure c). Given that the rate of a step growth equation is linearly related to the concentration of the rate limiting monomer, we infer that this inflection is akin to the shift in the gel point with the change in monomer concentration. We infer that this inflection in CA is likely related to the shift in size or viscosity of adducts that are attached to the surface. We infer that the sharp shift in particle properties is most likely in line with the two-stage (pre- and post-gel point) step growth polymerization process, especially when coupled with the observation that at a 100 μL/desiccator concentration, , no change in CA was observed for the first 10 min of reaction (Figure b). The latter observation is likely due to the oligomerized adducts not covalently attaching to the surface because the available silane does not sufficiently consume the nonfreezing fiber-adjacent H-bond immobilized layer of water. Figure d, however, shows that the amount of fluorine generally increases with reaction time. However, the variance (Figure e) and skew or kurtosis (Figure f) in contact angles show an initially very stochastic wettability. The lower standard deviation in CA observed at longer reaction times (Figure e) confirms the uniformity in surface modification, whereas the larger variability at 0–20 min highlights the stochastic nature of the initial reaction phase. These inferences are further supported by the variation in higher statistical moments (skewness and kurtosis) as shown in Figure f. The inverse relation in variance is observed in the surface concentration of F atoms, with short reaction times showing smaller standard deviations compared to longer reaction times. We can therefore infer that as the surface adducts increase in size, the surface uniformity changes, leading to a stochastic distribution of the F atoms (Figure d), most likely due to the increase in size of the features leading to an increase in surface asperities.

The availability of surface-bound water plays a critical role in this process. The initial oligomerization of alkylsilanes with free water dictates the extent of surface modification, demonstrating that the reaction extends beyond simple monolayer formation. At low silane concentrations (e.g., 100 μL), the reaction onset was delayed until ∼10 min, after which the CA increased to ∼120°, imparting hydrophobicity. In contrast, at intermediate concentrations (250–750 μL), the CA initially rose to 60–80° but failed to achieve full hydrophobicity, with large standard deviations indicating localized, stochastic, nonuniform reactions. These results suggest that silane oligomers form small, physisorbed domains, generating heterogeneous pockets of hydrophobicity. Incomplete surface coverage at shorter times allows water to diffuse into the microporous paper structure, whereas prolonged treatment promotes polymerization and more uniform hydrophobic surfaces. At higher silane concentrations (1 mL), the CA increased sharply, reaching ∼120° within 5 min and stabilizing at 137.91° ± 4.65° after 60 min (Figure b). The rapid increase and low standard deviation indicate consistent and homogeneous surface coverage. These observations suggest a three-step process: (1) rapid oligomerization with free water, (2) anchoring of oligomers to surface-bound water, and (3) progressive polymerization leading to complete surface modification, albeit with a different layer of surface roughness. The silane polymer network forms efficiently at higher concentrations, driven by greater reagent availability and residual fiber water diffusion, producing hierarchical surface structures.

These results were also supported by the nonlinear decrease in the OH group content during surface hydrophobization, as shown in Figure S2b. During trihalo perfluoroalkylsilane (RF) reactions, the RF releases HCl, which causes hydrolysis of cellulose, thereby increasing the OH group content while at the same time contributing to its decrease due to the functionalization of the OH groups by the oligomers. At 100 μL, the amount of HCl released is insufficient to hydrolyze the amorphous regions of the cellulose; consequently, the kinetics of OH group substitution are faster than those of hydrolysis, leading to a decrease in OH groups over time. At intermediate concentrations (250–500 μL), between 5 and 30 min, the OH group content fluctuates owing to increased hydrolysis of the cellulose, while the alkylsilane is insufficient to react with all the newly formed OH groups; in this case, the rate of hydrolysis exceeds the rate of OH group substitution. These results corroborate what is observed in contact angle measurements and by EDS. At higher concentrations (700 and 1000 μL), the RF releases large amounts of both HCl and alkylsilane grafts, leading to an excess of reactive species. HCl hydrolyzes the amorphous regions of the cellulose while leaving the crystalline regions intact, as shown by the XRD results through the absence of peak shifts (Figure S3), thereby preventing part of the HCl from reacting. At the same time, the alkylsilane reacts with both the hydrolyzed cellulose and the surface of the crystalline regions. In this case, the substitution kinetics exceed the hydrolysis kinetics, resulting in a greater decrease in OH content after only 5 min of reaction. The silane deposition occurs only on the exposed surface area of the paper fibers, leaving the interfiber regions intact due to the strong hydrogen bonds, which means that it does not affect the overall crystallinity. As shown by the X-ray diffraction patterns, changes in the crystallinity index are therefore difficult to detect, as the variations are very slight.

Statistical analysis (Figure f) supports these findings: kurtosis values at short treatment times fluctuated between positive and negative (1.5 to −0.75), reflecting irregular stochastic particle distributions, whereas after 60 min, the distribution became platykurtic (kurtosis ≈ 0), consistent with uniform particle coverage. SEM and EDS imaging further corroborated these results (Figure g,h and Figure S1). At 100 μL, fluorine mapping revealed discrete patches of silane deposition, whereas at 1000 μL, a higher fluorine intensity and atomic percentage confirmed the extensive and almost complete coverage (Figure S1 a–f). The surface-bound silane particles not only reduce surface energy but also introduce nanoscale roughness. This dual effect chemical modification, combined with the hierarchical topography, drives the system toward a Cassie–Baxter wetting state and hence enhanced hydrophobicity.

While EDS provided preliminary evidence of surface modification, higher resolution analysis was required to resolve the reaction mechanism. Therefore, XPS was employed to verify the chemical changes on the cellulose surface. Figure a–c shows the spectra for UNPr. The deconvoluted C 1s spectrum revealed four components: two dominant peaks at 284.2 and 285.9 eV corresponding to C–C and C–O bonds, respectively, that are intrinsic to cellulose; a peak at 287.0 eV associated with O–C–O groups; and a weak signal at 288.9 eV from COO functionalities. , The low intensity of the latter likely originates from the residual effects of bleaching during the cellulose extraction from Alfa fibers (see Materials and Methods). Additionally, cellulose derived from Alfa naturally contains silicon, giving rise to a strong Si 2p peak at 101.8 eV (O–Si–O, Figure b). The O 1s spectrum (Figure c) further confirmed the presence of C–O bonds at 532.2 eV, consistent with the polar, hydrophilic nature of cellulose. Following fluorosilane treatment, new chemical signatures emerged. In the C 1s spectrum (Figure a,d), the intensity of the C–OH bond decreased significantly after treatment. This drop indicates that the surface hydroxyl groups were consumed during the grafting reaction, which corresponds to the formation of siloxane based nanoparticles at the cellulose surface, while the OCOH peak disappeared. Simultaneously, two new peaks appeared at 291.6 and 293.7 eV, assigned to CF2 and CF3 groups, confirming the formation of fluorinated polymeric species on the fiber surface (Figure S2a). The deconvoluted Si 2p spectrum (Figure e) displayed bimodal peaks at 101.2 and 102.9 eV, corresponding to O–Si–O and C–Si–O, respectively, providing strong evidence that the silane molecules anchored onto the cellulose surface. Emergence of the F 1s peak at 688.2 eV (Figure f) further confirmed the successful incorporation of fluorine.

To quantify the extent of polymerization, F 1s intensities were compared across silane concentrations (Figure g). The nonlinear increase in F 1s counts indicated that fluorine incorporation did not proceed via a simple monolayer or first-order process; rather, it reflected progressive step growth polymerization (Figure S2b). To probe the arrangement of fluorine relative to silane, the F 1s/Si 2p ratio was calculated (Figure h). At a low concentration of 100 μL, the ratio is minimal up to 15 min and then rapidly increases (ratio: 15), consistent with the contact angle analysis, indicating delayed kinetics of polymerization dictated by the water availability. At 250 μL, there is a slight initial increase (ratio: 6.5) followed by low concentrations until 60 min (ratio: 0.5–5). At an intermediate concentration of 500 μL, the ratio is similar to that at the low concentration of 100 μL, where the ratio is very minimal until 15 min (ratio: 0.5) and then rapidly increases up to 60 min (ratio: 23). At 750 μL, however, the ratio is similar to that at 250 μL, with a high ratio at 5 min, followed by relatively lower ratios (ratio: 5–8) until 60 min, suggesting heterogeneous coverage with regions of physiosorbed oligomers insufficient for complete polymerization. At a concentration of 1 mL, the F 1s/Si 2p ratio indicates an increased average fluorine concentration, indicating uniform polymerization and nanoparticle formation on the fiber surfaces. The lower standard deviation at 1 mL, particularly after 60 min, reflects homogeneous coverage with fluorine-rich surfaces, consistent with the proposed schematic (Figure ) in which hydrophobic alkyl chains orient outward into the vapor phase.

These results demonstrate that silane modification is governed by the availability of surface-bound water, which mediates silane oligomerization, and by substrate morphology, which controls oligomer penetration and particle formation that ultimately modulate surface roughness. Finally, angle-resolved XPS (0–60°) was used to estimate polymer particle thickness (Figure i and Figure S2c). Samples treated with 100–500 μL exhibited films of ∼1.6 nm, whereas treatment with 1 mL yielded a thickness of ∼2.5 nm. This finding strongly supports the hypothesis that silane molecules undergo step growth polymerization, mediated by the depletion of free and bound water at the cellulose surface, leading to hierarchical fluoropolymer structures, rather than simple monolayer adsorption. EDS confirms the successful fluorine incorporation, while XPS reveals surface heterogeneity from amphiphobic islands. Together, these results indicate polymer island formation rather than a uniform monomeric layer on cellulose.

To ensure that the treatment did not affect the structure of the cellulose, XRD was performed on UNPr, heated UNPr, and treated paper (Figure S3). Herein, it can be observed that the crystallinity index (CrI) values for the heated and treated samples are not distinguishable, given that their standard deviations overlap. The slight increase observed relative to that of the pristine sample can be attributed to the loss of free water, which interacts with and amplifies the amorphous regions. Upon heating, the removal of this water reduces this effect, resulting in a higher apparent crystallinity index. However, when heating in the presence of hydrophobic trihalomethylsilane, the mechanism is different, as the trihalomethylsilane can penetrate the amorphous region and thus prevent the formation of hydrogen bonds between cellulose chains during heating; this slight increase is therefore due to the hydrolysis of cellulose. These results demonstrate that no structural modification occurs within the cellulose, and it remains intact, confirming that the polymerization process is surface-limited.

To establish whether the reaction kinetics were specific to fluorosilanes or more broadly applicable to other silanes, cellulose paper was also treated with trichloro alkylsilane. The contact angle (CA) evolution (Figure a) revealed similar overall behavior to the fluorosilane system, with characteristic dependence on concentration and exposure time. At 500 μL, the CA rose rapidly to ∼60° within 5 min, further increasing to ∼100° after 20 min and finally reaching ∼130° by 30 min. The progressive increase in CA, accompanied by relatively large standard deviations, reflects a growth-type process in which initial oligomers are physisorbed and remain mobile before progressively anchoring and polymerizing. A similar trend was observed at a concentration of 1 mL; however, the higher reagent availability enabled the system to reach maximum hydrophobicity within ∼20 min, highlighting the role of silane concentration in accelerating polymerization. The eventual reduction in surface energy, evident from the high CA values, is attributed to the growth of silane particles that introduce both hydrophobic chemical groups and nanoscale surface roughness (see Figure S4a–c). These results also show the effect of concentration on the gelation point (per Carothers theory and the step growth rate equation), as shown in Figure f. XPS measurements corroborated these observations. The intensity of Si 2p peaks increased with treatment time, confirming progressive surface coverage and polymer growth (Figure S2d). Importantly, alkylsilane reactions release HCl gas, which is hypothesized to interact with the cellulose substrate, altering the availability of hydroxyl groups and thereby modulating the effective surface area available for polymerization. This effect is supported by the observed decrease in C–OH bond intensity with time (Figure b). Collectively, these results suggest that Si–Cl bonds react with surface-bound water, enabling the continuous propagation of silane polymers rather than restricting growth to monolayer adsorption.

To directly compare the kinetics of fluoro- and alkylsilanes, estimation plots were used to evaluate population-independent mean differences in CA (Figure c). At low concentrations (100 μL), alkylsilanes exhibited slower polymerization kinetics. After 10 and 15 min of exposure, the mean differences relative to fluorosilane were −18 and −40, respectively, indicating that fluorosilane polymerizes more rapidly due to the presence of electronegative fluorine in the backbone, which enhances reactivity with surface-bound water and results in higher CAs at early stages. However, beyond 20 min, both systems converged to similar CA values. This suggests that once sufficient polymerization had occurred and the surface water was depleted, the chemical differences in silane reactivity became less significant, and it started pinning to the cellulose substrate after reaching its gelation point. At higher concentrations (1 mL), the behavior was more complex (Figure d). The mean difference remained at −40 with increased variability, which can be attributed to the incomplete anchoring of oligomers and the presence of physisorbed species. This variability diminished with extended reaction times: after 60 min, CA values stabilized at consistently high levels with narrow distributions, confirming the completion of polymerization and the formation of a continuous hydrophobic silane particle layer across the cellulose surface. Taken together, these results demonstrate that both fluorosilanes and alkylsilanes follow similar overall kinetic pathways, initiated by oligomer physisorption, followed by anchoring and surface polymerization; the kinetics are influenced by silane chemistry, concentration, and reaction byproducts. Despite these differences, both systems ultimately converge to produce hierarchical silane-modified cellulose surfaces with enhanced hydrophobicity, governed by nanoscale roughness and reduced surface energy.

Conclusion

Chemical grafting with alkylsilanes has long been assumed to yield self-assembled monolayers; however, our results demonstrate that, on cellulose fiber substrates, this process instead follows a nonlinear step growth polymerization pathway. In this mechanism, alkyl trihalosilanes exploit physisorbed water on cellulose fibers as a comonomer, initiating oligomerization prior to surface attachment. Subsequent partial pinning and continued polymerization lead to the formation of discrete siloxane particles rather than a continuous monolayer. Using cellulose as a model hierarchical substrate, we show that (i) surface-bound water critically governs gelation and oligomer growth in trihalo alkylsilanes and (ii) the multiscale roughness inherent to paper strongly influences morphology. These observations support a three-step process: (1) rapid oligomerization with free or capillary water, (2) anchoring of oligomers to surface-bound water, and (3) progressive polymerization that yields surface modification, accompanied by a new layer of surface roughness. At higher silane concentrations, the efficient formation of a siloxane polymer network is driven by increased reagent availability and the continued diffusion of residual fiber water, resulting in hierarchical surface structures. The resulting silane nanoparticles impart low surface energy by creating hydrophobic domains and amplifying surface roughness, consistent with a Cassie–Baxter wetting regime. Contact angle measurements, XPS, and angle-resolved XPS collectively confirm a particle based coating with a finite depth rather than a molecularly thin film. This mechanistic insight broadens the design space for cellulose based materials in functional coatings, devices, and sensing applications.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Sodium chlorite (NaClO2, 80%), glacial acetic acid (CH3COOH, 99.7%), tri­chloro­(1H,1H,2H,2H-per­fluoro­octyl)­silane (C8F13H4SiCl3, 97%), and octyltrichlorosilane (C8H17Cl3Si, 97%), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification. A standard extraction process was used to obtain cellulose microfibers (CMFs). First, the Alfa fibers were treated with a 4% sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution at 80 °C for 2 h. This was followed by a bleaching step using a mixture of acetate buffer (27 g of NaOH and 75 mL of glacial acetic acid, diluted in 1000 mL of distilled water) and aqueous sodium chlorite (1.7 wt % NaClO2 in water), as described in detail previously.

Cellulosic Paper Preparation

The papers were produced using a water based papermaking process with a Rapid Köthen sheet former (PTE Austria pulp testing equipment), offering standardized, controlled laboratory conditions. As a first step, CMFs were dispersed in water and mixed at 9000 rpm for 5 min using a laboratory disintegrator (PTE Austria pulp testing equipment). It ensured that the CMFs were separated uniformly and well distributed in the suspension. Next, the dispersion was transferred to a paper former for vacuum filtration through a membrane filter. In this step, water was removed, leaving behind a wet, fibrous paper of entangled CMFs. Then, the wet papers were vacuum-dried at 100 °C for 10 min.

Surface Modification of Fibers

Hydrophobic papers were prepared by placing precut papers in a clean, dry desiccator maintained with drierite and equipped with an indicator at the bottom. Different amounts of alkylsilane (0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1 mL) were added to a 10 mL scintillation vial inside the case. Then, it underwent evacuation for ca. 1 min until 25 mmHg was reached. The desiccator was then sealed and transferred to a preheated oven at 95 °C. The reaction time was varied from 5 to 60 min. Further, the desiccator was removed from the oven, and samples were taken out. The treatment was performed using octyltrichlorosilane or its fluorinated derivative, tri­chloro­(1H,1H,2H,2H-per­fluoro­octyl)­silane.

Methods

Contact Angle

Contact angle were measured using a Rame-Hart Model 200 goniometer. A 7 μL drop of distilled water was used to measure the contact angle on the treated surface. Images of the drop were captured using the DROPimage software, and further, ImageJ was used to measure and quantify the contact angle.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and Energy-Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS)

Samples were mounted onto a Ted Pella Inc. SEM stub and adhered to it with carbon tape (Ted Pella Inc.). A Hitachi Tabletop SEM TM4000PLUS-II instrument was operated under low vacuum to reduce charge at a voltage of 10 kV with a spot size of 10 at a working distance of 10 mm. Oxford Aztec energy-dispersive X-ray analysis was used to collect and characterize the silane deposition on the fiber surface.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)

Samples were adhered to carbon tape and mounted onto the sample holder. A Thermo Scientific Nexa G2 instrument was used with the Avantage software. Carbon, oxygen, silicon, and fluorine elements were selected with the preset binding energies. For AR-XPS, angles were measured from 0 to 60° with 10° increments.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD)

XRD patterns of UNPr, heated UNPr, and the treated paper were analyzed with a Anton Paar XRDynamic 500 instrument using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54056 Å) in the range of 2θ = 10–40° with a scanning rate of 2° min–1.
