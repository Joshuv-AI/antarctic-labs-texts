Epidemiology, Molecular Characteristics, Treatment Patterns, and Outcomes of Carbapenem-Resistant Enterobacterales Infections in a Tertiary Hospital in Southern Saudi Arabia: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Mushary Alqahtani, Ali Alshehri, Mohammed Alshehri, Bairam Jarallah, Naima Fasih, Sultan Alkahtani, Osama Asiri, Nejad Alnaem, Abdullah Gazwani, Abdulmajeed Alqahtani, Yahia Elsharif, Hatim Ayied, Maram Alshahrani, Ali Hawan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) represent a growing public health challenge worldwide, particularly in healthcare settings where the options for treatment are limited, and the mortality rate is high. Data on the epidemiology, molecular characteristics, and clinical outcomes of CRE infections in southern Saudi Arabia are limited.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was performed at a tertiary care hospital from 2021 to 2024, including adult patients with culture-confirmed CRE infections. Information on demographics, comorbidities, infection characteristics, antimicrobial therapy, molecular resistance mechanisms, and clinical outcomes was collected. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify antibiotic-related predictors of poor outcomes, defined as a combination of 30-day mortality and treatment failure.ResultsA total of 230 patients with infections caused by CRE were included in the study, with a mean age of 71.8 years; 96.1% of these patients had at least one comorbidity. The majority of the infections were hospital-acquired, with ventilator-associated pneumonia (31.7%) and bloodstream infections (27.8%) being the most prevalent. Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) was responsible for 96.1% of the isolated cases. OXA-48 was identified as the predominant carbapenemase, occurring in 50.4% of cases, followed by combinations involving New Delhi metallo-beta-lactamase (NDM) and Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemase (KPC). The mortality rates at 30 days and 60 days were 54.3% and 65.2%, respectively. In the logistic regression analysis, ceftazidime-avibactam was associated with lower odds of poor outcome, while colistin and tigecycline were linked to higher odds; these results should be interpreted carefully due to the observational nature of the study.ConclusionCRE infections in this cohort were associated with high mortality and were predominantly caused by OXA-48–producing K. pneumoniae. These results emphasize the significant impact of CRE in southern Saudi Arabia and highlight the need for continuous monitoring, effective antimicrobial management, and the enhancement of treatment approaches from a public health standpoint.Keywords: Carbapenem-resistant enterobacterales, Antimicrobial resistance, OXA-48, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Epidemiology, Clinical outcomes, Saudi Arabia

Introduction

Antimicrobial agents play an essential role in the treatment of infections and support medical procedures. However, resistance to antimicrobial agents has become a primary global health concern, reducing the effectiveness of these life-saving antibiotics. The resistance often arises from genes carried on mobile genetic elements, which can spread quickly between bacteria. This rapid transfer, combined with the widespread and sometimes inappropriate use of antibiotics in humans and agriculture, has contributed to the rise of the multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs) [1]. Among these agents, CRE are of particular concern, especially in hospital settings.

The carbapenems are usually considered the last line of treatment for severe infections that are caused by Gram-negative bacteria. The resistance to these drugs greatly limits the treatment options. The polymyxins, such as Colistin, are sometimes used as a last resort, but their effectiveness is decreasing due to rising resistance. In addition, these drugs are linked to serious side effects, such as kidney damage and higher mortality, which makes the treatment even more difficult [2]. To address this problem, global surveillance systems such as the World Health Organization’s Global Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance System (GLASS) have been developed [3]. Still, there are significant gaps that remain, especially in regions such as Southeast Asia and the Middle East, where limited healthcare access and misuse of antibiotics worsen the problem.

In Saudi Arabia, CRE has been increasingly reported, creating serious challenges for healthcare providers. Most studies have been conducted in Riyadh, with fewer reports from other provinces. In Madinah, the study found that all Escherichia coli (E.Coli) isolates remained susceptible to imipenem. In the Southern Province, however, Klebsiella pneumoniae was the predominant CRE species, with bla OXA−48 as the most common carbapenemase gene, followed by blaNDM. Similar results have also been observed in Makkah and Jeddah, where OXA-48 and NDM-1 remain the dominant resistance mechanisms [4]. Environmental monitoring during the Hajj also detected high levels of blaNDM-1-positive E. coli, demonstrating that mass gatherings can contribute to the spread of CRE. More recently, the first KPC-2-producing CRE was identified in the Gulf region, adding further complexity to the resistance profile [5].

These findings highlight the urgent need for ongoing surveillance, effective treatment strategies, and strong infection control programs. With this background, the current study aims to explore the molecular characteristics, risk factors, treatment approaches, and patient outcomes of CRE at AFHSR, thereby filling an essential gap in regional data.

Research Design and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective cohort study was carried out at the Armed Forces Hospital Southern Region (AFHSR), a tertiary care referral hospital located in Khamis Mushayt, Asir Province, southern Saudi Arabia. The AFHSR in Khamis Mushayt, Asir Province, Saudi Arabia, is an 882-bed, JCI-accredited, tertiary-care military hospital serving military personnel and their dependents. It acts as the main referral center for military hospitals in the southern region (including Najran, Jizan, and Sharurah), with about 40 intensive care unit beds and subspecialty services such as internal medicine, surgery, critical care, cardiac surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, and renal transplantation. The study was conducted from 2021 to 2024.

Study Population

The inclusion criteria comprised adult patients (≥ 18 years) admitted to AFHSR with culture-positive CRE results from clinical samples. CRE infection was distinguished from colonization based on established clinical and microbiological criteria. Infection was defined as the isolation of CRE from a clinical specimen obtained in the context of compatible signs and symptoms of infection (including fever, leukocytosis, localized signs of inflammation, or organ dysfunction), along with a clinician’s decision to initiate targeted antimicrobial therapy. In contrast, colonization was defined as CRE isolation from surveillance or screening cultures in the absence of clinical signs of infection, and such cases were excluded from the analysis.

Exclusion criteria included patients with carbapenem-susceptible Enterobacterales, patients with CRE-positive screening samples, patients who did not receive antibiotic treatment, and patients younger than 18 years. A consecutive sampling method was applied to include all eligible patients during the study period.

Microbiological Methods

All clinical samples except urine were inoculated on Chocolate agar [Saudi Prepared Media Laboratory Co. Ltd. (SPML), KSA], Sheep Blood agar (SPML, KSA), and MacConkey agar (SPML, KSA). The urine sample was inoculated onto cystine-electrolyte-deficient media (SPML, KSA). Organisms were identified by colony morphology, Gram stain, and biochemical characteristics using the Vitek 2 Compact automated system (BioMérieux, France) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined using the Vitek 2 Compact automated system (Biomerieux, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions for carbapenem agents (ertapenem, imipenem, and meropenem) and other antimicrobials (amikacin, gentamicin, ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, cefepime, ampicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanate, piperacillin-tazobactam, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, nitrofurantoin, and ciprofloxacin). Etest (Biomerieux, France) was used against ceftazidime-avibactam and tigecycline, while microbroth dilution was performed for colistin (Liofilchem®, ComASP® Colistin, Italy). For all antibiotics, susceptibility breakpoints were interpreted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [6], except for tigecycline, for which European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) breakpoint Table [7] were used. Laboratory policy mandated annual updates of MIC breakpoints based on the new CLSI M-100 guidelines. Breakpoints were set as follows: for ceftazidime-avibactam, MIC ≤ 8/4 µg/ml (susceptible) and MIC ≥ 16/4 µg/ml (resistant); for colistin, MIC ≤ 2 µg/ml (intermediate) and MIC ≥ 4 µg/ml (resistant); for gentamicin, before March 2023: MIC ≤ 4 µg/ml (susceptible), MIC 8 µg/ml (intermediate), MIC ≥ 16 µg/ml (resistant); after March 2023: MIC ≤ 2 µg/ml (susceptible), MIC 4 µg/ml (intermediate), MIC ≥ 8 µg/ml (resistant).

Amikacin (Before March 2023: MIC ≤ 16 µg/ml; susceptible, MIC 32 µg/ml; intermediate, MIC ≥ 64 µg/ml; resistant. After March 2023: MIC ≤ 4 µg/ml; susceptible, MIC 8 µg/ml; intermediate, MIC ≥ 16 µg/ml; resistant) [8] and tigecycline (MIC ≤ 0.5 µg/ml; susceptible, MIC > 0.5 µg/ml; resistant) were used. All Enterobacterale isolates were classified as CRE if they showed reduced susceptibility to at least one carbapenem antibiotic (MIC ≥ 1 µg/ml for ertapenem and ≥ 2 µg/ml for imipenem/meropenem) according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) definition [9].

Molecular Detection of Carbapenemase Genes

CRE isolates were tested for carbapenemase genes using the Xpert Carba-R assay (GeneXpert Cepheid, USA), a real-time PCR test that detects five carbapenemase genes (blaKPC, blaIMP, blaVIM, blaNDM, and blaOXA48), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Laboratory Notification Policy

All confirmed CRE-positive cultures with genotype were reported to the primary treating physician and the infection control team within two hours of finalizing the culture.

In line with hospital antimicrobial stewardship policy, all CRE-positive cases were reported to an infectious disease physician by the primary treating physician. The infectious disease physician offered antimicrobial therapy recommendations based on susceptibility results, genotype data, infection type, site, severity, and patient-specific clinical factors, following hospital policies and procedural guidelines.

Ethical approval and data collection.

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of AFHSR (Approval No. AFHSRMREC/2025/Internal Medicine Department/762), and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design. Data were extracted from microbiology laboratory records using a structured data collection sheet that captured demographic characteristics, comorbidities, risk factors, treatment modalities, and clinical outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software. Descriptive statistics summarized baseline characteristics: categorical variables were shown as frequencies and percentages. In contrast, continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate. Comparisons between groups used the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and Student’s t-test for continuous variables. Logistic regression analysis identified antibiotic-related predictors of a composite poor outcome, defined as 30-day mortality or treatment failure, whichever occurred first. A single logistic regression model was built with this composite endpoint as the dependent variable. All analyses were conducted at the patient level, with each patient contributing a single index infection episode to the dataset. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In the multivariable logistic regression model, only antibiotic-related variables were included as explanatory variables, as the primary objective of this analysis was to identify antibiotic predictors of poor outcome rather than to build a comprehensive prognostic model. All antibiotics administered post-culture confirmation were included regardless of frequency of use.

Outcome Definitions

The primary outcomes of this study were all-cause mortality and treatment failure. Mortality was measured at two time points: 30-day mortality, which is defined as death from any cause within 30 days of the initial CRE culture, and 60-day mortality, defined as death from any cause within 60 days of the initial CRE culture, whether the patient was discharged or still hospitalized. In-hospital mortality was also included as part of these endpoints, since all deaths during admission were recorded.

Treatment failure was defined as a composite outcome that includes: (1) death within 30 days of CRE culture confirmation, (2) microbiological failure, which is defined as persistent CRE growth on follow-up cultures obtained ≥ 72 h after starting targeted antimicrobial therapy, and/or (3) clinical failure, which is defined as the lack of clinical improvement—such as persistent fever, unresolved signs of infection, or the need to escalate antimicrobial therapy—after at least 72 h of targeted treatment. Patients who died before 72 h of starting treatment were classified as treatment failures.

10 Infection Type Definitions

Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (VAP) was defined according to CDC/NHSN VAP criteria as pneumonia occurring in a mechanically ventilated patient for more than 48 h at the time of, or within 48 h before, the onset of the infection event. It is supported by new or progressively infiltrating on radiographs, along with at least two of the following: fever (> 38 °C) or hypothermia (< 36 °C), leukocytosis or leukopenia, purulent tracheal secretions, or declining oxygenation [10].

Primary bloodstream Infection (Bacteremia) was defined as CRE being isolated from one or more blood cultures in a patient showing clinical signs of infection and no identifiable source at another body site [11].

Central Line-Associated Bloodstream Infection (CLABSI) was defined by CDC/NHSN criteria as a laboratory-confirmed bloodstream infection in a patient with a central venous catheter in place for more than 48 h before infection onset, with no other identifiable source of infection [11].

Complicated Urinary Tract Infection (cUTI) was defined as a symptomatic UTI in a patient with a structural or functional urinary tract abnormality or urinary catheter, with CRE isolated at ≥ 10⁵ CFU/mL accompanied by relevant clinical symptoms [12].

Skin and soft tissue infection (SSTI) was defined as CRE isolated from wound or tissue cultures in the presence of local signs of infection such as erythema, swelling, warmth, or purulent discharge, or systemic signs of infection [13].

Results

Total of 230 patients with CRE infections were included. The mean age was 71.8 ± 16.5 years, with 51.3% males. Most patients had baseline comorbidities (96.1%), and over half of the cases originated from intensive care units (53.5%). The medical wards combined contributed (23.9%), followed by the other wards (15.2%) (Table 1).

Discussion

Antimicrobial agents are essential for infection management; however, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) represents a major global health threat [14]. CRE are of particular concern due to their association with healthcare settings, limited therapeutic options, and high mortality [15].

The clinical profile of patients in this study highlights the vulnerability of elderly and multimorbid individuals to CRE infections, a finding consistently reported in international and regional studies [16–18]. Respiratory infections, particularly ventilator-associated pneumonia, and bloodstream infections were the predominant clinical syndromes, reflecting the nosocomial nature of CRE and their strong association with intensive care unit exposure.

Klebsiella pneumoniae was the dominant pathogen in our cohort (96.1%), with OXA-48 being the most prevalent carbapenemase. This epidemiological pattern aligns with regional and international reports indicating the endemicity of OXA-48–producing K. pneumoniae in the Middle East, in contrast to Western countries, where KPC-producing strains predominate [19–21].

Prior exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics, particularly carbapenems and piperacillin-tazobactam, was every day before culture confirmation, underscoring the role of empiric antibiotic pressure in CRE emergence [22]. Following culture confirmation, ceftazidime-avibactam and colistin were the most frequently used agents, reflecting their role as salvage therapies against multidrug-resistant organisms [23].

Ceftazidime-avibactam was associated with lower odds of poor outcomes in an exploratory logistic regression analysis (OR 0.438, 95% CI 0.21–0.94, p = 0.033). In contrast, colistin and tigecycline were linked to increased mortality, likely reflecting their use in more severe infections and concerns about toxicity and emerging resistance [24, 25]. However, ceftazidime-avibactam lacks activity against NDM-producing organisms, which made up about one-third of isolates in our cohort. In such cases, it is often combined with aztreonam to restore effectiveness. Therefore, the positive outcomes seen with ceftazidime-avibactam may partly result from combination therapy in NDM-producing cases rather than its standalone effect, and these results should be viewed with caution.

The relatively high prevalence of NDM-producing isolates in our cohort reflects the broader regional epidemiology of carbapenem resistance in the Arabian Peninsula and Middle East, where NDM has become an increasingly dominant carbapenemase type alongside OXA-48. Treating NDM-producing CRE remains particularly challenging due to limited therapeutic options. Ceftazidime-avibactam combined with aztreonam is the most evidence-supported regimen for NDM-producing infections, as aztreonam is stable against metallo-beta-lactamases, and avibactam protects it from co-existing serine beta-lactamases. Alternative options include cefiderocol, which maintains activity against metallo-beta-lactamase producers, and combination regimens with tigecycline, fosfomycin, or aminoglycosides, although clinical evidence supporting these combinations remains limited. These considerations highlight the importance of routine carbapenemase genotyping to guide individualized treatment decisions, especially in regions where NDM is endemic.

Overall mortality rates in this cohort were high and comparable to those reported globally, emphasizing the severe prognosis of CRE infections and the urgent need for optimized antimicrobial stewardship and early targeted therapy [26].

Limitations

One of the limitations of this study is its observational, non-randomized design, which introduces the risk of confounding by indication. Patients receiving ceftazidime-avibactam may have differed systematically from those treated with colistin or tigecycline in terms of disease severity, infection type, renal function, or clinician preference, all of which could independently affect outcomes. While multivariable logistic regression was used to adjust for measured confounders, residual confounding cannot be ruled out without propensity score analysis or randomization. Therefore, the observed association between ceftazidime-avibactam and improved outcomes should be interpreted with caution and does not prove causality. The single-center design limits the applicability of our findings to other settings with different patient populations and resistance profiles. The lack of formal disease severity scoring systems, such as qSOFA or Pitt bacteremia score, restricts the ability to fully account for illness severity in the analysis. Data on concurrent use of ceftazidime-avibactam and aztreonam were not collected. If both drugs were given together, their inclusion as separate variables in the regression model may not fully capture their combined effect. Molecular characterization of carbapenem resistance was limited to detecting carbapenemase genes and did not include non-carbapenemase mechanisms such as porin loss or efflux pump overexpression. The relatively long interval from culture confirmation to antibiotic adjustment may reflect institutional stewardship policies and empirical therapy practices, which could have contributed to the high mortality observed. Formal concordance between empiric antibiotic therapy and final susceptibility results was not evaluated, limiting the assessment of empiric treatment appropriateness. Isolates testing negative by the Xpert® Carba-R assay were classified as CRE based solely on phenotypic carbapenem MIC thresholds. No additional molecular tests were performed to identify non-carbapenemase resistance mechanisms, such as ESBL or AmpC production combined with porin loss, in these isolates.

Conclusion

This study highlights the urgent need for antimicrobial stewardship, surveillance, and infection control in southern Saudi Arabia, where CRE infections are predominantly caused by K. pneumoniae harboring OXA-48 carbapenemase. These infections showed high multidrug resistance, frequent ICU acquisition, and poor mortality outcomes. Ceftazidime-avibactam was linked to better outcomes in this observational cohort, while Colistin and tigecycline were linked to higher chances of poor outcomes. However, these results should be viewed with caution due to potential confounding by indication inherent in the non-randomized study design. Despite limitations, including its single-center, retrospective design and limited molecular testing, the findings align with global evidence and emphasize the need for rational antibiotic use and targeted strategies. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts, genomic profiling, and evaluation of preventive and therapeutic interventions are essential to reduce the burden of CRE and improve outcomes.
