Predictive value of CCL26 and CCR3 levels for prognosis assessment in children with Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia treated with azithromycin
Authors: Lingyan Ma, Shenghua Ge, Dongqing Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP), a prevalent form of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) in schoolchildren, frequently results in persistent fever or complications in 15–25% of cases despite azithromycin treatment. This highlights the urgent need for novel biomarkers to identify high-risk cases. This study aims to investigate the predictive value of serum C-C motif chemokine ligand 26 (CCL26) and C-C chemokine receptor 3 (CCR3) levels for treatment prognosis in children with MPP receiving azithromycin therapy.MethodsClinical data of 205 pediatric MPP patients treated in our hospital from February 2023 to February 2025 were collected. Serum levels of CCL26, CCR3, interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and D-dimer (DD) were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), while Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) antibody titers were determined using passive agglutination. Pearson correlation analysis evaluated relationships between CCL26 and CCR3 and inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α). Logistic regression identified risk factors for poor prognosis, with receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis assessing the predictive value of CCL26 and CCR3.ResultsCompared with healthy controls, MPP patients showed elevated serum CCL26, CCR3 and inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) both pre- and post-treatment (all P<0.05). These markers significantly decreased after therapy (all P<0.05). Pretreatment CCL26 and CCR3 levels positively correlated with inflammatory cytokines (P<0.05). Poor prognosis cases exhibited higher levels of CCL26, CCR3, TNF-α, IL-6, C-reactive protein (CRP), DD, MP antibody titers (≥1:160), fever duration (≥5 d), and peak temperature (≥38.5 °C) (P<0.05). Multivariate analysis identified MP antibody titer ≥1:160, elevated CCL26, CCR3, TNF-α, and IL-6 levels, fever ≥5 days, and temperature ≥38.5 °C as independent risk factors. The area under the curve (AUC) for CCL26 and CCR3 in predicting poor prognosis was 0.709 and 0.751, respectively, increasing to 0.798 when combined.ConclusionsSerum CCL26 and CCR3 levels decrease following azithromycin treatment and demonstrate significant associations with inflammatory markers and clinical outcomes, serving as reliable predictors for poor prognosis in MPP patients.Keywords: Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP), C-C motif chemokine ligand 26 (CCL26), C-C chemokine receptor 3 (CCR3), azithromycin

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study reveals that elevated serum C-C motif chemokine ligand 26 (CCL26) and C-C chemokine receptor 3 (CCR3) levels in children with Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) are associated with increased inflammation and poor prognosis. These levels significantly decrease after azithromycin treatment. CCL26 and CCR3 are identified as independent risk factors for poor outcomes, with combined detection enhancing prognostic accuracy.

What is known and what is new?

• MPP is a common pediatric infection causing significant morbidity. Dysregulated immune responses and elevated inflammatory markers (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) are linked to disease severity. Azithromycin is the first-line treatment, but reliable prognostic biomarkers are needed.

• This study demonstrates that serum CCL26 and CCR3 are reliable biomarkers for predicting poor prognosis in MPP. Their levels decrease significantly with azithromycin treatment, highlighting their potential for monitoring disease progression and treatment response.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Clinically, measuring CCL26 and CCR3 could help identify high-risk patients early, guiding more tailored interventions. Future guidelines may incorporate these biomarkers for better MPP management. Further multicenter studies are needed to validate their clinical utility.

Introduction

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) is a prevalent pathogen responsible for Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP), predominantly affecting preschool and school-aged children (1). The clinical manifestations of MPP, including fever and cough, lack specificity during early stages, with pulmonary symptoms becoming more evident as the disease progresses, potentially leading to multi-organ involvement and impaired growth and development in pediatric patients (2). The precise pathogenesis of MPP remains incompletely understood, though current research suggests that MP-induced inflammatory responses mediated through immune mechanisms play a crucial role in disease progression (3). C-C motif chemokine ligand 26 (CCL26) is a recently identified inflammatory modulator that specifically binds to its sole receptor, C-C chemokine receptor 3 (CCR3). This interaction facilitates eosinophil (EOS) recruitment and inflammatory mediator release, contributing significantly to inflammatory processes observed in allergic asthma (4). Following MP infection, respiratory epithelial cells secrete various pro-inflammatory cytokines that play pivotal roles in MPP pathogenesis, inducing localized inflammatory responses, airway obstruction, and pulmonary dysfunction (5). Azithromycin, a macrolide antibiotic, is widely employed in clinical treatment of MPP. Its mechanism involves inhibition of mycoplasmal ribosomal protein synthesis, while also exhibiting anti-inflammatory properties (6,7). This study aims to investigate the relationship between serum CCL26 and CCR3 levels and unfavorable treatment outcomes in pediatric MPP patients following azithromycin therapy. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2025-296/rc).

Methods

Sample size calculation and study population

Based on previous literature (5), the proportion of children with MPP showing poor prognosis (treatment failure) after azithromycin therapy was 20.23%. With a maximum allowable error of 5% (i.e., 10% difference between upper and lower confidence limits) and 1−α=0.9 (two-tailed test), PASS 15.0 software determined a minimum required sample size of 193 participants.

This single-center prospective study analyzed clinical data from 205 pediatric MPP patients treated at our hospital between February 2023 and February 2025, along with 56 healthy children from routine physical examinations serving as controls. The flowchart of participant enrollment in this study is illustrated in Figure 1. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shijiazhuang People’s Hospital (No. 2023032) and was conducted in accordance with the requirements of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants.

Flowchart of participants in this study. MP, Mycoplasma pneumoniae; MPP, Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia.

Inclusion criteria

Children were eligible if they (I) fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for MPP (8), which required respiratory symptoms (fever, cough, wheezing, or dyspnea), radiographic evidence of pneumonia, and at least one microbiological confirmation—either a single serum MP antibody titer ≥1:160, a four-fold rise in paired sera, or positive MP-DNA/MP-RNA in nasopharyngeal swabs; (II) were 5–12 years old; (III) were experiencing their first episode of MPP without prior documented MP infection; and (IV) had complete clinical and laboratory records available.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they presented with alternative etiologies of pneumonia (bacterial, viral, or fungal), co-existing chronic respiratory diseases (asthma, bronchiectasis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), immunological, hematological, or allergic disorders, significant cardiac, hepatic, renal, or cerebral dysfunction, use of immunosuppressive therapy within the preceding three months, malignancy, congenital or genetic diseases, neuropsychiatric conditions, non-adherence to treatment or follow-up, polymicrobial or mixed infections, major extrapulmonary manifestations or complications, or congenital anatomical abnormalities.

Treatment protocol

All MPP patients received supportive care (oxygen therapy, nebulization, antipyretics) plus intravenous azithromycin (Lijun Pharmaceutical Co., Xi’an, China; approval No. H20020269, 0.5 g:500,000 units) at 5–10 mg/kg/day for 3–5 days.

Data collection

Demographic data

Gender, age, white blood cell (WBC), neutrophil counts, and lactic dehydrogenase (LDH) levels were recorded. Clinical parameters (fever duration, peak temperature, hospitalization days, lesion extent) were compared between prognosis groups.

Laboratory measures

Venous blood (5 mL) was collected within 3 h of admission (fasting samples for controls). After centrifugation (2,000 rpm ×10 min), serum was stored at −80 °C. Analyses included: (I) WBC/neutrophils (BC-6800 hematology analyzer, Mindray, Shenzhen, China); (II) C-reactive protein (CRP) (CRP-M100 analyzer, Mindray); (III) enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) quantification of LDH (HM10217), CCL26 (HM11183), CCR3 (HM11577), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) (HM10001), interleukin-6 (IL-6) (HM10205), D-dimer (DD) (HM10865; Biorab Bioscience, Wuhan, China); (IV) MP antibody titers by passive agglutination (Livzon Diagnostics, Zhuhai, China).

Prognostic evaluation

One-week post-treatment outcomes were classified (9). Good prognosis (n=172): Symptom resolution + ≥50% radiographic improvement; Poor prognosis (n=33): No improvement/worsening requiring transfer, or complications (atelectasis, necrosis, death).

Statistical analysis

SPSS 27.0 statistical software was employed to conduct normality tests on the measurement data. For measurement data conforming to a normal distribution, the mean ± standard deviation was utilized for representation, and intergroup comparisons were performed using the t-test. For measurement data not adhering to a normal distribution, the median and interquartile range [M (P25, P75)] were adopted for presentation, with the Mann-Whitney U test employed for intergroup comparisons. Categorical data were expressed as case numbers and percentages (%), and intergroup comparisons were conducted using the χ2 test. Spearman correlation analysis was utilized to evaluate the correlation between serum CCL26 and CCR3 levels and inflammatory cytokine (IL-6, TNF-α) levels in children with MPP. Logistic regression analysis was applied to identify risk factors influencing poor prognosis in MPP patients. The diagnostic value of serum CCL26 and CCR3 levels in predicting poor prognosis in MPP children was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. A P value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Diagnostic value of serum CCL26 and CCR3 for poor prognosis

ROC curve analysis indicated the cut-off values for serum CCL26 and CCR3 were 1,086.00 and 452.70 pg/mL, respectively. The area under the curve (AUC) for combined detection was 0.798, which was significantly higher than that for individual detection. The combined detection of serum CCL26 and CCR3 had greater diagnostic value for poor prognosis in MPP children than individual detection of each marker (Table 5, Figure 4).

Discussion

MP represents the smallest pathogenic microorganism capable of independent growth in cell-free media, exhibiting characteristics intermediate between bacteria and viruses. Primarily transmitted via respiratory routes, MP induces inflammatory responses in terminal airways and alveoli. While clinical manifestations predominantly include fever and cough, rapid progression may occur in some cases, leading to severe complications such as atelectasis, pleural effusion, respiratory distress, and even mortality. Early prognostic evaluation is therefore clinically crucial (10). This study investigates the changes in serum CCL26 and CCR3 levels in children with MPP after treatment with azithromycin, as well as the relationship between pre-treatment CCL26 and CCR3 levels and inflammatory factors and prognosis. It provides potential biomarkers for early detection and adverse prognosis assessment in clinical practice.

Current evidence indicates that dysregulated host immune responses play pivotal roles in MPP pathogenesis and progression (11). MP infection activates multiple immune cell populations, triggering release of inflammatory mediators including TNF-α and IL-6, which not only participate in disease development but also correlate with severity (12). Our results align with these observations, showing elevated TNF-α and IL-6 levels in MPP patients versus controls, with further increases in poor-prognosis cases. Upon infection with MP, neutrophils are able to secrete cytokines that can subsequently recruit additional immune cells to the site of infection, thereby augmenting the intensity of the immune response (13). LDH is a major inflammatory marker. Studies have shown that serum LDH has good accuracy for the diagnosis of refractory MPP (14-16). In the present study, compared with the Control group, the levels of neutrophils and LDH were elevated in children with MPP.

EOS serve as key effector cells in airway inflammation (17). CCL26 (eotaxin-3), a 77-amino acid chemokine, promotes EOS activation and inflammatory cell recruitment (18). CCR3 is a receptor for C-C motif chemokines. Studies have demonstrated that CCR3 can activate Th2 immune responses and induce the massive release of inflammatory factors. Moreover, blocking the CCR3 receptor has been shown to reduce the recruitment of neutrophils to the lungs during acute inflammation (19,20). Moon et al. found that CCL26 may act as a signaling molecule during the inflammatory process, guiding the recruitment of CX3CR1+ macrophages to the site of inflammation (18). Another study has also indicated that inhibition of CCL26 can reduce the migration and aggregation of EOS, thereby alleviating the airway inflammatory response in asthma (21). Azithromycin, as a first-line treatment for MPP, exerts limited damage to hepatic and renal function, and its long half-life enables it to effectively inhibit the growth of MP. Additionally, azithromycin also possesses certain anti-inflammatory properties (22). In this study, we measured the serum levels of CCL26 and CCR3 in children with MPP to explore the changes after azithromycin treatment and the associations of pre-treatment CCL26 and CCR3 levels with inflammatory markers and prognosis. The results showed that, compared with the Control group, children with MPP had higher levels of serum CCL26, CCR3, and inflammatory markers, which decreased after azithromycin treatment. This indicates that azithromycin can suppress the inflammation caused by MPP and improve the prognosis of the children. Additionally, correlation analysis was performed between the serum levels of CCL26 and CCR3 and the levels of inflammatory markers before treatment in the children. The results showed that the concentrations of pretreatment CCL26 and CCR3 positively correlated with inflammatory markers, suggesting their involvement in disease progression. Multivariate analysis identified these chemokines as independent risk factors for poor outcomes. ROC curve analysis yielded AUC values of 0.7–0.9 for individual and combined markers, supporting their prognostic value.

In summary, elevated levels of serum CCL26 and CCR3 in children with MPP are associated with increased inflammatory responses and poor prognosis, and can serve as indicators for predicting poor prognosis in children with MPP. However, this study also has certain limitations, such as a limited sample size in a single-center study. Future research needs to conduct further in-depth studies with multicenter and large sample sizes. Meanwhile, the variability of the markers during treatment and their correlation with clinical symptoms and imaging indicators should also be analyzed to further verify the clinical application value of these indicators.

Conclusions

This study provides valuable insights into the role of serum CCL26 and CCR3 as potential biomarkers for predicting poor prognosis in children with MPP. Elevated levels of these chemokines, along with increased inflammatory factors, are closely associated with disease severity and adverse outcomes. Azithromycin, the first-line treatment for MPP, was shown to effectively reduce serum CCL26, CCR3, and inflammatory cytokine levels, thereby improving clinical outcomes. The findings suggest that CCL26 and CCR3 could serve as useful indicators for early detection and prognostic assessment in MPP. However, the study’s limitations, including its single-center design and limited sample size, highlight the need for further validation through multicenter and large-scale studies. Future research should focus on confirming the clinical utility of these biomarkers and exploring additional therapeutic strategies to optimize outcomes in MPP patients.
