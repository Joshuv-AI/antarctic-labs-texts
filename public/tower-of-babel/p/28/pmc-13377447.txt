Accurate quantification and antioxidant evaluation of avenanthramides in oat products using authentic reference standards
Authors: Min-Ho Song, Ji-Woo Yu, Jung-Hoon Lee, Hui-Yeon Ahn, Ji-Ho Lee, Young-Soo Keum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Avenanthramides are oat-derived phenolic amides. However, prior studies have predominantly focused on C-type avenanthramides. This study aimed to enable accurate determination of A-type avenanthramides in oat products using authentic reference standards. Fifty avenanthramide standards covering A-type and C-type structures were synthesized, characterized, and confirmed to be >98% pure. A quantitative method was developed and analytically validated for representative analytes using LC-MS/MS. Calibration slopes differed substantially between A-type and C-type counterparts, indicating that C-type-based calibration causes systematic underestimation of A-type compounds. Analysis of commercial oat products showed that extrapolation using C-type curves yielded 35.9–80.7% of concentrations obtained with authentic standards, and A-type compounds comprised 29.9–49.0% of total avenanthramides within the quantified panel (six compounds). Antioxidant evaluation using DPPH radical scavenging indicated that while C-type avenanthramides tended to show slightly higher activity on average, multiple A-type avenanthramides exhibited comparable or superior activity, underscoring the biological relevance of A-type structures.

Introduction

Oats (Avena sativa L.) are widely cultivated and consumed globally, with monthly production reaching 22.7 million metric tons and a projected market value of US$6.1 billion by 2028 (Tomar & Singh, 2024). Oat consumption has been associated with several health benefits, including a decreased risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and gastrointestinal disorders (He et al., 2016; Helnæs et al., 2016; Martínez-Villaluenga & Peñas, 2017; Thies et al., 2014). These effects are largely attributed to bioactive compounds, such as β-glucan, saponins, vitamins, and avenanthramides (AVNs) (Chen et al., 2020; Guleria et al., 2015; Raguindin et al., 2021).

AVNs, a class of phenolic alkaloids, are known to exist in more than 30 different congeners (Jágr et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2024). Collins initially assigned alphabetical designations (e.g., AVN-A, AVN-B) to categorize these compounds (Collins, 1989). Later, Dimberg et al. introduced a systematic nomenclature by using number to represent anthranilate derivatives (see Fig. 1, blue highlight) and lowercase alphabet to represent phenylpropionates (see Fig. 1, red highlight) (Liu & Wise, 2021). AVNs exhibited diverse bioactivities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-atherogenic activities (Hu et al., 2019; Perrelli et al., 2018; Turrini et al., 2019). Tranilast, a synthetic derivative of AVNs, has been clinically used for asthma and atopic dermatitis, highlighting their medicinal potential (Landberg et al., 2020). While C-type AVNs have been extensively studied, research on A-type AVNs remains limited. According to a previous study, A-type AVNs have been shown to have similar biological activities of C-type AVNs such as suppression of nitric oxide production in macrophages (Hu et al., 2019). However, accurate quantification of A-type AVNs remains challenging due to structural differences that lead to variation in UV extinction coefficients and MS response factors, thereby limiting the use of C-type-based calibration curves for quantifying A-type congeners (Hu et al., 2019; Jágr et al., 2020).

One of the key limitations in A-type AVN research is the lack of efficient synthetic routes to obtain pure reference compounds. Currently, C-type AVNs have been synthesized via Verley-Doebner modification of the Knoevenagel condensation (Fig. 2A) and acylation of anthranilic acid with substituted cinnamoyl chlorides (Fig. 2B) (Kamat & Parab, 2007; Speziale & Smith, 1963; Williams et al., 2008). However, only one synthetic method for A-type AVNs has been reported, which utilizes the acylation of anthranilic acid with 5-phenylpentadienoic acid which obtained through the Horner-Wadsworth-Emmons reaction (Bazin et al., 2008). This synthetic procedure involved multiple steps, requiring various reagents and leading to significant loss. For broader research and application of A-type AVNs, a more accessible and efficient synthetic pathway is necessary.Fig. 2Scheme for synthesis of avenanthramides using Verley-Doebner modification of the Knoevenagel condensation (a), avenanthramides using acylation of amine (b), and 5-phenyl-2,4-pentadienoic acid using the newly developed method (c).Fig. 2

To address the limited availability of A-type avenanthramide reference compounds and the resulting uncertainty in food compositional data, previously reported synthetic approaches for C-type avenanthramides were systematically examined and adapted to access A-type structures. Reaction conditions were optimized to streamline the synthesis with reduced step counts and improved practicality, and the double-bond configurations of the resulting products were confirmed by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy. The resulting authentic standards were then applied to food-relevant analyses, including liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry-based quantification and 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl radical scavenging assays, to evaluate their utility for accurate profiling and antioxidant assessment of avenanthramides in oat products.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and instruments

All solvents (for both synthesis and analysis), sodium chloride, anhydrous sodium sulfate, and the inorganic acids and bases were purchased from Samchun Chemicals (Seoul, Republic of Korea). Benzaldehyde and ethyl crotonate were purchased from Alfa Aesar (Haverhill, MA, USA). 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry (TCI, Tokyo, Japan). The deuterated NMR solvents, the derivatization reagent N,O-bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide containing 1% trimethylchlorosilane (BFTSA), pyridine, silica gel for column chromatography, and all other synthetic precursors were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). All reagents were of analytical grade and used without further purification. For rapid and reproducible analysis of reaction outcomes, gas chromatography coupled with a mass spectrometer (GC–MS) was employed to monitor the reactions. GC–MS was conducted with a GCMS-QP2010SE (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) after derivatization with BFTSA. The final products were confirmed using high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). HRMS analysis was conducted with an LCMS-9030 (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan), with samples dissolved in methanol. For NMR analysis, 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra were recorded using a JEOL-JNMECZ400S (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan), with samples dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)-d6 or methanol‑d4 as a solvent. In addition, the purity of all synthesized AVNs was confirmed to be greater than 98% by high-performance liquid chromatography with diode array detection (HPLC-DAD), with detection at 210 nm, in parallel with HRMS characterization. Qualitative analysis details for each compound were provided in the Supplementary material.

For quantitative analysis study, commercial oat products were purchased from local market, including oat grains (sample A, B, C), oatmeal (sample D, E), and oat grain powder (sample F).

Synthetic procedure of AVNs through the Verley-Doebner modification of the Knoevenagel condensation (Fig. 2A)

Meldrum's acid (1.73 g) was dissolved in toluene (100 mL), and an anthranilic acid derivative 1a-1e (10 mmol) was added. The reaction mixture was refluxed using a Dean-Stark apparatus for 4 h. After cooling to room temperature, the resulting suspension was filtered, washed with toluene (100 mL), and dried.

The dried intermediate was suspended in toluene (50 mL), and a benzaldehyde 2a-2e or cinnamaldehyde 3a-3e (2.26 mmol) was added. Piperidine (0.22 mL, 2.26 mmol) was then added dropwise, and the reaction mixture was refluxed for 4 h. After cooling, the suspension was filtered through a glass filter. The piperidinium salt was washed with toluene (50 mL), dissolved in methanol/water (5:2, v/v; total 7 mL), warmed at 40 °C, and acidified with concentrated HCl. Water (20 mL) was added to induce precipitation, and the precipitate was collected by filtration and dried.

Synthetic procedure of AVNs through acylation of amine (Fig. 2B)

A hydroxy-protected phenylalkenoic acid 4a-4e or 5a-5e (1.0 mmol) was dissolved in dry DCM (50 mL). Oxalyl chloride (0.38 g, 3.0 mmol, 3.0 equiv) and DMF (one drop) were added, and the mixture was stirred for 3–4 h to form the corresponding acid chloride. Volatile materials were removed under reduced pressure, and the crude acid chloride was used without isolation. The residue was redissolved in dry DCM (10 mL) and added to a stirred solution of a hydroxy-protected anthranilic acid 6a-6e (1.2 mmol, 1.2 equiv) in anhydrous pyridine (10 mL) at −10 °C. After 1 h at −10 °C, the reaction mixture was warmed to room temperature, stirred overnight, and diluted with water (100 mL). The mixture was acidified with 1 M HCl and extracted with EtOAc (2 × 100 mL). The combined organic layers were washed with brine (2 × 100 mL), dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and concentrated under reduced pressure to afford the protected amide intermediate. For deprotection, 1 M HCl in methanol (10 mL) was added to the residue and the mixture was stirred at room temperature overnight. The reaction mixture was worked up by extraction with EtOAc (200 mL), and the organic layer was dried over anhydrous Na2SO4 and concentrated. The residue was purified by silica gel column chromatography using EtOAc/methanol as the eluent. C-type and A-type AVNs used in the subsequent experiments were prepared by this procedure, and the isolated yields are summarized in the Supplementary material.

Synthesis of hydroxy-protected benzaldehydes 7b-7e

A benzaldehyde derivative 2b-2e (20 mmol) was dissolved in DCM (100 mL). DIPEA (7.8 g per phenolic hydroxyl group, approximately 60 mmol and 3 equiv. per hydroxyl group) and 6.5 M MOMCl solution (9.2 mL per phenolic hydroxyl group, approximately 60 mmol and 3 equiv. per hydroxyl group; Supplementary material) were added sequentially. The mixture was stirred for 9 h at room temperature and diluted with an equal volume of DCM. The organic phase was washed sequentially with 1 M HCl, brine, 1 M NaOH, and brine (200 mL each), dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and concentrated under reduced pressure. Isolated yields were as follows: 7b, 3.26 g (98%); 7c, 4.34 g (96%); 7d, 3.83 g (98%); 7e, 4.39 g (97%).

Synthesis of 5-phenylpentadi-2,4-enoic acid isomer mixtures 8b-8e

A hydroxy-protected benzaldehyde 7b-7e (10 mmol) was dissolved in DMSO (10 mL), and ethyl crotonate (1.48 g, 13 mmol, 1.3 equiv) was added. A solution of KOH (0.67 g, 12 mmol, 1.2 equiv) in water (2 mL) was added dropwise, and the mixture was stirred for 6 h at room temperature. The reaction mixture was diluted with water (200 mL) and acidified with 1 M HCl. The aqueous phase was extracted with DCM (200 mL), and the organic extract was back-extracted with 1 M NaOH (200 mL). The resulting basic aqueous phase was acidified with 2 M HCl and extracted with DCM (200 mL). The final organic layer was dried over anhydrous Na2SO4 and concentrated under reduced pressure. Isolated yields were as follows: 8b, 2.29 g (98%); 8c, 2.79 g (95%); 8d, 2.53 g (96%); 8e, 2.83 g (96%).

Synthesis of trans-trans-methyl 5-phenylpentadi-2,4-enoates 9b-9e

A 5-phenylpentadi-2,4-enoic acid isomer mixture 8b-8e (9.5 mmol) was dissolved in methanol (10 mL), and 10 M HCl aqueous solution (1 mL) was added dropwise. The mixture was stirred at room temperature overnight. After completion, the mixture was diluted with water (200 mL) and extracted with EtOAc (2 × 100 mL). The organic layer was dried over anhydrous Na2SO4 and concentrated under reduced pressure. Isolated yields were as follows: 9b, 1.92 g (99%); 9c, 2.05 g (98%); 9d, 2.20 g (99%); 9e, 2.45 g (98%).

Synthesis of methyl trans-trans-5-(3,4-bis(methoxymethoxy)phenyl)penta-2,4-dienoate 11

Methyl trans-trans-5-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)penta-2,4-dienoate 9c (9 mmol) was dissolved in DCM (50 mL), DIPEA (3.49 g, 27 mmol, 3.0 equiv) was added, and 6.5 M MOMCl solution (4.5 mL) was added dropwise. The mixture was stirred for 6 h at room temperature, diluted with 0.1 M HCl aqueous solution (200 mL), and extracted with DCM (200 mL). The organic phase was washed with 0.5 M NaOH aqueous solution and brine (100 mL each), dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and concentrated under reduced pressure to give 11 (2.64 g, 95%).

Syntheses of avenalumic acids 10b, 10d, and 10e and trans-trans-5-(3,4-bis-(methoxymethoxy)phenyl)penta-2,4-dienoic acid 12

For each substrate, trans-trans-methyl 5-phenylpentadi-2,4-enoate 9b, 9d, or 9e (9 mmol), or methyl trans-trans-5-(3,4-bis(methoxymethoxy)phenyl)penta-2,4-dienoate 11 (8.5 mmol), was dissolved in methanol (25 mL). Aqueous lithium hydroxide solution (0.4 M, 25 mL) was added, and additional methanol was added as needed until dissolution was complete. The mixture was stirred for 6 h at room temperature, acidified with 1 M HCl, and extracted with EtOAc (200 mL). The organic layer was dried over anhydrous Na2SO4 and concentrated under reduced pressure. Isolated yields were as follows: 10b, 1.66 g (97%); 10d, 1.92 g (97%); 10e, 2.16 g (96%); 12, 2.45 g (98%).

Synthesis of trans-trans-5-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)penta-2,4-dienoic acid 10c

Trans-trans-5-(3,4-bis-(methoxymethoxy)phenyl)penta-2,4-dienoic acid 12 (8 mmol) was dissolved in DMSO (9 mL), and 10 M HCl (1 mL) was added. The mixture was stirred for 9 h at room temperature, diluted with water (250 mL), and extracted with EtOAc (150 mL). The organic layer was washed with brine (2 × 100 mL), dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and concentrated under reduced pressure to give 10c (1.60 g, 97%).

Synthesis of hydroxy-protected 5-phenylpentadi-2,4-enoic acids 5b-5e

An avenalumic acid 10b-10e (5 mmol) was dissolved in anhydrous pyridine (20 mL), and DMAP (0.02 g, 0.16 mmol) and acetic anhydride (Ac2O, 0.61 g, 6.0 mmol per phenolic hydroxyl group) were added. The mixture was stirred for 6 h at room temperature and then diluted with 0.1 M HCl aqueous solution (100 mL). The aqueous phase was extracted with EtOAc (2 × 100 mL). The combined organic layers were washed with brine (2 × 100 mL), dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and concentrated under reduced pressure. Isolated yields were as follows: 5b, 1.09 g (94%); 5c, 1.32 g (92%); 5d, 1.23 g (93%); 5e, 1.36 g (93%).

Radical scavenging activity assay

The antioxidant activity was assessed using the synthetic avenanthramide standards prepared in this study (all >98% pure). The DPPH assay was performed according to a previous study (Lee et al., 2015). The DPPH reagent was prepared by dissolving DPPH (8 mg) in methanol (100 mL). To evaluate radical scavenging activity, 100 μL of the DPPH reagent was mixed with 100 μL of each synthetic avenanthramide solution (0.1 mM) in a 96-well microplate and incubated under dark conditions at room temperature for 30 min. After reaction, absorbance was measured at 514 nm and methanol was used as the control. Trolox was used as positive control. Extraction of commercial oat products

Sample extraction was conducted following a modified method based on a previous study (Jágr et al., 2020). Oat samples were pre-milled using a Waring 7010HG blender (Torrington, CT, USA) and finely ground using a mortar and pestle with liquid nitrogen. 10 μL of 4,5-dimethoxy-2-((2E,4E)-5-phenylpenta-2,4-dienamido)benzoic acid (3 mg/L) was added to 0.1 g of the oat sample as internal standard. For extraction, 1 mL of methanol/water (80:20, v/v) was added to the sample, followed by sonication at 50 °C for 60 min. After cooling, the samples were centrifuged at 18,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. Extraction was repeated five times, and the combined extracts were evaporated at 40 °C. The residues were redissolved in 1 mL of methanol and filtered through a 0.2 μm nylon syringe filter. Samples were stored at −24 °C prior to high-performance liquid chromatography-electrospray-tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC/ESI-MS/MS) analysis.

Quantitation of AVNs

Stock solutions of each AVN were initially prepared in methanol at a concentration of 500 mg/L and subsequently diluted to 0.01, 0.02, 0.04, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 1, and 2 mg/L to generate calibration standards. Two structurally analogous compounds were used as internal standards: Tranilast, which is structurally analogous to the C-type avenanthramides, and 4,5-dimethoxy-2-((2E,4E)-5-phenylpenta-2,4-dienamido)benzoic acid, which is structurally analogous to the A-type avenanthramides. Each internal standard was prepared at a concentration of 600 μg/L in methanol. Working standard solutions were obtained by mixing equal volumes of each AVN dilution with the internal standard solution (1:1, v/v). Quantification was carried out based on calibration curves constructed from the peak area ratios of individual AVNs to the internal standard.

Quantitation was performed using an HPLC/ESI-MS/MS system (LC: ExionLC AC; MS: API 3200, ABSciex, Toronto, Canada). Data were acquired in negative ion mode, with nitrogen used as curtain gas (35 psi), collision gas (6 psi), nebulizing gas (40 psi), and drying gas (60 psi). A Kinetex C18 column (100 × 4.6 mm, 2.6 μm, Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) was used for chromatographic separation. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (v/v) (mobile phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (v/v) (mobile phase B). The gradient elution was applied at a flow rate of 0.7 mL/min as follows: flow rate of 0.7 mL/min; 0–1 min, 15% B; 1–5 min, 15–40% B; 5–8 min, 40–75% B; 8–11 min, 75–100% B; 11–13 min, 100% B; 13–14 min, 100–15% B; 14–15 min 15% B. The optimized parameters were set as follows: ionspray voltage: 4.5 kV; heater temperature: 600 °C. The multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) conditions were set with a declustering potential energy of −40 eV, an entrance potential energy of −5 eV, a cell entrance potential energy of −20 eV, and a collision cell exit potential energy of −4 eV. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted to evaluate the effects of three structural features, such as anthranilic acid moiety (AA), phenylalkenoic acid moiety (CA), and the number of double bonds (DB), on the DPPH radical scavenging activity of AVN derivatives. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.0). Each variable was treated as a categorical factor, and their effects were first examined using a three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). To identify specific group differences within each factor, Duncan's multiple range test was applied as a post hoc comparison. In addition, a multiple linear regression model was constructed to quantify the relative contribution of each structural group to antioxidant activity, for which categorical variables were dummy-coded. Model performance was assessed by adjusted R2 and the F-statistic, and statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05.

During the reaction, the malonic acid derivative forms a carbanion through deprotonation by a base, which then reacts with the aldehyde to form an alkoxide intermediate (Mouterde & Allais, 2018). While C-type AVNs undergo a rearrangement process followed by decarboxylation, A-type AVNs proceeded extremely slowly through this pathway, resulting in diacid formation. This issue persisted despite using pyridine and β-alanine as the solvent and catalyst, respectively (Kamat et al., 2007).

According to previous studies, such results could potentially arise from resonance stabilization. Bermudez et al. emphasized the significance of reaction time, base, and temperature in the decarboxylation step (Bermúdez et al., 2010). In case of 4-hydroxybenzaldehyde, decarboxylation was favored at temperatures above 50 °C, leading to the formation of p-hydroxycinnamic acid (Rioux et al., 2020). In contrast, at lower temperatures, diacid formation was favored over p-hydroxycinnamic acid production. This suggested that the decarboxylation reaction required higher temperatures and greater energy compared to diacid formation (Mouterde et al., 2018). In reactions of C-type AVNs, decarboxylation proceeded efficiently at the set temperature. However, in A-type AVNs, resonance stabilization provided by the α,β-unsaturated system hindered the progression of decarboxylation. This resonance effect electronically stabilized the intermediate, making the formation of the transition state less favorable and reducing the thermodynamic driving force required for decarboxylation (Oae et al., 1968). Mechanistically, this decarboxylation proceeds through a six-membered cyclic transition state in which the carboxylate is expelled as CO2 while the developing carbanion is stabilized as the conjugated enol; in the A-type system the additional vinyl group extends conjugation across the dienylidene framework, delocalizing this incipient negative charge and stabilizing the diacid ground state, which raises the activation barrier of the cyclic transition state and renders decarboxylation kinetically disfavored relative to the mono-unsaturated C-type analogues.

Consequently, the conversion from the diacid intermediate to the final product through decarboxylation required a relatively longer reaction time. Although this method had the advantage of proceeding without deprotection, the reaction often did not proceed efficiently, and the formation of a difficult-to-separate diacid became problematic. In some cases, extending the reaction time helped, but for the key compounds, significant amounts of the diacid remained even after prolonged reactions, making this approach unsuitable.

Synthesis of A-type AVNs using acylation of amine

The synthesis of A-type AVNs through the conversion of hydroxy-protected avenalumic acids 5a-5e into the corresponding aryl chloride, followed by acylation and deprotection, was performed (Fig. 2B). To initially evaluate this synthetic approach, AVN 1ad, 1pd, 1fd, 1cd, and 1sd was synthesized from commercially available 5-phenylpentadienoic acid 5a, confirming the feasibility of the acylation strategy. Subsequently, structurally diverse 5-phenylpentadienoic acid derivatives 5b-e were synthesized via the method described in Section 3.3 and employed in the synthesis of A-type AVNs. This method resulted in high yields for all 25 compounds (1ad–5sd), which represent all possible combinations of five different anthranilic acids and five distinct 5-phenylalkenoic acids. Likewise, 25C-type AVNs were also synthesized using the same acylation strategy by employing cinnamic acid analogs instead of 5-phenylpentadienoic acid derivatives, completing the total library of 50 AVNs in this study.

For the acylation, compounds protected with acetic acid esters or methoxy methyl ethers (5a-5e and 6a-6e) were used as reactants. The synthetic routes of hydroxy-protected anthranilic acids 6a-6e are illustrated in Supplementary material. Hydroxy-protected 5-phenylpentadi-2,4-enoic acids 5a-5e was converted into acyl chloride using oxalyl chloride and then reacted with the amino groups of hydroxy-protected anthranilic acids 6a-6e. A-type AVNs were deprotected with methanolic HCl and purified by silica gel column chromatography.

This route decouples carbon–carbon double-bond construction from amide-bond formation: the dienoate is pre-installed in the avenalumic acid building block, so the rate-limiting decarboxylation that stalls the A-type Doebner pathway (Section 3.1) is bypassed, and the in situ–generated acyl chloride couples chemoselectively with the anthranilate amino group. This mechanistic separation accounts for the uniformly high yields obtained across all 25 A-type and 25C-type congeners, in contrast to the incomplete conversions of the condensation route.

Minimalizing synthetic route of hydroxy-protected 5-Phenylpentadi-2,4-enoic acids 5b-5e

In the synthesis of A-type AVNs using the acylation of anthranilic acids, 5-phenylpentadi-2,4-enoic acids 5a-5e served as the key intermediate. Previous studies required seven synthetic steps from ferulic acid to obtain 10c (total yield: 66.5%), leading to high reagent consumption and material loss (Bazin et al., 2008). This study presents an alternative approach that enables synthesis from substituted benzaldehyde in only four or six steps (Fig. 2C) with higher yields and using fewer and more economical reagents (total yield: 10b, 92.2%; 10c, 80.7%; 10d, 90.3%; 10e, 87.6%).

A mixture of isomers 8 was obtained by reaction of hydroxy-protected benzaldehydes 7b-7e with ethyl crotonate under strongly basic conditions, following a modified version of Backstrom et al.'s method (Backstrom et al., 1989). The previous method utilized N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone as the reaction solvent, resulting in the formation of its oxide. To minimize impurity formation and improve yield, both the solvent and base catalyst were optimized. Methoxymethyl ether deprotection was performed using methanolic HCl. During the reaction, the isomer mixture was converted into (2E,4E) esters 9b-9e. In the course of Fischer esterification using methanolic HCl, protonation generated allylic carbocation intermediates, which underwent free rotation around the single bonds due to resonance stabilization. This allowed thermodynamic equilibration, favoring the formation of the more stable (2E,4E) isomers (Kumpaty et al., 2009; Vafaeezadeh & Fattahi, 2015). Subsequently, avenalumic acids 10 was synthesized by removing the methyl group with NaOH. However, in case of methyl trans-trans-5-(3,4-bis(methoxymethoxy)phenyl)penta-2,4-dienoate 9c containing phenolic ortho-dihydroxy groups, the reaction resulted in darkening of the reaction mixture and a markedly low yield. To prevent degradation, methyl trans-trans-5-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)penta-2,4-dienoate 9c was protected with a methoxymethyl group (11), followed by sequential deprotection of the methyl ester (12) and methoxymethyl ether (10b). Before acylation, avenalumic acid 10b-10e was converted into the corresponding acetic acid esters 5b-10e and then reacted with hydroxy-protected anthranilic acids 6a-6e.

Importantly, our study is the first to report complete isomeric conversion from a cis-trans/trans-trans mixture into a pure trans-trans form. This was achieved through methoxymethyl ether deprotection using methanolic HCl, which induced thermodynamic equilibration via allylic carbocation intermediates. Conversion to the trans-trans form was confirmed by GC–MS analysis of methyl avenalumate 9a (single peak; Fig. 4A) and by 1H NMR spectra, which exhibited coupling constants in the range of 14–16 Hz characteristic of trans configuration (Fig. 4B) (Soderberg, 2012). Additionally, 1H NMR analysis of AVNs in subsequent steps confirmed the absence of other isomers (Supplementary material 8). This exclusive trans-trans configuration is crucial given the impact of geometric isomerism on bioactivity, as previously observed in natural compounds such as piperine (Li et al., 2023; Okwute & Egharevba, 2013; Quijia & Chorilli, 2020). Thus, our synthetic approach not only improves efficiency and yield but also ensures precise control over bioactive isomer formation, representing a novel contribution to A-type AVN synthesis. Based on a comparison of previously reported synthetic routes, we developed an optimized and more efficient method for synthesizing key 5-phenylpentadienoic acid intermediates. This improved synthetic strategy reduced the number of steps and increased the overall yield, enabling the successful synthesis of all 50 known AVNs (Supplementary material 5). The structures of these compounds were validated by HRMS and NMR spectroscopy (Supplementary material 7, 8). These findings are expected to accelerate future research on AVNs and related bioactive phenylalkenoic acid derivatives.

DPPH radical scavenging activity

DPPH radical scavenging activity is summarized in Supplementary material 9. To evaluate the contribution of each structural domain to antioxidant capacity, three structural components (AA, CA, and DB) were analyzed using box plots (Fig. 5) and a three-way ANOVA, which revealed statistically significant main effects for all three domains. Post hoc comparisons using Duncan's multiple range test further clarified distinct group-wise differences. For the CA region, group (c) exhibited the highest antioxidant activity, followed by groups (f) and (s), whereas groups (a) and (p) showed significantly lower activity, consistent with previous findings. These findings align with previous studies conducted on only C-type AVNs (Fagerlund et al., 2009). In the AA region, group (5) demonstrated the highest activity, followed by group (3), while groups (1) and (4) were significantly lower. Notably, groups (2) and (4) shared identical functional groups but differed in substitution position; group (2) showed significantly greater activity than group (4), indicating that antioxidant potential is also influenced by substitution position. Specifically, 5-hydroxy substitution in the AA moiety appeared to confer greater activity than 4-hydroxy substitution. This positional dependence suggests that the relative orientation of the phenolic hydroxyl group and the electron-withdrawing carboxamide group may influence aroxyl-radical stabilization within the anthranilate ring. However, the underlying electronic contribution has not yet been directly established and should be further evaluated using computational approaches, such as bond-dissociation enthalpy analysis. DPPH scavenging by these phenolic amides proceeds predominantly through hydrogen-atom transfer and sequential proton-loss electron-transfer from the phenolic hydroxyl (Foti et al., 2004; Litwinienko & Ingold, 2007), so that activity is governed by the phenolic O—H bond-dissociation enthalpy and by resonance stabilization of the resulting aroxyl radical (Leopoldini et al., 2011; Wright et al., 2001). This framework rationalizes the observed CA ranking: the catechol of the caffeoyl group (c) supports an intramolecular hydrogen bond and a resonance-stabilized ortho-semiquinone radical, lowering its O—H bond-dissociation enthalpy and conferring the highest activity (Foti et al., 2004; Wright et al., 2001), whereas the methoxy groups of the feruloyl (f) and sinapoyl (s) units stabilize the aroxyl radical by electron donation but less effectively than a second ortho-hydroxyl, and the p-coumaroyl (p) and unsubstituted (a) units, lacking radical-stabilizing ortho substituents, are the weakest. The predominance of these local substituent effects over the double-bond count is consistent with the small DB contribution (+2.79 units) and with the previous observation that conjugation across the amide bond contributes only marginally to radical scavenging (Fagerlund et al., 2009).

The radical scavenging capacity of compounds is mechanistically linked to their potential biological activities under oxidative stress conditions (Brand-Williams et al., 1995; Gulcin & Alwasel, 2023). Radical scavenging is a critical determinant in cellular redox homeostasis, and compounds with high DPPH activity have often been shown to attenuate oxidative damage by modulating antioxidant defense systems such as the Nrf2 pathway, or by directly inhibiting reactive oxygen species-induced signaling cascades (Gulcin et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2003; Valko et al., 2007). In the case of C-type AVNs, several studies have reported their biological relevance in both cell-based and in vivo models, where they demonstrated anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective effects, including the downregulation of inducible nitric oxide synthase and suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokine expression in activated macrophages (Xie et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2022). Given that multiple A-type AVNs evaluated in this study exhibited DPPH scavenging activities comparable to or even higher than their C-type counterparts, it is plausible that A-type AVNs are also potential to modulate similar biological pathways under oxidative conditions. Considering that structural features, such as double bond configurations, can influence membrane permeability and bioavailability in practical applications, A-type AVNs were potential bioactive materials in the future (Wadhwa et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2018).

Previous studies have quantified A-type AVNs, including AVN 2pd, 2fd, and 2cd, by extrapolating calibration curves from their corresponding C-type AVNs (AVN 2p, 2f, and 2c) (Jágr et al., 2020; Pridal et al., 2018). In some studies, A-type AVNs have been synthesized and used as standards; however, extrapolation still occurs in these cases (Hu et al., 2019). A comparison of the calibration curve slopes measured in this study revealed clear differences among individual AVNs. The slope of an LC–ESI-MS/MS calibration curve reflects the analyte-specific response factor, which depends on both electrospray ionization efficiency and collision-induced dissociation behavior of the selected MRM transition (Cech & Enke, 2001). These processes are affected by molecular properties, including acidity, ionizability, surface activity, charge competition, and the stability of precursor and product ions. Accordingly, even closely related AVN analogues may not exhibit comparable calibration responses. The additional conjugated double bond in A-type AVNs is expected to modify electron distribution relative to C-type analogues, which may influence deprotonation and product-ion formation (Kruve et al., 2014). For instance, the calibration slope of AVN 2p was 1.6587, whereas that of AVN 2pd was 1.0661, indicating a significant difference. These findings demonstrate that quantification using extrapolation is inaccurate.

To ensure precise quantification, it is essential to obtain authentic standards for each A-type AVN through the synthetic method presented in this study and establish individual calibration curves. This approach is expected to improve the accuracy and reliability of the analysis.

However, when A-type AVNs were quantified using the calibration curves of C-type AVNs, as in previous studies, the estimated concentrations of AVN 2pd, 2fd, and 2cd were significantly lower than the actual measured values, accounting for only 66.2%, 35.9%, and 80.7%, respectively (Jágr et al., 2020). This result indicates that the previous extrapolation-based quantification method underestimates A-type AVN concentrations. Actually, the proportions of A-type AVNs (2pd, 2 cd, and 2fd) relative to the total AVNs (2p, 2c, 2f, 2pd, 2 cd, and 2fd) in this study ranged from 29.9% to 49.0%, which are higher than the previously reported levels of A-type AVNs in oats (14.7% to 32.4%) (Jágr et al., 2020). While A-type AVNs were generally present in lower amounts than C-type AVNs, Product D contained a substantial proportion of A-type AVNs, accounting for 46.4% of the total detected AVNs. Although C-type AVNs are generally more abundant, the findings suggest that A-type AVNs may also contribute to the bioactivity of oats.

These results emphasize the necessity of individual quantification for each AVN, demonstrating that the conventional extrapolation method leads to inaccurate quantification. Furthermore, the calibration method developed in this study, utilizing synthetic AVN standards, enabled more precise quantification, confirming its critical role in accurately assessing AVNs content in oat products.

Because each A-type/C-type pair exhibits a distinct response-factor ratio (e.g., 0.64 for 2pd/2p), no single extrapolation factor can correct C-type-based estimates across analytes; accurate quantification therefore requires compound-specific calibration with authentic A-type standards. The synthetic standards established in this study provide exactly these analyte-matched calibrants, eliminating the slope-derived bias and enabling unbiased determination of A-type AVN content in oat matrices.

Conclusion

This study established a practical foundation for accurate compositional and functional evaluation of AVNs by expanding the availability of authentic reference standards and demonstrating their analytical necessity. A comprehensive set of fifty AVNs, including both A-type and C-type structures, was synthesized and rigorously characterized, with structural confirmation by NMR and HRMS and purity verification by HPLC-DAD (>98%). The resulting standards enabled compound-specific calibration for LC-MS/MS analysis and revealed substantial differences in calibration slopes between A-type and C-type counterparts. These findings demonstrate that conventional C-type-based calibration introduces systematic underestimation of A-type AVNs. When applied to commercial oat products, extrapolation using C-type calibration provided only 35.9–80.7% of the concentrations obtained with authentic standards, and A-type AVNs accounted for 29.9–49.0% of total AVNs within the quantified panel (2p, 2f, 2c, 2pd, 2fd, and 2cd). In parallel, the DPPH radical scavenging assay indicated that several A-type AVNs exhibit antioxidant capacity comparable to, or greater than, their C-type counterparts, supporting the functional relevance of A-type structures in oat matrices. Collectively, these results indicate that authentic standards and compound-specific calibration are essential to minimize quantification bias and to support reliable interpretation of avenanthramide composition and antioxidant potential in oat products. Future studies should extend standard-enabled quantification to a broader range of oat matrices and processing conditions and expand bioactivity profiling beyond radical scavenging to better link chemical composition with health-relevant functions.
