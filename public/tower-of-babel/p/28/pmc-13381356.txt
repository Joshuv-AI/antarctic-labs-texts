Prognostic Significance of Preoperative Albumin and Prealbumin in Predicting Adverse Events After Posterior Lumbar Interbody Fusion in Elderly Patients
Authors: Sitao Zhang, Peng Wang, Shuaikang Wang, Di Han, Shibao Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsThere is growing evidence that nutritional status is a predictor of prognosis for elderly patients, However, specific threshold values for albumin and prealbumin are still lacking for elderly patients undergoing posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) surgery. This study aims to determine these thresholds in elderly PLIF patients and to perform risk stratification analysis using the combination of albumin and prealbumin.MethodsA total of 660 elderly patients undergoing PLIF surgery at Xuan Wu Hospital from November 2018 to April 2024 were included in this retrospective analysis. The optimal cut-off concentrations of albumin and prealbumin for predicting adverse events (AEs) were determined via ROC curve analysis. Subsequently, patients were categorized into four groups according to these cut-off values: Coapa-hh (both markers above cut-off), Coapa-hl (albumin above, prealbumin below), Coapa-lh (albumin below, prealbumin above), and Coapa-ll (both below cut-off).ResultsChi-square analysis revealed that the Coapa-ll group had the highest perioperative complication rate (P < 0.001). Surgical site infection rates were lower in the Coapa-hh (0.90%) and Coapa-hl (0.70%) groups than in the Coapa-lh (5.63%) and Coapa-ll (4.14%) groups (P = 0.023). Patients in the Coapa-hh group also had significantly shorter total and postoperative hospital stays (P = 0.04 and P = 0.019, respectively). Multivariable logistic regression confirmed that the Coapa classification was independently associated with overall complications after PLIF surgery.ConclusionOur findings suggest that the Coapa should be viewed as a convenient screening tool. It can identify high-risk elderly patients undergoing PLIF surgery who may benefit from perioperative nutritional optimization. This provides valuable guidance for reducing postoperative complications, improving clinical outcomes, and informing nursing interventions.Keywords: nutrition, PLIF, elderly patients, prealbumin, Albumin, complication

Introduction

Degenerative disc disease, recurrent disc herniation, and sagittal imbalance are prevalent spinal disorders of the elderly.1–3 With the accelerating trend of global population aging, the incidence of lumbar degenerative diseases continues to rise, which pose a growing clinical challenge and frequently result in significant deterioration in the quality of life for affected individuals.4 Driven by the growing societal emphasis on extending life expectancy and improving functional well-being, an increasing number of elderly patients are opting for surgical intervention to alleviate refractory low back pain and lower extremity radicular symptoms. Posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) is widely recognized as a standard surgical treatment for such spinal conditions, demonstrating efficacy in pain relief and functional improvement.5 Although advancements in surgical techniques have substantially enhanced overall patient outcomes, postoperative recovery in the elderly remains a significant clinical challenge, with complication rates reported as high as 28.8%, which directly impacts long-term prognosis and quality of life.6,7 Therefore, identifying risk factors for postoperative adverse events (AEs) and developing targeted strategies to optimize surgical outcomes hold considerable clinical importance.

Perioperative nutritional status is closely linked not only to the risk of postoperative complications but also to long-term patient prognosis.8–10 Malnutrition can lead to immune dysfunction, heightened inflammatory responses, and delayed or impaired wound healing, all of which are critical determinants of surgical outcomes and contribute directly to increased complication rates following PLIF.11,12 Elderly patients are particularly susceptible to malnutrition due to age-related physiological declines. Albumin, synthesized by hepatocytes with a plasma half-life of approximately 15–19 days, is commonly utilized as a biomarker for assessing preoperative nutritional status.13 Previous studies have established that hypoalbuminemia is associated with a higher incidence of perioperative AEs following elective spinal surgery.14,15 Prealbumin, also known as transthyretin, has a substantially shorter half-life of about 2 days and exhibits greater sensitivity to acute changes in protein-energy balance, making it a more responsive indicator of current nutritional status.16,17 Accordingly, low serum prealbumin levels have shown a stronger correlation with AEs after spinal surgery. From a mechanistic perspective, protein-energy malnutrition impairs collagen synthesis by reducing the availability of essential amino acids, which in turn slows fibroblast proliferation and delays wound closure, ultimately compromising tissue repair after spinal surgery. Hypoalbuminemia reflects chronic protein depletion impairing collagen synthesis and immune function; low prealbumin indicates acute-phase response and inadequate protein-energy intake.

Low prealbumin levels trigger the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, amplifying the acute-phase response and promoting systemic inflammation, which further exacerbates postoperative complications. Furthermore, in the elderly population, poor nutritional status accelerates the loss of muscle mass (sarcopenia) and reduces physiological reserve (frailty). Conversely, sarcopenia and frailty impair dietary intake and nutrient absorption, creating a vicious cycle. This sarcopenia–frailty–malnutrition interaction leads to reduced mobility, increased fall risk, and poorer tolerance to surgical stress, all of which collectively elevate the risk of postoperative AEs following PLIF. Conventional thresholds for malnutrition, such as albumin <3.5 g/dL and prealbumin <15 mg/dL, have been widely applied in previous research.18,19 However, these cut-off values are derived from mixed-age populations. Given the reduced physiological reserve and inherently higher surgical risk in the elderly, the applicability of these cut-off values in this vulnerable population remains unclear. Therefore, establishing elderly-specific thresholds is not only a scientific necessity but also a clinical priority.

Critically, relying on a single biomarker may be insufficient for preoperative risk assessment in geriatric patients. Albumin, as a marker of chronic protein status, responds slowly to acute changes, whereas prealbumin, although more sensitive to recent nutritional intake, can be confounded by inflammation and renal function. Consequently, neither marker alone captures the full spectrum of malnutrition—from chronic depletion to acute decompensation—that characterizes the high-risk elderly surgical population. To address this gap, a novel composite index integrating both albumin and prealbumin is proposed. This index is designed to harness the complementary strengths of these two markers, providing a more holistic and dynamic assessment of nutritional status than either biomarker alone.

Therefore, establishing elderly-specific thresholds is not only a scientific necessity but also a clinical priority. The central innovation of this study lies in establishing and validating an elderly-specific composite threshold. Specifically, this study aims to determine preoperative serum albumin and prealbumin cut-off values tailored to patients aged ≥75 years undergoing PLIF and to further evaluate the predictive utility of their combined use for postoperative AEs. The findings are intended to provide a clinically relevant reference for more accurately identifying high-risk elderly patients, guiding targeted perioperative nutritional management, and ultimately improving surgical outcomes within enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways.

Materials and Methods

Participants

This study was conducted in the department of orthopedics, Xuanwu Hospital Capital Medical University, also known as National Clinical Research Center for Geriatric Diseases, from November 2018 to April 2024. Subjects were patients older than 75 years who underwent 1 to 5-level interbody fusion by PLIF. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) circumferential fusion (ie, combined anterior and posterior surgery); (2) prior lumbar spinal surgery; (3) missing preoperative blood biochemical indices. Prior approval from the Ethics Committee for Human Subjects of Capital Medical University Xuanwu hospital (permit data 2018.4.3; no. 2018086) and fully complies with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent prior to the start of the study, potential risks and benefits, and data privacy protection measures were thoroughly explained to ensure participants fully understood and voluntarily agreed to take part.

Data Collection

Clinical data were retrieved from the institutional electronic medical record system. Recorded preoperative variables included demographics, smoking and alcohol consumption status, primary diagnosis, modified 5-item frailty index (mFI-5), body mass index (BMI), and comorbidities. Perioperative laboratory parameters, including albumin and prealbumin, were systematically recorded, along with intraoperative data such as operative duration, number of fused levels, and estimated blood loss. Blood samples were collected in the morning after 8hour fast, 1 day after admission. Serum albumin and prealbumin levels were measured using standard automated techniques.

AEs were defined as any Clavien-Dindo grade ≥I event occurring during index hospitalization or within 90 days post-discharge, including: surgical site infection (superficial/deep/organ-space), delirium, urinary tract infection, pneumonia, deep vein thrombosis, cardiac events, reoperation and prolonged length of hospital stay (Prolonged LOS). Prolonged LOS was defined as postoperative hospital stay longer than the 75th percentile. Postoperative complications were graded according to the Clavien-Dindo classification. All patients were assessed by the same multidisciplinary team.

Grouping Criteria

Optimal cut-off values for albumin and prealbumin were established via ROC curve analysis. Using these thresholds, patients were categorized into four groups based on the combination of their biomarker levels relative to the cut-offs: both above (Coapa-hh); albumin above and prealbumin below (Coapa-hl); albumin below and prealbumin above (Coapa-lh); and both below (Coapa-ll). The Coapa-hh group was defined as having good nutritional status, whereas the Coapa-hl, Coapa-lh, and Coapa-ll groups were classified as exhibiting varying degrees of malnutrition.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Differences in mean values were tested with one-way analysis of variance and Student’s t-test for normal data. Data were expressed as means and standard deviations (Mean ± SD). Statistical analysis for categorical variable (such as nutritional status) was performed by the Chi-square test/ Fisher’s exact test, respectively. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed P value < 0.05. The primary endpoint was defined as the occurrence of any adverse event. Candidate variables with a P-value < 0.1 on univariate analysis were subsequently included in a multivariate logistic regression model. In addition, body mass index (BMI) and the 5-item modified frailty index (mFI-5) were identified as core confounders based on prior knowledge and were forced into the multivariable model regardless of their statistical significance in univariate analysis. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P-value < 0.05.

Logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association between albumin, prealbumin, and complications. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To address concerns regarding potential overfitting and data circularity, internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling with optimism correction (200 resamples). The following performance measures were evaluated: (1) discrimination, assessed by the C-statistic (area under the ROC curve, AUC); (2) calibration, assessed by calibration intercept, calibration slope, Eavg (mean absolute error), E50, E90, and Emax; and (3) overall prediction error, assessed by the Brier score. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.2) with the “pminternal” and “pROC” packages. Comparisons of the area under the ROC curves (AUCs) between Coapa and GNRI were performed using DeLong’s test for paired samples. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The difference in AUCs and its 95% confidence interval were also calculated to quantify the magnitude of the performance improvement.

Results

Of the 660 patients initially screened, 604 met the first three inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. This cohort was used to establish the cut-off values, stratify patients, and assess primary outcomes (Figure 1). The study population had a mean age of 78.76 ± 3.35 years, and 65.73% (n=397) were female.

Flow chart of the study.

ROC curve analysis identified optimal preoperative cut-off values for predicting perioperative AEs: serum albumin < 37.4 g/L (AUC = 0.62; 95% CI: 0.0.558–0.671; Figure 2) and serum prealbumin < 220.5 mg/L (AUC = 0.59; 95% CI: 0.545–0.653; Figure 2). In a supplementary analysis comparing the predictive performance of the Coapa classification with the widely used GNRI, Coapa demonstrated a higher AUC of 0.625 (95% CI: 0.573–0.676) compared with GNRI, which yielded an AUC of 0.570 (95% CI: 0.512–0.627). Paired DeLong’s test for correlated ROC curves revealed a statistically significant difference between the two AUCs (p = 0.0426), indicating that Coapa had superior discriminative ability for predicting postoperative AEs in this cohort.

Bootstrap internal validation (200 resamples) with optimism correction was performed to assess overfitting. For albumin, the apparent AUC was 0.6144, the optimism-corrected AUC was 0.6123 (optimism estimate: 0.34%), and the 95% CI was 0.5542–0.6749. For prealbumin, the apparent AUC was 0.5990, the optimism-corrected AUC was 0.5998 (optimism estimate: 0.12%), and the 95% CI was 0.5461–0.6565. These results demonstrate minimal overfitting and good generalizability.

There were no significant intergroup differences in gender, smoking, or alcohol consumption across the four groups. In contrast, the Coapa-ll group demonstrated significantly older age (79.34±3.63, p=0.027; Table 1), lower BMI (24.44±3.86, p=0.004; Table 1), and a higher proportion of frail patients (25.44%, p<0.001; Table 1). Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that a malnutrition index, calculated from the preoperative biomarkers albumin and prealbumin, serves as an effective and novel predictor of perioperative adverse events in elderly patients undergoing PLIF surgery.

Malnutrition is widely recognized as a detrimental factor in surgical patients across a wide spectrum of specialties, including cardiothoracic and orthopedic surgery, leading to prolonged hospitalization, increased healthcare expenditure, and higher rates of infection and mortality.16,20 Albumin, a classic marker of nutritional status, has been extensively investigated in studies involving elderly spinal patients, and it is well established that hypoalbuminemia is an independent risk factor for postoperative complications.21–23 Specifically, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Hu et al, which included over 86,000 participants, demonstrated that preoperative hypoalbuminemia was associated with a 3.17-fold increased risk of overall postoperative complications.24 Of note, this magnitude of effect underscores the central role of albumin assessment in the elderly population. However, given its relatively long half-life and the dynamic nature of nutritional changes in surgical patients, prealbumin (PA)—characterized as a negative acute-phase protein with a half-life of merely two days—has emerged as a valuable risk assessment tool in elderly patients undergoing spinal surgery.17,25 A study by Bao et al revealed a significant inverse correlation between prealbumin levels and the risk of wound complications, with a clear dose-response pattern: patients exhibiting prealbumin <0.10 g/L had a wound complication rate of 44.44%, substantially higher than those with preoperative levels ≥0.16 g/L.26 These results underscore the prognostic significance of prealbumin in this clinical setting. In addition, the 2021 CNS guideline recommends routine preoperative nutritional assessment for spinal surgery patients using serum albumin or prealbumin, noting that prealbumin < 20 mg/dL (0.20 g/L) increases risks of infection, wound complications, nonunion, and readmission.22 The distinct kinetic properties of these two markers underpin their complementary clinical utility. Albumin, with a half-life of approximately 20 days (requiring up to 100 days to reach steady state), serves as a reliable indicator of long-term nutritional status. In contrast, prealbumin’s short half-life of 2–3 days and rapid attainment of steady state within 10–15 days render it a more sensitive marker for detecting acute nutritional changes.27,28

In the present study, we developed a composite albumin-prealbumin index to evaluate perioperative AEs in 604 elderly patients (>75 years) undergoing PLIF. Unlike traditional single-threshold approaches, we instead employed the stratification methodology of Parel et al29 (2025), establishing population-specific cut-offs (albumin <37.4g/L and prealbumin <220.5mg/L) via ROC analysis. Using this classification, we found that patients stratified by these combined biomarkers exhibited significantly different perioperative outcomes. In particular, the Coapa-ll group had the highest incidence of perioperative complications (22.49%), 2.3-fold higher than the Coapa-hh group, and also demonstrated prolonged total and postoperative hospital stays. Moreover, multivariate analysis confirmed the independent predictive value of this combined classification system. In the present study, we observed that only the Coapa-hh group demonstrated a significant postoperative recovery in albumin levels, whereas the other three groups showed only modest, non-significant increases. Regarding prealbumin, no significant postoperative recovery was observed in any of the four groups. This finding contrasts with that of Bao et al26 who reported gradual postoperative prealbumin recovery in spinal tuberculosis patients aged >65 years. We postulate that this discrepancy may be attributed to the more advanced age of our cohort (all >75 years), who likely have diminished physiological reserve and an attenuated nutritional response to surgical stress. PLIF surgery imposes a substantial metabolic burden, typically lasting several hours with considerable blood loss, and is frequently complicated by postoperative gastrointestinal dysfunction that impedes adequate nutritional intake.30 Therefore, the dual assessment of albumin and prealbumin yields clinically meaningful prognostic insights and holds potential for guiding perioperative nutritional optimization strategies in elderly patients undergoing spinal surgery.31 We further compared the predictive performance of the Coapa classification with that of the GNRI (Figure 4), a well-established nutritional screening tool. In our cohort, Coapa achieved a higher AUC (0.625, 95% CI: 0.573–0.676) than GNRI (0.570, 95% CI: 0.512–0.627), with a statistically significant difference confirmed by paired DeLong’s test (p = 0.0426). This finding suggests that the Coapa classification may offer superior predictive value for postoperative AEs compared with GNRI in elderly PLIF patients, further supporting its potential as a practical and effective screening tool in this population.

ROC curves comparing the predictive performance of Coapa and GNRI. Coapa (red curve) achieved an AUC of 0.625, compared with 0.570 for GNRI (blue curve). The difference was statistically significant (p = 0.043 by DeLong’s test).

One notable finding in this study is that the Coapa-hl group (high albumin, low prealbumin) had a complication rate comparable to the well-nourished Coapa-hh group and significantly lower than the Coapa-lh group. This result appears counterintuitive given the established role of prealbumin as a sensitive nutritional marker. We offer the following interpretations. First, prealbumin is not only a nutritional marker but also a negative acute-phase protein that declines rapidly in response to inflammation. Low prealbumin in the Coapa-hl group may reflect mild subclinical inflammation rather than true protein-energy malnutrition, while their normal albumin levels indicate adequate physiological reserve. Second, albumin and prealbumin reflect different time windows of nutritional status. The Coapa-hl group may represent patients who are chronically well-nourished but experienced transient acute stress (eg, pain or anxiety) prior to blood sampling. Therefore, isolated low prealbumin should not be interpreted as a reliable predictor of poor outcomes without considering albumin levels. This paradox actually supports our rationale for developing a composite albumin-prealbumin classification.

Malnutrition in the elderly represents a critical clinical and public health challenge.32,33 Accordingly, preventing or correcting malnutrition in elderly patients is essential for improving postoperative outcomes. To address this issue, our center has long been dedicated to research on elderly spinal patients and has integrated preoperative nutritional optimization into the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocol, initiating nutritional intervention at the time of the first outpatient visit. Consistent with this approach, the findings of this study have clinical implications for optimizing preoperative nutritional reserves in elderly patients. Coapa should be viewed as a convenient screening tool to identify high-risk elderly patients who may benefit from perioperative nutritional optimization, rather than as a replacement for comprehensive nutritional evaluation.

It should be noted that in our study cohort, the mean albumin level was 38.35 ± 3.40 g/L and the mean prealbumin level was 222.44 ± 53.73 mg/L, which differs from findings reported in some previous studies. Furthermore, the overall incidence of perioperative complications was 14.74%. This favorable outcome may be attributed to the long-standing implementation of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols for elderly patients at our center.

The Coapa index offers clinicians a simple, low-cost screening tool to help identify high-risk elderly patients undergoing PLIF who may benefit from perioperative nutritional optimization. It is not meant to replace comprehensive nutritional assessment but rather serves as a convenient bedside screening approach. Future studies should compare the Coapa index against other established tools such as MNA-SF and NRS-2002 in larger, multi-center cohorts to further validate its utility.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the single-center retrospective design may introduce selection bias. The ERAS protocol already implemented at our center might have mitigated some complications, potentially underestimating the true effect size. Second, our data were confined to short-term outcomes following PLIF (perioperative complications and 30-day reoperation rates). Third, the study cohort consisted exclusively of elderly patients; whether these findings can be extrapolated to younger populations warrants further investigation. Fourth, although bootstrap internal validation showed minimal overfitting, the lack of external validation in an independent cohort limits the generalizability of our findings. Future prospective, multi-center studies with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up, and broader age ranges are needed to address these limitations and externally validate the Coapa index.

Conclusions

In summary, the Coapa offers clinicians a simple, low-cost screening tool. It helps identify high-risk elderly patients undergoing PLIF surgery who may benefit from perioperative nutritional optimization. Using this tool can help reduce postoperative complications, improve clinical outcomes, and guide nursing interventions. It is not meant to replace comprehensive nutritional assessment but rather serves as a convenient bedside screening approach.
