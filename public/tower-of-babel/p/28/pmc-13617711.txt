Peptide nanoarchitectonics of biomimetic nanozyme synergistically inhibits smooth muscle cell proliferation and promotes endothelial repair to reduce poststenting complications
Authors: Xiyue Rong, Wenli Zhang, Sijin Chen, Tingting Xia, Yun Liu, Yi Wang, Lian Xu, Qianying Du, Ying Luo, Bo Liu, Jia Liu, Jie Xu, Yu Zhang, Dajing Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrug-eluting stents are effective for treating cardiovascular disease (CVD), but the procedure can cause mechanical damage to the endothelium, and the released antiproliferative agents delay reendothelialization and impair barrier function, ultimately leading to in-stent restenosis and disruption of vascular homeostasis. Current poststenting therapies, aimed at reducing proliferation and inflammation, often neglect endothelial cell (EC) repair and vascular homeostasis. To address this, we propose the use of a biomimetic nanozyme, HMPB-RAPA@PM&LXW7 (HRPL). Hollow mesoporous Prussian blue (HMPB) nanoparticles serve as carriers for the antiproliferative drug rapamycin (RAPA), which is further modified with a peptide nanoarchitectonics platelet membrane (PM&LXW7). This modification not only targets smooth muscle cells (SMCs) and ECs but also promotes EC repair to accelerate reendothelialization. In response to the acidic inflammatory microenvironment, HRPL releases RAPA to inhibit SMC proliferation and prevent restenosis. Additionally, the nanozyme scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS), alleviates inflammation, and improves the vascular microenvironment. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments demonstrated that HRPL effectively accelerated reendothelialization, regulated SMC behavior, and reduced inflammation. In conclusion, this peptide nanoarchitectonics biomimetic nanozyme can promote endothelial repair, inhibit neointimal hyperplasia, modulate the inflammatory microenvironment, prevent restenosis, and restore vascular homeostasis, offering a promising approach to reduce complications following clinical stent implantation.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04737-z.Keywords: Smooth muscle cell proliferation, Repair of endothelial injury, Vascular microenvironment, Biomimetic nanozymes, ROS

Introduction

Stents provide mechanical support for narrowed blood vessels, effectively preventing acute blockage [1]. In particular, drug-eluting stents play a crucial role in the treatment of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [2, 3]. However, they can inhibit endothelial cell (EC) migration and proliferation, delaying healing but also causing vascular endothelial injury (VEI) and leading to complications such as inflammation, neointimal hyperplasia, and thrombosis [4, 5]. Postprocedural treatment after stent implantation primarily involves reducing inflammation and preventing neointimal hyperplasia, which are key factors in restenosis [6, 7]. Current treatment methods include the use of anti-inflammatory drugs, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, and drug-coated stents that release sirolimus or paclitaxel [8, 9]. These therapies alleviate restenosis by reducing inflammation and inhibiting the proliferation of smooth muscle cells (SMCs) [10, 11]. However, achieving a balance between preventing restenosis and reducing the risks of delayed endothelial healing and stent thrombosis remains challenging. Therefore, new strategies are urgently needed for the treatment of complications after stent implantation.

Complications after stent implantation are closely related to EC damage and SMC proliferation [12–14]. EC damage disrupts the vascular barrier and accelerates platelet adhesion and activation at the damaged site [15, 16]. Activated platelets trigger the coagulation cascade reaction, promoting thrombosis within the stent [17]. Moreover, they release excessive amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS), increasing oxidative stress and exacerbating local inflammation, thereby worsening the vascular inflammatory microenvironment [18, 19]. Endothelial hyperplasia is the main challenge after vascular stent implantation. The absence of ECs, activation of platelets, and release of growth factors prompt SMCs to transform from contractile to synthetic types and migrate to the intima [20]. These activated SMCs proliferate and secrete extracellular matrix (ECM), which leads to impaired endothelial function and abnormal thickening of the basement membrane, ultimately causing vascular lumen obstruction and affecting the long-term therapeutic effect [21]. Therefore, a synergistic treatment strategy targeting ECs, SMCs, and the microenvironment is crucial for restoring vascular homeostasis and improving the prognosis of patients after stent implantation [22, 23].

Nanomedicine has greatly transformed treatment methods for CVD [24, 25]. Early applications focused mainly on nanodrug coatings, which could increase the bioavailability of drugs, prolong their release time, and precisely target affected areas, thereby minimizing systemic side effects to the greatest extent possible [26, 27]. For instance, Wang et al. developed electrospun core–shell nanofibers composed of TEMPOL and rapamycin (RAPA), which have strong anti-inflammatory effects and can inhibit neointimal hyperplasia [28]. However, the preparation of such coatings requires high-precision technology, which leads to high costs and difficulties in large-scale production. The long-term safety of these coatings still requires further research. Recently, the use of nanozymes with different biophysical and chemical properties has emerged as a promising approach for preventing and treating CVD [29]. Li et al. developed antioxidant nanodrugs that effectively prevent the formation of new endomembrane tissue and restore vascular homeostasis [30]. Although progress has been made in inhibiting restenosis, the inability to repair endothelial damage limits its effectiveness in preventing thrombosis. With outstanding stability, low cost, and tunable catalytic performance, nanozymes have emerged as powerful alternatives to natural enzymes, and their applications have expanded across various biomedical fields including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and cardiovascular interventions [31–33].

To address these issues, it is necessary to develop nanozymes that can target damaged sites and interact synergistically with ECs, SMCs, and the surrounding microenvironment. Prussian blue nanozymes stand out because of their high surface area and mesoporous structure and because they possess efficient drug delivery and surface modification capabilities. Moreover, their strong ability to scavenge ROS can reduce oxidative stress and alleviate the inflammatory microenvironment [34]. Given the crucial role of platelets in hemostasis, inflammation, and neointimal hyperplasia, functionalizing nanozymes with platelet membrane (PM) can increase their targeted delivery capacity and enable their stable attachment to damaged ECs and inflammatory areas [35–37]. Integrin αvβ3 is highly expressed in activated ECs and SMCs, making it a key target. LXW7 is a disulfide-linked octapeptide (cGRGDdvc) that can act as a ligand for integrin αvβ3, further enhancing the targeted therapeutic effect by specifically targeting ECs and SMCs [38, 39]. LXW7 also promotes EC proliferation [40, 41], helping to restore the endothelial barrier while maintaining targeted accuracy. However, inhibiting the proliferation of SMCs to prevent neointimal hyperplasia remains a challenge. Studies have shown that RAPA can effectively inhibit the proliferation of SMCs in the early stage, thereby reducing the occurrence of neointimal hyperplasia. Therefore, the combined application of HPMB, RAPA, PM, and LXW7 as a synergistic treatment for post-stenting complications has promising application prospects [42, 43].

This study proposes an innovative strategy using the HMPB nanozyme as a carrier for RAPA. The surface of the HMPB nanozyme is coated with PM and functionalized by integrating the LXW7 peptide, which binds to integrins, resulting in a peptide nanoarchitectonics biomimetic nanozyme (HRPL). The combination of PM and LXW7 peptide enables targeted delivery to endothelial injury sites, promoting endothelial repair and reepithelialization. In an acidic inflammatory microenvironment, HRPL releases RAPA to inhibit smooth muscle cell proliferation and migration, preventing restenosis. HRPL also scavenges ROS, reducing oxidative stress and local inflammation and thereby improving the vascular microenvironment. A typical balloon-induced rat carotid artery injury model was used to evaluate the therapeutic effect, and the dual-targeting mechanism not only promoted endothelial repair but also alleviated restenosis and inflammation, indicating significant clinical therapeutic potential. In particular, after stent implantation, beyond conventional drug-eluting strategies, HRPL addresses the complexity of vascular healing by integrating precise dual-targeting with synchronous regulation of ECs and SMCs. This approach enables a comprehensive transition from passive restenosis prevention to active vascular microenvironment remodeling, restoring long-term vascular homeostasis (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Materials

Poly(vinylpyrrolidone) (PVP, K30), potassium ferricyanide (K₃[Fe(CN)₆]·3 H₂O), hydrochloric acid (HCl), ferric chloride (FeCl₃), and chloroform (CHCl₃) were purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). RAPA was obtained from MedChemExpress (MCE; Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). Prostaglandin E₁ was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Shanghai, China). Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) was acquired from Sigma‒Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Calcein-AM, 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), DiI, DiR, fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), paraformaldehyde, and 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). 1,2-Dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DPPC), DSPE-PEG₂₀₀₀, and cholesterol were obtained from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, AL, USA). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was sourced from Inner Mongolia Opcel Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Inner Mongolia, China). Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated anti-mouse/rat CD61 antibody was purchased from BioLegend (San Diego, CA, USA). Antibodies against CD62P, CD41, CD61, and CD47 were obtained from AiFang Biological (Hunan, China). Carrageenan was acquired from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). ELISA kits for interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-10 (IL-10), plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), and D-dimer were obtained from Jiangsu Sumeike Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China).

Synthesis of the HRPL nanozyme

First, PM extraction was carried out following procedures approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University and in compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Platelets were isolated from female SD rats (male, 6–8 weeks) using gradient centrifugation. EDTA-anticoagulated whole blood was centrifuged at 100 × g for 20 min at room temperature to obtain platelet-rich plasma (PRP). The PRP fraction was then transferred to PBS supplemented with 1 mM EDTA and 1 µM prostaglandin E₁ and centrifuged at 800 × g for 20 min to pellet the platelets. The pelleted platelets were resuspended in PBS containing protease inhibitors and subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles alternating between − 80 °C and room temperature. PM fragments were collected by centrifugation at 4,000 × g for 3 min and resuspended in PBS for further use.

Second, MPB was synthesized by dissolving potassium hexacyanoferrate (III) trihydrate (K₃[Fe(CN)₆]·3 H₂O, 132 mg) and polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, 3.0 g) in 40 mL of 0.01 M HCl. The solution was stirred for 30 min at room temperature and then maintained at 80 °C for 24 h with continuous stirring. The resulting MPB nanozyme was collected by centrifugation, washed extensively, and dried. For the hollowing process, dried MPB (20 mg) and PVP (100 mg) were dispersed in 20 mL of 1 M HCl. The dispersion was stirred at room temperature for 24 h and subsequently heated to 140 °C for 4 h. After cooling to room temperature, the HMPB nanozyme was isolated by centrifugation.

Finally, HMPB nanozyme (5 mg) was dispersed in 10 mL of ethanol supplemented with 1 mg of RAPA and agitated overnight at room temperature. The HR complex was collected by centrifugation. The PM suspension was sonicated in an ice bath for 5 min. For membrane functionalization, DSPE-PEG2k-LXW7 (5 mg) was added to 1 mL of PM suspension and incubated for 4 h to allow membrane insertion of the targeting ligand. The functionalized PM was then mixed with the HR dispersion containing 5 mg of HMPB. Subsequently, the mixture was extruded through a polycarbonate membrane (200 nm pore size) using a liposome extruder to obtain the final HRPL nanozyme. During preparation, a fixed feeding mass ratio of HMPB to DSPE-PEG2k-LXW7 (1:1) was used to provide sufficient ligand input for membrane anchoring and reproducible surface functionalization. The resulting HRPL was purified by centrifugation to remove unbound components.

Characterization

Morphological evaluation was performed using TEM (FEI Tecnai G2 F30, USA) to characterize the microstructure of MPB, HMPB, and the drug-loaded composite HRP. The elemental composition and spatial distribution within HRP were analyzed by EDX coupled with TEM. Structural characterization included crystallographic properties assessed by XRD (Bruker D8 ADVANCE, Germany), surface chemical states probed via XPS (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, USA), and molecular vibrational modes examined using FTIR (Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS 5, USA). The porosity characteristics, including the specific surface area and pore size distribution, of MPB and HMPB were determined using BET analysis (ASAP 2460, USA). Drug loading capacity was quantified by detecting unbound RAPA in the HR supernatant using UV‒Vis spectrophotometry. EE and DLC were calculated as follows: EE (%) = [(WTotal added RAPA – WUnloaded RAPA)/WTotal RAPA] × 100; and DLC (%) = [(WTotal added RAPA – WUnloaded RAPA)/(WLoaded RAPA + WTotal added HMPB)] × 100. The colloidal properties of PM, HR, HRP and HRPL, including the hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential, were evaluated by DLS (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS 90, UK). The size stability of HRPL was monitored over a 28-day period, with measurements obtained on days 1, 4, 7, 14 and 28). The protein profiles of the PM and HRPL samples were compared using sodium dodecyl sulfate‒polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and Western blotting.

Drug release

Due to the damaged area after the implantation of the scaffold, the extracellular microenvironment usually becomes slightly acidic due to the infiltration of inflammatory cells and the accumulation of lactic acid (pH 6.5–7.0) [44, 45]. After the cells take up the HRPL, the targeted HRPL will further be internalized into endosomes and lysosomes, and the pH values of these structures are usually between 5.0 and 5.5 [46, 47]. Therefore, a pH value of 5.5 was selected as the representative condition to simulate the acidic environment within endosomes and lysosomes, in order to ensure the realization of spatially controllable drug delivery at the target site. Meanwhile, in drug delivery, the normal physiological (pH 7.4) and acidic environment (pH 5.5) are usually used as control groups to prove that this material has a significant pH-responsive release capability. HR and HRPL were suspended in 10 mL of PBS adjusted to target pH values (5.5 or 7.4), each containing RAPA equivalent to 1 mg. The suspensions were placed into dialysis tubing with a molecular weight cutoff of 3.5 kDa and immersed in 200 mL 5of release medium (PBS at the corresponding pH, 37 °C) under continuous shaking at 100 rpm. At designated time points (0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 h), 1-mL aliquots were collected from the release medium and replaced with an equal volume of fresh prewarmed PBS. After centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 10 min, the RAPA concentration in the supernatant was quantified by UV‒Vis spectroscopy at 278 nm using a standard calibration curve. The cumulative drug release was expressed as a percentage of the total loaded drug content.

Assessment of multi-enzyme mimetic activities

OH-scavenging property: Hydroxyl radicals (•OH) were generated using a TiO₂/UV photocatalytic system under 340-nm ultraviolet irradiation. The system was subsequently treated with 50 µg/mL HRPL and allowed to react for 5–10 min. Residual •OH levels were quantified using electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy.

OH-scavenging property: Hydroxyl radicals (•OH) were generated using a TiO₂/UV photocatalytic system under 340-nm ultraviolet irradiation. The system was subsequently treated with 50 µg/mL HRPL and allowed to react for 5–10 min. Residual •OH levels were quantified using electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy.

O2−scavenging property: Superoxide anion radicals (•O₂⁻) were generated enzymatically via the reaction of xanthine oxidase with xanthine. The resulting •O₂⁻ was incubated with HRPL (50 µg/mL) for 5–10 min. Residual •O₂⁻ levels were quantified using EPR spectroscopy.

O2−scavenging property: Superoxide anion radicals (•O₂⁻) were generated enzymatically via the reaction of xanthine oxidase with xanthine. The resulting •O₂⁻ was incubated with HRPL (50 µg/mL) for 5–10 min. Residual •O₂⁻ levels were quantified using EPR spectroscopy.

H2O2-scavenging properties: A 10 mM H₂O₂ solution served as the control. HRPL was introduced to the reaction mixture and incubated for 5–10 min. The hydrogen peroxide-scavenging capacity was subsequently quantified by EPR spectroscopy.

O2 concentration monitoring: Aliquots (2 mL) of 30% H₂O₂ were incubated with HRPL at concentrations ranging from 6.25 to 100 µg/mL. Dissolved oxygen levels were monitored continuously for 5 min using an oxygen-selective electrode during incubation.

Hemolysis assay

Fresh whole blood was collected from SD rats. Erythrocytes were isolated by centrifugation at 3,000 × g, washed three times with PBS, and resuspended in PBS. Aliquots of erythrocytes were incubated for 4 h with HMPB, RAPA, HR, HRP or HRPL. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 5 min. The absorbance of the supernatants was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader. Erythrocytes suspended in ultrapure water and PBS served as the positive control and negative control, respectively. The hemolysis rate was calculated as follows: Hemolysis (%) = (experimental group absorbance − negative group absorbance)/ (positive group absorbance – negative group absorbance) × 100%.

Cell viability (CCK-8) assay

HUVECs were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h to achieve complete adhesion. The cells were then treated with MPB, HMPB, LXW7, HR, HRP, RAPA or HRPL. Cytotoxicity in HUVECs was then assessed by CCK-8 assay.

In vivo routine blood and biochemical analyses and histopathological examination of major organs

To evaluate the in vivo biosafety of the nanomedicine, HRPL was administered intravenously through the tail vein of KM mice. The experimental process began by acclimating the mice for one week under standard laboratory conditions and a 12-h light/dark cycle with free access to food and water. The mice were randomly divided into experimental groups based on the time points for sample collection: 1, 3, 5, 7, and 14 days after HRPL administration. Prior to administration, HRPL was prepared in a sterile saline solution, ensuring the proper dosage based on the average body weight of the animals, and was intravenously injected into the tail vein using a fine needle. After HRPL administration, blood samples were collected from the tail vein at the designated time points (1, 3, 5, 7, and 14 d) for biochemical and hematological analyses. Blood was drawn using a sterile syringe, and serum was separated for biochemical profiling, which included measurements of liver enzymes (ALT and AST), kidney function markers (BUN and creatinine), and lipid levels (cholesterol and triglycerides). The remaining blood was subjected to complete blood count (CBC) analysis to assess changes in red blood cells, white blood cells, hemoglobin levels, hematocrit, and platelet counts, which provided insight into the potential hematological impact of HRPL administration. Upon completion of the experimental timeline, the mice were humanely euthanized using CO2 asphyxiation, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, kidneys, and lungs) were harvested for further analysis. These organs were immediately fixed in 10% formalin for 24 h and then processed through a series of dehydration steps using graded ethanol solutions, followed by clearing with xylene. The tissues were then embedded in paraffin wax, and Sects. 5–10 μm thick were prepared using a microtome. These tissue sections were stained with H&E to visualize any histopathological changes, such as inflammation, necrosis, or vascular damage. The stained slides were examined under a light microscope for detailed histopathological evaluation. All collected data were analyzed statistically to determine the biosafety profile of HRPL. The biochemical data, CBC results, and histopathological findings were compared across different time points, with the aim of identifying any potential toxicological effects of HRPL on the mice.

Evaluation of uptake by HUVECs

HUVECs were plated in glass-bottom confocal dishes at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells per dish and cultured for 24 h. HUVECs were pretreated with or without 50 ng/mL TNF-α. All cells were then incubated with DiI-labeled nanocomplexes (HR, HRP or HRPL) for 4 h. After the cells were washed with PBS, fixed with paraformaldehyde, and subjected to nuclear staining with DAPI for 15 min, subcellular localization was examined using CLSM, and quantitative analysis was performed by flow cytometry.

Cell viability and live/dead staining

Cells in the model group were treated with 0.1 µM H₂O₂ for 24 h. Cells in nanozyme-treated groups were preincubated with the respective nanozyme for 4 h, followed by replacement with medium containing 0.1 µM H₂O₂ for an additional 24 h. After treatment, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. Following 2 h of incubation at 37 °C in the dark, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. After the experimental treatments were performed, the culture medium was aspirated, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. The working solution from the calcein-AM/PI kit was added to cover the cells. Following incubation for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark, live and dead cells were imaged using fluorescence microscopy.

Intracellular ROS-scavenging assay

Intracellular ROS were detected using DCFH-DA. HUVECs and SMCs were cultured in complete medium in confocal dishes and 6-well plates, respectively. Cells were stimulated under type-specific conditions: HUVECs were treated with 400 µM H₂O₂ for 20 min, and SMCs were treated with 20 ng/mL PDGF-BB for 24 h. After stimulation, the cells were incubated with designated nanozyme formulations for 4 h. For confocal imaging, DCFH-DA-stained HUVECs and SMCs were examined using CLSM. For flow cytometric analysis, SMCs and HUVECs were detached with 0.05% trypsin EDTA, stained with 10 µM DCFH-DA for 1 h at 37 °C, washed with ice-cold PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Anti-inflammatory effect on HUVECs

After stimulation, culture supernatants were collected from HUVECs. Inflammatory cytokine levels were quantified using ELISA kits, and total protein concentrations were determined with a BCA assay kit.

HUVEC proliferation assay

HUVECs under oxidative stress were exposed to 0.1 M H₂O₂ for 24 h. HUVECs were subsequently incubated with the designated nanozyme formulations for 24 h. After a 2-h EdU labeling period, the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for spatial reference. Subcellular localization was visualized by CLSM. The proportion of EdU-positive cells was quantified from three randomly selected fields per dish using ImageJ software.

HUVEC migration and scratch assays

HUVEC migration was assessed using a Boyden chamber system with inserts. Briefly, 5 × 10⁴ cells of each type were suspended in 200 µL of complete medium supplemented with 0.5% FBS and seeded into the upper chambers. The lower chambers contained 500 µL of medium supplemented with 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 24 h of incubation, the HUVECs were treated with 500 µM H₂O₂ in combination with the designated nanozymes. Following an additional 8 h of incubation, nonmigrated cells were gently removed from the upper membrane surface. The cells that migrated to the lower membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and imaged using an inverted microscope. Quantitative analysis was performed by counting cells in three randomly selected fields per insert using ImageJ software.

A wound healing assay was performed to evaluate the effects of HMPB, RAPA, HR, HRP, HPL, and HRPL on the migratory and proliferative capacities of HUVECs. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 10⁵ cells per well and pretreated overnight with 0.8 mM H₂O₂, followed by washing with PBS to remove debris. Nanozymes were then administered for 12 h. Wound areas were imaged at 0 h and 12 h after scratching using an inverted microscope, and closure was quantified using ImageJ software.

Antithrombosis assay of HUVECs

HUVECs were cultured in 6-well plates for 48 h until they reached > 80% confluence and were then stimulated with 10 ng/mL TNF-α for 2 h. Total RNA was extracted using a column-based kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol for qRT‒PCR analysis. The isolated RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix. qPCR amplification was performed in triplicate with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR System. Data acquisition and analysis were conducted using CFX Manager Software. Gene expression levels were normalized to those of GAPDH, and the relative expression of target genes was calculated using the 2^(−ΔΔCT) method. After stimulation, culture supernatants were collected from HUVECs. Inflammatory cytokine levels were quantified using ELISA kits, and total protein concentrations were determined with a BCA assay kit.

Evaluation of uptake by SMCs

SMCs were plated in glass-bottom confocal dishes at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells per dish and cultured for 24 h. All cells were then incubated with DiI-labeled nanocomplexes (HR, HRP or HRPL) for 4 h. After the cells were washed with PBS, fixed with paraformaldehyde, and subjected to nuclear staining with DAPI for 15 min, subcellular localization was examined using CLSM and quantitative analysis by flow cytometry.

SMC proliferation assay

The proliferation of SMCs was stimulated with PDGF-BB for 24 h. The SMCs were subsequently incubated with the designated nanozyme formulations for 24 h. After a 2-h EdU labeling period, the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for spatial reference. Subcellular localization was visualized by CLSM. The proportion of EdU-positive cells was quantified from three randomly selected fields per dish using ImageJ software.

SMC migration and scratch assays

SMC migration was assessed using a Boyden chamber system with inserts. Briefly, 5 × 10⁴ cells of each type were suspended in 200 µL of complete medium supplemented with 0.5% FBS and seeded into the upper chambers. The lower chambers contained 500 µL of medium supplemented with 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 24 h of incubation, the SMCs were stimulated with PDGF-BB in the presence of the corresponding nanozyme. Following an additional 8 h of incubation, nonmigrated cells were gently removed from the upper membrane surface. The cells that migrated to the lower membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and imaged using an inverted microscope. Quantitative analysis was performed by counting cells in three randomly selected fields per insert using ImageJ software.

A wound healing assay was performed to evaluate the effects of HMPB, RAPA, HR, HRP, HPL, and HRPL on the migratory and proliferative capacities of SMCs. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 10⁵ cells per well and pretreated overnight with PDGF-BB. Linear scratches were generated using a sterile 200-µL pipette tip, followed by washes with PBS to remove debris. The designated nanozyme was then administered for 12 h. The wound area was imaged at 0 h and 12 h after scratching using an inverted microscope, and closure was quantified using ImageJ software.

SMC apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was assessed using a TUNEL assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cytoskeletal architecture and nuclear morphology were examined simultaneously by costaining with phalloidin and DAPI, followed by imaging with CLSM. Quantitative analysis was carried out using flow cytometry.

Western blot analysis in vitro

Protein samples were denatured by heating to 95 °C in sample buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl, pH 6.8, 40% glycerol, 2% SDS, 2% β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.02% bromophenol blue) under reducing conditions, separated by SDS–PAGE, and electrophoretically transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% BSA and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against the following proteins: CNN1, SM22α, OPN, PCNA, and β-actin. After the membranes were incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, the protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and imaged with a GelDoc Go system.

Coculture of HUVECs and SMCs

HUVECs and SMCs were stained with 5 µM CellTracker™ Deep Red and 5 µM CellTracker™ Green CMFDA, respectively. Except for those in the control group, HUVECs were stimulated with 10 ng/mL TNF-α for 24 h, while SMCs were treated with 20 ng/mL PDGF-BB for 24 h. Pretreated HUVECs and SMCs were cocultured at a 1:1 ratio (total density: 5 × 10⁴ cells/cm²) in confocal dishes. After 24 h of treatment with the designated nanozyme (50 µg/mL), imaging was performed using CLSM at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h.

Rat carotid artery balloon injury model

Male SD rats (400–450 g) were orally administered aspirin (20 mg/kg) and then anesthetized via an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (0.7 mg/kg). After surgical exposure of the right external carotid artery, endothelial denudation of the common carotid artery was performed using a 2-French balloon catheter. The catheter was advanced through the proximal common carotid artery lumen to the aortic bifurcation and withdrawn gently, and this procedure was repeated three times. All surgical procedures were performed by a single operator.

In vivo biodistribution and fluorescence imaging

Nanozymes from each experimental group were labeled with DIR fluorescent dye. Biodistribution was evaluated in vivo following tail vein injection. Immediately after the carotid artery endothelial injury model was established in the rats, the respective nanozyme formulation was administered intravenously. Carotid artery regions were imaged at predetermined time points (0.5, 2, 4, and 8 h) using an in vivo imaging system to detect fluorescence signals. At 8 h after injection, the rats were sacrificed, and major organs (including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were harvested for ex vivo fluorescence imaging.

Morphometric analysis of neointimal hyperplasia

Rats with carotid artery injury were administered nanozyme formulations via tail vein injection every third day for one month. Rats in the injury model control group received injections of 200 µL of PBS on the same schedule. At the experimental endpoint, the animals were euthanized, and the common carotid arteries were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Paraffin-embedded Sect. (4 μm thick) were prepared, stained with H&E, and examined using an Olympus CK31 light microscope. Intimal and medial areas were quantified from histological sections using ImageJ software, and the ratio of the intimal to media area was calculated as a proliferation index.

Evaluation of endothelial barrier recovery

Reendothelialization of balloon-injured arteries was assessed in a rat model of vascular injury after one month of nanozyme treatment. Under anesthesia, 0.5% Evans blue dye (1 mL) was administered via tail vein injection. After 60 min of circulation, systemic perfusion was performed with PBS. Carotid artery segments extending from the proximal region to the aortic arch were harvested, opened longitudinally, flattened on a white background, and photographed. The blue-stained areas indicated denuded endothelium, whereas the unstained regions indicated reendothelialized areas.

In vivo regulation of SMC phenotype transition

Carotid artery tissues from balloon-injured rats were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C. The tissues were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitors using a mechanical homogenizer. The protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (30 µg per lane) were separated by 10% SDS–PAGE and electrophoretically transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against SM22α, PCNA, OPN, CNN1, and β-actin. After three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated species-matched secondary antibodies (1:5,000) for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were detected using ECL substrate and imaged with a ChemiDoc MP system. Band intensities were quantified using Image Lab software.

Comprehensive evaluation of therapeutic efficacy

The right common carotid artery was divided into proximal and distal segments. The proximal segment was fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, and transversely sectioned for Masson’s trichrome staining or immunohistochemical analysis of OPN, α-SMA, and SMMHC. Images were obtained using a fluorescence microscope, and the fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Reconstructing the vascular microenvironment

Serum levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and CRP were measured using ELISA kits. Major organs, namely, the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, were collected and subjected to H&E staining to assess the systemic toxicological effects of nanozyme treatment. The distal part of the blood vessels was subjected to cryosectioning, and the generation of ROS within the blood vessels was evaluated using fluorescent probes. Images were obtained using a fluorescence microscope, and the fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Transcriptome analysis

The total RNA quantity and purity were analyzed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 and RNA 6000 Nano Chip Reagent Kit (Agilent Technologies, California, USA, Cat. 5067 − 1511). High-quality RNA samples with a RNA Integrity Number greater than 7.0 were used for sequencing library construction. Subsequently, reverse transcription was carried out using SuperScript™II Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, Cat. 1896649, USA). Sequencing was performed on the MJ-Bio platform (MJ-Bio Technology Co. China) using the 2 × 150 bp paired-end (PE150) method, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Library sequencing was completed by Major Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China) on the MJ Bio Cloud Platform. All transcripts from the samples were merged, and a comprehensive transcriptome was reconstructed using the gffCompare software. After constructing the final transcriptome, expression levels of all transcripts were estimated using StringTie and Ballgown, and the expression abundance of mRNAs was calculated as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differential gene expression between two groups was analyzed using DESeq2 software (with Edger software for differential expression analysis between two samples). Genes with a false discovery rate < 0.05 and an absolute fold change ≥ 2 were considered as differentially expressed genes. These differentially expressed genes were further subjected to Gene Ontology functional enrichment analysis, heatmap analysis, volcano plot analysis, correlation analysis, and pathway enrichment analysis. Principal component analysis was performed using the Printcomp function in R.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. The differences were analyzed by Student’s t test and one-way ANOVA; *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Characterization of HRPL

Based on previous studies [48, 49], HMPB was synthesized through the hydrothermal etching of mesoporous Prussian blue (MPB) and systematically characterized. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed the hollow structure of MPB (Fig. 2a, b) and the well-defined mesoporous morphology of HMPB (Fig. 2c, d). TEM clearly revealed the presence of the PM coating (Fig. 2e). Elemental mapping (Fig. 2f) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX, Figure. S1) illustrated the spatial distributions of Fe, N, K, and P within HRP. A distinct phosphorus signal confirmed successful PM coating and the successful synthesis of HRP. XRD patterns (Fig. 2g) exhibited characteristic peaks at 17.4°, 24.7°, 35.2°, and 39.5°, corresponding to the (200), (220), (400), and (420) crystal planes, respectively. These results indicate a well-defined face-centered cubic crystal structure in both MPB and HMPB (PDF card no. 97-007-7340), which remained intact after the hollowing process. Furthermore, XPS analysis (Fig. S2) revealed characteristic peaks for Fe, C, and N. Specifically, the high-resolution Fe 2p spectrum confirmed the coexistence of Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ valence states, with characteristic binding energy peaks for Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2 at approximately 708.5 eV and 721.4 eV, respectively. BET analysis revealed that the etching process increased the specific surface area from 40.62 m²/g to 107.65 m²/g, with an average pore size of 20.48 nm (Fig. 2h, i), confirming the mesoporous nature and drug loading potential.

The results of loading RAPA onto HMPB to obtain the UV absorption curve of HR are shown in Fig. 2j. The characteristic absorption peaks of HMPB and RAPA confirmed successful loading of the drug. Based on the ultraviolet absorption curve of RAPA (Fig. S3a) and its standard curve (Fig. S3b), the EE rate of RAPA in HR was approximately 84.53%, and the DLC was approximately 8.07%. FTIR spectroscopy of HRPL revealed specific chemical bonds (–C ≡ N–, –C–H–, and –N–H–) derived from HMPB, RAPA, and LXW7, further verifying successful synthesis (Fig. 2k). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements revealed hydrodynamic diameters of 132.67 nm for HR, 161 nm after PM coating (HRP), and 159 nm after LXW7 conjugation (HRPL) (Fig. 2l). Zeta potential values were as follows: PM (–18.23 mV), HR (–6.83 mV), HRP (–15.91 mV), and HRPL (–12.94 mV) (Fig. 2m). The above results show that the coating with PM and the functionalization with LXW7 were successful. HRPL also demonstrated excellent colloidal stability for more than one month (Fig. 2n, o), supporting its potential for use in biomedical applications.

In vitro drug release studies at pH 7.4 and 5.5 indicated that under acidic conditions, HR and HRPL released nearly 75% of the loaded RAPA within 16 h, compared with approximately 45% at physiological pH (Fig. 2p), demonstrating a pH-responsive release behavior. This discovery indicates that HRPL achieves efficient release within the endoplasmic reticulum/lysosome compartments of the cells after being taken up by them. This is particularly relevant given the mildly acidic microenvironment at vascular injury sites, facilitating spatially controlled drug release. SDS‒PAGE confirmed the retention of characteristic PM proteins in HRPL (Fig. 2q). Western blot analysis revealed key platelet adhesion and activation markers, such as P-selectin (CD62P), CD41, and CD61, in both native PM and the nanocomplexes (Fig. 2r, S4), confirming the preservation of platelet-mimetic functionality in HRPL, as also supported by previous reports [50, 51].

ROS-scavenging properties of HRPL

ROS play distinct roles in maintaining vascular homeostasis [52–54]. However, excessive ROS production causes significant vascular damage that is often irreversible, triggering inflammatory cascades and aggravating the progression of neointimal hyperplasia. To evaluate the scavenging capacity of HRPL, we evaluated its interactions with key pathological ROS, including ·O₂⁻, ·OH, and H₂O₂.

We evaluated the ROS-scavenging performance of HRPL against three major pathological free radicals using electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 3a-c, the characteristic signals of ·O₂⁻, ·OH, and H₂O₂ were significantly reduced after 5 and 10 min of treatment with HRPL, demonstrating its robust multi-enzyme mimetic activity. Based on these findings, we further assessed the free radical-scavenging capacity of this nanozyme. The intracellular antioxidant activity of HMPB was evaluated in H₂O₂ stimulated HUVECs. Compared with those in the Model group, increasing concentrations of HMPB strongly reduced intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 3d), as further confirmed by quantitative analysis (Fig. S5), which showed a concentration-dependent decrease in fluorescence intensity. These results confirm the enzyme-mimetic antioxidant properties of HMPB and its function in cellular ROS reduction and cytoprotection. In addition, dissolved oxygen measurements revealed a clear increase in the O₂ concentration in the HRPL + H₂O₂ reaction system, indicating that HRPL catalyzes the decomposition of H₂O₂ into O₂ (Fig. 3e).

Together, these results indicate that the HMPB-based nanocomposite HRPL possesses potent ROS-scavenging abilities and provides notable cytoprotective effects. This work provides a basis for modulating the local vascular microenvironment via ROS elimination, a strategy that could contribute importantly to the regulation of vascular homeostasis.

Safety evaluation

First, we conducted a hemolysis test to assess the potential damage that this nanomedicine might cause to red blood cells. The results of the hemolysis test revealed that the hemolysis rate in the nanomedicine treatment group was significantly lower than that in the aqueous control group. All the values were within the safe threshold of 5% (Fig. S6), indicating that HRPL has excellent blood compatibility and safety.

Second, a CCK-8 assay was performed, and the results further confirmed that after 24 h of treatment, neither MPB nor HMPB had significant cytotoxic effects on HUVECs (Fig. 3f, g). However, RAPA alone was toxic to cells, different concentrations of LXW7 have a significant promoting effect on the proliferation of HUVECs (Fig.S7). Although RAPA alone exhibited dose-dependent cytotoxicity, in the presence of the composite nanozyme HRPL, the toxicity of RAPA was significantly reduced (Fig. 3h). This might be because LXW7 promotes the proliferation of HUVECs [55, 56], and the PM encapsulation enables the sustained release of RAPA [50]. We subsequently determined the optimal concentration of RAPA and evaluated the cytotoxicity of HR and HRP toward HUVECs (Fig. 3i, j). As shown in Fig. 3h, cell viability remained above 80% after treatment with 4 µg/mL RAPA but decreased to approximately 60% at 5 µg/mL. In contrast, even at an HRPL concentration of 50 µg/mL, cell viability exceeded 80%, In contrast, even at an HRPL concentration of 50 µg/mL, cell viability exceeded 80%, demonstrating reduced toxicity compared with that of conventional RAPA and supporting its potential for in vivo applications.

Finally, the in vivo biological safety of intravenous administration in KM mice was evaluated. Blood samples were collected on days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 14 for biochemical and hematological analyses. Samples were obtained from major organs and subjected to histopathological examination using H&E staining. Clinical biochemical tests revealed no significant differences in alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine (CREA), or urea levels among the groups (Fig. 3k), indicating that there were no adverse effects on liver or kidney function. Because the absolute values of certain biochemical parameters showed relatively large inter-individual variation, each indicator was further analyzed separately. All measured values remained within the normal physiological range (Fig. S8). No significant differences in hematological parameters were detected between the treatment group and the untreated control group (Fig. 3l). H&E staining further confirmed that there were no significant pathological changes in the major organs (including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) in any of the groups (Fig. S9).

Targeting and protective effects of HRPL on HUVECs

Ability to target HUVECs

The targeted delivery of HRPL in the circulation is essential for achieving site-specific drug release at sites of vascular injury. HRPL incorporates the specific protein marker LXW7, which critically directs the nanozyme to HUVECs and SMCs through binding to the αvβ3 receptor. Based on the platelet-mimetic properties of the nanocomposite, we evaluated its adhesion to activated ECs, a key target in vascular injury. We further examined its ability to adhere to activated ECs, which play a central role in VEI.

In activated HUVECs, HRPL exhibited stronger adhesion than HRP and HR, with cellular uptake increasing over time (Fig. 4a, b). Flow cytometric quantification confirmed these results: after 4 h of incubation, compared with nonactivated cells, activated HUVECs internalized significantly more of the nanozyme, with HRPL showing the greatest uptake (Fig. 4c, d). PM&LXW7 conjugation enhances targeting precision beyond the use of a PM coating alone, demonstrating the potential of this nanozyme for the systemic targeting of injured vasculature in vivo.

Protecting HUVECs from oxidative stress induced damage

Compared with healthy arteries, arteries with VEI exhibit elevated ROS production, resulting in irreversible damage to lipids, carbohydrates, proteins, and DNA. To first validate the fundamental biochemical antioxidant capacity of the HRPL nanozyme, we employed the H2O2 induced oxidative stress model, which is a widely accepted gold standard for evaluating nanozyme-mediated cytoprotection. We therefore assessed the cytoprotective effect of HRPL against H₂O₂ induced oxidative stress. CCK-8 assays revealed a significant reduction in HUVEC viability 24 h after H₂O₂ exposure, confirming cellular injury (Fig. 4e). Compared with H₂O₂ alone, treatment with RAPA, HMPB, HR, HRP, or HRPL substantially increased cell survival, indicating that HRPL effectively attenuates H₂O₂ induced toxicity and protects cells from oxidative damage. Live/dead staining, wherein viable cells exhibit green fluorescence (calcein-AM) and dead cells display red staining (propidium iodide), further corroborated these results (Fig. 4f). These findings demonstrate that HRPL most effectively shields HUVECs from oxidative stress, establishing a basis for endothelial barrier repair and the restoration of normal cellular function. The intracellular overproduction of ROS activates multiple signal transduction cascades that promote inflammation and exacerbate VEI [57, 58]. Evaluating intracellular antioxidant activity in H₂O₂-stimulated HUVECs (Fig. 4g) and PDGF-BB stimulated SMCs (Fig. S10a) revealed significantly reduced ROS levels across all treatment groups (RAPA, HMPB, HR, HRP and HRPL). Notably, HRPL resulted in the highest ROS-scavenging efficiency, which was corroborated by the results of flow cytometric analysis (Fig. 4h, S10b).

Protecting HUVECs from damage caused by inflammatory responses

We further investigated the downstream regulatory effects of HRPL on inflammatory using a more pathologically relevant TNF-α stimulation. In TNF-α-stimulated HUVECs, the expression of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β (Fig. 4i), TNF-α (Fig. 4j), and IL-6 (Fig. 4k), was markedly upregulated, whereas the expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 4l) was downregulated. Treatment with HRPL significantly suppressed the expression of these proinflammatory markers while restoring IL-10 levels. These results demonstrate that HRPL exhibits notable integrated ROS clearance and anti-inflammatory activity, contributing to the substantial improvement of the microenvironment and inflammatory response at VEI sites.

Enhanced proliferation ability of HUVECs

The loss of ECs leads to disruption of endothelial barrier integrity. Therefore, promoting the proliferation and migration of ECs is crucial for restoring endothelial function after vascular injury. In an H₂O₂-induced injury microenvironment, nanozyme treatment significantly increased the number of EdU positive cells, with the HRPL group showing the strongest proliferative effect compared with the RAPA, HMPB, HR, HRP, and HPL groups (Fig. 5a, c), demonstrating the superior ability of the fully integrated nanoplatform to promote endothelial proliferation. The inclusion of the HPL group further suggested that the combined presence of platelet membrane coating and LXW7 contributed to endothelial protection, whereas the incorporation of RAPA within the optimized HRPL formulation maintained anti-restenosis potential while preserving endothelial reparative capacity. Enhancing the migration ability of HUVECs

The key to treating VEI lies in promoting the proliferation of adjacent ECs and their migration to the damaged area. These pathological characteristics underscore the clinical necessity for nanotherapeutic strategies that can selectively modulate the behavior of HUVECs. Transwell migration assays demonstrated that the nanozyme significantly influenced the migratory capacity of HUVECs (Fig. 5b). Quantification with crystal violet revealed that H₂O₂ treatment increased HUVEC migration by 90% (Fig. 5d). All treatment groups showed the restoration of migration to levels comparable to those at baseline, with the HRPL group showing the most pronounced effect. Notably, the HPL group exhibited a significant promotional effect on HUVEC migration compared to the model group, highlighting the intrinsic bioactivity of the LXW7-functionalized platelet membrane coating in guiding endothelial cell behavior. The HRPL group showed the most pronounced effect, suggesting that the synergistic integration of ROS-scavenging HMPB and the targeted carrier provides the most favorable microenvironment for endothelial mobility. Additionally, wound healing assays further corroborated these findings (Fig. S11).

Preventing thrombosis and maintaining normal HUVEC function

The synthesis of EC adhesion molecules, including VCAM-1 and E-selectin, is established during vascular injury and inflammation [59]. Under normal physiological conditions, the endothelial surface is anti-thrombotic. However, when stimulated by inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, HUVECs will shift from the “anti-thrombotic phenotype” to the “pro-thrombotic phenotype”. This is the main biological basis for thrombosis that occurs after vascular injury and stent implantation. TNF-α stimulation for 2 h significantly increased the mRNA expression of VCAM-1 (mRNA: Fig. 5e; protein: Fig. 5i) and E-selectin (mRNA: Fig. 5g; protein: Fig. 5j). HRPL treatment strongly suppressed both the gene and protein expression of these molecules.

Clinical evidence indicates that TNF-α release following balloon catheterization promotes sustained vascular inflammation through endothelial signaling activation. HMPB counteracts this process by scavenging ROS and inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation. The potent anti-inflammatory effects of HRPL thus align with clinical needs for postprocedural anti-inflammatory therapy.

Furthermore, the endothelial-derived prothrombotic factors TF and PAI-1 accelerate thrombus formation. Nanozyme treatment effectively reversed the TNF-α-induced upregulation of TF (mRNA: Fig. 5h; protein: Fig. 5k) and PAI-1 (mRNA: Fig. 5f; protein: Fig. 5l) expression. These data demonstrate that compared with RAPA or HMPB alone, HRPL achieves optimal synergistic efficacy, which is consistent with our previous findings showing that HMPB maintains redox homeostasis, while integration with RAPA and a membrane-targeting peptide system leads to the synergistic reduction of inflammation and risk of stent thrombosis.

Targeting and inhibitory effects of HRPL on SMCs

Ability to target SMCs

SMCs express integrin αvβ3 receptors on their surface. To evaluate the internalization of the nanozyme, DiI-labeled nanozyme was tracked using CLSM. DAPI stained nuclei (blue) revealed pink fluorescence signals in the perinuclear region (red–blue colocalization) (Fig. S12a), indicating successful perinuclear accumulation of the nanozyme. Compared with the HMPB and HRP groups, the LXW7 conjugated HRPL group exhibited significantly greater cellular uptake after 4 h. Flow cytometry further confirmed the enhanced internalization efficiency of HRPL (Fig. S12b).

Inhibition of SMC proliferation and migration

Beyond inflammation, the pathological proliferation and migration of SMCs represent another critical facet of vascular injury that is closely intertwined with the oxidative microenvironment. To independently assess the platform’s performance against this specific pathological process, we further utilized the PDGF-BB stimulated SMC model. Mammalian target of RAPA (mTOR) is an evolutionarily conserved protein kinase that acts as a central signaling hub, integrating various internal and external cues to precisely coordinate cell growth, proliferation, metabolism, and survival [60]. EdU assays of PDGF-BB stimulated SMCs revealed a substantial increase in the percentage of EdU positive cells from 24.94% to approximately 60%, whereas treatment with HRPL effectively suppressed proliferation to below basal levels (Fig. 6a, b). In this study, the divergent biological outcomes specifically, the promotion of HUVEC repair versus the inhibition of SMC proliferation are driven by the cell-type-specific downstream coupling of αvβ3 integrin. In HUVECs, LXW7 mediated targeting triggers pro-survival and pro-migration signaling (PI3K/Akt) [61]. Conversely, in activated SMCs, although LXW7 mediates the targeted uptake of the nanozyme, the biological response is dominated by the intracellularly released RAPA. RAPA acts as a potent molecular brake by inhibiting the mTOR/p70S6K axis [62], thereby overriding potential growth signals and enforcing G0/G1 phase arrest [63]. Furthermore, effective treatment of vascular injury requires the inhibition of medial SMC migration into the intima. Transwell migration assays demonstrated that our platform significantly attenuated SMC migration (Fig. S13a). While Crystal violet staining showed that H2O2 induced oxidative stress influenced SMC mobility (Fig. S13b), HRPL treatment restored migration toward baseline levels with the most potent inhibitory effect. These observations were further corroborated by wound healing assays (Fig. S14). This sophisticated dual regulatory mechanism ensures that HRPL can selectively modulate the behavior of different vascular cells, which is essential for the subsequent restoration of vascular homeostasis in vivo.

Promotion of SMC apoptosis

To assess the inhibitory effect of HRPL on SMCs, TUNEL fluorescence staining was used to evaluate nanozyme-induced apoptosis (Fig. 6c). The control and HMPB-treated groups exhibited minimal apoptosis, while treatment with HRP or HRPL for 24 h resulted in apoptosis rates of 70.32% and 83.63%, respectively (Fig. 6d). HRPL induced a significantly greater proportion of apoptotic cells. RAPA incorporation led to a morphological transition of SMCs from their characteristic spindle shape to an irregular “moribund” phenotype, confirming the proapoptotic activity of RAPA. Although HMPB synergistically suppressed SMC proliferation, it did not independently induce cell death. These findings suggest that HRPL selectively inhibits SMCs while preserving endothelial barrier function. Quantitative analysis by annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry further confirmed the degree of apoptosis (Fig. S15a). HMPB alone did not significantly alter apoptosis, whereas RAPA containing nanozymes markedly increased both early and late apoptotic populations (Fig. S15b). As shown in Fig. S14a and S14b, after the introduction of RAPA, the number of apoptotic SMCs significantly increased, especially the number of late apoptotic cells. Therefore, we can infer that RAPA inhibits the proliferation of SMCs by promoting their apoptosis.

Regulation of SMC phenotypic transition

During the response to vascular injury, activated inflammatory cells, platelets, and SMCs secrete various growth factors, among which PDGF-BB plays a key role in promoting the transition of SMCs from a quiescent contractile phenotype to a dedifferentiated synthetic phenotype [64, 65]. This phenotypic switching process is typically characterized by the downregulation of the expression of contractile markers such as calponin (CNN1) and transgelin (SM22α) and the upregulation of the expression of the synthetic marker osteopontin (OPN). Furthermore, mTOR signaling plays a central role in SMC dedifferentiation and proliferation. Thus, inhibiting mTOR by RAPA effectively maintains the contractile phenotype of SMCs [66]. Western blot analysis further elucidated the regulatory effects of HRPL on biomarkers associated with phenotypic switching in SMCs. As shown in Fig. 6e, S16, the nanocomposite significantly reversed the PDGF-BB-induced transition of SMCs from a contractile to a synthetic phenotype. The nanozyme upregulated the expression of the contractile markers CNN1 (Fig. 6f) and SM22α (Fig. 6g) while downregulating the expression of the synthetic marker OPN (Fig. 6h). Notably, HRPL exhibited the most pronounced phenotype-modulating effects. Furthermore, expression of the proliferation marker proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) was effectively suppressed in both the RAPA-containing group and the nanocomposite-treated group (Fig. 6i). RAPA regulates the phenotypic transformation of SMCs, which represents a fundamental process underlying neointimal hyperplasia, vascular remodeling, and in-stent restenosis.

Differential regulation of two types of cells by HRPL

To simulate the complex microenvironment of chronic inflammation and abnormal proliferation after stent implantation, we established a cell co-culture system to study the competitive advantage of HUVECs over SMCs, which is crucial for the recovery of vascular endothelium after injury. We pre-labeled the pre-treated cells with different fluorescent markers and inoculated them at a similar density, and cultured them for 72 h (Fig. 7a). The ratio of HUVECs to SMCs was quantified based on cell counts (Fig. 7b-d). In the model group, the number of HUVECs decreased, whereas the number of SMCs increased, reflecting the different responses of cells in the microenvironment of the simulated vascular injury site. The HPL group showed a higher HUVEC/SMC ratio compared to the model group, indicating that the LXW7-functionalized coating independently provides a competitive edge to HUVECs by fostering their attachment and growth. However, the HRPL treatment group achieved the most significant shift in cell population dynamics. This suggests that while the HPL shell initiates endothelial recovery, the inclusion of RAPA in HRPL is essential to decisively suppress SMC hyperplasia and induce their apoptosis. Consequently, HRPL maximizes the therapeutic effect through a dual-regulatory mechanism: it simultaneously promotes the expansion of the endothelial coverage and constrains the overgrowth of SMCs, thereby effectively re-establishing vascular homeostasis and reducing non-targeted toxicity.

In vivo targeted therapy and anti-restenosis efficacy of HRPL

Targeting damaged areas and internal distribution

VEI has been shown to induce platelet aggregation and facilitate the proliferation and migration of SMCs through inflammatory mediators such as PDGF-BB, thereby contributing to vascular stenosis. In this study, the PM was functionalized with LXW7 to increase targeting precision and promote endothelial barrier repair. Fluorescence imaging (FLI) was employed to monitor the in vivo accumulation and distribution of HRPL, and near-infrared (NR) signals were tracked at multiple time points. This class of nanozyme was initially validated for fluorescence imaging in vitro (Fig. S17a, b). After HRPL was labeled with DiR, its fluorescence intensity increased in a concentration-dependent manner, reaching a stable level at higher concentrations. Next, we evaluated the ability of HRPL to target sites of carotid artery injury in SD rats. The functionalized PM coating significantly improved the targeting efficiency of the nanozyme, enabling HRPL to specifically accumulate at thrombi and in damaged vascular regions, thus ensuring precise targeting of injured blood vessels.

Here, we present a schematic diagram of the treatment after balloon-induced injury of the carotid artery in rats (Fig. 8a). In vivo imaging using NR tracing revealed that both HRP and HRPL accumulated rapidly at carotid injury sites within 0.5–8 h after injection (Fig. 8b). The HRPL group exhibited the strongest fluorescence, significantly surpassing that in the HRP group, indicating that LXW7 conjugation facilitates rapid injury-specific binding. Quantitative analysis further confirmed that the dual-targeted HRPL achieved the highest fluorescence intensity at the injury site (Fig. S18). At eight hours after injection, the SD rats were sacrificed, and the major organs were extracted for imaging (Fig. S19a). To further evaluate the targeting efficiency, we performed quantitative biodistribution analysis (Fig. S19b) and compared the injury-site uptake with organ accumulation (Fig. S20). For all formulations, the fluorescence was primarily detected in the liver and to a lesser extent in the spleen, which is consistent with the typical metabolic pathway of nanoparticles being cleared by the reticuloendothelial system (RES) through phagocytosis by Kupffer cells in the liver. Notably, the HR group showed the highest hepatic accumulation and minimal signal at the injured carotid artery. In contrast, the HRPL group demonstrated the lowest liver uptake and the highest carotid accumulation; the signal intensity at the injury site was only slightly lower than that in the liver. This remarkable shift in biodistribution is attributed to the synergistic targeting effect of the platelet membrane and LXW7 peptide, which actively steers the nanozymes toward the denuded endothelium and activated SMCs, thereby reducing non-specific sequestration by metabolic organs and enhancing therapeutic delivery efficiency. These results indicate that HRPL displays rapid homing and high arterial injury specificity, which may significantly improve targeted drug release and therapeutic efficacy [67, 68].

The vascular intima, composed of HUVECs, not only functions as a physical barrier between blood flow and vascular tissues but also plays a central role in maintaining vascular homeostasis. Loss of endothelial integrity underlies multiple pathological events in CVD, making rapid functional restoration critical for preventing adverse outcomes such as restenosis. Using a balloon-induced carotid artery injury model, we evaluated the regulatory effects of the nanozyme on endothelial repair. At 28 d after injury, blood samples were collected for CBC evaluation (Fig. S21a) and biochemical analysis (Fig. S21b). Major organs and tissues were harvested, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and histopathologically examined via H&E staining (Fig. S21c). To ensure a more precise and rigorous evaluation of the biosafety profile, especially considering the inherent variance in absolute values across different biochemical parameters, we provided individualized bar graphs for each blood biochemical marker (Fig. S21d). No significant systemic toxicity was observed in any group after nanomedicine treatment, indicating a favorable safety profile that supports potential clinical translation.

HRPL inhibits intimal hyperplasia in vivo

In the model group of rats, obvious neointimal hyperplasia was observed in the injured blood vessels, which is the main cause of vascular restenosis. After one month of treatment with different types of nanozymes, vascular sections were collected for H&E staining (Fig. 8c), as local magnified images of the vascular cross-section (Fig. 8d) can provide a clear view of the therapeutic effect. The HPL group exhibited a modest improvement in reducing neointimal thickening compared to the HMPB group, suggesting that the targeted PM shell alone contributes to vascular recovery, likely by providing a physical barrier and promoting partial re-endothelialization. However, the therapeutic effect of HPL was significantly lower than that of the drug-loaded HR group, and HRPL treatment remained the most effective in suppressing pathological thickening (Fig. 8e–g). RAPA alone, owing to its lack of targeting ability and its hydrophobic nature in the body, had an unsatisfactory therapeutic effect when applied in vivo. In contrast, HRPL, with its precise targeting ability and stable adhesion, precisely released the drug at the damaged site and restored the normal vascular microenvironment. Therefore, among the several treatments, HRPL had the best therapeutic effect, almost completely inhibiting neointimal hyperplasia. However, free RAPA did not fully mitigate hyperplasia, highlighting the advantages of nanocarrier-mediated delivery and addressing the limited efficacy of certain conventional drugs in vivo.

Acceleration of endothelial barrier repair

Reendothelialization was assessed using Evans blue staining (Fig. 8h); extensive luminal staining in the model groups indicated endothelial denudation, whereas markedly reduced staining in the HRPL group indicated enhanced endothelial barrier regeneration. PM-coated nanozymes minimized endothelial cytotoxicity while improving targeting to SMCs, resulting in smaller denuded areas. Importantly, HRPL resulted in the largest reendothelialized area without compromising HUVEC viability.

In vivo regulation of SMC phenotypic transition

Western blot analysis was performed to further investigate the regulatory mechanism of HRPL on markers of phenotypic switching in SMCs. Both the nanocomposite and RAPA significantly attenuated the PDGF-BB-induced transition of SMCs toward a synthetic phenotype (Fig. 9a, S22). This effect was accompanied by upregulated expression of the contractile markers CNN1 (Fig. 9b) and SM22α (Fig. 9c) and downregulated expression of the synthetic marker OPN (Fig. 9d). HRPL demonstrated the most substantial effect in promoting this phenotypic reversal. Additionally, the aberrant expression of the proliferation marker PCNA was effectively suppressed (Fig. 9e). Phenotypic switching of SMCs represents a central mechanism underlying neointimal hyperplasia, vascular remodeling, and in-stent restenosis. During this process, SMCs undergo profound phenotypic reprogramming and adopt a synthetic state characterized by enhanced proliferative and migratory capacities. Therefore, targeted regulation of the phenotypic transformation of SMCs is key to reducing complications after stent implantation.

Comprehensive evaluation of in vivo therapy

To elucidate the effects of the nanocomposite on smooth muscle phenotypic modulation and neointimal suppression, vascular sections were immunofluorescently stained for CD31, α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), OPN, and smooth muscle myosin heavy chain (SMMHC) (Fig. 9f), and quantitative analysis was performed (Fig. S23a-d). CD31 staining (green) revealed continuous linear fluorescence in the HRPL group, indicating nearly complete restoration of endothelial integrity. The HPL group exhibited a marginal but detectable improvement in the expression of contractile markers (α-SMA and SMMHC) compared to the HMPB group, suggesting that the LXW7 functionalized shell facilitates a pro-healing microenvironment that partially supports smooth muscle homeostasis. However, this effect was significantly less potent than that observed in the RAPA loaded HR group. The nanocomposite significantly reversed the PDGF-BB-induced transition of SMCs from a contractile to a synthetic phenotype, as shown by downregulation of OPN expression and upregulation of SMMHC expression, accompanied by reduced intimal thickening. α-SMA, a marker of contractile SMCs, is associated with preserved vascular elasticity and contractility. Staining with an α-SMA antibody (green) revealed optimal therapeutic efficacy in the HRPL group, with most SMCs exhibiting a nonproliferative, contractile phenotype.

Masson’s trichrome staining (Fig. 9g) further confirmed a substantial decrease in SMCs in the HRPL-treated group, contributing to attenuated neointimal formation. The immunohistochemical and statistical analysis of PCNA expression (Fig. S24) revealed significant reduction in proliferating cells in the HRPL group, supporting the above results and demonstrating effective inhibition of SMC proliferation. While HPL showed a slight reduction in PCNA-positive cells relative to HMPB, it did not match the strong anti-proliferative efficacy of the drug-loaded formulations. Collectively, these findings underscore the limitations of single-factor therapies and emphasize the differential regulatory effects of the nanocomposite on ECs and SMCs.

Restoration of in vivo vascular microenvironment homeostasis

Building on the distinct ROS elimination and anti-inflammatory properties exhibited by the nanodrug, we assessed it‘s in vivo anti-inflammatory activity. HRPL treatment significantly reduced the levels of key proinflammatory mediators, including IL-6 (Fig. 9h), IL-1β (Fig. 9i), C-reactive protein (CRP) (Fig. 9j) and TNF-α (Fig. 9k), but increased the level of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 9l) compared with those in the model group. These results demonstrate the synergistic anti-inflammatory effects of the nanocomposite in vivo and underscore its therapeutic mechanism in alleviating endothelial injury through inflammation modulation. Furthermore, DHE assays revealed strong in vivo ROS-scavenging capacity: while arteries in the model group displayed intense red fluorescence, those in the HRPL group exhibited minimal fluorescence intensity (Fig. 9m), as determined by quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity (Fig. S25), indicating the suppression of neointimal hyperplasia via efficient ROS clearance.

Transcriptomic insights into the mechanisms of HRPL in accelerating neointimal remodeling

To further investigate the potential mechanism by which HRPL facilitates vascular repair, a transcriptomic analysis was performed on the carotid arteries 14 days following injury and treatment. Principal component analysis revealed distinct transcriptional differences between the model and HRPL groups, as shown in Fig. 10a. Notably, the samples from the HRPL group exhibited a tight clustering pattern, indicating a high degree of consistency in gene expression after HRPL treatment, in contrast to the model group. Differentially expressed genes were selected based on the criteria of |log2FC| ≥ 1 and P < 0.05. Volcano plot analysis (Fig. 10b) showed that 940 genes were significantly upregulated and 743 genes were downregulated in the HRPL group compared to the model group. Functional annotation and expression quantification further confirmed the upregulation of gene expression levels post-treatment (Fig. 10c). The heatmap in Fig. 10d, e highlights the significant DEGs between the two groups, with color intensity corresponding to gene expression levels. In the HRPL group, several genes associated with vascular remodeling, anti-inflammatory responses, and endothelial cell proliferation, such as Fgf2 [69], Mef2c [70], Lrp6 [71], Ror1 [72], Bcl2l1 [73], Hrf [74], Mef2a [75], and Adcy1 [76] were notably upregulated compared to the model group. Conversely, genes linked to coagulation, platelet activation, and pro-inflammatory responses, including Btk [77], Plcb2 [78], Nrf1 [79], Cybb [80], CD14 [81], and Syk [82] were significantly downregulated in the HRPL group.

To gain deeper insights into the functional implications of the differentially expressed genes on the repair and remodeling of ECs, we performed GO functional enrichment analysis (Fig. 10e). The results of the GO enrichment analysis suggest that HRPL may facilitate vascular healing and remodeling by modulating critical processes such as calcium ion signaling, coagulation, vascular remodeling, and inflammatory responses. To further explore the pathways associated with endothelial cell growth and vascular development, we employed the chord diagram and KEGG bubble chart analysis (Fig. 10f, g). Both analyses revealed significant enrichment of differentially expressed genes in key pathways related to vascular remodeling, ECs proliferation, coagulation, platelet activation, and inflammation. Additionally, a network diagram was constructed to illustrate the relationships and interactions among the differentially expressed genes within these critical pathways, which include vascular remodeling, ECs proliferation, coagulation and platelet activation, and inflammation-related pathways (Fig. 10h). The close interactions observed among these genes form a highly interconnected regulatory network, indicating that HRPL may promote vascular healing through the coordinated modulation of multiple interconnected signaling pathways.

The favorable therapeutic performance of HRPL is likely attributed to the coordinated regulation of multiple pathological events after vascular injury. Unlike conventional single-drug strategies, HRPL simultaneously modulated oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and smooth muscle hyperplasia, which are closely interconnected during restenosis progression. Reduction of excessive ROS may help preserve endothelial viability and suppress inflammatory activation, thereby creating a microenvironment more conducive to vascular repair. Meanwhile, LXW7-mediated endothelial targeting enhanced reendothelialization, whereas sustained intracellular release of RAPA effectively restrained SMC overproliferation. This multi-target mechanism may explain the superior in vivo outcomes observed in the HRPL-treated group and highlights the potential advantages of multifunctional biomimetic nanoplatforms for postinterventional vascular therapy.

Conclusion

We successfully developed the HRPL nanozyme by incorporating RAPA into an HMPB framework, coating it with PM, and functionalizing the surface with LXW7 peptide. These modifications enabled the nanozyme to specifically target ECs and SMCs at vascular injury sites, thereby promoting endothelial regeneration while preserving the contractile phenotype of SMCs. In addition, the nanozyme effectively scavenged ROS, alleviated inflammation, and restored vascular homeostasis. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed its therapeutic efficacy in alleviating poststenting complications. Although the current study was conducted in small animal models, and differences in vascular structure and physiology may limit direct clinical translation, the present findings provide a solid basis for further investigation. Future studies should focus on validation in large animal models, long-term biosafety, and therapeutic evaluation at different postoperative time points. The modular nanostructure design of HRPL allows for the incorporation of additional drugs or targeting ligands, enabling the treatment of other inflammatory vascular diseases such as atherosclerosis and peripheral artery disease. This advancement supports the goal of achieving multifaceted restoration of vascular homeostasis in complex clinical scenarios.
