Hypertension, a major risk factor for adverse cardiovascular events like stroke, heart failure, and death, remains one of the most common chronic diseases and a leading global health challenge.1 Epidemiological studies show that only about 50% of patients who are being treated with antihypertensives achieve desired blood pressure (BP) goals, shedding further light on the complex nature of hypertension management.2 Resistant hypertension (RH) is defined as BP that stays above guideline-specified goals despite the use of maximally tolerated doses of at least 3 antihypertensives or is controlled when on 4 or more.3 Prior studies report that up to 20% of patients with high BP are found to be resistant to treatment.4 This subgroup of patients is at significantly higher risk of end-organ damage, overall morbidity, and mortality.5

Project Rationale

Since management often requires multifactorial approaches, the health burden of this condition becomes most profound in regions where there is a lack of specialized RH programs. Although the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) have clinical recommendations for screening, detecting, and managing RH, there is a clinically significant gap in screening for secondary causes and achieving target BP.6 In response to this need, we established a specialized RH clinic at a major academic health center in Northern California with the goal of improving awareness across primary care specialties to increase screening rates for secondary causes of hypertension and improve access to care. This quality-improvement initiative aimed to educate physicians on early detection of RH, increase adherence to ACC/AHA recommendations to screen for and treat secondary causes, and integrate remote BP monitoring to help patients take active control of their heart health.

Project Description

A real-time population health-based registry was created in collaboration with information technology (IT) to identify specific patients in large health systems via electronic medical records (EMR) who have uncontrolled BP while on at least 3 antihypertensive medications. A retrospective chart review was then conducted to confirm that patients were appropriately identified (Figure 1). To alert the primary care physician (PCP) of patients identified with RH and raise awareness of this condition, EMR-based outreach was performed monthly via telephone encounters. To optimize this process, a standardized prechecked order set was created and pended to each PCP via EMR. With a single click, PCPs were able to approve initial workup for secondary causes of hypertension and refer patients to the RH clinic. This order set included plasma renin and aldosterone, along with a renal artery ultrasound (Figure 2).

Chart Review and Exclusion Criteria

In collaboration with Information Technology and UC Population Health, a patient registry was created to identify patients with systolic blood pressure (SBP) above 160 mm Hg. Exclusion criteria were applied to deduce truly resistant cases, and initial clinic visits were completed promptly.

After patients completed their initial screening, they were scheduled at clinic to receive thorough evaluation by a cardiologist. If any secondary causes of hypertension were identified, a nephrologist or endocrinologist was consulted promptly for further evaluation and treatment. This team approach allowed for clinically appropriate patients to receive advanced therapies like renal artery stenting, angioplasty, and denervation in a timely manner. In addition, antihypertensive regimens were optimized according to the ACC/AHA guidelines. This was achieved by in-clinic visits or enrollment into a remote BP-monitoring program where accurate and consistent readings were obtained daily. At-home BP trends were monitored by our dedicated team of medical students, and care was escalated to the cardiologist as needed.

In collaboration with the IT department, a 2-way short message service–based text-messaging platform was created to provide automated reminders for medication adherence and consistent communication between patients and the care team (Figure 3). Changes in medication regimens, rate of screening for secondary causes, mean systolic BP, and overall clinical outcomes were assessed longitudinally.

Project Deliverables

This quality-improvement initiative established a specialty clinic dedicated to the management of RH in a region of California where there is an apparent lack of specialized care for such complex conditions. By weaving a multispecialty collaboration, remote monitoring, 2-way automated text message communication, and advanced therapies, our specialty clinic provided a multimodal approach to hypertension care. Our work delivered a systems-based approach to detecting patients with RH by utilizing population health registries, educating PCPs on guideline-driven care for early diagnosis, and facilitating workup and treatment of secondary causes of hypertension. We also provided expedited care for this high-risk patient population through multispecialty collaboration, remote BP monitoring, and real-time medical therapy escalation to break therapeutic inertia. Our initiative aimed to improve the quality of care by continued identification of patients whose BP remains at significantly elevated levels despite the use of antihypertensive therapy, enabling early guideline-recommended care.

Project Outcomes, Impact, and Future Directions

The initiative presented here is a collaboration between physicians, medical students, UC Population Health, and IT to raise awareness, achieve early diagnosis, and expedite multispecialty care for patients with RH. Initially, in collaboration with UC Population Health, the program focused on identifying patients on a system-wide level who were on 3 or more antihypertensives with systolic BP above 160 mm Hg. Chart review was completed for 271 patients, 52% of whom were excluded due to suboptimal medication doses or white-coat hypertension. The remaining 130 patients were suspected to have true RH. Their PCP was approached via a telephone encounter with notification about the identified patient and a single-click order set for referral and secondary workup. Thus far, our dedicated referral pathway and clinic have yielded 80 initial clinic visits, with an equal distribution of male and female patients aged 29 to 88 years (67 ± 15 years). This cohort includes patients with comorbidities like type II diabetes mellitus (41%) and coronary artery disease (28%).

Prior to being seen in clinic, only 24% had received any kind of workup for secondary causes of hypertension, highlighting the gap in the execution of ACC/AHA guidelines in routine clinical practice. The secondary workup was initiated for 100% of patients to bridge this gap, with over 80% of patients completing screening prior to their initial visit. Once seen in the clinic, patients often received further workup if needed.

Our data suggest that 68% of screened patients have at least one secondary cause of hypertension. Of these, primary hyperaldosteronism was found in 33% (n = 66), renal artery stenosis was found in 22% (n = 58), and obstructive sleep apnea was documented in 51% (n = 80). Our results highlight a successful quality-improvement initiative that leads to better adherence to guideline-directed therapy and current screening recommendations for patients who are suspected to have RH because it tremendously improves the detection of modifiable secondary causes.

Given the high-risk nature of RH, patients in this population often require multispecialty care. In our program, 33% of patients received referral to nephrology or endocrinology (n = 80), and 20% (n = 80) required advanced therapies like renal denervation, stenting, and angioplasty.

Remote BP monitoring was offered to 25% of patients (n = 80), where communication between patients and the clinical team consisted of weekly check-in calls, along with more frequent calls when escalating care. Patient responsiveness was achieved with 1 to 2 calls on average, allowing for consistent patient-physician communication.

Therapeutic interventions were impactful. Medication changes were implemented for 58% of patients (n = 80), the majority of which included adding new medications or uptitrating existing regimens. A drop in systolic BP below 140 mm Hg was seen in almost 60% of those with medication changes. A total of 52 patients (70%, n = 74) achieved systolic BP below 140 mm Hg, with 45 patients (61%, n = 74) documenting a decrease of 10 mm Hg or more, and 24 patients (32%, n = 74) documenting 20 mm Hg or more. A decrease in mean systolic BP of this magnitude is often described in literature as being associated with decreased risk of future adverse cardiovascular events and overall mortality, highlighting the positive impact this initiative has on a very-high-risk patient population. Future steps in our work involve expanding the data registry to include more patients than just those at highest risk (systolic BP >160 mm Hg) to cast a wider net and identify RH in patients whose BP continues to remain above therapeutic goals.

Discussion

The specialized RH clinic we established has led to significant improvement in screening rates for secondary causes of hypertension, diagnostic accuracy, and clinical outcomes in a very-high-risk patient population. Our findings confirm the under-recognition of secondary hypertension in routine clinical practices. Only 24% of patients had ever undergone workup despite meeting the criteria for RH, displaying a major lack of adherence to current ACC/AHA guidelines. By creating a standardized order set and referral pathway, we provided all patients meeting criteria with an opportunity to be evaluated for secondary causes of hypertension that may be treatable. We showed that novel system-wide approaches are necessary to consistently apply guideline-recommended screening and diagnostic tools in routine clinical practice and close the gap in hypertension care.

By establishing our multidisciplinary approach, we optimized medication regimens in a timely manner, enhanced patient engagement through remote monitoring, and addressed contributing comorbidities through additional specialty care. This model yielded clinically significant reductions in mean systolic BP, demonstrating our work's positive impact on cardiovascular care.

Project Description and Take-Home Messages

Conclusions

Our quality-improvement initiative demonstrates that a specialized clinic dedicated to management of RH can successfully increase early diagnosis, achieve clinically significant reductions in mean systolic BP, and enhance quality of hypertension care through multidisciplinary collaboration.
