Cost-effective and sustainable ultra-high-performance concrete with electric arc furnace slag and calcium carbonate: a multi-objective optimization and experimental validation approach
Authors: Joaquin Abellan-Garcia, Nemesio Daza-Marquez, Marielena Molinares
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ultra-high-performance concrete (UHPC) delivers exceptional performance, but it is still largely a high-clinker material, which drives up both cost and embodied CO₂. Here, we present a low-clinker UHPC matrix that incorporates electric arc furnace slag (EAFS) and calcium carbonate powder (CaC), with quartz flour fully replaced by CaC. The mixtures were proportioned using the Modified Andreasen & Andersen particle-packing approach and then explored through a central composite design to quantify how cement dosage, water-to-binder ratio, and high-range water-reducer (HRWR) dosage control fresh flow and strength development. A desirability-based optimization was finally used to minimize cement content while maintaining self-compacting workability (240–260 mm slump flow) and UHPC-level 28-day strength (≥ 150 MPa). Relative to the control mixture, the optimized formulation reduced cement from 852 to 598 kg/m³ (− 29.8%), which corresponds to an approximate ≈ 30% reduction in cement-related embodied CO₂ (≈ 127–178 kg CO₂/m³ avoided on a cement-only basis). Under ambient curing, the optimized mix achieved a slump flow of 252 mm and compressive strengths of 151.1 ± 7.9 MPa at 28 days and 158.9 ± 3.1 MPa at 90 days (+ 5.1% from 28 to 90 days). Young’s modulus increased from 40.1 ± 1.5 GPa at 7 days to 45.0 ± 1.4 GPa at 90 days. RCPT charge passed decreased with age (281 ± 11 C at 28 days to 78 ± 7 C at 90 days), indicating improved resistance to chloride-ion penetration over time, although values remained higher than the control. Overall, the results show that UHPC-level strength and self-compacting flow can be achieved with a substantially reduced clinker demand using a particle packing-theory and statistic-guided mixture design strategy.

Introduction

Background

Ultra-high-performance concrete (UHPC), introduced in the 1990s, is a cement-based composite characterized by an exceptionally dense microstructure and very low permeability, typically achieving compressive strengths above 150 MPa1–3. UHPC is commonly produced without coarse aggregate and relies on optimized particle packing of cement and fine powders - e.g., microsilica (MS) and quartz flour (QF) -, fine sand, and a high-range water-reducer to attain self-compacting behavior at very low water-to-binder ratios. Recent matrix-focused design strategies that combine particle packing concepts with statistical/data-driven approaches have enabled more efficient UHPC formulations and motivate the framework adopted in this study4. UHPC has been adopted in a range of infrastructure and building applications, particularly in precast elements, bridge components and joints, overlays/rehabilitation works, and selected transportation or marine structures, where its high strength and durability enable slender sections and reduced maintenance. Its use is also compatible with accelerated bridge construction (ABC) strategies that rely on rapid assembly and short closure windows5–8.

Despite the superior mechanical performance and durability of UHPC, its broader adoption remains constrained by the high binder demand and the associated cost and embodied CO29. Typical UHPC mixtures incorporate around 800 kg/m3 of OPC4, which is a major contributor to both material cost and CO2 footprint10,11. In addition, quartz-rich fine powders (e.g., quartz flour) raise occupational exposure concerns during handling10,12, while the high specific surface of microsilica increases HRWR demand and can adversely affect rheological robustness and cost10,13. Therefore, reducing clinker content and replacing conventional fine powders with alternative SCMs/fillers are key pathways to improve UHPC sustainability without compromising performance.

Literature survey

In recent years, substantial research efforts have focused on reducing clinker demand and improving the environmental profile of cementitious materials by incorporating industrial by-products and other alternative powders as supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) and fillers. In cementitious mortars, for instance, partial replacement of Portland cement with finely ground waste-derived powders has been shown to reduce embodied emissions/energy while promoting longer-term strength development through delayed pozzolanic activity and microstructural refinement, despite reduced early-age strength14. On the other hand, in conventional concrete, eco-friendly systems combining multiple SCMs have been assessed through mechanical testing and durability-related indicators (including RCPT and transport-related measurements), highlighting that synergistic filler/packing effects may improve strength and reduce chloride-ion penetrability, although durability under chemical exposure can remain mixture-dependent15.

While most of these studies are reported for mortars and conventional concretes, the underlying mechanisms—particle packing enhancement, nucleation/filler effects, and longer-term pozzolanic reactions—are also central to UHPC matrix design, where performance is strongly governed by powder packing density and binder chemistry. Accordingly, these developments support the broader rationale for investigating alternative SCMs and fillers in UHPC systems aimed at reducing clinker content. For instance, the use of alternative mineral admixtures, such as Electric arc furnace slag (EAFS) and milled calcium carbonate (CaC), has been investigated in concrete to reduce cement consumption and modify material performance. In the case of EAFS, it has been studied as an aggregate and as supplementary cementitious materials as well16–18. Its influence on pore structure has been associated with reductions in permeability, which affects durability and resistance to aggressive agents, including chlorides and sulfates19,20. Additionally, its dark coloration has been observed to alter the thermal properties of hardened concrete, potentially influencing heat absorption and dissipation21. In high-strength concrete, the refinement of the interfacial transition zone by EAFS has been linked to improvements in mechanical properties and crack resistance22,23.

CaC has been recognized for its impact on rheology and hydration kinetics. Its interaction with cementitious phases has been reported to influence setting times and early-age strength development24,25. Studies have indicated that its presence stabilizes ettringite formation, reducing the risk of delayed ettringite expansion in mixtures exposed to elevated curing temperatures26,27. As a filler material, it has been observed to contribute to matrix densification, affecting porosity and workability. Additionally, limestone powder has been shown to act as a nucleation site for hydration products, modifying the reaction kinetics and influencing the formation of calcium-silicate-hydrate (C-S-H) phases28,29. When combined with EAFS, adjustments in hydration kinetics and the balance between pozzolanic activity and filler effects have been noted20,30.

Although previous studies have examined the combined use of EAFS and CaC powder in relation to volumetric stability, particularly in low water-to-binder ratio mixtures, there remain significant gaps in understanding their long-term durability performance in UHPC formulations. While the inclusion of these materials has been reported to mitigate autogenous shrinkage31 and contribute to early-age stability, comprehensive assessments of their effects on key parameters, such as compressive strength, elasticity modulus, or chloride ion penetration, are still lacking32. Furthermore, the role of EAFS in enhancing long-term strength development and the impact of CaC on early hydration kinetics have not been fully explored in optimized UHPC mixtures designed for reduced cement consumption. The present study addresses these gaps by systematically evaluating the mechanical and durability performance of UHPC incorporating EAFS and CaC under a multi-objective optimization framework. This research provides critical insights into the compatibility of these materials with other SCMs and their potential to enhance UHPC sustainability by minimizing clinker content while maintaining self-compacting workability and UHPC-level mechanical performance, and by reporting an initial durability indicator under chloride-ion penetrability testing conditions (RCPT).

Significance, scope, and objectives of the study

Despite the rapid strides made in UHPC research in recent years, persistent concerns related to cost and sustainability remain, largely driven by the high binder content typically required to achieve UHPC-level performance. In this context, incorporating locally available industrial by-products and replacing specific fine powder constituents with more readily available alternatives are practical strategies to improve the environmental and economic attractiveness of UHPC while preserving fresh-state and mechanical requirements.

This study focuses on UHPC matrix development using EAFS and CaC. The EAFS investigated in this work was sourced from an electric arc steel furnace in Boyacá, Colombia, and its valorization in UHPC contributes to waste reduction and circular-economy implementation. In addition, this research fully replaces quartz flour (QF) with CaC as part of the binder/filler system. Importantly, recycled glass is not used in any mixture in this study; the fine aggregate employed is conventional quartz microsand, consistent with the mixture tables and validation program.

The primary objectives of the study are: (i) to proportion fixed matrix constituents using the Modified Andreasen & Andersen (MAA) model33; (ii) to quantify, via a Central Composite Design (CCD) and response surface methodology (RSM), the effects of three factors (Portland cement dosage, water-to-binder ratio, and superplasticizer dosage) on rheology and compressive strength development34; (iii) to perform an RSM-based multi-objective optimization to identify an UHPC mixture that satisfies self-compacting rheological performance and UHPC-level 28-day compressive strength (≥ 150 MPa) with the lowest feasible Portland cement content; and (iv) to experimentally validate the optimized mixture against a control UHPC using the testing program reported in this manuscript.

Materials

In this present study, the UHPC formulation incorporated the following components: OPC, MS, EAFS, CaC, and quartz microsand. The cement used complied with ASTM C150 Type I specifications and was classified as CEM I 52.5R according to EN 197-1. Quartz microsand, with an average particle size (d50) of 165 μm and a maximum particle size (dmax) of 600 μm, was used as the fine aggregate in all UHPC mixtures reported in this study. A visual depiction of the granular raw materials used in the production of UHPC is presented in Fig. 3. To ensure adequate dispersion and workability at the low water-to-binder ratios characteristic of UHPC, an HRWR was incorporated. The HRWR used in this study was a polycarboxylate-ether-based superplasticizer, supplied by a commercial manufacturer. According to the technical datasheet, the admixture had a solid content of approximately 40% and a specific gravity of 1.07 at 20 °C, and it was used in liquid form as received, without further dilution.

The factor ranges adopted in Table 2 were defined based on our previous UHPC mix-design and optimization studies incorporating sustainability-oriented constituents42 and were confirmed through preliminary screening mixtures to ensure practical feasibility within the intended design space. Specifically, the selected cement-dosage interval was chosen to represent a meaningful clinker reduction while still allowing UHPC-level strength to be reached under ambient curing. The w/b interval was selected within the typical UHPC practice window to preserve matrix density while maintaining self-compacting workability. The HRWR interval was set to span the dosage levels required to achieve the targeted flowability at such low w/b ratios, while avoiding overdosing regimes that can increase viscosity, impair air release, and increase sensitivity to cement–admixture compatibility.

On the other hand, MS was fixed at 100 kg/m³ to enhance practicality and cost-effectiveness and to avoid introducing an additional highly coupled variable in the DoE. MS is among the most expensive UHPC constituents, and, due to its very high specific surface area, it typically increases the demand for polycarboxylate-based HRWR. Higher HRWR dosages not only raise cost but may also exacerbate cement–admixture compatibility sensitivity, which can affect rheology and robustness42.

A total of 20 DPs were randomly distributed according to a CCD framework within the DoE. To optimize UHPC mixes, a multi-objective model is proposed, where key characteristics (treated as response variables) are: (1) self-compacting ability, (2) compressive strength, and (3) low OPC content. Significant factors are adjusted as inputs. The optimization process employs an analytical technique within the Response Surface Methodology (RSM) framework, utilizing the Derringer and Suich function44. The evaluation of desirability functions will change according to the preferred maximization or minimization of the response or even its constraint within a range defined by lower and upper values denoted as L and U respectively44.

Table 5 presents the optimization objectives for this research, specifying the desired response variable values. The identified simultaneous optimal points correspond to mathematically derived solutions that ensure all relevant variables remain within defined limits. It should be emphasized that these points do not necessarily align with the initial control parameters but provide viable alternatives that achieve the target responses while adhering to the imposed constraints45. The values and goals outlined in Table 5 are justified by both technical and practical considerations in multi-objective optimization. Factor A, the cement dosage, is minimized to reduce the carbon footprint of UHPC, as cement production is a major contributor to greenhouse gas emissions. Factor B, the water-to-binder ratio, is constrained between 0.16 and 0.18, aligning with the factorial points defined in the experimental design. Factor C, the HRWR concentration, is maintained within a narrow range to optimize performance and cost efficiency. While HRWR is essential for achieving the low w/b ratio characteristic of UHPC, excessive amounts can increase viscosity, hinder air release, and compromise mix performance46,47.

For the response variables, compressive strength at 28 days is maximized to exceed the minimum threshold of 150 MPa as specified by ACI 239 for UHPC classification48. An upper limit of 155 MPa was introduced to define a practical optimization window, accounting for experimental variability while preventing the optimization from converging toward high-cement mixtures. Within the experimental domain, mixtures exceeding this value were associated with increased OPC and MS contents and reduced workability, without proportional gains in overall performance, as demonstrated in previous UHPC optimization studies by the authors42. Similarly, the WR is constrained within the range of 240–260 mm to maintain the self-compacting behavior critical for the application of UHPC in practice49,50.

Experimental procedure

In the RSM phase of this research, the rheology of fresh concrete was assessed through the slump flow diameter (WR), while the compressive strength of hardened concrete was analyzed at 1, 7, 14, and 28 days (R1, R7, R14, and R28 respectively) without a predefined curing regimen. These four (4) keys response variables were central to the analysis. Figure 8 provides a visual representation of the tests performed in this study.

Fig. 8(a) Rheology test. (b) compression test.

(a) Rheology test. (b) compression test.

All UHPC batches were prepared using a planetary laboratory mixer. For the CCD mixtures (cast as 50-mm cubes), a 5-L mixer was used, whereas the control and optimized validation mixtures were produced in a 20-L mixer to provide sufficient volume for 100 × 200-mm cylinders and RCPT discs. The same mixing sequence and nominal mixing durations were applied in both mixers: (i) all powder constituents (OPC, MS, CaC, and EAFS) were dry-mixed for 10 min; (ii) mixing water pre-blended with the HRWR was added gradually and the paste was mixed for an additional 5 min; (iii) quartz microsand was then incorporated gradually and mixed for 3 min; and (iv) a final 2 min mixing stage was applied to ensure full homogenization (total mixing time ≈ 20 min). Immediately after mixing, workability was assessed according to ASTM C143751, reporting the mean of four perpendicular spread diameters. The mixture was then re-mixed for 1 min prior to casting. Specimens were cast with slight external vibration during casting and for ~ 1 min after casting, covered to prevent moisture loss, demolded after 24 h, and cured in a moisture-controlled chamber at 20 °C until testing.

For the CCD stage, compressive strength was determined on 50-mm cubes following ASTM C10952 (three replicates per age). For validation, compressive strength was evaluated on 100 × 200-mm cylinders according to ASTM C3952 at 1, 7, 28, and 90 days (3 replicates per age), and Young’s modulus was measured following ASTM C46953 at 7, 28, and 90 days (also 3 replicates per age).

Chloride resistance was assessed by RCPT according to ASTM C120254 using discs cut from the cylinders (100 mm diameter; 50 ± 3 mm thickness). The lateral surface of each disc was sealed with an epoxy resin to promote essentially one-dimensional ion transport through the specimen faces, and specimens were conditioned as specified in ASTM C1202 prior to testing. A 60-V DC potential was applied for 6 h using 3% NaCl and 0.3 M NaOH solutions in the respective cells, and the total charge passed (Coulombs) was reported as the mean of three replicate discs per curing age.

Standardized main effects and interactions

To evaluate how the factors of the experiment, i.e. the cement dosage (A), water-to-binder ratio (B), and HRWR concentration (C), individually influence the responses, a graphical analysis of main effects is conducted. While this analysis is insightful in explaining the physical and chemical behavior of the mix and the response variation given the direct impact of each factor, it is not a sufficient criterion to understand the response variables’ behavior under the combined effects and select optimal experimental conditions. To overcome this limitation, the effects of interactions description and analysis are also included to examine how the combination of factors could amplify or mitigate the individual effects.

Responses analysis

Slump flow

Illustrated in Fig. 10, the slump is negatively affected by OPC content (A) due to increased hydration demand, reducing free water availability and promoting early C-S-H and ettringite formation which enhances cohesion and restricts flow. Conversely, the w/b ratio (B) positively influences slump by increasing fluidity, though excessive levels compromise matrix stability through segregation. HRWR improves workability by dispersing cement and MS particles, which reduces electrostatic interactions and lowers viscosity. However, at high A levels and low B values, rapid hydration limits the effectiveness of HRWR in enhancing fluidity. Previous studies55,56 indicate that the hydration of C3S and C2S is directly influenced by w/b, with a lower ratio reducing the size of capillary pores, thereby impacting workability and long-term performance.

Main effects plot of (A–C) effects over WR (slump) response.

Figure 11 displays the interaction between the water/binder ratio ( Factor B), and HRWR content (Factor C) on UHPC workability across different binder content (factor A) levels. For A = − 1, with low cement and high supplementary materials, an increase in w/b raises slump due to excess of free water, while HRWR enhances fluidity by dispersing cement and MS particles, which reduces viscosity and promotes water mobility. At A = 0, where OPC and supplementary materials can be considered balanced, higher w/b still increases slump but to a lesser extent, as a denser microstructure reduces HRWR’s impact on fluidity and enhances mix cohesion. At A = 1, high OPC content accelerates hydration, quickly consuming water and limiting HRWR’s effectiveness, which results in a more stable but less workable mix. Chemically, low A reduces early C-S-H and ettringite formation that leaves more free water, while HRWR supports water movement and particle distribution. For A = 0, a balance of OPC, and MS forms more C-S-H, reducing free water and slightly limiting HRWR’s effect. At A = 1, rapid hydration consumes water, restricting HRWR’s ability to lower viscosity, which increases stability but limits slump enhancement.

UHPC’s compressive strength at one day of curing

As indicated in Fig. 12, R1 is influenced by A, where higher OPC content increases hydration product formation, which densifies the microstructure and improves early strength. However, excessive A levels elevate water demand, which leads to heterogeneous particle distribution and reduces the hydration efficiency. The w/b ratio exhibits an inverse correlation with R1, as higher values dilute cementitious concentration, which reduces the hydration rates, whereas lower B values promote a confined hydration environment that enhances strength. The initial hydration reactions of C3S and C2S are highly dependent on w/b, as low values accelerate C-S-H precipitation and reduce porosity, whereas higher w/b results in a more disconnected microstructure57. Additinally, early strength is influenced by the degree of particle packing, where a denser packing configuration enhances contact points between reactive phases, which contributes to higher mechanical performance at this stage36,58.

Compressive strength

Figure 20 illustrates the results obtained from the compressive strength measurements of the control and optimized samples at 1, 7, 28, and 90 days. At 1 day, the optimized mixture reached 41.03 ± 3.43 MPa, corresponding to a 45.0% reduction relative to the control mixture (74.60 ± 2.13 MPa). By 7 days, compressive strength increased to 79.85 ± 2.51 MPa for the optimized mixture and 119.78 ± 2.05 MPa for the control, resulting in a 33.3% difference. As curing progressed to 28 days, the optimized mixture achieved 151.12 ± 7.87 MPa, compared to 167.25 ± 2.81 MPa for the control, reducing the strength gap to 9.6%. At extended curing age (90 days), compressive strength further increased to 158.88 ± 3.11 MPa for the optimized mixture and 168.21 ± 2.91 MPa for the control, corresponding to a 5.55% difference. Despite the slightly lower compressive strength values of the optimized mixture, its performance can be considered satisfactory, as it remained within above the UHPC compressive strength threshold adopted in this study (≥ 150 MPa at 28 days), particularly noteworthy given the significant reduction in cement content.

Fig. 20Compressive strength development across ages and formulations.

Compressive strength development across ages and formulations.

The control mixture, with a higher content of OPC and MS, promoted the early formation (1 and 7 days) of C-S-H gel due to the high reactivity and the greater availability of cementitious materials. Additionally, the superior reactivity of the control mix, particularly in the early stages, can be ascribed to its higher MS content, which enhances packing density and minimizes the available surface area for CH generation. The advantages of MS over EAFS lie in its higher amorphous silicon dioxide content (Table 1; Fig. 5) and smaller particle size (Figs. 4 and 6), which collectively improve its specific surface area and pozzolanic reactivity.

In contrast, the optimized mixture, with reduced OPC and MS content, relied on the incorporation of CaC and EAFS to achieve comparable performance at later ages (28 and 90 days). These additions contributed to densifying the matrix by enhancing granular packing efficiency and reducing porosity. Furthermore, EAFS’s pozzolanic activity, although less reactive than MS in the early stages, became more significant over time, aiding the formation of secondary C-S-H and refining the microstructure. This delayed reactivity, combined with the filler effect of CaC, compensated for the lower cementitious material availability, demonstrating the potential of these components to maintain mechanical performance while supporting a more sustainable mixture design. These findings not only validate the effectiveness of the optimized UHPC mix but also highlight the potential of EAFS as a valuable additive for efficient and sustainable UHPC formulations.

Young’s modulus

As illustrated in Fig. 21, Young’s modulus of the control mixture was 42.20 ± 1.20 GPa at 7 days, compared to 40.10 ± 1.50 GPa for the optimized mixture, corresponding to a 5.0% lower modulus (relative to the control). At 28 days, the control reached 48.30 ± 1.20 GPa while the optimized mixture reached 43.20 ± 1.90 GPa, increasing the gap to 10.6%. At 90 days, both mixtures exhibited a modest additional increase, reaching 50.20 ± 1.20 GPa (control) and 45.00 ± 1.40 GPa (optimized), corresponding to a 10.4% difference. Overall, the optimized mixture consistently shows lower stiffness than the control, which is consistent with its reduced PC and MS contents; nevertheless, it maintains a high modulus level while meeting the UHPC strength requirement adopted in this study with substantially reduced cement content.

Chloride ion penetration resistance

Figure 22 reports the RCPT charge passed (ASTM C1202) at 7, 28, and 90 days for the control and optimized mixtures. At 7 days, the optimized mixture recorded 1322 ± 6 C, compared to 1161 ± 11.6 C for the control, i.e., a 13.9% higher charge passed. After 28 days of curing, the optimized mixture exhibited 281 ± 11 C versus 80 ± 12 C for the control, corresponding to a 251% increase, which is consistent with delayed pore-structure refinement due to the reduced MS content. By 90 days, the difference narrowed, with 78 ± 7 C for the optimized mixture compared to 49 ± 10 C for the control (59% higher), indicating a marked age-dependent reduction in charge passed for both mixtures. This trend is attributed to the long-term pozzolanic contribution of EAFS and the filler effect of CaC, which progressively reduces pore connectivity75,76.

Here, it is important to note that from a practical standpoint, although the optimized mixture shows lower early-age strength and a higher 28-day RCPT charge passed than the control under ambient curing, its 28-day strength still meets the UHPC threshold and its 28-day charge passed remains within the ‘very low’ penetrability range defined by ASTM C1202, while time-critical production scenarios (e.g., precast turnover) can mitigate early-age penalties through the steam/thermal curing practices commonly adopted for UHPC to accelerate strength development77.

Conclusions, limitations, and implications

In this research, a cost-effective and sustainable UHPC mix was successfully developed through a multi-objective optimization approach, which integrates EAFS and CaC as mineral admixtures. This formulation effectively minimized OPC content while maintaining superior mechanical properties. The optimized mixture exhibited a compressive strength exceeding 150 MPa at 28 days with improved rheological behavior and sustainability benefits. The incorporation of EAFS contributed to the pozzolanic reactivity, enhancing secondary hydration product formation. Moreover, its 2 μm mean particle size, allowed for a reduction in silica content without compromising packing density. CaC with a particle size of 15 μm was used for improving the particle packing density instead of the typical quartz powder. Furthermore, the optimized UHPC mix reduced superplasticizer demand, leading to a more economical formulation without affecting workability. The experimental campaign validated the optimized UHPC by evaluating compressive strength, Young’s modulus, and chloride ion penetration resistance. Compared to a reference mix, the optimized UHPC exhibited slightly lower early-age compressive strength and higher chloride ion penetration at 28 days. However, over time, the pozzolanic activity of EAFS contributed to microstructural densification, reducing these differences at 90 days, although the optimized mix never fully matched the reference in terms of strength and durability. Additionally, a progressive reduction in RCPT charge passed was observed with curing age; however, since durability assessment in this study is limited to RCPT, complementary tests (e.g., chloride migration/diffusion, shrinkage, freeze–thaw, and exposure-based evaluations) are required for a comprehensive durability validation. These findings highlight the feasibility of using EAFS and CaC as sustainable alternatives in UHPC, which promotes resource efficiency and lowers environmental impact. Recommendations for future research

While this study demonstrates the feasibility of reducing clinker content in UHPC through the incorporation of EAFS and the full replacement of quartz flour with CaC, the evidence provided is bounded by the scope of the experimental program. In particular, durability was assessed only through RCPT (ASTM C1202), and the mechanical characterization focused on compressive strength and Young’s modulus. No testing was performed on shrinkage-related behavior (autogenous/drying), tensile/flexural response, or exposure-based durability under service-relevant conditions. Therefore, further research should prioritize the following directions, explicitly addressing these limitations:

Durability and hydration/microstructure—addressing RCPT-only limitation. Future studies should expand durability assessment beyond RCPT by including complementary transport and exposure-related indicators (e.g., chloride migration/diffusion coefficients and exposure-based chloride ingress), as well as other relevant durability mechanisms (e.g., freeze–thaw resistance and chemical attack, where applicable). These tests are needed to validate whether the age-dependent reduction in RCPT charge passed observed in this study translates into robust long-term performance under service conditions. In parallel, a more detailed investigation of hydration kinetics and microstructural evolution (pore refinement and secondary hydration product formation) should be conducted to clarify the mechanisms governing early-age penalties and later-age development, and to refine the EAFS/CaC dosage balance.Volume stability and structural performance—addressing missing shrinkage and tensile/flexural data. Given the reduced OPC and MS contents, future work should quantify autogenous and drying shrinkage and assess cracking susceptibility under restrained conditions to establish volume-stability performance. In addition, tensile and flexural characterization (including fracture-related parameters) is required to support structural design use cases, particularly if fiber reinforcement is considered in subsequent developments. These data would complement the compressive-strength and modulus results and enable a more complete performance-based assessment.Scale-up and implementation—addressing laboratory-scale limitation. Finally, the optimized mixture should be validated at larger batch volumes and in representative structural elements (e.g., beams, slabs, and precast components) to evaluate mixing robustness, placement/finishing, and mechanical performance at scale. This step is needed to confirm that the optimized formulation remains practical and reproducible under field-oriented production constraints.
