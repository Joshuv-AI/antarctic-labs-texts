An exploratory study of physicians’ familiarity, knowledge, and willingness to prescribe pre-exposure prophylaxis in the MENA region
Authors: Jihane Naous, Jad El Khoury, Lea Feghali, Remie El Helou, Georgeio Sader, Nesrine A Rizk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is among the most effective tools used for HIV prevention. Primary care providers (PCPs) play a crucial role in identifying people at risk and initiating care. There is a gap in research on PrEP use in the MENA region; this study aims to assess PCPs’ knowledge, familiarity with PrEP and willingness to prescribe it. An online survey targeted PCPs and infectious disease (ID) specialists across the MENA region, with most responses coming from Lebanon. Data included demographics, practice characteristics, perceived knowledge, familiarity with PrEP, willingness to prescribe it, and barriers. Descriptive statistics and bivariate analysis were applied to compare groups. Seventy-five providers responded, sex ratio nearly 1:1, most being PCPs. ID specialists more often treated STIs and cared for people living with HIV than PCPs (p = 0.003 and p < 0.001, respectively). 40% rated themselves “somewhat unfamiliar/unfamiliar” with PrEP, and only 24% felt confident answering patients’ questions. While 73% were willing to prescribe PrEP, 64% had never done so. More PCPs (78.3%) than ID specialists (19.6%) had never prescribed PrEP (p = 0.005). Barriers to prescribing PrEP included lack of knowledge, financial coverage (p < 0.001), governmental policy regulating PrEP use (p < 0.001), and counselling time (all p < 0.001). Findings highlight significant gaps in knowledge and practice, particularly among PCPs, despite high willingness to prescribe. Introduction

Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is among the most effective tools available for the primary prevention of HIV in individuals at risk for acquiring the infection through sexual contact or injectable drug use1. PrEP is an integral component of HIV prevention strategies advocated by the World Health Organization (WHO) for high-risk populations, including men who have sex with men, people who inject drugs, and sex workers2. PrEP is effective in reducing transmission of HIV by up to 99% and is available in various forms, including oral pills, long-acting injections, and vaginal rings. In some low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), health authorities adopted PrEP for HIV prevention and developed national implementation strategies, including education to healthcare providers (HCPs) and providing the medication free of charge3.

According to a UNAIDS report, the MENA region is one of only two global regions where the incidence and prevalence of HIV infections are increasing4. One of the most important steps to address the HIV epidemic in the MENA is to prioritize and adopt effective HIV prevention strategies, such as PrEP. However, only one country in the MENA, Morocco, has officially approved PrEP in its national policies. In Lebanon, PrEP is being piloted as part of a national plan to decrease HIV’s rate of transmission among high-risk groups. Multiple reports indicate that in the MENA region, people at risk are using PrEP without medical supervision, a practice known as “wild PrEP.”5. Beyond national policies, several key factors also influence HIV prevention strategies in a country. These include social determinants such as stigma, discrimination, and gender inequality; health system capacity and infrastructure; socioeconomic conditions like poverty and education; community engagement and cultural context; individual risk behaviors and local epidemiology; and the availability of biomedical interventions6,7. These interact to shape the effectiveness of prevention strategies as highlighted by the International Antiviral Society -USA Panel8,9. Successful PrEP implementation requires HCPs to understand PrEP, communicate effectively with patients, and prescribe it safely or provide appropriate referrals10,11. While Infectious Diseases (ID) /HIV specialists and PCPs should be prescribing PrEP12, this practice is relatively recent, and not all providers have received adequate training on prescribing, dosing and administration13. Enhancing providers’ understanding of PrEP, addressing misconceptions, and promoting PrEP adoption in clinical practice can improve PrEP access for high-risk populations and help reduce HIV transmission13–17.

Several studies in North America and Europe have shown that ID and HIV specialists exhibit more knowledge and comfort with PrEP prescription compared to PCPs18. Research in the MENA region has primarily focused on patients’ use of PrEP19–22 while there is scant research assessing physicians’ knowledge and willingness to prescribe it. PCPs, in general, interact more frequently with individuals eligible for HIV prevention than ID/HIV specialists18,23. Therefore, improving PCPs’ familiarity and comfort with PrEP is crucial to achieve the implementation and integration of this intervention with other routine and preventive measures24,25. To our knowledge, this is the first study in the MENA region to assess PCPs’ knowledge, familiarity, and willingness to prescribe PrEP. It also aims to compare PCPs’ PrEP practices with ID specialists and to examine associations with key variables relevant to PrEP prescription.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted using an anonymous web-based questionnaire, created with Lime Survey and disseminated to ID specialists and PCPs practicing in the MENA region (see Fig. 1). As this was a cross-sectional design, the study identifies associations between variables but cannot establish causality.

Map of the MENA region highlighting countries of practice for survey respondents.

In this study, PrEP refers broadly to pre-exposure prophylaxis, without distinguishing between oral and other formulations. A PCP is defined as a physician who has completed a family medicine residency. This excludes medical doctors practicing as generalists solely after completing six or seven years of medical school, which occasionally occurs in some parts of this region. PCPs were recruited through four rounds of emails sent through the Arab Board of Family Physicians (250 physicians). ID specialists (75 physicians) were contacted through the Lebanese ID Society and other regional networks of HIV providers. Social media platforms, including X (formerly Twitter), LinkedIn, and Facebook, were also utilized to reach eligible participants.

Sample

A convenient sample was approached through emails, professional societies, and social media posts from March 2022 to February 2023. Eligible participants included PCPs or infectious diseases specialists practicing in the MENA region at the date of recruitment, and who are proficient in either English or French. Three reminders were sent at one-week intervals to encourage completion of the questionnaires.

Measures

The questionnaire was developed based on similar surveys reported in the literature that primarily utilized questions from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and adapted them to their respective health systems, such as the DocStyles surveys by Smith et al. (2016) and the provider-focused survey by Bacon et al. (2017)13,14,16,17,26. To ensure linguistic accuracy and minimize misinterpretation, a professional certified translator reviewed the questionnaire in both English and French. Furthermore, native English and French speakers piloted the survey to verify the clarity, accuracy, and consistency of the questions across both versions.

The variables included in this survey assessed demographic characteristics, professional background, work setting, prior knowledge of PrEP, and exposure to patients who may benefit from PrEP. Additionally, our team developed questions to assess knowledge, familiarity, confidence, and willingness to prescribe PrEP while exploring potential barriers to prescribing it. Familiarity was defined as “being familiar with the drug regimen”, and confidence was defined as “feeling confident about answering patients’ questions regarding PrEP”. Knowledge was defined as “overall self-perceived knowledge, knowing it is recommended for a patient to be or not on PrEP and knowing how to provide follow up for a patient on PrEP”.

Ethical consideration

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the American University of Beirut (AUB) (IRB ID SBS-2021-0388, approved March 2022) and the University of Florida (IRB 202102759 for data use and transfer only, approved December 2021) approved the study protocol, and informed consent was obtained electronically from participants before they completed the questionnaire. The questionnaire was completely anonymous, with no identifying information collected or reported in the results, ensuring confidentiality and protecting the privacy of the respondents.

Data management and statistical analysis

The collected data were entered into an Excel sheet and exported to SPSS statistical software (version 29.0.2.0) for data cleaning, management, and analysis. Additionally, the map was generated using Tableau software (version 2024.2; Tableau Software, LLC, Seattle, WA, USA). Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations (SD), and categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. A chi-square test was employed to compare the characteristics and outcomes between ID specialists and PCPs. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sample characteristics

The online survey was completed by 75 providers practicing in the MENA region. Among the respondents, 50.7% were male, with the largest proportion (56%) aged between 30 and 49 years (Table 1). 72% of the participants practiced in Lebanon, while the remaining participants were distributed across other countries in the MENA region. Most physicians were PCPs (61.3%) (Table 1).

Providers’ self-perceived knowledge, familiarity, comfort, and confidence regarding PrEP

In our study, 58.1% (25/43) of the PCPs perceived their overall knowledge of PrEP as unfamiliar or somewhat unfamiliar compared to 11.1% (3/27) of ID specialists (p = 0.002). Similarly, 60.4% (26/43) of PCPs reported being unfamiliar or somewhat unfamiliar with the drug regimen, in contrast to 7.7% (2/26) of ID specialists (p < 0.0001). Furthermore, 34.8% (16/46) of the PCPs reported being either not confident or somewhat not confident in answering patients’ questions about PrEP, compared to only 3.4% of ID specialists (p = 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Comparison of PrEP prescription practices, willingness, knowledge, familiarity, and confidence among non-ID and ID physicians in the MENA region. PrEP pre-exposure prophylaxis, ID infectious diseases specialists, non-ID primary care physicians (PCPs and other non-ID providers).

ID specialists were more likely than PCPs to have engaged in discussions about PrEP with colleagues (86.2% [25/29] vs. 54.3% [25/46], p = 0.005), to have received training in HIV management (82.8% [24/29] vs. 32.6% [15/46], p < 0.001), and to have gained knowledge about PrEP through peer-reviewed journal articles (75.9% [22/29] vs. 52.2% [24/46], p = 0.040) or medical conferences (86.2% [25/29] vs. 52.2% [24/46], p = 0.003). They were also significantly more likely to have been asked by their patients about PrEP (62.1% [18/29] vs. 34.8% [16/46], p = 0.023), to have ever treated sexually transmitted infections (100% [29/29] vs. 73.9% [34/46], p = 0.003), and to have provided care for people living with HIV (89.7% [26/29] vs. 45.7% [21/46], p < 0.001). Additionally, ID specialists were more likely than PCPs to believe that PrEP should only be prescribed by infectious disease specialists (51.7% [15/29] vs. 8.7% [4/46], p < 0.001) (Table 2).

ID specialists were also more likely to consider certain populations at high risk for HIV, including people who inject drugs or share needles (65.5% [19/29]vs. 87.0% [40/46], p = 0.027), HIV-negative individuals with HIV-positive partners whose viral load is detectable (75.9% [22/29] vs. 93.5% [43/46], p = 0.029), and individuals who exchange sex for money, goods, or housing (72.4% [21/29] vs. 93.5% [43/46], p = 0.012) (Table 2).

Willingness to prescribe PrEP

Among PCPs, 69.6% (32/46) reported willingness to prescribe PrEP, compared to 79.3% (23/29) of ID specialists (p = 0.183); however, a significantly higher percentage of PCPs,78.3% (36/46), reported never having prescribed PrEP when compared to ID specialists, 41.4% (12/29) (p = 0.005) (Fig. 2). Among the primary variables assessed (self-perceived knowledge, familiarity, confidence, and prior prescription), only prior prescribing was significantly associated with willingness to prescribe PrEP (p = 0.014) (Table 3). Additional factors significantly associated with willingness include: practicing in a group outpatient practice (p = 0.027), having ever treated patients with STIs (p = 0.021), having previously discussed PrEP with colleagues (p < 0.001), and comfort discussing sexual history and health issues (p < 0.001).

Barriers to the willingness to prescribe PrEP

Almost half (45%) of physicians reported being asked by their patients about PrEP, with PCPs being approached more frequently than ID specialists (p = 0.023) (Table 2). The most frequently cited barriers to prescribing PrEP included: the need to enhance their knowledge about PrEP (p < 0.001), absence of financial coverage for PrEP drugs (p < 0.001), practicing in a country with no governmental policy regulating PrEP use (p < 0.001), and insufficient time for providing risk-reduction counseling (p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study, one of the first to assess physicians’ knowledge, confidence, and willingness to prescribe PrEP in the MENA region, identified key differences between primary care physicians (PCPs) and infectious disease (ID) specialists.

By comparing PCPs to ID specialists, we found notable differences in their self-perceived knowledge, familiarity and confidence in prescription in relation to PrEP. In fact, a majority of PCPs (58.1%) reported being unfamiliar with PrEP, and 60.4% reported not being familiar with its prescription regimen, in stark contrast to only 11.1% and 7.7% of ID specialists, respectively. These findings are consistent with studies conducted in high-income countries, such as the U.S.27,28. Chaponda et al. (2025), in their systematic review, similarly noted that general practitioners in MENA countries often reported limited familiarity and confidence with PrEP, reflecting the disparities we observed between PCPs and ID physicians in our sample27.

Importantly, in our study, both PCPs and ID specialists reported a high level of willingness to prescribe PrEP if received proper education and improved their knowledge of the practice (69.6% and 79.3%, respectively), yet actual prescribing rates were lower among PCPs (21.7% vs. 58.6%). This mirrors Petroll et al.’s findings, where fewer PCPs than ID specialists had prescribed PrEP (17% vs. 64%) in the U.S28. A systematic review by Edelman et al. (2020) highlights factors that may explain why some providers hesitate to prescribe PrEP, even with adequate education. The review found that PCPs were most willing to prescribe PrEP to individuals in sero-discordant relationships (91%) but less willing to prescribe it to people who inject drugs (74%), possibly reflecting underlying stigma toward certain high-risk groups29. Additionally, Edelman et al. observed a positive relationship between the number of people with HIV in a provider’s care and the provider’s willingness to prescribe PrEP to people who inject drugs, suggesting that increased familiarity with people with HIV may help decrease biases and enhance comfort in prescribing PrEP to higher-risk individuals. Similarly, Sell et al. found that PCPs’ willingness to re-prescribe PrEP was influenced by their past experiences with the medication, emphasizing the role of familiarity in reducing hesitation and improving prescribing willingness28,30. It is worth noting that these trends appear consistent with findings from the MENA region as well. Studies conducted in Morocco and Lebanon similarly found that while physicians, particularly specialists, expressed willingness to prescribe PrEP, few had actually done so22,31. The gap between willingness and real-world prescribing was attributed to a lack of experience, inadequate training, and structural barriers such as the absence of clear guidelines and limited drug availability.

Although all surveyed physicians in this study are involved in sexual and reproductive health, our findings identify a significant gap regarding PrEP knowledge, especially among PCPs. This also aligns with our finding that PCPs are less likely to have received education on HIV management, or to have engaged in discussions, or attended conferences and meetings about PrEP. All of these are important opportunities for learning and staying informed about PrEP which PCPs have missed. These findings are in line with the results of Chaponda et al., which highlighted consistently low awareness and training on PrEP among healthcare providers across the MENA region. The review emphasized that PCPs in particular lack education on key PrEP-related competencies, such as eligibility criteria, side effects, and patient monitoring, especially in countries like Morocco and Turkey. These gaps emphasize the need for targeted professional development, both in undergraduate medical curricula and continuing education27. The role of infectious disease specialists, professional societies, and non-governmental organizations is particularly critical in expanding PrEP delivery in the MENA region. ID specialists, with their greater clinical experience in HIV prevention, are well-positioned to lead training, guideline development, and mentorship for PCPs29. In parallel, NGOs such as ALCS in Morocco have piloted awareness campaigns, provider education, and service delivery where national programs remain underdeveloped. As Chaponda et al. (2025) emphasize, these actors are key partners in building sustainable and regionally adapted PrEP strategies27.

Although stigma was not directly assessed in our survey, previous literature suggests it remains a major barrier to PrEP implementation in MENA6,7. Reports by UNAIDS and other regional assessments indicate that perceived or internalized stigma can discourage both patients and healthcare providers from engaging in HIV prevention practices, including PrEP12,28,32. This context is particularly relevant given the low participation rate in our study. While we did not establish this relationship empirically in our study, it remains an important contextual factor to consider when interpreting our findings and planning future interventions Training providers in culturally sensitive and non-judgmental care is essential to overcoming this barrier and improving outcomes. Chaponda et al. also recommend formalizing national guidelines, expanding prescriber education, and enhancing collaboration between ministries of health and NGOs to reduce such barriers27. As shown in our study, many physicians were unfamiliar with PrEP-related conferences, training sessions, or medical literature, which further limits their knowledge acquisition. Professional societies, regional conferences, and infectious disease specialists all have an important role to play in supporting broader medical education and knowledge-sharing across disciplines.

A significant finding of this study is the array of barriers physicians report facing in prescribing PrEP. Beyond their need for comfort, familiarity, and confidence levels with sexual health topics and HIV/PrEP, several additional obstacles were identified. Time constraints, the lack of financial coverage for the drugs, limited availability in the country, and the absence of supportive governmental policies were all highlighted as significant challenges. These barriers are consistent with those identified in Chaponda et al., which reported stigma, lack of national guidelines, insufficient governmental engagement, and limited access to medications as key structural challenges across the MENA region27. These findings align with existing literature, reinforcing the need for systemic changes to improve PrEP accessibility and adoption33–36. Even in wealthier MENA countries, funding for HIV prevention and treatment is insufficient, and HIV has not been prioritized in healthcare planning and spending37. The dedicated budget for HIV programming is less than 20% of what is needed to reach targets38. This lack of support from policymakers may be due to the perceived low prevalence of HIV, leading to a false sense of security despite the rise in transmission rates.

To address these challenges, it is vital to advocate for increased funding for HIV prevention and treatment, especially for PrEP. Partnering with international organizations such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and UNAIDS can be instrumental in securing the necessary funding and technical support for PrEP implementation. Governments must prioritize HIV prevention and treatment within their healthcare budgets, recognizing the long-term benefits of such investments. Clear governmental guidelines and policies can also encourage providers to shift from merely considering PrEP to actively prescribing it. This formal approach can help reduce stigma, as PrEP prescriptions will be seen as following national guidelines rather than being solely based on individual medical practice or patient preference.

Strengths and limitations

This study represents the first known assessment of PCPs’ familiarity and attitudes towards PrEP in the MENA region. The anonymity provided to participants enabled them to express their attitudes about PrEP freely.

Our study’s findings should be interpreted with caution due to several limitations. First, although the study aimed to assess physicians across the MENA region, 72% of respondents were based in Lebanon. This overrepresentation limits the generalizability of our findings to other MENA countries, where HIV-related policies, healthcare systems, and training pathways may differ substantially. As this was an exploratory study, no formal sample size calculation was conducted, and the sample was not designed to ensure representativeness across MENA countries. Future studies should aim for more balanced regional sampling, possibly through partnerships with national medical associations or licensing bodies. In addition, the use of a convenience sample and a response rate of approximately 23% (75 out of ~ 325 contacted) may have introduced self-selection bias. Physicians with greater interest or comfort in HIV prevention may have been more likely to respond, which could skew results toward more favorable attitudes or awareness. These factors further limit the extent to which our findings can be generalized to the broader physician population in the MENA region.

Second, the survey was distributed in English and French only, which may have excluded Arabic-only speakers and introduced a language-based participation bias. Translating future surveys into Arabic could increase inclusivity and response rates across the region.

Third, we did not collect data on the country where participants received their medical education. This limits our ability to assess the influence of different curricula or training systems on physicians’ knowledge of PrEP. Future research should include this variable to interpret education-related knowledge gaps better. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported responses introduces the possibility of social desirability bias, particularly regarding sensitive topics like HIV and sexual health. Participants may have overstated their willingness to prescribe PrEP or understated discomfort or stigma-related attitudes. While stigma was not directly measured in this survey, its potential influence must be acknowledged. In particular, we believe that stigma may have played a role in the low response rate, as some physicians may have felt uncomfortable participating in a study addressing HIV and PrEP. Future studies may benefit from more targeted recruitment strategies that ensure confidentiality and address these concerns. Finally, this study did not include multivariable analyses to adjust for potential confounding variables, which limits our ability to determine the independent effect of specific factors on outcomes such as willingness or confidence to prescribe PrEP.

These factors underscore the need to consider these constraints when evaluating our results.

Conclusion

This study offers a preliminary but important look into the familiarity, confidence, and prescribing behaviors of physicians toward PrEP in the MENA region. Despite the overrepresentation of Lebanese physicians and the use of a convenience sample, our findings reveal clear gaps in knowledge and training—particularly among primary care providers—that may hinder the wider uptake of PrEP across the region. Improving PrEP implementation will likely require a multifaceted approach that strengthens provider education, addresses systemic and policy-level barriers, and confronts persistent stigma in clinical settings. These efforts should engage a broad set of stakeholders, including medical schools, professional societies, ministries of health, and non-governmental organizations that have been central to HIV prevention efforts in countries such as Lebanon, Morocco, and Egypt.

While this study does not warrant immediate or prescriptive interventions, it highlights the need for regionally tailored strategies that are evidence-informed, inclusive, and adaptable. Further research with more representative samples is needed to build on these findings and guide the development of sustainable PrEP programs in the region.
