Academic and non-academic psychological determinants of nursing students’ achievement: structural equation modelling
Authors: Talal Ali Hussein Alqalah, Sameer Abdulmalik Alkubati, Gamil Ghaleb Alrubaiee, Habib Alrashedi, Abdulhafith Alharbi, Faris Mohammed Mahdi, Saleh Zaghir Mohammed Alhetar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNursing students often face challenges balancing personal pursuits with academic demands, which can hinder learning outcomes and impact psychological well-being. Previous studies have focused primarily on certain aspects of academic or non-academic factors influencing nursing student achievement; however, the combined and interactive effects of these factors remain underexplored.AimThis study aimed to explore the academic and non-academic psychological factors that influence nursing students’ achievement.MethodsAn analytical cross-sectional design was conducted using a stratified random sample of 421 nursing students at the College of Nursing. Data were collected through self-administered questionnaires. Pearson correlations and path analysis were used to explore the variables’ relationships.ResultsThe model demonstrated good fit: Chi-square (χ2) = 0.71.5, p < .55, NFI = 0.967, RFI = 0.945, CFI = 0.992, and RMSEA = 0.028. Academic motivation(β = 0.30), well-being (β = 0.19), social support (β = 0.17), task performance (β = 0.13), self-esteem (β = 0.06), and study hours (β = 0.06) were the strongest positive predictors of Grade Point Average (GPA). Conversely, academic anxiety (β=-0.10) and negative affect (β=-0.07) were significant negative predictors. The findings also reveal a pattern of associations between key psychological resources and a range of academic and personal outcomes. Specifically, academic motivation and social support are both positively associated with a broad spectrum of adaptive outcomes, including measures of well-being, life satisfaction, self-esteem, self-efficacy, positive emotions, resilience, and performance (task performance, study hours). Moreover, both constructs are inversely associated with maladaptive states, such as academic anxiety and negative affect.ConclusionThe path analysis model elucidates the mechanisms through which facilitating and hindering factors influence nursing students’ academic achievement, thereby promoting or impeding their success. Academic motivation and social support serve as crucial contributors to academic success by ameliorating the impact of academic and non-academic factors on nursing students. This study highlights the importance of multifaceted interventions in nursing education, emphasizing strategies that enhance student motivation and social support through mentorship and faculty-led workshops. Cultivating a supportive academic environment and incorporating interactive curricula, self-monitoring, and simulations are crucial for improving academic and non-academic outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04015-7.

Introduction

Nursing education is a challenging and demanding discipline that requires students to develop a unique set of knowledge, abilities, and professional attitudes [1]. Success in nursing is essential for both students and the healthcare system, as it ensures a continuous supply of skilled and proficient nursing practitioners [2].

University years are crucial for nursing students’ psychological and social development since they face a variety of challenges. These challenges include a complicated interaction of academic pressures, financial constraints, professional ambitions, and personal well-being, putting these future healthcare caregivers at the top of a worldwide crisis characterized by a severe nursing shortage. A comprehensive understanding of the factors impacting students’ views and academic outcomes during this important stage is crucial for developing a resilient nursing workforce capable of handling the healthcare demands of the future [3, 4].

Previous studies have revealed a wide range of factors that influence academic achievement [5, 6]. However, a thorough understanding of the intricate relationships among academic factors such as anxiety, self-efficacy, task performance, and resilience, as well as non-academic factors such as self-esteem, personality traits, affective states, life satisfaction, and overall well-being, as mediated by academic motivation and social support, in either amplifying or mitigating their effects on academic success among nursing students remains lacking.

Academic achievement, a crucial indicator of nursing student progress, results from a complex interplay of variables. Previous studies have explored the relationships between noncognitive factors (such as self-concept, motivation, social support, and test anxiety) and academic performance [7–9]. Additionally, research has shown associations between self-efficacy, resilience, anxiety, negative emotion, self-esteem, life satisfaction, well-being, and students’ academic performance [10–13].

Academic stress, exacerbated by environmental, academic, and personal challenges, significantly impacts college students, especially nursing students, who strive for greater academic achievement to deliver quality patient care. The interplay of factors such as academic pressure, self-esteem, and social support influences nursing students’ psychological well-being and academic performance [5]. While academic pressure remains a primary stressor, its effects are mitigated by self-efficacy, self-regulatory fatigue, academic motivation, and social support. Furthermore, the positive correlations among social support, life satisfaction, academic motivation, and engagement underscore the importance of a supportive environment in fostering student success. Ultimately, cultivating high-quality academic motivation is essential for nursing students’ success in their studies and future careers [14–17].

Figure 1 displays the hypothetical model is informed by complementary psychological theories that together explain how personal, behavioral, and contextual factors shape academic achievement. Social Cognitive Theory [18] highlights reciprocal interactions among self-efficacy, resilience, and study behaviors, while recognizing the buffering role of social support against anxiety. Extending this perspective, Self-Determination Theory [19] positions academic motivation as a central explanatory pathway, with well-being, self-esteem, and affect strengthening competence and autonomy, and social support fostering relatedness and intrinsic engagement. Insights from the Conservation of Resources theory [20] and the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping [21] further emphasizes the protective role of psychological resources in mitigating stress. Together, these perspectives justify the hypothesized links among the study variables [22, 23]. GPA functions bidirectionally, not solely reflecting achievement but also shaping self-efficacy, motivation, and well-being, thereby reinforcing or diminishing academic behaviors and psychological resources through iterative feedback processes. The model’s holistic approach, therefore, moves beyond a narrow focus on intellectual ability, offering a comprehensive and ecologically valid perspective on the determinants of academic achievement.

A thorough understanding of the complicated interplay between psychological factors such as academic factors (study hours, anxiety, self-efficacy, task performance, and resilience), non-academic factors (self-esteem, personality, positive/negative affect, life satisfaction, and well-being), and the role of academic motivation and social support in either mitigating or amplifying these influences on academic achievement among nursing students remains unclear. This study aims to address a critical gap in literature by establishing a robust empirical framework for evidence-based policies in nursing education. The research examines the complex interplay between academic and non-academic variables and their influence on nursing student outcomes. Findings from this investigation can inform the development and implementation of effective strategies by educational institutions, faculty, and support systems. Ultimately, this work seeks to cultivate an academic environment that not only promotes superior academic performance but also enhances student psychological well-being, thereby mitigating the adverse effects associated with the demanding nature of professional training.

Method

Study design

This analytical cross-sectional study employs path analysis, a specialized application of structural equation modeling (SEM). While SEM is a powerful tool for testing complex theoretical models, the cross-sectional nature of the data fundamentally precludes the inference of causality.

Setting and participants

This study recruited undergraduate nursing students in their later semesters (fourth to eighth) of the Bachelor of Science in Nursing program at the College of Nursing, University of Ha’il. Students experiencing academic difficulty requiring intervention, who were enrolled in earlier semesters (first and second) within the preparatory college. Additionally, any students in their third semester (their first in the nursing faculty) who had only completed a basic, non-specialized nursing course were also excluded from participation. Finally, individuals who did not provide informed consent were not included in the study.

Sample size and recruitment

To ensure an adequate sample size for robust statistical analysis, a multistage approach was implemented. First, a power analysis software program (EPI Info V7.2) was used to calculate the base sample size. This calculation considered a population of 1401 nursing students and targeted a desired level of precision (margin of error) of 5% at a 95% confidence level, using a conservative anticipated proportion of 50%. The initial sample size generated by the software was 302 students. Second, following established guidelines in path analysis research, the sample size was further adjusted to account for the recommended minimum number of participants per independent variable (10–20 cases per estimated parameter; given that this study has 14 estimation parameters, a sample size of 140–280 would be recommended) [24]. Finally, an additional buffer was incorporated to compensate for a potential nonresponse rate of 10%. This multistep process resulted in a final target sample size of 421 students.

Sampling method

A stratified random sampling methodology was employed to select participants from the nursing student population. The sampling frame was a comprehensive list of all undergraduate nursing students enrolled during the 2024–2025 academic year, sourced from the college registrar’s office and including students from both male and female sides. Nursing students were stratified sequentially, first by their designated nursing specialty departments: medical-surgical, maternity and pediatric health, community health, nursing administration, and psychiatric and mental health, and then by the specific courses in which they were enrolled. This dual stratification approach ensured a representative sample. Within each final stratum, a simple random selection was performed using a computer-generated random number sequence to achieve the predetermined sample size. This method ensured that each student had an equal probability of selection, thereby preserving the tenets of probability sampling and effectively mitigating selection bias.

Instrument

This study employed a multipart validated and reliable survey instrument (English version) to assess various factors potentially influencing nursing students’ academic achievement.

Part I: Demographic characteristics include age, sex, study level, marital status, monthly family income, sleep duration (hours per night), napping duration (minutes), and daily physical exercise duration.

Part II: Academic factors

Study hours: Time dedicated to reviewing and memorizing course material for tests and assignments (measured in hours).

Academic Anxiety: The Academic Anxiety Scale, developed by Cassady et al., is an 11-item instrument designed to assess student academic anxiety. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 “Not at all typical of me” to 4 “Very typical of me”. Higher total scores (ranging from 11 to 44) indicate greater levels of academic anxiety [25].

General Academic Self-Efficacy Scale (GASE): The GASE is a 5-item instrument designed to measure academic self-efficacy [26]. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 “strongly disagree” to 5 “strongly agree”. The total score ranges from 5 to 20, with a high score indicating greater academic self-efficacy.

Academic Task Performance is a 7-item instrument designed to measure student academic task performance; it consists of two subscales, 3 items in time management and 4 items in task efficiency, and is designed to measure student academic task performance on a 6-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 “Never” to 6 “Always” [27]. The total score ranges from 7 to 42, with a high score indicating greater academic task performance.

Academic Resilience Scale (ARS-6): The ARS-6 is a 6-item measure of student academic resilience scale [28]. Each item was rated on a 7-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 “Strongly Disagree” to 7 “Strongly Agree”. The total scores ranged from 7 to 42, with a higher score reflecting greater academic resilience.

Short Academic Motivation Scale (SAMS): The SAMS is a 14-item instrument designed for academic motivation; it consists of seven subscales, including “intrinsic motivation to know”, “intrinsic motivation toward accomplishment”, “intrinsic motivation to experience stimulation”, “identified regulation”, “introjected regulation”, “external regulation”, and “amotivation”. Each subscale has two items rated on a 7-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 “Does not correspond at all” to 7 “Corresponds exactly” [29]. Two items of Amotivation were reversed from 7 (“Does not correspond at all”) to 1 (“Corresponds exactly”). The total score ranges from 14 to 98, with a high score indicating greater academic motivation.

Part III: Non-academic factors

Personality: The short version of the Big Five Inventory-10 (BFI‐10) is a 10-item scale that is used to measure personality with five subscales: “Extraversion, 1 and 5”, “Agreeableness, 2 and 7”, “Conscientiousness 3 and 8”, “Neuroticism, 4 and 9”, and “Openness to Experience, 5 and 10” [30]. Each subscale had an item rating on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 “Disagree strongly” to 5 “Agree strongly”. The reverse items were “1, 7, 3, 4, and 5. The total score ranges from 10–50, with a high score indicating better personality.

Rosenberg Self-Esteem scale (RSE): The RES scale is a 10-item instrument used to measure both positive and negative feelings of students about themselves [30]. The students rated their responses on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 “Strongly agree” to 4 “Strongly disagree”, with reverse items 2, 5, 6, 8, and 9 ranging from 4 “Strongly agree” to 1 “Strongly disagree”. The total score ranges from 4 to 40, with higher scores indicating higher self-esteem.

Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS): The PANAS consists of two subscales [31]. The Positive Affect Scale emphasizes positive emotional states (e.g., enthusiasm, excitement, determination). It has 10 items, “1, 3, 5, 9, 10, 12, 14, 16, 17, and 19”, with scores ranging from 10 to 50, with a higher score indicating greater positive emotion. In contrast, the Negative Affect Scale captures the experience of unpleasant engagement, and subjective distress encompasses a variety of negative emotions, such as fear, nervousness, guilt, and shame. It has 10 items “2, 4, 6, 7, 8, 11, 13, 15, 18, and 20”, with scores ranging from 10 to 50, with a higher score indicating greater negative emotion (Watson et al., 1988). The students rated their responses on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 “very sightly” to 5 “extremely”.

Satisfaction with life scale: The Satisfaction with Life scale is a 5-item scale used to measure cognitive judgments of one’s life satisfaction globally [32]; each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 “Strongly disagree” to 7 “Strongly agree”. The total score ranges from 7 to 35, with higher scores indicating higher life satisfaction.

WHO-5 Well-being Index: The WHO-5 Well-being Index is a 5-item scale used to measure well-being [33]. Each item was rated on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 0 “At no time” to 5 “All of the time”, with scores ranging from 0 to 25; higher scores indicate greater well-being.

Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS): The MSPSS is a 12-item scale used to measure social support with three subscales: family support (items 3, 4, 8, and 11); friends (items 6, 7, 9, and 12); and significant other (items 1, 2, 5, and 10) [34]. The students responded to 12 items on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 “very strongly disagree” to 7 “very strongly agree”. The total scores range from 12 to 84, with higher scores indicating greater social support.

Academic achievement: Measured by self-reported student grade point average (GPA).

Instrument reliability

The internal consistency of the measurement instruments was robust, as indicated by the reliability coefficients (Cronbach’s alpha). The instruments demonstrated excellent to good reliability across all constructs. Specifically, the scales for academic motivation (α = 0.91), social support (α = 0.91), WHO-5 well-being (α = 0.90), and negative Affect (α = 0.92) exhibited excellent reliability. The instruments for positive affect (α = 0.88), academic anxiety (α = 0.87), and satisfaction with life (α = 0.85), academic task performance (α = 0.84), self-esteem (α = 0.84), personality (α = 0.82), academic resilience (α = 0.80), academic self-efficacy (α = 0.79) all demonstrated good reliability.

Procedure

Following the acquisition of ethical approval, a sample of students was strategically selected from various college departments. A systematic random sampling technique was employed. These students were comprehensively informed about the study’s objectives, methods, procedures, and data collection instruments. This detailed explanation was provided both verbally and in writing, ensuring transparency and participant comprehension prior to obtaining informed consent. Students were explicitly informed of the voluntary nature of their participation, along with guarantees of confidentiality and the right to withdraw from the study at any point. Researchers collected data via an anonymous, English-language questionnaire. The survey was designed for completion in approximately 10 to 15 min and distributed in a paper-based format. Upon retrieval, the questionnaires underwent a meticulous review process to identify any missing data. If inconsistencies were detected, the questionnaires were returned to the respective students for completion. The participants who were willing to complete the questionnaire provided informed consent to participate in the study. A total of 450 questionnaires were distributed to a stratified sample of students. Of these, 421 were returned, yielding a response rate of 93.5%. The data collection phase spanned a six-week period, commencing in April 2024 and ending in May 2024.

Statistical analysis

This study employed IBM SPSS Statistics 26 for descriptive analyses and Analysis of Moment Structures (AMOS) 24 for path analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the sociodemographic characteristics and study variables. Following normality and linearity assessments, t-tests and ANOVA were used to test differences in means. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were used to explore relationships between the dependent variable “GPA” and independent variables (academic and non-academic factors). Path analysis, with GPA modeled as the outcome variable. Academic motivation and social support are hypothesized to influence both academic and non-academic factors, ultimately impacting GPA. Various goodness-of-fit indices were evaluated to assess the overall model’s adequacy. Acceptable model fit was indicated by a nonsignificant chi-square value, a “Tucker‒Lewis Index” (TLI), a “Comparative Fit Index” (CFI), and an “Incremental Fit Index” (IFI) all exceeding 0.90, along with a “Root Mean Square Error of Approximation” (RMSEA) below 0.08 [35]. The goodness-of-fit index (GFI) and adjusted goodness-of-fit index (AGFI) range from 0 to 1.0, with 1.0 representing a perfect model fit. Scores can fall below 0 for exceptionally poorly fit models [36]. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance. All values were within acceptable thresholds (VIF < 5, tolerance >0.2) [37]. The hypothesized theoretical model (Fig. 1) was evaluated and subsequently refined to enhance its goodness-of-fit. Modification indices were systematically examined to identify potential unmodeled covariance relationships that, when incorporated, would significantly improve the model’s fit. This process was strictly guided by and limited to the model’s a priori theoretical underpinnings [36]. Accordingly, the model was respecified to include post-hoc covariance paths between the latent constructs of academic anxiety and academic self-efficacy, and between academic motivation and social support, which demonstrated statistical significance in the revised model (see supplementary file). Mardia’s coefficient of multivariate kurtosis was 1.73, with a critical ratio of 7.35, which suggests non-normality. The direct, indirect, and total effects were evaluated using bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap confidence intervals derived from 5,000 resamples. Statistical significance was determined at the p < .05 level; effects were considered statistically significant if their 95% confidence intervals did not contain zero.

Ethics consideration

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the University of Hail Research Ethics Committee (H-2024-398). The participants provided informed consent through the completion of the self-report questionnaire, which was accompanied by an information sheet. To mitigate potential power imbalances between researchers and participants, no personally identifiable information was collected. The study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki [38].

Results

Concerning socioeconomic status, 41.8% of the students reported a family income between 5000 and 10,000 SAR. A small proportion of the students were married (5.5%). Personal habits revealed that slightly more than half of the students slept less than six hours per night, and 63.4% napped for more than 30 min. In terms of physical activity, 42.8% and 41.3% of nursing students engaged in less than and more than 60 min of exercise, respectively. Importantly, none of these variables were significantly associated with student GPA.

Correlation matrix

Descriptive statistics were computed for the primary independent and dependent variables. Nursing students presented higher mean scores for academic motivation, academic resilience, life satisfaction, social support, academic task performance, positive affect, negative affect, personality, and GPA (5.63, 5.25, 4.61, 4.54, 4.3, 3.85, 3.52, 3.36, and 3.27, respectively). Conversely, self-esteem, academic self-efficacy, study hours, and well-being were characterized by moderate mean scores (2.78, 2.69, 2.64, and 2.51, respectively). Academic anxiety among the nursing students was low (M = 1.71, SD = 0.52). Of the academic factors, academic motivation (r = .734, p < .001) and task performance (r = .584, p < .001) were the most highly correlated with GPA. These strong positive associations suggest that higher levels of motivation and task-specific performance are closely linked to academic achievement. Conversely, academic anxiety was moderately and negatively correlated with GPA (r = − .500, p < .001), indicating that higher anxiety is associated with lower academic achievement. Among the non-academic factors, well-being (r = .747, p < .001) and social support (r = .604, p < .001) exhibited the strongest positive correlations with GPA. Life satisfaction also showed a strong positive correlation (r = .596, p < .001). These results suggest that a nursing student’s personal well-being and social environment are substantial correlates of their academic success. The intercorrelations among the predictor variables also warrant attention. Notably, there is a strong correlation between academic motivation and well-being (r = .762, p < .001). Similarly, life satisfaction and social support (r = .569, p < .001) indicate that a student’s satisfaction with their life may be intertwined with the social support they receive. Additionally, a moderate correlation between life satisfaction and academic motivation (r = .538) and between well-being and task performance (r = .494, p < .001) (details in supplementary file).

Structural equation model

The hypothesized model demonstrated good fit: Chi-square (χ2) = 71.52, p = .055, df = 54, CMIN/DF = 1.324, AGFI = 0.950, NFI = 0.967, RFI = 0.945, IFI, 0.992, TLI = 0.986, CFI = 0.992, and RMSEA = 0.028. Several variables were found to be significant positive predictors of GPA: academic motivation (β = 0.30, p < .001), well-being (β = 0.19 p < .001), social support (β = 0.17, p < .001), academic task performance (β = 0.13, p = .009), self-esteem (β = 0.06, p = .043), and study hours (β = 0.06, p = .030). The negative predictors included academic anxiety (β= − 0.10, p = .001) and negative affect (β= − 0.07, p = .009). Academic motivation demonstrated a significantly positive direct effect on well-being (β = 0.67, p < .001), life satisfaction (0.39, p < .001), academic task performance (β = 0.38, p < .001), positive affect (β = 0.31, p < .001), academic self-efficacy (β = 0.29, p < .001), self-esteem (β = 0.27, p < .001), study hours (β = 0.25, p < .001), academic resilience (β = 0.20, p < .001), and personality (β = 0.15, p = 013). In contrast, it had a negative direct effect on academic anxiety (β= − 0.29, p < .001) and negative affect (β= − 0.10, p = .035). Social support had a significantly positive direct effect on life satisfaction (β = 0.13, p < .001), academic resilience (β = 0.37, p < .001), positive affect (β = 0.34, p < .001), academic task performance (β = 0.33, p < .001), wellbeing (β = 0.29, p < .001), academic self-efficacy (β = 0.22, p < .001), study hours (β = 0.19, p < .001), and self-esteem (β = 0.13, p = .019). It also demonstrated a negative direct effect on anxiety (β= − 0.34, p < .001) and negative affect (β= − 0.27, p < .001). The structural equation model is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Discussion

This study offers a novel perspective on the multifaceted determinants of nursing students’ success by examining the intricate interplay between academic and non-academic factors. The study findings highlight that academic motivation and social support are not merely associated with academic achievement but appear to operate through a complex network of psychological and behavioral variables. This analysis, employing structural equation modeling, offers a nuanced understanding of these relationships, which extends beyond simple bivariate associations.

The findings of this study, as demonstrated by path analysis, have substantial implications for nursing education. The findings indicate that a higher academic achievement of nursing students is associated with several key factors, including dedicated study time, effective academic task performance, heightened self-esteem, well-being, and a positive emotional state. Conversely, academic anxiety and negative emotions were identified as significant negative predictors of achievement. These findings suggest that intrinsic and extrinsic drivers of behavior are central to both academic performance and a student’s overall well-being. Students who put in focused effort and perform well on assignments are more likely to grasp the material and translate that knowledge into a higher GPA. Additionally, a strong sense of self-worth and a positive outlook can be powerful motivators. Students who believe in themselves are more likely to persevere through challenges and approach learning with confidence. Positive emotions can also enhance focus and concentration, leading to better information retention. Well-being encompasses physical and mental health. Feeling rested, managing stress effectively, and having a good support system all contribute to a student’s ability to concentrate, learn, and perform well. Conversely, the study also suggested that excessive anxiety and negative emotions can hinder focus, concentration, and performance on exams and assignments. It can be debilitated by motivation and social support. This phenomenon is most effectively interpreted through the lens of the Broaden-and-Build theory of positive emotions [39]. This theory posits that positive emotions, which can arise from the intrinsic satisfaction of learning and goal attainment, do more than just signal well-being; they broaden an individual’s thought-action repertoire, leading to the development of enduring personal resources. In this context, the drive for academic mastery may lead to an increase in positive emotions and self-esteem, which, in turn, can foster resilience and a more adaptive approach to academic challenges. Our findings therefore suggest a virtuous cycle in which academic pursuits not only lead to scholastic success but also cultivate a robust foundation of psychological resources.

Study findings corroborate and extend existing research by providing a more integrated, systems-level perspective. Existing literature establishes clear links between family distress, emotional negativity, and self-esteem, and poor students’ academic performance [5]. Furthermore, research indicates a positive relationship between higher self-concept and greater academic success, with intrinsic motivation mitigating the negative impact of anxiety on academic performance [7]. These findings suggest that motivated students are better equipped to manage anxiety and achieve academic goals. This is supported by positive associations between academic motivation and GPA [10, 11]. Similarly, social support is a known predictor of academic success [40], a finding corroborated by Perveen et al., who reported significant positive associations between academic performance, life satisfaction, and self-esteem [12]. Additionally, self-efficacy significantly predicts learning engagement [41] and influences their learning-related emotions and metacognitive learning strategies, which subsequently impact academic performance [42], suggesting that a student’s belief in their capabilities is a central pathway through which motivation influences success. Conversely, anxiety has been associated with lower GPAs [43]. Therefore, fostering both extrinsic and intrinsic motivation is critical for enhancing student engagement and performance. However, amotivation presents a distinct challenge that is often associated with depressive symptoms. It is imperative to mitigate amotivation to optimize both academic performance and overall student well-being [44, 45].

This study delineates a cascading motivational model, elucidating the mechanistic pathways through which academic motivation influences both academic and personal domains. The findings posit that students characterized by intrinsic goal orientation and a mastery approach to learning invest greater time and effort in academic pursuits (e.g., dedicated study hours). This enhanced engagement, in turn, serves as a primary antecedent to the development of robust academic self-efficacy. This fortified sense of competence functions as a critical psychological resource, promoting adaptive resilience when confronting academic challenges and facilitating stronger task performance. Consequently, this trajectory culminates in elevated academic achievement, representing a core objective of effective educational outcomes. Notably, the positive effects extend beyond the academic sphere. The findings provide evidence for a beneficial spillover, whereby heightened academic motivation is associated with a generalized enhancement of well-being. Specifically, a significant association with increased self-esteem, greater frequency of positive affect, higher life satisfaction, and improved overall psychological functioning. This phenomenon may be attributed to the self-deterministic nature of intrinsic motivation, which potentially mitigates the experience of academic anxiety and negative emotional states by aligning behavior with core interests and values. This phenomenon can be attributed to the intrinsic drive that fuels academic passion, potentially leading to a reduced pressure cooker of academic anxiety and negative emotions. Intriguingly, the study suggests a reciprocal relationship: lower anxiety and negative affect, coupled with a heightened sense of well-being, further bolster academic achievement. These cyclical dynamics underscore the potential of fostering academic motivation as a multifaceted intervention to not only propel students toward academic success but also cultivate a more positive and flourishing personal landscape.

Our findings align with previous research, highlighting the pivotal role of self-esteem, life satisfaction, resilience, emotional regulation, well-being, and academic motivation in shaping academic achievement. This study corroborates several key relationships established in the literature. For instance, Chen et al. [6]. reported that academic motivation partially mediates the link between life satisfaction and academic achievement. Similarly, Bulfone et al. [46] found a direct influence of self-efficacy on academic success, while Sharififard et al. [47] identified internal motivation, amotivation, and family work management as key predictors of academic burnout. Furthermore, our findings corroborate those of Yun et al. [48], who demonstrated the substantial impact of academic motivation on resilience. This aligns with a broader body of research that highlights the interconnections between psychological factors and academic outcomes, including studies by Perveen et al. [12] and Kwek et al. [49], who found significant positive correlations and predictive relationships between academic performance, life satisfaction, self-esteem, and resilience. This is also supported by findings on the significant association between psychological empowerment, resilience, and spiritual well-being with academic success [50], and the established correlation between academic achievement and subjective well-being [51]. Self-determination theory [13] posits that motivation is fueled by three psychological needs: competence, autonomy, and relatedness. When fulfilled through academic pursuits, these needs can lead to increased study time, higher self-efficacy (the belief in one’s ability to succeed), and positive emotions like self-esteem. This process, in turn, fosters academic resilience and overall well-being, ultimately impacting a student’s GPA. Additionally, the practice of setting and working toward academic goals can enhance motivation and focus learning efforts [52]. This goal pursuit, in turn, leads to increased study time, self-efficacy, and improved task performance.

This study underscores the pivotal role that social support plays in composing a student’s academic experience. The findings indicate that support networks, including family, friends, and significant others, serve as a critical and powerful resource that significantly enhances various academic metrics. Specifically, we observed a direct and positive correlation between social support and increased study hours, heightened academic self-efficacy, and superior task performance. This fortified foundation demonstrably contributes to enhanced academic resilience, a student’s capacity to navigate and overcome the challenges inherent in academic life. The positive effects of this support extend beyond academic achievement, fostering a more robust sense of self-esteem, cultivating positive affect, and ultimately contributing to greater life satisfaction and overall well-being. Beyond its role in promoting flourishing, social support also functions as a powerful mitigating factor against academic adversity. Our data indicates a significant reduction in academic anxiety and negative emotions among supported students, suggesting a buffering effect that effectively diminishes the debilitating influence of academic pressure. This finding aligns with previous research, which has documented a significant negative correlation between perceived social support and academic stress among student populations [17].

Our findings corroborate existing literature by establishing a robust positive relationship between academic self-efficacy and social support [16]. This result reinforces the conceptualization that social support is instrumental in fostering resilience and in facilitating peer-driven learning initiatives under the guidance of faculty and educators. Consistent with the study of Warshawski [53], we found that resilience and social support collectively contribute to the enhancement of academic self-efficacy. Moreover, social support is a significant and positive predictor of academic engagement. This relationship is mediated by both life satisfaction and academic motivation. Specifically, social support exerts both a direct and indirect influence on academic engagement via its positive impact on these two psychological constructs [14].

Limitations

This study offers a valuable snapshot of the intricate relationships between various academic and non-academic factors and their influence on academic achievement among nursing students, and role of academic motivation and social support. However, it is imperative to acknowledge the limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships, suggesting a need for future longitudinal studies to delineate causal pathways. The reliance on self-reported GPA introduces potential bias; thus, subsequent research should incorporate verified academic records to enhance validity. Furthermore, the findings’ generalizability is constrained by the use of a single-university sample. To overcome this, future multisite studies with larger, more diverse cohorts are warranted. Additionally, we advocate for exploring the nuanced role of cultural factors in shaping academic motivation and the perception of social support.

Conclusion

This study provides a nuanced understanding of the multifaceted determinants of academic achievement among nursing students, underscoring the dynamic interplay between academic and non-academic factors. Study findings reveal that academic achievement is not merely a product of innate ability but is significantly shaped by both intrinsic student attributes and extrinsic support systems. Specifically, academic motivation, task performance, and dedicated study hours emerged as significant positive predictors of achievement. Conversely, academic anxiety and negative affect were identified as substantial impediments. Beyond these academic constructs, the study highlights the critical and often overlooked role of non-academic factors, including well-being, self-esteem, and social support. Robust academic motivation and social support not only function as protective resources, buffering the negative effects of academic anxiety and negative affect, but also act as catalysts, directly enhancing study hours, academic task performance, resilience, and self-efficacy. This positive influence extends to non-academic domains, fostering improved well-being, life satisfaction, personality, self-esteem, and positive affect.

Implications for the academic achievement of nursing

This research highlights the importance of a holistic approach to pedagogical interventions in nursing education, emphasizing academic and non-academic factors. Findings suggest implementing strategies like formal mentorship programs and motivational interviewing workshops to enhance motivation and social support. Cultivating a supportive academic environment through improved faculty-student communication is also crucial. Future studies should evaluate the efficacy of these interventions across diverse educational settings, focusing on multifaceted strategies such as problem-solving, deep learning, and simulation-based learning to improve self-efficacy, resilience, well-being, and life satisfaction.
