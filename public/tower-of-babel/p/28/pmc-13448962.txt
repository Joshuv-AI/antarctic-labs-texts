Application of the Synergistic Effect of Coal Slime and Silica Calcium Slag in Desertified Soil Amendment
Authors: Ruixin An, Boda Zhang, Jiaxin Liu, Quanquan Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To expand the resource utilization pathways of coal industry byproducts and explore the improvement approaches of desertified soil, this study investigated the effects of the synergistic application of coal slime and silica calcium slag on the physical and chemical properties of desertified soil and wheat growth. The results demonstrated that the synergistic application of coal slime and silica calcium slag significantly reduced soil particle density and bulk density while increasing soil moisture content. Concurrently, the contents of soil organic matter, nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium were all elevated. The promotional effects on the emergence ratio and seedling length were more obvious in the low content group (<15%) and the relatively high content group (>35%). Across all application rates, the emergence ratio and seedling length were relatively stable when the ratio of coal slime to silica calcium slag was 1:1. A comparative analysis of soil chemical properties before and after wheat cultivation revealed that the consumption of organic matter and nitrogen was more significant during the early growth stage of wheat. In conclusion, the synergistic addition of coal slime and silica calcium slag can significantly improve the desertified soil environment and increase the emergence ratio and seedling length of wheat.

Introduction

Soil desertification (also termed dryland degradation) refers to land degradation in arid, semiarid, and dry subhumid areas resulting from multiple interacting factors, including climatic variations and human activities. Its typical characteristics include a significant reduction in vegetation coverage, depletion of soil organic matter, deterioration of soil structure (e.g., increased bulk density and decreased porosity), diminished water retention capacity, and losses of soil carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus pools. ,

41% of the Earth’s land surface consists of drylands, and approximately 25% of these arid areas are at risk of desertification. , Globally, desertification has inflicted economic losses exceeding 40 billion US dollars. China is among the countries most severely affected by desertification in the world. Research shows that potential desert-prone areas account for approximately 50% of China’s total land area. Desertified land in China is highly concentrated in five provinces/autonomous regionsXinjiang, Inner Mongolia, Tibet, Gansu, and Qinghaiwhose combined desertified area makes up 95.48% of the national total. Based on field experiments conducted in Inner Mongolia, Zhao Hailin found that compared with nondesertified land, the fine particle content in the soil of severely desertified areas decreased by up to 89.2%, while the sand content increased by 47.2%. In addition, soil clay, organic matter, total nitrogen, total phosphorus, available nitrogen, and available phosphorus all decreased to varying degrees. Soil erosion also caused its pH value to increase from 8.66 to 8.92. In recent years, China has made remarkable achievements in desertification control, but it still faces the challenges of expanding the scale of comprehensive management and strengthening in-depth research.

At present, there are numerous methods for improving desertified soil. Jiyue et al. applied mixed nanoparticles (a composite of carb-based, Al2O3, SiO2, and TiO2) to improve rocky desertified soil and cultivated okra. The research showed that the treatment of nanoparticles could dramatically improve the growth, photosynthetic characteristics, chlorophyll content, and antioxidant enzyme activity of okra. Xiuwen et al. evaluated the effects of NPK compound fertilizer, slow-release fertilizer and bio-organic fertilizer in improving the soil characteristics of rocky desertification and encouraging the growth of medicinal plants (Mahonia fortune). The results showed that bio-organic fertilizer could dramatically increase the contents of soil organic matter, total nitrogen and phosphorus, and enhance enzyme activity and microbial diversity. Slow-release fertilizers are the most beneficial for plant growth. Among the tested plant species, boxwood exhibited the most significant increases in height and ground diameter; notably, it also significantly enhanced the availability of soil trace elements such as copper (Cu). Yu et al. ’s research indicates that coal slime, as a soil conditioner for sandy soil, can improve the physical and chemical properties of the soil, increase organic matter and nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium in the soil, enhance soil porosity and cation exchange capacity, and improve soil stability and water and nutrient retention capacity.

Coal slime (CS) is a byproduct of coal washing and processing,which is characterized by high ash content, low calorific value, elevated moisture content, and small particle size, , thus limt its application in combustion. High moisture and ash contents render coal slime highly viscous, posing challenges to transportation, loading, and unloading, which restricts its large scale utilization. Therefore, it is imperative to explore more efficient coal slime treatment solutions to achieve effective resource utilization and mitigate the negative environmental impacts. In terms of the physicochemical properties of coal slime, its key features include a high proportion of fine particles, excellent water retention capacity, and substantial organic matter content, , along with rich essential trace elements like nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). It ameliorates the inferior physicochemical properties of desertified soil, such as poor water-holding and nutrient-retention capacity, thus augmenting soil fertility and tillage performance and facilitating the sustainable and healthy growth of crops.

Silica calcium slag (SCS) is a residue generated during the alumina extraction from high-alumina fly ash via the alkali-lime sintering process. It possesses a porous structure and is rich in mineral elements such as calcium (Ca) and silicon (Si). Currently, SCS has been extensively utilized in the production of glazed tiles, ceramics, glass, and cement, as well as in flue gas desulfurization applications. , However, its porous structure facilitates the formation of a loose soil matrix conducive to root proliferation, thereby promoting plant uptake of calcium (Ca) and silicon (Si). This not only enhances plant resistance to biotic stress (e.g., pathogens and insect pests) and abiotic stress (e.g., drought, heavy metal toxicity, and cold injury) but also endows it with distinct advantages in soil amendment. Zhenzhong et al. demonstrated that blending coal-based solid waste (silica-calcium slag, fly ash, and desulfurization gypsum) with cow dung at a 40% ratio and applying it to desertified soil significantly reduced soil pH and bulk density, optimized soil porosity, and improved comprehensive soil fertility. , These characteristics collectively render it a promising material for remediating desertified soil.

Although several researchers have separately applied coal slime or silica calcium slag to soil improvement, both materials require additional organic fertilizers as auxiliary additives. There are limited reports on the synergistic effects of the two materials on soil improvement. Therefore, this study takes the emergence and growth of wheat as an indicator to investigate the improvement effect of the synergistic application of coal slime and silica calcium slag on desertified soil.

Experimental Design

Potted wheat planting experiment was used to measure the improvement effect of desertified soil. 0.5-gallon pots with a diameter of 12 cm and a height of 13 cm were used in this research. The total amount of coal slime and silica calcium slag was 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, 25%, 30%, 35%, 40% (mass fraction). At the same time, under the same total addition amount, the ratio of coal slime and silica calcium slag used were 3:1, 1:1, and 1:3, respectively (all the ratios in the following are coal slime: silicon calcium slag denoted as M-G), and the three different ratios were recorded as M-G 3:1, M-G 1:1, and M-G 1:3, respectively. Three repeated tests were scheduled for each test group.

The treated coal slime and silica calcium slag are fully mixed with desertified soil. The absolute mass of 1000 g of soil sample is put into a flowerpot. About 250 mL water is added to the pot at one time in order to make the soil fully soaked and the pH is set to 6.5 to 7 with phosphoric acid. Eight wheat seeds were soaked for 24 h and were evenly placed in each pot, and the daily seed emergence ratio and seedling height were recorded. During planting, water was inserted as needed, and the position of the pot was changed every 2–3 days to avoid the impact of light.

Test Method

Soil density: Dry density method: Air-dried soil samples were compacted following a standardized procedure and then filled into a graduated cylinder. The stable packed volume and sample mass were measured to calculate density Soil moisture content: Drying method.

Soil bulk density: Ring knife method: Collect undisturbed soil using a fixed-volume cutting ring to ensure the ring is fully filled with soil. After weighing the sample, calculate the mass of oven-dried soil per unit volume, namely the soil bulk density Soil pH: Potentiometric method.

Soil organic matter content: High-temperature incineration weight loss method: Soil samples were weighed and placed in a muffle furnace, ignited at 380 °C for 6 h, cooled naturally, and weighed again to calculate the organic matter content.

Soil potassium content determination: ammonium acetate extractionflame spectrophotometry: Accurately weigh 5.00 g of air-dried soil samples passing through a 1 mm sieve into a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask, add 50 mL of 1 mol·L–1 neutral NH4OAc solution, and conduct sealed shaking for 30 min. The solution is filtered under normal pressure, and the filtrate is collected in a small Erlenmeyer flask. The absorbance of the filtrate and potassium standard solutions is determined by a flame spectrophotometer, and the concentration of available potassium is calculated based on the standard curve.

Soil phosphorus content determination: Sodium bicarbonate extraction- molybdenum–antimony anticolorimetric method: Accurately weigh 2.50 g of air-dried soil sample sieved through a 2 mm sieve and place it in a 200 mL polyethylene centrifuge bottle, followed by the addition of 50.00 mL sodium bicarbonate extractant. The mixture is shaken at a constant temperature of 25.0 °C for 30 min and filtered immediately after shaking. Transfer 10.0 mL of the filtrate into a 50 mL volumetric flask, add 5.0 mL of molybdenum–antimony-ascorbic acid chromogenic reagent, and mix thoroughly to eliminate CO2 Color development and absorbance determination are conducted under the same conditions as the standard curve, and the available phosphorus content is calculated based on the standard curve.

Soil nitrogen content determination: Kjeldahl nitrogen determination method: Accurately weigh 1.00 g of soil sample, 3.00 g of K2SO4 and 0.20 g of CuSO4, and transfer all reagents into a digestion tube. Slowly add 10 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid. Secure the digestion tube in a graphite digester, and heat the sample to 420 °C via a three-stage temperature rising program for 100 min of digestion. After the digestion process, cool the digestion tube naturally to room temperature. Finally, the nitrogen content was determined using the Kjeldahl apparatus.

Analysis of soil metal elements (XRF method):

The samples were ground and crushed to a particle size finer than 200 mesh, and pressed into pellets under 30 MPa. The prepared pellets were placed into a ZSX Primus IV spectrometer. When X-rays irradiated the samples, internal atoms were excited to emit characteristic X-ray fluorescence. These fluorescence signals were captured by the detector and converted into electrical signals, enabling the qualitative and quantitative analysis of elements. The oxides and their corresponding contents presented in the XRF results are the converted mass fractions of individual elements.

Results and Discussion

Main Metal Elements and Relative Content of the Sample

The metal element analysis of samples in this paper needs to integrate data from Tables and . It can be seen from Table that coal slime possesses high levels of moisture, organic carbon and nitrogen, whereas desertified soil shows relatively low contents of these substances. Taking per kilogram of sample as the research unit, the relative contents of metal elements were analyzed after excluding moisture, organic carbon and nitrogen. Taking aluminum (Al) as an example, the Al content in coal slime is about 14%, while that in desertified soil is approximately 12%. Meanwhile, this result does not account for the estimation errors derived from other undetected substances containing C, H, O and N. In conclusion, the addition of coal slime will not induce severe aluminum toxicity in desertified soil.

With respect to the potential phytotoxicity of aluminum, relevant literature has documented that aluminum toxicity only occurs under conditions of low phosphorus, low pH, and low calcium, whereas chelated aluminum exhibits no phytotoxicity. Another study demonstrated that the contents of exchangeable aluminum and soluble aluminum increase with declining pH, and high levels of soil organic matter can enhance the soil’s acid buffering capacity and suppress the mobilization and release of aluminum in soil.

In this study, the desertified soil is slightly alkaline, and silica calcium slag is alkaline and rich in calcium and phosphorus, which can regulate soil pH and mitigate potential phytotoxicity. Furthermore, coal slime contains a substantial amount of organic matter, which can also effectively inhibit the activation and release of soil aluminum.

Physical Properties of the Test Soil

All soil samples for physicochemical property analysis were derived from desertified soil solely amended with coal slime and silica calcium slag in varying proportions.

Figure illustrates the relationship between various physical properties of the test soil and the addition amounts of coal slime and silica calcium slag. As shown in Figure a, the density of the test soil exhibits a decreasing trend with the increase of aggregate addition. When the addition amount was the same, the density of the M-G1:3 group was lower, indicating that silica calcium slag plays a more significant role in reducing soil density. As presented in Figure b, the moisture content of the test soil shows an upward trend with the increase of aggregate addition. Under the same addition amount, soil samples with a higher proportion of coal slime had a higher moisture content, which reflects that coal slime has a greater effect on increasing soil moisture content.

Effects of different addition amounts of coal slime and silica calcium slag on the physical properties (a) density, (b) moisture content, (c) bulk density, and (d) pH of desertified soil

As shown in Figure c, with the increase of the aggregate addition amount, the bulk density of the test soil all showed a downward tendency. Under the parallel addition amount, the bulk density of M-G 3:1 was consistently higher than those of M-G 1:3 and M-G 1:1. This indicates that an increase in the proportion of silica calcium slag plays a major role in reducing soil bulk density, with the maximum decrease reaching 19.9%. It can be recognized from Figure d that with the increase of the aggregate addition amount, the pH values of M-G1:3 and M-G1:1 gradually increase, while the pH of M-G3:1 slightly decreases. This indicates that an increase in the proportion of coal slime contributes to the reduction of soil pH. This is because coal slime is inherently acidic, while silicon–calcium slag is alkaline. Adjusting the ratio of the two materials can effectively regulate soil pH.

In summary, coal slime and silica calcium slag play a complementary role in changing the physical properties of desertified soil. Coal slime can increase soil moisture content and lower soil pH, while silica calcium slag helps to decrease the density and bulk density of the soil.The synergistic addition of coal slime and silica calcium slag can improve the physical properties of desertified soil.

Wheat Growth

In the pure desertified soil blank control planting experiment of this article, a total of three pots and 24 seeds were planted. The seed emergence ratio was 0. Therefore, the blank control group does not appear in the subsequent result analysis of emergence ratio and seedling length. Accordingly, the results merely illustrate the relative performance of desertified soil improved by varying proportions of coal slime and silica calcium slag, instead of absolute growth gains.

Wheat Emergence Ratio

Figure shows the changes in the emergence ratio of wheat in separate groups with distinct addition amounts within 30 days under the M-G1:3 ratio. As can be seen from the figure, when the aggregate addition amount is 15%, the emergence ratio is the highest, reaching 87.5%. The 40% addition amount group ranked second with a germination rate of 75%. Overall, in the early stage of planting (1–4 days), the low addition group (≤15%) showed a faster germination rate. After 5 days, the emergence ratio of the 40% content group rose rapidly. This might be attributed to the higher coal slime content providing more abundant nutrient support for the subsequent growth of seeds.The proportion of silica calcium slag in M-G1:3 is relatively high. This might be explained by the porous structure of calcium silicate residue, which makes the soil in the low addition group have better air permeability and the seeds are easier to germinate. The growth of seeds requires stable organic matter and nitrogen sources. Therefore, the 40% group has an advantage in the later germination of seeds with a higher coal slime content.

Emergence ratio of M-G 1:3.

Figure shows the changes in the emergence ratio of wheat in distinctive groups with unusual addition amounts within 30 days under the M-G1:1 ratio. It can be concluded that the emergence ratio of the 40% group was the highest, reaching 87.5%. The 15% group, followed by 79.2%. Based on the analysis of emergence ratio, physical properties and nutritional components of coal slime and silica calcium slag, it is speculated that the germination of seeds may mainly be influenced by two factors: one is the structure of the soil, such as its aeration, and the other is the nutritional components in the soil. Therefore, the lower content group (≤15%) and the higher content group (40%) have an advantage in terms of emergence ratio.

Emergence ratio of M-G 1:1.

This further confirms our conjecture that the emergence ratio of wheat seeds is mainly influenced by nutrients (organic matter and nitrogen) and soil structure.

Conclusion

Based on the experimental research on the synergistic improvement of desertified soil by coal slime and silica calcium slag, we have drawn the following conclusions:1.The synergistic application of coal slime and calcium silicate slag markedly improves the physicochemical properties of desertified soils. Increases in soil organic matter and nitrogen contents are primarily attributable to coal slime, whereas elevations in available potassium and phosphorus contents are driven by calcium silicate slag.2.The synergistic addition of coal slime and silica calcium slag can substantially increase the emergence ratio and growth height of wheat. As no wheat germination was observed in the blank group of pure desertified soil, the absolute growth improvement effect on wheat cannot be validated herein. Regardless of the coal slimesilica calcium slag ratio (M-G), the emergence ratio of wheat seeds was higher in both the lower content group (≤15%) and the higher content group (40%). By comparing the planting groups with all contents and proportions, it can be found that when M-G1:1, the seed emergence ratio is the most stable, all exceeding 50%.3.By comparing the low addition efficiency groups, it was observed that when the absolute addition amount of coal slime is about 37.5–50 g, the growth of wheat seedlings in the low addition group can also reach a better state.
