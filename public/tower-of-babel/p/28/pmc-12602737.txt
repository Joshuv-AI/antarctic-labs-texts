Superior Effectiveness and Estimated Public Health Impact of Cell- Versus Egg-Based Influenza Vaccines in Children and Adults During the US 2023–2024 Season
Authors: Alicia N. Stein, Anusorn Thanataveerat, Kimberly McDermott, Alex Dean, Stephanie Wall, Cory Pack, Ian McGovern, Sheena G. Sullivan, Mendel Haag
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction The aim of this study was to assess the relative vaccine effectiveness (rVE) of cell-based versus egg-based quadrivalent influenza vaccines (QIVc versus QIVe) in preventing test-confirmed influenza during the 2023–2024 US influenza season.MethodsrVE was estimated using a test-negative design applied to a large, linked, real-world dataset. QIVc or QIVe recipients aged 6 months–64 years who were tested for influenza within ± 7 days of an acute respiratory or febrile illness were included. rVE was estimated using doubly robust logistic regression. Analyses were performed for the full, pediatric, adult, outpatient and high-risk populations and by influenza type. Public health impact was assessed using a compartmental influenza burden averted model.ResultsThe analysis included 2119 QIVc-cases, 14,750 QIVc-controls, 14,559 QIVe-cases, and 75,351 QIVe-controls. QIVc was superior to QIVe in preventing test-confirmed influenza with an rVE of 19.8% (95% CI 15.7–23.8%) in the full population, and with rVEs of 19.6% (13.6–25.3%) in the pediatric population aged 6 months–17 years and 18.5% (12.1–24.5%) in adults aged 18–64 years. Consistent results were observed for all sensitivity and subgroup analyses against any influenza. If all vaccinated individuals aged 6 months–64 years in the US received QIVc over QIVe, an estimated 2,379,395 additional symptomatic illnesses would have been prevented, with proportionate reductions in related complications.ConclusionsOur analysis showed superior effectiveness of QIVc over QIVe in preventing test-confirmed influenza among persons aged 6 months–64 years, and provided the first demonstration of superiority in pediatric populations from 6 months of age.A Graphical Abstract is availible for this article.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-025-01230-2.

Introduction

Vaccination against influenza is the most effective way to prevent infection and reduce the risk of complications from influenza [1]. Most influenza vaccines are manufactured by propagating candidate vaccine viruses (CVV) in fertilized hens’ eggs [2, 3]. However, CVVs may acquire mutations within the hemagglutinin (HA) protein that allow more efficient growth in eggs [4, 5] but can alter the antigenicity of the vaccine virus and affect vaccine effectiveness (VE) [6–13]. Moreover, one study has suggested that childhood vaccination with an egg-adapted vaccine [7], or repeated vaccination of adults with an egg-adapted vaccine [10], could shape the recipient’s immunological memory towards dominant egg-adapted epitopes not present in circulating viruses [7].

Using non-egg-based methods of vaccine production, such as cell-based or recombinant platforms, avoids egg adaptation [13]. Flucelvax® Quadrivalent (CSL Seqirus USA Inc., Summit, NJ, USA) is a quadrivalent influenza vaccine manufactured using cell-derived CVVs that are propagated in a qualified mammalian cell line, hereafter termed QIVc [14]. QIVc is the only non-egg-based influenza vaccine currently approved for pediatric patients from 6 months of age; it was indicated for individuals aged ≥ 4 years during the 2017–2020 seasons, over which time the cell-derived CVV components were progressively introduced for all four strains [15, 16], and received approval in October 2021 for use in patients ≥ 6 months of age [14, 17]. A large body of evidence has demonstrated superior VE of QIVc compared with egg-based quadrivalent influenza vaccines (QIVe) [16, 18–26], including large, retrospective test-negative design (TND) studies conducted on the 2017–2020 [16] and 2022–2023 seasons [24]. There is ongoing interest in the relative VE (rVE) of QIVc versus QIVe in patient subpopulations, specifically pediatric cohorts aged ≥ 6 months. This is particularly relevant given that the World Health Organization and the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommend immunizing children from ≥ 6 months of age against influenza [27, 28].

This study aimed to evaluate the rVE of QIVc versus QIVe in preventing test-confirmed influenza among individuals aged 6 months–64 years during the 2023–2024 influenza season in the US. The large sample size available in the linked real-world data source allowed for analyses in patient cohorts defined by age, test setting, presence of high-risk conditions, and analyses of the prevention of either influenza A or influenza B. In addition, we estimated the burden of influenza that would have been averted by using QIVc instead of QIVe during this season.

Methods

Study Design

The primary objective of this study was to estimate the rVE of QIVc versus QIVe in preventing test-confirmed influenza among individuals aged 6 months–64 years during the 2023–2024 influenza season in the US. QIVe represents standard-dose (i.e., 15 µg HA antigen per strain) inactivated egg-based quadrivalent influenza vaccines from several manufacturers (Afluria® [CSL Seqirus USA Inc., Summit, NJ, USA], FluLaval [GlaxoSmithKline, Durham, NC, USA], Fluarix [GlaxoSmithKline, Durham, NC, USA], and Fluzone® [Sanofi Pasteur Inc., Swiftwater, PA, USA]). QIVc (Flucelvax® Quadrivalent), from a single manufacturer (CSL Seqirus USA Inc., Summit, NJ, USA), is also a standard-dose influenza vaccine. We used a retrospective TND, similar to that used for previous seasons [16, 24]. The study was designed, implemented and reported in accordance with the International Society for Pharmacoepidemiology Guidelines for Good Pharmacoepidemiological Practice, applicable local regulations, and the ethical principles laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki, as well as the Reporting of Studies Conducted using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data [29].

Data Sources

This analysis was conducted using a linked dataset (data from 1 June 2022–31 May 2024) provided by HealthVerity that integrates US data from multiple sources of electronic health records (EHR), laboratory test data, medical claims, and pharmacy claims data (see Supplementary Appendix 1). An independent third-party reviewer ensured that this linked and de-identified dataset (Supplementary Appendix 2) was compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and statistically certified for research use via Expert Determination.

Study Population

Included patients were aged 6 months–64 years with a record of a valid influenza test within ± 7 days of an acute respiratory or febrile illness (ARFI) occurring during the 2023–2024 influenza season in the US (between October 1, 2023 and May 18, 2024). Patients had to have been vaccinated at least 14 days prior to the ARFI episode, between August 1, 2023 and May 4, 2024, and have at least 6 months of EHR transcript or claims activity prior to vaccination. Patients aged < 2 years were required to have received either a second homologous dose of vaccine at least 28 days following the first qualifying vaccination (and before 4 May 2024), or to have at least one record of vaccination during the previous influenza season (Supplementary Appendix 3). Patients were excluded if: they tested positive for influenza between May 21 and September 30, 2023; had more than one influenza vaccination between August 1, 2023 and the test date or, for patients aged < 9 years, received more than two influenza vaccines during the 2023–2024 influenza season or two different influenza vaccines between August 1, 2023 and the test date; had any influenza vaccination between May 20, 2023 and August 1, 2023; or were missing sex or geographic data in both the EHR and claims databases. Administrative codes used to identify and characterize patients from EHR, claims, and laboratory databases are listed in Supplementary Tables 1–4. The administrative codes used to identify conditions for calculation of the Charlson Comorbidity Index have been described previously [30].

Outcome Definitions

Patients were classified according to the results of their influenza test(s) (‘Positive’/ ‘Detected’ or ‘Negative’/’Not Detected’) during the influenza season: a case was defined by a positive test result and a control was defined by a negative test with no positive tests. Among tests with a known test type, results from molecular (e.g., polymerase chain reaction) and antigen influenza tests were used, whereas antibody tests were excluded due to their lack of specificity, and culture tests were excluded because a patient who had a culture test may be quite different from those who had molecular or antigen tests as the Infectious Disease Society of America guidelines recommend against the use of viral culture tests for the initial or primary diagnosis of influenza [31] and they are not routinely used in clinical practice. However, cases with an unknown test type were included, so it cannot be guaranteed that all antibody and culture tests were excluded. Test results were ascertained using current procedural terminology or Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes (Supplementary Table 3) and unstructured text found in the EHR and laboratory data sources (Supplementary Appendix 4).

Statistical Methods

Primary Analysis

Logistic regression was used to calculate the adjusted odds ratio (ORAdjusted) for the odds of testing positive for influenza among QIVc recipients relative to the odds among QIVe recipients. Influenza test status (case/control) was modeled as the outcome and vaccination type (QIVc versus QIVe) as the exposure. Adjusted rVE was calculated using the formula rVE = (1–OR Adjusted)*100% and reported with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The analysis was adjusted using a doubly robust approach combining inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) and multivariable adjustment as described in the Supplementary Appendix 5.

The exposure model used to define the inverse probability of treatment weights was adjusted for five a priori-defined covariates – age at vaccination date (as spline), sex, region, influenza test date (as spline), and COVID-19 vaccination within 6 months prior to test index date – as well as any other covariates that were imbalanced between vaccine groups. Standardized mean differences (SMD) were calculated to assess if other selected covariates were imbalanced between the test-negative controls in the QIVc group compared with the controls in the QIVe group (before and after weighting), and the a priori-defined threshold for imbalance was an absolute SMD value of >0.1 [32]. The covariates that were evaluated for imbalance (in addition to the five a priori-defined covariates) included week of vaccination, test type, payer, mean CCI, each individual high-risk condition flag, mean number of high-risk conditions and healthcare resource utilization (HCRU) in the 6 months prior to vaccination (number of outpatient visits [categorical], number of inpatient admissions [categorical], and number of emergency room visits [categorical]).

Stabilized IPTW was performed as follows: firstly, among the test-negative controls, logistic regression models predictive of the vaccine received (QIVc versus QIVe) provided propensity scores based on the study covariates (including a priori-defined and any additional covariates identified as imbalanced). These fitted models were then used to calculate propensity scores for the vaccine received by the cases. Propensity scores were then used to calculate stabilized weights for both cases and controls. Lastly, the stabilized weights were ‘winsorized’ to mitigate the impact of any outliers, i.e., weights that were outside of the a priori-defined thresholds of 1st and 99th percentile were set to that threshold [33].

For the doubly robust analysis, the IPTW sample was used in a multivariable logistic regression model adjusted for the five a priori covariates and any other covariates that remained unbalanced after weighting. Full details of each of the analyses are included in Supplementary Table 5.

Secondary and Post Hoc Analyses

Secondary analyses were pre-specified subject to having ≥ 80% statistical power to detect the rVE estimated in the primary analysis. These included analyses in patients sub-grouped by age (pediatric [6 months–17 years] and adults [18–64 years]), and in patients who were tested in an outpatient setting, and analyses of rVE against either influenza A only or influenza B only. In addition to conducting subgroup analyses by age, the full population analysis was re-run with the addition of an age group (pediatric/adult) by treatment interaction term as a model-building step. The two models (with and without interaction terms) were compared using a likelihood ratio test (LRT) to assess age group as a potential effect modifier.

Several post hoc analyses of rVE against any influenza were determined to be feasible (i.e., ≥ 80% statistical power), including further subgrouping by age (6 months–8 years; 9–17 years, 4–64 years). The pediatric subgroups match those used by the CDC for reporting VE [34] and align with the age threshold (9 years) below which vaccine-naïve children require two doses of influenza vaccine [35]. Additional post hoc analyses included those in subgroups defined by age and setting (outpatients aged 6 months–17 years, and 18–64 years), and in patients with at least one medical condition associated with a higher risk of influenza-related medical complications (referred to as high-risk conditions thereafter) (6 months–64 years; 18–64 years; 5–64 years). The full list of high-risk conditions, as defined by the CDC [36], is shown in Supplementary Table 6, and the administrative codes used to identify these conditions are listed in Supplementary Appendix 6. Analyses matching on test week and limited to the pediatric population were also performed against influenza B (Supplementary Appendix 7).

The doubly robust methodology used for the main analysis was also applied to each of these subgroup analyses. As part of this, the stabilized IPTW step was re-done for each subgroup, so that covariates were re-weighted according to how they varied in each subpopulation, and the age (as spline) and test index date (as spline) covariates were refitted based on the specific subgroup.

Unadjusted ORs are provided for each analysis in Supplementary Table 7.

Sensitivity Analyses

Two pre-specified sensitivity analyses were conducted. The ‘propensity to be tested’ analysis addressed the potential bias of which patients are tested for influenza through additional adjustment by the inverse of the ‘propensity to be tested’ score, which was estimated irrespective of vaccination status. The ‘matched on test week’ analysis matched cases and controls that were tested for influenza during the same week as an additional method to address potential confounding associated with the timing of the test.

Burden-Averted Model

The additional burden of influenza that would be averted if all people aged 6 months–64 years vaccinated in the US during the 2023–2024 influenza season had received QIVc instead of QIVe was estimated, as described previously [37]. Briefly, the modeling method used by the CDC for estimating overall burden averted due to influenza vaccination was adapted to an rVE context [37]. The model utilized CDC data on influenza vaccine uptake, influenza incidence, and influenza-related HCRU (outpatient visits, hospitalizations, and intensive care unit [ICU] visits) and deaths from the 2023–2024 influenza season; Supplementary Table 8 details the assumptions, parameters, and inputs used in the burden-averted model. End-of-season estimates of absolute VE (aVE; i.e., effectiveness relative to no vaccination) from the CDC [34] were used as a proxy for the aVE of QIVe, given that most US patients are vaccinated with QIVe. The aVE of QIVc was estimated as described by Lewis et al. [38], by applying the rVE from the pediatric (6 months–17 years) and adult (18–64 years) subgroup analyses to the aVE of QIVe. The base-case model applied the estimates, and the burden averted was expressed as symptomatic cases and related complications (outpatient visits, hospitalizations, ICU visits, and deaths prevented). The uncertainty around base-case results (including the impact of varying the incidence of influenza, the aVE of QIVe and the rVE of QIVc versus QIVe) was evaluated with deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses. Supplementary Table 8 includes the ranges over which the input parameters were varied in these sensitivity analyses (95% CI).

Ethical Approval

The study was conducted using a database linking de-identified electronic medical records with medical and pharmacy claims data. The database operates under a certification of statistical de-identification by a third-party HIPAA Expert Determination provider. The certification process ensures that users only have access to de-identified data and cannot pose a risk to re-identification. Since the database is HIPAA-certified based on a validated independent assessment and users receive unidentifiable/de-identified or coded data (with identifiers kept separately), the study does not fall within the regulatory definition of research involving human participants as outlined by the Code of Federal Regulations (policy 46.102(f)). The study is therefore exempt from institutional review board approval and informed consent was not needed. The study was designed, implemented, and reported in accordance with the ISPE Guidelines for Good Pharmacoepidemiological Practice, with applicable local regulations, and with the ethical principles laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its later amendments.

Results

Full Study Population

Of 16,917,416 individuals vaccinated with QIVc or QIVe, a total of 106,779 patients met the eligibility criteria (Fig. 1). This included 2119 (13%) test-positive cases and 14,750 (87%) test-negative controls in the QIVc group and 14,559 (16%) cases and 75,351 (84%) controls in the QIVe group (Fig. 1). The timing of vaccination was similar between vaccine groups (majority occurring September to November, peaking mid-October), as was the timing of testing (increasing from early October to a peak in mid-December, remaining high until February and then generally decreasing to the end of the study period) (Fig. 2). Most patients were tested in an outpatient setting (97,159 patients; 91%). rVE in Pediatric Patients

The total pediatric population (6 months–17 years) comprised 60,990 patients. QIVc demonstrated superior VE over QIVe in the pediatric subpopulation, with an adjusted rVE (95% CI) of 19.6% (13.6–25.3%) for prevention of test-confirmed influenza (Fig. 4).

Approximately two-thirds of the pediatric population were aged 6 months–8 years (n = 40,193), and one-third were aged 9–17 years (n = 20,797). QIVc was significantly more effective than QIVe in preventing test-confirmed influenza in both these pediatric subgroups, with adjusted rVE (95% CI) estimates of 17.6% (9.3–25.1%) and 24.3% (15.1–32.5%) in the younger and older pediatric subgroups, respectively (Fig. 4).

rVE in Adult Patients

The adult population comprised 45,789 patients aged 18–64 years. The adjusted rVE (95% CI) among the adult population was 18.5% (12.1–24.5%) (Fig. 4).

rVE in Outpatients

The population tested in the outpatient setting comprised 97,159 patients (91% of the full population), including 56,181 pediatric and 40,978 adult patients. Analyses on outpatients only resulted in an adjusted rVE (95% CI) estimate of 19.7% (15.3–23.8%), and estimates were similar in pediatric and adult subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 5).

rVE in High-Risk Patients

Demographics and clinical characteristics of 57,139 patients with ≥ 1 high-risk condition are shown in Supplementary Table 10. The most common high-risk conditions (overall and in those aged 5–64 or 18–64 years) were metabolic disorders, heart disease and related conditions, and endocrine disorders. Similar to the main analysis, QIVc was also significantly more effective than QIVe in patients with ≥ 1 high-risk condition, with adjusted rVE (95% CI) estimates of 14.7% (8.7–20.3%) in high-risk patients of any age, 17.1% (10.9–22.9%) in patients aged 5–64 years, and 17.1% (9.3–24.1%) in high-risk adults (Fig. 4).

rVE Analyses by Influenza Type

Compared with influenza A cases (n = 9799), influenza B cases (n = 4377) tended to be younger across both vaccine groups (mean age 26.4 versus 19.6 years among QIVc recipients, respectively, and 19.6 versus 15.4 years among QIVe recipients), with the highest proportion of influenza B cases detected in the 5–17 years age group. Influenza B cases were also less likely to have one or more high-risk conditions. Demographics and clinical characteristics were otherwise generally similar except for some differences in geographic region, test type, and prior HCRU (Supplementary Table 11). Limiting analyses to influenza A cases or influenza B cases (and all test-negative controls) resulted in adjusted rVE (95% CI) estimates of 19.3% (14.0–24.2%) for prevention of influenza A and 36.8% (30.0–42.9%) for the prevention of influenza B (Fig. 4). Consistent rVE against influenza B was estimated in the pediatric population and when cases and controls were matched on test week (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Sensitivity Analyses

The analysis addressing potential bias in the propensity to be tested was conducted in the full study population. For the matched-on-test-week analysis, all cases and 78.8% of controls were included. Each case was matched to either three (1538), four (9321) or five (5819) controls. The results of the sensitivity analyses were consistent with the primary analysis, with adjusted rVE estimates of 20.2% (16.0–24.1%) (propensity to be tested analysis) and 20.3% (16.4–23.9%) (matched-on-test-week analysis) (Fig. 4).

Burden Averted

Applying the estimated adjusted rVE of 19.6% in the pediatric population and 18.5% in the adult population to the base case aVE for QIVe [34] resulted in an aVE for QIVc of 61% (6 month–4-year-olds), 67% (5–17 years), 49% (18–49 years) and 32% (50–64 years), which corresponds to incremental aVEs (difference in aVE of QIVc versus QIVe) of 9%, 8%, 11%, and 16%, respectively. Other model input data for the burden averted model are shown in Supplementary Table 8. If all individuals who were vaccinated against influenza in the US during the 2023–2024 season received QIVc instead of QIVe, this model estimated that QIVc would have prevented an additional 2,379,395 symptomatic illnesses, with proportionate reductions in related complications (Table 3). Pediatric cases comprised 45% of the additional symptomatic illnesses averted, 54% of the additional outpatient visits, 29% of hospitalizations, 29% of ICU visits and 9% of additional deaths averted (Table 3). Results of sensitivity analyses conducted to explore the uncertainty in the estimates are shown in Supplementary Fig. 7 and 8. Deterministic sensitivity analyses showed that the rVE estimate and the influenza burden estimates were associated with the most variability in the estimated results (Supplementary Fig. 7). Discussion

In this retrospective analysis of a large, real-world dataset, QIVc was superior to QIVe in the prevention of test-confirmed influenza, with an rVE (95% CI) of 19.8% (15.7–23.8%) among individuals aged 6 months–64 years during the 2023–2024 influenza season in the US. The effectiveness of QIVc against any influenza was consistently superior to that of QIVe across patients sub-grouped by age, those tested in the outpatient setting and in patients with high-risk conditions, with adjusted rVE estimates ranging from 14.7% to 24.3%. Importantly, this study provides the first demonstration of the superior effectiveness of QIVc compared with QIVe in the prevention of test-confirmed influenza in pediatric subpopulations from 6 months of age (rVE ranging from 17.6% to 24.3%). QIVc also had superior effectiveness against either influenza A or influenza B alone (rVE 19.3% and 36.8%, respectively). A sensitivity analysis assessing the impact of the patients’ likelihood of receiving an influenza test and another matching cases and controls by test week also supported the main analysis, with rVE point estimates of 20%, suggesting minimal bias due to testing practices or epidemic timing. Furthermore, we estimated that, if all individuals aged 6 months–64 years in the US had received QIVc over QIVe in this season, an additional 2,379,395 symptomatic illnesses, 1,128,661 outpatient visits, 14,930 hospitalizations and 574 deaths would have been averted.

The 2023–2024 US influenza season was similar to pre-COVID-19 influenza seasons in terms of timing as well as intensity [37], with activity in the US increasing from early November to a peak in late December, remaining elevated through to February and then gradually declining to inter-season levels by May [39]. Influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 was the predominant virus during this season, responsible for 69% of positive cases overall [40], and with co-circulation of two genetic clades: clade 6B.1A.5a.2a.1 (to which the reference CVV belonged; 5a.2a.1 hereafter) and clade 6B.1A.5a.2a (5a.2a) [34]. Influenza A(H3N2) and B/Victoria viruses were identified more frequently than A(H1N1)pdm09 viruses later in the season as overall influenza activity began to decrease [39]. The end-of-season estimates of VE reported by the CDC (Chung et al., [34]) were 41% against any influenza, and differed substantially by influenza type: the VE against A(H1N1)pdm09 was uncharacteristically low, at 28%, similar to the 30% VE against A(H3N2), while the VE against B/Victoria was 68% [34].

The superior effectiveness of QIVc over QIVe reported in this study is likely due to mismatches between egg-based vaccine viruses and the circulating influenza strains, as a result of egg-adapted mutations. Detailed sequence analyses of egg-based CVVs used between 2010 and 2024 have identified egg-adapted mutations in every season and across all four strains, with the majority occurring in major antigenic sites [41]. During the NH 2023–2024 season under study, amino acid substitutions occurred in antigenic sites and/or the receptor binding site (RBS) of the HA proteins of all four egg-based CVVs (Supplementary Table 12) [41]. In contrast, no amino-acid changes were detected in the corresponding cell-based CVVs (Supplementary Table 12). Egg-adapted mutations identified in the A(H1N1)pdm09 vaccine strains included R142K in antigenic site Ca2, a reversion mutation found in the egg-derived high growth reassortants (HGR) of inactivated and live vaccines [42]. Analyses of clade-specific VE in the NH 2023–2024 influenza season suggest that this egg adaptation may have contributed to the lower VE observed against A(H1N1)pdm09 [34, 42]. Specifically, lower VE (23%) was estimated against the predominant clade 5a.2a.1 strains [34], which have an arginine (R) at position 142, compared with the less prevalent 5a.2a strains (VE 59%), which have a lysine (K) at that position, thus matching the egg-derived vaccines that constituted the majority of influenza vaccines administered in the US [42]. The rVE reported in this study could thus partly stem from the avoidance of this mutation in the cell-based vaccine. Other egg-adapted mutations found in important antigenic sites of A(H3N2) and B/Victoria vaccine strains (Supplementary Table 12) would also have contributed to the benefit observed with QIVc. In particular, the G141R mutation in the B/Victoria vaccine virus is located in the 150-loop antigenic site, a long protruding loop in which single amino-acid substitutions are frequently found to alter antigenicity, and this mutation has been reported in escape mutant viruses [43], suggesting an impact on recognition by neutralizing antibodies. These data add to the evidence demonstrating the impact of egg-adapted mutations on VE [41].

The present study adds to the data from four previous seasons since the introduction of cell-based CVVs [16, 18–25, 44–47], which generally favored QIVc over QIVe, and thus demonstrates the benefit of avoiding egg-adapted mutations [41]. This includes several retrospective cohort studies [18–23], multiple small prospective TND studies [44, 45, 47] and one large, multi-season, retrospective TND study of the 2017–2020 influenza seasons in the US that reported rVE estimates of 10.0% to 14.8% [16]. Two studies of the 2022–2023 US influenza season have generated conflicting results: one large retrospective TND study including 7423 cases that showed superior VE with QIVc [24], and one retrospective cohort study including only 112 cases showing comparable VE of QIVc versus QIVe in prevention of influenza hospitalization [48]. Notably, the rVE estimates available from large, adequately powered studies all showed a statistically significant benefit of QIVc over QIVe in individuals from either 6 months or 4 years to 64 years of age [16, 18–21, 23–26]. The magnitude of benefit observed with QIVc has varied over the seasons, and this is not unexpected, as – for any influenza vaccine – many season-specific inter-connected factors affect VE [49, 50]. That said, these findings align with results of a detailed sequence analysis demonstrating the presence of egg-adapted mutations in important antigenic sites of every egg-based influenza vaccine strain and in each of these seasons [41].

This retrospective TND study provides the first evidence of a significant benefit of cell- over egg-based influenza vaccine in patients from 6 months of age, with rVE ranging from 17.6% to 24.3% across pediatric cohorts including infants, children, and/or adolescents. Our findings build on those from retrospective cohort studies showing a consistent – although not always statistically significant – benefit of QIVc over QIVe in preventing medically attended influenza and hospitalizations among children aged 4–17 years during the northern hemisphere (NH) 2017–2020 seasons [18–20, 22, 23, 51]. It is crucial to optimize vaccination strategy in the pediatric population, as children younger than 5 years old are considered at higher risk of serious influenza complications [36] and they are key vectors of influenza transmission [51]. Furthermore, early lifetime exposures to viruses shape an individual’s antibody repertoire; this phenomenon is known as immune imprinting [7, 52]. The advantage of avoiding egg-adapted mutations in childhood vaccinations has also been highlighted by Liu et al., who demonstrated the negative impact of immune imprinting to dominant egg-adapted epitopes among children aged < 3 years who were vaccinated with QIVe, with antibody responses in subsequent seasons being skewed towards egg-adapted epitopes not present on circulating viruses [7]. While it is not possible to assess any impact of immune imprinting in our study, it is of interest for future research to examine whether the expected long-term benefit of avoiding imprinting to dominant egg-adapted epitopes is realized with repeated childhood vaccination with QIVc.

Patients with high-risk conditions are another vulnerable sub-population for whom influenza vaccination is particularly important, as they are more likely to have severe influenza outcomes [28]. Although vaccination coverage tends to be higher among these patients, it remains suboptimal [53]. Four other studies have examined the potential benefit of cell-based vaccines in high-risk populations [20, 21, 23, 54, 55], and a meta-analysis estimated a pooled rVE of 10.3% (95% CI 5.7–15.0%) for QIVc versus QIVe in the prevention of influenza-related medical encounters [55]. Consistent with this, our study demonstrated superior effectiveness of QIVc compared with QIVe among the overall high-risk subpopulation and those aged 18–64 or 5–64 years (rVE range: 14.7% to 17.1%). We assessed the latter age group because many countries outside the US only include patients aged 5–64 years in publicly funded vaccination programs if they have high-risk conditions [53].

We were also able to examine rVE in patients who tested positive for either influenza A or B. The estimated rVE when limiting to those who tested positive for influenza A (19.3%), the predominant circulating influenza type, was similar to that of the full population, while the influenza B subgroup analysis yielded an rVE of 36.8%. It is important to consider the results of this analysis in the context of the type-specific absolute effectiveness of the influenza vaccines. The rVE of QIVc versus QIVe represents the incremental benefit afforded by QIVc relative to QIVe (i.e., the proportion of influenza cases remaining after QIVe that are additionally prevented by QIVc) [38, 56]. The end-of-season estimate of aVE (representative of the aVE of QIVe) was much higher against B/Victoria (68%) than against influenza A (28% and 30% against influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 and A(H3N2), respectively) [34]. Therefore, the rVE of QIVc versus QIVe for the prevention of influenza B represents a proportion based on a smaller number of residual influenza cases compared with the influenza A subgroup. As a result, in this situation, the estimated rVE against influenza B represents a similar incremental difference in aVE across the two influenza types.

Modeling the burden averted by QIVc versus QIVe during the 2023–2024 influenza season in the US indicated that the use of QIVc would have had a substantial public health impact on individuals aged 6 months–64 years, with prevention of an estimated additional 2,379,395 symptomatic illnesses, as well as proportional reductions in associated HCRU and deaths. Results of sensitivity analyses conducted to explore the uncertainty in the estimates support the robustness of the findings. These findings are dependent on the severity of the season (classified as moderate) [37] and the aVE of QIVe (assumed to be 52%, 59%, 38%, and 16% in patients aged 6 months–4 years, 5–17, 18–49, and 50–64 years, respectively, based on end-of-season CDC estimates of aVE) [34]. The model likely underestimates the burden averted, since it uses the CDC estimates of influenza burden in the general population (i.e., including vaccinated and unvaccinated individuals) as the benchmark for influenza burden among the unvaccinated population and does not account for reduced transmission through vaccination [37].

Strengths and Limitations

Study strengths include the large real-world dataset, allowing precise estimates of rVE in pediatric patients aged ≥ 6 months and other subpopulations of interest that are representative of the population(s) under study; application of doubly robust adjustment methodology to control for confounders; and the requirement for test-confirmed influenza outcomes in temporal proximity to a diagnosis of acute respiratory or febrile illness, as this allows differentiation from COVID-19 and other co-circulating respiratory viruses and minimizes bias associated with differences in healthcare-seeking behavior. One limitation of our study is that the sensitivity and specificity of the influenza tests used (mostly as part of routine outpatient care) is unknown. However, the FDA requires that rapid antigen tests have at least 95% specificity [57], and misclassification of cases as controls (sensitivity) would not be expected to have a major impact on rVE estimates. Another limitation of our analysis is that vaccination was not randomly assigned. The ability to attribute causality is limited as residual confounding cannot be fully excluded and it remains a potential source of bias, as in all observational research. The study methodology leveraged all available data to adjust for measured confounders, but clinical and claims data sources can be incomplete. For example, our dataset had high amounts of missing data for race, ethnicity, test type and payer type, and only 6 months of EHR/claims history were available. In contrast, it was possible to identify influenza type in this dataset, thus allowing further investigation of VE.

Conclusions

In this large-scale, retrospective TND analysis of cell-based versus egg-based vaccines in the 2023–2024 influenza season, QIVc demonstrated superior effectiveness in preventing test-confirmed influenza compared with QIVe among individuals aged 6 months–64 years in the US, with an estimated rVE of 19.8%. Superior effectiveness was consistently reported across all sensitivity and sub-analyses, including those in the pediatric, adult, outpatient, and high-risk populations and analyses of rVE against either influenza A or influenza B. Notably, this analysis provides the first demonstration of QIVc superiority among the pediatric population following the expanded indication to include children from 6 months of age. Translating the rVE to a public health perspective, it is anticipated that using QIVc instead of QIVe would have prevented an additional 2,379,395 symptomatic illnesses, 1,128,661 outpatient visits, 14,930 hospitalizations and 574 deaths among those aged 6 months–64 years in the US.
