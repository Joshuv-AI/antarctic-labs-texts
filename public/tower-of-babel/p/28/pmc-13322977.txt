Detectable safety gestalt of rheumatoid arthritis treatments from pivotal clinical drug trials
Authors: Victoria Konzett, Andreas Kerschbaumer, Laurenz Willmann, Daniel Aletaha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo provide a meta-level comparison of standardized safety profiles (‘gestalt’) of currently licensed biologic and targeted synthetic disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (bDMARDs and tsDMARDs) in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) from pivotal clinical trials.MethodsWe systematically searched EMBASE, Medline and the Cochrane Library for randomized controlled RA drug approval trials of bDMARDs and tsDMARDs. The Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) hierarchy system was applied to account for the heterogeneous safety reporting across trials, and random-effects meta-analyses of adverse events (AE) were performed at lower MedDRA levels as well as for major system organ classes. Serious AEs (SAE) and AEs of special interest (AESI) were studied descriptively. Forest plots and radar charts were used for visualization, to generate distinct safety profiles, referred to as ‘safety gestalt’, for each compound.ResultsSixty-four randomized, placebo-controlled drug approval trials were considered, of which 25 studied TNF-α inhibitors, 10 studied IL-6 receptor inhibitors, 5 studied rituximab, 7 studied abatacept and 18 studied Janus kinase inhibitors. Meta-level comparisons of the total number of AEs and SAEs showed comparable overall safety and tolerability, while a more granular insight into distinct organ classes and AESIs disentailed differences between compounds and drug classes.ConclusionStandardized safety reporting and profiling of bDMARDs and tsDMARDs from pivotal clinical drug trials provides an overview of safety outcomes of commonly used RA therapies, supports safety-oriented treatment selection in individual patients, and provides essential context for safety evaluation of novel therapies during periods before long-term safety data become available.Keywords: rheumatoid arthritis, DMARDs, outcome measures, clinical trials, safety, treatment selection, biomarker, meta-analysis

Introduction

Contemporary biologic and targeted synthetic disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (bDMARDs, tsDMARDs) have transformed rheumatoid arthritis (RA) management, offering profound improvements in disease control and outcomes for patients [1]. New treatments are constantly emerging and entering clinical development programmes. While there are clear claims regarding the expectations of efficacy from these programmes, the evaluation of safety compared with other already licensed compounds in the field is not trivial. Trials are notoriously underpowered to address safety questions, and safety reporting is highly heterogeneous [2]. Yet, safety data from clinical trials are typically all that is available at the time of market access. Additional and relevant safety data may become evident only later through pooled analyses, long-term extensions or registry data; this particularly relates to adverse events of special interest (AESI), such as tuberculosis reactivation with TNF-α inhibitors (TNFi) [3], gastrointestinal perforations with IL-6 receptor inhibitors (IL-6Ri) [4, 5], or cardiovascular and thromboembolic events as well as malignancies with Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi) [6]. While these AESIs often shape clinical perceptions of drugs with a certain mode of action, treatment adherence in real-world is also influenced strongly by less severe but more common adverse events (AE) (such as nausea, headaches or injection-related reactions) [7], which should therefore also be considered when assessing drug safety.

A new drug entering the market will always have less comprehensive safety information, typically drawn from narrower sources and more restricted patient populations, than any established drug. Balanced safety evaluation of a new compound at launch therefor entails a comparison with existing drugs based on information generated at equivalent stages, that is, the information coming from the respective drug development programmes, and particularly from phase 3 trials. While unavoidably incomplete, these data provide the least biased basis for comparison possible at that time immediately before market access, and can reveal relevant differences in safety profiles of drugs, thereby supporting appropriate treatment selection.

Here, we provide a comparative assessment of drugs available for RA by class, based on their respective clinical trial programmes, to depict typical AE patterns associated with different compounds and mechanisms of action, and to allow an assessment of novel drugs entering the market against a comparable ‘entry’ reference. These safety profiles are analysed using typical reporting domains in clinical trials and are quantitatively depicted, providing a distinct ‘gestalt’ for each compound.

Methods

Database search, data extraction and quality assessment

Three investigators (V.K., L.W. and A.K.) independently screened Medline, EMBASE and Cochrane CENTRAL (Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials) for all double-blind, randomized, controlled, phase 2 and phase 3 trials that investigated bDMARDs and tsDMARDs currently licensed for treating adult patients with RA, diagnosed with the 1987 or 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria [8, 9]. All literature published between 1 January 1995, and 4 May 2023 in English language was searched for eligibility. A search update was performed on 22 January 2025, to also include recently published manuscripts. We included studies with a minimum sample size of 50 participants and a placebo (PLC)-controlled study duration of at least 12 weeks. We excluded studies that were not fully published in peer-reviewed journals, had a head-to-head comparator or strategic trial design, as well as those lacking comprehensive safety reporting in manuscripts or supplements, including phase 1 and phase 2 dose-finding trials.

Full-text assessment, study selection and data extraction were performed independently by two researches (V.K. and L.W.). We collected study and patient characteristics as well as efficacy and safety outcomes of all eligible studies. Data were extracted from texts, tables, figures and online supplements, as well as from ClinicalTrials.gov, and were stored in a specifically developed relational database. Risk of bias assessment (RoB) was done using the Cochrane Collaboration’s tool for assessing RoB in randomized trials, version 2 (RoB 2) [10]. The database search, data extraction, quality control and subsequent meta-analyses were conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews and the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis) statement [11, 12].

Safety event categorization

To address the heterogeneity in safety reporting across studies and timepoints, we applied the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) classification system, an internationally standardized medical terminology system for AE reporting [13]. MedDRA was developed under the auspices of the International Council for Harmonization of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH), and provides a five-level classification framework for safety terms, reaching from >80 000 lowest level terms to 27 major system organ classes. For the current project, we extracted all safety terms as reported in the manuscripts (1705 safety variables in total), and allocated them to a respective lowest level term in the MedDRA web-based browser (MedDRA Version 27.1, September 2024) [14]. The MedDRA hierarchy system was then applied to create a five-level hierarchy structure of all safety outcomes, where outcomes could be summarized in predefined groups at higher MedDRA levels. The MedDRA system allows for multiaxial grading, meaning that event categorization is not mutually exclusive, and safety outcomes can be included in more than one hierarchy arm (e.g. pneumonia can be considered both as a respiratory event and as an infectious event).

Safety events were further categorized by severity into AEs, serious adverse events (SAE; commonly defined as AEs leading to death, hospitalization, prolongation of existing hospitalization, or necessitating intravenous antibiotic treatment), and non-serious adverse events (NSAE; reported only in ClinicalTrials.gov as total AEs minus SAEs). This categorization was maintained throughout the whole study and all analyses.

Statistical analysis

The total amount of patient-years (PY) in each treatment arm, as well as the incidence rate (IR) of each safety outcome per patient year of exposure (IR/PY), were calculated to correct for variances in study duration. Event rates were compared between treatment and control arms on all five MedDRA levels, as well as per study, compound and mechanism of action. As the preferred term level (MedDRA level 4; detailed analyses) and system organ class level (MedDRA level 1; overview of different major organ classes) were revealed as the most informative (providing either a detailed insight into safety signals or broad overview of patterns across organ classes), we focused further analyses on these two layers.

Meta-analyses of log transformed IR/PY as well as IR differences (IRD) vs PLC were then performed for all AEs that were reported in more than one study on different MedDRA hierarchy levels. I2 statistics and visual inspection of funnel plots were used to assess statistical heterogeneity, and random-effects models were calculated to account for this variability. Model parameters were estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method, and sensitivity analyses were performed with the DerSimonian and Laird estimator and generalized linear mixed models, as well as using fixed-effects modelling approaches [15–17]. We applied a continuity correction (0.5-correction) to all zero-event counts.

Statistical results were visualized using forest plots and radar charts. For the main analysis, we considered only MedDRA system organ classes that can reasonably be impacted by drug therapies (e.g. congenital disorders were not considered), and organ classes were further summarized into a reduced number of groups to enable a clearer visual inspection of safety patterns for different compounds and mechanisms of action. In cases of double reporting of AEs (e.g. overall number of infections as well as the most frequent infections reported in a trial), only the overall event rates were considered for analysis. General AE terms (like overall number of AEs and withdrawals due to AEs), were not assigned to MedDRA terms, and meta-analysed separately.

Results

We screened 20 173 records, of which 367 were selected for full text reading, and 64 finally included in the analysis; 5 trials were available on adalimumab (ADA), 4 on infliximab (INF), 6 on etanercept (ETA), 7 on certolizumab pegol (CZP) and 3 on golimumab (GOL). Eight trials were further available on tocilizumab (TCZ), 2 on sarilumab (SAR), 5 on rituximab (RTX), 7 on abatacept (ABA; of which 1 included also an INF arm), as well as 6 on tofacitinib (TOFA), 4 on baricitinib (BARI), 5 on upadacitinib (UPA) and 3 on filgotinib (FILGO). Fifty-five studies compared treatments to PLC with background conventional synthetic DMARD (csDMARD) therapy and were considered in the main analysis. Of these, 10 studied DMARD naïve patients, 36 studied csDMARD insufficient responder (IR) patients, and 9 studied bDMARD IR patients.

An overview of the systematic database search and study selection process is provided in Supplementary Data S1 to S3. The main reasons for excluding manuscripts were studies on compounds or dosing regimens that are currently not licensed, and studies with PLC-controlled periods shorter than 12 weeks. Strategic trials, head-to-head studies as well as phase 1 and 2 dose-finding studies were also excluded.

Study and patient characteristics summarized by compound and mechanism of action are provided in Table 1. Overall, baseline characteristics were balanced across compounds and drug classes. Years of study publication varied across compounds, resulting in considerable differences in safety reporting due to substantial evolvements in reporting standards and regulatory requirements across the observation periods. A tabular overview of baseline characteristics as well as RoB analyses of all studies is provided in the Supplementary Data S4 and S5.

General safety and tolerability were comparable across most compounds. We calculated incident rate differences (IRD) per 100 patient-years (PY) of active treatment arms vs placebo separately for each treatment. This metric allows a comparison of how many events occur during 100 years of combined follow-up. For example, an IRD of 5 means that for every 100 PY of treatment with a given compound, there occurred 5 AEs more with active treatment compared with placebo. In other words, on average there occurred one additional event if 20 patients were treated for 1 year. A relevant risk difference in the overall number of patients with AEs for active treatment vs PLC was found for TCZ, SAR, BARI and UPA (Fig. 2A), SAEs were more frequently observed in CZP (IR 3.83/100 PY; 95% CI: 0.84, 6.83), TCZ (IR 2.78/100 PY; 95% CI: 0.40, 5.15), SAR (IR 5.73/100 PY; 95% CI: 2.14, 9.32) and UPA (IR 3.07/100 PY; 95% CI −0.6, 6.76) studies (Fig. 2B), and an increase in withdrawals due to AEs vs PLC was found for GOL, TCZ, SAR, TOFA and BARI (Fig. 2C).

Distinct AESIs were observed for all compounds in trials and observational data and commonly guide treatment decisions from a safety perspective. Tables 2–4 provide a quantitative overview of these outcomes: a numeric increase in serious infectious events (SIE) was observed with most compounds. Exceptions included ETA, RTX, BARI and FILGO, partly due to higher AE rates in the PLC groups of some trials. Opportunistic infection rates were generally low across trials. Numeric risk increases were observed with TNFi, particularly ADA, INF and CZP, while ETA might again be an exception, as well as with SAR. A risk increase of herpes zoster infections has previously been described with JAKi, and was also seen in our analyses, for TOFA (IRD vs PLC: 1.04/100 PY; 95% CI: −0.14, 2.21), BARI (1.79/100 PY; 95% CI: 0.87, 2.72), UPA (0.61/100 PY; 95% CI: −0.99, 2.21) and FILGO (0.36/100 PY; 95% CI: −0.34, 1.06). A relevant risk increase was further observed for INF (1.13/100 PY; 95% CI: −1.08, 3.33).

Serious infusion-related reactions like anaphylaxis were observed in five patients with INF (IR 0.85/100 PY; 95% CI: 0.28, 1.98), three patients with RTX (IR 0.77/100 PY; 95% CI: 0.16, 2.24), and one patient with ABA (IR 0.27/100 PY; 95% CI: 0.01, 1.49). Two gastrointestinal perforations were observed with UPA (IR 0.48/100 PY; 95% CI: 0.06, 1.75), one with FILGO (IR 0.14/100 PY; 95% CI: 0.01, 0.79) and one was further observed across all PLC groups. For IL-6Ri, one gastrointestinal perforation and one case of diverticular haemorrhage without perforation were distinctly described in the TCZ trials, while 15 serious gastrointestinal events were not further categorized in the original publications (as opposed to eight with PLC), but a pooled safety analysis later described an IR of 0.2/100 PY for gastrointestinal perforations with TCZ in the PLC-controlled periods of five trials, and an IR of 0.28/100 PY when including also open-label extension periods [21]. One gastrointestinal perforation was observed with SAR 150 mg every 2 weeks (E2W), and one serious gastrointestinal event not classified with SAR 200 mg E2W. Five cases of severe neutropenia (defined as neutrophil count <500/μl, febrile neutropenia or agranulocytosis) were observed with UPA (IR 1.21/100 PY; 95% CI: 0.39, 2.82), five with SAR (IR 0.98/100 PY; 95% CI: 0.31, 2.29), two with TCZ (IR 0.14/100 PY; 95% CI: 0.02, 0.50) and one with ADA (IR 0.48/100 PY; 95% CI: 0.01, 2.69).

Antinuclear antibodies were detected in 118 vs 29 patients with INF vs PLC (IRD 20.95/100 PY; 95% CI: 14.42, 27.47), 96 vs 59 with ADA vs PLC (IRD 9.24/100 PY; 95% CI: 2.83, 15.64), 56 vs 22 with GOL vs PLC (IRD 2.91/100 PY; 95% CI: −5.51, 11.33) and 19 vs 15 with ABA vs PLC (IRD −8.48/100 PY; −22.78, 5.81). Double-stranded DNA antibodies were reported in 71 vs 1 patient with INF vs PLC (IRD 18.05/100 PY; 95% CI: 13.72, 22.37), in 45 vs 3 with ADA vs PLC (IRD 10.90/100 PY; 95% CI: 7.36, 14.43), in 4 vs 1 with ETA vs PLC (IRD 7.45/100 PY; 95% CI: −12.72, 27.66) and in 4 vs 12 with ABA vs PLC (IRD −16.19/100 PY; 95% CI: −27.73, −4.65). Two cases of drug-induced lupus or lupus-like syndrome were observed with CZP (IR 0.23/100 PY; 95% CI: 0.03, 0.81), while no cases of multiple sclerosis or other demyelinating disorders were reported during the analysed study periods. Anti-drug antibodies were found against almost all bDMARDs, but at highest rates for INF (IRD 13.71/100 PY; 95% CI: 10.24, 17.18), CZP (IRD 6.40/100 PY; 4.29, 8.45), SAR (IRD 10.16/100 PY; 95% CI: 6.38, 13.94), TCZ (IRD 2.85/100 PY; 95% CI: 0.54, 5.17) and RTX (IRD 4.46/100 PY; 95% CI: −0.56, 9.47), all again compared with placebo. Regarding MACE and malignancies, no clear safety signal could be established across compounds and drug classes. Overall mortality was low and within the expected range for the overall RA population (Tables 2–4).

While these SAEs and AESIs are crucial for evaluating drug safety, they do not fully capture the real-world tolerability profile of a drug. This is commonly also determined by less severe but more frequent AEs—like nausea, common infections, injection-related reactions or headaches—which can substantially impact patients’ everyday health-related quality of life and treatment adherence to a certain drug. Taken together, these elements shape the overall ‘safety gestalt’ of a compound or drug class.

Fig. 3 provides a visualization of these safety shapes for all currently licensed bDMARDs and tsDMARD in RA at system organ class level, the highest MedDRA level, with distinct safety patterns emerging across drug classes: TNF inhibitors were associated with higher rates of injection- or infusion-related reactions and laboratory abnormalities, including elevations in antinuclear and double-stranded DNA antibodies as well as anti-drug antibodies, alongside increased frequencies of infections and respiratory disorders such as upper respiratory tract infections. Gastrointestinal events did not increase, and some agents even showed reduced rates of specific AEs, such as diarrhoea with ADA. Discussion

Using data from PLC-controlled pivotal drug trials published over the past three decades, we delineate the ‘safety gestalt’ of currently licensed DMARDs in RA. By applying a standardized and internationally recognized safety categorization framework, distinct safety patterns emerge across drugs and classes; differences that are often obscured in general impressions or in traditional statistical analyses.

This analysis intentionally focuses on safety data from randomized, PLC-controlled trials to allow for a standardized and methodologically fair comparison of safety profiles, with the randomized placebo arm aiming to address between-study differences across compounds, populations and time. While post-marketing and registry data are invaluable for detecting rare or long-term AEs (important AESIs and SAEs like MACE or malignancies are often not yet detected in the PLC-controlled periods of trials), they are inherently heterogeneous; affected by variations in patient selection, disease severity, comorbidities, concomitant therapies and reporting intensity. Such real-world variability introduces substantial bias by indication and limits comparability between agents. Additionally, real-world data often become available only years after market entry of a novel compound, meaning that early safety assessments and between-drug comparisons must rely solely on clinical trial data. This creates a benchmark regarding the completeness and contextual clarity that safety reporting in clinical trials should strive to achieve in order to support a safe and well-tolerated launch and use of a new drug. Pivotal clinical trials offer harmonized conditions, consistent definitions, balanced control arms and well-defined exposure periods. Under these uniform settings, AEs are systematically recorded, enabling observed safety signals to be attributed more confidently to the drug itself rather than to external factors. Although this approach does not capture the full spectrum of long-term safety, it provides a reproducible baseline with an approach to minimize bias; placing new compounds within the same evidentiary framework as existing therapies at the time of their approval. Due to the long timespan since their approval leading to substantial heterogeneity in history safety reporting, csDMARDs were deliberately excluded from this study. However, we included only studies with background csDMARD therapy (mostly MTX) in the main analysis to account for csDMARDs as important confounder. Conversely, current data were not powered for the comparison of monotherapy with combination therapy, which remains an important question for future research.

Indeed, RA DMARDs exhibit various safety signals, some shared across compounds and others specific to individual drugs or classes. Examples for this include tuberculosis reactivation, rare cases of demyelinating diseases, and drug-induced lupus with TNFi [3, 22, 23], gastrointestinal perforations or severe neutropenia with IL-6Ri [4], hypogammaglobulinaemia with RTX [24], COPD exacerbation and a potential increase of malignancies with ABA [25], and an increased risk for MACE, malignancies, thrombotic events and herpes zoster with JAKi [6]. Recent analyses of bDMARD and tsDMARD safety tend to focus on these AESIs rather than overall drug safety profiles [25–27], and AESIs currently guide safety-based treatment decisions particularly in patients with relevant comorbidities, but are generally rare and variably weighted by different stakeholders. Beyond that, less severe but more frequent AEs, like gastrointestinal symptoms, headaches or injection-related reactions, substantially contribute to the overall AE burden on patients and can impair treatment tolerability and adherence in routine care. This is highly relevant when aiming to treat patients effectively to a target of remission, as a drug can only be effective if it is actually taken. The basic precision medicine concept of giving ‘the right drug to the right patient at the right time’ therefore clearly extends beyond the consideration of potential or predicted efficacy, and relates also to an optimal balance between benefits and risks of treatments, which remains challenging in clinical practice. Ultimately, both ‘minor’ but more frequent AEs and severe AESIs, which are rare but hold stronger psychological mechanisms in place, can influence treatment prescription, initiation, adherence and persistence; thereby shaping the overall tolerability and burden of a drug.

Limitations of the current analysis include those inherent to meta-analyses of clinical trials and especially safety reporting: (i) the short duration, relatively small sample size and limited generalizability of clinical trials to real-world populations (PLC-controlled periods commonly limit to 12–24 weeks, and SAEs like MACE or malignancies are often missed); (ii) differences in safety reporting across time periods and different compounds, which we here aimed to address by using the MedDRA classification system; (iii) data are not powered to allow statistical comparison of rare AEs, resulting in a purely descriptive comparison of SAEs and AESIs in our study; (iv) the heterogeneity of trial populations and changes in recruiting patterns over time, with a strong trend towards more difficult-to-treat patients and globalizations of recruitment (a substantial number of patients is nowadays being recruited in low and middle income countries, which potentially introduces safety event differences between trials) [28]; (v) inconsistent reporting of background glucocorticoids and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), which may lead to underestimation of comparative safety signals in the treatment arms, given their likely higher use in PLC groups; and (vi) the limited reflection of the actual burden of drug-related AEs on individual patients, which extends beyond a simple dichotomization into non-severe and severe events, and will depend on personal perception, preference and the clinical context of disease severity; dimensions that are currently not captured in clinical trials. While challenges and inherent limitations to safety analyses are numerous, they should not discourage continuous methodological efforts to further elucidate this important topic, as well as critical evaluation of current reporting to improve safety assessments. Future research will aim to extend beyond the PLC-controlled period of clinical trials, and include head-to-head studies, LTE and registry data, although confounding will remain challenging.

To summarize, we here provide a ‘safety gestalt’ for currently licensed RA DMARDs, that allows the assessment of differences in safety signals across compounds and serves as a transparent benchmark for contextualizing new therapies within an established safety landscape. This supports clearer evaluation of benefit–risk profiles on a study level and advances the understanding of drug safety in RA clinical trials and beyond.
