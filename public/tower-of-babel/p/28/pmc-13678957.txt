Impact of the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes on oral health-related quality of life: roles of disaster exposure, anxiety, and clinical safety concerns
Authors: Ahmet Tohumcu, Serhat Efeoglu, Karahan Ucar, Ercan Ucar, Halil Ibrahim Durmus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe February 6, 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquake sequence was associated with widespread disruption across eleven provinces in Turkey. Studies examining earthquake exposure, psychological responses, oral health behaviors, and oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) in adult survivors remain scarce. This study aimed to evaluate these associations.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 284 adult patients attending Adıyaman University Faculty of Dentistry. Data collected via online questionnaire assessed earthquake exposure (EEI), retrospectively assessed pre- and post-earthquake anxiety (EA), clinical safety concerns (CSCS-3), oral health behaviors (HU-DBI), and OHRQoL (OHIP-14).ResultsRetrospectively reported post-earthquake anxiety scores were significantly different from retrospectively reported pre-earthquake scores (17.70 vs. 14.92; p < 0.001). EEI, pre-earthquake anxiety, post-earthquake anxiety, and CSCS-3 were positively correlated with OHIP-14 (r = 0.283, 0.261, 0.159, and 0.276; all p ≤ 0.007). The HU-DBI showed low internal consistency (α = 0.544), limiting interpretability of oral health behavior findings. Chronic physical disease was associated with worse OHRQoL in both bivariate and multivariate analyses, whereas older age showed an association only in bivariate analyses. Although nominal sex differences were observed in post-earthquake anxiety and OHIP-14 scores, these differences did not retain statistical significance after false discovery rate correction.ConclusionsIn bivariate analyses, earthquake exposure, anxiety measures, and clinical safety concerns were each significantly correlated with worse OHRQoL. However, in hierarchical regression analysis, only chronic physical disease (β = 0.182, p = 0.006) and clinical safety concerns (CSCS-3, β = 0.165, p = 0.018) remained significant independent predictors. Pre-earthquake anxiety (β = 0.112, p = 0.081), post-earthquake anxiety (β = 0.045, p = 0.512), and earthquake exposure (β = 0.077, p = 0.227) were not independently associated with OHRQoL. Psychiatric disorder alone was not associated with OHRQoL (β = 0.034, p = 0.572). These findings suggest that integrating dental care with psychological support may improve oral health-related quality of life among disaster survivors, particularly those with chronic physical diseases. Because participants were recruited from a single dental clinic, the findings are most applicable to individuals who had already sought dental care, and the potential influence of clinical safety concerns on dental attendance in the wider survivor population cannot be determined. Furthermore, the findings related to the newly developed CSCS-3 should be regarded as preliminary and hypothesis-generating until confirmed through external validation.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09327-x.Keywords: Earthquake, Oral health-related quality of life, Anxiety, Natural disaster, Dental care, Public health

Background

The February 6, 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquake sequence, one of the most destructive seismic events in Turkey’s recorded history, caused extensive physical destruction across eleven provinces and profoundly disrupted living conditions, the continuity of health services, and psychosocial wellbeing [1]. In the aftermath of major disasters, elevated prevalence of anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress symptoms has been consistently documented, with risk substantially heightened among those experiencing bereavement, displacement, economic loss, and reduced access to essential services [1, 2]. Beyond acute psychological morbidity, these burdens have been shown to influence health-related behaviors and healthcare-seeking patterns in post-disaster populations [3].

Disasters also intensify pre-existing social determinants of health, including disrupted housing, forced relocation, income loss, and restricted access to basic goods and services. Following the 2023 Turkey–Syria earthquakes, compromised healthcare access among vulnerable groups and the need for service prioritization were prominently documented [3]. Within this context, oral health, which is frequently treated as secondary in disaster response frameworks, represents an integral component of overall health and quality of life. The World Health Organization recognizes oral health as a key indicator of general health and wellbeing (WHO, n.d.), and its neglect during disaster periods may carry long-term consequences for affected populations [4].

Maintaining oral health requires consistent oral hygiene practices, access to dental care products, and timely utilization of professional services. Disaster conditions, including restricted water access, displacement, and logistical constraints, can disrupt oral hygiene routines and impede care access [5]. In the aftermath of the 2023 earthquakes, reductions in toothbrushing frequency were observed among affected children, with greater decreases among those who experienced displacement [6]. Evidence from other disaster contexts similarly suggests that oral health behaviors are vulnerable to disruption, although data from adult populations remain limited.

The impact of oral health on daily functioning extends beyond clinical indicators to encompass functional, emotional, and social dimensions, collectively captured by the concept of oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL). The Oral Health Impact Profile-14 (OHIP-14) is among the most widely validated instruments for assessing OHRQoL and has been validated in Turkish populations [7, 8]. Post-disaster deterioration in OHRQoL has been reported in several studies, though the pathways linking disaster exposure to diminished oral health-related wellbeing, including the possible roles of anxiety and disrupted care-seeking, remain incompletely understood.

Psychological responses to earthquakes extend beyond generalized anxiety to include disaster-specific fears that may influence health service utilization. Persistent fear of aftershocks and avoidance of enclosed spaces represent well-documented behavioral sequelae of seismic trauma, conceptualized within cognitive models of PTSD as safety-seeking responses to perceived ongoing threat [9, 10]. Such concerns may be particularly salient in dental contexts, where appointments require extended time in enclosed spaces. Examining these concerns alongside objective exposure indicators may offer a more complete account of how disasters affect oral health behaviors and OHRQoL.

Despite growing recognition of disasters’ broad health consequences, studies simultaneously examining earthquake exposure, psychological responses, oral health behaviors, and OHRQoL remain scarce; methodological heterogeneity and context-specific limitations further constrain generalizability [11]. To address this gap, the present cross-sectional study evaluated the associations among earthquake exposure, clinical safety concerns, earthquake anxiety, oral health behaviors (HU-DBI), and OHRQoL (OHIP-14) in adult survivors of the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Faculty of Dentistry, Adıyaman University, located in one of the provinces most severely affected by the earthquake sequence. Ethical approval was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of Adıyaman University Faculty of Dentistry (Approval No: 2026/1–2; Date: 19 January 2026), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants; data were collected anonymously.

Participants

The study population comprised adult patients attending Adıyaman University Faculty of Dentistry between January 2026 and March 2026. Data were collected via an online questionnaire administered through Google Forms. Inclusion criteria were: age ≥ 18 years; presence in the earthquake-affected region on February 6, 2023; attendance at Adıyaman University Faculty of Dentistry; and provision of electronic informed consent with complete questionnaire responses. Exclusion criteria were: age < 18 years; absence from the earthquake-affected region at the time of the earthquakes; failure to provide informed consent; incomplete or internally inconsistent responses; and duplicate entries. A total of 284 participants met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled.

Instruments and measurements

Participants provided sociodemographic information including age, sex, educational attainment, income level, chronic disease status, and province of residence at the time of the earthquake.

Earthquake exposure was assessed using the Earthquake Exposure Index (EEI), a six-item composite instrument constructed for the present study, with item domains drawn from the disaster exposure literature [2]. Five items covering housing damage, bereavement, displacement, income loss, and difficulty accessing healthcare services reflect domains consistently documented as correlates of postdisaster psychological outcomes across 160 disaster samples [2]. A sixth item assessing difficulty accessing oral hygiene products was added to capture a disaster-specific barrier relevant to the study’s oral health focus. Consistent with Norris et al. (2002), items were summed to create a composite exposure score on the basis that increasing numbers of stressors are associated with increasing symptom severity [2]. Housing damage was scored as none, partial, or severe/total collapse (0–2); bereavement as yes or no (0–1); displacement as none, temporary, or permanent (0–2); income loss as none, some, or substantial (0–2); difficulty accessing healthcare services as none, some, or substantial (0–2); and difficulty accessing oral hygiene products as none, some, or substantial (0–2). Total scores ranged from 0 to 11, with higher scores indicating greater exposure. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha.

The EEI items were generated based on the disaster exposure domains identified by Norris et al. (2002), including housing damage, bereavement, displacement, income loss, and healthcare access difficulty [2]. An additional item on access to oral hygiene products was added to reflect the study’s oral health focus. Content validity was ensured through domain saturation from established literature. No formal expert panel was convened; this is acknowledged as a limitation. The EEI is conceptualized as a formative composite index rather than a reflective scale. Therefore, high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) is not expected, as items represent heterogeneous disaster stressors that are not required to covary. The reported α = 0.624 is acceptable for such formative measures.

Earthquake-related anxiety was assessed using six items selected from the nine-item Earthquake Anxiety Scale developed by Gül (2024) [12], reflecting core anxiety symptoms in the context of earthquake fear (palpitations, fear of death, functional impairment, avoidance of buildings, inability to perform daily activities, and social triggering of anxiety). Items assessing imagery of building collapse and informing family members about earthquakes were excluded as they were considered less directly reflective of the anxiety construct under investigation. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), yielding a total score ranging from 6 to 30; higher scores indicate greater anxiety [12]. Participants completed the scale twice: retrospectively for the three-month period preceding the earthquake (EA-Pre), and for the one-month period following the earthquake (EA-Post). It is important to emphasize that both assessments were obtained retrospectively during the same post-earthquake data collection session in 2026; neither measure reflects contemporaneous anxiety at the time of assessment. Because prospective pre-disaster data collection is not feasible given the unpredictable nature of seismic events, retrospective pre-earthquake assessment represents a recognized methodological approach in post-disaster research; however, its susceptibility to recall bias should be noted in interpreting pre-post comparisons. The comparison between EA-Pre and EA-Post should therefore be interpreted as a contrast between two retrospectively recalled anxiety states rather than a direct measure of within-person change over time.

Perceptions of safety in the dental clinical environment following the earthquake were assessed using the Clinical Safety Concern Scale (CSCS-3), a 3-item instrument constructed for the present study, as no validated measure of earthquake-specific clinical safety concerns existed in the literature at the time of data collection. Item content was grounded in established post-earthquake behavioral phenomena, including persistent fear of aftershocks as a key predictor of PTSD [9] and safety-seeking behaviors such as avoidance of enclosed spaces as recognized responses to seismic trauma [10]. Items assessed avoidance of dental appointments due to building safety concerns, anxiety regarding aftershocks during treatment, and preference for ground-floor clinics when scheduling appointments. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater concern. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha.

The CSCS-3 items were derived from post-earthquake behavioral phenomena documented in the literature, including persistent fear of aftershocks [9] and safety-seeking behaviors such as avoidance of enclosed spaces [10]. The three items were designed to capture earthquake-specific concerns relevant to the dental clinical environment. Face validity was assessed informally by the research team. The scale demonstrated high internal consistency (α = 0.850); however, no formal psychometric validation (e.g., exploratory factor analysis, construct validity testing, or external criterion validation) was conducted, and the CSCS-3 should therefore be regarded as a preliminary, study-specific instrument rather than a validated measure.

To evaluate the factor structure of the three-item Clinical Safety Concern Scale (CSCS-3), a principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was 0.74, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ²(3) = 482.1, p < 0.001), confirming that the correlation matrix was suitable for factor analysis. The analysis extracted a single component with an eigenvalue of 2.29, accounting for 76.4% of the total variance. Factor loadings for the three items ranged from 0.84 to 0.90 (Item 1: 0.90; Item 2: 0.84; Item 3: 0.87), indicating strong convergence on a single underlying construct. These findings provide preliminary evidence for the unidimensional structure of the CSCS-3 and support the use of a total sum score in the present study.

Oral health-related attitudes and behaviors were assessed using an 8-item version of the Hiroshima University Dental Behavioural Inventory (HU-DBI) [13]. Items are scored dichotomously according to a validated scoring key, with a total range of 0–8; higher scores indicate more positive oral health attitudes and behaviors. The Turkish version of the HU-DBI, whose cross-linguistic equivalence was established through forward-back translation and test-retest reliability in bilingual participants, was used [14].

OHRQoL was assessed using the Turkish version of the OHIP-14 validated by Balcı et al. (2017) [7], following the original 14-item structure of Slade (1997) [15]. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = never, 4 = very often), yielding a total score of 0–56; higher scores indicate greater negative impact on OHRQoL. In accordance with the original validation protocol, no items were reverse-scored [15]. Neither the original Slade (1997) OHIP-14 nor the Turkish adaptation by Balcı et al. (2017) specifies reverse-scoring for Item 5, and the negative item-total correlation is consistent with a scoring artifact rather than an expected psychometric property of the instrument. We therefore also report a sensitivity analysis excluding Item 5 entirely (13-item version; see Section "Hierarchical regression analysis and sensitivity analysis"); the principal findings were robust to this alternative scoring approach.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Prior to analysis, the dataset was screened for missing, erroneous, and inconsistent responses; variables were recoded as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages; continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation with minimum–maximum ranges. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, skewness–kurtosis values, and graphical methods. For the paired pre- versus post-earthquake anxiety comparison, both parametric (paired-samples t-test) and non-parametric (Wilcoxon signed-rank test) results are reported in parallel. For the demographic subgroup comparisons in Table 7, parametric tests (independent-samples t-test, one-way ANOVA) were used; given the sample size (n = 284, with most subgroup cells exceeding 20–30 participants), parametric tests are generally considered robust to moderate departures from normality under the central limit theorem. We acknowledge this as a methodological choice rather than a strict assumption-driven decision, and findings from Table 7 should accordingly be interpreted as exploratory. Internal consistency was evaluated with Cronbach’s alpha.

Differences between retrospectively assessed pre-earthquake and post-earthquake anxiety scores were analyzed using paired-samples t-test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test. For two-group comparisons, independent-samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used as appropriate; for three or more groups, one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test was applied, with post-hoc analyses where applicable. Associations between categorical variables were examined using Pearson’s chi-square test with Cramer’s V as the effect size measure. Correlations between scale scores were assessed using Pearson or Spearman coefficients based on normality assumptions. To examine the independent contributions of retrospectively assessed pre-earthquake anxiety (EA-Pre), earthquake exposure (EEI), post-earthquake anxiety (EA-Post), and clinical safety concerns (CSCS-3) to OHIP-14 scores after controlling for demographic variables, a four-block hierarchical multiple regression analysis was conducted. Demographic variables (age, sex, education, income, chronic physical disease, and psychiatric disorder) were entered in Block 1, EA-Pre in Block 2, EEI in Block 3, and EA-Post together with CSCS-3 in Block 4. Collinearity diagnostics (tolerance, variance inflation factor [VIF], and condition index) were examined for all predictors retained in the final model. To assess the stability of the regression estimates and examine the influence of including retrospectively assessed pre-earthquake anxiety (EA-Pre), collinearity diagnostics were examined for models both with and without EA-Pre. In the model without EA-Pre, VIF values ranged from 1.092 to 1.493 for all predictors, with a maximum condition index of 16.42. In the full model including EA-Pre, VIF values remained within acceptable limits (range: 1.092–2.009; maximum condition index: 16.85), indicating that the inclusion of EA-Pre did not introduce problematic multicollinearity. An a priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power 3.1. For a two-tailed Pearson correlation with a medium effect size of r = 0.25, α = 0.05, and power = 0.80, the required sample size was calculated as 95 participants. To account for potential incomplete or invalid responses, we aimed to recruit at least 280 participants. The final sample of 284 participants exceeded this requirement. For the primary hierarchical regression analysis, a post-hoc power calculation was also performed. With 14 predictors in the final model, a sample of 284 participants provides 80% power to detect an R² increment of approximately 0.04 (f² = 0.036) at α = 0.05. The observed R² increments in Blocks 3 and 4 were 0.013 and 0.020, respectively, indicating that the study had limited power to detect the smaller incremental effects. Consequently, non-significant findings in the later regression blocks, particularly the attenuation of EA-Pre and EA-Post, should be interpreted with appropriate caution, and the absence of a statistically significant association should not be equated with absence of a true effect. A sensitivity analysis was also conducted excluding OHIP-14 Item 5 due to its negative corrected item-total correlation (r = -0.36). For the demographic subgroup comparisons reported in Table 7, Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied across the full set of p values to account for multiple comparisons, and findings are interpreted with reference to the FDR-adjusted q values. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses. Age was entered into the hierarchical regression model as a continuous variable (years). Sex, education level, income level, chronic physical disease, and psychiatric disorder were entered as categorical variables using dummy coding.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 284 adults participated in the study. The sample was approximately balanced by sex, with 146 male (51.4%) and 138 female (48.6%) participants. The largest age group was 35–44 years (n = 96; 33.8%), followed by 18–24 years (n = 67; 23.6%), ≥ 45 years (n = 61; 21.5%), and 25–34 years (n = 60; 21.1%). The majority held a university degree (n = 111; 39.1%), followed by high school (n = 89; 31.3%), primary/middle school (n = 75; 26.4%), and postgraduate education (n = 9; 3.2%). Most participants reported a middle income level (n = 171; 60.2%), with 20.8% reporting low income and 6.0% high income; 13.0% preferred not to disclose. Chronic disease was present in 33.1% of participants, most commonly cardiovascular disease (12.3%), hypertension (11.6%), and diabetes mellitus (10.2%). The majority resided in Adıyaman at the time of the earthquake (n = 263; 92.6%). Sociodemographic characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Earthquake exposure

The mean EEI score was 4.23 ± 2.38 (range 0–10; Cronbach’s α = 0.624). The EEI’s Cronbach’s α of 0.624 is at the lower boundary of conventionally acceptable reliability. This is expected for composite exposure indices that sum heterogeneous stressors rather than measuring a unidimensional construct. Participants were classified into three exposure groups based on total score: low (0–3; n = 107; 37.7%), moderate (4–7; n = 151; 53.2%), and high (8–10; n = 26; 9.2%). These categories represent researcher-defined score ranges intended to reflect descriptive gradations of exposure severity rather than clinically validated cutpoints. Housing damage was reported by 76.4% at least to some degree; bereavement by 41.9%; displacement by 47.2%. Difficulty accessing healthcare was reported by 55.6%, and difficulty accessing oral hygiene products by 44.4%. Earthquake exposure data are presented in Table 2.

Scale reliability and descriptive statistics

Internal consistency coefficients and descriptive statistics for all scales are presented in Table 3. The Earthquake Anxiety Scale demonstrated excellent reliability at both pre-earthquake (α = 0.942) and post-earthquake (α = 0.968) administrations. The CSCS-3 showed high internal consistency (α = 0.850), and the OHIP-14 demonstrated very high reliability (α = 0.911). The EEI showed marginal internal consistency (α = 0.624), a value at the lower boundary of conventionally acceptable levels for composite exposure indices. The HU-DBI exhibited the lowest reliability (α = 0.544), falling below the conventional threshold of 0.70; this limitation substantially constrains interpretation of findings involving this scale, as reduced reliability attenuates the capacity to detect true associations. Mean retrospectively assessed pre-earthquake anxiety was 14.92 ± 6.88 (range 6–30) and mean post-earthquake anxiety was 17.70 ± 7.95 (range 6–30). Mean HU-DBI score was 4.88 ± 1.87 (range 0–8) and mean OHIP-14 score was 18.60 ± 10.84 (range 1–53).

Pre- versus post-earthquake anxiety

Post-earthquake anxiety scores were significantly higher than retrospectively reported pre-earthquake scores (paired t-test: t(283) = 6.611, p < 0.001; Wilcoxon: Z = -6.025, p < 0.001), indicating that retrospectively assessed post-earthquake anxiety scores were significantly higher than retrospectively recalled pre-earthquake anxiety scores. Given that both assessments were obtained retrospectively during the same post-earthquake session, this difference should be interpreted as evidence of a statistically detectable contrast between two retrospectively recalled anxiety states rather than a direct measure of within-person change over time, as recall bias may have influenced its magnitude (Table 4).

Hierarchical regression analysis and sensitivity analysis

Hierarchical multiple regression analysis was conducted to examine the independent contributions of earthquake exposure, pre- and post-earthquake anxiety, and clinical safety concerns to OHRQoL after controlling for demographic variables. Chronic disease was separated into two categories: chronic physical disease and psychiatric disorder (Table 6).

The inclusion of earthquake exposure (EEI) in Block 3 yielded a modest but significant increment (ΔR² = 0.013, F-change = 4.421, p = 0.036), with total variance reaching 21.4%. Finally, the addition of post-earthquake anxiety (EA-Post) and clinical safety concerns (CSCS-3) in Block 4 produced a significant increment (ΔR² = 0.020, F-change = 3.482, p = 0.032), with the full model explaining 23.4% of the variance in OHIP-14 scores.

In the final model (Block 4), chronic physical disease (β = 0.182, p = 0.006) and CSCS-3 (β = 0.165, p = 0.018) emerged as the only independent predictors of OHIP-14 scores. Although EA-Pre demonstrated a significant bivariate correlation with OHIP-14 (r = 0.261, p < 0.001; Table 5) and was associated with a significant increment in model fit when entered alone (Block 2), its effect was attenuated and did not retain independent significance in the final model (β = 0.112, p = 0.081). Similarly, EA-Post (β = 0.045, p = 0.512) and EEI (β = 0.077, p = 0.227) were not independently associated with OHRQoL.

Importantly, when chronic disease was separated into physical and psychiatric categories, psychiatric disorder alone was not significantly associated with OHIP-14 (β = 0.034, p = 0.572), suggesting that the observed chronic disease effect in previous analyses was primarily driven by physical health conditions rather than pre-existing psychiatric morbidity.

Collinearity diagnostics indicated no evidence of problematic multicollinearity. Tolerance values ranged from 0.498 to 0.916, and variance inflation factor (VIF) values ranged from 1.092 to 2.009. The maximum condition index was 16.85, which was below the commonly accepted threshold, indicating that multicollinearity was unlikely to have affected the regression estimates.

To verify the robustness of our findings, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using a 13-item version of OHIP-14 excluding Item 5, as this item showed a corrected item-total correlation below the conventional threshold (r = -0.36). The 13-item version yielded a higher Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.942 vs. 0.911). Correlations with the other study scales were marginally stronger for most variables (EEI: r = 0.288 vs. 0.283; EA-Post: r = 0.190 vs. 0.159; CSCS-3: r = 0.298 vs. 0.276), while correlations with EA-Pre (r = 0.258 vs. 0.261) and HU-DBI (r = 0.018 vs. 0.012) remained essentially unchanged (Supplementary Table 1).

The hierarchical regression model was also rerun using the 13-item OHIP-14 as the dependent variable. The pattern of significance remained materially unchanged: chronic physical disease (β = 0.179, p = 0.007) and CSCS-3 (β = 0.172, p = 0.014) remained the only significant independent predictors, while EA-Pre (β = 0.108, p = 0.092), EA-Post (β = 0.052, p = 0.450), and EEI (β = 0.071, p = 0.265) did not retain independent significance. The full model R² increased slightly from 0.234 to 0.241 (Supplementary Table 1). These results confirm that the primary findings are robust to the inclusion of Item 5.

Scale scores by demographic variables

Results of demographic comparisons are presented in Table 7. Because multiple subgroup comparisons were performed across the 25 p values in Table 7, a Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied, and interpretation below is based on the FDR-adjusted q values. Male participants showed nominally higher EEI (t(282) = -2.211, p = 0.028), and female participants showed nominally higher post-earthquake anxiety (t(282) = 2.291, p = 0.023) and OHIP-14 scores (t(282) = -2.246, p = 0.025); however, none of these sex differences retained significance after FDR correction (q = 0.063 for all three). No significant sex differences were found for CSCS-3 (p = 0.673, q = 0.731) or HU-DBI (p = 0.873, q = 0.873).

After FDR correction, age group differences remained significant for EEI (q = 0.003) and OHIP-14 (q = 0.021). Tukey HSD post-hoc analysis indicated that EEI scores were significantly higher in the 35–44 and ≥ 45 age groups compared to the 25–34 age group; OHIP-14 scores were significantly higher in the ≥ 45 age group compared to both the 18–24 and 35–44 age groups. Educational attainment remained significantly associated with post-earthquake anxiety after FDR correction (q = 0.021), with Tukey HSD analysis indicating higher scores in the university group compared to the high school group. EEI and OHIP-14 differences by income level did not retain significance after FDR correction (q = 0.060 and q = 0.052, respectively), and the uncorrected pattern observed for OHIP-14 by income should be interpreted with particular caution given that the high-income subgroup comprised only 17 participants (6.0%).

Chronic disease status remained significantly associated with EEI, CSCS-3, and OHIP-14 after FDR correction (all q < 0.001), indicating that participants with chronic disease reported greater earthquake exposure, greater clinical safety concerns, and poorer OHRQoL. The association with post-earthquake anxiety (p = 0.040) did not retain significance after FDR correction (q = 0.084). HU-DBI did not differ significantly by any demographic variable, before or after correction.

Psychometric properties of the CSCS-3

Principal component analysis of the three CSCS-3 items yielded a single component with an eigenvalue of 2.29, explaining 76.4% of the total variance (Table 8). All three items demonstrated strong factor loadings (0.84–0.90), confirming that they measure a coherent underlying construct. The KMO value of 0.74 exceeded the acceptable threshold of 0.60, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ²(3) = 482.1, p < 0.001), indicating that the data were appropriate for factor analysis. These results, together with the high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.850), support the use of the CSCS-3 total score in the present analyses. However, these findings are preliminary, and formal validation in independent samples is required before the scale can be recommended for broader clinical or research application.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study examined the associations among earthquake exposure, earthquake anxiety, clinical safety concerns, oral health behaviors, and OHRQoL in adult survivors of the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes. The principal findings were: (1) post-earthquake anxiety scores were significantly higher than retrospectively reported pre-earthquake scores; (2) earthquake exposure, both anxiety measures, and clinical safety concerns were each significantly and positively correlated with worse OHRQoL; (3) hierarchical regression analysis identified chronic disease and CSCS-3 as the only independent predictors of OHRQoL after controlling for demographic variables, with EEI and EA-Post not retaining independent significance; (4) HU-DBI scores showed no significant association with any other scale; and (5) chronic disease and older age were consistently associated with worse OHRQoL, while income level showed a statistically significant but counterintuitive pattern that warrants cautious interpretation.

Earthquake anxiety: post-earthquake elevation

The statistically significant elevation in post-earthquake relative to retrospectively reported pre-earthquake anxiety is consistent with a substantial body of evidence documenting elevated psychological morbidity following major seismic events. A systematic review and meta-analysis of studies conducted during the first 18 months following the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes reported persistently high levels of probable PTSD, depression, and anxiety among Turkish adult survivors, with one survey conducted 50 days post-earthquake indicating that approximately 69% of respondents exceeded the clinical cutoff for PTSD symptoms [1]. The present finding of higher post-earthquake anxiety in female participants is consistent with this literature, in which women consistently report higher psychological morbidity following major seismic events across diverse cultural contexts [2].

An important methodological consideration warrants explicit attention. In the present study, pre-earthquake anxiety was not measured prospectively; rather, participants completed the Earthquake Anxiety Scale retrospectively for the three-month period preceding the earthquake, during the same post-earthquake assessment session. Retrospective pre-earthquake assessment is a recognized approach in post-disaster research given the inherently unpredictable nature of seismic events [2]. Nevertheless, recall bias remains an unavoidable limitation of such designs. Participants completing the retrospective assessment under conditions of ongoing post-earthquake distress may systematically underestimate, or in some cases overestimate, their pre-earthquake anxiety levels due to mood-congruent memory effects or contrast effects [16, 17]. Accordingly, the paired comparison between EA-Pre and EA-Post should be interpreted as evidence of a statistically detectable difference between two retrospectively recalled anxiety states – anxiety recalled for the post-earthquake period and anxiety recalled for the pre-earthquake baseline – rather than as a direct measure of within-person change. Because both EA-Pre and EA-Post were collected retrospectively during the same 2026 assessment session, neither variable should be characterized as reflecting participants’ current or contemporaneous anxiety state. This distinction carries implications for the strength of causal inference that can be drawn from these data.

That retrospectively reported pre-earthquake anxiety scores were already relatively elevated (mean 14.92 on a 6–30 scale) may reflect genuine pre-existing seismic risk awareness in a region with documented historical earthquake exposure; however, it may also reflect upward telescoping in retrospective recall under conditions of current distress. These competing explanations cannot be adjudicated in the present cross-sectional design, and future studies in earthquake-prone regions would benefit from prospective baseline data collection where feasible.

Earthquake exposure and OHRQoL

The significant positive correlation between the EEI and OHIP-14 scores (r = 0.283, p < 0.001) indicates that greater disaster exposure was associated with worse self-reported OHRQoL. This finding is consistent with evidence from prior major disaster studies. Kishi et al. (2015) documented significant negative effects of the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake and Tsunami on both OHRQoL and psychological distress among survivors, concluding that the disaster’s impact extended to oral health-related dimensions of wellbeing [18]. The mechanisms through which earthquake exposure may impair OHRQoL are likely multifactorial, encompassing direct disruption of dental care access, loss of oral hygiene resources, physical injury, and the amplifying effect of psychological distress on self-perceived oral health. In the present sample, 55.6% of participants reported difficulty accessing healthcare services and 44.4% difficulty accessing oral hygiene products, both of which may have contributed independently to perceived deterioration in oral health-related wellbeing.

Earthquake anxiety, clinical safety concerns, and OHRQoL

Both anxiety measures were significantly correlated with OHIP-14 scores (EA-Pre: r = 0.261, p < 0.001; EA-Post: r = 0.159, p = 0.007), supporting the hypothesis that psychological burden contributes to diminished OHRQoL in disaster contexts. These findings align with the broader literature on anxiety and OHRQoL. Alimoradi et al. (2024) conducted a meta-analysis including 14,419 participants from nine countries and reported that both anxiety and depression were significantly, albeit weakly to moderately, associated with OHRQoL [19]. Obeidat et al. (2025) similarly found a moderately positive correlation between anxiety scores and OHIP-14 in a cross-sectional study across two Arab countries (r = 0.416, p < 0.001) [20].

It is noteworthy that the EA-Post correlation with OHIP-14 (r = 0.159) was substantially weaker than the EA-Pre correlation (r = 0.261), although both measures were obtained retrospectively within the same 2026 assessment session and therefore share an equivalent methodological basis. Because EA-Post is not a measure of current or contemporaneous anxiety, this differential correlation cannot be attributed to EA-Post capturing a more acute or transient state response relative to a trait-level EA-Pre. A more plausible explanation is that shared method variance, recall salience, or differential recall accuracy across the two retrospective time-referents shaped the relative strength of these associations; for instance, participants may recall their pre-earthquake anxiety in a more trait-like, generalized manner, while post-earthquake anxiety recall may be more event-specific and therefore less uniformly elevated across the sample. These competing explanations cannot be disentangled in the present cross-sectional design and warrant attention in future investigations using prospective or ecological momentary assessment methods.

The significant correlation between CSCS-3 and OHIP-14 (r = 0.276, p < 0.001) represents a contextually novel contribution to the disaster oral health literature. Clinical safety concerns, specifically earthquake-related fears regarding building safety and aftershocks in dental settings, were associated with poorer OHRQoL. Hashim et al. (2026) demonstrated in a cross-sectional study of adult dental patients attending an academic dental clinic in the UAE that higher dental anxiety scores were independently associated with poorer OHRQoL after adjustment for age and gender [21]. Similarly, the present findings suggest that earthquake-related clinical safety concerns may be associated with poorer OHRQoL. However, because all participants were recruited from a university dental clinic, the present study cannot determine whether these concerns prevented dental attendance among earthquake survivors in the community. Instead, the findings indicate that such concerns were present even among individuals who sought dental care following the earthquake. Future community-based studies are needed to determine whether earthquake-related safety concerns influence the decision to seek dental treatment.

The behavioral content of the CSCS-3 items reflects well-established post-earthquake psychological phenomena. The three items cover avoidance of buildings due to seismic safety concerns, fear of aftershocks in enclosed clinical spaces, and preference for ground-floor locations. Persistent fear of aftershocks has been identified as the strongest predictor of PTSD development following major seismic events, increasing the likelihood of PTSD diagnosis by more than 57-fold in a six-month prospective study of L’Aquila earthquake survivors [9]. At the behavioral level, avoidance of enclosed spaces and proximity-to-exit preferences represent recognized safety-seeking behaviors in earthquake survivors, conceptualized within Ehlers and Clark’s (2000) cognitive model as maladaptive threat management strategies that maintain perceived danger and perpetuate PTSD and complex PTSD symptoms [10, 22]. That the CSCS-3 captures clinically meaningful variance in OHRQoL is therefore theoretically coherent: the same mechanisms driving avoidance in everyday contexts appear to extend specifically to dental clinical environments, where appointments require extended time in enclosed spaces, which is precisely the condition that triggers safety-seeking behaviors in post-earthquake populations.

It is important to emphasize the preliminary nature of the CSCS-3. Although the scale demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.850), and the principal component analysis provided preliminary support for its unidimensional structure, these findings alone do not establish full construct validity. No external criterion (such as an established dental anxiety or PTSD measure) was administered to assess convergent or discriminant validity, and face validity was evaluated informally by the research team rather than through a structured expert panel or cognitive interviewing with participants. Consequently, the CSCS-3 should be regarded as an exploratory, study-specific instrument, and the observed association between clinical safety concerns and OHRQoL should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Future research should prioritize comprehensive psychometric validation of the CSCS-3, including confirmatory factor analysis in independent samples, assessment of convergent and discriminant validity, and test–retest reliability, before the scale is adopted for broader clinical or research use. The PCA findings provide preliminary support for the unidimensional structure of the CSCS-3, with all three items loading strongly (0.84–0.90) on a single factor explaining 76.4% of the total variance. Together with the high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.850), these findings support the use of the total CSCS-3 score in the present analyses. Nevertheless, additional psychometric evaluation including confirmatory factor analysis in independent samples, assessment of convergent and discriminant validity, and test–retest reliability is required before the instrument can be recommended for broader clinical or research use.

Regarding the clinical significance of the CSCS-3 finding, the unstandardized regression coefficient (B = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.10–0.96) indicates that each one-point increase on the CSCS-3 (range 3–15) is associated with a 0.53-point increase in OHIP-14 score. While this effect size is modest, several considerations are relevant. First, established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) estimates for OHIP-14 vary across populations and contexts; a difference of 2–3 points is often considered clinically meaningful. The maximum potential difference across the CSCS-3 range would be approximately 6.4 points (12 × 0.53), which exceeds typical MCID thresholds. Second, the CSCS-3 captures a specific, potentially modifiable barrier to dental care, suggesting that even modest associations may have practical implications if interventions can successfully address safety concerns. Third, the additional variance explained by CSCS-3 in the regression model was small (ΔR² = 0.020), indicating that clinical safety concerns account for a limited proportion of OHRQoL variance in this sample. This is not unexpected given the multifactorial nature of OHRQoL and the absence of objective clinical oral health measures in the model.

A sensitivity analysis excluding Item 5 was conducted due to its relatively low corrected item-total correlation (r = -0.36). The 13-item version of OHIP-14 yielded a higher internal consistency (α = 0.942 vs. 0.911) and marginally stronger correlations with the other study scales, while the pattern of significance in the regression analysis remained materially unchanged. These findings confirm that the principal results of this study are robust to the exclusion of this item (Supplementary Table 1).

Absence of association between HU-DBI and other scales

A notable finding was that HU-DBI scores showed no significant correlation with any other scale, including OHIP-14 (r = 0.012, p = 0.846), and did not differ by any demographic variable. Several explanations merit consideration.

First, the HU-DBI demonstrated the lowest internal consistency in this sample (α = 0.544). A systematic review of oral health attitude questionnaires reported Cronbach’s alpha values for attitude subscales ranging from 0.55 to 0.81 across instruments [23], placing the present value at the lower boundary of acceptable reliability. It should be noted that the HU-DBI was retained in the present study because no alternative validated Turkish-language oral health behavior instrument was available for this population at the time of data collection, and because documenting its performance in a post-disaster community sample was itself considered informative. Given α = 0.544, the HU-DBI as used here cannot reliably differentiate between individuals with genuinely different oral health attitudes and behaviors; absence of a statistically detectable association is therefore not equivalent to absence of a true association when measurement error is this substantial. Second, the HU-DBI was originally developed and validated primarily in dental student populations; its psychometric performance in community patient samples experiencing acute disaster may diverge from standardization conditions [13]. A ceiling effect is also plausible: clinic-attending patients may represent a sub-population with relatively positive oral health attitudes, compressing score variance.

Third, the absence of association may reflect a genuine attitude–behavior dissociation in the post-disaster context, wherein individuals retain positive oral health attitudes while being structurally unable to act on them due to resource constraints, displacement, and disrupted access, a pattern consistent with attitude–behavior gap frameworks in health psychology [24]. This interpretation remains speculative given the scale’s psychometric limitations noted above, and future studies in post-disaster populations should employ instruments with stronger reliability in community samples before drawing firm conclusions about attitude–behavior relations following disaster exposure.

Chronic disease, age, and income as vulnerability markers

Participants with chronic disease showed significantly worse scores on EEI, post-earthquake anxiety, CSCS-3, and OHIP-14 (all p ≤ 0.040). The association between chronic disease and worse OHRQoL is well-established and reflects the compounding effects of oral manifestations, pharmacological side effects on oral tissues, and heightened psychosocial distress. In post-disaster contexts, individuals with chronic conditions face additional vulnerability through disrupted medication access, interrupted follow-up care, and the physiological and psychological toll of displacement [3].

Older participants (≥ 45 years) showed significantly worse EEI and OHIP-14 scores, consistent with the established literature on age as a determinant of OHRQoL and disaster vulnerability. The income level findings warrant careful interpretation. Post-hoc analysis indicated that the high income group reported significantly higher OHIP-14 scores than the middle income and non-disclosure groups, a pattern that runs counter to the well-established literature associating lower socioeconomic status with worse OHRQoL [2]. However, this finding should not be taken at face value: the high income group comprised only 17 participants (6.0% of the sample), which substantially limits statistical stability and increases the risk of a spurious result. A plausible explanation is that higher-income individuals in this disaster-affected region may have sustained greater material losses relative to their pre-disaster assets including higher-value housing and businesses, or may report oral health concerns more readily due to higher health awareness, inflating OHIP-14 scores independently of actual clinical status. Alternatively, the small cell size may simply be driving a chance finding. Given these constraints, the income-OHRQoL association in this sample cannot be reliably interpreted in either direction, and the finding should not be used to draw conclusions about socioeconomic vulnerability without replication in larger samples with more balanced income representation.

The finding that higher educational attainment was associated with greater post-earthquake anxiety is less immediately intuitive. One plausible explanation is that more educated individuals engage more actively in risk-related information-seeking, which may paradoxically amplify perceived threat under conditions of uncertainty [1]. This interpretation is consistent with risk perception research demonstrating that information access can, under certain conditions, heighten rather than mitigate anxiety [25]. Alternatively, the pattern may partly reflect cohort confounding given the significant association between educational attainment and age in this sample.

Sex differences

Male participants showed nominally higher EEI and OHIP-14 scores, whereas female participants showed nominally higher post-earthquake anxiety scores. However, none of these sex differences remained statistically significant after false discovery rate correction. Although these subgroup differences did not remain statistically significant after false discovery rate correction, they may still be of descriptive interest and warrant cautious interpretation. The nominally higher post-earthquake anxiety scores observed among women are broadly consistent with previous post-disaster mental health research. In contrast, the nominally higher OHIP-14 scores observed among men differ from the pattern reported in many previous studies and should therefore be interpreted cautiously until replicated in independent samples [1, 2]. While evidence on sex differences in OHRQoL is inconsistent across contexts, women more commonly report worse OHRQoL in general population samples, making the present finding somewhat atypical. A plausible explanation is that the higher EEI scores in men reflect greater objective exposure severity in this sample, which may have driven worse self-perceived oral health independently of sex-specific vulnerability. Alternatively, men attending this dental faculty clinic may have presented more frequently with acute dental conditions, inflating OHIP-14 scores independently of earthquake exposure. These competing explanations cannot be disentangled in the present cross-sectional design, and the finding warrants replication before substantive interpretation. It should be noted that this sex difference reflects an unadjusted bivariate subgroup comparison (Table 7); in the final hierarchical regression model, sex was not an independent predictor of OHIP-14 scores after adjustment for other covariates (β=-0.084, p = 0.179).

The role of pre-earthquake anxiety

An important finding that emerged after including retrospectively assessed pre-earthquake anxiety (EA-Pre) in the regression model deserves particular attention. While EA-Pre showed a moderate bivariate correlation with OHIP-14 (r = 0.261, p < 0.001) and contributed significantly to the model when entered alone (Block 2: ΔR² = 0.059, p < 0.001), its independent effect was attenuated and did not retain statistical significance after controlling for earthquake exposure, post-earthquake anxiety, and clinical safety concerns in the full model (β = 0.112, p = 0.081). This attenuation suggests that the association between retrospectively recalled pre-earthquake anxiety and OHRQoL is largely explained by other disaster-related factors, particularly clinical safety concerns, rather than representing a direct, independent pathway.

More importantly, even after controlling for EA-Pre, clinical safety concerns (CSCS-3) remained a significant independent correlate of OHRQoL (β = 0.165, p = 0.018), whereas post-earthquake anxiety (EA-Post) did not (β = 0.045, p = 0.512). This finding supports the study’s central observation: disaster-specific clinical safety concerns, rather than generalized earthquake-related anxiety, are more directly relevant to oral health-related quality of life. The findings suggest that interventions aimed at improving OHRQoL among earthquake survivors may benefit from addressing practical and psychological barriers to dental care utilization, such as concerns about aftershocks and structural safety, in addition to general anxiety symptoms.

The attenuation of EA-Pre’s effect also highlights the importance of distinguishing between trait-like anxiety propensity and disaster-specific psychological responses when examining post-disaster health outcomes. While pre-existing anxiety may contribute to general vulnerability, the present findings suggest that earthquake-related safety concerns specific to the clinical environment represent a more proximal and modifiable determinant of OHRQoL. This distinction has practical implications for post-disaster mental health and dental service planning, as interventions targeting clinical safety concerns may be more effective in improving oral health outcomes than broad-based anxiety reduction programs.

However, the retrospective nature of EA-Pre and EA-Post assessments warrants caution. Both measures were collected during the same 2026 assessment session, introducing the possibility of recall bias. Participants’ recollections of their pre- and post-earthquake anxiety may have been influenced by their current psychological state and mood-congruent memory effects. Moreover, the observed attenuation of EA-Pre and EA-Post in the final regression model may partly reflect a difference in measurement timing: these anxiety measures were retrospectively recalled perceptions of past psychological states collected three years after the disaster, whereas clinical safety concerns (CSCS-3) were assessed with reference to participants’ current perceptions of the dental environment. This distinction may render the contemporaneously measured CSCS-3 more proximally associated with current OHRQoL, rather than indicating that earthquake-related anxiety is intrinsically less relevant to oral health outcomes. Future studies with prospective pre-disaster baseline data and contemporaneous post-disaster assessments are needed to confirm these findings and establish the temporal precedence of these relationships.

The present study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, the sample was drawn from a single dental faculty clinic in Adıyaman, which limits generalizability to non-clinic-attending survivors and other affected provinces. Third, pre-earthquake anxiety was assessed retrospectively, introducing potential recall bias. Fourth, objective clinical oral health data were not collected. Fifth, the HU-DBI showed low internal consistency in this sample (α = 0.544), substantially constraining interpretation of oral health behavior findings. Sixth, the CSCS-3 was developed specifically for this study and, although PCA supported its unidimensional structure and internal consistency was high (α = 0.850), formal psychometric validation including confirmatory factor analysis in independent samples, convergent/discriminant validity testing, and test-retest reliability assessment has not been conducted. Consequently, findings based on this instrument should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating, and replication in independent post-disaster populations is required. Seventh, we separated chronic disease into physical and psychiatric categories, which allowed us to identify that the chronic disease effect was primarily driven by physical conditions. However, the small number of participants with psychiatric disorders (n = 12, 4.2%) limited statistical power to detect associations in this subgroup, and findings regarding psychiatric morbidity should be interpreted with caution. Finally, a particular methodological concern relates to the clinic-based recruitment strategy. Participants were recruited exclusively from a university dental clinic, which necessarily excludes individuals who may have completely avoided seeking dental care because of earthquake-related fear or safety concerns. This is not merely a limitation of generalizability but introduces a specific statistical mechanism collider bias, often referred to as Berkson’s paradox. Because the sample is implicitly conditioned on having sought care, if both greater dental treatment need (reflected by poorer OHIP-14 scores) and lower levels of clinical safety concern independently increase the likelihood of attending a dental clinic, conditioning on clinic attendance can induce a spurious or distorted association between these variables. Under this mechanism, the observed association between CSCS-3 and OHIP-14 in the present clinic-based sample may be attenuated relative to the association that would be observed among the broader population of earthquake survivors, including those who avoided dental care entirely because of safety concerns. Consequently, the present findings are primarily applicable to clinic-attending survivors, and the true burden of earthquake-related clinical safety concerns on OHRQoL may be underestimated in this study.

Importantly, while earthquake exposure and post-earthquake anxiety showed significant bivariate associations with OHRQoL, they were not statistically significant in the final hierarchical regression model after mutual adjustment with CSCS-3 and demographic variables. Collinearity diagnostics indicated that this attenuation is unlikely to reflect problematic multicollinearity, as tolerance and VIF values for all predictors, including EA-Post and CSCS-3, remained within acceptable ranges. Importantly, collinearity diagnostics confirmed that the attenuation of EA-Pre’s effect in the full model was not attributable to multicollinearity, as VIF values remained well below the conventional threshold of 10 in models both with and without EA-Pre. We do not interpret the attenuation of EA-Post as evidence of a mediating mechanism: the cross-sectional design does not establish the temporal ordering required for mediation, and we therefore limit our interpretation to statistical association rather than causal or mediating pathways. Whether clinical safety concerns causally intervene between earthquake exposure and OHRQoL, or instead represent a correlated but distinct disaster-related construct that more directly predicts oral health outcomes, remains an open question that only longitudinal designs with repeated measures can resolve. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting the bivariate findings as direct effects of disaster exposure or anxiety on OHRQoL. The independent correlates in the final model were chronic disease and clinical safety concerns (CSCS-3).

The final hierarchical regression model explained 23.4% of the variance in OHIP-14 scores (R²=0.234; adjusted R²=0.194), indicating that a substantial proportion of variance remains unexplained. Unmeasured factors such as pre-earthquake oral health status, dental pain, tooth loss, social support, and access to dental care after the disaster likely contribute importantly to OHRQoL in this population.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study investigated the associations among earthquake exposure, earthquake anxiety, clinical safety concerns, and OHRQoL in adult survivors of the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes. In bivariate analyses, greater disaster exposure, higher post-earthquake anxiety, and elevated clinical safety concerns were each significantly correlated with worse OHRQoL. However, after adjustment for demographic variables in hierarchical regression, only chronic physical disease and clinical safety concerns (CSCS-3) were statistically significant in the final model. Earthquake exposure and post-earthquake anxiety were not independently associated with OHRQoL after mutual adjustment.

Chronic disease status and older age emerged as consistent vulnerability markers across multiple outcome dimensions. The income level finding was statistically significant but produced a counterintuitive pattern attributable to the small high-income subsample (n = 17), and should be interpreted with caution. Due to the low internal consistency of the HU-DBI (α = 0.544), conclusions regarding oral health behaviors in this sample are limited. Future studies should employ instruments with stronger psychometric properties in post-disaster populations. Because participants were recruited exclusively from a single university dental clinic, these findings apply primarily to earthquake survivors who sought dental care, and generalization to the broader survivor population, including those who avoided care altogether, should be made with caution.

From a public health perspective, these findings underscore the need to integrate dental care services and psychological support into disaster response and recovery frameworks, with particular attention to the material barriers to oral healthcare access and the psychological determinants of care-seeking, including disaster-specific clinical safety concerns, that may drive long-term oral health deterioration following major seismic events. Longitudinal studies with prospective baseline data and objective clinical assessments are warranted to establish causal pathways and inform evidence-based intervention.
