Ferroptosis as a therapeutic target in cancer: mechanisms, immune interactions, and emerging strategies
Authors: Bufu Tang, Yaling Lin, Songhua Cai, Jingqin Ma, Wei Zhang, Tong Jiang, Ruiqing Gao, Yongjie Zhou, Xuran Jin, Yuyu Guan, Wen Zhang, Lingxiao Liu, Zhiping Yan, Peng Luo, Xudong Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ferroptosis is a novel form of programmed cell death defined by iron-dependent, reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated peroxidation of membrane phospholipids containing polyunsaturated fatty acyl chains. Since 2020, research interest in ferroptosis has surged exponentially, outpacing that of other cell death modalities and establishing it as a pivotal regulatory target in oncology. This review offers three key novel contributions compared to prior summaries: first, it systematically integrates the context-dependent roles of ferroptosis either synergistic sensitization or resistance in cancer multi-disciplinary treatment (MDT), including chemotherapy, targeted therapy, radiotherapy, interventional therapy and immunotherapy, with a focus on clinical trial evidence from the past five years; second, it deciphers the dynamic crosstalk between ferroptosis and core components of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), while clarifying the regulatory impacts of microenvironmental factors (pH, hypoxia, gut microbiota); third, it comprehensively summarizes breakthrough advances in nanoplatform based ferroptosis regulation, structured around two complementary perspectives: endogenous TME-responsive systems and exogenous stimulus triggered systems. This review aims to bridge basic mechanisms with clinical applicability, providing a framework for translating ferroptosis targeted strategies into optimized MDT regimens.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12943-026-02590-0.

Introduction

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent, non-apoptotic type of cell death, marked by the peroxidation of membrane PLs with PUFA-PL, a process driven by both iron and ROS [1, 2]. When bis-allylic hydrogen atoms are removed from polyunsaturated fatty acid chains (PUFA) molecules, reactive carbon centers are exposed, making them highly susceptible to reactions that generate large amounts of PUFA-phospholipid hydroperoxides (PUFA-PLOOH). This accumulation ultimately impairs membrane integrity and induces cell death. The initiation of ferroptosis is modulated by a range of factors, including the synthesis of PUFA-containing phospholipids, lipid peroxidation processes, iron handling, and mitochondrial metabolic activity. Several antioxidant defense systems protect against ferroptosis, with glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) serving as the key enzyme through glutathione consumption. Upon depletion of GPX4, other defensive systems—including the CoQH₂ system and the GCH1-BH4 system—are mobilized. The terminal phase of ferroptosis is characterized by plasma membrane damage, underscoring the significance of membrane repair systems. These findings underscore the crucial relationship between metabolism and ferroptosis.

Since 2020–2021, research focusing on ferroptosis, has grown at an exponential rate. This surge has outpaced the research progress seen in necroptosis and pyroptosis, positioning ferroptosis as a rapidly rising and critical regulatory focus in the field of cancer-related cell death [3]. Ferroptosis exhibits notable duality in cancer therapy: while ferroptosis induction effectively eliminates cancer cells, the lipid peroxidation microenvironment mediated by ferroptosis may paradoxically promote immunosuppression and tumor metastasis. This dual regulatory effect is closely associated with tumor heterogeneity, encompassing histological subtypes, molecular classifications, and dynamic characteristics of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) [4].

This review provides an analysis of ferroptosis drivers and defensive barriers, elucidating their crosstalk with key components of the TIME, particularly tumor-associated macrophages(TAMs) and T cell exhaustion. Furthermore, it systematically examines the double-edged effects of ferroptosis in multiple therapeutic modalities, while analyzing the dynamic regulatory influences of microenvironmental factors (pH, hypoxia, and gut microbiota). Finally, based on clinical trial evidence from the past five years, this review focuses on cutting-edge nanomaterial research, summarizing breakthrough advances in ferroptosis regulation through nanoplatforms from two perspectives: endogenous responses and exogenous regulation (Fig. 1).

Molecular mechanisms of ferroptosis

Ferroptosis exhibits unique features at multiple levels [5]. From a biochemical perspective, ferroptosis is driven by membrane-localized lipid peroxidation and characterized by the depletion of antioxidant defense mechanisms. Mechanistically, unlike other forms of cell death which are mediated by specific executor proteins (e.g., caspases in apoptosis), ferroptosis is executed directly by the accumulation of membrane lipid peroxides to a lethal threshold [2]. Morphologically, ferroptotic cells are characterized by mitochondrial shrinkage, increased membrane density, and reduced or absent cristae structures [6] (Fig. 2).

Drivers of ferroptosis

PUFA-PL synthesis and lipid peroxidation

The oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acid chains(PUFAs) represents the central biochemical event in ferroptosis [7]. Oxidized PUFAs alone cannot initiate ferroptosis; their oxidized tails must remain anchored to membrane-bound phospholipids to induce this process. Therefore, ferroptosis is not a general response to oxidative stress, but rather specific to the localized accumulation of lipid peroxides within membranes. PUFAs are embedded into membrane phospholipids through esterification, forming PUFA-PL complexes [8]. The C-H bonds adjacent to the C = C double bond in these phospholipids are highly unstable and susceptible to reactive oxygen species (ROS) attack, triggering lipid peroxidation chain reactions [9, 10]. During the synthesis of PUFA-PL, ACSL4 and LPCAT3 play crucial roles as key enzymes [10]. ACSL4 facilitates the catalysis of long-chain PUFAs’ activation into acyl-CoA; this product thereafter undergoes transfer by LPCAT3 to lysophospholipids, ultimately yielding PUFA-rich phospholipids (such as PE-AA and PE-AdA) [8, 11]. ACSL4 and LPCAT3 are therefore crucial, as their inactivation can suppress or attenuate ferroptosis [12]. However, Chu et al. discovered that ALOX12 influences p53-mediated ferroptosis through an ACSL4-independent pathway, though the exact mechanism remains unclear [13]. These PUFA-PLs, under the action of iron-dependent lipoxygenases or non-enzymatic Fenton reactions, generate lipid peroxidation products, ultimately compromising cell membrane integrity [14] (Fig. 2).

Iron metabolism

Cellular iron uptake and storage play crucial roles in ferroptosis [15]. Cells primarily acquire iron through transferrin/transferrin receptor-mediated endocytosis [16]. Ferric ions (Fe³⁺) in the blood associate with transferrin and gain entry into cells through TfR1, at which point they undergo reduction to ferrous ions (Fe²⁺) [17]. These ferrous ions are subsequently translocated by SLC11A2 into the intracellular cytoplasmic LIP. Excess iron ions are sequestered within ferritin, thereby safeguarding against iron-induced cytotoxicity [18]. Iron metabolism promotes ferroptosis through two major mechanisms: (1) as a catalyst in non-enzymatic Fenton reactions that directly promote PUFA-PL peroxidation; (2) as an essential cofactor for lipid peroxidation enzymes. Iron accumulation through both pathways leads to lipid peroxidation and ultimately ferroptosis [19, 20]. As demonstrated by Hou et al., autophagy leads to the degradation of ferritin, resulting in the release of iron ions and disruption of cellular iron metabolism. These free iron ions catalyze the conversion of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) into highly reactive hydroxyl radicals via the Fenton reaction, driving the process of lipid peroxidation and ultimately causing damage to the cell membrane structure and inducing ferroptosis [21]. In the enzymatic lipid peroxidation of PUFA-PLs, ALOXs and cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase (POR) serve as two key catalytic enzymes [22]. Among them, lipoxygenases directly catalyze the incorporation of molecular oxygen into membrane-bound polyunsaturated fatty acids or PUFA-containing lipids [23]. Wang et al. found that EBF3 overexpression suppresses acute myeloid leukemia growth by promoting ferroptosis [24]. In melanoma studies, they also demonstrated that ALOX5 upregulation promotes autophagy-dependent ferroptosis [25]. The POR P450 electron transfer system constitutes an ALOX independent pathway that drives ferroptosis. Specifically, P450 enzymes can directly catalyze the peroxidation of membrane PUFA-PLs, serving as a significant alternative source of lipid peroxides [22, 26].

Mitochondria and ferroptosis

During ferroptosis, mitochondria play crucial roles not only through morphological changes but also through several functional aspects [27]. Mitochondria are the primary sites for the generation of ROS within cells. During the electron transport process in the mitochondrial respiratory chain, complexes I and III act as critical points where electron leakage occurs. This leads to the single-electron reduction of oxygen molecules, resulting in the production of O₂•⁻. This process is considered a major source of intracellular oxidative stress [20]. These superoxide anions are rapidly converted to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). These species, collectively known as mitochondrial ROS, participate in Fenton reactions with labile iron, generating products that promote PUFA-PL peroxidation and subsequent ferroptosis [28]. Second, mitochondrial energy sensing functions have bidirectional effects on ferroptosis: AMPK regulates ferroptosis through ACC phosphorylation and inactivation, while also inhibiting ferroptosis by suppressing fatty acid biosynthesis [29, 30]. Third, mitochondria contain crucial apoptotic regulators, including the VDAC family and BCL2 family members, both of which play significant roles in ferroptosis regulation [31]. VDACs are central regulators of mitochondrial membrane permeability. VDAC opening enhances mitochondrial membrane permeability, leading to glutathione (GSH) depletion and ROS accumulation, thereby accelerating ferroptosis [32]. Within the BCL2 family, anti-apoptotic members like BCL2 inhibit mitochondrial membrane permeabilization, indirectly reducing ferroptosis sensitivity, while pro-apoptotic proteins like BAK/BAX promote ferroptosis through mitochondrial ROS generation [33]. Furthermore, cysteine-induced ferroptosis requires glutamine degradation, which provides α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) [34]. α-KG supports the mitochondrial TCA cycle, consistent with mitochondrial involvement in ferroptosis [35]. Finally, DHODH exerts a protective effect against ferroptosis. It achieves this by reducing dihydroorotate and ubiquinone, converting them to orotate and CoQH₂ respectively [36].

Defense mechanisms against ferroptosis

GPX4 antioxidant system

The core defense mechanism against ferroptosis relies on the activity of GPX4, whose functional state is directly regulated by the intracellular concentration of GSH [37]. GSH biosynthesis is accomplished through an ATP-dependent condensation reaction using glutamate, cysteine, and glycine as substrates [38]. This synthesis pathway is subject to multiple layers of precise regulation, including the availability of cysteine, the activity of the rate-limiting enzyme GCL, and feedback inhibition by GSH itself [39]. Cysteine is primarily sourced through two major pathways: endogenous generation via the transsulfuration pathway and uptake from the extracellular environment via the system xc⁻ cystine/glutamate antiporter. Composed of two subunits, SLC7A11 and SLC3A2, system xc⁻ is primarily responsible for transporting extracellular cystine into cells, where it is subsequently reduced to cysteine—a key precursor for GSH synthesis [40–42]. Within the transsulfuration pathway, cystathionine γ-lyase (CTH/CGL) catalyzes the breakdown of cystathionine to generate cysteine, a process under the rate-limiting control of cysteinyl-tRNA synthetase (CARS1/CARS). As a unique selenoenzyme, GPX4 specifically catalyzes the reduction of toxic PLOOH into non-toxic phospholipid alcohols, thereby effectively inhibiting lipid peroxidation-induced cell death [43]. The molecular mechanism involves the oxidation of the selenol group (–SeH) at the enzyme’s active site by PLOOH, generating selenenic acid (–SeOH). This intermediate then reacts with GSH to form a seleno-glutathione conjugate (–Se–SG), while simultaneously producing oxidized glutathione (GSSG), completing the antioxidant cycle. Although all GPX4 isoforms originate from the same gene, alternative transcription initiation gives rise to three distinct forms localized in the mitochondria, cytosol, and nucleus [44]. Accumulating evidence indicates that cytosolic GPX4 serves as a central player in ferroptosis by decreasing lipid peroxides [40]. Deletion of mitochondrial GPX4 in murine models causes male infertility, underscoring its essential contribution to reproductive function [45]. Nuclear GPX4 may protect nuclear membranes and DNA from oxidative damage, though its potential relationship with ferroptosis remains less studied [46]. GPX4 depletion alone is not a definitive indicator of ferroptosis, as it is also implicated in alternative cell death pathways, thereby requiring supplementary markers for accurate identification. In the application of this mechanism, Erastin and RSL3 serve as representative compounds. Erastin inhibits cystine transport through system xc-, while RSL3 inhibits GPX4 activity, both promoting ferroptosis [47]. At present, some studies have been conducted on drugs targeting the GPX4 or GPX4-GSH axis. For instance, Yu et al. screened a natural product, acetylformate (ACE), which targets PCBP1/2 and inhibits GPX4 to induce ferroptosis in cancer cells [48]. Notably, a class of compounds known as RAS selective lethal agents, which includes the System Xc- inhibitor IKE (an erastin derivative), operate by depleting cellular cysteine and consequently GSH, leading to GPX4 inactivation [49] (Fig. 3, Table 1).

Substances that regulate ferroptosis

The CoQH2 system

Some cancer cells survive even after GPX4 depletion, indicating alternative ferroptosis defense mechanisms. The CoQH2 system primarily comprises two subsystems: Based on subcellular localization, these can be divided into the mitochondrial DHODH/CoQH2 system and the non-mitochondrial FSP1/CoQH2 system [50, 51].

In the context of ferroptosis, FSP1 mediates cellular protection through distinct mechanisms. It operates primarily through two pathways: first, as a vitamin K reductase, converting vitamin K to its corresponding hydroquinone form to inhibit lipid peroxidation; second, using N-myristoylation and NAD(P)H as substrates to reduce ubiquinone to ubiquinol (CoQH2) [52–54]. Derived from the mevalonate pathway, ubiquinol plays a dual role by facilitating mitochondrial respiration and exerting antioxidant effects within cellular membranes [55]. Other mevalonate metabolites also play roles in ferroptosis, such as IPP serving as a precursor for GPX4 and CoQ10 synthesis, while squalene synthase can accumulate ROS [36]. Initially, FSP1 was defined as a downstream target of p53 and hypothesized to function as a tumor suppressor [56]. But subsequent studies demonstrated that its role in p53-mediated tumor suppression was relatively minor. For example, silencing AIFM2 in murine erythroleukemia models failed to induce notable changes in cell death levels [57].

Functionally, the subcellular compartmentalization of these antioxidant systems is critical. Studies have demonstrated that mitochondrial GPX4 and DHODH exhibit functional redundancy in limiting mitochondrial lipid peroxidation, whereas their cytosolic counterparts, GPX4 and FSP1, lack this compensatory capacity, thereby emphasizing the critical role of subcellular compartmentalization [58]. This highlights the specific importance of the mitochondrial DHODH/CoQH₂ axis. Dihydroorotate dehydrogenase is essential for the de novo synthesis of pyrimidine nucleotides [59, 60]. By catalyzing the oxidation of dihydroorotate (DHO) to orotate (OA) coupled with the reduction of coenzyme Q (CoQ) to its reduced form (CoQH₂), DHODH contributes to the suppression of mitochondrial lipid peroxidation [61]. According to Alvan Amos et al., DHODH influences ferroptosis through two main mechanisms: First, complementing GPX4 beyond their shared mitochondrial localization [62]. Studies show that treating cancer cells with GPX4 inhibitors leads to N-carbamoyl-L-aspartate depletion and uridine accumulation, which are DHODH’s upstream and downstream products respectively. Second, through deoxynucleotide biosynthesis, where purine-pyrimidine pairing is crucial for nucleoside triphosphate (NTP) synthesis, with deficiency in either potentially terminating base pairing [63, 64]. The role of DHODH extends beyond ferroptosis defense into core tumor metabolism. DHODH’s relationship with tumor cells is complex, involving mitochondrial ETC complex III [65]. Tumor cells require substantial pyrimidine supply during S-phase, obtainable only through de novo synthesis, where DHODH is the rate-limiting enzyme. Additionally, DHODH reduces CoQ to CoQH2, which transfers electrons to complex III in the ETC [66]. Complex III inhibitors can thus block electron flow through the ETC and inhibit DHO oxidation to OA by DHODH, leading to disrupted cell growth and tumor suppression [67]. For example, antimycin A inhibits AML cell proliferation by suppressing mitochondrial ETC complex III [68]. In summary, the CoQH₂ based antioxidant systems, comprising the extra-mitochondrial FSP1 and the mitochondrial DHODH, constitute a critical and parallel layer of ferroptosis defense that operates independently of GPX4, enabling cancer cell survival even when the canonical GPX4-GSH axis is compromised.

GCH1–BH4 system

Tetrahydrobiopterin serves as a cofactor for aromatic amino acid hydroxylases and other enzymes, while also functioning as a lipid peroxyl radical scavenger [69]. In biosynthetic pathways, BH4 participates in the synthesis of various neurotransmitters, including dopamine, melatonin, and norepinephrine [70]. However, whether the BH4-dependent biosynthesis of endogenous dopamine and melatonin modulates ferroptotic pathways remains unclear. GCH1 helps cells evade ferroptosis by producing BH4, which is oxidized to BH2, leading to NO synthase uncoupling and subsequently inducing lipid remodeling to suppress ferroptosis [71]. Unlike the previously described systems, the subcellular compartmentalization of the GCH1-BH4 system awaits further experimental clarification.

The membrane repair system

The exact location of ferroptotic lipid peroxidation remains unclear [72]. Using bioorthogonal imaging techniques and the fluorescent lipid peroxidation probe LiperFluo, researchers discovered that lipid peroxidation primarily accumulates in the endoplasmic reticulum, with additional distribution in mitochondria and lysosomes [34, 73]. This suggests that the plasma membrane and various organelle membranes may be sites of ferroptotic lipid peroxidation, representing a dynamic process involving multiple cellular membrane systems [74]. During the terminal phase of ferroptosis, plasma membrane damage occurs, with the ESCRT complex-mediated membrane budding repair mechanism playing a crucial role. Within the ESCRT machinery, ESCRT-III proteins serve as the primary functional components [75]. When cells undergo ferroptosis, small nanopores open, leading to elevated intracellular Ca²⁺ levels. In response, ESCRT-III subunits, particularly charged multivesicular body proteins (CHMPs), accumulate at damage sites and facilitate repair [76]. This constitutes the primary repair mechanism. For instance, in pancreatic cancer studies, ferroptosis activators erastin and RSL3 induced CHMP accumulation in cancer cell membranes, while CHMP knockdown enhanced cancer cell susceptibility to ferroptosis [72].

Ferroptosis and cancer biology

The role of ferroptosis in the three phases of cancer immunoediting

Elimination phase: immune system attacks via ferroptosis

The dynamic crosstalk between tumors and the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) can be framed within the “Three Es” of immunoediting (Elimination, Equilibrium, Escape), with ferroptosis playing a crucial modulating role [77–80] (Fig. 4).

In the escape phase, Tregs, MDSCs, and TAMs release suppressive cytokines and VEGF, compromising T cell and NK cell functions and facilitating tumor immune escape and ferroptosis resistance

During the “Elimination” phase, innate and adaptive immune systems synergize to eliminate tumor cells, with ferroptosis serving as a critical crosstalk hub between immune cells and tumor cells [81, 82]. CD8 + T cells exhibit significant crosstalk with tumor cell ferroptosis: they secrete IFN-γ, which suppresses SLC3A2 and SLC7A11 transcription and expression via the Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) pathway, thereby sensitizing tumor cells to ferroptosis [83, 84]. Conversely, ferroptosis related lipid metabolism in tumor microenvironment (TME) affects CD8 + T cell function: arachidonic acid (AA), an ω−6 polyunsaturated fatty acid transported by CD36, induces ferroptosis in CD8 + T cells, reducing the secretion of IFNγ, TNFα, and perforin (a key effector molecule for tumor cell apoptosis) and ultimately impairing their anti-tumor activity [85, 86]. M1 macrophages promote tumor elimination through ferroptosis-dependent mechanisms: first, they secrete IL-6, which binds to transmembrane iron exporters via the JAK/STAT1 pathway to promote hepcidin production and iron accumulation, and simultaneously activates extracellular regulated protein kinase (EPK) signaling to downregulate xCT; [87–89] Second, they activate CD8 + T cells to induce tumor cell ferroptosis. These mechanisms collectively sensitize tumors to oxidative stress, activating tumor elimination immune responses in the tumor microenvironment(TME) [90, 91]. DCs primarily regulate anti-tumor immunity through antigen presentation, but this function is impaired by tumor derived ferroptosis-related factors: ROS and lipid peroxidation byproduct 4-HNE induce endoplasmic reticulum stress, upregulate X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1) levels, and peroxisome proliferator activated receptor gamma (PPARG)-mediated ferroptosis in lipid metabolism directly impedes DC maturation [92–95]. B cells possess the ability to differentiate into plasma cells and directly secrete cytokines to kill tumor cells [96]. Currently, ferroptosis-mediated crosstalk in B cells hasn’t been extensively studied. Compared to follicular B2 cells, marginal zone B cells and B1 cells are more susceptible to GPX4 inhibition-induced ferroptosis [97]. During the Elimination phase, ferroptosis acts as a direct anti-tumor weapon of adaptive immunity—CD8 + T cells and M1 macrophages induce tumor ferroptosis via specific signaling pathways, while tumor derived ferroptosis related factors impair DC anti-tumor function. A critical unknown is how the balance between pro-ferroptotic signaling and the counterproductive damage to immune cells is spatially and temporally regulated within the TIME. Additionally, B cell-ferroptosis crosstalk in tumor elimination remains understudied, with specific regulatory mechanisms to be explored.

During the “Equilibrium” phase, sustained immune pressure drives tumors to adapt by acquiring resistance to immune-mediated ferroptosis, primarily through reshaping the tumor microenvironment (TME). To evade T cell attack, tumors employ broad immunosuppressive strategies, such as downregulating antigen presentation and secreting inhibitory cytokines (e.g., TGF-β, IL-10) [98–100]. A more ferroptosis specific adaptation involves the reprogramming of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). Tumors promote the polarization of TAMs towards an M2 phenotype, which actively protects tumor cells from ferroptosis [101]. M2 TAMs suppress ferroptosis by downregulating T cell recruiting chemokines (e.g., CXCL9/10/12) and upregulating PD-L1 on tumor cells [102, 103]. Mechanistic studies, for instance, show that ferritin light chain (FTL) promotes this M2 polarization by inhibiting iPLA2β, thereby altering ferroptotic pathways in the TME [104, 105]. Notably, this adaptation reveals a therapeutic vulnerability: M2 macrophages themselves show greater susceptibility to GPX4 inhibition-induced ferroptosis compared to their M1 counterparts [106]. Furthermore, conditions of ferroptotic stress (iron overload, ROS) can further reinforce M2 polarization, creating a feedback loop [107]. Therefore, strategically inducing ferroptosis in M2 TAMs—while preserving M1 functions—presents a promising therapeutic approach to break the equilibrium, as evidenced by studies using compounds like Erastin to promote M2-to-M1 repolarization [108, 109]. The Equilibrium phase is characterized by tumor adaptations that actively suppress ferroptosis, notably through the education of M2 TAMs that shield tumors from this form of cell death. This establishes the M2 TAM-ferroptosis axis as a critical leverage point in the immune-tumor stalemate.

During the “Escape” phase, immunosuppressive cells (e.g., Tregs, MDSCs, TAMs) dominate the TIME, suppressing effector immune functions and promoting tumor growth through cytokine (e.g., TGF-β, IL-6, VEGF) secretion [110, 111]. Critically, these cells engage with ferroptosis in distinct and pivotal ways. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), particularly the polymorphonuclear subset (PMN-MDSCs), exhibit a paradoxical relationship with ferroptosis. They are highly susceptible to ferroptosis, which can be triggered by increased arachidonic acid uptake via FATP2 [112, 113]. However, their ferroptosis does not stimulate immunity; instead, it exacerbates immunosuppression. Dying PMN-MDSCs release oxygenated lipids that directly suppress CD8⁺ T cell activity, thereby accelerating tumor escape [114]. This mechanistic insight underscores ferroptosis induction in PMN-MDSCs as a double-edged sword, necessitating precise therapeutic targeting. Regulatory T cells (Tregs), in contrast, are remarkably resistant to ferroptosis, primarily due to their high GPX4 expression [115]. This resistance is crucial for maintaining their suppressive function in the TIME. Intriguingly, inducing ferroptosis in GPX4-deficient Tregs can have pro-inflammatory consequences, such as IL-1β production, which may potentially disrupt the immunosuppressive microenvironment [116, 117]. This reveals a context-dependent vulnerabilityof Tregs that could be exploited therapeutically, though balancing efficacy and immune related adverse events remains a challenge [118]. Natural Killer (NK) cells see their function compromised when tumor-derived metabolites (e.g., L-kynurenine) induce ferroptosis in them, a process counteracted by endogenous GPX4 and NRF2 pathways [119–121]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) play a dual role: they promote ferroptosis in NK cells by dysregulating iron metabolism, while simultaneously protecting tumor cells from ferroptosis by supplying antioxidants (e.g., GSH), thereby contributing to therapy resistance [121, 122]. Specific CAF subtypes with enhanced iron metabolism exhibit intrinsic ferroptosis resistance [123]. In the Escape phase, ferroptosis is fundamentally hijacked to fortify immunosuppression. Key immunosuppressive cells either undergo ferroptosis to release inhibitory molecules (PMN-MDSCs) or are exceptionally resistant to it to maintain their presence (Tregs). Concurrently, anti-tumor immune cells like NK cells become victims of ferroptosis induction, and stromal cells like CAFs are reprogrammed to suppress ferroptosis in tumors. This illustrates ferroptosis as a central, yet complex and cell type specific, regulatory node in the established immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

Immunomodulation of tumor cells mediated by ferroptosis

Immunogenic cell death (ICD) is a regulated form of cell death that elicits adaptive immunity by mediating the generation of neoantigens as well as the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and cytokines [124]. Following ferroptosis, cancer cells trigger a complex cascade of immunomodulatory responses, primarily through the release of DAMPs and cytokines. These signaling molecules activate multiple immune response pathways, exerting broad and profound effects on the TME [125].

DAMPs play a dual role in tumor development: when cancer cells undergo death, released DAMPs act as endogenous adjuvant signals, influencing the TME through multiple mechanisms [126]. In promoting anti-tumor immunity, intracellular DAMPs activate the cGAS/STING pathway, leading to interferon release, which stimulates DC cells, CD8 + cells, and phagocytes, enhancing anti-tumor immune function [127, 128]. Notably, DC cell maturation is closely related to HMGB1, with early ferroptotic cells showing stronger immunogenicity [125]. HMGB1 functions through multiple pathways: cytoplasmic HMGB1 forms autophagosomes by binding to BECN1, while extracellular HMGB1 induces autophagy through macrophage AGER receptors, ultimately being inactivated primarily through oxidation rather than immune responses [129–131]. Multiple studies have confirmed HMGB1’s crucial role: Kong et al. found that inhibiting SIRT1 suppresses HMGB1 cellular translocation and ACSL4 expression, activating the HMGB1/ACSL4 pathway to reduce cytarabine resistance in AML patients [132]. Additionally, oxidative stress-induced calreticulin translocation promotes anti-tumor effects of DC cells and CD8 + cells, while oxidized PE and SAPE-OOH enhance macrophage function and clearance of ferroptotic tumor cells through macrophage TLR2 recognition [133]. However, in terms of immunosuppression, cancer cell DNA peroxidation damage products (8-OHG and cGAS) activate the TMEM173/STING pathway, promoting macrophage infiltration and M2 polarization, accelerating pancreatic cancer progression; in hepatocellular carcinoma, GPX4 deficiency-induced ferroptosis triggers tumor-suppressive immune responses through PD-L1 upregulation and HMGB1-mediated MDSC infiltration [134, 135].

Beyond DAMPs, cytokines also contribute significantly. Recent lung cancer research demonstrates that receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 1 (ROR1)-targeted CAR-T cells working synergistically with interferon γ (IFN-γ) promote the expression of ACSL4, significantly enhancing anti-tumor immunity [136]. TGFβplays a critical role in establishing the immunosuppressive TME: lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) studies revealed that long non-coding RNA YTHDF3-DT alleviates ferroptosis-induced cell death by activating TGF-β and related signaling pathways, indicating YTHDF3-DT as a prospective therapeutically relevant target molecule; in gastrointestinal cancer research, highly expressed anoctamin-1 (ANO1) suppresses ferroptosis-mediated TGFβ release, thereby weakening immune cell function [137, 138]. However, research on how ferroptosis affects tumor cell antigenicity remains relatively scarce, indicating an area requiring further investigation.

In summary, the immunomodulatory effects of DAMPs during ferroptosis are characterized by distinct context dependence. However, the viewpoint derived from prophylactic vaccination models that “ferroptosis does not conform to the criteria of ICD” has inherent limitations. First, there are substantial discrepancies between the vaccination setting and the actual clinical context of tumor therapy. Second, this model fails to fully consider the dynamic changes in the tumor microenvironment, as supported by existing evidence. Therefore, whether ferroptosis should be categorized as a form of ICD remains to be further clarified through comprehensive validation using multiple models and scenarios [139, 140] (Fig. 5).

Tumor progression and ferroptosis

Tumor susceptibility to ferroptosis is shaped by diverse environmental stimuli, signaling cascades, and gene expression profiles, all of which contribute to the overall trajectory of tumor development. As tumors evolve, dynamic alterations in these regulatory factors lead to stage-specific changes in ferroptotic vulnerability, ultimately influencing malignant behaviors (Fig. 6).

RAS

The RAS gene family, comprising KRAS, HRAS, and NRAS, is frequently mutated across a broad spectrum of malignancies, representing a major focus in cancer genomics. Intriguingly, the ferroptosis inducers erastin and RSL3 were originally identified through synthetic lethality screening targeting RAS-driven oncogenic pathways [141]. Clinical studies have shown that while KRAS inhibitors G12Ci (such as AMG510 and MRTX849) have achieved clinical success, they face drug resistance challenges. Wang et al. showed that ferroptosis inducers including sorafenib and lapatinib suppress the expression of SLC7A11 and the iron exporter SLC40A1, thereby providing a prospective approach against drug resistance [142]. RIT1, a member of the RAS subfamily, requires further investigation. Ma et al. found that RIT1 enhances ferroptosis sensitivity in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma through the RAS/MAPK and PI3K/AKT pathways, while its dysregulation correlates with poor prognosis in advanced disease. These findings indicate that the role of RAS signaling in ferroptosis regulation is context-dependent during tumor progression [143].

TP53

TP53 is a gene that mitigates oxidative stress in tumor cells by enhancing glycolytic flux to produce reducing equivalents and activating apoptotic pathways to safeguard genomic integrity [144]. Studies have shown that baicalin activates TP53-mediated ferroptosis to effectively reduce oxidative stress caused by DNA damage in hepatocytes [145]. In diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, accumulation of glutamine metaboliteα-KG promotes oxidative stress, leading to high ROS levels that activate TP53-mediated ferroptosis, ultimately inhibiting tumor growth [146]. TP53 mutations result in loss of p53 function and increased cancer risk. Mutant KRAS and TP53 drive the overexpression of kinesin family member 18 A (KIF18A). In pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), this overexpression promotes ferroptosis resistance, and KIF18A is associated with poor prognosis [147]. In glioblastoma (GBM), TP53 mutations lead to decreased ferritin (FTH) expression and increased free iron levels, enhancing tumor cell sensitivity to ferroptosis [148]. This duality highlights the role of different TP53 statuses in regulating ferroptosis during tumor progression.

NFE2L2

Nrf2 is a central transcriptional regulator orchestrating the cellular response to oxidative stress and maintaining redox homeostasis. Nrf2 promotes tumor development by upregulating antioxidant defense mechanisms to suppress ferroptosis. These findings imply that suppressing NFE2L2 under oxidative stress may increase tumor cell vulnerability to ferroptosis-inducing agents, thereby potentiating the therapeutic outcomes of conventional modalities, including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy [149]. According to recent evidence, Huachansu (HCS) exerts antitumor effects in multiple myeloma by promoting ferroptotic cell death, a process mediated through activation of the NRF2/HO-1 signaling cascade [150]. Under glucose deprived conditions, cervical cancer cells treated with the small molecule drug Gboxin exhibit activation of the AMPK signaling pathway, leading to reduced p62 protein levels, enhanced Nrf2 degradation, and subsequent induction of ferroptosis [151]. Additionally, NFE2L2 can suppress oxidative damage caused by ferroptosis by activating genes related to ROS detoxification enzymes and GSH metabolism [152].

ATF4

ATF4 serves essential functions in endoplasmic reticulum stress and oxidative management. Research has shown that the ATF4 pathway exhibits dual regulatory effects on ferroptosis [153]. Regarding ferroptosis inhibition, ALKBH3 (AlkB Homolog 3), an α-KG and Fe²⁺-dependent dioxygenase, is crucial for maintaining genome stability and tumor development. In AML, Liu et al. found that downregulating ALKBH3 induces tumor cell apoptosis by upregulating ATF4 expression through m1A demethylation [154]. In terms of the promoting effect, studies on cholangiocarcinoma (CC) have provided mechanistic insights involving the RNA methyltransferase METTL16. METTL16 is a S-adenosylmethionine (SAM)-dependent RNA methyltransferase that installs N⁶-methyladenosine (m⁶A) modifications on specific RNAs. Its canonical physiological function is to maintain intracellular SAM homeostasis by regulating the stability and translation of MAT2A mRNA. Furthermore, METTL16 can maintain mitochondrial homeostasis, reduce lipid peroxides, and prevent ferroptosis in CC by inhibiting the degradation of ATF4 mRNA [155].

Gut microbiota

The gut microbiota comprises various microorganisms including bacteria, viruses, and fungi. Research shows that gut microbiota dysbiosis or their metabolites can regulate host ferroptosis directly or indirectly, thereby influencing tumor development and treatment outcomes. In colorectal cancer(CRC) research, gut microbiota-derived glutathione S-transferase α4 (Gsta4), a specific detoxifying enzyme for 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), protects macrophages from ferroptosis. However, Enterococcus faecalis infection produces 4-HNE, and Ju et al. observed that E. faecalis colonization in Il10/Gsta4 mice leads to reduced macrophages and Gpx4 expression, inducing ferroptosis and promoting colorectal cancer [156]. Fusobacterium nucleatum induces oxaliplatin resistance by suppressing tumor cell ferroptosis through GPX4 inhibition [157]. Peptococcus anaerobius produces tryptophan metabolite trans-3-indoleacrylic acid (IDA), which inhibits ferroptosis by affecting reduced coenzyme Q10 synthesis [158]. In other cancer studies, specific gut bacteria Enterococcus faecium (Efm) in HCC induces IL-12 and IFN-γ production, synergizing with sorafenib to promote HCC cell ferroptosis; in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), Zhang et al. discovered that gut microbiota modulates glutamine metabolism to suppress ferroptosis in HuCCT1 cells [159, 160]. The gut microbiota can also indirectly regulate ferroptosis by remodeling the tumor microenvironment. For instance, studies have demonstrated that activated cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) secrete interferon-γ (IFN-γ), which downregulates the expression of SLC7A11 and SLC3A2 in tumor cells, thereby sensitizing these cells to ferroptosis [161]. However, the current research on this subset of mechanisms remains relatively scarce, which provides promising avenues for our subsequent investigations.

Epithelial-mesenchymal transition and cancer stem cells

Mesenchymal and epithelial cells are two fundamental vertebrate cell types that participate in epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [162]. Post-EMT cells exhibit varying degrees of transformation: some retain epithelial characteristics while acquiring mesenchymal properties, while others completely transform into mesenchymal cells [163, 164].

EMT is a key driver of tumor progression and metastasis, and has been associated with enhanced susceptibility of cancer cells to ferroptosis [165]. Multiple transcription factors including ZEB1, TWIST1, and NAI1 influence EMT-mediated tumor metastasis and drug resistance. ZEB1, a GPX4-regulated transcription factor involved in cell migration, increases cellular ferroptosis sensitivity when overexpressed [166]. Li et al. found that EMT reprogramming enhances HNC cell susceptibility to ferroptosis [162]. Moreover, KDM3B, a histone demethylase, modulates ferroptosis through transcriptional activation of SLC7A11 [167].

Ferroptosis and EMT exhibit bidirectional regulation. ROS play crucial roles in inducing DNA damage and promoting tumor cell proliferation. ROS generated from lysosomes and labile iron pools during the Fenton reaction contributes to EMT inhibition, while ROS production increases during EMT [168]. For example, 2,2’-dipyridine dithiocarbamate (DpdtC) inhibits EMT through these mechanisms. Its homolog 2,2’-dipyridine hydrazone dithiocarbamate s-butyric acid (DpdtbA) has similar effects and promotes ferritin autophagosome formation [169, 170].

EMT not only endows cancer cells with invasive potential but also induces them to acquire cancer stem cell (CSC) properties. CSCs contribute to chemoresistance and poor prognosis in cancer treatment [171]. In a study focusing on hepatocellular carcinoma, He et al.. demonstrated that by virtue of their self renewal and multi directional differentiation capacities, CSCs can sustain chemoresistance by evading chemotherapy induced cellular damage. Meanwhile, ferroptosis also exerts a regulatory effect on chemoresistance. The authors identified that non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) may participate in the maintenance of CSC stemness and the regulation of ferroptosis simultaneously through the construction of complex regulatory networks, thereby synergistically modulating the process of chemoresistance in liver cancer. This finding provides a crucial theoretical basis for developing combined therapeutic strategies targeting CSC and ferroptosis pathways to overcome chemoresistance in liver cancer [172].

Hypoxia

Research shows that hypoxic environments play crucial roles in tumor cell resistance to ferroptosis. Hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) are key regulators in maintaining oxygen homeostasis [173]. In hypoxic regions, increased HIF-1α expression protects tumor cells against ferroptosis through multiple mechanisms, including enhanced lactate secretion, increased SLC1A1 transcription, and promoted cystine uptake [174].

In gastric cancer research, HIF-1α regulates the transcription of PMAN, which promotes the expression of ELAVL1, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis [175]. Additionally, hypoxic tumor cells undergo various adaptive metabolic changes: hypoxia induces the Warburg effect leading to acid accumulation, while abnormal neovascularization causes acid accumulation and nutrient deficiency, resulting in microenvironmental pH changes and reprogramming of lipid and glucose metabolism [176]. In HCC studies, transporters activate the AMPK pathway through lactate absorption, leading to increased membrane monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA) and decreased ACSL4 expression, enhancing cellular resistance to ferroptosis [177].

Other pathways

In addition, several other pathways (e.g., MAPK, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, AMPK) are involved in the regulation of ferroptosis and tumor progression. Regarding the MAPK pathway, G12Ci-resistant cells exhibit low intracellular GSH levels but high iron contentby activating MAPK signaling and suppressing SLC7A11 expression, ultimately resulting in hypersensitivity to ferroptosis inducers [142]. The PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway plays a pivotal role in regulating diverse cellular processes. Recent studies have indicated that aberrant activation of this pathway contributes to therapy resistance, including chemoresistance, radioresistance, and immunotherapy resistance. Therefore, targeting the PI3K/Akt pathway can sensitize cancer cells to ferroptosis inducers, providing a promising strategy to overcome treatment resistance. For instance, in renal cell carcinoma (RCC), pharmacological inhibition of the PI3K-Akt pathway enhances the sensitivity of RCC cells to ferroptosis induced by combination therapy [178]. As for the AMPK related pathway, AMP activated protein kinase (AMPK) acts as a key regulator of cellular energy homeostasis, which directly modulates ferroptosis by coordinating lipid metabolism, redox balance, and iron transport. Studies have revealed that AMPK inactivation renders cancer cells susceptible to ferroptosis [179]. In conclusion, exploring the regulatory mechanisms of these pathways in ferroptosis will not only expand our understanding of the ferroptosis tumor progression network, but also provide potential targets for optimizing antitumor therapeutic strategies.

In summary, although signaling pathways and microenvironmental factors—including RAS, TP53, NRF2, ATF4, EMT, gut microbiota, hypoxia, MAPK, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, and AMPK—derive from diverse origins, they converge on three core biochemical pillars: lipid metabolism, iron homeostasis, and antioxidant defense, thereby collectively shaping the ferroptosis sensitivity of tumor cells. Despite the divergent regulatory modes of these factors and pathways (e.g., some promote ferroptosis while others suppress it), they ultimately converge on the central regulatory network of iron metabolism-lipid peroxidation-antioxidant defense. This convergence remodels the ferroptotic phenotype and immune microenvironment of tumor cells synergistically, and further modulates the malignant progression of tumors.

The role of ferroptosis in cancer therapy

The TME is characterized by its complex and heterogeneous nature, in which the dynamic crosstalk between immune and tumor cells presents major obstacles to the efficacy of ferroptosis-oriented combinational treatments. The impact of ferroptosis in multidisciplinary cancer treatment varies depending on the microenvironment and manifests differently across various tumor stages. Based on the tumor immune phenotype (i.e., key immune-related phenotypic characteristics such as the infiltration level, activation status, and spatial distribution features of immune cells in the tumor immune microenvironment), the TME can be classified into three categories: immune hot tumors, immune cold tumors, and immune altered tumors [180] (Fig. 7, Table 2, Table 3).

Clinical and preclinical examples of ferroptosis modulation across major cancer therapeutic modalities

Ferroptosis and chemotherapy

Ferroptosis plays dual roles in different chemotherapeutic contexts: it can either contribute to drug resistance or help overcome it. Cisplatin, a typical alkylating agent, exerts its anticancer effects through tumor cell DNA damage [181, 182]. In lung cancer experiments using the syngeneic transplant tumor model of C57BL/6 mice, cisplatin has been shown to remodel the TME, enhancing immune infiltration and shifting it from an immunologically “cold” to a “hot” state. However, tumor cells may acquire resistance by perturbing ferroptosis-related signaling pathways within the TME [183] (as part of the resistance mechanisms outlined in Fig. 8, left panel). To overcome cisplatin resistance in non-small cell lung cancer, L et al. designed a mitochondria-targeted iridium-based compound, Ir-rhod, which induces both ferroptosis and apoptosis by regulating the expression of ACSL4 and GPX4 [183]. DNAJC15 is a mitochondrial TIMM23 associated co chaperone protein that is well-known for its roles in regulating oxidative phosphorylation efficiency, oxidative stress responses, and lipid metabolism. The discovery of mitochondrial chaperone protein DNAJC15’s role in regulating cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer provides new directions for future research [184]. In vitro studies revealed that linear deubiquitinase OTULIN induces cisplatin resistance in osteosarcoma by preventing proteasomal degradation of GPX4 [185] (see Fig. 8, left panel, for this specific regulatory axis). In the animal study conducted by Zhang et al.., it was discovered that eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) exerts multiple effects: inhibiting tumor growth, promoting apoptosis, and enhancing cisplatin-induced ferroptosis via GPX4 suppression, thus providing novel strategies for overcoming cisplatin resistance [186]. The resistance to oxaliplatin (OXA), a third-generation platinum-based chemotherapeutic agent, has been closely linked to ferroptosis, particularly in the treatment of colorectal cancer (CRC) and HCC where dysregulation of the GPX4 system has been observed. Research into OXA resistance in CRC treatment has revealed multiple contributing mechanisms involving the GPX4 regulatory system, gut microbiota, and Nrf2 [187] (key contributors illustrated in Fig. 8, left panel). Elevated GPX4 expression has been observed in advanced CRC cells relative to adjacent non-tumorous tissues, and targeting GPX4 has been demonstrated to reverse oxaliplatin resistance [188, 189]. Given that Nrf2 upregulation promotes antioxidant factor transcription while its inhibition reduces GPX4 expression, The regulatory activity of Nrf2 has been implicated as a key contributor to OXA resistance [187]. Additionally, gut microbiota components including Fusobacterium and Nucleospora can induce OXA resistance in CRC through ferroptosis-related mechanisms [157, 190].

Ferroptosis can also be an effective strategy to combat the resistance of cancer drugs. Erastin, a prototypical ferroptosis inducer, enhances the responsiveness of cancer cells to anti-PD-L1 treatment in both lung cancer and HCC via xCT inhibition, and also exerts effects through modulation of key regulators including p53 and ACSL4 [191]. However, due to poor water solubility, Erastin’s clinical application is limited, necessitating combination with other chemotherapeutic agents, such as doxorubicin or topotecan [192]. Sorafenib is a multi-kinase inhibitor administered orally [193]. It induces ferroptosis by inhibiting SLC7A11 to reduce intracellular GSH levels and promote lipid peroxide accumulation, while also inducing mitochondrial dysfunction, potentially related to electron transport function or GPX4 dephosphorylation [193, 194]. Sorafenib combination therapies have shown significant efficacy: with carbon nanoparticle-Fe(II) complex (CNSI-Fe) in TNBC, reducing GPX4 levels; effectively inhibiting tamoxifen-resistant breast cancer cell growth; and enhancing tumor cell ferroptosis when combined with natural triterpene asiatic acid (AA) in HCC through GPX4 and FTH1 modulation [195–197] (refer to Fig. 8, right panel). In another recent study on breast cancer involving both cellular and xenograft tumor models, SB-T-101,141, a novel ferroptosis inducer, exhibits promising clinical potential for overcoming paclitaxel resistance by promoting the generation of ferric iron (Fe³⁺) and ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) [198]. In mouse experiments, Yang et al.. identified the role of Achaete-scute complex homolog 1 (ASCL1) in breast cancer therapy; inhibition of ASCL1 can activate ferroptosis, thereby enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of paclitaxel [199]. In gastric cancer research, researchers employed W1131—a novel STAT3 inhibitor developed by their team—in in vitro gastric cancer models. This agent overcomes 5-fluorouracil (5-Fu) resistance via ferroptosis induction, thus exerting a favorable effect on tumor treatment [200] (Fig. 8).

Although numerous preclinical studies have suggested that ferroptosis plays a crucial role in chemoresistance and chemosensitization, there remains a clear stratification in existing evidence. Most mechanistic studies are based on cell lines or mouse models, with limited clinical translation data. For instance, GPX4 inhibitors can reverse oxaliplatin resistance in vitro, but their efficacy and safety in humans have not yet been clarified. In addition, many reported “ferroptosis-related drugs” (e.g., Erastin, Sorafenib) were not specifically designed to induce ferroptosis, and their effects may involve crosstalk among multiple pathways, which poses complexity for mechanistic interpretation. Future efforts should focus on conducting more prospective clinical trials and developing more specific ferroptosis inducers or biomarkers to clarify their practical value in combination chemotherapy strategies.

Ferroptosis and radiotherapy

Radiotherapy, a crucial treatment modality for various solid tumors, primarily functions through ionizing radiation (IR) generating ROS and free radicals, causing structural damage to cellular DNA and biomolecules including lipids, with ferroptosis playing a significant role [201, 202]. IR exhibits dual effects on ferroptosis regulation: influencing ACSL4 expression to promote lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, while also modulating SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression. Studies indicate that while ferroptosis is not directly involved in IR-induced DNA damage, it can enhance tumor cell sensitivity to IR [203]. For example, Zhang et al. found in cellular models and mouse tumor models that ferroptosis enhances the sensitivity of hepatocellular carcinoma to radiotherapy. The mechanism is that the RNA methyltransferase METTL3 promotes the stability of SLC7A11 mRNA by targeting adenine (+ 1795) on SLC7A11 mRNA and reduces the ubiquitination of SLC7A11 protein. This site specific m⁶A methylation increases the stability of SLC7A11 transcripts and simultaneously decreases ubiquitination-mediated degradation of SLC7A11 protein, leading to elevated xCT expression and enhanced cellular defense against radiation-induced ferroptosis [204]. Hydrated electrons produced by X-rays can directly reduce Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺ and protein ferric succinate without additional ligands, with both in vitro and in vivo experiments confirming significant tumor growth inhibition through ferroptosis [205]. Radiation-induced bystander effect (RIBE) demonstrates that radiation not only directly causes DNA damage but also affects surrounding cells through radiation-triggered microparticles (RT-MP) [206]. These microparticles induce ferroptosis in surrounding cells, triggering ICD and enhancing TAM infiltration. Notably, TAM can enhance PD-L1 expression in tumor cells [206]. In combined radiotherapy and immunotherapy, SLC7A11 serves as a key determinant: immunotherapy activates CD8⁺ T cells while radiotherapy releases IFN-γ through ATM, jointly suppressing SLC7A11 and enhancing tumor cell lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis [207] (Fig. 9).

Ferroptosis and immunotherapy

Recent years have witnessed rapid development in immunotherapy, including CAR-T cells, ICIs, cytokine therapy, and dendritic cell vaccines [208]. While the emergence of ICIs (such as anti-PD1 and anti-PD-L1) represents a breakthrough in cancer treatment, their efficacy remains limited, particularly in “cold tumors” lacking effective T cell infiltration. The synergistic effects of ferroptosis and immunotherapy operate by targeting tumor cells to induce ferroptosis and release DAMPs, enhancing tumor immunogenicity, while also targeting immune cells to improve the suppressive microenvironment, promoting “cold” to “hot” tumor transformation. In TNBC treatment, combining GPX4 inhibitors with anti-PD1 shows superior efficacy by enhancing antitumor immunity through tumor cell ferroptosis. This combinatorial strategy exhibited markedly superior antitumor efficacy compared with either the GPX4 inhibitor or anti-PD-1 antibody monotherapy in the TNBC mouse xenograft model [209]. Dual PI3K and HDAC inhibitor BEBT-908 enhances ICI efficacy by increasing p53 acetylation and ferroptosis signaling, creating an inflammatory microenvironment rich in immune cells in preclinical studies [210]. Studies on TAMs demonstrate that iron-based metal-organic framework nanoparticles combined with ferroptosis inducers enhance TAM resistance to anti-inflammatory factors and activate pro-inflammatory signaling pathways [211]. In validation experiments conducted on nude mouse xenograft models of glioblastoma (GBM), ferritin light chain (FTL) enhances anti-PD1 treatment sensitivity by regulating TAM polarization toward M2 phenotype and modulating the immune-evasive microenvironment [105]. Simvastatin induces ferroptosis during in vitro experiments by inhibiting ILF3 in gastric cancer cells and regulates PD-L1 expression through the DEPTOR/mTOR pathway, enhancing immunotherapy efficacy [212]. As a pivotal adaptor protein involved in innate immune signaling cascades including TLRs and RIG-I, TRAF3 overexpression augments ferroptosis processes and improves responsiveness to immunotherapeutic regimens in murine GBM models [213]. In murine glioblastoma models, upregulation of fatty acid binding protein 7 (Fabp7) represses the transcription of ferroptosis-inducing genes (e.g., Lpcat3). As a brain enriched lipid chaperone, Fabp7 mediates fatty acid uptake and intracellular trafficking; additionally, it disrupts the expression of clock genes in CD8 + T cells and triggers their apoptosis, thereby contributing to anti-PD-1 resistance [214]. These findings indicate that Fabp7 targeting may facilitate the enhancement of immunotherapy efficacy [215]. A study has developed a DSPE PEG micelle nanocarrier (TFDD) encapsulating doxorubicin (DOX) with tannic acid - iron ions (TA-Fe³⁺). Immunotherapy is frequently combined with other therapeutic approaches. Several combination strategies have been identified: Icariin (ICA) in in vitro experimental assays combined with anti-PD-1 promotes cellular ferroptosis through mitochondrial damage while enhancing CD69 and TCRβ signaling, achieving immune enhancement [217]. The antiarrhythmic drug propafenone, when combined with the GPX4 inhibitor RSL3 in xenograft models, induces mitochondria-associated ferroptosis and enhances immunotherapy-induced antitumor immunity [218]. Radiotherapy promotes lipid peroxidation by inducing ROS generation and upregulating ACSL4 expression. Its combination with immunotherapy significantly induces ferroptosis through reduced DNA damage-activated kinase ATM and decreased cystine uptake mediated by SLC7A11 downregulation [203, 219]. Mouse experiments demonstrate that carbon ion radiotherapy (CIRT) combined with immunotherapy produces stronger synergistic anticancer effects [220]. Additionally, the combination of radioligand therapy (RPT) with ferroptosis and PD-L1 immune checkpoint blockade significantly enhances antitumor immune activity, effectively addressing challenges in RPT treatment such as insufficient drug accumulation and resistance [221].

However, the combination of ferroptosis and immunotherapy does not always produce synergistic effects. In certain cases, under certain circumstances, ferroptosis released DAMPs exert an impact on KRASG12D, a prevalent driver mutation in pancreatic and colorectal cancers that constitutively activates proliferative signaling pathways. Phagocytosis of KRASG12D by macrophages drives their polarization toward an M2 like protumorigenic phenotype [222]. Furthermore, tumor cell reprogramming induces changes in the TME, altering various cells’ sensitivity to ferroptosis. For instance, T cell metabolic reprogramming increases lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis sensitivity. Notably, CD8⁺T cells show higher ferroptosis sensitivity compared to tumor cells, making both the induction of tumor cell ferroptosis and the inhibition of CD8⁺T cell ferroptosis potentially detrimental to antitumor immunity. Current research limitations primarily manifest in oversimplified approaches to studying the interactions between ferroptosis, tumor cells, and T cells, often focusing on only two factors while neglecting the holistic nature of TME and the comprehensive impact of different tumor subtypes, gene expression patterns, and metabolic alterations [223]. This highlights the importance of considering specific circumstances in clinical practice and developing individualized treatment strategies (Fig. 10).

Ferroptosis and targeted therapy

Targeted therapy, a treatment approach focusing on tumor-associated molecular markers, exerts anticancer effects through specific inhibition of tumor-driving genes, signaling pathways, and proteins promoting tumor survival and development. Recent research demonstrates extensive applications of ferroptosis in targeted therapy. In advanced gastric cancer treatment with apatinib, clinical observations reveal that many patients develop apatinib resistance due to ferroptosis resistance in gastric cancer cells. Wang et al. discovered this resistance mechanism is related to decreased expression of key enzymes involved in polyunsaturated ether phospholipid synthesis (such as AGPS) and transcription factor ELK1 [224]. Similarly, PDAC shows poor patient survival rates due to ferroptosis resistance and immunosuppression. In the study conducted by Lin et al., cTRIP12 specifically binds to O-linked N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase (OGT), thereby elevating the global level of protein O-GlcNAcylation in cells. This modification process positively regulates the expression of ferritin heavy chain (FTH) and the immune checkpoint protein PD-L1. Thus, suppression of cTRIP12 expression leads to the downregulation of FTH and PD-L1 levels, which in turn induces ferroptosis in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) cells. These findings provide a rationale for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies in our subsequent research [225] (Fig. 11).

Ferroptosis and other therapies

Ferroptosis has also been integrated with other therapeutic strategies, such as interventional and endocrine therapies. Transcatheter arterial chemoembolization (TACE) is currently one of the most commonly used and preferred methods for treating intermediate to advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) that is not amenable to surgical resection. Tang et al.. demonstrated through in vitro cellular assays and in vivo animal experiments that high expression of NDRG1, a hub gene associated with transcatheter arterial chemoembolization (TACE) response, predicts poor prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). As a multifunctional stress-responsive protein, NDRG1 not only participates in cell differentiation and metal ion homeostasis but also acts as a ferroptosis guardian in HCC. The underlying molecular mechanism lies in the fact that NDRG1 enhances cellular antioxidant capacity by maintaining the expression and activity of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and inhibiting reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, thereby effectively suppressing the ferroptosis process driven by GPX4 inactivation and lipid peroxidation. These findings lay a solid foundation for the development of novel targeted therapies [226]. However, TACE still faces challenges such as suboptimal efficacy, procedural risks, and poor prognosis. Many researchers have attempted to enhance the therapeutic effects of TACE by targeting ferroptosis. TACE often utilizes lipiodol-based chemoemulsions, but the stability and release kinetics of such formulations frequently limit their effectiveness. Wang et al. developed a lipoxygenase (LOX)-loaded, highly stable oil-in-water iodinated Pickering emulsion (LPE), which facilitates the conversion of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), thereby inducing ferroptosis in cancer cells and overcoming the instability of traditional lipiodol emulsions [227]. Chen et al. utilized gelatin as a microsphere wall material and combined it with doxorubicin and Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles (ADM/Fe₃O₄-MS). Their study showed a significant decrease in GPX4 and a marked increase in ACSL4 expression, effectively enhancing the anti-tumor efficacy of TACE through ferroptosis induction [228]. Endocrine therapy is primarily used in the treatment of breast and prostate cancers. Research by Ozge Saatci revealed that silencing LINC00152 enhances the sensitivity of metastatic estrogen receptor-positive (ER⁺) breast cancer to tamoxifen. The underlying mechanism involves elevated levels of cAMP and Ca²⁺, leading to increased ROS production and lipid peroxidation [229]. Similarly, Cao et al. found that ERα suppresses ferroptosis in ER⁺ breast cancer, contributing to resistance against endocrine therapy. This occurs via transcriptional upregulation of SLC7A11 and SLC3A2, which in turn inhibits ferroptosis [196].

Based on the 8 registered ferroptosis-related clinical trials (Table 5), it is evident that current research is still in the early exploratory stage. In terms of tumor type coverage, trials related to solid tumors account for a high proportion, with only 1 trial focusing on hematological malignancies. This is consistent with the conclusion from basic research that ferroptosis pathway abnormalities are more prominent in solid tumors; however, there is a lack of targeted exploration for high incidence and sensitive tumors such as breast cancer and colorectal cancer. Regarding therapeutic strategies, only 1 trial uses inducers directly targeting the core ferroptosis pathways (iron metabolism, GPX4/SLC7A11), while the others mostly involve indirect modulators or mechanism exploration. This reflects that the clinical translation of direct ferroptosis inducers still requires safety verification. Additionally, the absence of ferroptosis specific biomarkers hinders patient selection and therapeutic efficacy monitoring. These issues provide insights for future clinical trials. It is necessary to incorporate core ferroptosis biomarkers into stratification and endpoint design, expand the coverage of high-incidence tumors and special populations, and conduct multi center, large sample Phase III trials. Only in this way can ferroptosis-based therapies be promoted from laboratory research to clinical application.

Ferroptosis and nanotherapy

Despite the remarkable antitumor potential of ferroptosis targeted therapy observed in preclinical settings, its clinical translation is impeded by intrinsic limitations: the majority of ferroptosis regulators exhibit inadequate biocompatibility, poor aqueous solubility, and insufficient targeting ability, rendering them prone to causing off target effects. Furthermore, precise modulation of drug release remains a challenge, as these agents are unable to surmount TME-associated barriers including hypoxia and elevated GSH levels, leading to suboptimal tumor accumulation. Nanomaterials, endowed with distinctive physicochemical characteristics, offer a promising solution to these predicaments: they facilitate efficient loading and targeted delivery of ferroptosis-inducing agents, thereby enhancing biocompatibility and mitigating toxic side effects. Moreover, via TME responsive engineering, nanomaterials enable controlled drug release while counteracting TME induced suppression of ferroptosis, providing pivotal support for the translational advancement of ferroptosis-targeted therapy. These nanomaterials are primarily categorized into two types: first, the “internal response” systems that respond to intrinsic TME signals (e.g., metabolic disorders, redox balance, specific enzymes, or ions), designed to capitalize on the inherent weaknesses of tumors; second, the “external response” systems that respond to externally applied physical stimuli (e.g., light, ultrasound, or radiation) [230–232] (Tables 4 and 6).Table 6Nanomaterials inducing ferroptosis in cancer therapyNanoparticlesMechanismsQuoteOsmium-Peroxo ComplexUnder light irradiation, it generates multiple types of ROS without requiring molecular oxygen to initiate ferroptosis[252]Zr(Cu)-MOF@Au@DHAGSH depletion increases bladder cancer cell susceptibility to ferroptosis[253]Ir-FcGenerates ROS and induces ferroptosis in tumor cells[254]Ce6/NI@PEP@HA(CNPH)Depletes GSH and enhances the efficacy of gas therapy to generate ROS with oxidative damage[255]HMnO2-VC@mPEG-Ce6(HMVC)HMnO₂ catalyzes the decomposition of H₂O₂ to produce oxygen. Nanomaterials inducing ferroptosis in cancer therapy

Internally responsive nanosystems

Internally responsive nanosystems are activated by sensing and responding to the intrinsic characteristics of the TME, such as weak acidity, high ROS levels, overexpressed enzymes, or metabolites. They target key ferroptosis pathways (e.g., iron metabolism, lipid peroxidation, antioxidant defense) to achieve tumor specific ferroptosis induction. These systems are mainly categorized into three aspects, with the first being oxidative stress amplification to break through cellular antioxidant defense. Tumor cells exhibit higher ROS levels than normal cells, making ROS a promising target for drug design. A ferroptosis inducer (FIN) engineered based on rumen vesicles can cause mitochondrial damage, elevate ROS levels, and activate ferritinophagy, collectively inducing ferroptosis in tumor cells and inhibiting colorectal cancer progression [233]. The Exo-AMNCs complex, composed of inner ultrasmall AuMn nanoclusters (AMNCs) and outer NK cell derived exosomes, generates ROS by decomposing H₂O₂. With excellent biocompatibility and water solubility, it efficiently induces ferroptosis in OSCC cells [234]. The nanoparticle Ce6@HGMOF, integrating photosensitizer (Ce6), glucose oxidase (GOX), chemotherapeutic drug (HCPT), and iron based metal-organic framework (MOF), produces gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide in the TME. This process further generates oxygen to remodel the hypoxic TME, enhances ROS production, and induces oxidative stress damage [235]. Bimetallic copper-ruthenium (Cu@Ru) core-shell nanoparticles promote ROS generation through adsorbing small molecules (e.g., water and oxygen), thereby triggering tumor cell ferroptosis [236]. The high efficiency nanoplatform Zn-Co₃O₄/NC-GOx can decompose H₂O₂ into oxygen and further convert oxygen into superoxide anions (O₂∙⁻). Simultaneously, it depletes glutathione in tumor cells, overcoming TME limitations in chemodynamic therapy [237]. Gold (Au)-iron oxide (Fe₃O₄) Janus nanoparticles (GION@RGD) enhance the Fenton reaction in the treatment of TNBC, releasing a large number of hydroxyl radicals that induce extensive lipid accumulation and ferroptosis in tumor cells [238].

Secondly, remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment represents a key strategy. Specifically targeting immunologically “cold” tumors, which are characterized by minimal immune cell infiltration, ferroptosis focused interventions can reprogram the TIME, reverse the immunosuppressive niche, and enhance therapeutic efficacy. For instance, the nanomedicine HM/Ef/LNT-MOFMIL-101(Fe), upon reaching the tumor, induces IFN-γ to drive iron metabolism and the Fenton reaction. It additionally repolarizes tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) toward the antitumor M1 phenotype and promotes T-lymphocyte infiltration, collectively inducing ferroptosis [239]. In another approach, Zhang et al. assembled a stable nanoparticle (PA NPs) from a paclitaxel prodrug and diacyl phosphatidylethanolamine-PEG2000. These NPs release paclitaxel intracellularly, causing oxidative stress-triggered ferroptosis while boosting dendritic cell (DC) function and T-lymphocyte activation, resulting in potent antitumor activity [240]. Li et al. developed a sequentially dual locked Pt(IV) nanoprodrug that dissociates in the acidic tumor environment, enhancing both chemotherapy and photodynamic therapy. This agent also drives TAM repolarization and induces ferroptosis [241]. A photoactivatable nanozyme, Fe-TCPP-R848-PEG (Fe-MOF-RP), catalyzes H₂O₂ decomposition in the TIME to generate oxygen and initiate ferroptosis, thereby augmenting photodynamic efficacy. It further promotes DC maturation and M1-like TAM polarization, contributing to TIME remodeling [242]. Furthermore, a complex based on diethyldithiocarbamate (DDC) chelated with copper and ferrous ions (DDC/Cu-Fe), delivered via a hybrid albumin/lactoferrin nanoparticle (Alb/LF NP) carrier, effectively activates T-cell immunity, suppresses protumor macrophages, stimulates antitumor immunity, and induces ferroptosis in glioma cells [243].

Thirdly, targeting key metabolic and defensive nodes of ferroptosis—such as GPX4, cholesterol homeostasis, lipid accumulation, and iron metabolism—constitutes a pivotal therapeutic approach. Cholesterol, for instance, plays a crucial supportive role in tumor cell proliferation and migration. Shen et al. developed a biomaterial hydrogel designated DA-COD-OD-HCS, designed for use following microwave ablation therapy. This hydrogel releases cholesterol oxidase (COD), which degrades cholesterol, promotes ferroptosis in tumor cells, and enhances the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy [244]. Another nanomaterial, TDN@EVs, combines the advantages of extracellular vesicles (EVs) and tetrahedral DNA nanostructures (TDNs), offering precise delivery and enhanced drug internalization. TDN@EVs specifically target oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) cells, leading to GPX4 degradation and subsequent tumor cell ferroptosis [200]. The nanodrug FeSHS/BQR/YC-1-PEG simultaneously disrupts the GPX4/DHODH/FSP1 inhibitory pathways, thereby inducing ferroptosis [245]. Biomimetic nanovesicles (FiFe@RBM) function by releasing iron, effectively causing mitochondrial and ROS dysfunction while increasing unsaturated fatty acids. This disrupts lipid metabolism and ultimately induces ferroptosis, showing significant potential in treating castration-resistant prostate cancer [246]. The bionanocomplex AbDA-Lim focuses on iron metabolism by converting Fe(III) to Fe(II), elevating the labile iron pool (LIP), and reducing glutathione (GSH) levels, thereby triggering non-canonical ferroptosis [247]. Furthermore, a nanodrug based on chondroitin sulfate (CS), arachidonic acid (AA), and the redox-sensitive linker cysteamine (CYS), designated CSAA/Fe@PPI, concurrently targets iron metabolism and lipid accumulation while inhibiting GPX4 activity, resulting in robust ferroptotic cell death [248].

Nevertheless, internally responsive nanosystems still face multiple challenges in clinical translation, including precise control over the in vivo behavior of nanomaterials, ensuring response efficiency within complex tumor microenvironments, evaluating long-term biosafety, and achieving feasibility for large-scale production. To overcome these limitations, externally stimulus responsive nanosystems have emerged. Such strategies utilize external physical energies such as light, ultrasound, or radiation to enable spatiotemporal control over the activation of nanomedicines, thereby achieving remote and precise induction of the ferroptosis process.

Externally responsive nanosystems

Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is a tripartite therapeutic strategy based on photosensitizers, light irradiation, and oxygen. Photosensitizers are administered to accumulate in tumor regions and then activated by light of a specific wavelength, generating high levels of ROS to induce tumor cell apoptosis or necrosis [249]. However, this therapy is limited by insufficient photosensitizer selectivity, adverse effects of high GSH levels, and hypoxic regions in tumor tissues, which restrict its efficacy [250]. To address the efficacy limitation caused by hypoxia, Lu et al.. developed an osmium peroxo complex that generates multiple types of ROS upon light irradiation without requiring molecular oxygen, thereby triggering ferroptosis [251]. Zhao et al.. incorporated Cu²⁺ into the porphyrin ring of PCN-224 to construct a nanoreactor Zr(Cu)-MOF@Au@DHA, which enhances ferroptosis sensitivity in bladder cancer cells by depleting GSH [252]. To simultaneously overcome hypoxia and high GSH levels, an imine bond was introduced into an Ir(III)-ferrocene complex to form the small-molecule drug Ir-Fc. Ir-Fc can decompose into Fc-CHO and Ir-NH₂: Fc-CHO generates ROS to induce tumor cell ferroptosis, while the substantial oxygen produced by GSH depletion alleviates hypoxia [253]. PDT is often combined with other therapies. For example, in combination with NO-based gas therapy, Lin et al.. developed an engineered photoactivatable nanomicelle Ce6/NI@PEP@HA (CNPH), which depletes GSH and enhances the efficacy of gas therapy to generate ROS with oxidative damage, achieving 89.1% tumor inhibition [254]. In the treatment of melanoma, a hybrid delivery system (IHEL) derived from liposomes and macrophage derived exosomes was used to deliver heme and the phototherapeutic agent IR780. Both components collaboratively promote the reprogramming of the tumor microenvironment, reduce apoptosis, and enhance ferroptosis to avoid drug resistance. Additionally, they polarize macrophages into anticancer active M1 macrophages, significantly improving antitumor efficacy [255].

Sonodynamic therapy (SDT) is a non-invasive therapeutic modality that utilizes ultrasound to activate sonosensitizers, generating ROS to eliminate tumor cells. Compared with PDT, SDT exhibits high tissue penetration depth of tens of centimeters, facilitating the treatment of deep seated tumors [256]. Similar to PDT, ROS produced by SDT directly oxidizes PUFAs on the cell membrane to form LPO, thereby triggering ferroptosis. However, the therapeutic efficacy of SDT is also susceptible to tumor hypoxia and high GSH levels in the TME. To address these challenges, Shen et al.. developed a multifunctional nanoplatform HMnO₂-VC@mPEG-Ce6 (HMVC). In this system, HMnO₂ catalyzes the decomposition of H₂O₂ to generate oxygen, vitamin C (VC) drives ROS production, and the valence change of Mn depletes GSH. This synergistic design effectively overcomes hypoxia and high GSH barriers, demonstrating promising therapeutic effects in prostate cancer [257]. Additionally, iron atom-dispersed nanoparticles (Fe-N-C) induce ferroptosis by acting as peroxidases and catalases to regulate ROS generation, while depleting GSH via glutathione oxidase like activity, thereby enhancing antitumor efficacy [258]. SDT can also be combined with radiotherapy for synergistic antitumor effects. Qian et al.. constructed a biodegradable bismuth based heterojunction radiosensitizer BiF₃-Bi₂O₃₋ₓ:Sₓ-PEG (BFOSP). By introducing anions to improve charge carrier mobility, BFOSP enhances ROS production. Furthermore, the degradation of BFOSP remodels the TME, inducing ferroptosis and activating systemic immune responses to augment therapeutic outcomes [259].

Building on the previously detailed association between RT and ferroptosis, numerous nanomaterials have been incorporated into cancer treatment. Iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs) are widely used in biomedicine, as they enhance the radiotherapeutic effect by emitting secondary X-rays upon irradiation and regulating the intracellular balance of ROS. Tang et al.. developed a nanoplatform composed of superparamagnetic IONPs, erastin, and PEG. This platform not only improves erastin solubility and induces ferroptosis but also enables disease monitoring via its MRI contrast agent properties, demonstrating enhanced antitumor activity against nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) both in vitro and in vivo [260]. The peroxidase Ir@Au nanozyme can sustainably generate hydrogen peroxide and reduce pH. In combination with RT, the nanozyme absorbs photons, emits electrons, amplifies ROS production, enhances DNA damage and lipid peroxidation, thereby boosting RT efficacy and promoting ferroptosis [261]. The nanodrug Bi₂Se₃-RSL3/diABZi (DP-HBN/RA) acts as a radiosensitizer by precisely concentrating X-ray energy within tumors, leading to the accumulation of lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. Additionally, DP-HBN/RA augments RT efficacy, resulting in irreparable DNA damage [262].

Taken together, ferroptosis focused nanotherapy represents a cutting edge direction in cancer treatment, with its core lying in two nanosystem categories: externally and internally stimulus-responsive nanosystems. Internally responsive nanosystems achieve endogenous ferroptosis activation by targeting TME features and biological metabolic pathways, while externally responsive counterparts precisely amplify ferroptotic effects. Both systems surmount the inherent drawbacks of traditional ferroptosis inducers (e.g., poor water solubility, inadequate targeting, and TME-related restrictions such as hypoxia and high GSH levels). Via functionalized nanocarrier design, they realize precise drug delivery, controlled release, and multi-therapy synergy, markedly improving ferroptosis specificity and antitumor activity, and offering novel solutions for cold to hot tumor conversion and therapeutic resistance reversal. Nevertheless, notable limitations persist: tumor heterogeneity impairs the targeting accuracy of internally responsive systems, externally responsive systems depend on specialized equipment and suffer from limited tissue penetration, and nanocarrier-related issues (e.g., biocompatibility, circulation stability, biological barrier penetration, and long-term toxicity) hinder clinical translation. Future efforts should focus on optimizing nanomaterial design, enhancing stimulus response efficiency, reinforcing in vivo safety assessments, and promoting clinical translation to advance ferroptosis-based nanotherapies from bench to bedside, paving the way for precision cancer treatment.

Conclusion

This review offers a systematic summary of the research developments of ferroptosis in multidisciplinary tumor management through three main sections. The core mechanisms of ferroptosis involve PUFA oxidation, iron metabolism, and mitochondrial metabolism, with antioxidant systems including GPX4, CoQH2, and GCH1-BH4, along with membrane repair systems playing crucial roles in ferroptosis resistance. Despite increasing research on ferroptosis, several challenges remain: the specific mechanism of POR-mediated PUFA-PL peroxidation catalysis independent of ALOXs remains unclear; the precise role of fragmented oxidation products in terminal plasma membrane rupture needs further investigation; and the exact location of ferroptosis-related lipid peroxidation remains undefined. Furthermore, regarding clinical translation, there are limited clinical trial drugs based on GPX4 antioxidant mechanisms, while comprehensive understanding of ferroptosis bioactivity is crucial for advancing clinical applications. Future research should focus on developing more targeted clinical intervention strategies, and promoting translation from basic research to clinical applications.

Ferroptosis demonstrates significant dual roles in tumor biology: functioning as a tumor suppression mechanism to limit tumor growth while potentially promoting tumor immune escape and metastasis. Its interaction with the TIME is particularly complex, involving dynamic crosstalk between tumor cells and immune cells including CD8 + T cells, macrophages, and NK cells. The DAMPs released during ferroptosis can activate immune responses, though their ultimate effects depend on the dynamic characteristics of the TME. Moreover, there is a close correlation between ferroptosis and genes related to tumors, including RAS, TP53, NFE2L2, and ATF4, as well as environmental factors including gut microbiota, EMT, and hypoxia. Given that an increasing number of drugs exert anticancer effects through ferroptosis mechanisms, accurately distinguishing ferroptosis from other forms of cell death becomes crucial. The debate over whether ferroptosis constitutes a form of ICD continues, and future research should employ comprehensive evaluation through multi-omics approaches including genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, while focusing on identifying specific biomarkers.

The combined application of ferroptosis inducers with traditional cancer treatments yields complex outcomes. Beyond synergistic anticancer effects, research has shown that ferroptosis may induce immunosuppression in the TME under specific conditions. In chemotherapy, ferroptosis may lead to drug resistance in lung, ovarian, and colorectal cancers through multiple factors including GPX4 regulatory systems, gut microbiota, and Nrf2; in immunotherapy, DAMPs released from ferroptotic tumor cells may induce macrophage polarization toward M2-like protumor phenotypes and reshape the TIME. Additionally, differential sensitivity to ferroptosis among immune cells may lead to increased T cell susceptibility. Developing nanotherapeutic strategies that can simultaneously induce ferroptosis and reverse immunosuppressive TME shows potential value. This review summarizes recent advances in nano-targeting platforms based on external and internal stimuli, demonstrating promising prospects in combination with various therapies. However, the development of nanodelivery systems still faces numerous challenges including biocompatibility, circulation stability, precise targeting capability, and biological barrier penetration ability, which require further in-depth research and technological innovation to address.

In conclusion, to fully realize the potential of ferroptosis in clinical cancer treatment, more high quality preclinical studies and rigorous clinical trials are needed. Notably, many reported “ferroptosis related agents” (e.g., sorafenib, erastin) were not specifically designed to induce ferroptosis, and their effects often involve crosstalk among multiple cell death or signaling pathways, complicating mechanistic interpretation and clinical application. Current evidence remains largely stratified, with most mechanistic insights derived from cell lines or animal models, and a pronounced lack of clinical translation data. Key barriers include the absence of validated, ferroptosis specific biomarkers for patient stratification and therapy monitoring, as well as limited understanding of ferroptosis-related gene signatures in different cancer contexts. Future work should focus on identifying ferroptosis-specific biomarkers and clarifying their clinical utility, ultimately achieving personalized precision medicine strategies based on individual patient characteristics.
