Quantitative analysis of pleural effusion after prone percutaneous nephrolithotomy based on routine postoperative abdominal computed tomography: prevalence, risk factors, and clinical implications
Authors: Yi Luo, Dan Luo, Genfang Lin, Kebing Yang, Mi Zhou, Xiaobo Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDetailed analysis of post-percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) pleural effusion (PE) and its relationship with respiratory symptoms is lacking. This study quantitatively investigates the prevalence, volume, and clinical significance of post-PCNL PEs detected on abdominal computed tomography (CT).MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 252 consecutive patients who underwent prone PCNL between January 2018 and April 2020. PEs and stone volume were quantified using three-dimensional (3D) segmentation of post- and pre-operative abdominal CT images, respectively. Respiratory symptoms were categorized as asymptomatic, mild, moderate, or severe based on vital signs and complaints documented in nursing records. Zero-inflated Gaussian models were used to analyze effusion volume, whereas ordinal regression analyses were employed to identify predictors of symptom severity.ResultsThe incidence of post-PCNL PE was 77.0%, with a mean volume of 31.8 mL [95% confidence interval (CI): 27.8–36.3]. Most patients (69.8%) were asymptomatic, yet 4.0% experienced severe symptoms, with only 1.2% requiring drainage. Notably, 27.8% of PE was not mentioned in radiology reports. PE volume showed moderate positive correlation with respiratory symptom severity (r=0.44, 95% CI: 0.33–0.53, P<0.001). A volume cutoff of 51.24 mL was identified as predictive of moderate-to-severe symptoms [area under the curve (AUC) =0.86, 95% CI: 0.78–0.93]. Supracostal puncture [odds ratio (OR) =2.02, 95% CI: 1.04–3.93; P=0.038], post-operative lymphocyte counts (OR =0.46, 95% CI: 0.25–0.87; P=0.016), and activated partial thromboplastin time (OR =1.11, 95% CI: 1.01–1.23; P=0.035) were significantly associated with PE occurrence. Although supracostal puncture was associated with greater PE volume (β=0.334, 95% CI: 0.071–0.597; P=0.013), postoperative lymphocyte count was associated with lower volume (β=−0.310, 95% CI: −0.577 to −0.043; P=0.023). Effusion volume (OR =1.02, 95% CI: 1.02–1.03; P<0.001) predicted respiratory symptom severity, whereas higher post-operative total protein levels (OR =0.94, 95% CI: 0.89–0.99; P=0.017) were associated with milder symptoms.ConclusionsPost-PCNL PE is common but frequently asymptomatic and under-reported in radiology reports. Clinical management should be tailored based on PE volume, supracostal puncture, and post-operative total protein levels, independent of stone burden. Enhanced awareness among radiologists may improve detection and reporting of this common complication.Keywords: Pleural effusion (PE), perioperative medicine, surgical complications, post-operative management, respiratory syndrome

Introduction

Urinary stones can cause renal colic, infection and kidney damage and are a major burden on the healthcare system. Although retrograde intrarenal surgery is increasingly adopted, percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) is still recommended as the first-line therapy for large or complex renal calculi, due to its high efficacy (1,2). However, PCNL carries substantial risks of complications, including bleeding, infection, and injury to surrounding structures. Given the anatomical proximity of renal puncture to the pleura and lung, the most common organs compromised are the diaphragm, pleura, and lungs, resulting in a pulmonary complication rate of 8–32.5% (3,4), one of the major postoperative concerns of medical staff.

Post-PCNL pleural effusions (PE) vary considerably in volume and clinical presentation, ranging from asymptomatic to causing significant respiratory distress requiring prompt recognition and intervention (5). Although most PCNL patients undergo postoperative abdominal computed tomography (CT) scans to assess outcomes such as stone clearance (6), we have observed that small-volume PEs are frequently overlooked both clinically and radiologically. This suggests that even clinically significant effusions may be underreported. There is little data on this underreporting phenomenon and the relationship between the volume of PE detected on abdominal CT and respiratory symptoms. This study aimed to quantitatively investigate PE following PCNL and its relationship with perioperative factors and respiratory symptoms. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1157/rc).

Methods

Study participants

All consecutive patients who underwent PCNL between January 2018 and March 2020 were identified through the Hospital Information System (HIS). April 2020 was selected as the endpoint due to the coronavirus disease of 2019 (COVID-19)’s potential to confound respiratory symptom assessment. All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation in the prone position. Patients routinely underwent abdominal CT scans on postoperative days 3–5 to assess stone clearance and detect complications. Initially, 373 patients older than 18 years were identified. The exclusion criteria were as follows: known lung or heart disease (n=12), solitary or transplanted kidney (n=5), postoperative hemothorax or pneumothorax (n=4), active postoperative bleeding requiring transarterial embolization (n=6), sepsis meeting Sepsis-3 criteria (n=19), preoperative hypoalbuminemia <35 g/L (n=24), history of any surgery within the preceding 3 months (n=8), incomplete perioperative CT scan data (n=27), and incomplete medical records or laboratory data (n=16). We excluded active bleeding, sepsis, and preoperative hypoalbuminemia, because each reflect systemic physiological stress known to generate PE by hemorrhagic accumulation or fluid resuscitation, inflammatory exudation, or low-oncotic transudation—thus confounding attribution to PCNL, as evidenced by their respective effusion rates of 66.7%, 84.2%, and 87.5% in our dataset. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital (No. QT2022433) and the requirement for individual consent for this analysis was waived due to the retrospective nature.

Data collection

Data were collected by systematically reviewing clinical notes, nursing records, surgical records, picture archiving and communication system (PACS), and laboratory information system by two independent reviewers. The two reviewers cross checked against each other. Respiratory symptom scores were determined via nursing records, which provided detailed hourly documentation of vital signs and patients’ complaints during the first 2–3 postoperative days. Symptoms were classified as asymptomatic [oxygen saturation (SpO2) ≥96%, normal respiratory rate, no dyspnea]; mild (minor symptoms without extra oxygen needs, SpO2 >93%); moderate (breathing distress, increased oxygen needs, or SpO2 ≤93% and >90%); and severe (PE drainage required or SpO2 ≤90%). This classification method maximized the utility of available clinical data in this retrospective study.

Abdominal CT scans were acquired using 64- or 128-row scanners without contrast during full inspiration in the supine position. The scan range extended from just above the diaphragmatic dome to below the ischial tuberosity, guided by scout radiographs. PE was defined as a dependent fluid-attenuation collection within the pleural space, distinguished from pleural thickening and adjacent parenchymal atelectasis. Two reviewers collaboratively segmented PE areas in both lungs using the 3D Slicer image computing platform (7), via manual segmentation with consensus-based verification. Effusion volumes were automatically calculated by 3D Slicer. A representative high-volume effusion case was selected to illustrate the segmentation and 3D reconstruction process (Figure 1). Stone burden was assessed using the KidneyStoneCalculator extension (8) via semi-automated segmentation with a threshold range of 110 Hounsfield units (HU) to maximum (Figure 2).

Data analysis

The analyses were performed using Python (3.9.7), along with packages of scikit-learn (0.24.2), tableone (0.7.10), pingouin (0.5.5), and R (3.6.3) with glmnetcr (1.0.6). To determine the diagnostic accuracy of radiological reports for PEs, manual reviews were used as the gold standard. Diagnostic performance was assessed using 2×2 contingency tables, and agreement between radiological reports and manual reviews was quantified using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient.

Continuous and categorical variables were summarized using standard descriptive statistics and compared between groups using appropriate parametric or non-parametric tests. Given that a substantial proportion of patients had no PE, a zero-inflated Gaussian model (ZIGM) was employed. Standard linear regression would be inappropriate due to the excess zeros violating normality assumptions, whereas dichotomizing the outcome would discard valuable volumetric information. ZIGM addresses this data structure by simultaneously modeling both the probability of effusion occurrence (logistic component) and the volume among those with effusion (linear component). Variable selection followed a multi-step approach: First, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) method was applied independently to both components, and the intersection of selected variables was identified. These overlapping variables were retained, and additional variables were subsequently incorporated based on clinical knowledge and significant univariate associations. Ordinal multinomial logistic regression with LASSO-based variable selection was used to evaluate respiratory symptom severity, as the outcome categories (asymptomatic, mild, moderate, severe) represent ordered but not equally spaced intervals, precluding standard linear regression. All tests were two-sided and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics and PE incidence

A total of 252 patients were included, with a median age of 53.0 years [interquartile range (IQR): 44.0–62.0 years]; 66.3% were male, and 42.5% underwent supracostal access. The mean stone burden was 3.8 [standard deviation (SD) 0.4] (log10-transformed volume in mm3). PE occurred in 77.0% of patients following PCNL, with a mean volume of 31.8 mL [95% confidence interval (CI): 27.8–36.3, log-normal distribution]. Regarding the severity of respiratory symptoms, 176 (69.8%), 48 (19.0%), 18 (7.1%), and 10 (4.0%) cases were rated as asymptomatic, mild, moderate, or severe, respectively. Volume of PE and respiratory symptoms

PE volume showed a moderate positive correlation with respiratory symptom severity (r=0.44, 95% CI: 0.33–0.53, P<0.001) (Figure 3). To establish a clinically applicable volume threshold, we re-categorized the severity of respiratory symptoms into two groups: asymptomatic/mild (negative group) and moderate/severe (positive group). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis showed an optimal cutoff of 51.2 mL, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.78–0.93) (Figure 4).

Radiological reporting of PE

No cases were incorrectly reported as having PE. However, 27.8% (54/194) of PE cases were not mentioned in radiological reports despite visible effusion on imaging. These unreported cases had significantly lower PE volumes compared to the reported cases (40.0 mL, 95% CI: 34.4–46.3 vs. 17.6 mL, 95% CI: 14.1–22.1, P<0.001), although the distributions showed substantial overlap (overlap coefficient =0.63). When evaluating radiological reports as a diagnostic test, the diagnostic accuracy was 78.6%, with 72.2% sensitivity, 100.0% specificity, and 51.8% negative predictive value. Agreement between radiological reports and manual review was moderate (Cohen’s kappa =0.54).

Statistical models for PE volume and respiratory symptom severity

We analyzed 57 parameters, including pre- and post-operative laboratory values, patient characteristics, and surgical variables (Table S1). The LASSO-selected variables common to both components of the ZIGM were puncture location, post-operative lymphocyte counts, and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT). Post-operative total protein levels, stone burden and operation duration were incorporated based on clinical experience, whereas C-reactive protein levels were added due to significant differences between effusion and non-effusion groups.

The logistic component of our ZIGM showed that supracostal puncture location [odds ratio (OR) =2.02, 95% CI: 1.04–3.93; P=0.038], post-operative lymphocyte counts (OR =0.46, 95% CI: 0.25–0.87; P=0.016), and post-operative APTT (OR =1.11, 95% CI: 1.01–1.23; P=0.035) were associated with PE occurrence. The linear component revealed that supracostal puncture location (β=0.334, 95% CI: 0.071–0.597; P=0.013) was positively associated with higher effusion volume, whereas postoperative lymphocyte count (β=−0.310, 95% CI: −0.577 to −0.043; P=0.023) showed a negative association. Post-operative total protein and procedure duration showed no significant associations in either component (Table 2).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first quantitative study to evaluate PE after PCNL. Our findings demonstrate that PE following PCNL, detected by abdominal CT scans, was a common occurrence, albeit typically of limited volume. Although abdominal CT covered only the lower part of the pleural cavity, our results could still represent the entire volumes, as the shape is similar longitudinally. Most patients were asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic, requiring no intervention. Our ZIGM demonstrated that post-operative lymphocyte counts were inversely associated with PE occurrence, whereas supracostal puncture and post-operative APTT showed positive correlations. Additionally, supracostal puncture positively correlated with PE volume. Ordinal regression revealed that respiratory symptom severity significantly correlated with effusion volume and post-operative total protein. Meanwhile, we found that the radiological reports often failed to diagnose PE, yielding suboptimal diagnostic accuracy for this readily identifiable finding on CT imaging.

PE is the most common pulmonary complication after PCNL (9), alongside pneumothorax/hemothorax, atelectasis, and pneumonia. Reported incidence varies substantially by access route and ascertainment method; our study addresses this by quantifying effusion volume on routine postoperative abdominal CT. Our study found a higher incidence of post-PCNL PE than previously reported in literature (2.3–8.6%) (9-12), likely due to our thorough review capturing even small volumes typically overlooked in routine reports. These results align with studies employing dedicated radiological analyses, revealing similarly high prevalence (13,14). Nevertheless, the intervention rate (1.2%) remained within established ranges (1.04–3.6%) (10,12,13,15,16), and we observed no PE-related mortality, consistent with literature. Our cases underwent prone PCNL exclusively, the predominant approach globally. Although some studies suggest higher pleural complication rates with prone PCNL, the latest meta-analysis found no significant difference in PE between positions (supine vs. prone: risk ratio =0.5564, 95% CI: 0.2830–1.0955, P=0.0898) (17), indicating that post-PCNL PE is multifactorial.

The exact etiology of PE after PCNL remains incompletely understood. Potential mechanisms include surgical pleural violation, intraoperative irrigation, and post-operative inflammatory response due to surgical trauma (3,14,18). Although several studies have investigated pulmonary complications post-PCNL, few have specifically focused on PE. Previous studies identified right-sided surgery, puncture location, and transfusion as risk factors for pulmonary complications post-PCNL (3,4). Our findings corroborate the significance of puncture location but demonstrate no significant association with operative side or transfusion. This disparity may be attributed to our specific focus on PE, in contrast to the broader spectrum of pulmonary complications examined in previous investigations. Notably, thoracic events are driven primarily by the level of renal access rather than position: a meta-analysis reported markedly higher odds of hydrothorax with supracostal vs. infracostal access (OR =10.47; 95% CI: 4.77–22.95) (19), consistent with our finding that supracostal puncture predicted both effusion occurrence and volume.

Our models revealed several clinically significant associations. The inverse relationship between post-operative lymphocyte counts and PE occurrence suggests that lower lymphocyte levels might reflect a heightened inflammatory response after surgery, potentially increasing vascular permeability and fluid extravasation. This finding is consistent with previous research (20) suggesting that inflammatory responses are associated with PE. The positive association between APTT and PE occurrence might indicate that alterations in coagulation status contribute to pleural fluid accumulation. Furthermore, our finding that higher post-operative total protein levels correlate with less severe respiratory symptoms could be explained by better maintained plasma oncotic pressure preventing fluid migration into the pleural space, or might reflect better overall nutritional status. However, whether effusion volume directly impairs respiratory mechanics or both findings reflect surgical trauma severity cannot be determined from our observational data. These physiological connections need further investigation in future studies.

We also addressed the underreporting of PE in radiological reports. Despite PEs being readily identifiable on CT scans (14), radiologists may overlook them due to their primary focus on abdominal pathology. Additional contributors may include the absence of a dedicated pleural-space item in abdominal CT reporting templates, time pressure in routine workflow, and the perception that small dependent effusions on limited lower-chest coverage are clinically insignificant and therefore not reported. This reporting inconsistency, reflected in the variable incidence rates documented in the literature, likely extends beyond our institution. Although we found volume differences between reported and unreported effusions, the minimal disparity and substantial distribution overlap (overlap coefficient =0.63) suggest that volume alone cannot account for this underreporting phenomenon, underscoring the need for enhanced radiological vigilance in post-PCNL imaging interpretation.

Our findings have several important implications. The identified cutoff of 51.24 mL provides a quantitative reference for evaluating respiratory risk in post-PCNL patients with routine post-operative abdominal CT. This cutoff should be interpreted as a trigger for heightened clinical vigilance (symptom and oxygenation monitoring) rather than an automatic threshold for invasive intervention, given the predominantly mild and self-limiting nature of most effusions. Patients undergoing supracostal puncture or presenting with reduced post-operative total protein levels warrant closer monitoring given their associations with larger PE volumes and more severe respiratory symptoms, respectively. Although most PEs following PCNL are asymptomatic and of minimal volume, our findings suggest that a risk-stratified approach, with targeted imaging for high-risk patients rather than routine chest CT for all PCNL patients, should be implemented. However, the substantial under-reporting rate (27.8%) highlights the need of careful pleural space evaluation in abdominal CTs, particularly in at-risk patients. Additional chest imaging (e.g., ultrasound or chest CT) may be considered in symptomatic patients or those with oxygenation decline and/or a larger quantified effusion, rather than applied routinely to all post-PCNL patients.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces inherent methodological constraints, including potential selection bias and limitations in data collection. In particular, respiratory symptoms were retrospectively abstracted from routine clinical/nursing documentation and may have been under-recorded or inconsistently described, leading to symptom under-capture and misclassification. A key limitation is the lack of standardized respiratory symptom scoring, which limits the precision and granularity of symptom severity assessment. Moreover, we could not collect data on intraoperative irrigation volume, encompassing potential pulmonary complication risk factors (4). Second, although abdominal CT enabled quantitative assessment of PE, the limited scan range (incomplete chest coverage) may underestimate total effusion volume and precludes comprehensive evaluation of other pulmonary complications (e.g., atelectasis or pulmonary edema) that could also contribute to respiratory symptoms. Third, as preoperative hypoalbuminemia and sepsis cases were excluded to maintain cohort homogeneity, our findings may not apply to septic settings. Fourth, all procedures were performed in the prone position, reflecting our institutional practice; therefore, our findings may not be generalizable to supine or other modified PCNL. Additionally, formal interobserver agreement metrics for PE segmentation were not calculated, as we employed a consensus-based approach rather than independent duplicate measurements. Finally, multicenter data are needed to confirm that under-reporting is prevalent.

Conclusions

This study presents the first quantitative analysis of PE on post-PCNL abdominal CT, revealing its high prevalence despite minimal intervention requirements. PE occurrence inversely correlates with post-operative lymphocyte counts and positive associations with supracostal puncture and elevated post-operative APTT. Supracostal puncture was also associated with increased PE volume. Respiratory symptom severity correlated significantly with both effusion volume and reduced post-operative total protein levels. The substantial under-reporting of PE in radiological reports identified in our study underscores the need for enhanced awareness and warrants validation through future multicenter investigations.
