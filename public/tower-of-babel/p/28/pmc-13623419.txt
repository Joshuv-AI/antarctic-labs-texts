Machine learning applications in androgenetic alopecia: a systematic review
Authors: Jay Huang, Eric McMullen, Sana Gupta, Katya Peri, Miranda Branyiczky, Joy Soliman, Daniel Rayner, Ilya Mukovozov, Jeffrey Donovan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs applications of artificial intelligence (AI) continue to grow in dermatology, it is necessary to understand the potential of artificial intelligence, including machine learning, in the treatment of androgenetic alopecia. Accordingly, this study aims to evaluate AI applications for AGA diagnosis, severity assessment and predicting treatment outcomes.

Dear Editor, Androgenetic alopecia (AGA) is the most common form of nonscarring hair loss and is characterized by progressive follicular miniaturization. In dermatology, applications of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) commonly include computer vision–based approaches for diagnosis and severity assessment. This study evaluated AI applications for AGA diagnosis and severity assessment, and predicting treatment outcomes.

Following PRISMA guidelines, we searched five databases (CRD420251128486) from inception to January 2025 using keywords related to ‘androgenetic alopecia’, ‘machine learning’ or ‘artificial intelligence’ [Figure 1; Table S1 (see Supporting Information)]. English-language studies evaluating AI for AGA diagnosis, severity assessment and outcome prediction were included (Table S2; see Supporting Information). Risk of bias was assessed using the PROBAST (Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool) checklist. Risk of bias was high in 47.1% of studies and low in 52.9% (Table S3; see Supporting Information).

PRISMA flowchart diagram of literature screening.

Fifteen retrospective cohort studies and two case series were included, consisting of image-based (n = 13/17) and non-image-based ML models (n = 4/17). Three studies were validated in clinical settings; the remaining were validated using retrospective datasets.

Computer vision techniques were used for AGA diagnosis and severity assessment. In one study, an AI-assisted camera system evaluated postoperative hair transplantation outcomes against preoperative baseline images.1 Two case series reported AI-powered quantification of hair density and longitudinal changes in scalp health during platelet-rich plasma treatment. Convolutional neural networks were the most commonly used ML algorithms (n = 7/17), often using transfer learning on pretrained models to improve performance and reduce training requirements.

Regarding non-image-based data, one retrospective gradient-boosted decision tree analysis in female-pattern hair loss (FPHL) identified age of onset and disease duration as the strongest predictors of six 24-month changes in hair density and calibre.2 Another study tracked AGA progression via a hidden Markov model, identifying elevated fasting glucose and hypertension as statistically significant risk factors. Metabolic syndrome was another contributor in patients with moderate-to-severe AGA, after adjusting for age, sex and family history.3

Two studies evaluated genetic markers to predict AGA in men. One study trained models using 117 single-nucleotide polymorphisms from genetic data of European men, achieving moderate discriminatory ability in predicting any vs. no hair loss [area under the curve (AUC) = 0.702]. External validation in a small independent cohort yielded similar results (AUC = 0.830).4 Another neural network analysis using five single-nucleotide polymorphisms differentiated patients with moderate-to-­severe AGA from controls, after adjusting for serum dihydrotestosterone levels within the reference range (AUC = 0.85).5

The advantages of AI in AGA management include provision of objective reproducible data for monitoring AGA progression. Computer vision can also detect small changes in hair quality, leading to earlier diagnosis. Disadvantages include high implementation costs and algorithmic bias. AI training data historically lack certain ethnoracial groups, which may lead to lower accuracy in these groups.

This review demonstrates AI’s utility in AGA diagnosis, severity grading and treatment monitoring, underscoring its potential expansion into dermatology. Future research should prioritize prospective real-world clinical validation, and expand data sets to include more cases of FPHL and a range of skin tones. AI integration across the disease course may enable earlier identification, facilitate timely treatment and potentially limit further disease progression.
