Emotional Context Modulates the Response to Somatosensory Stimuli Within 20 milliseconds
Authors: Luis Carretié, Vanessa E. DaSilva‐Lárez, Victoria Branchadell, Uxía Fernández‐Folgueiras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe processing of discrete stimuli is modulated by the emotional context in which they appear. According to the limited data available, this modulation could occur from the initial levels of processing. Our aim was to find the first trace of emotional context modulation in the neural response to discrete stimuli presented within that context. We focused on the somatosensory domain, characterized by faster and better localized responses within the ascending pathway than the more widely studied visual modality. Early components of somatosensory evoked potentials (SEPs), particularly those reflecting the thalamic and primary somatosensory cortex activity occurring within the first 20 milliseconds from stimulus onset, were analyzed to test whether they are already affected by the emotional context. Participants (n = 37) received somatosensory stimulation via electrical pulses over the median nerve of the right arm while viewing 12 stereoscopic (3D) images of animals categorized as emotionally positive, negative, or neutral to generate different affective contexts. SEPs elicited by somatosensory stimuli—which were identical in all contexts‐ were recorded through a bipolar CP3‐CP4 channel, revealing a P17 (originating in the thalamus) and an N20 (primary somatosensory cortex or S1) component. The negative contexts evoked significantly smaller P17‐N20 peak‐to‐peak amplitudes than positive and neutral contexts, an attenuation of somatosensory perception probably reflecting a rapid defensive mechanism. These results reveal the earliest emotion‐modulated brain response described to date in humans (17–20 milliseconds and thalamic/S1 origin) and point to the somatosensory ascending pathway as an active emotional processor.

Introduction

The processing of discrete stimuli is modulated by the emotional context in which they appear. In the laboratory, this context is usually generated through previous or concurrent images or texts presenting different affective valence. For example, neutral stimuli are memorized differently (both in the encoding and retrieval stages: Erk et al. 2005), capture attention to varying degrees (Gao et al. 2022), or cause variable response inhibition (Albert et al. 2010), depending on the emotional load of the context. Importantly, perception, the earliest processing stage, interacts with emotion in a bidirectional way (see reviews by Niedenthal and Wood 2019; Vuilleumier and Driver 2007). Thus, visual perception, the most studied modality in this regard, is also sensitive to the affective context. For example, neutral visual stimuli are perceived with a higher level of contrast after a fearful facial expression than after a neutral expression (Phelps et al. 2006), and are perceived as larger when they are preceded by a positive word than when preceded by a negative or neutral word (Veltkamp et al. 2008). The activity of sensory ascending pathways—the gateway to perception—is modulated top‐down through back projections from high‐order regions in the brain. Thus, in the visual modality, cortico‐cortical back projections to V1 and other early visual cortices are well‐known (e.g., Salin and Bullier 1995), as are cortico‐thalamic projections to the lateral geniculate nucleus (e.g., Marrocco et al. 1996). This top‐down mechanism would allow centralized or high‐order emotional processes generated by the context to modulate the perceptual input.

This study aimed to investigate the first trace of emotional context modulation in the processing of discrete stimuli presented within that context, which led us to focus on the sensory route. In this regard, sensory modalities other than vision would be of utmost interest, not only because of the need to better understand non‐visual modalities (on the bias towards vision in cognitive neuroscience: Hutmacher 2019), but also because they are characterized by shorter latencies in their ascending sensory pathways. For example, while the first visual cortex activity in humans is produced at 40 ms from stimulus onset (e.g., Carretié et al. 2024; Proverbio et al. 2021), the first response of the somatosensory cortex is recorded at 20 ms (e.g., Eisen 1982; Muzyka and Estephan 2019). The somatosensory modality is of special interest in the affective domain due to the embodied emotion perception phenomenon: perceiving visual or auditory emotional stimuli is usually accompanied by somatosensory sensations in different parts of the body (Nummenmaa et al. 2014) and by activity in somatosensory cortices (Kragel and LaBar 2016; Pitcher et al. 2008). Crucially, somatosensory perception—the one on which we will focus‐ is sensitive to the emotional context. For example, seeing certain insects produces itching sensations in some circumstances (van de Burgt et al. 2023), and somatosensory stimuli presented while viewing negative images are felt as less pleasant than when the same tactile stimuli are administered with positive pictures (Etzi et al. 2018). Moreover, fear reduces tactile sensitivity (Kelley and Schmeichel 2014). In fact, in the somatosensory modality, there are top‐down projections to the early stages of sensory processing, allowing for contextual modulation, both cortico‐cortical to the primary somatosensory cortex or S1 (Vogt and Pandya 1978) and cortico‐thalamic to the ventral posterolateral nucleus (Zhang and Deschênes 1997).

Evoked potentials are of particular interest to our objectives due to their unmatched temporal resolution compared to other noninvasive techniques. Particularly, somatosensory evoked potentials (SEPs) have been very well defined and standardized in the literature in terms of stimulation protocols, component definition, and source location of the main components within the somatosensory ascending route (e.g., Becker et al. 2017; Eisen 1982; Markand 2020; Muzyka and Estephan 2019). Two previous studies by Ravaja et al. (2017) and Sel et al. (2014) demonstrate the usefulness of SEPs to explore the early effects of the emotional context. The first group stimulated, via pressure, the back of the hand while participants observed the expression of the person (a semi‐realistic male avatar) who supposedly touched them, and observed a SEP amplitude modulation by the emotional faces from 25 ms (Ravaja et al. 2017). Source localization analyses pointed to the association somatosensory cortex (BA 5) as the main focus (Ravaja et al. 2017). Sel and colleagues mechanically stimulated one finger after the onset of different facial expressions, and they observed a significant effect of these faces on SEP amplitudes from 40 ms, this activity being located at S1 (BA1/2/3) by source analyses (Sel et al. 2014).

Both studies mark the starting point in the search for the earliest emotional modulation of somatosensory perception by centralized emotion representations, both in temporal terms (25 ms: Ravaja et al. 2017) and spatial terms (S1: Sel et al. 2014). To advance beyond these milestones, we resorted to the standardized median nerve stimulation protocol, given its ability to elicit even earlier SEP components, both in temporal and spatial terms. Thus, the N18/P18 (18 ms) component evoked by this protocol reflects the processing of the haptic input at the ventral posterolateral nucleus (VPL) of the thalamus, and P20/N20 (20 ms) is the first SEP component originating in the somatosensory cortex (e.g., Eisen 1982; Muzyka and Estephan 2019; the polarity and latency of SEP components may vary depending on the location of the reference electrode). The specific objective of this exploratory study was to test whether these two components are already modulated by the emotional context. Regardless of whether the results are confirmatory or not, their theoretical implications are highly relevant for understanding early emotional processing. Specifically, it is still unclear whether the first neural structures that show differential activity depending on the emotional meaning of sensory information are post‐perceptual (e.g., the amygdala and other limbic and paralimbic structures: Adolphs 2008; Costafreda et al. 2008; Ohman 2002; Zald 2003) or whether the ascending sensory pathways themselves, from the sensory organs to the primary sensory cortices, are already sensitive to affective information (“perceptual” proposals: Carretié et al. 2021; Kryklywy et al. 2020; Méndez et al. 2022). Observing differential responses around 18–20 ms (and therefore originating in S1 or even earlier) to physically invariant stimuli presented in different contexts would support the perceptual hypothesis by showing that the structures of the ascending somatosensory route are active emotional processors rather than passive transmitters of information.

Methods

Data and Supporting Information Availability

The data associated with this experiment is available at an Open Science Framework repository (https://osf.io/jpg9c/). Supporting Information mentioned hereafter is also available at that link.

Participants

Forty‐four individuals participated in this experiment, although data from only 37 of them could eventually be analyzed due to the insufficiency of valid trials or to technical issues, as detailed below. These 37 participants (age range from 17 to 29 years, mean = 19.65, SD = 2.18, 33 right‐handed, 29 women) were students of Psychology, provided their written informed consent, and received academic compensation for their participation. This final sample size slightly exceeded that calculated a priori 1 for a statistical power = 0.8 using G × Power 3.1. software (Faul et al. 2007): n = 36. The study was designed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and had been previously approved by the Universidad Autónoma de Madrid's Ethics Committee.

Stimuli and Procedure

Participants were placed in an electrically shielded, sound‐attenuated room. They were seated so their eyes were ~60 cm from the screen (VIEWpixx, 120 Hz, 24‐in. size) throughout the experiment. Two modalities of stimulation were presented to participants: somatosensory and visual (Figure 1). Somatosensory stimuli consisted of a train of electrical constant current square‐wave pulses applied over the median nerve of the right arm. Pulses were administered through a DSM7 stimulator (Digitimer Ltd.) and controlled through Matlab thanks to the MMBS trigger interface box (Neurospec). To this aim, the cathode was placed at 2 cm from the wrist crest, between the tendons of the flexor carpi radialis and the palmaris longus muscles, and the anode 2–3 cm distal to the cathode (e.g., Becker et al. 2017; Muzyka and Estephan 2019). Each pulse lasted 0.2 ms, and the average inter‐pulse interval was 125 ms (i.e., pulses were administered at an average rate of 8 pulses per second). However, a jitter was introduced in the inter‐pulse interval, so it varied from 100 to 150 ms to avoid stimulus‐EEG rhythm coupling, especially in the high‐theta, low‐alpha band. The intensity was previously calibrated (before the stimulation and recording run) for each participant starting at 0 mA, and gradually and manually raised until the participant felt it unequivocally (this was confirmed by administering several sporadic pulses without prior warning) but never reaching an uncomfortable level.

Schematic representation of the stimulation sequence. Each of the 12 contexts (presented in 3D), four per emotional condition, was presented for 180 s. The median nerve was electrically stimulated 480 times during the last 60 s within each context (each electrical pulse lasted 0.2 ms, and inter‐pulse interval varied from 100 to 150 ms, average 125 ms). A single (spider) context is depicted. Actual stimuli were realistic (see Supporting Information).

Visual stimuli consisted of 12 stereoscopic images of different animals. The sizes of each image were 27° (height) × 47° (width). These stereoscopic images (i.e., presenting a different perspective for the left and the right eye) were perceived through 3D glasses to increase ecological validity and the emotional effect of these pictures. Specifically, we employed 3DPixx/Nvidia LCD shutter glasses that occluded one eye at a time, 60 times/s per eye, in congruence with the refresh rate of the monitor (120 Hz). This setting led the participant to perceive a different image in each eye and recreated a three‐dimensional space (in front of and behind the actual screen plane). The 12 stereoscopic images were of three categories: emotionally positive, negative and neutral (four images per category): see supplemental material. These images were selected from an initial pool of 45 3D pictures, all freely available on the internet, based on their spatial frequencies and after a previous evaluation from an independent sample of participants (n = 45) who rated them in terms of emotional valence and arousal. The selected positive, negative and neutral images presented spatial frequencies that differed by less than 23% (of the total range of spatial frequencies) from each other in all analyzed bands. This final selection of pictures was also evaluated by the experimental sample itself (n = 37), who also rated them through a Likert scale in Valence (ranging from 1 = “Very negative” to 5 = “Very positive”; 3=“Neutral”) and Arousal (from 1 = “Very relaxing” to 5 = “Very arousing”; 3 = “Neutral”). Two repeated‐measures ANOVAs on these ratings, one for Valence and the other for Arousal, showed significant differences in both dimensions (see Table 1 for details). Additionally, post hoc tests (Holm‐Bonferroni correction) confirmed that each emotional category significantly differed from the rest both in Valence and Arousal (see Table 2 and Figure 2 for details).

The 12 stereoscopic images were presented in random order for 180 s each. The first 120 s were without somatosensory stimulation. This only‐visual phase ensured participants perceived each picture in a 3D fashion (some participants needed some seconds to stereoscopically perceive the images, especially those stimuli presenting more binocular divergence) and contributed to generating an affective state. After these 2 min, and with the image still presented, somatosensory stimulation was presented for 60 s at the rate described above, yielding a total of 480 somatosensory pulses per image and 1920 (480 × 4) pulses per condition. During this visual plus somatosensory stimulation, a green fixation dot (RGB = 0, 255, 0) appeared in the center of the screen. To facilitate a constant level of attention to visual stimuli, the dot color randomly changed from green to red (RGB = 255, 0, 0) during a whole somatosensory trial (125 ms average), which was eliminated for analysis, and from 1 to 4 times in each picture. This affected one somatosensory trial, which was eliminated for analysis. Participants were instructed to look continuously at the fixation dot and to mentally count the number of color changes. After each image presentation and somatosensory stimulation, participants were asked to report the number of color changes in the fixation dot, to report whether the picture was indeed perceived in 3D or not, and to evaluate the valence and arousal of images through the Likert scales described above (presented on the screen), to which they responded verbally. One participant reported doubts about whether they had actually seen three of the 12 pictures (of different emotional categories) in 3D, and another expressed doubts about one picture. Another participant deviated more than one color change from the correct answer in one of the 12 images, and none of the others exceeded this deviation. We considered that these deviations were not sufficiently relevant to exclude these three participants from the analyses.

Recording and Pre‐Processing

Electroencephalographic (EEG) activity was recorded using an electrode active cap (Biosemi) with Ag‐AgCl electrodes, in which the EEG signal is preamplified at each electrode. Ten active electrodes were analyzed: the two target electrodes (CP3 and CP4 forming a bipolar channel as recommended in the literature describing the median nerve stimulation protocol; e.g., Becker et al. 2017; Muzyka and Estephan 2019), and eight electrodes surrounding both so we could interpolate those two target locations if necessary (C3, CP5, CP1, and P3 in the case of CP3; C4, CP2, CP6, and P4 in the case of CP4). Only one participant required interpolation of one of the two target electrodes. The ground electrode was placed in the right forearm, and recordings were referenced online to the nosetip. CP3 and CP4 (original or interpolated) were set offline as a bipolar channel. Additionally, electrooculographic (EOG) data were recorded supra‐ and infraorbitally (vertical EOG) as well as from the left versus right orbital rim (horizontal EOG) to detect blinking and ocular deviations from the fixation point. No online analog filters were established. Recordings were continuously digitized at a sampling rate of 1024 Hz. An offline digital Butterworth bandpass filter of 10 to 500 Hz (2nd order, zero‐phase forward and reverse—twopass—filter) was applied to continuous (pre‐epoched) data using the Fieldtrip software (http://fieldtrip.fcdonders.nl; Oostenveld et al. 2011). The continuous recording was divided into 60 ms epochs per trial, beginning 10 ms before the somatosensory stimulus onset.

As indicated, EEG epochs corresponding to trials in which the fixation dot changed its color (see the previous section) were eliminated, to avoid the effect of this control, irrelevant (to our scopes) task. Blinking‐derived artifacts were removed through an independent component analysis (ICA)‐based strategy (Jung et al. 2000), as provided in Fieldtrip. After the ICA‐based removal process, a second stage of inspection of the EEG data was conducted to automatically discard trials in which any EEG channel surpassed ±40 μV and/or its average global amplitude (i.e., maximum minus minimum amplitude) across trials ±3.5 standard deviations. Data from participants not reaching at least 1000 accepted trials in any of the three conditions (i.e., positive, negative and neutral 3D images), which is the minimum recommended in the median nerve stimulation procedure (e.g., Markand 2020; Muzyka and Estephan 2019), were excluded from analysis. This occurred in six of the excluded participants (see Participants section), and data from another had to be discarded due to a failure in the interface responsible for marking the temporal onset of somatosensory stimuli in the EEG recording (i.e., “triggers” were not stored in several blocks). This trial and participant rejection procedure led to the average admission of 1576 (SD = 188), 1548 (206), and 1574 (172) trials for neutral, positive and negative conditions, respectively, the difference among stimulus categories being non‐significant (F(2, 70) = 1.328, p = 0.272, ƞ 2 p = 0.037).

Data Analysis

After baseline correction, the first analytic task was identifying and quantifying the relevant SEP components. As may be appreciated in Figure 3, a N20 component is clearly visible in grand averages, and also ‐although less pronounced‐ a P17. We decided to measure the differential P17‐N20 amplitude, or peak to peak amplitude, a classical way of computing amplitudes (e.g., Begleiter et al. 1980; Hillyard and Picton 1978; Verleger and Cohen 1978) that has recently been revealed as useful to explore early components (e.g., Carretié et al. 2022, 2024). Two advantages of this measure may be underlined. First, it is less susceptible to be affected by data processing settings such as the high‐pass filter cut frequency, which significantly affects traditional (monophasic) amplitude measures in early components (Acunzo et al. 2012; Widmann and Schröger 2012), or by the characteristics of the baseline (e.g., its length or its regularity), another critical aspect in this regard. Second, it allows to quantify neural processes transversally affecting neighbor components by modulating absolute amplitude in both, as is apparently the case here (Figure 3).

Grand averages of the bipolar CP3‐CP4 recordings obtained in the whole sample (n = 37). The peak‐to‐peak amplitude is schematically depicted for Positive contexts to illustrate this amplitude measurement method. A filter was applied to the 0–4 ms window to attenuate the electrical stimulation spike for graphical purposes only. “Shadows” surrounding lines represent the standard error of means.

To measure P17 and N20 amplitudes (prior to computing their peak‐to‐peak difference), the windows of interest (WOIs) were ±3 ms for each peak: 14–20 ms for P17, and 17–23 ms for N20. The maximum value within the former WOI per participant and condition was set as the P17 amplitude, and the minimum value within the latter WOI, as the N20 amplitude. Finally, the individual difference between P17 and N20 amplitudes was calculated for each condition (Figure 3). Peak‐to‐peak P17‐N20 amplitudes were then submitted to repeated‐measures ANOVAs on factor Emotion (three levels: positive, negative, and neutral) using JASP software (JASP Team 2021). Post hoc comparisons to determine the significance of pairwise contrasts were performed using the Holm‐Bonferroni correction procedure. The Greenhouse–Geisser epsilon (G–G) correction to adjust the degrees of freedom was applied when appropriate, and the partial eta‐squared formula (η 2 p ) was employed to compute effect sizes.

Results

Figure 3 shows the grand averages of SEPs elicited by somatosensory stimuli in the three emotional conditions: positive, negative and neutral 3D images. As may be appreciated, the two components of interest, P17 and N20, are patent in the grand average. Table 3 and Figure 4 show the average values and the individual distribution for the P17‐N20 peak‐to‐peak amplitude. Repeated‐measures ANOVAs on these peak‐to‐peak amplitudes revealed a significant effect of Emotion (F(2, 72) = 4.460, G–G corrected p = 0.015, η 2 p = 0.110). Post hoc pairwise comparisons using the Holm‐Bonferroni correction showed that P17‐N20 peak‐to‐peak amplitude was significantly smaller during the Negative images than during Positive and Neutral (p = 0.025, Cohen's d = 0.285, 95% CI for d [0.016, 0.533] and p = 0.049, d = 0.258, 95% CI for d [−0.028, 0.543], respectively).

We also analyzed the relationship between these effects and the emotional content of stimuli as rated by participants, specifically whether the somatosensory effects were better explained by Valence or Arousal of the contextual visual stimuli. Since neither Valence nor Arousal fit normality (Shapiro–Wilk test p = 0.014 and 0.050, respectively), we computed non‐parametric (Spearman) correlations. On the one hand, we performed simple or zero‐order correlations, Valence showing greater association with N17‐N20 than Arousal, but not reaching significance (r = 0.160, p = 0.094, z = 0.161, and r = −0–013, p = 0.893, z = −0.013, respectively). On the other hand, since Valence and Arousal were highly ‐and inversely‐ correlated (r = −0.721, p < 0.001, z = −0.909), we additionally performed two partial non‐parametric correlations to better isolate the association of each of both emotional dimensions with P17‐N20 amplitudes. These correlation analyses were exploratory given that in this case the required sample size in terms of statistical power was not determined a priori. In one of them we analyzed the correlation between P17‐N20 and Valence controlling for Arousal, which resulted significant (ρ = 0.219, p = 0.022, z = 0.222). In the other, we computed a Spearman correlation between P17‐N20 and Arousal controlling for Valence, with no significant results (ρ = 0.152, p = 0.133, z = 0.153).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to find the earliest evidence of emotional context modulation in the processing of discrete somatosensory stimuli. Importantly, the context was not generated by somatosensory stimuli themselves—which were identical in neutral, positive, and negative contexts‐ but by visual stimuli that, as later discussed, generated a centralized emotional state capable of influencing perception through top‐down mechanisms. Previous studies have demonstrated that the emotional context influences how the same haptic stimulus is felt (Etzi et al. 2018; Kelley and Schmeichel 2014), and that this influence may be already patent in the ascending sensory route from 25 ms (Ravaja et al. 2017) or 40 ms (Sel et al. 2014). We generated the emotional context via the stereoscopic presentation of different animals, which provided more realistic and ecological visual images than usual 2D pictures. Results show that the invariant somatosensory stimuli evoke different amplitudes in P17‐N20 as a function of the contextual emotional load. This P17‐N20 complex reflects the flow of information from the VPL nucleus of the thalamus to the primary somatosensory cortex or S1 (Eisen 1982; Muzyka and Estephan 2019). To our knowledge, this novel finding reflects the earliest sign of emotional influence on perception ‐and therefore on neural processing‐ described to date for any sensory modality.

The capability of emotional visual stimuli to modulate the activity of S1 has been previously reported. Thus, Sel and colleagues found different activity in this cortex in response to the left index finger stimulation when participants viewed angry faces as compared to happy faces (Sel et al. 2014), and angry compared to sad faces (Sel et al. 2020). This effect was observed in the 40–60 ms latency window and the 225–258 ms, respectively. These results show, on the one hand, that S1 may indeed be modulated by the emotional visual context. On the other hand, these latencies suggest that this activity does not reflect the initial arrival of somatosensory information to the primary cortex, but probably recurrent processing after receiving feedback information from other sensory modalities and/or multimodal brain areas.

In our study, the emotional effects are observed much earlier (17–20 ms), a latency that, in the median nerve stimulation protocol, corresponds to the ascending course of the haptic input, specifically the thalamocortical stage (Eisen 1982; Muzyka and Estephan 2019). The use of (i) a higher cut‐off frequency in the low‐pass filter (40 Hz maximum in previous studies on SEPs during emotional visual stimulation—a value that may leave out the earliest SEP components‐, 500 Hz in our case), (ii) a higher number of trials per emotional condition in order to increase the signal‐to‐noise ratio (300 maximum per condition in previous studies, 1920 in our case), and the canonical procedure of median nerve stimulation, which evokes well established components with a known latency and neural source, have probably contributed to make these effects patent. Specifically, we found that the negative context elicited smaller P17‐N20 amplitude than non‐negative contexts, and that this effect was more related to emotional valence than to emotional arousal. However, such dimensional bias should be taken with caution and require additional research, as these exploratory correlation analyses, of exploratory nature, may have been underpowered. This result is in line with data showing decreased tactile sensitivity in participants experiencing fear (Kelley and Schmeichel 2014). In our opinion, this finding suggests that tactile perception may be attenuated in an emotionally threatening context, probably as a rapid defensive mechanism against possible harmful skin contact. This hypothesis should be confirmed through future experimental approaches to this scarcely studied topic.

As mentioned in the introduction, these results have important implications at the theoretical level regarding the initial stages of emotional processing. In the post‐perceptual hypotheses, limbic and paralimbic regions (amygdala, anterior insula, ACC, and OFC, among others) are the structures responsible for the first differential responses to emotional stimuli reaching them through sensory pathways (Adolphs 2008; Costafreda et al. 2008; Ohman 2002; Zald 2003). In contrast, perceptual hypotheses postulate that the sensory ascending pathways themselves are the first to respond differently to stimuli with different affective load (Carretié et al. 2021; Kryklywy et al. 2020; Méndez et al. 2022). This rapid emotion‐dependent perceptual activity is modulated by contextual information thanks to top‐down or back projections from higher‐order ‐or centralized‐ structures to the ascending somatosensory pathway (Vogt and Pandya 1978; Zhang and Deschênes 1997). Such back projections would allow the centralized emotion representations generated by the affective context, as well as the emotional states derived from it (given the advantage of visual stimuli over other modalities or procedures to induce emotions: Siedlecka and Denson 2019), to modulate perception. Considering the observed latencies, this top‐down modulation appears to generate a “sensory state” that persists as long as the context remains unchanged, rather than mediating the sensory response to each discrete stimulus presented within that context.

Our results suggest that the nature of these top‐down mechanisms regulating the somatosensory input appears to be heterogeneous. Within the nociceptive domain, top‐down affective (and cognitive) modulation mechanisms of painful somatosensory perception are a widely studied topic, especially in the context of gate control theory (e.g., Melzack and Casey 1968; Melzack and Katz 2004). In this case, this descending regulation is ultimately executed at the spinal level, mainly in the dorsal horn, the same place where painful inputs first arrive from the skin to the central nervous system via peripheral afferent Aδ and C fibers (Heinricher et al. 2009; Ossipov et al. 2010; Tracey and Mantyh 2007). Thus, cognitive and affective brain circuits send back projections to this area and either inhibit (close the gate to pain) or do not inhibit (open the gate) Aδ and C inputs to the dorsal horn (Heinricher et al. 2009). As for non‐painful somatosensory perception, top‐down control mechanisms have been explored much less. In this case, haptic inputs are transmitted by Aβ fibers, which have a much higher conduction velocity (50 m/s on average; more than three and 40 times faster than Aδ and C fibers, respectively: Erlanger and Gasser 1930). Therefore, the emotional modulation of P17‐N20 observed in our study reflects an extremely rapid gating mechanism involving exclusively Aβ afferences. These results point to a top‐down gating mechanism of somatosensory perception different from that described in the gate control theory (which inherently involves the activity of Aδ and C fibers, as explained), at least in the non‐nociceptive domain, a possibility that needs further study.

Regarding possible alternative interpretations of the observed effects, one question that may arise is whether the observed effects can be explained, at least in part, by autonomic changes. Specifically, whether the stereoscopic images caused variable electrodermal activity (EDA) depending on their emotional content, thus causing variable skin conductance and, ultimately, different intensities of the electrical stimulus in each condition. However, two lines of evidence make this possibility very remote. First, the eccrine glands (responsible for EDA) are low in density on the inner wrist, where the stimulation electrodes were placed. Indeed, the wrist is characterized by having the lowest EDA responsiveness as compared to other areas of the body (Payne et al. 2016). Second, even if an EDA effect occurred, it should have led to effects opposite to those we found. Negative images were the most arousing (Table 1) and EDA is highly sensitive to the emotional arousal of the stimulation (rather than to emotional valence): skin conductance increases as arousal increases (e.g., Lang et al. 1993; Sato et al. 2020). Therefore, the greater amplitude in SEP components should have occurred in the negative context, since the amplitude of early SEP components, including those analyzed in this experiment, increases as the intensity of stimulation in the median nerve increases (Gerber and Meinck 2000). However, in our experiment, negative stimuli evoked the lowest amplitudes. In any case, the autonomic mediation of SEPs evoked by median nerve stimulation is a relevant issue that deserves further exploration in the future.

In conclusion, these results confirm that emotional processes influence our perception of the environment and, importantly, they also show that such influence occurs earlier than previous studies suggested (17–20 ms from stimulus onset), affecting S1 and earlier thalamic function. Neural strategies that allow emotional information, such as an aversive situation, to modulate our processing of the environment from its earliest stages would have a clear evolutionary advantage, and this study confirms the suitability of evoked potentials as a tool to explore such rapid mechanisms. This tool, combined with methodological implementations to increase the signal‐to‐noise ratio and the use of realistic stimulation, is indeed necessary in this field of research. Future evoked potential studies should explore this issue in other sensory modalities. For example, testing whether the initial components of the auditory evoked potential, such as the component VI and middle‐latency components of auditory evoked potentials, originated in the thalamus (medial geniculate nuclei) and the primary auditory cortex, respectively (e.g., Seubert and Herman 2017), are also sensitive to the affective context. In any case, our findings reveal the earliest affective modulation detected to date in humans and confirm the active role of ascending sensory pathways in emotional processes. Although, as indicated, the origin of each component of SEPs evoked within the framework of the median nerve stimulation paradigm is clearly defined in the literature, it would also be interesting to continue investigating this in different emotional contexts, such as those presented here, to examine possible modulations at neural source level.
