Global and regional molecular epidemiology of HIV-1 among men who have sex with men: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ruiyu Li, Yin Gao, Ting Song, Nan Li, Rui Deng, Feng Jiao, Chaofang Yan, Ying Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe diversity of HIV-1 genotypes among Men who have sex with men (MSM) globally has changed considerably. The purpose of this study to assess the global prevalence of HIV-1 genotypes among MSM.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science were systematically searched to identify the articles. Pooled prevalence of HIV-1 genotypes was calculated and subgroup analyses were performed to examine the prevalence estimates across time and locations.ResultsA total of 95 studies were included in the final analysis, including 84,622 successfully genotyped samples. The predominant strains were CRF01_AE (34.46%), subtype B (31.16%), and CRF07_BC (24.72%). In subgroup analyses, Subtype B and C showed a declining trend over the years. However, CRF07_BC exhibited a consistent year-on-year increase, while CRF01_AE experienced a slight reduction after 2018. Notably, both subtypes currently account for more than 35% of the total. In addition, the distribution of HIV-1 subtypes in this population shown a clear regional distribution. Regionally, subtype B predominated in Latin America and Europe, CRF01_AE and CRF07_BC in Asia and China, while subtype C and CRF02_AG were dominant in Africa and the Middle East.ConclusionsGlobal and regions MSM HIV-1 subtypes are becoming more complex over time and the prevalence of recombinant viruses is increasing. Ongoing and effective surveillance of the global and regional molecular epidemiology of HIV-1 in MSM is critical for developing targeted preventive control measures against HIV.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12981-025-00776-y.

Introduction

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection and prevalence continue to pose a significant public health challenge worldwide. According to the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS), approximately 39.9 million people were living with HIV globally in 2023 [1]. Despite advancements in the effectiveness of antiretroviral treatment, approximately 630,000 individuals died from AIDS-related illnesses, and 1.3 million new infections were reported by the end of 2023.

Men who have sex with men (MSM) represent one of the five key populations that are particularly vulnerable to HIV infection due to high-risk behaviors [2], including unprotected anal sex, having multiple sexual partners, and injecting drugs [3]. Currently, the HIV epidemic among MSM continues to expand, characterized by persistently high prevalence rates and significant regional variations [4]. The global median HIV prevalence among MSM is 7.7%, with rates ranging from 0% in Samoa to 34% in the Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela [2]. Research indicates that the risk of HIV infection among MSM is 23 times higher than that in the general population in 2022 [1]. MSM play a crucial role as a bridge for the transmission of HIV from high-risk groups to the general population [4], significantly influencing the speed, scope, and intensity of HIV transmission within the broader community [5]. Therefore, to effectively prevent and control the spread and evolution of HIV, it is essential to prioritize the identification and support of HIV-infected individuals within the MSM community.

A key characteristic of the HIV pandemic is the diversity of its genotypes and the high degree of genetic variability [6]. Since the transmission of HIV from primates to humans in the 20th century [7], the HIV-1 group M viruses have undergone extensive genetic mutation, resulting in the emergence of multiple subtypes, including subtypes A, B, C, D, F, G, H, J, and K [8]. Recombinants formed between these subtypes are classified as circulating recombinant forms (CRFs) or unique recombinant forms (URFs). CRFs are defined as individuals recombinantly derived from different subtypes of HIV-1 group M that are epidemiologically unrelated, specifically involving three or more strains; otherwise, they are classified as URFs [9]. As of now, 157 CRFs have been documented in the Los Alamos National Laboratory HIV Sequence Database (https://www.hiv.lanl.gov/components/sequence/HIV/crfdb/crfs.comp). While specific data on URFs are less readily available, they also make a significant contribution to the pool of recombinants [10]. Furthermore, CRFs can recombine with other subtypes or CRFs, resulting in the formation of second-generation recombinants (SGRs) [11]. The spread and evolution of the HIV virus have led to a diverse array of genotypes, contributing to the varied distribution of HIV-1 subtypes, CRFs, and URFs across the global.

Over the past few years, the diversity of HIV-1 genetic diversity among MSM globally has changed considerably. Such changes significantly contribute to disease progression and dissemination, posing substantial challenges to the diagnosis and quantification of viral load, antiretroviral response, and vaccine development [6, 12]. Accurate information regarding the distribution of HIV subtypes and recombinants is crucial for enhancing prevention efforts and interventions aimed at further reducing HIV transmission. Consequently, long-term molecular epidemiological surveillance of HIV-1 in MSM community is essential [9, 10].

To date, only one global survey [13] covering the period from 1990 to 2015 has reported on the geographic distribution and temporal trends of HIV-1 genotypes in MSM populations. However, the synthesis of data from more recent studies remains insufficient. Consequently, the purpose of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to synthesize the existing literatures to assess the global prevalence of HIV-1 genotypes transmitted through MSM, explore the geographic distribution and dynamic trends of HIV-1 genotypes over time, and provide a more nuanced understanding of the current status of HIV-1 prevalence among MSM worldwide.

Methods

Systematic literature review

This systematic review was registered with PROSPERO: number CRD42024607389 and steered by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [14], which provides a comprehensive and standardized reporting framework. We conducted a systematic search of the literature in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science for relevant studies reporting HIV-1 subtyping data among men who have sex with men (MSM). A reference search of the included literature was performed simultaneously. The articles considered for inclusion were published up to September 10, 2024, with a restriction to those written in English. We employed Mesh headings, free-text terms, and synonyms in our database search, including “Men Who Have Sex with Men”, “HIV”, “network”, “epidemiology” and “recombinant”. The search process and terms utilized are detailed in the Appendix of Supplement 1. All search results were managed using EndNote reference management software, and duplicate entries were removed. Researchers (R.L. and Y.G) independently screened the titles and abstracts of the identified studies, subsequently retrieving the full texts of potentially eligible articles. These articles were then evaluated against the predefined eligibility criteria.

Eligibility criteria

Research studies that met the following criteria were included: (1) the primary focus of the articles was on HIV-1 subtypes or molecular epidemiological studies; (2) the study population comprised men who have sex with men (MSM) who were HIV-1 positive, or the route of HIV infection was clearly classified as male-to-male transmission, studies focusing exclusively on novel, rare, or region-specific HIV-1 variants were excluded because such studies often target isolated or atypical transmission events and may not reflect the broader molecular epidemiology among MSM. Including them could introduce selection bias and inflate the apparent prevalence of uncommon subtypes; (3) the subtyping method was clearly defined and specific, allowing for the use of any genomic segment for subtyping (e.g., pol, env, gag, or other genomic segments) or the full-length genome; (4) reliable information regarding the prevalence of different HIV-1 subtypes, including location and study period, was provided; and (5) each study included a minimum of 10 samples.

The following types of articles were excluded: (1) conference abstracts, review papers, meta-analyses, case reports, or studies that focused solely on unique or novel HIV-1 genetic variants; (2) publications containing data from the same study conducted in the same region with overlapping sample collection periods, where the earlier study with a smaller sample size was excluded.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (R.L. and Y.G.) independently extracted the following information from potentially eligible articles: first author, country, year(s) of sample collection, sample size (i.e., the number of successfully identified HIV-1 subtypes), HIV-1 subtyping method(s), and the proportion or frequency of different HIV-1 subtypes. After the extraction was completed, all information was carefully compared. In cases where discrepancies in the extracted data were identified, both authors reviewed the full text of the relevant studies to determine whether a consensus could be reached through discussion. If they were unable to reach an agreement, a senior researcher (Y.C.) conducted a secondary data extraction.

Data analysis

Countries were categorized into regions according to the UNAIDS classification, with minor adjustments made based on data availability. China was analyzed separately from its region due to the unique characteristics of subtype distribution and the availability of data. Studies were further divided into four time periods (~ 2005, 2006–2011, 2012–2017, and 2018–2024) to examine the spread of datasets and samples across the years.

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.3 and its associated packages. A random-effects meta-analysis model was employed to assess the pooled proportions of different HIV-1 subtypes and recombinants. Heterogeneity was evaluated using the Cochran Q-test and I² statistics [15]. The potential risk of publication bias was assessed by visualizing funnel plots and further examined using the Egger test and a non-parametric trim-and-fill method [16]. To gain a comprehensive understanding of the distribution of HIV-1 subtypes and recombinants among MSM, subgroup analyses were performed based on study region and time period. Sensitivity analyses were also conducted to evaluate the robustness of the combined estimates.

Results

Study selection and general characteristic

A total of 84,622 successfully genotyped HIV-1 samples were included for pooled analysis. The systematic literature review process is illustrated in a flowchart (Fig. 1). A total of 70,895 records were identified through the literature search. After removing duplicate articles and conducting an initial screening, 365 papers were retrieved for a more detailed assessment. Ultimately, 95 studies were included in the final analysis. The study period ranged from 1988 to 2022, encompassing a total of 31 different countries (e.g., the United States, United Kingdom, France, Singapore, Senegal, Poland, and China) across the 95 independent studies. In these studies, a total of 84,622 samples were successfully genotyped, with sample sizes ranging from 12 to 20,957. Detailed information about these studies is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Table 2 provides a summary of the number of genotyped samples and predominant HIV-1 subtypes by geographic region. China is presented separately due to its substantial sample size and distinct molecular subtype profile.

Global distribution of HIV-1 subtypes, crfs, urfs in MSM

Meta-analysis was employed to estimate the overall proportions of different HIV-1 subtypes and recombinants among men who have sex with men (MSM). As shown in Table 3, the predominant strains among the included studies were CRF01_AE, subtype B, and CRF07_BC, accounting for 34.46% (95%CI: 25.76–44.34%), 31.16% (95%CI: 21.36–43.00%), 24.72% (95% CI: 19.83–30.35%), respectively. Additionally, CRF02_AG accounted for 4.88%(95% CI: 1.51−14.62%) of infections, while CRF55_01B accounted for 4.06% (95% CI: 2.57−6.36%). Subtype A contributed 1.63% (95% CI: 0.69−3.83%) of infections, subtype C accounted for 1.83% (95% CI: 0.73−4.51%), and CRF08_BC represented 0.59% (95% CI: 0.38−0.91%). Other subtypes, including D, F, and G, accounted for 2.00% (95% CI: 0.94−4.21%) of infections among MSM globally. Uncharacterized recombinants (URFs) constituted 4.26% (95% CI: 3.18−5.69%).

The global distribution of HIV-1 subtypes and recombinants in MSM reveals distinct temporal trends. Prior to 2005, subtype B and C accounted for 75.18% (95% CI: 51.13−89.76%) and 5.08% (95% CI: 1.53−15.55%) of infections, respectively; both showed a declining trend over the years, decreasing to 3.72% (95% CI: 2.22−6.18%) and 0.38% (95% CI: 0.16−0.91%) after 2018. Subtype A increased its proportion until 2017, followed by a decrease post-2018. Other subtypes (D, F, G) exhibited variable fluctuations over time. Among the recombinants, CRF08_BC, CRF02_AG, and URFs increased their proportions until 2017, followed by a decline thereafter. Conversely, other CRFs and CRF55_01B demonstrated an opposite trend. Furthermore, CRF07_BC exhibited a consistent year-on-year increase, while CRF01_AE experienced a slight reduction after 2018. Notably, both subtypes currently account for more than 35% of the total, positioning them as the primary recombinant strains at present. Overall, the proportion of recombinants among MSM has shown a consistent upward trend (Fig. 3; Table 3).

Regions distribution of HIV-1 subtypes, crfs, urfs in MSM

Based on the UNAIDS classification, with minor adjustments to the distribution of the collected samples, we categorized the 31 countries into five distinct regions: Africa and the Middle East, Asia and the Pacific (including China), Latin America and the Caribbean, Europe and Central Asia, and China. The distribution of HIV-1 subtype diversity across these regions is illustrated in Fig. 4 and detailed in Table 3.

In the Africa and Middle East region, subtype C and CRF02_AG were the predominant HIV strains, accounting for 58.02% (95% CI: 34.79−78.18%) and 42.06% (95% CI: 21.52−65.77%), respectively. Furthermore, CRF02_AG is the most significant recombinant strain and plays a crucial role in the elevated proportion of HIV-1 recombinants among MSM in this region.

In Latin America and the Caribbean, the prevalence of subtype B is notably high, comprising 82.37% (95% CI: 72.82−89.07%), as well as in Europe and Central Asia, where it accounts for 90.28% (95% CI: 84.17−94.19%). This contributes significantly to the global prevalence of subtype B among MSM. However, the proportion of recombinants in these two regions remains relatively low, at less than 10%.

In the Asia and Pacific region, as well as in China, CRF01_AE emerged as the key circulating strain, accounting for 47.03% (95% CI: 40.10−54.08%) and 47.00% (95% CI: 40.11−54.00%), respectively. Additionally, CRF07_BC was the second most prevalent strain in both Asia and the Pacific, and China, contributing to 24.72% (95% CI: 19.83−30.35%) and 25.98% (95% CI: 21.30−31.29%) of HIV infections, respectively. Notably, the proportion of HIV-1 recombinants among MSM exceeded 50% in the Africa and Middle East, Asia and Pacific, and China regions, further contributing to the elevated global proportion of recombinants.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis for various HIV-1 subtypes and recombinants demonstrated that the estimated rates changed by less than 3% after the omission of any individual study, indicating that the stability of the pooled results was acceptable (Fig. S2-S14). Funnel plots, along with the Egger’s test and the trim-and-fill method, indicated publication bias for subtype B, CRF01_AE, CRF07_BC, other CRFs, total CRFs, URFs, and total recombinants (Fig. S15-S21).

Discussion

The primary objective of this meta-analysis was to elucidate the geographic distribution and temporal trends of HIV-1 genotypes among men who have sex with men (MSM) globally. This study enhances our understanding of the distribution and trends of HIV-1 genotypes within this population, ultimately providing essential insights for developing recommendations aimed at preventing and controlling the further spread and evolution of HIV.

A meta-analysis of 95 studies showed that the major prevalent HIV-1 strains via MSM were CRF01_AE, followed by subtype B and CRF07_BC. Notably, the proportion of subtype B exhibited a consistent annual decline among global MSM throughout the study period. Despite this decline, subtype B remained the most prevalent strain globally until 2011. However, following this period, the increasing prevalence of CRF01_AE and CRF07_BC strains surpassed that of subtype B in this population after 2012. This finding diverges slightly from the results of a systematic review covering the years 1990 to 2015 [13]. This discrepancy may indicate that recombinant forms continue to emerge as different genotypes of HIV-1 co-circulate, thereby diminishing the representation of pure subtypes in the population [17].

Moreover, the incidence of the CRF02_AG strain significantly increased between 2012 and 2017, positioning it as the second most prevalent strain. This suggests that this recombinant form may be widely disseminated in specific regions. Overall, the global incidence of recombinants among MSM demonstrated a significant upward trend, escalating from 23.41% (95% CI: 6.78–56.22%) to 97.20% (95% CI: 94.72–98.53%) (See Table 3 and Table S3). Given that HIV viral diversity is primarily driven by recombination events [18], this finding underscores the critical role of circulating recombinant forms (CRFs) in the HIV epidemic, indicating that the recombination of HIV viruses is intensifying among MSM. This evidence collectively suggests that the diversity of HIV-1 is becoming increasingly complex within this population, posing significant challenges to HIV prevention and control efforts. It highlights the necessity for continuous monitoring of HIV-1 CRFs to inform public health strategies effectively and adapt interventions to address the evolving landscape of HIV transmission among MSM.

Furthermore, global HIV-1 diversity among MSM exhibits a distinct geographic distribution. Specific dominant strains circulate in different regions, indicating that targeted interventions are essential. To effectively reduce the incidence and spread of these dominant strains among MSM, it is crucial to conduct vaccine evaluation trials and implement targeted prevention and control measures that address the local dominant strains [19].

The present study found that the distribution of HIV-1 strains in Latin America and the Caribbean is relatively straightforward compared to several other regions. Consistent with prior research, subtype B was identified as the predominantly endemic strain in this region [6]. Additionally, although subtype F comprised a smaller percentage, it contributed to the overall prevalence of HIV-1 in the area. Souza’s research [20] indicated that recombination events between subtypes F and B were frequent and diverse, leading to the emergence of viable and transmissible viral strains that further enhance the genetic diversity of HIV-1. Brazil and Argentina serve as representative countries for the prevalence of subtypes B and F, as well as their corresponding recombinants [20]. Therefore, it is logical to focus on developing and testing specific HIV vaccines targeting subtypes B and F in these countries, where the number of HIV-infected individuals is highest. Furthermore, subtype B is also the predominant strain in Europe and Central Asia [21], a distribution that can be attributed to the founder effect of subtype B infections originating from the United States, followed by population movement between countries and transmission among native individuals [22]. To effectively prevent and control the occurrence and evolution of subtype B and its variants, it is essential to conduct research on specific vaccines targeting subtype B, with the United States, Brazil, and Argentina as primary focus countries.

Consistent with existing findings [6, 10], subtype C and CRF02_AG are predominantly endemic in Africa and the Middle East. The highest prevalence of subtype C is observed in southern Africa [23], where the epidemics are almost exclusively attributed to this subtype. In light of this, conducting an evaluative trial of a vaccine based on sequences from subtype C isolates would be most appropriate in southern Africa. CRF02_AG epidemics have been driven by the spread of several founding strains in Central Africa [24]. When introduced into the West African region, CRF02_AG demonstrated a broader geographic spread compared to other strains. Therefore, it is advisable to conduct vaccine evaluation trials targeting the CRF02_AG strain in both Central and West Africa.

CRF01_AE and CRF07_BC recombinants were the two most prevalent strains of HIV-1 in Asia and the Pacific, accounting for more than half of all HIV infections among MSM in the region. Our study identified that much of the research on HIV-1 genetic diversity among MSM in this area originated from China, which aligns with the findings of a bibliometric analysis indicating that China was a leader in research contributions to HIV-1 genetic diversity [25].

CRF01_AE originated in Thailand and was subsequently introduced to China through commercial sex workers [26]. This strain has since spread widely in China via sexual transmission and has become the dominant strain in the country. A multicenter cohort study demonstrated that CRF01_AE is an independent risk factor for faster progression from HIV to AIDS and advanced immunodeficiency, possibly due to a high proportion of X4-tropic variants among infected individuals [27]. This characteristic of CRF01_AE increases the risk of opportunistic infections and mortality among those infected, posing significant challenges to AIDS prevention and control efforts.

CRF07_BC was first reported among people who inject drugs in southwestern China and has since rapidly spread through sexual transmission across the country [26]. A related study found that CRF07_BC exhibited extensive clustering among MSM but was not observed in heterosexual populations, suggesting that MSM play a pivotal role in the transmission of this strain [28]. Sustaining attention and implementing targeted measures to interrupt this transmission chain is crucial for controlling the continued spread of the disease. CRF01_AE and CRF07_BC are predominantly endemic in the Asia-Pacific region, particularly in China. This stable epidemiological trend will facilitate the development of targeted vaccines and the conduct of regional efficacy trials for these vaccines [29].

HIV subtypes are typically determined by amplifying and sequencing specific genomic segments. While whole genome sequencing is the ideal approach [30], resource limitations often necessitate the sequencing of smaller fragments, most commonly from the pol region. Consequently, the prevalence of subtypes and recombinants reported in our study may have been underestimated, as few studies utilized more than one gene fragment for typing. For instance, the vif region exhibited a higher frequency of the pure F subtype due to its adaptive advantages. Therefore, when feasible, studies should prioritize the analysis of multiple gene fragments (e.g. pol, env, gag, vif, nef, etc.) or, ideally, entire genomes.

Despite the comprehensive database search and the rigorous enforcement of pre-defined exclusion criteria in this meta-analysis, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while this study aimed for global coverage, the overall representation was moderate; thus, results should be interpreted with caution. This limitation may be attributed to the decision to include only English-language studies. Future research should consider lifting this language restriction to enhance the breadth of coverage. Second, significant heterogeneity was observed in the subgroup analyses, which may be due to the high diversity of HIV-1 subtype distribution among MSM across different regions and time periods. Additionally, this study included only published literature, and the imposition of language restrictions may have introduced publication bias. Therefore, future studies that utilize larger sample sizes and national datasets, along with more detailed analyses, are necessary to gain a better understanding of the diversity of HIV-1 genotypes among MSM.

Conclusion

In summary, our meta-analysis and systematic review reveal significant global diversity in HIV-1 genomes among MSM and dynamic trends over time. The findings suggest an increasing incidence of recombinants in this population worldwide, characterized by complex diversity and distinct geographical distributions. Therefore, ongoing and effective surveillance of the global and regional molecular epidemiology of HIV-1 in MSM is critical for informing vaccine design, testing, and implementation, as well as for developing targeted preventive control measures against HIV.
