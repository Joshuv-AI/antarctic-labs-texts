Phase II study of dual epigenetic targeting with chidamide and azacitidine in patients with high-risk acute myeloid leukemia after allo-HSCT
Authors: Ya-Xue Wu, Chao-Ling Wan, Kai-Wen Tan, Yang Zhang, Yan-Ming Zhang, Hao Zhang, De-Pei Wu, Su-Ning Chen, Ying Wang, Xiao Ma, Hai-Ping Dai, Chong-Sheng Qian, Shan-Shan Jiang, Hai-Yan Bao, Xiao-Hui Hu, Zheng Li, Sheng-Li Xue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAllogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) is potentially the only curative option for high-risk acute myeloid leukemia (AML) patients. However, disease relapse remains the principal cause of treatment failure of these patients, and outcomes of salvage treatments are poor. This research seeks to evaluate the efficacy and safety of a dual epigenetic targeting maintenance therapy with chidamide and azacitidine (AZA) in patients with high-risk AML post-allo-HSCT.MethodsThis multicenter, open-label, phase 2 prospective clinical trial (ChiCTR2300067593) recruited and followed up 48 patients diagnosed with high-risk AML post-allo-HSCT from 3 hospitals in China from November 2021 to March 2024. Chidamide (5 mg) was administered orally once daily for 5 days, combined with AZA (75 mg/m2) subcutaneously daily for 5 days, respectively. Treatment started as early as 3 months after transplantation. All patients were in complete remission before each maintenance cycle. A total of 6 cycles was recommended.ResultsThe 2-year cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR) was 8.4% (95% CI 4.4–12.4%), 2-year relapse-free survival (RFS) was 91.6% (95% CI 87.6–95.6%), and overall survival (OS) was 97.9% (95% CI 95.8–100%). At the end of the follow-up period, 4 patients relapsed, of which 1 patient died of leukemia recurrence; the other three patients underwent second allo-HSCT and are still alive in remission. The most common adverse events (AEs) were hematological toxicity. No grade > 3 AEs were noted. Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) occurred in 29.2% (14/48) of patients. Six cases (6/48, 12.5%) were complicated with infection. No treatment-related mortality occurred.ConclusionsDual epigenetic targeting maintenance treatment with CHI-AZA has an acceptable toxicity profile and might potentially be effective to prevent relapse in high-risk AML patients after allo-HSCT.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, ChiCTR2300067593. Introduction

High-risk acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is generally associated with poor outcomes. Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) is considered a potentially curative treatment option for such patients. However, the cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR) after allo-HSCT for AML has been reported to reach 40–50%, with long-term survival rates remaining as low as 10–15% [1, 2]. Relapse continues to be the primary cause of treatment failure among patients undergoing allo-HSCT for AML [3, 4]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for novel strategies to reduce relapse rates and improve survival outcomes.

Azacitidine (AZA), a DNA hypomethylating agent (HMA), has demonstrated efficacy in treating AML and myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS). Among patients who relapsed after allo-HSCT, 15–20% achieved complete remission (CR) when treated with AZA [5]. Its potential to increase expression of leukemia antigens to induce the graft-versus leukemia (GVL) effect [6] and to reduce graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) through induction of immunomodulatory regulatory T cells [7] highlighted a potential opportunity to optimize AZA therapy post-allo-HSCT.

Chidamide is a selective histone deacetylase inhibitor (HDACi) and can exert antitumor effects in various cancers. Our previously published study has demonstrated that chidamide exhibits synergistic activity with AZA in leukemia cells, inhibiting cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis in vitro. Chidamide markedly inhibited AML cell proliferation at low concentrations. The calculation of the confidence interval (CI) value suggested that chidamide combined with AZA also had a clear synergistic effect at their suitable concentrations [8]. Recent data indicate that HDACi may offer potential therapeutic benefits following allo-HSCT in several experimental models of GVHD and GVL [9].

Several clinical trials have investigated the clinical benefit of HDACi in combination with DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) inhibitors for AML patients [5, 10, 11]. Based on these observations, we hypothesized that chidamide plus AZA (CHI-AZA) dual epigenetic targeting maintenance therapy may both provide direct antileukemic effects to eradicate minimal residual disease and decrease the incidence of GVHD. Therefore, we initiated a single-arm prospective phase II study to report the outcomes of dual epigenetic targeting maintenance therapy with CHI-AZA to prevent relapse in high-risk AML patients after allo-HSCT.

Materials and methods study design and treatment

This study was conducted at Soochow Hopes Hematology Hospital, the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, and Canglang Hospital of Suzhou. The study was registered under the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (www.chictr.org) number ChiCTR2300067593. The study was approved by the institutional review board committee at each participating center and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient population

The study population consisted of high-risk AML patients aged ≤ 60 years who had received allo-HSCT. AML was diagnosed according to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria in 2022 [12], and its high-risk characteristics were defined according to one or more of the following criteria: (1) patients with poor prognosis according to the genetic risk stratification assessment of European Leukemia Net (ELN) in 2022; (2) primary refractory or relapsed AML; (3) secondary AML (secondary to MDS or treatment-related AML). Exclusion criteria for maintenance treatment post-HSCT included the following: (1) with other malignant tumors; (2) with acute or chronic GVHD; (3) morphological relapse, central nervous leukemia, extramedullary infiltration; (4) previous treatment with other pharmacological interventions after transplantation; (5) those who researchers assessed as unfit. Written informed consents were obtained from all patients, and consents could be withdrawn at any time.

Procedure and objectives

The therapeutic regimen consisted of chidamide at a dose of 5 mg/d and azacitidine (AZA) at a dose of 75 mg/m2, administered for 5 consecutive days, followed by an 8 to 12 week rest interval. This sequence was defined as one cycle. A total of 6 cycles was recommended to constitute a complete treatment course. For patients undergoing allo-HSCT, modified busulfan/cyclophosphamide (Bu/Cy) was given as the myeloablative conditioning regimen [13]. The patients’ enrollment flow chart and diagram of the prophylaxis regimen are summarized in Fig. 1. The primary endpoint was cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR). The secondary endpoints included rates of overall survival (OS) and relapse-free survival (RFS).

Assessment of treatment response

Bone marrow assessment of disease status was performed once a month in the first 3 months after HSCT. Minimal residual disease (MRD) was detected with multiparameter flow cytometry (FCM) and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). MRD-positive threshold was 0.1% by FCM [14] or 0.001% by RT-qPCR. Patients were assessed as MRD positive if they had 2 consecutive positive samples using FCM or PCR, or both FCM and PCR were positive in a single sample. Cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR) was defined as the rate of relapse after allo-HSCT in patients with high-risk AML. Complete remission (CR), partial remission (PR), and no remission (NR) will be assessed according to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network® Acute Myeloid Leukemia Guidelines Version 3.2021. Overall survival (OS) was measured from the time of transplantation to death from any cause. Relapse-free survival (RFS) was calculated from the time of transplantation to the date of relapse or death from any cause. The follow-up cutoff date was January 10, 2025.

Assessment of safety

Regimen-related hematological toxicity was monitored once a week in the outpatient clinic, including routine blood, liver, and kidney function. Investigator-assessed AEs were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE-Version 5.0). Non-relapse mortality (NRM) was measured from the time of transplantation to death from any cause except for disease relapse or disease progression.

Statistical analysis

Patients’ baseline and disease characteristics are summarized using descriptive statistics, mean and range for continuous variables, and frequency and percentage for categorical variables. Survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier curves. Statistics were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Sample size was estimated based on 2-year CIR according to one-sample tests for exponential hazard rate details that are described in the Supplement 6.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between November 2021 and June 2024, a total of 48 AML patients were enrolled in the study, including 22 (45.8%) males and 26 (54.2%) females, with a median age of 39 (17–60) years. According to the ELN2022 risk stratification standards, 15 (31.3%) patients were evaluated as having favorable cytogenetic risk, 2 (4.2%) were in intermediate risk group, and 31 (64.6%) were in adverse risk group. Seven patients (14.6%) had AML that transformed from MDS. Additionally, 24 patients (50%) were either primary refractory or had relapsed prior to transplantation. Before transplantation, 27 patients were in first CR (CR1), 12 patients were in second CR (CR2), and 9 patients were in NR status. Twenty-four (50.0%) patients were in MRD-negative remission, 16 (33.3%) patients were in MRD-positive remission, and 8 (16.7%) were NR.

Epigenetic dysregulation plays a central role in AML pathogenesis, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and chromatin remodeling [15]. Among the 48 patients, 15 (31.3%) patients had mutations in DNA methylation regulators, including DNMT3A (n = 6), TET2 (n = 3), IDH1/2 (n = 8). Thirteen patients had alterations in histone-modifying genes, including ASXL1 (n = 7), MLL-PTD (n = 4), EZH2 (n = 2), and KMD6A/B (n = 2). And 17 patients had alterations in chromatin remodeling, including AML1-ETO (n = 5), KMT2A-rearrangements (n = 9), BCOR (n = 3).

All 48 patients received modified busulfan/cyclophosphamide (Bu/Cy) myeloablative conditioning [13]. As for donor type, 6 (12.5%) were from HLA-identical sibling donors, 26 (54.2%) were from haplo-identical donors, and 16 (33.3%) were from unrelated donors. For GVHD prophylaxis, recipients of haplo-identical or unrelated donor transplants received anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG) in combination with cyclosporine A (CSA), mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) and short-course methotrexate (MTX). Patients undergoing HLA-identical sibling donor transplants received CSA, and short-course MTX without ATG [16, 17].The median time from HCST to the initiation of maintenance therapy was 4.5 (range 2.8–9.1) months, and the median number of completed maintenance cycles was 6 (range, 1–6) cycles. Demographic and disease characteristics for the overall population are shown in Table 1. Among the patients, 40 patients (83.3%) received at least 3 cycles, and 26 patients (54.2%) completed 6 planned cycles of maintenance therapy. The numbers of courses given are shown in Table S1.

Survival outcomes

As of the cutoff date for the final survival analysis on January 10, 2025, the median follow-up time was 26.9 (range, 14.2–43.4) months. The clinical outcome of CHI-AZA maintenance therapy after transplantation is shown in Fig. 2. The 2-year OS rate was 97.9% (95% CI 95.8–100.0%). The 2-year RFS rate was 91.6% (95% CI 87.6–95.6). The 2-year CIR was 8.4% (95% CI 4.4–12.4%) (Fig. 3).

By the end of follow-up, a total of 4 patients experienced relapse. One patient died of leukemia recurrence, while the other 3 patients underwent a second transplantation and remain alive in remission. Pre-transplant MRD positivity had no significant impact on OS (P = 0.607) or RFS (P = 0.608) (FIGURE S3 A and B).

For the 27 patients carrying at least one epigenetic alteration, their transplant outcomes showed no significant differences when compared with those who lacked epigenetic alterations ( 2-year OS 96.3% versus 100%, P = 0.378; 2-year RFS: 88.9% versus 95.2%, P = 0.461) (FIGURE S3 C and D).

Safety

A summary of adverse events (AEs) observed in the study is shown in Table 2. No grade > 3 AEs were noted. There was no treatment-related mortality. The most common AE was hematological toxicity, with an incidence of 89.6% (43/48). However, most patients experienced only mild bone marrow suppression, which required either outpatient administration of hematopoietic growth factors or simple observation without medication. Only 11 (22.9%) patients received platelet transfusions, and 1 (2.1%) patient received red blood cell transfusions. Among the 6 infected patients, 4 had pulmonary infections, including 1 patient with EB viremia reactivation. Additionally, 1 patient had an intestinal infection following unclean eating, and 1 had cytomegaloviremia. All AEs were tolerable with active treatment and did not affect subsequent therapy.

Discussion

Relapse after allo-HSCT for high-risk AML is associated with a poor prognosis despite salvage chemotherapy, donor lymphocyte infusions (DLI), and/or second transplants [18, 19]. In prior studies, patients with high-risk AML who underwent allo-HSCT without prophylactic treatment exhibited CIR as high as 39–47.9% within 1–3 years [20–22]. Therefore, novel maintenance therapies are needed to provide long-term disease control and extend remission in the post-allo-HSCT setting.

The hypomethylating agents (HMAs) AZA and decitabine (DEC) have been the most extensively studied non-targeted agents used for post-transplant maintenance for patients with AML or MDS [20, 22, 23]. In previously published studies on post-transplant maintenance with HMAs in high-risk AML and MDS, the reported 2-year RFS and OS rates ranged from 56 to 71% and from 71 to 74%, respectively [23, 24]. In a randomized trial of post-transplant maintenance with AZA in high-risk AML or MDS, AZA maintenance did not improve outcomes compared with placebo [20]. On the other hand, a phase II trial evaluating post-transplant maintenance with granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) plus minimal-dose DEC versus placebo in AML demonstrated a reduction in relapse incidence with G-CSF/DEC. The estimated 2-year CIR was 15.0% in the G-CSF/DEC group compared with 38.3% in the control group (P < 0.01) [22]. Taken together, these findings implied that the efficacy of hypomethylating agents alone may be suboptimal.

Epigenetic dysregulation plays a crucial role in AML pathogenesis, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and chromatin remodeling [15]. Epigenetic alterations play a crucial role in the development of most AML. Massive parallel sequencing has revealed that over 70% of AML cases have mutations in genes involved in DNA methylation or histone modification [25]. Our previous study demonstrated that chidamide markedly inhibited AML cell proliferation at low concentrations, while the combination of chidamide and AZA suppressed proliferation in a dose‑dependent manner, exerting a stronger inhibitory effect than either agent alone [8], highlighting the potential advantage of synergistic treatment strategies in this setting.

In our report, 2-year CIR was 8.4% (95% CI 4.4–12.4%), 2-year RFS was 91.6% (95% CI 87.6–95.6%), and OS was 97.9% (95% CI 95.8–100.0%). The 2-year CIR exhibited a maximum 95% confidence interval (CI) of 12.4%, which was also significantly better than the results reported previously. These outcomes were superior to those achieved with DLI or AZA alone in other centers [20, 23, 24, 26–28]. Post-transplant maintenance with HMA plus venetoclax (VEN) is actively being investigated. As a single agent, VEN 400 mg achieved a 1-year OS rate of 70% in patients with high-risk AML [29]. When administered together with HMAs, the combination of low-dose DEC (15 mg/m2 on days 1–3) and VEN (200 mg on days 1–21) resulted in a 2-year OS of 85.2% and a CIR of 15.3%, respectively [30]. In another report, low-dose DEC/VEN was associated with a low rate of post-transplant relapse in older patients with high-risk AML or MDS. The 1-year non-relapse mortality and OS were 11% and 84%, respectively [31]. Compared with these published studies, our results are at least comparable, but further clinical trials, especially in older patients, are needed for validation.

Another non-targeted approach that has shown promise has been to boost post-transplant immune surveillance with prophylactic/pre-emptive DLI. However, the risk of death from DLI-induced GVHD (6–11%) remains a significant barrier to its broader application [32, 33]. In our study, 29.2% of patients experienced acute or chronic GVHD, but all patients improved after treatment, and no GVHD-related deaths occurred.

In a retrospective analysis report, the 2-year OS was 44.4% in 44 adult patients with relapsed and refractory AML (R-R AML) who underwent salvage allo-HSCT while in NR or with concomitant extramedullary leukemia [34]. While in our report, the 8 patients who underwent salvage allo-HSCT while in NR were all alive at 2 years after allo-HSCT. Whether MRD turns negative or not before transplantation did not affect OS and RFS. While previous reports indicated that the rates of OS and RFS in MRD-negative patients were better than those in MRD-positive patients after allo-HSCT for AML [35, 36]. This interesting result may be due to our novel maintenance treatment strategy. However, validation of this putative association will require larger patient cohorts and longer-term follow-up in further research.

For patients with FLT3-ITD AML, targeted maintenance with the FLT3 inhibitor sorafenib has demonstrated both safety and efficacy. In the SORMAIN randomized phase II trial, patients receiving post-transplant maintenance with sorafenib experienced significantly improved outcomes, with a 2-year RFS rate of 85%, compared to 53% for those receiving placebo. Similarly, the 2-year OS rate was 91%, versus 66% for the placebo group. Notably, the FLT3-ITD allele ratio did not affect the treatment's efficacy [37]. Thus far, results with other FLT3 inhibitors, such midostaurin [38] and gilteritinib [39, 40], in the post-transplant maintenance setting have been less compelling. In a randomized trial of post-HSCT maintenance with the gilteritinib monotherapy for adults with FLT3-ITD AML who underwent HSCT, the 2-year RFS rate was 77.2% for participants receiving gilteritinib and 69.9% for those receiving placebo. But the difference did not meet the predetermined threshold for significance [39]. In our study, there were 7 patients with FLT3 mutation-positive status. Among them, 3 patients were FLT3-TKD positive, and 4 had FLT3-ITD mutations. The median follow-up period was 27.1 (range, 20.4–38.1) months, and all patients were in remission and alive. Due to the limited patient number, the effectiveness of CHI-AZA as maintenance therapy for AML patients with FLT3 mutations post-transplant still requires further validation.

Although AML with RUNX1::RUNX1T1 generally has a favorable prognosis, 5-year OS was 50% and CIR was 53% [41]. The 5-year probability of relapse was 29% after HLA-matched sibling HSCT [42]. Epigenetic approaches have been explored in RUNX1::RUNX1T1-positive AML models, and HDAC inhibitors have been shown to degrade the RUNX1::RUNX1T1 fusion protein [43]. Several HDAC inhibitors currently in use have emerged as attractive therapeutic agents [44–46]. In a prospective study [47], prophylactic treatment with AZA or chidamide alone was administered to RUNX1::RUNXT1-positive AML patients with MRD-negative status post-allo-HSCT. The 2-year CIR was 13.8% in the prophylactic group. In our study, two agents were administered in combination and were administered at differing dosages and on divergent schedules. In our study, there were 5 patients with RUNX1::RUNX1T1 fusion genes. All patients are still in complete remission, and the median survival time was 20.4 (range, 16.7–32.3) months. For RUNX1::RUNXT1-positive AML patients post-allo-HSC, CHI-AZA may become a novel option. Whether the combination regimen confers superior efficacy to azacitidine or chidamide monotherapy remains to be definitively established in future prospective studies.

Mutations in the TP53 gene are found in nearly 6–8% of patients with de novo AML [48–50]. TP53 mutations are associated with poor outcomes and remain long-standing therapeutic challenges [51, 52]. The 2-year OS was 29.7% and the relapse rate was 61.4% after allo-HSCT [53]. There are only 3 of 48 patients (3/48, 6.3%) with TP53 mutation, and 2 of them relapsed during maintenance treatment. One patient experienced molecular recurrence after 2 courses of maintenance treatment, subsequently abandoned treatment, and passed away. Another patient relapsed before the second course of treatment but achieved remission again after undergoing umbilical cord blood transplantation following active treatment and remains in remission. Although 66.7% of patients with TP53 mutations relapsed in our study, the small sample size limits our conclusions. Therefore, further research is needed to evaluate the effectiveness of this regimen in preventing recurrence of AML with TP53 mutations after transplantation.

The GVHD occurred in 29.2% (14/48) of patients, with only 1 patient had aGVHD (2.1%). GVHD in all patients was well controlled following immunomodulatory treatment. According to previous literature, the incidence of both aGVHD and cGVHD in the DLI-based regimen was higher than that in our cohort [54–56]. In the setting of venetoclax alone as post-transplant maintenance therapy, the 1-year cumulative incidence rate of any cGVHD was 29% [29]. In Gao et al. study, the rate of cGVHD was 34.0% in the G-CSF/DEC group and 48.0% in the no intervention group [22]. Compared with their study, our cohort had a similar median age and inclusion/exclusion criteria, and the incidence of GVHD was also comparable, supporting the safety and feasibility of our regimen.

Our maintenance therapy showed no influence on the incidence of cGVHD. In addition, the impact of CHI-AZA on immune cells has been explored previously. As a potential mechanism, AZA can upregulate tumor antigens and induce CD8+ T cell response, creating an anti-tumor response [57]. Murine models have suggested that AZA could expand CD4+ regulatory T cells (Tregs), which may reduce the risk of severe GVHD [7, 58]. This has also been recently shown in patients with AML receiving AZA maintenance or salvage therapy following allo-HSCT [59, 60]. Recent studies have shown that HDAC inhibitors not only inhibit the growth of tumor cells but also regulate the cytotoxic activity of T cells. These studies have demonstrated that HDAC inhibitors can negatively regulate the activation, proliferation, and differentiation of T cells associated with the development of GVHD [9, 61, 62]. These studies on the mechanisms of action of dual epigenetic targeting with the two drugs suggest they may have a positive effect in preventing the occurrence of post-transplant GVHD. However, all conclusions require further research for validation.

Chidamide inhibits HDAC1, 2, 3, and 10 in the low nanomolar concentration range and demonstrates broad-spectrum antitumor activity in vitro and in vivo. Therefore, compared with other HDAC inhibitors, chidamide has the lowest toxicity [63]. In contrast, no significant apoptosis was observed in normal CD34 + hematopoietic cells [64]. Post-HSCT maintenance with CHI-AZA had an acceptable safety profile and was well tolerated. In our research, a total of 89.6% (43/48) of patients experienced varying degrees of bone marrow suppression. But only 22.9% (11/48) received blood transfusions. Apart from one patient who relapsed and subsequently abandoned treatment, no other fatalities were recorded, yielding a non-relapse mortality (NRM) incidence of 0.0%. In another study, the 2-year NRM was reported to be 6% among patients with high-risk AML or MDS who received low-dose DEC plus VEN as maintenance therapy following allo-HSCT. This finding underscores the safety profile of our study, which aligns with the favorable outcomes observed in similar settings [30].

In conclusion, the results of our study suggested that dual epigenetic targeting maintenance treatment with CHI-AZA has an acceptable toxicity profile and might potentially be effective to prevent relapse after allo-HSCT. Given the single-arm design of our study and the absence of a comparator arm, our findings warrant further prospective, controlled studies with larger sample sizes to validate furtherly.
