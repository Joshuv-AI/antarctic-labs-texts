Optimal acquisition time of [68Ga]Ga-PentixaFor PET/CT for initial staging of multiple myeloma: an ancillary study to the PentiMyelo protocol
Authors: Chloé François, Thomas Carlier, Cyrille Touzeau, Anna Janz, Constance Dubégny, Bastien Jamet, Françoise Kraeber-Bodéré, Clément Bailly, Caroline Bodet-Milin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background[68Ga]Ga-PentixaFor, a CXCR4-targeted PET tracer, has shown potential in multiple myeloma. However, [68Ga]Ga-PentixaFor-PET/CT acquisition protocols lack standardization. This is a prospective ancillary study to the PentiMyelo trial aimed to determine the optimal acquisition time for [68Ga]Ga-PentixaFor-PET.ResultsTwenty multiple myeloma patients underwent [68Ga]Ga-PentixaFor-PET/CT at 60 min post-injection, with additional acquisitions at 30, 40, 50 min (Group 1) or 75, 85, 95 min (Group 2). Semi-quantitative metrics including SUV, tumor-to-background ratios and image noise — assessed by the coefficient of variation, were systematically analyzed. Physiological liver, spleen and mediastinal blood-pool uptakes decreased over time while image noise increased. A total of 343 lesions were detected (316 in Group 1, 27 in Group 2). Neither earlier nor later acquisition time affected the positivity of [68Ga]Ga-PentixaFor-PET, which was positive in 10 of 20 patients (5 in Group 1 and 5 in Group 2). In Group 1, tumor-to-background ratio was significantly higher at 60 min; in Group 2, tumor-to-background ratio remained stable.ConclusionsAn acquisition time of 60 min post-injection provided optimal lesion contrast, supporting its adoption as the standard imaging time point for [68Ga]Ga-PentixaFor-PET/CT.Trial registrationName of the registry: ClinicalTrials.gov. Background

While [18F]FDG-PET/CT (FDG-PET) is recommended for staging and therapeutic evaluation of multiple myeloma (MM), it has limitations, including false positives due to inflammation and false negatives due to low hexokinase 2 expression [1, 2]. The C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4), highly expressed in MM, plays a key role in disease progression and drug resistance [3–5]. Pilot studies reported higher sensitivity with [68Ga]Ga-PentixaFor (INN: Gallium (68Ga) boclatixafortide), a CXCR4-targeted PET tracer, than with FDG in MM patients [6–9]. CXCR4 expression has been suggested to be associated with poor prognosis [10, 11].

The ongoing prospective PentiMyelo study (NCT04561492) evaluates [68Ga]Ga-PentixaFor for initial staging and therapeutic assessment compared to FDG-PET in symptomatic MM patients. As with any new radiopharmaceutical, it is essential to determine the optimal acquisition time.

This ancillary study aimed to determine the optimal acquisition time for [68Ga]Ga-PentixaFor-PET/CT (PentixaFor-PET) by analyzing different acquisition delays, both earlier and later than 60-minute post injection adopted from FDG-PET practice.

Methods

Population

All patients were recruited at a single center (Nantes University Hospital) and the planned sample size of 20 patients for this sub-study was fully achieved. Given the prolonged acquisition protocol without direct clinical benefit, inclusion was limited to 20 patients based on feasibility and ethical committee considerations.

Twenty of the 60 patients enrolled in the PentiMyelo trial were prospectively included in this ancillary imaging sub-study. Eligible patients were aged 18 years or older, had symptomatic MM (IMWG criteria) and measurable disease based on serum monoclonal component or free light chain assay (serum or urine).

Investigational products and labeling

The PentixaFor peptide precursor was provided by Pentixapharm AG and [68Ga]Ga-PentixaFor was produced using a 1.85-GBq (at calibration time) pharmaceutical-grade 68Ga-generator (Eckert-Ziegler) with radiochemical purity greater than 97% and endotoxin results below 12 EU/mL per injected dose [12]. Each patient received a fixed target dose of 150 MBq of [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-PentixaFor (range: 100–200 MBq, depending on the calibration date of the generator), containing less than 50 µg of peptide. Administered activity was not weight-adjusted.

Study design

Patients underwent both FDG-PET and PentixaFor-PET before therapy. Patients were randomized to group 1 (G1) with early acquisitions at 30, 40, 50 and 60 min after [68Ga]Ga-PentixaFor injection, or to group 2 (G2) with late acquisitions at 60, 75, 85 and 95 min.

The gold standard for lesion detection was defined as a composite reference based on functional and conventional imaging performed for initial staging or therapeutic evaluation. A lesion detected on PentixaFor-PET was considered to be related to multiple myeloma if it was also identified on baseline FDG-PET or conventional imaging (CT and/or MRI) and/or showed a concordant response on follow-up imaging. Any discordant findings were resolved through a consensus among nuclear medicine physicians, taking into account all available imaging and clinical data.

PET/CT imaging

All PentixaFor-PET scans were performed on the same Siemens Biograph Vision 600, ensuring consistent imaging parameters across all patients. One CT was acquired per patient with the following acquisition parameters: 120 kV, adaptive mAs, pitch of 1.0. The CT reconstructed slices were 0.98 × 0.98 × 1 mm3. The imaging protocol took 50 min with no additional radiation. In G1, acquisitions began 30 min post-injection with a whole-body CT, followed by three sequential vertex-to-mid-thigh PET acquisitions at 30, 40, and 50 min post-injection and a whole-body PET at 60 min. In G2, acquisitions started with a whole-body PET/CT at 60 min post-injection, followed by three vertex-to-mid-thigh PET scans at 75, 85 and 95 min post-injection. A continuous bed motion of 1.5 mm/s was used for both the multi-pass and whole-body acquisitions. As continuous bed motion required approximately 10 min for head-to-mid-thigh acquisitions and 15 min for whole-body acquisitions, the intervals between successive PET acquisitions were determined accordingly. PET images were reconstructed using 3D ordinary Poisson OSEM with time-of-flight and point spread function available (4 iterations, 5 subsets and a 3 mm-FWHM Gaussian post-smoothing filter, voxel size: 1.6 × 1.6 × 1.6 mm3). No motion correction was applied. PET images were interpreted independently and under blinded conditions by two nuclear medicine physicians experienced in hematology. Inter-reader agreement was prospectively assessed in the first 13 consecutively analyzed patients. Concordance was evaluated for lesion count category (0; 1–3; 4–10; >10 lesions), diffuse bone marrow (BM) positivity and paramedullary lesion (PML) positivity. Agreement was excellent, with Cohen’s kappa values of 1.00 for lesion count category and BM positivity and 0.80 for PML positivity. MM lesions were classified as focal bone lesion (FL), PML, and extramedullary lesion (EML). Lesion positivity on PentixaFor-PET was defined using standardized visual criteria adapted from FDG-PET interpretation guidelines in multiple myeloma [13]. Tumor-to-background ratios (TBR) were defined as the ratio of lesion SUVmax to MBP SUVmean. Image noise was assessed using the coefficient of variation (CoV), which is calculated by dividing the standard deviation (SD) of voxel values by the SUVmean within each VOI (CoV = SD/SUVmean). This metric was computed in the liver and MBP VOIs.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.1). Continuous variables were summarized as median and range or mean and SD; categorical variables as counts and percentages.

For within-group comparisons (multiple acquisitions per patient), repeated-measures ANOVA or Friedman’s test with post-hoc Tukey or Wilcoxon Signed-Rank (Holm-Bonferroni correction) was used. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons between acquisition times were performed only when the global test was statistically significant. Comparisons between G1 and G2 at 60 min used t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.

A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Quantitative analysis of physiological uptake

Liver, spleen and MBP uptakes showed a significant decrease over time (Fig. 1). In G1, SUVmean at 30, 40, and 50 min were significantly higher than at 60 min for liver (p = 0.031), MBP (p < 0.0001 for 30 and 40 min, p = 0.0053 for 50 min), and spleen (p < 0.0001 for 30 and 40 min, p = 0.0118 for 50 min). In G2, SUVmean at 85 and 95 min were significantly lower than at 60 min for liver (p < 0.0001), MBP (p = 0.0053, p < 0.0001), and spleen (p < 0.0001). Additional significant differences were found for liver, spleen, and MBP, consistent with a gradual decrease in physiological uptake over time.

Statistical analyses revealed no significant differences between G1 and G2 for liver SUVmean (p = 0.97), MBP (p = 0.13) and spleen SUVmean (p = 0.07) at 60 min post-injection.

BM uptake remained stable over the acquisition times, with no significant variation (p > 0.05) (Fig. 1). No significant difference in SUVmean was found between G1 and G2 (p = 0.12), despite the apparent higher BM uptake in G2 compared to G1.

Boxplots suggested inter-patient variability in bone marrow uptake and milder variability in splenic uptake (Fig. 1).

Image noise

Image noise, assessed using the CoV within liver and MBP VOIs, increased with acquisition time in both groups. In G1, CoV was significantly higher at 60 min compared to earlier acquisition times (p = 0.0001 for liver at 30 and 40 min; p = 0.0029 for liver at 50 min and for MBP at 30 and 40 min; p = 0.0331 for MPB at 50 min). In G2, a similar trend was observed, with significantly higher CoV at 85 and 95 min compared to 60 min, especially in the liver (p = 0.00017 for liver at 85 min; p < 0.0001 for liver at 95 min; p = 0.0157 for MBP at 95 min) (Fig. 2).

At the 60-minute acquisition time point, no significant inter-group difference was observed in image noise as assessed by liver CoV (p = 0.22) or MBP CoV (p = 0.19).

Detectability

Neither earlier nor later acquisition time affected the positivity of PentixaFor-PET, which was positive in 10 of 20 patients (5 in G1 and 5 in G2).

A total of 343 lesions were detected: 313 FL (including 250 in patient 9) and 3 PML in G1, and 25 FL and 2 PML in G2. In terms of detectability, the majority of lesions were consistently identified at each time point (early and late acquisitions). Specifically, only 4 of 343 lesions (1.2%) were not visualized at all time points, meaning that approximately 98.8% of lesions were detectable at each time point. No lesion was exclusively detected at a late time point after being absent on earlier acquisitions.

Of the 343 PentixaFor-positive lesions, all were classified as true positives according to the gold standard. Lesions were considered positive if they were either: positive on FDG-PET or demonstrated a response on follow-up PET imaging.

Conversely, 19 FL and 2 PML that were FDG-positive did not show PentixaFor uptake and were therefore classified as false negatives according to the gold standard.

Quantitative analysis of lesions

Lesion SUVmax did not differ significantly between early and 60 min acquisitions in G1 or late and 60 min acquisitions in G2. In G1, the TBR of lesions increased significantly between early acquisition times (30, 40, and 50 min) and 60 min (p < 0.0001 for all comparisons), whereas in G2, the TBR showed no significant differences between the different acquisition times (Figs. 3, 4 and 5). At the 60 min acquisition time, no significant difference was observed between G1 and G2 for median lesion TBR per patient (p = 0.55).

Given that one patient contributed a large proportion of lesions (250/343), an analysis was performed by limiting the dataset to a maximum of 10 lesions per patient, selected based on the highest SUVmax at 60 min in accordance with IMPeTUs criteria [14]. The results remained unchanged with no significant modification of SUVmax comparisons and persistent TBR increase in G1 but not in G2.

All additional and subgroup analyses were conducted using a maximum of 10 lesions per patient to minimize potential clustering effects.

Additional analyses using SUVmean-based TBR (ratio of lesion SUVmean to MBP SUVmean) showed similar temporal patterns. In G1, SUVmean-based TBR increased significantly between 30 and 60 min (p < 0.02 for all comparisons). In G2, SUVmean-based TBR did not show significant variation over delayed acquisition times (p = 0.22).

Subgroup analyses according to lesion type (FL, PML, EML) did not modify these findings. The number of PML was limited (n = 3 in G1 and n = 2 in G2) and no EML was observed.

An exploratory subgroup analysis according to disease status (newly diagnosed vs. relapsed) was performed. In G1, SUVmax increased in newly diagnosed patients (11 lesions) between the earliest acquisition time (30 min) and 40, 50 and 60 min (p = 0.03 for all comparisons) whereas TBR did not vary significantly. Conversely, in relapsed patients (22 lesions), TBR increased between early acquisition times (30, 40, and 50 min) and 60 min (p < 0.001, 0.002 and 0.008) while SUVmax did not show significant variation. In G2, neither newly diagnosed (16 lesions) nor relapsed patients (11 lesions) demonstrated significant variation in SUVmax or TBR over delayed acquisition times.

Regarding diffuse BM uptake, an additional analysis restricted to patients with visually positive BM uptake showed no significant variation in SUVmax, SUVmean or TBR between the different acquisition times in G1 and G2 (all p > 0.05).

Discussion

Semi-quantitative analyses identified 60 min post-injection as the optimal acquisition time for PentixaFor-PET. Lesion contrast peaked at 60 min and remained stable up to 95 min. The increase in TBR at 60 min was likely due to reduced MBP uptake rather than increased lesion uptake, as tumor SUVmax remained stable. Image noise increased with acquisition time in both groups. A 60-minute delay thus offers the best compromise between contrast, noise, detectability and patient comfort, minimizing time spent in the department while aligning with FDG-PET practice and taking into account the short half-life of 68Ga.

Our findings refine the preliminary data of Hermann et al. who investigated [68Ga]Ga-PentixaFor biodistribution in five patients, of which only two had MM-related lesions [15]. Our physiological uptake results are consistent with their time-activity curves showing progressive washout of the radiotracer up to 4 h. Although Hermann et al. suggested an optimal acquisition time between 30 and 60 min post-injection, the small number of MM-related lesions (2 patients) limited their conclusions. Our study provides additional insight by highlighting a higher TBR at 60 min in a larger and more representative MM cohort.

We observed inter-patient variability in BM uptake, likely reflecting BM involvement rather than physiological uptake. Zhou et al. recently correlated plasma cell infiltration with [68Ga]Ga-PentixaFor uptake in the lumbar spine [16]. Unlike FDG-PET, where the liver background based on Deauville scale defines the threshold for BM positivity [17], there is no standard for PentixaFor-PET. Ongoing analyses in the PentiMyelo trial will help to establish positivity thresholds and clarify the prognostic significance of BM uptake. Given recent evidence that decreased splenic uptake of [68Ga]Ga-PentixaFor is associated with poor prognosis in MM [18], variability in splenic uptake will also be further investigated for its prognostic impact.

Our study has several strengths, including the prospective design, standardized imaging protocol and repeated acquisitions at multiple time points. The number of lesions analyzed, especially in the early acquisition group, allowed robust lesion-level comparisons.

However, our study also has limitations. First, although all patients underwent a standardized 60 min acquisition used as a common reference, early (30–50 min) and delayed (75–95 min) acquisitions were performed in two separate groups. This design did not allow direct intra-patient comparisons between early and delayed imaging and limited control over inter-individual variability. It was not feasible to perform repeated acquisitions from 30 to 95 min post-injection with a total imaging protocol duration of 85 min in routine practice, especially in patients with painful disease. Second, the lesion-level analyses are subject to limitations. In Group 1, one patient accounted for most of the lesions (250 out of 343), which introduced a clustering effect. However, an additional analysis limiting the number of analyzed lesions to a maximum of 10 per patient yielded consistent results, indicating that the observed findings were not driven by the high lesion burden of a single individual. Additionally, the wide range of TBR values observed in this group was partly driven by outliers with markedly high uptake in a low-background context.

It should be noted that this ancillary study was not designed to evaluate the clinical impact of acquisition timing on patient management. Although image contrast and noise properties were optimized at 60 min post-injection, other acquisition times had no significant impact on lesion detectability or PET positivity, suggesting no immediate diagnostic or therapeutic consequences. The increase in image noise was not associated with reduced lesion detectability or impaired diagnostic confidence. As PentixaFor-PET is still being evaluated for use in multiple myeloma, including in the ongoing PentiMyelo study, its precise role in clinical decision-making remains to be determined.

Conclusion

A 60-minute acquisition after [68Ga]Ga-PentixaFor injection provides the best compromise between lesion contrast, image noise, detectability and practicality, supporting its use as the standard in multiple myeloma.
