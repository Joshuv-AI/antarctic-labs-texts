Citywide metagenomics reveals microbial community and resistome dynamics in urban wastewater
Authors: Zhiying Xu, Jiabao Xing, Xiaohong Zeng, Yuqing Wu, Yao Wang, Yue He, Xialu Lin, Haiyan Huang, Zeyu Zhao, Hua Wu, Zhinan Guo, Tianmu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urban wastewater systems connect hospitals, residential communities, transport hubs, and wastewater treatment plants, creating opportunities for the dissemination of microorganisms and antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs). Here, we conduct a three-month, citywide metagenomic survey of wastewater in Xiamen, China, comprising 252 samples from seven hospital sites (n = 16), 27 residential sites (n = 55), 16 wastewater treatment plant sites (n = 159), and individual international flights (n = 22). Genome-resolved analyses reveal source-specific microbial community structures, resistome profiles, lineage-sharing patterns, and associations between ARGs and mobile genetic elements across wastewater sources. Hospital wastewater harbors the most diverse resistome, while international flight wastewater introduces microbial taxa and ARGs absent from local wastewater networks. Wastewater treatment plants accumulate ARGs from multiple upstream sources, exhibiting frequent lineage sharing and signals of potential horizontal gene transfer. Compared with within-environment sharing, cross-environment lineage sharing is associated with lower nucleotide diversity, consistent with possible genetic bottlenecks. Among potential correlates, pH shows strong non-linear associations with microbial diversity and resistome composition. These findings indicate that urban wastewater systems function as interconnected networks for microbial and ARG dissemination and identify potential hotspots for targeted antimicrobial resistance surveillance.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is recognized as one of the top ten global public health threats by the World Health Organization1,2. Urban wastewater systems are increasingly viewed as important reservoirs and transmission interfaces3. Coastal cities, where terrestrial and marine ecosystems meet, are particularly vulnerable, making wastewater management essential for public and environmental health3–5. Wastewater surveillance frequently detects viable ESKAPE pathogens (Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter spp.)6,7, indicating that urban wastewater may serve as a reservoir and conduit for high-risk microorganisms and ARGs8. Whether wastewater systems attenuate or amplify ARGs remains debated9, due to differences in treatment processes, environmental factors, and monitoring methods. Under rapid urbanization, coastal human activities and sewage discharge accelerate the release of ARGs, mobile genetic elements (MGEs), and antibiotics into receiving waters, ultimately impacting marine ecosystems. Coastal cities are thus central to marine pollution, highlighting the need for genomic surveillance to track resistance dissemination10–12.

Traditional genomic epidemiology, relying on isolate cultivation and sequencing, cannot capture the full strain-level diversity or ARG-MGE dynamics within the complex wastewater microbiome. Genome-resolved metagenomics offers a culture-independent approach for detecting strain transmission, resolving single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), and identifying ARG-MGE co-occurrence, enabling strain-level and evolutionary analyses13–17. In urban wastewater, horizontal ARG spread depends not only on microbial diversity and abundance, but also on horizontal transfer potential, strain genetic backgrounds, and connectivity between wastewater sources and human exposure points. A high spatial resolution genomic-resolved approach is therefore essential to understand both ecological structures and evolutionary processes sustaining ARG persistence. Yet, despite mounting concern over ARG dissemination and strain transmission, the relative contributions of residential pipelines, hospital effluents, treatment plants, and other inflows to strain-level transmission in large cities remain poorly characterized3, limiting our ability to assess ecological and public health impacts.

Here, we show that urban wastewater systems facilitate interconnected microbial and ARG dissemination across heterogeneous urban environments. Through a high-density citywide metagenomic survey of 252 wastewater samples, we reveal that distinct urban infrastructures exhibit unique microbial and ARG transmission patterns (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Fig. 6). Specifically, hospital effluents harbor the most diverse resistome, whereas international flights introduce novel microbial taxa and ARGs absent from the local network. Wastewater treatment plants (WTP) act as major convergence points for ARG accumulation, strain sharing, and potential horizontal gene transfer. Cross-environment transmitted strains display signatures of purifying selection during dissemination, while geographical proximity increases microbial connectivity between healthcare and residential regions. Together, these findings provide a high-resolution view of microbial ecology and ARG dissemination in urban wastewater networks, offering evidence to guide environmental management and safeguard public health.

Results

Overview of field sampling in Xiamen wastewater

To address the critical gaps in understanding horizontal transmission mechanisms of ARGs and microbial strains within urban wastewater systems, we conducted a large-scale, cross-sectional, multi-site sampling survey in Xiamen, a coastal island city in southeastern China (118°04′04″E, 24°26′46″N; population 5.33 million) with 16 WTP serving residential communities, hospitals, and industrial sources. Between February and April 2023, over 300 samples were collected from 50 sites across all six administrative districts: Tong’an (TA), Jimei (JM), Haicang (HC), Xiang’an (XA), Huli (HL), and Siming (SM), comprising 27 residential communities (RES), 7 hospitals (HOS), and 16 WTP sampling sites. Importantly, all sampled locations were embedded within the same physical wastewater network. Hospital effluent, after passing through multistage disinfection, is discharged into shared underground pipelines where it merges with untreated residential sewage before co-treatment at centralized WTPs. Residential wastewater was sampled at primary pumping stations following community-level aggregation, whereas WTP influent samples represented the integrated input of mixed urban wastewater sources (Supplementary Fig. 2). To further evaluate the contribution of externally sourced wastewater, we additionally sampled wastewater from 22 arriving international flights (FLI) (see Methods for details). After quality control, DNA extraction, and library preparation, 72 samples were excluded, yielding a final dataset of 252 samples (22 FLI, 16 HOS, 55 RES, and 159 WTP), with a median sequencing depth of 1.24 × 108 reads per sample (Supplementary Data 1, 2). Sampling density ranged from 24 to 66 samples per district. Community structure and diversity across wastewater sources

Across February to April, microbial community composition within each wastewater compartment showed minimal temporal fluctuation (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. 3), although low-abundance genera in HOS shifted from Zoogloea (February) to Moraxella (March) and Orrella (April) (Supplementary Fig. 4). International flight wastewater consistently exhibited greater richness than locally sourced wastewater systems (RES, HOS, and WTP), with diversity metrics (Shannon) remaining consistent over time (Fig. 1E). Within domestic wastewater system, HOS and WTP diversity peaked in March before returning to baseline (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Fig. 5, and Supplementary Data 3). WTP displayed higher Shannon diversity than RES (p = 0.0016), while the WTP–HOS contrast was similar in direction but not statistically significant (Fig. 1E). These results were further recapitulated in β-diversity analyses. As expected, FLI microbiomes were compositionally distinct from the local wastewater microbiomes (p < 0.001, permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA), Fig. 1F). On the first-dimension mapping index (PCoA index1), FLI samples explained substantially more variance than the three domestic wastewater types. Differential abundance analysis showed that FLI wastewater was enriched in Enterococcus, Blautia, and Mediterraneibacter, and depleted in Cloacibacterium, Comamonas, and Acinetobacter (Fig. 1H and Supplementary Data 4). Family-level profiles were also concordant (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Data 5).

We specifically surveyed the presence of ESKAPE pathogens in wastewater. Only Enterobacter spp. and K. pneumoniae were detected, with significantly increased abundance in March followed by a return to February levels in April, primarily concentrated in HOS and RES wastewater, suggestive of persistent public health risks associated with hospital and residential wastewater discharge (Fig. 1G). By extending the scope into common opportunistic pathogenic bacteria in wastewater, our analysis revealed significantly elevated abundance of Prevotella, Bifidobacterium, Streptococcus salivarius, Clostridium, and Bacteroides fragilis (Supplementary Fig. 7). Functionally, metabolic response suggested source-specific functions18. WTPs were enriched in fatty acid metabolism, TCA cycle, and nitrogen pathways; HOS in nucleotide biosynthesis; and FLI showed a marked enrichment in fermentation processes, alongside a significant depletion in branched-chain amino acid biosynthesis19 (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Data 6).

Collectively, these findings indicate that international flight wastewater harbors a compositionally distinct and more diverse microbiome than urban internal wastewater systems, plausibly reflecting heterogeneous passenger microbiota from diverse geographic origins20,21. Regarding the urban internal wastewater system, WTP and RES microbiomes were comparatively stable, whereas HOS microbiomes exhibited pronounced instability, plausibly driven by fluctuating patient turnover and heterogeneous antibiotic exposure typical of healthcare settings22.

Antibiotic resistomes are heterogeneously shaped by wastewater source and context

International flight wastewater showed markedly lower ARG richness and significantly reduced Shannon diversity (median 3.9, p < 0.001) relative to locally sourced wastewater (Supplementary Fig. 9). Within domestic sources, HOS harbored higher richness, while WTP exhibited higher Shannon diversity. Over the February–April sampling window, ARG diversity in urban wastewater increased gradually, with the most pronounced fluctuations in HOS, while FLI remained comparatively stable. Divergence between Shannon and richness measures, e.g., the April increase in HOS Shannon index not mirrored by richness, may suggest shifts in evenness rather than taxon gain/loss. Bray–Curtis dissimilarity further suggested a clear compositional separation between FLI and domestic wastewater, with WTP being the most stable cluster and HOS/RES showing greater temporal dispersion (Fig. 2C). Instability quantified using the average variation degree (AVD) index (see Methods for details) mirrored this pattern: HOS and RES exhibited the highest variability, whereas FLI and WTP were more consistent (Fig. 2D). Co-occurrence analysis revealed WTP shared ARGs with RES 37 times, including 25 intra-WTP links, followed by 18 links between RES and HOS (Fig. 2F, G). FLI shared comparatively fewer ARGs with domestic systems, supporting the interpretation that WTP functions as the key internal convergence point for ARG circulation within the urban network. Notably, throughout the three-month sampling window, specific resistance determinants (including CfxA6, aadA8, and aadA1) exhibited high-frequency occurrence and broad spatial distribution across clinical, residential, and municipal wastewater compartments (Supplementary Fig. 10). These findings imply that ARG diversity in HOS and RES is more sensitive to fluctuations in contributing human populations, whereas WTP may act as a baseline resistome reference for the city. Functionally, resistance profiles reflected source-specific selection pressures. RES appeared to significantly enrich more tetracycline-related resistance genes, while HOS significantly enriched beta-lactam resistance genes (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. 11). Cross-system comparison with agricultural and marine resistomes revealed marked heterogeneity in both ARG types and functions (Supplementary Figs. 12–14), consistent with differing antibiotic usage patterns significantly influencing the emergence of resistance phenotypes13,23–25. ARG coexistence network revealed distinctive correlation patterns across wastewater sources (Fig. 2E). HOS exhibited the most complex network with numerous negative correlations between ARGs, notably between beta-lactams and sulfonamides, suggesting competitive selection pressures where metabolic constraints drive microbial specialization toward dominant antibiotic threats in clinical settings26. In contrast, WTP displayed predominantly positive correlations, indicating co-selection of resistance determinants. FLI networks featured stronger positive associations between aminoglycosides, quinolones, and MLS (macrolide-lincosamide-streptogramin) resistance genes. April network showing intensified connectivity, particularly among beta-lactams, tetracyclines, and sulfonamides, coinciding with elevated detection of opportunistic pathogens. Furthermore, the risk of ARGs was defined as a composite metric integrating their abundance, mobility potential, and association with human pathogens, reflecting the likelihood of ARG transmission and clinical impact (see ref for detailed explanation of risk definition27). SARG-based risk ranking revealed spatial heterogeneity in potential public health hazards. Elevated risk in FLI reflected the introduction of high-risk ARGs via international travel, while the high risk in HOS indicated the persistence of clinically associated ARGs despite disinfection, representative of both imported and domestic ARG hotspots with high risk (Fig. 2B). Compared with residential wastewater, FLI showed a substantial increase in the abundance of 435 ARGs, with tetracycline, erythromycin, and macrolides determinants being the most prominent, whereas only 29 genes showed a significant decrease in abundance. High-risk ranks (I–II) were largely associated with mobile ARGs such as erm, mph, CARB, and tet families, indicating broad-spectrum resistance and horizontal transfer potential (Supplementary Fig. 15A). In contrast, HOS exhibited an increase in the abundance of 368 ARGs and a decrease in the abundance of only 8 (Supplementary Fig. 15B). These patterns collectively demonstrate that FLI serves as a major external input of globally disseminated ARGs, while HOS functions as an internal hotspot maintaining clinically relevant resistance traits under continuous antibiotic pressure. Taken together, these findings show that wastewater resistomes comprise non-random, source-dependent assemblages shaped by distinct selection pressures, with direct implications for refining public health surveillance and assessing antibiotic resistance risk in urban wastewater networks.

Horizontally disseminating ARGs between urban wastewater

Procrustes analysis based on Bray-Curtis distances revealed good fit and significant correlation between overall ARG profiles and microbial community composition (M2 = 0.7807, p < 0.001, and Supplementary Fig. 16). We identified a total of 2454 MGE structures, with slightly higher numbers in FLI compared to the other three sample types. Following established protocols for identifying horizontally transferred regions in bacterial genomes, we identified all near-identical DNA fragments (≥99% BLAST identity) present in pairs of otherwise distantly related MAGs (<95% pairwise average nucleotide identity) from different sources (Methods for details). We identified 458 instances of horizontally transferred fragments, most commonly shared by members of the Arcobacteraceae, Burkholderiaceae, Pseudomonadaceae, and Sulfurospirillaceae families (Fig. 3B, left panel). These horizontally transferred regions were enriched in ARGs compared to the remainder of the microbial genomes, indicating their associated role in ARG dissemination. Following screening for horizontally transferred regions in bacterial genomes, 24.08% of MGE fragments could mediate the horizontal transfer of ARGs, suggesting that the role of these MGEs is not limited to ARG dissemination. Among horizontally transferable ARG types, Sulfonamide, Aminoglycoside, and MLS were the most common transferable resistance phenotypes, with WTP serving as the primary dissemination hub (Fig. 3B, right panel), similar to our observations in resistome compositional analysis (Fig. 2).

Interestingly, the sul1 resistance gene was found to be horizontally transferred in RES and WTP wastewater through multiple insertion sequences. We highlighted three classic MGEs: the insertion sequence ISArsp9 found in soil actinomycetes (Arthrobacter), the highly transposable IS6100, and ISTha3 identified in bacteria isolated from Thailand (Fig. 3C). The macrolide phosphotransferase gene mphE was frequently found to genomically coexist with IS26 in international flight samples. AAC(6’)-Ib7 was more frequently mediated by ISTha3 in hospital wastewater. In international flights, we detected the super-resistance gene tetX3 mediated by ISCph4. The large-scale introduction and widespread dissemination of this gene into local bacterial populations would challenge local clinical use of tigecycline, further highlighting the public health threat posed by resistance genes imported via international flights.

Environmental factors influence microbiome and resistome community variability

To evaluate the influence of abiotic conditions on wastewater ecology, we used WTP samples as a model system. Generalized linear models (GLMs) explained a substantial proportion of variation for both microbiome (Conditional R2 = 0.613, Marginal R2 = 0.573, df = 127) and resistome profiles (Conditional R2 = 0.668, Marginal R2 = 0.63, df = 127) (Fig. 4A, B and Supplementary Data 7, 8). Microbiome composition was dominated by a positive association with resistome diversity (effect estimate = 0.73, p < 0.001), whereas pH exerted a significant negative effect (effect estimate = -0.18, p < 0.01). Other hydrological parameters showed an undetectable effect. For the resistome, microbiome diversity was again the dominant positive predictor (effect estimate = 0.62, p < 0.001), alongside positive effects of color fold (effect estimate = 0.27, p < 0.001) and pH (effect estimate = 0.20, p < 0.01), and a negative effect of TSS (effect estimate = −0.30, p < 0.05). To resolve non-linear responses, generalized additive models (GAM) were applied to the significant GLM-identified predictors (Fig. 4C–H and Supplementary Data 9, 10). Microbiome diversity exhibited a peaked, non-linear response to pH, maximized between 7.0 and 7.5 and declining beyond 7.5 (p < 0.01 and Fig. 4C), indicating that wastewater microbial communities have an optimal pH range, beyond which specific microbial taxa may decline. For its relationship with resistome diversity steepened once the resistome Shannon index exceeded a threshold (~4.2) (Fig. 4D), suggesting that high ARG diversity may precipitate ecological restructuring of the microbiome. For the resistome, color fold displayed an approximately linear positive effect (p < 0.001 and Fig. 4E); microbiome diversity showed a saturating influence at high microbial diversity (Microbial Shannon > 2.5) (p < 0.001 and Fig. 4F), and pH showed a weak but consistent positive correlation (Fig. 4G and p < 0.01).

Collectively, these analyses indicate that microbiome–resistome coupling is the primary driver of compositional variation, modulated by a small set of environmental parameters whose effects can be non-linear and, in the case of pH, divergent between microbial and resistance communities.

Multiple pairwise permutation analyses identified distinct connectivity patterns (Fig. 5B). Relative to RES (left column), HOS effluent shared markedly more lineages than WTP (z-score = −4.75, p = 2.62e-6), a difference amplified when comparing samples from different districts (z-score = -5.9, p = 3.09e-9), suggesting that hospital effluent has a substantially stronger influence on residential wastewater microbial communities compared to WTPs. Interestingly, when examining samples from the same geographical district, no significant difference was observed (z-score = 1.17, p = 0.25), indicating that geographical proximity may diminish the differences in microbial transmission patterns. A similar pattern emerged when using HOS as the benchmark (middle column), with WTP showing fewer shared lineages than RES (z-score = −6.23, p = 4.61e-10 for district-independent analysis; z-score = −6.36, p = 2.9e-10 for different districts). In contrast, WTP exhibited stronger lineage connectivity with RES than with HOS (right column), a relationship that persisted within districts (z-score = 2.89, p = 0.0034) and weakened only slightly across districts (z-score = 2.05, p = 0.04).

Collectively, these findings reveal a complex network of microbial transmission among different urban wastewater environments, with hospital effluent showing particularly strong connections to residential wastewater. This suggests that hospital-associated microorganisms, potentially including those carrying antibiotic resistance genes, may readily connect with residential settings. The influence of geographical proximity on these transmission patterns indicates that local environmental factors play an important role in shaping microbial community dynamics across different wastewater sources.

Significant selection pressure purified strains of transmission that deemed in urban wastewater systems

To further understand the genetic characteristics of microorganisms in different wastewater environments, we analyzed nucleotide diversity (π) (Fig. 5C) and SNV distance (Fig. 5D), which reflect the degree of genetic variation and evolutionary relationships of microbial communities (see ref. 31 for methodological details). SNV distance did not differ significantly across the four wastewater types, suggesting that, despite compositional turnover, underlying mutation rates may be broadly comparable across environments. Likewise, no significant difference in SNV distance was observed between microorganisms involved in inter-environment transmission compared to those in intra-environment transmission.

Nucleotide diversity analysis revealed varying degrees of genetic diversity among microbial communities from different wastewater sources (Fig. 5C). Residential wastewater samples harbored the highest nucleotide diversity, whereas international flight wastewater samples showed significantly lower nucleotide diversity (p = 0.0039). Hospital effluent and wastewater treatment plant samples displayed intermediate levels of nucleotide diversity, with no significant differences compared to other compartments. These findings suggest that residential environments may promote higher levels of microbial genetic diversity, potentially reflecting more diverse selection pressures on microbes in such environments.

Motivated by recent evidence that evolutionary and ecological forces jointly shape bacterial responses to environmental heterogeneity over long timescales35,36, we next asked whether strains undergoing cross-environment transmission incur evolutionary trade-offs during environmental adaptation. When comparing microbial strains involved in cross-environment transmission (Inter) versus within-environment transmission (Intra), we observed that strains undergoing cross-environment transmission had significantly lower nucleotide diversity than those involved in within-environment transmission (p = 0.012), corroborating our hypothesis that microorganisms successfully transmitting between different environments may have undergone genetic bottleneck effects or purifying selection, leading to reduced genetic diversity (Fig. 5C).

To elucidate the finer dynamics underlying this cross-environment purifying selection, we performed a fine-scale, intra-district investigation centered on Aliarcobacter cryaerophilus, the most prevalent and highly transmissible species in our dataset. Focusing specifically on the interconnected infrastructure within the TA district, we mapped the resistome and microbiome trajectories across particular RES and WTP nodes (Supplementary Fig. 17A). Resistomic profiling revealed a highly integrated co-occurrence network, with a vast majority of ARGs extensively shared across RES and WTP sites, underscoring persistent genetic connectivity within the local urban water infrastructure (Supplementary Fig. 17B). Furthermore, spatiotemporal tracking of A. cryaerophilus captured a discrete clonal expansion event. Following initial detection at a residential source in February 2023, near-identical clones (popANI > 99.984%) rapidly disseminated to downstream specific sites across the district. Crucially, examining the intra-population genetic signatures during this transmission revealed a precipitous drop in nucleotide diversity at WTP1, marking the lowest diversity point within the local network (Supplementary Fig. 17C–E). This high-resolution observation suggests the occurrence of a severe genetic bottleneck: as the microbial population transitioned from residential sources into the treatment facility, intense environmental filters effectively restricted the standing genetic variation. Together, these fine-scale findings corroborate our broader macro-ecological observations, demonstrating that the urban water system does not merely transport pathogens, but may actively reshape their evolutionary template through discrete, high-pressure selective events at specific infrastructural nodes.

Discussion

In one of the most granular field investigations of urban wastewater to date, we have investigated the microbial community dynamics across all mainstream wastewater-generating systems in a highly developed coastal city37. A crucial prerequisite for accurately quantifying microbial communities’ dynamics is the representativeness of sampling points38,39. In other words, this depends on whether the wastewater sampling points in our cross-sectional surveillance accurately represent the dynamics of wastewater distributions throughout the city40,41. To address this, we systematically collected influent samples from all WTPs in Xiamen, securing at least two successfully sequenced libraries per point. This design yielded a temporally narrow yet city-scale “snapshot” of microbiome and resistome features and enabled construction of an ecological network spanning aircraft, hospital, residential, and treatment compartments42 (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Graphic summary of the interconnected network of microbiota and ARG in the urban wastewater system.Here represents a hypothetical underground municipal pipeline that illustrates wastewater convergence at the treatment plant. Our integrated, assembly-free analysis revealed a dynamic ecological network connecting wastewater sources through both microbial community composition and ARG profiles43. Distinct microbial signatures characterized each source, shaped by unique origins and selective pressures. Notably, international flight wastewater consistently harbored greater diversity and richness than domestic urban sources, underscoring how global mobility can introduce novel taxa into urban wastewater8,44. Within the urban infrastructure, WTP and residential wastewater maintained relatively stable community structures, whereas hospital wastewater exhibited pronounced instability, plausibly reflecting fluctuating patient loads, diverse clinical activities, and variable antibiotic usage patterns intrinsic to healthcare settings, consistent with wastewater-based antibiotic consumption45. Metabolic signatures were also stratified by source, suggesting environment-specific selection. Specifically, WTP communities tend to optimize resource recovery, whereas the FLI microbiome favors resource conservation and dormancy to endure intermittent nutrient availability. HOS microbiomes, in contrast, exhibited stress-induced adaptations that plausibly defend against antimicrobial exposure. Together, these metabolic distinctions underscore strong environment-specific fitness strategies rather than stochastic variation. However, these interpretations require validation, ideally through proteomics, since nucleotide-level evolutionary response may modulate the extent of functional redundancy within these microbial communities46.

Assembly-free observations converged with genome-resolved analyses. While HOS wastewater harbored greater ARG richness, WTPs exhibited higher ARG diversity (Shannon index), consistent with their role as convergence points for resistance determinants in the urban water cycle. Strain-level genomic transmission analysis, based on assembled contigs, confirmed this structure, revealing particularly strong microbial sharing between hospital effluent and residential wastewater. This congruence across methodological frameworks strengthens the inference that ARG dissemination in the city follows a hub-and-spoke architecture, with WTPs functioning as central nodes. Such a configuration underscores WTPs as both key monitoring targets and potential intervention points for curbing ARG flow, representing choke points of strategic public health importance47. Geographical proximity further modulated similarity patterns, suggesting that local infrastructure and neighborhood context shape microbial and ARG connectivity within cities3,8.

Importantly, source-specific ARG risk patterns aligned with these ecological and genomic structures. The SARG-based risk ranking revealed that hospital and flight wastewater constituted distinct high-risk reservoirs27. Elevated risk in FLI was primarily driven by the introduction of globally disseminated and mobile resistance genes, reflecting the movement of both travelers and their associated microbiomes. Such imported ARGs are particularly concerning because they can bypass local selective regimes and become integrated into local microbial communities once discharged into urban wastewater networks48. Their convergence with local microbiota increases opportunities for recombination and horizontal gene transfer, thereby enhancing the genetic connectivity between imported and local resistomes49. Consequently, FLI should be regarded not only as a high-risk reservoir in isolation but also as a dynamic interface where global ARGs may infiltrate and reshape local resistance landscapes, highlighting the need for targeted surveillance and containment strategies at major ports of entry, particularly during periods of high-volume international air traffic. HOS exhibited similarly elevated yet locally generated risk, characterized by the presence of clinically associated and multidrug-resistance genes despite disinfection. This pattern reflects sustained selective pressures within healthcare environments, where high antibiotic loads and dense pathogen populations promote the maintenance and diversification of high-risk ARGs22. By comparison, WTPs, while exhibiting the highest ARG diversity, showed markedly reduced composite risk. This reduction likely reflects multiple attenuation processes during wastewater convergence, where dilution and mixing lower the abundance of high-risk ARGs, and the influx of diverse, non-pathogenic microbes enhances ecological competition and filtering, decoupling many ARGs from their clinical hosts28. Collectively, these risk profiles reinforce the concept that ARG dissemination potential is not solely determined by diversity or richness, but by the specific composition and mobility of ARGs shaped by their anthropogenic context.

The evolutionary implications of these transmission patterns proved particularly significant, as strains undergoing cross-environment transmission exhibited substantially lower nucleotide diversity than those undergoing within-environment transmission. This genetic bottleneck effect points to strong purifying selection during environmental adaptation, aligning with emerging evidence that both ecological context and evolutionary filtering jointly mediate bacterial responses to habitat variation18,43. These selection pressures plausibly influence which horizontally transferable ARGs successfully navigate between environments, with our findings highlighting sulfonamide, aminoglycoside, and macrolide-lincosamide-streptogramin resistance genes as particularly successful in traversing different wastewater niches.

Environmental factor analysis further contextualized these dynamics within physicochemical constraints19,50. Non-linear relationships, such as a bell-shaped microbial diversity response to pH and a saturation effect in the microbiome–resistome association, suggest that a limited set of parameters can modulate community composition and ARG profiles in a threshold-dependent manner. The opposing effects of pH on microbiome and resistome diversity suggest that environmental filtering may differentially shape microbial taxa and MGEs, potentially creating windows of opportunity for horizontal gene transfer under certain conditions. Furthermore, our results indicate that TSS is a relevant structural factor. The observed inhibitory effect of TSS beyond the 400 mg/L threshold raises the possibility that excessive particulate matter may restrict niche breadth through mass-transfer limitations or the competitive dominance of a few generalist taxa. Such particulate-induced ecological filtering could potentially fragment the horizontal transmission pathways of ARGs by sequestering free DNA or limiting the physical contact required for conjugation.

By integrating both assembly-free and genome-resolved perspectives into a city-wide transmission network linking aircraft, hospital, residential, and wastewater treatment ecosystems, we provide a coherent, cross-validated view of the ecological, evolutionary, and environmental processes structuring urban wastewater microbiome and resistome. The consistent patterns observed across analytical methods not only reinforce the robustness of our inferences but also highlight a fascinating model of microbial ecology where human mobility, infrastructure design, and natural selection interact to shape community structure and gene flow across interconnected niches. Our fine-scale tracking of specific intra-district transmissions provides direct mechanistic validation of this dynamic, suggesting that localized infrastructural filters likely impose strong genetic bottlenecks. Within this model, WTPs emerge as critical nodes: they are endpoints of urban wastewater hydrology yet potential amplifiers of resistance risk if unmanaged. Although hospital effluents in our study underwent robust disinfection, we nonetheless observed extensive connectivity and persistence of clinically important determinants, indicating that complex networks can sustain the flow of ARGs across the urban landscape. These findings argue for enhanced monitoring and targeted process optimization at both hospitals and WTPs to mitigate the dissemination of resistance.

Finally, we acknowledge that this study has several noteworthy limitations. First, we utilized intensive sampling to establish a high-resolution spatial baseline of the urban microbiome and resistome. While our sampling timeframe (February–April) was strategically designed to capture a period of meteorological stability and avoid the confounding effects of heavy seasonal rainfall, we acknowledge that this cross-sectional approach cannot fully resolve seasonal dynamics or temporal directionality in community change. Seasonal variability is a well-documented driver of microbial community composition and resistome dynamics in wastewater systems51,52, yet this factor was not fully captured within our study period. Similarly, capturing the long-term ecological integration of novel ARGs introduced via international air travel remains inherently challenging within a restricted timeframe. Although our three-month window revealed a compelling pattern of high-frequency occurrence and broad spatial distribution for key externally introduced determinants, suggesting their capacity for rapid local stabilization, their extended evolutionary trajectories are yet to be fully elucidated. Longitudinal sampling across multiple seasons and healthcare-utilization cycles will be necessary to quantify variability, trend structure, and potential lags in microbiome–resistome coupling. Second, although hospital effluent exhibited distinctive ARG compositions, the absence of citywide, compartment-resolved data on antibiotic prescribing and clinical resistance limits causal attribution of usage patterns to wastewater resistomes. Integrating clinical prescribing, resistance phenotypes, and wastewater metagenomes in future work should clarify how human activities imprint environmental resistomes45. Third, while short-read sequencing provided valuable insights, it has inherent limitations in resolving complex genomic structures. Future studies using long-read technologies such as Oxford Nanopore or PacBio could offer improved assembly resolution for recovering complete microbial genomes, identifying strain-level information, and facilitating better characterization of ARGs and MGEs.

As urbanization continues globally, the rational utilization of wastewater metagenomic techniques for understanding these complex interactions becomes increasingly crucial for developing effective strategies to mitigate antibiotic resistance, as one of the most pressing public health challenges of our time. Our city-scale analysis shows that urban wastewater is not a set of isolated compartments but an interconnected microbiome–resistome network spanning international flights, hospitals, residential districts, and WTPs. By pinpointing hotspots of microbial and ARG dispersal, as well as enrichment of high-risk ARGs in flight-derived and hospital-derived wastewater, public-health surveillance for antibiotic resistance can be made more granular and targeted rather than uniformly deployed. Such network-informed prioritization provides a realistic pathway toward implementable public-health policies aimed at mitigating microbiome and resistome transmission.

Methods

Wastewater sample collection and concentration

Xiamen is a coastal island city in southern China bordering the South China Sea, with a permanent population of approximately 5.327 million residents and 16 WTPs that process raw sewage from residential communities and hospitals, along with treated industrial effluents. We selected Xiamen as an ideal study site to investigate the microbial and antibiotic composition of wastewater, with particular emphasis on assessing the sharing dynamics between common wastewater categories.

We conducted a three-month field campaign across 50 sampling sites spanning six administrative districts, including residential communities (RES, N = 27), hospitals (HOS, N = 7), and WTPs (WTP, N = 16) to provide a comprehensive snapshot of microbial and ARG dynamics. Generally, HOS wastewater was systematically sampled at designated effluent discharge nodes within healthcare facilities, undergoing multistage disinfection protocols compliant with China’s Medical Institution Water Pollutant Discharge Standards (GB 18466-2005). After disinfection, hospital effluent entered shared underground pipelines where it mixed with residential wastewater before co-treatment in centralized WTPs. In contrast, RES wastewater was collected at primary pumping stations following community sewage aggregation, entering municipal networks untreated. WTP samples were obtained from untreated water in inlet collection channels (influent). To evaluate microbial and antibiotic burden from external sources, we also collected wastewater from international flights arriving in Xiamen (FLI, N = 22) (Supplementary Fig. 1). FLI wastewater was collected through two protocols: (1) immediate sampling from international aircraft vacuum wastewater retainers post-landing, and (2) composite sampling of 2–3 flights consolidated in mobile decontamination units at airport aprons. All FLI samples implement sodium hypochlorite disinfection, with treated effluents discharged to municipal networks. Although we were unable to obtain the specific passenger count for each international flight due to commercial confidentiality, we collected data on the annual number of international flights processed by the airport and the total annual passenger volume (Supplementary Data 11). As one of our primary objectives was to determine microbial sharing patterns between different water systems, we also documented detailed underground wastewater flow patterns to examine whether sewage confluence dynamics corresponded with microbial exchange patterns (Supplementary Fig. 2).

For each sampling event, 24-hour composite sampling was conducted, and four 500 mL wastewater samples were collected using sterile composite samplers and immediately transported to the laboratory under 4 °C storage conditions. We recorded environmental parameters, including location, date, sampling type, ambient temperature, water temperature, and daily precipitation. Samples underwent centrifugation at 3000 g at 4 °C to remove sediments and debris, followed by concentration to 2 mL using a CPS enrichment concentration system (CP Select, Innovaprep) with 0.05μm concentration pipettes (CC08020, hollow fiber type, Innovaprep). Concentrated samples were stored at −80 °C pending further analysis. In total, we collected 324 samples, with a minimum of 2 samples per sampling point. Detailed sample information and metadata of post-quality-controlled libraries are provided in Supplementary Data 1, and the fully illustrated workflow is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

DNA extraction and Metagenomic sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from processed samples using the qEx-DNA extraction Kit (Tianlong, Suzhou, China) on a GeneRotex automated nucleic acid extractor (Tianlong, Suzhou, China). DNA concentrations were quantified using double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) on a Qubit fluorometer. Qualified samples were subjected to library construction using the MGIEasy FS Library Prep Set (BGI, Shenzhen, China), followed by DNB preparation with DNBSEQ OneStep DNB Make Reagent Kit (BGI, Shenzhen, China). Paired-end sequencing (PE150) was performed on a DNBSEQ-T7 sequencer (BGI, Shenzhen, China) using the DNBSEQ-G50RS High-throughput Sequencing Set (BGI, Shenzhen, China). A minimum of 5×107 paired-end reads were obtained per sample to ensure reliable downstream analysis.

De novo assembly and binning

Raw sequencing files underwent adapter trimming and quality filtering using fastp (v.0.23.4)53, yielding clean metagenomic reads for further analyses. Library quality control metrics are detailed in Supplementary Data 2. Human reference genome databases were generated using Bowtie2 (v2.4.5)54 with the Homo sapiens reference genome GRCh38.p13 (NCBI accession number GCA_000001405.28). The clean reads obtained after quality control were mapped against reference genome databases using KneadData (v0.10.0, https://github.com/biobakery/kneaddata) to remove host DNA contamination. This process yielded quality-filtered short read FASTQ files for subsequent analyses.

Quality-controlled reads were then assembled into contigs using MEGAHIT (v1.2.9)55. Contigs were subsequently binned into metagenomic assembled genomes based on GC content and read depth using MetaWRAP (v1.3.1) with the binning algorithms MetaBAT (v2.12.1)56, MaxBin2 (v2.2.6)57, and CONCOCT (v1.0.0)58. Draft MAGs were refined using the MetaWRAP59 bin_refinement module with CheckM (v1.0.12)60 to select high-quality bins (>50% completeness and <10% contamination). The resulting bins were dereplicated using dRep (v3.4.2)61 at 95% similarity threshold, yielding 68 high-quality bins. Bin abundance across samples was calculated using CoverM (0.7.0)62. The taxonomic classification of the selected MAGs was conducted with GTDB-Tk63 classify_wf workflow against the GTDB genome database (v2.3.2, database Release207)64. Phylogenetic trees for bacterial and archaeal MAGs were constructed from multiple sequence alignments generated by the GTDB-Tk workflow. The unrooted bacterial and archaeal phylogenetic trees were rooted using the midpoint rooting method, implemented through midpoint.root function in the phytools R package65, and visualized using the online tree display tool iTOL (v6.6)66.

Metagenomic profiling of the microbiome and encoded pathways

Quality-filtered short reads were profiled using MetaPhlAn4 (v4.1.1)67 with default parameters to quantify relative abundances of bacterial species. Species with relative abundance thresholds above 0.1% were selected for analysis. Richness and Shannon diversity indices were calculated using the specnumber and diversity (index = ‘shannon’) functions, respectively, from the vegan R package68. Bray-Curtis distances were calculated using the vegdist function (method = ‘bray’) from the same package, and results were visualized using ggplot269.

The encoded microbial pathways were assessed using HUMAnN3 (version 3)70. All 252 samples were processed using the biobakery/humann docker container with the Chocophlan nucleotide database and Uniref90 protein database. HUMAnN3 utilizes MetaPhlAn (v4.1.1)67 as an intermediate step to assign organism-specific functional profiling, employing the developer-provided Metaphlan4 bowtie2 database. The HUMAnN3 pipeline generated MetaCyc pathway abundance profiles for each sample. The “humann_renorm_table” script was used to convert Reads Per Kilobase (RPK) values in the MetaCyc abundance table to Copies Per Million (CPM), enabling cross-sample comparisons.

Identification of antibiotic resistance genes, mobile genetic elements, and virulence factors

To compare the ARG burden across different environments, we performed a systematic analysis of wastewater samples from Xiamen City in comparison with adjacent marine, farm effluents, and municipal wastewater from other Chinese cities. For this purpose, we retrieved relevant raw sequencing data meeting these criteria (post-2020) from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive. Details about the datasets were summarized in Supplementary Data 12. These external datasets were combined with our locally generated wastewater sequencing data and subjected to uniform bioinformatics processing. All samples underwent comprehensive ARG profiling and quantification using the ARGs-OAP (v3.2.4)71 with the SARG (v2.2) reference database, enabling standardized comparison of ARG abundance patterns across different aquatic environments. To evaluate the potential health risks associated with the identified ARGs, we applied a risk classification approach in which each ARG was matched with the risk level of that specific gene as proposed in established frameworks for assessing AMR27. This allowed us to assign risk categories to each gene based on its abundance, mobility, and potential for horizontal gene transfer. We further used RES samples as a baseline to benchmark differences in risk levels between external inputs and locally high-risk areas.

ARGs were identified in contigs using ResFinder (v4.6.0)72 against the CARD database (v3.3.0)73, and fragments shorter than 500 bp were filtered to obtain ARG-bearing contigs. MGEs and VFGs were quantified using blastp74 against the ISfinder75 and VFDB76 databases, respectively, with MGEs located more than 5 kb from ARGs being excluded. This analysis yielded contigs carrying mobile ARGs with associated VFG profiles, enabling assessment of horizontal transfer risks. All mobile ARG patterns are documented in the supplementary material (Supplementary Data 13). We visualized the genomic organization of high-risk ARGs that were either prevalent across different sampling types or warranted further investigation using the gggenes package in R77.

Microbial lineage sharing events

We employed inStrain (v1.9.0)31 to identify microbial strain-sharing events across different water sources. Initial sequence deduplication was performed using dRep (v3.4.2)61 with a 95% similarity threshold, yielding 68 high-quality MAGs. These MAGs served as reference genomes for read mapping, which was performed by creating indices and aligning reads from all samples using Bowtie2 (v2.5.4)54.

The “inStrain profile” module was used to analyze MAG presence across all samples, followed by the “compare” module to quantify strain-level relationships through popANI calculations between samples in inStrain. Strains were considered identical when meeting the benchmark of both popANI ≥ 99.5% and percent genome compared ≥ 25% in “inStrain compare”31. We identified strain-level sharing events between: i) samples from the same water source and ii) samples from different water sources. For cross-source sharing events, we calculated nucleotide diversity and other metrics to characterize microbial dispersal patterns. Detailed statistical analyses are described in the statistical analysis section.

Additionally, we calculated SNV profiles and nucleotide diversity (π), among other metrics, and compared these indices between shared and non-shared microbial populations to capture underlying genomic variation.

Environmental correlates and statistical analysis

Environmental factors

Xiamen, with its 5.327 million residents and 16 WTPs, provided an ideal setting for studying microbial communities and antibiotic resistance dissemination. We collected comprehensive ecological metadata, including average daily temperature, average water temperature, average humidity, Chemical Oxygen Demand, Color Fold, Flow Rate, Hydraulic Retention Time, Longitude, Latitude, Maximum Population, Mean Population, pH, Rainfall, Total Ammonia-Nitrogen, Total Chlorine, Total Nitrogen, Total Phosphorus, and Total Suspended Solids to evaluate potential ecological drivers of microbiological communities and antibiotic resistance dissemination (Supplementary Data 14 and Supplementary Notes 1). Detailed definitions of all environmental factors are available in the Supplementary Materials.

Assessment of community diversity

Taxonomic richness and Shannon diversity indices of microbial taxonomy and ARG subtypes were calculated using the specnumber and diversity (index = ‘shannon’) functions, respectively, within the vegan package68. Bray–Curtis distances were calculated using the vegdist function (method = ‘bray’) within vegan68. The resultant distance matrix was subjected to principal coordinates analysis (PCoA), with the coordinate system centered at the centroid of all samples such that the coordinate origin (0,0) represents the average community composition. Community composition (taxonomic and ARG) differences were determined using PERMANOVA in R with the adonis2 function of vegan68. Differences in Shannon diversity, richness (taxonomic and ARG), pairwise Bray–Curtis distances, and amino acid identities were calculated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with the compare_means function within the R package ggpubr (v0.6.0)78. Differentially abundant taxa, microbial pathways, ARGs, and antibiotic classes were determined using the zero-inflated negative binomial (ZINB) model in MaAsLin2, setting sample groups as the fixed effect, and subject ID, sampling time, and collection sites as random effects. In all cases, P values were corrected for multiple hypotheses using the Benjamini–Hochberg method via the p.adjust command (method = ‘BH’) in the stats R package (v4.1.3)79. To reveal the correlation between the ARG profile and microbial community composition, Procrustes analysis was done with the “vegan” package79, and the significance of the Procrustes statistic was estimated with the protest function using 999 permutations.

Quantification of microbial sharing events between samples

To quantify the enrichment of strain-level microbial co-occurrence events between HOS, RES, and WTP groups, we first calculated the number of possible pairwise sample combinations between these three groups. We then distributed the total number of observed lineage-sharing events between each group pair using the choices method within the Python random module, setting the calculated total possible sample pairs as weights. We repeated this process 10,000 times, resulting in a theoretical distribution of lineage co-occurrences, allowing us to calculate a z-score for the observed number of lineage co-occurrences using the statistics module in Python. The calculated z-scores were used to obtain p-values using the pnorm function (lower. tail = FALSE) within the stats package79. As with other analyses, the Benjamini-Hochberg method was used to correct for multiple hypothesis testing.

Ecological correlates analysis

To determine environmental factors influencing microbial populations and ARG subtype diversity, we fitted generalized linear models (GLMs) with Poisson distribution encompassing all possible combinations of available variables (determined by AIC). For the GLM with microbial Shannon diversity as the response variable, potential explanatory variables included all collected environmental parameters. For the GLM with resistome Shannon diversity as the response variable, the variable list was identical except Resistome Shannon was replaced with microbial Shannon to test the relationship between these metrics. Model results were visualized using the jtools package in R81. All Poisson GLMs were evaluated for overdispersion, and all models were assessed for normality of residuals (Supplementary Figs. 18, 19).
