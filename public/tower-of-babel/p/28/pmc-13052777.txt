Long-term and short-term outcomes of curative-intent resection for elderly and younger patients with gallbladder carcinoma: a multicentre propensity score-matched cohort study
Authors: Yu Pan, Chun-Yue Ye, Peng Zhao, Hai-Su Dai, Yan Jiang, Yu-Cheng Huang, Cheng Chen, Yu-Le Luo, Jun-Jie Ding, Rui Ding, Ping Yue, Fan Huang, Chao Yu, Zhao-Ping Wu, Wei Chen, Jin-Xue Zhou, Wei Guo, Da-Long Yin, Wei Chen, Yi Zhu, Jie Bai, Yan-Qi Zhang, Dong Zhang, Ya-Yun Wang, Yan-Ling Yang, Zhi-Peng Liu, Rui Guo, Zhi-Yu Chen, The Biliary Surgery Branch of the Chinese Society of Digestive Surgery
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile curative-intent resection for gallbladder cancer (GBC) is being increasingly performed in elderly patients, perioperative morbidity and long-term oncological outcomes in this population remain unclear.MethodsConsecutive patients with GBC who underwent curative-intent resection (2016–2020) were identified from a multicentre database and stratified as elderly (>70 years) or younger (≤70 years). Propensity score matching (PSM) and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) were used to minimise selection bias. The outcomes compared included overall survival (OS), cancer-specific survival (CSS) and short-term outcomes. Logistic regression was used to identify risk factors for major morbidity and Cox regression was used for CSS, with the Fine-Gray competing risk model further applied to account for non-cancer-related death in the analysis of CSS.ResultsAmong the 575 patients enrolled, 432 were younger and 143 were elderly. After 1:1 PSM and IPTW, elderly patients had significantly higher 90-day major morbidity rates than younger patients (p=0.029 and p=0.010, respectively), but also demonstrated better CSS in both cohorts (p=0.038 and p<0.001, respectively). OS was significantly longer in elderly patients only after PSM (p<0.001), with no significant difference after IPTW (p=0.684). Multi-adjusted analysis confirmed that advanced age was an independent risk factor for major morbidities (original cohort: OR 1.71 (95% CI 1.12 to 2.59); PSM: OR 1.88 (95% CI 1.08 to 3.30); IPTW: OR 1.83 (95% CI 1.17 to 2.82)) but was associated with longer CSS (original cohort: HR 0.65 (95% CI 0.44 to 0.97); PSM: HR 0.29 (95% CI 0.18 to 0.45); IPTW: HR 0.62 (95% CI 0.42 to 0.90); Fine-Gray model: HR 0.34 (95% CI 0.30 to 0.39)).ConclusionDespite a higher risk of major postoperative morbidity, advanced age itself might not be considered a contraindication for curative-intent resection. Selected elderly patients with GBC may achieve superior CSS compared to their younger counterparts.

Introduction

Gallbladder cancer (GBC) is the sixth most common gastrointestinal malignancy and comprises >80% of biliary tract cancers.13 Curative-intent resection of GBC typically necessitates removal of the gallbladder, partial liver, regional lymph nodes and possible reconstruction of the bile duct and digestive tract.4 5 Extensive resection may be accompanied by significant trauma and a relatively higher risk of perioperative morbidities. Despite these challenges, surgery remains recommended by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines because of its profound prognostic benefits. Notably, the mean age of patients with GBC who undergo resection is approximately 55 years.6 Against a backdrop of an ageing global population and extended life expectancy, the number of elderly GBC candidates for curative-intent resection has risen substantially.6 However, it remains unclear whether curative-intent resection of GBC can yield similar outcomes in elderly patients comparable to those in younger patients.

Compared with younger patients, elderly patients present with poorer physical status, higher comorbidity burdens and a persistent decline in multi-organ functional reserve.7 8 This undoubtedly increases the complexity of perioperative management, but there is currently a lack of high-quality evidence quantifying the magnitude of this risk. Furthermore, although a few studies have investigated the long-term oncological benefit of surgery in elderly patients with GBC,912 these studies have limitations that restrict the generalisability of their conclusions. In a previously reported study, surgical strategies for GBC did not strictly follow the latest NCCN guidelines, and some patients underwent only laparoscopic cholecystectomy, limiting the relevance of these studies for current or future surgical decision-making. Moreover, single-centre studies have difficulty fully reflecting the actual situation in the overall region or country.1315 In addition, when long-term oncological outcomes are discussed, especially in the elderly population, competing risk events, such as non-cancer-related death or death due to other organ failure, should be thoroughly considered. Compared with overall survival (OS), which has been used in previous studies, cancer-specific survival (CSS) can more accurately reflect the oncological prognosis in patients.

The objective of this study was to explore the short-term outcomes, CSS and OS of elderly (>70 years) and younger patients (≤70 years) after curative-intent resection for GBC using a multicentre database prospectively maintained by 13 tertiary centres, with propensity score matching (PSM) and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) used to adjust for confounding biases and enhance comparability.

Methods

Patient selection

The data were obtained from a multicentre database that was prospectively established, retrospectively collected and dynamically maintained by the Biliary Surgery Branch of the Elite Group of Chinese Digestive Surgery, covering 13 centres in the database, as shown in online supplemental table 1.

All consecutive patients were newly diagnosed with GBC and underwent curative-intent resection between January 2016 and December 2020. Postoperative pathology confirmed the diagnosis. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) recurrent GBC; (2) missing data for more than 10% of any important variables; (3) palliative resection and (4) loss to follow-up with unavailable survival outcome data at the last contact date. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all the institutions involved obtained ethics approval. Each patient signed a preoperative informed consent form.

Surgical procedure

Patients with obstructive jaundice necessitated preoperative percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography drainage (PTCD) until total bilirubin (TB) levels fell within a safe range. The latest NCCN guidelines recommend that curative-intent resection include at least cholecystectomy, en bloc hepatic resection and lymphadenectomy.16 Other procedures, such as hepaticopancreaticoduodenectomy, bile duct resection and reconstruction and vascular resection and reconstruction, were performed by the surgical team on the basis of tumour invasion. To minimise inter-institutional variation in surgical practice, only high-throughput centres (≥five surgeries for GBC per year) were included and all the operating surgeons were at the level of associate professors or above.

Baseline data

In this study, patients >70 years of age were defined as elderly patients, and those ≤70 years of age were defined as younger patients, which was in line with the age classification reported in previous studies.1720

The following information was collected: demographics, preoperative liver function, tumour biomarkers, surgical variables, postoperative pathological findings and postoperative treatment. Specific variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), preoperative PTCD, TB levels, albumin (ALB) levels, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels, the international normalised ratio (INR), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9), incidental GBC, adenocarcinoma, tumour size, degree of differentiation, macrovascular invasion, the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer staging system,21 bile duct procedure, type of hepatectomy, intraoperative blood loss and adjuvant therapy.

The WHO defines a BMI ≥30 kg/m2 as obesity. For laboratory parameters, all patients were grouped according to the upper or lower limit of the normal range: 17.1 µmol/L for TB, 35 g/L for ALB, 40 U/L for ALT, 1.15 for INR, 5 µg/L for CEA and 37 U/L for CA 19-9. Pathology variables were determined on the basis of the results of the pathological examination. R1 resection was defined as histologic tumour-free margins <1 mm.22 The bile duct procedure included hepatic duct or common bile duct resection and reconstruction.

Short-term and long-term outcomes

The short-term outcomes included intraoperative adverse events of grade ≥2 (as per the Oslo classification definitions),23 postoperative grade B/C bile leak (as defined by the International Study Group of Liver Surgery),24 postoperative grade B/C liver failure (as defined by the International Study Group of Liver Surgery),25 postoperative major morbidity (Clavien-Dindo grade III–IV) within 90 days,26 readmission within 90 days due to surgery-related major morbidity, mortality within 90 days and length of stay (LOS).

The long-term outcomes included OS and CSS. OS was defined as the interval between surgery and death from any cause or the last follow-up, whereas CSS was defined as the time from surgery to death from cancer or the last follow-up.

Follow-up

Prognostic information and causes of death were obtained by telephone calls, network contact, onsite follow-up evaluation and medical and nursing records. All patients received standardised follow-up: every 2–3 months for the first 2 years after surgery; every 3–4 months for 3–5 years after surgery and every 6 months since the 6th year after surgery. Liver function, serum tumour markers and imaging were assessed at each follow-up. If the tumour recurred, metastasised or progressed, patients received PTCD, endoscopic nasobiliary drainage, adjuvant therapy, secondary surgery or conservative treatment. The last follow-up date was 31 December 2024. Loss to follow-up was defined as any patient who withdrew from the study or could not be contacted by the research team, resulting in unavailable survival outcome data at the last follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Normality was tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean±SD, and comparison between groups was analysed by independent sample t test. Variables that were not normally distributed were expressed as median (IQR), and Mann Whitney U test was used for comparison between groups. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages, and the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used when appropriate. Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank tests was used to compare OS and CSS in the original, PSM and IPTW cohorts. Median survival with 95% CI was reported for OS and CSS. For data with a missing proportion of <10%, multiple imputation by chained equations was performed. Continuous variables were imputed using linear regression models, whereas categorical variables were imputed with logistic or multinomial logistic regression models according to the number of categories. A total of 10 imputed datasets were generated. Statistical analyses were performed separately on each dataset, and parameter estimates and SEs were combined using Rubin’s rules. Identical statistical testing procedures were employed in the original, PSM and IPTW cohorts. Specifically, p values for IPTW-adjusted between-group comparisons were derived using weighted statistical tests.

To address the potential confounding variables between the younger and elderly groups, both PSM and IPTW based on the propensity score were applied. This study employed a logistic regression model to estimate the PS, with covariates including sex, BMI, preoperative PTCD, TB, ALB, ALT, INR, CEA, CA 19-9 and incidental GBC. Since ASA score and CCI are known age-related intrinsic variables, these variables were not included in the matching model for PSM in this study.27 In addition, for PSM, 1:1 nearest-neighbour matching was performed using a caliper width of 0.2 SD of the logit of the PS. To assess the balance of the baseline characteristics between the two groups, the standardised mean difference (SMD) before and after matching was calculated in this study.28 An SMD of <0.20 denoted a negligible difference between the two groups.29

Logistic regression models were used to estimate ORs (95% CI) for postoperative major morbidity, whereas Cox regression models were used to determine HRs (95% CI) for OS and CSS. In addition, the Fine-Gray competing-risk model was used to analyse CSS with a competing risk of non-cancer death. In particular, variables with a significant p value <0.10 in the unadjusted analysis were included in the multi-adjusted regression analysis. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF).

Stratification analyses were performed in the subgroup of elderly and younger patients according to histological grade and adjuvant therapy. In addition, given that the number of younger patients in the original cohort was much more than that of elderly patients, 1:2 PSM was further performed as an online supplemental analysis.

All data analyses were performed using SPSS software Version 29.0 (Armonk, New York, USA) and R software (Version 4.3.2, http://www.r-project.org/). A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Long-term outcomes

Long-term outcomes before matching for the two groups are shown in online supplemental table 4. The median follow-up time was 16.4 months in the younger group and 15.1 months in the elderly group. No significant difference was observed in the OS rate between the two groups (p=0.440), but the CSS rate was significantly greater in the elderly group than in the younger group (p=0.004), as shown in figure 4A,B.

Overall survival and cancer-specific survival in the younger and elderly groups before matching (A, B), after 1:1 propensity score matching (C, D) and after inverse probability of treatment weighting (E, F).

After PSM, in the younger group, the 5-year OS rate was 8.2%, and the 5-year CSS rate was 8.9%. In the elderly group, the 5-year OS rate was 17.3% and the 5-year CSS rate was 34.1%. There were significant differences in the OS rate and CSS rate between the two groups (OS: p<0.001; CSS: p<0.001), as shown in table 2 and figure 4C, D.

After IPTW, in the younger group, the 5-year OS rate was 21.6%, and the 5-year CSS rate was 23.7%. In the elderly group, the 5-year OS rate was 17.9%, and the 5-year CSS rate was 33.4%. No significant difference was observed in the OS rate between the two groups (p=0.648), but the CSS rate was significantly greater in the elderly group than in the younger group (p=0.002), as shown in table 2 and figure 4E,F.

Multi-adjusted regression analyses for major morbidity, OS and CSS

The VIFs for all variables included in the multi-adjusted regression analyses were below 2, indicating no multicollinearity, as shown in online supplemental table 5. Variables with a significant p value<0.10 in the unadjusted analysis were included in the multiadjusted regression analysis (online supplemental tables 6 and 7). Multiadjusted logistic regression analyses indicated that advanced age was independently associated with increased postoperative major morbidity in the original cohort (OR 1.83; 95% CI 1.17 to 2.87; p=0.008). Consistent results were obtained after 1:1 PSM (OR 1.88; 95% CI 1.08 to 3.30; p=0.027) and IPTW (OR 1.83; 95% CI 1.17 to 2.82; p=0.007), as shown in table 3.

As shown in online supplemental tables 8 and 9, results from the original and IPTW cohorts indicate no significant association between advanced age and OS. The significant association observed in the PSM cohort is likely due to the reduced sample size and selection effects arising from this rigorous matching process and does not alter our overall interpretation.

Sensitivity analysis

Stratification analyses according to poor differentiation and adjuvant therapy revealed no significant association between age >70 years and OS across all subgroups (all p>0.05). In contrast, age >70 years was consistently associated with a significantly better CSS rate in every subgroup, regardless of differentiation status or adjuvant therapy (all p<0.05), as shown in online supplemental table 10.

After 1:2 PSM, 256 younger patients and 136 elderly patients were included. After matching, the distribution of baseline characteristics were well-balanced between the two groups, as shown in online supplemental table 11. All SMDs were below 0.20, suggesting a negligible imbalance. Propensity score distributions before and after matching were assessed and plotted (online supplemental figure 1). The results demonstrated greater overlap between groups after matching than before, confirming the effectiveness of the matching procedure. Short-term outcomes after 1:2 PSM are displayed in online supplemental figure 2 and summarised in online supplemental table 12. After 1:2 PSM adjustment, the elderly group had significantly higher rates of postoperative liver failure (p=0.017) and 90-day major morbidities (p=0.009) than the younger group. No significant differences were observed between the two groups in the incidence of intraoperative grade ≥2 incidents, postoperative bile leakage or 90-day readmission due to major morbidities, as shown in online supplemental table 12. There were significant differences in the CSS rate between the two groups (p<0.001), as illustrated in online supplemental figure 3A, B.

Discussion

Rising life expectancy increases the incidence of age-related malignancies such as GBC in elderly individuals.27 30 At present, curative-intent resection remains the treatment with the greatest curative potential for GBC. 3133 However, whether elderly and younger patients can achieve similar outcomes remains controversial. These concerns stem from the potentially limited life expectancy of elderly patients, uncertainty about whether OS can be significantly improved and an increased comorbidity burden, all of which increase surgical complexity. Although advances in surgical techniques and perioperative care have reduced overall hepatobiliary surgery risks and expanded indications, elderly patients still encounter higher perioperative complication and mortality risks than younger patients.34 This combination of uncertain survival benefit and increased perioperative risk often leads to undue surgical apprehension in clinical practice, where advanced age is sometimes considered a relative contraindication.8 Crucially, this frequently results in treatment bias, favouring conservative approaches such as limited resection, palliative chemotherapy or even forgoing therapy altogether. Evidence from breast cancer research indicates that, even when comorbidities and functional status are controllable, non-standard treatment is still more common among elderly patients, highlighting that age itself may discourage surgical intervention. Notably, the latest research suggested that curative-intent resection might still significantly improve survival in elderly patients with GBC who have good performance status and effectively controlled comorbidities.35 36

Age is widely considered an independent prognostic factor for malignancies, including gastric,37 pancreatic38 and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) among others.39 40 In patients with HCC, Santambrogio et al suggested a greater rate of major postoperative morbidities after surgery in patients aged ≥75 years,39 whereas Danijel Galun et al reported no statistically significant difference in major morbidity between patients aged ≥70 years and their younger counterparts, despite higher overall complication rates in elderly patients.40 Notably, evidence regarding GBC is particularly scarce. Yasuhiro Ueda et al revealed no significant difference in overall postoperative morbidity between patients aged ≥75 and those aged <75 years,41 but the reliability of this finding requires verification due to the small sample size. With respect to long-term GBC prognosis, existing studies also show inconsistency: Li P et al reported poorer OS in patients aged >65 years with GBC,3 whereas Ueda’s team reported no significant difference in OS between patients aged ≥75 years and those aged <75 years.41

Previous studies comparing elderly and younger patients have certain limitations. First, they primarily relied on OS to assess long-term outcomes in elderly individuals and failed to adequately account for competing risks such as non-cancer deaths. Therefore, CSS was used to provide a more accurate assessment of oncological prognosis. Second, surgical procedures in prior studies often did not adhere to current NCCN guidelines. Since the extent of resection significantly influences perioperative complication rates in elderly patients—which is crucial for evaluating prognosis and comparing outcomes—this represents a major limitation. Third, many single-centre or public database studies lack critical covariates influencing treatment and prognosis (eg, comorbidities, preoperative status, complications), limiting generalisability, and most failed to match baseline characteristics, introducing selection bias. To address these limitations, we employ PSM and IPTW, which may mitigate them to some extent and enhance the reliability of the study results.

This study used IPTW and PSM to control for confounding factors and compared the short-term and long-term outcomes of elderly and younger patients with GBC after curative-intent resection. Analysis of the baseline characteristics revealed that the comorbidity status and basic functional reserve of elderly patients were worse than those of younger patients, which was consistent with previous studies.40 In terms of surgical strategy, we found that compared with younger patients, a greater proportion of elderly patients underwent R1 resection, but a lower proportion of elderly patients underwent bile duct surgery combined with hemihepatectomy. These findings suggest that younger patients with GBC tend to receive more aggressive surgeries, such as bile duct surgery and more extensive hepatectomy, with the aim of improving long-term survival. Comorbidities can directly influence treatment selection,42 and elderly patients with poorer comorbidity status may be more conservative in their choice of surgical procedure. Therefore, in real-world clinical practice, selection bias is inevitable when comparing elderly and younger patients undergoing curative-intent resection for GBC.

After IPTW and PSM, we adjusted for the baseline characteristics, such as surgical procedures, between the elderly and younger patients. By comparing the short-term and long-term outcomes of the two groups, we found no significant difference in OS. These results suggest that for patients with GBC who meet the indications for curative-intent resection, advanced age should not be considered an absolute contraindication to surgery. Instead, treatment decisions should be guided by a comprehensive assessment of physiological reserve, burden of comorbidities and tumour characteristics, ensuring that medically fit elderly patients are not unfairly excluded from interventions that may offer significant benefits. Second, when long-term oncological outcomes were evaluated, we found that elderly patients with GBC had significantly higher 1-year, 3-year and 5-year CSS rates than younger patients did. Multiadjusted Cox regression analysis conducted across multiple dimensions, including PSM and IPTW cohorts, further supports these findings, confirming that age over 70 years is independently associated with better CSS after curative-intent resection of GBC. The lower subdistribution hazard of cancer-specific death in patients aged >70 years in the Fine-Gray model (SHR=0.34, p<0.001) is mainly attributed to differences in tumour biological characteristics. The immune micro-environment of biliary tract tumours, particularly the degree of regulatory T cell (Treg) infiltration, significantly influences tumour progression and patient prognosis.43 This perspective equally applies to GBC: in elderly patients, reduced recruitment of Tregs within the tumour micro-environment or diminished immune-suppressive activity may weaken the tumour’s capacity for immune evasion.44 Consequently, tumours exhibit lower invasiveness and metastatic potential, directly reducing cancer-specific mortality risk. In addition, individualised conservative treatment strategies for elderly patients, avoiding excessive treatment-related damage, may also help maintain better CSS, while adjustment for the competing risk of non-cancer death further clarifies this protective effect. The phenomenon of elderly patients with cancer deriving significant CSS benefits from radical surgery is not unique. Recent studies in HCC, breast cancer and prostate cancer have also reported better CSS among elderly patients.35 45 46 A possible explanation is that the higher burden of non-cancer comorbidities in elderly patients potentially leads to increased postoperative non-cancer mortality, thereby masking OS benefits. After excluding such causes of death, the effective cancer control achieved through curative-intent resection allows the survival advantage for elderly patients to become apparent. Furthermore, some elderly patients experience relatively slow cancer progression, potentially due to biological characteristics associated with ageing, such as metabolic slowing, altered microenvironment and reduced cellular stemness.47

Taken together, these findings support our observation that selected elderly patients with GBC achieved superior cancer-specific outcomes following curative-intent resection compared with younger patients. Yet, elderly patients face higher perioperative risks, with a greater incidence of major morbidities and significantly longer LOS. This difference may be due mainly to the overall decline in physical condition of elderly patients: the high incidence of diabetes, cardiopulmonary diseases and other comorbidities directly weakens surgical tolerance and reduces organ functional reserve, which leads to a decreased ability to compensate for postoperative stress; moreover, functional decline and high comorbidity burden synergise to double the risk of malnutrition.48 49 Among them, pleural effusion is the most common type of morbidity, which may be related to slower recovery of respiratory function, limited diaphragmatic activity and a potentially increased burden of cardiopulmonary disease in elderly patients. Therefore, we need to further evaluate patients’ respiratory function during the perioperative period and strengthen perioperative airway management to reduce pneumonia and other related complications.

More importantly, our study reveals that the increased major morbidity among elderly patients is not accompanied by a rise in 90-day mortality. This suggests that successful surgical treatment for elderly patients is achievable, but it also places greater demands on early warning, timely identification and management of their complications.

A global multicentre prospective study reported a 90-day mortality rate of 3.8% and a major morbidity of 15.8% following elective liver resection.50 In the overall cohort of our study, the 90-day mortality rate was 11.8% (68/575) and the major morbidity was 24.3% (140/575), both exceeding the global average. This discrepancy may stem from the specific nature of our study population, which focused on GBC cases undergoing major or complex hepatectomy. In contrast, the global cohort included all patients undergoing liver surgery (including patients without cancer) and had a lower mean Complexity of Liver Surgery Score (0.6 points). Specifically, the incidence of bile leakage (grade B/C) in this study was 14.1% (81/575), higher than the 5.4% (117/2159) observed in the global cohort. This confirms that curative-intent resection for GBC poses greater challenges in biliary tract management, consistent with its higher surgical complexity.

On the basis of the above findings, we conclude that age alone should not be considered a contraindication for GBC surgery after thorough preoperative evaluation and preparation. Clinical decision-making needs to shift from ‘risk avoidance’ to ‘precise risk management and control’, and a risk‒benefit balance should be achieved through multidimensional perioperative intervention. We suggest more accurate preoperative evaluation, stricter patient selection and targeted control of comorbidities in elderly patients whenever possible. On the premise of ensuring R0 resection, surgical strategies with controllable trauma should be preferred. More importantly, an early warning system for elderly-specific complications should be systematically established to reduce bile leakage, abdominal infection and cardiovascular events by strengthening perioperative management, rational drainage tube placement and strict postoperative monitoring. In addition, the enhanced recovery after surgery concept can be used to optimise perioperative management, such as by shortening the fasting time and the duration of catheter indwelling, to reduce the risk of infection.51 Ultimately, through the integration of accurate assessment, dynamic risk regulation, early warning and rehabilitation optimisation, a new paradigm of safe and efficient surgical treatment for elderly patients with GBC will be constructed, and the leap from individualised treatment to refined management will be realised.

This study has the following limitations. First, this was a retrospective study and has inherent bias. Therefore, the purpose of performing PSM and IPTW in this study was to reduce the potential bias of retrospective data analysis, although this statistical method cannot completely eliminate it. Second, as a multicentre study, the statistical analyses did not employ a random-effects model to account for intercentre heterogeneity. While all centres were high-volume tertiary care centres with fixed inclusion periods and consecutive patient recruitment, this may have resulted in insufficient adjustment for between-centre differences. Third, patients who underwent laparoscopic or robotic surgeries were excluded from this study. Previous reports revealed that laparoscopic GBC resection is associated with fewer postoperative complications and a shorter length of hospital stay.52 Therefore, differences in surgical methods may introduce additional confounding, and this will be studied separately in the future. Fourth, some elderly patients voluntarily opted out of curative-intent resection because of poor physical status (eg, severe frailty or uncontrolled comorbidities), resulting in the elderly patients included in the analysis potentially having better surgical tolerance. This treatment selection bias cannot be corrected by the existing statistical methods, implicating that we should be more cautious in making surgical decisions for elderly patients in clinical practice.

Conclusion

The results of this study revealed that although elderly patients had longer hospital stays and an increased risk of major morbidities after surgery, elderly patients achieve better CSS. Surgical decision-making for the elderly patients with GBC needs to be reconsidered, and the risks and benefits of surgery should be evaluated in greater detail.
