Carbon-fixing bacteria and sediment organic carbon response to the introduction of mangrove plant Sonneratia caseolaris
Authors: Xiaofang Huang, Junde Dong, Youshao Wang, Hongbin Liu, Weiguo Zhou, Hao Cheng, Xi Yang, Tongyin Liang, Bing Yang, Juan Ling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe fast-growing Sonneratia caseolaris has been widely used in restoration efforts to accelerate the recovery of degraded mangrove ecosystems. Carbon-fixing bacteria (CFB) are recognized as major microbial contributors to sediment carbon accumulation in the mangrove wetlands. However, the response of the CFB community and sediment organic carbon (SOC) to introduced S. caseolaris is still unknown.ResultsIn this study, the sediment properties (especially SOC), the abundances of carbon-fixing genes (cbbL and cbbM), as well as the CFB community composition based on cbbL and cbbM functional genes and their interaction network, were compared between the introduced mangrove species (S. caseolaris) and native species (Kandelia obovata, Aegiceras corniculatum, and Acanthus ilicifolius) in the Niutianyang Mangrove Forest, Shantou, China. Our results showed that S. caseolaris markedly enhanced the SOC content and the abundances of the carbon-fixing genes (P < 0.05). The dominant CFB taxa were “purple sulfur bacteria” (PSB) (e.g., Chromatiales) and “purple nonsulfur bacteria” (PNSB) (e.g., Rhodobacterales). The relative abundances of some well-known plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR), especially the Rhizobiales and Burkholderiales, were significantly increased (P < 0.05) by the S. caseolaris introduction. In addition, there was a more complex but less stable CFB community interaction network in the S. caseolaris sediments. It was well worth noting that a large number of the keystone taxa in the network were the sulfur-oxidizing bacteria (SOB), such as Sulfuriferula and Thioalkalivibrio, suggesting a tight connection between the carbon and sulfur cycles in mangrove wetlands, and these key genera might have decisive roles in reshaping CFB community structure and functioning.ConclusionsThese findings confirmed that introduced S. caseolaris can strongly affect the CFB communities and the SOC content, and provided new insights into the microbial carbon sequestration in the mangrove wetlands.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-026-02631-6.

Background

Mangrove wetlands have an excellent potential for carbon fixation, particularly in the sediment. Although covering only 0.5% of the tropical forest area, they contribute to about 10% terrestrial dissolved organic carbon (DOC) exportation to the ocean, and are characterized as the “blue carbon” [1]. Mangrove sediments are the main reservoir for organic carbon, which contain nearly 90% of the total carbon pool in the mangrove ecosystem [2]. However, mangrove wetlands have suffered from large degradation, having lost 20–35% total cover over the past 70 years [3]. The global decline in tropical mangrove wetlands can severely weaken their ecosystem services, leading to the release of previously stored sediment carbon into the atmosphere, increasing CO2 emissions and potentially contributing to global warming [4].

Since the 1970 s, mangrove restoration projects have been widely initiated to compensate for the mangrove loss or degradation, which is increasingly recognized as an essential driver for mitigating climate change and enhancing the carbon sequestration capacity [5–7]. Existing research has focused on the sediment carbon drivers for mangrove restoration, such as carbon sequestration technology and vegetation structure modification [8, 9], but little attention has been directed towards the sediment microbial communities induced by mangrove restoration, which may be highly correlated with the carbon storage in mangrove ecosystems.

Sediment microorganisms are known to be major drivers in the carbon biogeochemical cycle. In the mangrove sediments, the autotrophic carbon-fixing bacteria (CFB) can utilize carbon dioxide (CO2) to synthesize microbial biomass carbon, and then transform it into the sediment organic carbon (SOC) pool in the form of microbial residues [10]. There are six autotrophic pathways for carbon fixation by CFB communities, and the major pathway used by CFB communities is the Calvin cycle [11]. Ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (RuBisCO) is a key Calvin Cycle enzyme involved in CO2-fixation (Saini et al., 2011), and the RuBisCO large-subunit genes (i.e., cbbL and cbbM) were widely regarded as molecular biomarkers for the CFB community [12, 13].

Exotic plants Sonneratia sp. (e.g., S. caseolaris and S. apetala) were widely planted in the mangrove restoration because they can quickly form dense mangrove forests, and they are the main contributors to the increase in mangrove wetland area in China [14, 15]. Recent studies found that the S. apetala can accumulate biomass production in the short term, but it had a lower ecosystem carbon stock than the native mangrove species in the long term [16, 17]. Apart from mangrove species, the sediment nutrient (e.g., TN and TP) and salinity were also the distinct drivers for SOC stock in mangrove restoration [6]. The mangrove wetland is a hotspot for “blue carbon” research, but there are few studies on soil functional microbial communities in mangrove restoration, especially CO2-fixing bacterial communities, which are highly likely to drive the SOC storage.

Hence, this study mainly focused on the effects of S. caseolaris introduction on the SOC content, cbbL and cbbM gene abundances, and the CFB community composition in mangrove wetlands. Following the establishment of the mangrove restoration project in Shantou, Guangdong Province, Kandelia obovata, Aegiceras corniculatum, Acanthus ilicifolius, and the introduced species S. caseolaris have been widely planted in the Niutianyang Mangrove Forest in Shantou, China, in 2018 [18]. Sediment samples were collected from these four typical mangrove species (K. obovata, A. corniculatum, A. ilicifolius, and S. caseolaris). In addition, the sediment properties, especially the SOC, were also measured to elucidate the complicated interaction among the mangrove plant species, the sediment nutrients, and the CFB community. The results would supplement knowledge about potential impacts of introduced S. caseolaris on local mangrove wetlands, as well as reveal microbial drivers for carbon stock, providing theoretical guidance to enhance carbon sequestration in mangrove restoration.

Results

S. caseolaris introduction increased the sediment organic carbon

Sediment organic carbon, the main focus of this study, differed significantly among mangrove species. In particular, the introduction of S. caseolaris resulted in a marked increase in SOC compared with native species (P < 0.05). Generally, sediment physicochemical properties (sediment nutrients, pH, and salinity) exhibited significant differences among these four mangrove species, except the water content showed no variation (P < 0.05; Fig. 1). Compared with other mangrove species, the salinity level of S. caseolaris (SC) sediment was the highest (P < 0.05). Mangrove sediments were consistently slightly acidic, with pH values ranging from 6.0 to 6.9. The pH of K. obovata (KO) sediment was significantly higher than that of other mangrove species (P < 0.05), while A. ilicifolius (AI) and S. caseolaris (SC) sediments showed the lowest pH, reaching only pH 6.0. In addition, the introduced SC sediments observed significantly (P < 0.05) higher SOC (26.97 ± 7.95 mg/g) and total nitrogen (TN) (2.52 ± 0.80 mg/g) compared with native mangrove species (KO and AC) except for AI. The NO3−-N contents (1.73 ± 0.24 mg/g) in SC sediments were significantly higher than those of the three native mangrove species (P < 0.05). The total sulfur (TS) content was highest in the KO sediment, while its NH4+-N content was the lowest among these four mangrove species. S. caseolaris introduction improved the cbbL and cbbM gene abundance

The absolute abundance of the cbbL and cbbM genes differed significantly among these four mangrove species (P < 0.05; Fig. 2). The introduced SC sediment had a higher abundance of cbbL and cbbM genes (P < 0.05), with the maximum copy numbers reaching 4.77 × 108 and 4.35 × 107 copies/g dry sediment (Fig. 2a and b), and was approximately 3–6 times higher than that in the native mangrove species. Diversity patterns of carbon-fixing bacterial communities associated with cbbL and cbbM genes

Analysis of carbon-fixing bacterial diversity demonstrated that the introduced mangrove species harbored a distinct microbial community compared to native species. In total, 677,685 sequences and 49,832 OTUs were gained by the cbbL gene sequencing, while 1,286,978 sequences and 76,669 OTUs were acquired by sequencing of the cbbM gene. The NMDS ordination plots revealed clear differences in the community composition of CFB harboring cbbL and cbbM genes among the four groups (Fig. 3). The results of cbbL gene sequencing showed that OTUs were affiliated to 36 phyla, 72 classes, 222 orders, 335 families, and 791 genera (including unclassified). Rhizobiales, Chromatiales, Steroidobacterales, Burkholderiales, Acidiferrobacterales, and Enterobacterales were dominant orders, reaching average relative abundance of 11.22%, 7.11%, 6.48%, 5.88%, 3.93%, and 3.09%, respectively (Additional file 1: Fig. S1a). A large number of OTUs were unclassified taxonomically, accounting for 42.88%, and could only be identified to “unclassified bacteria” (Additional file 1: Fig. S1a). The sequencing of cbbM genes indicated that all OTUs belonged to 20 phyla, 25 classes, 90 orders, 137 families, and 347 genera (including unclassified). Among them, the orders Burkholderiales, Xanthomonadales, Rhodobacterales, Woeseiales, Chromatiales, and Thiohalomonadales were more abundant, with average relative abundance of 16.09%, 4.90%, 3.02%, 2.55%, 2.52%, and 2.34%, respectively (Additional file 1: Fig. S1b). Consistently, approximately 50.90% of OTUs were assigned as “unclassified bacteria” (Additional file 1: Fig. S1b). Notably, many bacterial orders such as Chromatiales, Burkholderiales, Acidiferrobacterales, Woeseiales, Rhodobacterales, Mariprofundales, Rhodospirillales, Thiohalomonadales, and Thiotrichales contained both the cbbL and cbbM gene (Additional file 1: Fig. S1).

The LEfSe results revealed that CFB taxa differed significantly (P < 0.05) among four mangrove species, which could be identified as “biomarkers” or “indicators” (Fig. 4). In the CFB communities harboring the cbbL gene, Rhizobiales, Halothiobacillales, and Thiohalobacterales were relatively more abundant in the S. caseolaris sediment. The relative abundances of Steroidobacterales, Woeseiales, Acidiferrobacterales, and Mariprofundales in the A. corniculatum sediment were relatively high, while Chromatiales were relatively abundant in the A. ilicifolius sediment. There were 11 abundant taxa in the K. obovata sediment, such as Enterobacterales, Thiohalomonadales, Thiotrichales, and Acidimicrobiales (Fig. 4a). In the CFB communities containing the cbbM gene, Burkholderiales, Acidithiobacillales, Caulobacterales, Actinomycetales, and Rhizobiales were relatively abundant in the S. caseolaris sediment. The relative abundances of Woeseiales, Xanthomonadales, Rhodobacterales, and Mariprofundales were relatively high in the A. corniculatum sediment, while Acidiferrobacterales, Flavobacteriales, and Propionibacteriales were relatively abundant in the A. ilicifolius sediment. Moreover, Nitrosococcales, Chromatiales, Thiotrichales, and Thiohalomonadales were relatively abundant in the K. obovata sediment (Fig. 4b). TS showed significant and positive correlations with pH and C/N, while it showed negative correlations with water content, salinity, and NH4+-N (P < 0.05). The NO3−-N was significantly and positively correlated with salinity, while it was significantly negatively associated with pH and C/N (P < 0.05). While the cbbM-harboring CFB community network consisted of an average of 565 nodes and 11,801 edges, 84.73% were positive correlations out of the total edges (Additional file 1: Table S2). The average degree (avgK) was a key topological property to describe the network connectivity, and a higher avgK means a more complex network. Keystone taxa in the CFB community were identified using Zi-Pi plots. A total of 4 module hubs (highly connected nodes within a module) and 230 connectors (nodes linking different modules) were detected across all the bacterial networks containing the cbbL genes, while 7 module hubs and 140 connectors were found in the networks containing the cbbM genes (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Notably, there were a large number of keystone taxa that were related to sulfur oxidation bacteria (SOB), such as Sulfuriferula, Sulfuritortus, Sulfurivermis, Thiothrix, Thioalkalivibrio, and Thiodiazotropha (Additional file 1: Table S3), implicating that the carbon-fixing keystone taxa were functionally versatile microbes.

Overall, the combined analyses of diversity, correlation, and network consistently indicated that the introduced mangrove species S. caseolaris harbored a distinct carbon-fixing bacterial community compared with native mangrove species. Among the native species, A. ilicifolius appeared to be more similar to the introduced S. caseolaris in terms of sediment carbon-related microbial attributes, whereas K. obovata and A. corniculatum displayed more marked differences. These patterns suggested that the introduced mangrove species may be associated with shifts in microbial community structure and potential carbon-cycling dynamics in mangrove sediments.

Discussion

Effects of S. caseolaris introduction on the SOC and carbon-fixing genes abundances

Abundance of cbbL and cbbM was abundantly observed (10−7 ~ 10−8 copies/g dry sediment) in the mangrove sediments in this study, which was consistent with a previous report for coastal salt marsh wetlands (10−7 ~ 10−9 copies/g dry sediment) [19], potentially reflecting high microbial biomass of CFB communities in these “blue carbon” ecosystems. Besides, our study consistently showed that the abundance of the cbbL gene was considerably higher than the cbbM gene [11]. This difference may be explained by the fact that the autotrophic CFB harboring the cbbM gene are typically microaerophilic or facultative anaerobic species; therefore, the higher oxygen availability in the surface sediments might decrease the cbbM abundance [20]. Notably, the highest abundances of cbbL and cbbM genes were found in the introduced S. caseolaris sediments, potentially supporting an enhancement of SOC content (Fig. 1). Indeed, this study observed a significant linear positive correlation between the abundance of carbon-fixing genes (cbbL and cbbM) and the SOC contents (Fig. 2). This finding indicates that, to some extent, CFB may play a significant role in facilitating the rapid growth and carbon accumulation of the introduced species S. caseolaris [18]. The increased SOC content could reflect the higher litter falls or root input in the introduced S. caseolaris sediments [16], but the stable carbon storage should be further confirmed by combining carbon fractionation, carbon-mineral association, or stable carbon isotopic measurements. Recent studies have emphasized that the introduced species S. apetala just enhanced SOC retention in the short term, but potentially reduced long-term carbon sequestration compared with native mangrove species in mangrove restoration [16, 17, 21]. Therefore, long-term monitoring of the CFB community and SOC storage in introduced S. caseolaris sediment is recommended as a supplemental assessment method for their ecological benefits.

Besides, except for the mangrove species, our study also demonstrated that sediment nutrients (e.g., TN and NO3−-N), pH, and salinity were the distinct drivers of SOC content (Fig. 5). A previous study consistently revealed that nutrient (e.g., TN) and salinity were the sediment carbon drivers [6]. These findings consistently underscore the need for nutrient and salinity management to achieve carbon neutrality goals in mangrove restoration. It was worth noting that the native mangrove species, A. ilicifolius, which has been extensively explored for its chemistry and pharmacology, had similar high nutrient contents (e.g., SOC, TN, and NH4+-N) with the introduced S. caseolaris (Fig. 1), indicating that A. ilicifolius can also be used for ecological mangrove species in effective carbon sequestration associated with mangrove restoration [18].

Effects of S. caseolaris introduction on the CFB community structure

The mangrove species significantly affect the diversity of sediment carbon-fixing bacteria, particularly those utilizing the RuBisCO enzymes encoded by cbbL and cbbM genes (Fig. 3, beta diversity). Our previous study has consistently suggested that the different mangrove species harbored distinct bacterial communities, which in turn support the growth of their host plants [22]. The dominant cbbL-bearing bacterial orders were Rhizobiales, Chromatiales, Steroidobacterales, Burkholderiales, Acidiferrobacterales, and Enterobacterales. While Burkholderiales, Xanthomonadales, Rhodobacterales, Woeseiales, Chromatiales, and Thiohalomonadales were the dominant orders in the CFB community containing the cbbM gene. A previous study confirmed that carbon-fixing microbes were active in mangrove sediments, and Rhizobiales, Burkholderiales, and Chromatiales were the dominant taxa containing the cbbL or cbbM carbon-fixing pathway [23]. Most of these carbon-fixing microbes have been described in a comprehensive review [10], including a huge number known as “purple sulfur bacteria” (PSB) and “purple nonsulfur bacteria” (PNSB) [24, 25]. For example, Chromatiales was observed as the dominant order containing both the cbbL and cbbM genes in the mangrove sediments. Members in this order mostly belong to the categories of PSB, which were obligate or facultative photoautotrophs by carbon fixation, with coupled nitrogen fixation and sulfide oxidation pathways [26]. Besides, most members of Rhodobacterales were also found in large numbers in the CFB community in the present study. Rhodobacterales belong to the PNSB and can grow photoautotrophically by fixing CO2 or photolithoautotrophically with S2− and S2O32− as electron donors [27]. In addition, many other bacterial orders in the CFB community of mangrove sediments both participated in nitrogen and sulfur cycles, such as Rhizobiales (a well-known diazotroph), Thiohalomonadales, and Thiotrichales (common SOB taxa), which were consistent with previous reports in karst wetland [11, 12] and mangrove wetland [23]. These findings demonstrated a tight connection between carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling [28], and these CFB taxa were functionally versatile microbes driving the coupling of C, N, and S cycles in the mangrove wetlands.

Plant species or vegetation types are decisive factors in determining the carbon-fixing bacterial community composition [19, 29]. Our study revealed that the S. caseolaris introduction reshaped the CFB community composition significantly. In this study, the CFB indicators, such as Rhizobiales, Halothiobacillales, Burkholderiales, Thiohalobacterales, and Acidithiobacillales, were strongly associated with the introduced S. caseolaris (Fig. 4). Notably, most members of these indicators, especially the Rhizobiales and Burkholderiales, were the well-known plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) and could regulate mangrove plant growth by fixing nitrogen or solubilizing mineral phosphate [30, 31]. Therefore, the introduced S. caseolaris may involve selective recruitment of rhizosphere microbiota and shape a unique and beneficial microbial community to enhance their nutrient acquisition for fast growth [32, 33]. It should be noted that a high proportion of OTUs remained unclassified in this study, which may be due to the primer selection or database limitations. Further studies are required to comprehensively characterize the carbon-fixing bacterial OTUs and their ecological roles.

The unseen mangrove-sediment-microbe interactions for mangrove carbon sequestration

Carbon-fixing microorganisms play crucial roles in fixing CO2 into organic carbon, and are functionally equivalent to plants in the sediment carbon cycle [10], shaping the unseen plant–microbe interactions for the carbon sequestration [34]. Our study found that native and introduced mangrove plants can remarkably affect the SOC content and other sediment properties (Fig. 1), which further modified the carbon-fixing bacterial community structure and abundance (Figs. 2, 3, 4), thus optimizing complicated mangrove-sediment-microbe interactions to jointly promote carbon sequestration in mangrove ecosystems (Fig. 8).Fig. 8The complicated mangrove-sediment-microbe interactions for carbon sequestration in mangrove sediments

The complicated mangrove-sediment-microbe interactions for carbon sequestration in mangrove sediments

Notably, the present study revealed that the S. caseolaris introduction enhanced nutrient contents (e.g., SOC and TN) in the sediments, which were mainly derived from faster litter and root decomposition of this fast-growing mangrove plant [16, 35]. Besides, the introduction of S. caseolaris also significantly reshaped the bacterial community structure, especially the sediment carbon-fixing gene abundance and the relative abundance of PGPR taxa such as Rhizobiales and Burkholderiales (Figs. 2, 3, 4). The recruitment of beneficial microbiota may be an ecological strategy of the introduced mangrove species to not only survive but also adapt themselves and compete with the native species, which resulted in a faster growth rate and increased efficiency in nutrient availability for the mangrove plant [22, 36]. The increased gene abundance of the CFB community harboring cbbL and cbbM genes might have resulted in increased microbial residue inputs; hence, the increased microbial- and plant-derived residuals consequently contributed to increasing SOC storage in introduced mangrove sediments (Fig. 8) [37]. In turn, the SOC and other sediment factors (e.g., pH, C/N, and TS) affected the CFB community structure significantly in mangrove wetlands (Figs. 5, 6). Consistently, previous studies have also found that SOC was the major factor influencing CFB community structure [11, 38]. All the results indicated there were complicated interactions between the mangrove plant and sediment microbes.

With a shift of the microbial community composition, the microbial community stability and functioning might shift as a cascading effect [39]. Our study observed distinct CFB community interaction patterns among the four mangrove species, and the CFB communities were more complex and less stable within introduced S. caseolaris sediments (Fig. 7), which was similar to a previous study with more complex CH4-cycling microbial communities in introduced S. apetala sediments [35]. Previous studies predicted that increased network complexity would be associated with reduced network stability, and this puzzle can be illustrated with the so-called “complexity-stability” paradox [40, 41]. Consistent with the stability-complexity theory, our study demonstrated that S. caseolaris introduction could complicate the CFB community structure and destabilize the network in the mangrove ecosystem. Notably, the keystone taxa in the carbon-fixing bacterial network were mostly the SOB taxa (e.g., Sulfuriferula and Thioalkalivibrio), suggesting they were “ecosystem engineers” in the mangrove wetlands and had considerable influence on the microbiome structure and functioning [42].

In summary, the introduction of S. caseolaris increased the CFB community abundance and the SOC content in the short term, while reshaping a more highly complex but less stable microbial network in the mangrove sediments. These complicated interactions among plant-sediment-microbe symbiosis may be influenced by the benefit-driven selection of the mangrove plant host through modifying plant signals or exudates [43]. As demonstrated in this study, the impact of introduced S. caseolaris on carbon storage was only investigated within the surface sediment layer. Our study intentionally focused on the 0–10 cm layer because it represents the most biogeochemically active zone, where plant–microbe interactions and fresh organic matter inputs are strongest, and where initial responses to plant community changes are most likely to be detected. However, it remains unclear how the microbes, especially the CFB community and SOC content, respond in the deeper sediment layers in response to S. caseolaris introduction. Therefore, further studies on the sediment depth effects are needed, which will enable a more comprehensive evaluation of the introduced S. caseolaris on the carbon sequestration and associated microbial community. Additionally, as the introduced S. caseolaris has been dominant in mangrove restoration in the past few decades, long-term monitoring of the mangrove ecosystems is vital, particularly regarding their carbon sequestration ability and the microbial drivers, which are necessary for evaluating the ecological impacts of the introduced S. caseolaris on the mangrove ecosystem in the future.

Conclusions

The present study examined the impacts of the S. caseolaris introduction on the carbon-fixing genes abundance and CFB community structure. The SOC content, carbon-fixing genes abundance (including cbbL and cbbM), relative abundance of some CFB taxa (e.g., Rhizobiales and Burkholderiales), and the CFB community network complexity were all significantly increased (P < 0.05) while stability was decreased in the introduced S. caseolaris sediments. These shifts in CFB communities and their abundance may result in a change in the carbon accumulation in mangrove sediments. Taken together, the present study improved the understanding of the unseen mangrove-sediment-microbe interactions underneath the S. caseolaris introduction, providing new insights into the underlying mechanism of introduced S. caseolaris on microbial carbon sequestration in mangrove wetlands.

Methods

Study site and sampling

Shantou, a coastal city in Guangdong Province, is also a special economic zone in China and has typical urban mangrove ecosystems. The Niutianyang Mangrove Forest, located in the Rongjiang River Estuary, Shantou, is a well-known mangrove restoration area. Sediment samples were collected from the surface layers (0–10 cm) in the mangrove rhizosphere in the Niutianyang Mangrove Forest (23.3849 N, 116.5782 E) in October 2023. These mangrove plants were approximately 5 years old after short-term mangrove restoration (planted in 2018). The introduced (S. caseolaris) and native (K. obovata, A. corniculatum, A. ilicifolius) mangrove species within the restored mangrove area were investigated. To minimize the potential effects of mangrove habitat on the environmental gradients, our sampling strategy focused on adjacent geographical sites with the same or similar hydrological and geomorphological conditions. Six replicate sediment samples of each mangrove species were randomly collected from each sampling site, totaling 24 sediment samples. Then, the sediment samples were sent to the laboratory immediately on dry ice. The subsamples were stored at 4 °C for sediment physicochemical properties analysis, or − 80 °C for DNA extraction.

Analysis of sediment properties

A range of sediment physicochemical properties was analyzed, including water content, pH, salinity, and nutrient contents. To calculate the water content, the sediment sample was freeze-dried until a steady weight was reached. The sediment pH and salinity were analyzed at a 1:2.5 and 1:5 sediment dilution, respectively, with a YSI multi-parameter water quality analyzer. The air-dried sediment was finely ground, and an elemental analyzer (Germany) was used to determine the SOC, total nitrogen (TN), and total sulfur (TS) contents. The sediment ammonium (NH4+-N) contents were measured with the salicylate assay, while nitrate (NO3−-N) concentrations were analyzed using spectrophotometric analysis [26].

DNA extraction, qPCR analysis, and sequencing

The abundances of two key functional genes involved in microbial carbon fixation (cbbL and cbbM) were determined using quantitative PCR (qPCR). DNA was extracted from fresh sediment samples (1.0 g) with the HiPure Soil DNA Kits (Magen, China). DNA quality was evaluated using 1% agarose gel, and the DNA yield was further quantified using a spectrophotometer (Nanodrop 2000c, Thermo Scientific, USA). Real-time qPCR was performed using the ABI 7300 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA) to determine the absolute abundance of the carbon-fixing genes.

The cbbL and cbbM gene amplification was conducted using the primers exhibited in Additional file 1: Table S1 as previously described [19]. Each qPCR analysis was run in triplicate, and the reaction mixture (20 μL) contained 10 μL 2X Taq Plus Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), 0.8 μL of each primer, 1 μL of template DNA, and 7.4 μL of ddH2O [19]. The qPCR reaction was conducted as previously reported [11]. The PCR products were then sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq2000 sequencing system. The cbbL and cbbM gene sequences were submitted to the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database (PRJNA1327769).

Bioinformatic sequence analysis

The acquired raw sequences were extracted, trimmed, and quality screened using QIIME v1.9.1 [44]. All the singletons were removed, and putative chimeric sequences were identified and removed using the UCHIME algorithm [45]. These highly qualified sequences were assigned to operational taxonomic units (OTUs) (97% identity) by UPARSE v9.2.64 [46]. Finally, representative sequences for each OTU were classified using the Ribosomal Database Project (RDP) Classifier v 2.2 [47].

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the mean values. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post-hoc Tukey’s HSD (Honestly Significant Difference) test was conducted to analyze the effects of the mangrove species on sediment properties and the cbbL and cbbM gene abundance. Linear regression analysis was conducted to determine the correlation of carbon-fixing gene abundance and SOC. The non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) was performed to analyze whether the CFB community clustered among different mangrove species [48]. Moreover, changes in beta diversity of the CFB community in different mangrove species were analyzed using analysis of similarity (ANOSIM) based on Bray–Curtis distances. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) coupled with effect size (LEfSe) analyses was used to identify the different biomarkers with LDA scores > 2 [49]. Pearson’s correlation coefficients and Mantel tests were compositely analyzed to determine the relationship between the CFB community and sediment properties [39]. Distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA) was also conducted to identify the microbial variation contributed by the environmental factors based on the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity metric [50]. A co-occurrence correlation was calculated using the psych package v1.8.12, and the network visualization and topological analysis were carried out with Gephi v0.9.2 [51]. The possible topological roles of individual metwork nodes were identified using within- (Zi) and among- (Pi) module connectivity plot [52]. The robustness analysis was conducted by random node removal using natural connectivity as a general measure of stability, and the robustness or stability was negative with the slope [40]. All these microbial community analyses (e.g., NMDS, LEfSe, Pearson, and network analyses) were based on OTUs derived from the functional genes cbbL and cbbM.
