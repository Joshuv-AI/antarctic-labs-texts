Comparison of Human Melanoma Single Cell Profiles to Evolutionary Medicine Model Xiphophorus Provides Insights in Disease Control
Authors: Yuan Lu, Will Boswell, Rachel Carroll, Maggs X, Yanting Xing, Wesley C Warren, Manfred Schartl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMelanoma remains one of the deadliest forms of cancer. Despite recent therapeutic advances, including immune checkpoint inhibitors and small‐molecule kinase inhibitors, patients frequently develop treatment resistance. Novel models are needed to devise strategies that overcome resistance and further reduce melanoma‐related mortality. Interspecies hybrid fish from the Xiphophorus lineage develop mutant Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR)‐driven melanomas that display morphology, bulk gene expression, disease initiation, and progression processes mimicking those of human melanomas. These similarities have enabled their comparative use in evaluating why human melanomas exhibit cancer cell plasticity and how the cancer cells interact with diverse immune populations. However, it remains unclear whether Xiphophorus melanomas recapitulate some or all these features. To address this, we performed single‐nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNAseq) analysis of Xiphophorus melanomas. Employing comparative analyses with results from human melanoma studies, we demonstrate that Xiphophorus melanomas faithfully mimic the cellular heterogeneity observed in human melanoma, including cancer cells, endothelial cells, cancer‐associated fibroblasts, B cells, M1 and M2 macrophages, N1 and N2 neutrophils, various T helper cells, and less cytotoxic, but not exhausted CD8+ T cells. Immune and cancer cell subtyping showed Xiphophorus melanoma represented an immune‐evaded status.

Introduction

Despite recent advancements in melanoma therapies and a corresponding decline in melanoma‐related mortality, melanoma still remains one of the most lethal cancers, accounting for approximately 75% of skin cancer deaths (Wang et al. 2025; Boutros et al. 2024; Gonzalez et al. 2013; Maio 2012). The five‐year survival rate for patients with advanced melanoma stays low, ranging from 34% to 52% (Hamid et al. 2019). Currently available treatments include immunotherapies (e.g., PD‐1 inhibitor, CTLA‐4 inhibitor, LAG‐3 inhibitor), small‐molecule kinase inhibitors targeting BRAF and MEK, and recently approved tumor‐infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy (Boutros et al. 2024; van Not et al. 2024; Hsieh et al. 2024; Dhanyamraju and Patel 2022; Leonardi et al. 2020).

However, these therapies often lead to the development of resistance (Hsieh et al. 2024; Christofi et al. 2019; Chen and Mellman 2017). Resistance arises from both intrinsic and extrinsic mechanisms, including innate and acquired resistance to kinase inhibitors, changes in melanoma immunogenicity, impaired immune cell trafficking, inefficient T cell priming, evasion of cell death, neovascularization, modulation of the tumor microenvironment, extracellular matrix remodeling, metabolic competition, T cell exhaustion, immune checkpoint upregulation, and alterations in the gut microbiota (Boutros et al. 2024; Ziogas et al. 2023).

Given these challenges, there is an urgent need for evaluating new models of melanoma progression that can either target key steps in the phenotype‐switching process or bypass resistance altogether. Interspecies hybrids between select Xiphophorus species can spontaneously develop genetically programmed melanoma (Schartl and Lu 2024). The Xiphophorus cancer model has been instrumental in elucidating critical steps in tumor initiation, progression, and invasion. It has also led to the identification of several clinically relevant melanoma biomarkers, including SLC45A2 for early diagnosis, Osteopontin for malignancy, and Cystathionase (for senescence escape). Notably, the discovery of the oncogene xmrk in Xiphophorus preceded and helped inform the discovery of the RAS/RAF/ERK pathway as a key driver in human melanomagenesis (for a comprehensive review see Schartl and Lu 2024).

Melanomagenesis in these hybrids follows Mendelian segregation patterns, involving inheritance of a well‐characterized oncogene and variant alleles of modulator genes. Well‐characterized hybrid combinations that develop melanoma include X. maculatus × X. hellerii , X. maculatus × X. couchianus , and X. birchmanni × X. malinche (Schartl and Lu 2024). In each case, the melanoma‐bearing hybrids inherit a mutant epidermal growth factor receptor gene (xmrk) from one parent and lack functional modulators (such as Cdkn2ab, Rab3d, Myrip, Adgre5/CD97, or Long Neurotoxin OH‐57‐like) from the other (Soria et al. 2025; Lu et al. 2020; Powell, Garcia‐Olazabal, et al. 2020; Garcia‐Olazabal et al. 2025; Regneri et al. 2019; Kazianis et al. 1998). The xmrk oncogene activates multiple downstream signaling pathways that promote melanocyte dedifferentiation, tumor proliferation, survival, angiogenesis, invasion, and escape from senescence (Schartl and Lu 2024). However, melanoma does not develop in the parental species that naturally carry the oncogene xmrk because the oncogenic activity is completely suppressed, and melanocytes remain in a nevus‐like state. This clearly points to the presence of co‐evolved suppressor mechanisms that restrain xmrk‐driven tumorigenesis, warranting further investigation into these evolutionarily developed defenses for novel anti‐melanoma strategies.

Melanoma cells exhibit profound plasticity at the molecular, cellular, and morphological levels, allowing for reversible phenotypic transitions between a locally restricted, proliferative state and an invasive, nonproliferative state. Single cell studies of human melanoma underscore this diversity of malignant cell states within a single tumor, including proliferative, invasive, and drug‐tolerant phenotypes (Jerby‐Arnon et al. 2018; Tirosh, Izar, Prakadan, Wadsworth 2nd, et al. 2016). One of the most comprehensive evaluations of the melanoma tumor microenvironment (TME) showed the malignant cells exhibited two distinct transcriptional cell states, high and low levels of the MITF transcription factor; the low MITF‐expressing cells also show elevated levels of the AXL kinase (Tirosh, Izar, Prakadan, Wadsworth 2nd, et al. 2016). The proliferative phenotype is generally more sensitive to both kinase inhibitors and immunotherapy, whereas the invasive phenotype demonstrates greater therapeutic tolerance (Song et al. 2024; Subhadarshini et al. 2023; Shi et al. 2023; Hossain and Eccles 2023; Huang et al. 2021; Smith et al. 2021; Liguoro et al. 2020; Emmons et al. 2016). In addition, the tumor microenvironment is composed of diverse stromal and immune populations, such as cancer‐associated fibroblasts, endothelial cells, tumor‐associated macrophages, exhausted T cells, and B‐cell aggregates, each contributing to tumor growth and immune evasion (Jerby‐Arnon et al. 2018; Davidson et al. 2020). These findings highlight both the phenotypic plasticity of melanoma cells and the complex cellular ecosystem that underlies disease progression and therapeutic response. Despite the identification of melanoma driving and modulating genes, tumor heterogeneity in the Xiphophorus model remains understudied. Prior work has described tumor morphology (van der Weyden et al. 2020) and bulk transcriptomic changes (Lu et al. 2018), but these approaches cannot resolve how diverse stromal, immune, and pigment cell populations interact to shape tumor ecosystems. Recent advances in single‐cell and single‐nucleus RNA sequencing (sc/snRNA‐seq) have transformed our understanding of melanoma biology by revealing lineage hierarchies, dynamic immune states, and signaling networks that regulate tumor growth and immune evasion (Tirosh, Izar, Prakadan, Wadsworth 2nd, et al. 2016; Stuart and Satija 2019; Wagner and Klein 2020). Applying these approaches to Xiphophorus is essential not only to uncover the organization of its tumor microenvironment but also to provide a comparative framework for understanding conserved versus model‐specific features of melanoma biology.

Accordingly, we performed snRNA‐seq on spontaneous melanomas from Xiphophorus hybrids and resolved the cellular and transcriptional heterogeneity encompassed in Xiphophorus melanoma. By comparing to human melanoma, we identify conserved cell populations between human and Xiphophorus melanoma cancer cells and similar cellular composition. By analyzing immune cell subtypes, we found that Xiphophorus melanoma represents immune‐evaded cancer.

Results

Global Heterogeneity in Xiphophorus Melanoma

Despite Xiphophorus melanoma model being established for over a decade, there is currently no understanding of cell type composition of the melanoma tumors. To characterize the cellular heterogeneity of Xiphophorus melanoma, we performed single‐nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA‐seq) on melanoma dissected from two interspecies Xiphophorus hybrids exhibiting nodular growth of melanoma in the dorsal fin and penetration into musculature. The dataset includes quality‐filtered 5839 sequenced nuclei.

A total of 26 clusters were identified (Figure 1A,B; Table S1; Figure S1), followed with transcriptional markers identification. The marker genes were used for reference‐based annotation with manual correction using zebrafish and Xiphophorus reference datasets. Cell types identified within the melanoma tumors include normal pigment cells and melanoma cells (clusters 0, 8, 11, 13; Figure S1A–C), basal and epithelial cells (clusters 1, 4, 5, 12, 14, 17; Figure S1D,E), cartilage cells (3 and 21; Figure S1G), osteoblasts (15 and 16; Figure S1L), endothelial cells (cluster 20; Figure S1N), neurons (cluster 9; Figure S1J), muscle cells (clusters 7 and 24; Figure S1I), and several populations of immune cells including myeloid lineages (macrophages: clusters 6 and 19; monocyte: cluster 18; and neutrophils: cluster 23; Figure S1H,M,O), lymphoid lineage (T cells: clusters 2 and 22; B cells: cluster 10; Figure S1F,K) and dendritic cells (cluster 25; Figure S1P).

Melanoma Interaction Within the Tumor Microenvironment

To characterize intercellular communication between melanoma cells and various cell types within the tumor microenvironment, we investigated known ligand and receptor expression patterns to infer cell interactions using CellChat (Jin et al. 2021) software embedded with zebrafish ligand‐receptor reference data. A total of 32 pairs of ligand‐receptor interactions were uncovered, including 6 pairs involving melanoma cells where the ligand or receptor gene(s) serve as melanoma cluster markers (Figure S2). These interactions are cancer‐stromal cell interactions related to Ptn‐Alk signaling and Csf1b‐Csf1ra signaling (Figure 3A,B); and adhesion‐related interactions, that is, Jam3b‐Jam3b (Figure S2O), Nrxn1b‐Nlgn2a/Nlgn3a, and Sema6a‐Plxna2 (Figure 3C–E).

Sub‐Cell Types Analyses

For all identified cell types, we primarily focused on subtyping cell populations belonging to melanocyte, melanoma, osteoblast, cartilage, basal, T cells, macrophages, and neutrophils because they exhibited high similarity to humans in cross‐species comparison. Other cell types and their respective markers are included in Figure S1.

Melanocytes and Melanoma Cells

The melanocytes comparably highly expressed ribosomal subunit genes (rpl and rps genes; Figure 1B; Tables S1 and S2; Figure S5) and elongation related genes eef1b2 and eif4ebp2 (Figure 1B; Table S1; Figure S5). However, we don't have evidence to show if these cells are tissue resident melanocytes that differentiated from neural crest‐pigment progenitor cells or descendants of dedifferentiated melanoma cells (Wellbrock et al. 2002).

Melanoma clusters (0 and 8) exhibited 25 differentially expressed genes (pct.1 or pct.2 > 0.1, |pct.1‐pct.2| > 0.2, adjusted p value < 0.05, average log2FC > 0.25; Table S2) that do not serve as melanocyte or xanthophore markers. Geno Ontology (GO) analyses showed Biological Processes (BP) related to migration, cell adhesion, PDGFR signaling are over‐enriched (p‐value < 0.05; Figure 4A; Figure S3). Melanoma cells are often distinguished by the proliferative to migratory switch. However, we did not find differentially expressed genes between the two clusters that represent a proliferative to migratory phenotype switch. There is no expression difference in cell cycle related genes, or mesenchymal phenotype related genes between the two melanoma populations. For example, the marker gene for mesenchymal like phenotype of melanoma, axl, is equally expressed in the two clusters (Figure S4).

Cartilage and Osteoblast Cells

Cartilage and osteoblasts exhibited human CAF marker gene expression and dominate the inter‐cellular interactions (Figure S2). We annotated two cartilage and two osteoblast populations and identified differentially expressed genes between the subclusters (cartilage subclusters: 208 differentially expressed genes; osteoblast subclusters: 264 differentially expressed genes; Tables S3 and S4). The differentially expressed genes include those involved with inter‐cellular interactions (Figure 4B,C): Osteoblast expressed Vegfd and endothelial cell expressed Flt4 (Figure S2C), endothelial cell expressed Pdgfbb and cartilage and osteoblast expressed Pdgfra (Figure S2B).

Immune Cells

Using known human and zebrafish genes hallmarking different immune cell types, we identified several T cell populations, including helper T cells (CD4+; cd4‐1, zbtb7b, tbx21), cytotoxic T cells (CD8+; runx3, cd8a), T memory cells (il7r, ccr7), and T regulator cells (foxp3). PD‐L1 (programmed cell death 1 ligand 1‐like) and the currently debatable fish ortholog of human PD1 (i.e., B‐ and T‐lymphocyte attenuator, btla) are both expressed in T cells, suggesting activated T cells are present within the melanoma. However, it is observed there were very few CD8+ cells expressing gzma, gzmb, ifng, and proferin1 gene (Figure 5A). Although expressions of exhausted T cell markers (ctla4, lag3, and tox and nr4a1) are detected, they are only present in a small fraction of the T cells (Figure 5A). Observed gene expressions suggest there is a lack of cytotoxic T cells within the tumor.

Subtyping of macrophage cells showed the Xiphophorus melanoma encompassed both pro‐inflammatory, anti‐tumor M1 macrophages (mpeg1.2, card9), and anti‐inflammatory and pro‐angiogenesis M2 macrophages [mrc1b (CD206) and csf3r; Figure 5B]. Similarly, subtyping neutrophils showed the melanoma included both N1 (mpx) and N2 neutrophils (mrc1b; Figure 5C).

Discussion

This study presents a Xiphophorus melanoma cell atlas and provides insights into the cellular heterogeneity, immune landscape, and intercellular communication networks of a naturally occurring melanoma.

The Xiphophorus melanoma in interspecies hybrid is driven by the xmrk oncogene inherited from one parental species, and loss of a co‐evolved regulator gene (Lu et al. 2020; Schartl and Lu 2024). Earlier studies relying on bulk RNAseq showed xmrk expression occurs in dorsal fin and skin harboring nevi‐like pigment cells, dorsal fin exhibiting melanotic hyperplasia, and nodular melanoma (Lu et al. 2018, 2019). The presented single‐nucleus level data showed that in the cancer tissue the oncogene is restrictively expressed in the pigment cell lineage (Figure S1A).

Inspecting the melanoma single cell profiles leads to several novel discoveries:

First, we identified a normal pigment cell population that is characterized by high tyrosinase (tyr) and low mitfa expression, and lack of anti‐apoptotic genes bcl2b and mcl1b as in melanoma cells. Despite these cells not forming subgroups, they exhibited different expression patterns of the xmrk gene and higher expression of the proliferation marker pcna, suggesting some of the melanocytes are in a state en route to melanoma (Figure S1A; Table S2). One of the most notable findings of these melanocytes is featured with elevated expression of genes involved in protein translation, including translation initiation and elongation factors, as well as a wide array of ribosomal subunits (Figure 1B; Table S1). This expression pattern suggests there is a global elevation of ribosome biogenesis and translational activity in melanocytes (Higdon et al. 2013).

Second, melanoma subclusters are different by gene expressions related to cell migration, morphology and adhesion, indicating the melanoma subcluster resembles diverged cellular proliferative or invasive phenotypes. However, mitfa (marker for proliferative phenotype) and axl (marker for invasive phenotypes) are not differentially expressed between the two subpopulations (Table S2; Figure S4), showing the melanoma subpopulations are not different in invasiveness. Investigating the inter‐cellular communication led to the findings that csf1ra, the receptor for mesenchyme secreted csf1b; adhesion molecule jam3b; synaptic cell adhesion related nlgn2a of Nrxn1b‐Nlgn2a interaction, and sema6a of juxtracrine Sema6A‐PlxnA2 signaling pathway are higher expressed in cluster 8 melanoma cells (Figure 2; Table S2), while cluster 0 melanoma cells did not show unique cellular interactions. Among the above interactions, neuron related NRXN1‐NLGN2 interaction is best known for their normal function in formation of synapse. NLGN2 expression is associated with breast cancer proliferation and metastasis (Zhang et al. 2021), while its function in melanoma has not been studied. Neuronal regulation is related to cancer progression. For example, sensory neuron‐specific overactivation can elevate immune surveillance by increasing tumor‐infiltrating anti‐tumor lymphocytes (Costa et al. 2021). Therefore, the neuron‐melanoma interaction in Xiphophorus melanoma may offer a unique opportunity to study neuronal regulation of melanoma. In human melanoma cells, expression of CSF1 ligand can be induced by CD8+ T cells, and form a tumor derived CSF1‐CSF1R autocrine signaling and drive proliferation and immune resistance (Tomassetti et al. 2024; Neubert et al. 2018). In comparison, the fish melanoma cells did not express the CSF1 ligand (i.e., Csf1a), and ‐ differently from human melanoma ‐ form a paracrine signaling with tumor stromal osteoblast and cartilage cells. These results indicate the fish melanoma may be naïve to T cells (See below for further discussion).

Third, presence of B cells and diverse myeloid populations, including M1‐ and M2‐like macrophages, N1‐ and N2‐like neutrophils, monocyte and dendritic cells within the fish melanoma is broadly consistent with features described in the human melanoma (Tirosh, Izar, Prakadan, Wadsworth, et al. 2016). In contrast, the T cell compartment exhibits distinct characteristics: (1) T cells show elevated expression of the CD8‐lineage transcription factor gene runx3, but showed limited or undetectable expression of canonical cytotoxic effector genes such as ifng1 (Interferon gamma 1), gzma (Granzyme A), gzmb (Granzyme B), or PRF1 (perforin 1; Figure 5A). This pattern may suggest reduced cytotoxic activity. (2) These T cells exhibit low to not detectable expression of commonly reported exhaustion‐associated markers, including tox, nr4a1, lag3, and ctla4 (Figure 5A). These suggest the T cells are not exhausted. (3) Notably, melanoma, despite being chronic, does not show detectable expression of PD‐L1‐like gene (Figure S5C). The PD‐L1‐like and fish homolog to PD1, i.e., B‐ and T‐lymphocyte attenuator (btla), are co‐expressed in T cells (Figure 5A). These observations suggest that noncanonical immune regulatory mechanisms may be present, although their functional relevance remains to be established.

In parallel, melanoma cells showed low expression of IFN gamma receptors (ifngr1 and ifngr2; Figure 5A; Figure S5A,B), which could be consistent with reduced responsiveness to interferon signaling. In contrast, normal melanocytes, particularly those expressing xmrk, show expression of b2m gene, which facilitates the MHC 1 cluster stabilization (Figure S5A,B). Expression of b2m was reported in melanocytes that line melanoma cancer pocket [i.e., cancer region of antigen presentation and T cell engagement and retention (CRATER)] that can retain CD8+ T cells (Ludin et al. 2025). These observations suggest there is potential capacity for antigen presentation. Together, these data are consistent with a model in which early oncogene activation may permit immune engagement, whereas sustained oncogene expression during melanoma progression is associated with features suggestive of reduced immune responsiveness.

The overall status of the Xiphophorus melanoma represents a status of both immune suppression or tolerance and cancer cell immune evasion. This scenario is seen in immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) resistance in patients. Current strategies in reversing ICI resistance including cancer cell centered [e.g., interferon signaling, antigen presenting mechanism, apoptosis resistance inhibitor; (Vanpouille‐Box et al. 2017; Zaretsky et al. 2016; Andtbacka et al. 2015; Ott et al. 2017; Schumacher and Schreiber 2015; Gubin et al. 2014; Souers et al. 2013)] and immune cell centered [e.g., myeloid reprogramming, inhibiting inhibitory cytokines, broadening checkpoint blockage, overcome metabolic suppression, enhance T cell priming, and reversing T cell exhaustion; (Ott et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2024; Hegde et al. 2016; Beatty et al. 2011; Tauriello et al. 2018; Mariathasan et al. 2018; Sharma and Allison 2015; Leone and Powell 2020; Allard et al. 2017; Rosenberg and Restifo 2015)] approaches. While Xiphophorus melanoma show similarities to the full spectrum of melanoma initiation and progression (Schartl and Lu 2024), pigment cell phenotypes ranging from healthy melanocyte, benign hyperplasia and melanoma are all genetically controlled by dosage of a regulator locus (Lu et al. 2020; Schartl and Lu 2024; Powell, García‐Olazábal, et al. 2020). The uncover of the immune suppression/tolerance and evasion in this study implies the cancer suppressive function by the regulator locus can be immune related, so that when the regulator locus dosage is high the immune surveillance is effective enough to clear early‐stage transformed tumor cells while reduced regulator locus dosage leads to compromised immune function. Therefore, leveraging on this regulatory mechanism can provide us novel strategies in overcoming resistance currently encountered in cancer immune therapies.

In summary, our data indicate the Xiphophorus melanoma model recapitulates major cell types of human melanoma. The novel findings that Xiphophorus melanoma is loaded with cold cancer cells and ineffective T cells position Xiphophorus as a powerful discovery and comparative model for developing novel strategies in curing immune evading cancers.

Conclusion

We deconvoluted the cellular landscape of Xiphophorus melanoma, identifying a spectrum of cell populations residing in the melanoma microenvironment. These findings deepen our understanding of melanoma and microenvironment interactions and underscore the utility of this evolutionary model for deciphering translational cancer biology.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Texas State University. All efforts were made to minimize stress and ensure animal welfare. No human participants or human‐derived data were used in this study.

Animal Model

Two melanoma samples were collected from two xmrk introgressed hybrid fish. The introgression line was established by repetitively crossing hybrid fish exhibiting melanoma to X. hellerii for over 100 generations. At dissection, all fish were euthanized by decapitation. Upon loss of gill movement, animals were decapitated. Tumors were snap‐frozen using ethanol dry ice bath. All BC1 fish were kept and samples taken in accordance with protocols approved by Texas State University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC 9048).

Nuclei Isolation

Nuclei were isolated from melanoma using the Singulator2 instrument per manufacturer's protocols (S2 Genomics; Livermore, CA). After mechanical disruption, cell filter straining steps, and washes, the cell nuclei were suspended in PBS/0.1% BSA or for thymus a nuclei isolation buffer (S2 Genomics; Livermore, CA), with all buffers containing 0.4 U/ul RNAase inhibitor, prior to microfluidic encapsulation on the 10X Chromium instrument (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA) to nanoliter‐scale.

Single‐Nuclei Library Preparation

Single‐nuclei libraries were generated using the GemCode Single‐Cell Instrument and Single Cell 3′ Library and Gel Bead Kit v3 and Chip Kit (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Before sequencing, every library was analyzed on a Bioanalyzer high sensitivity chip to ensure the expected cDNA fragment size distribution was achieved. The appropriate number of individually barcoded GEM libraries was pooled and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 instrument (Illumina) with 2 × 150 bp length using these sequencing parameters: 26 bp read 1–8 bp index 1 (i7) – 98 bp read 2 with 200 cycles.

The Cell Ranger software pipeline (version 7.0.1) was used to demultiplex cellular barcodes, map reads to the X. maculatus reference genome (GCA_002775205.2) and transcriptome using the STAR aligner, and down‐sample reads required to generate normalized aggregate data across samples, producing a matrix of gene counts versus cells. The individual tissue‐specific sequenced Gel Bead‐In Emulsion (GEM) libraries were each processed with the CellRanger pipeline (10X Genomics) to create a cellular barcode by genomic feature matrix. Bender was used to filter the data before further analyses.

Cell Clustering

The resulting matrices were imported into R for downstream analysis using the Seurat package (v4.4.0) (Butler et al. 2018). Expression data were normalized using the NormalizeData method, followed by identification of highly variable genes with the FindVariableFeatures function. The data were scaled and subjected to linear dimensional reduction through principal component analysis (PCA). Subsequent clustering was performed using the Louvain algorithm implemented by the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions, with cluster resolution optimized to balance granularity and biological interpretability (Principle Component used 1:30; resolution: 1.2). Visualization and assessment of clusters were performed using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Cluster markers, and inter‐cluster differentially expressed gene analyses were conducted using Seurat functions FindAllMarkers and FindMarkers. Differentially expressed genes were identified by adjusted p‐value < 0.05, pct.1 ≥ 0.01, pct.1‐pct.2 ≥ 0.1, average log2FC ≥ 0.25.

Cell Type Annotation

Xiphophorus cluster marker genes were mapped to human, mouse and zebrafish orthologs using Ensembl BioMart (Ensembl Release 109, October 2022 archive). We implemented phased auto annotation using reference datasets included in R package celldex, including: BlueprintEncodeData, DatabaseImmuneCellExpressionData, HumanPrimaryCellAtlasData, ImmGenData, MonacoImmuneData, MouseRNAseqData, and NovershternHematopoieticData; zebrafish developmental atlas (Farnsworth et al. 2020), bulk RNAseq data pf Xiphophorus cultured melanoma, and then followed with manual curation. For celldex included and Xiphophorus reference datasets, genes with high cross‐cell type expression variation (top 5000), with high expression (top 1000) were used to compare to Xiphophorus cluster marker genes using hypergeometric enrichment test (p < 0.05). For zebrafish reference cell type markers, they were directly compared to Xiphophorus cluster marker genes using hypergeometric enrichment test (p < 0.05). Each cluster will be assigned with the cell type name with the lowest p‐value per data to reference dataset comparison. During manual curation and correction phase, if an automatically annotated cell type from reference that covers a broader range of cell types, that is, HumanPrimaryCellAtlasData, MouseRNAseqData, or BlueprintEncodeDatais is not immune related, and agree with zebrafish cell type annotation, the Xiphophorus cluster is annotated following the zebrafish cell type. If the HumanPrimaryCellAtlasData, MouseRNAseqData, or BlueprintEncodeDatais annotations are immune cell related, and annotations from NovershternHematopoieticData, DatabaseImmuneCellExpressionData, ImmGenData, or MonacoImmuneData are consistent with zebrafish reference, the Xiphophorus cluster was annotated as zebrafish cell type. Manual correction (cluster 0, 1, 4, 5, 8, and 11) was conducted by querying cluster markers to zebrafish developmental atlas (Farnsworth et al. 2020) and thymus (Rubin et al. 2022) using their respect UCSC cell browsers.

Comparative Analyses Between Xiphophorus and Human Melanoma

Human cell type markers identified in earlier studies (Tirosh, Izar, Prakadan, Wadsworth, et al. 2016) hallmarking melanoma, cancer‐associated fibroblast, T cells, B cells, macrophages, and endothelial cells were projected to Xiphophorus cell clusters. Human orthologs of Xiphophorus cell cluster markers were identified using Ensembl BioMart (October 2022 archive). Next, human cell type markers were queried to fish markers and projected on fish UMAP, with color labeling their human cell identity.

Inter‐Cluster Communication Analysis

Intercellular communication analysis was conducted using the CellChat package (v1.6.0), employing a zebrafish ligand‐receptor interaction reference database. Raw data was retrieved from the zebrafish dataset object name “interactions,” with ligand and receptor gene names converted to Xiphophorus orthologs. Ligand and receptor genes were separately mapped to Xiphophorus cell type markers. A ligand‐receptor interaction is decided to be present if both ligand and receptor can be mapped to Xiphophorus cell type(s).

Gene Ontology

Differentially expressed genes between the two melanoma clusters were first mapped to human orthologs using the R package Biomart in the Ensembl genome database. Gene ontology (GO) term over‐enrichment analysis is conducted using the web tool g:Profiler (Reimand et al. 2007). Adjusted p‐value < 0.05 was used as the statistical threshold to determine GO term over‐enrichment.
