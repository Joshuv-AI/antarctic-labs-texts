Cross-cultural evidence that shame is a defense against reputational damage
Authors: Yiftach Argaman, Daniel Sznycer, Jan Crusius, Florian van Leeuwen, Yohsuke Ohtsubo, Hitomi Ishihara, Jin-Ying Zhuang, Qi-Jun Zhou, Thomas Castelain, Félix Neto, Joana Neto, Assaf Kron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe question of whether a psychological system is adaptive or maladaptive, and whether it is universal or culturally variable, depends on the researcher’s frame of reference, and different frames can yield radically different answers. Yet natural selection is the only known naturalistic process capable of producing complex functionality, making theories of adaptive function a privileged metatheory. We illustrate this with shame. Shame has been viewed as maladaptive because it motivates evasions and other behaviors condemned by victims and third parties, and as culturally constructed, varying along individualism–collectivism and honor–nonhonor dimensions. In contrast, data from six countries and two US regions suggest that shame is everywhere organized to protect individuals against reputational damage, supporting its status as an adaptation.

Is the emotion of shame maladaptive, a cultural construction, or an adaptation that is part of human nature? And how would one know? It is difficult to understand the structure of a human-made tool without considering the function it was designed to serve. Likewise, it is difficult to understand psychological phenomena without considering theories of adaptive function (1) and data from diverse cultures (2). Although various competing views have been advanced about the nature of shame, emerging theory and findings suggest that this emotion was shaped by natural selection because it minimized the likelihood and cost of reputational damage for ancestral humans (3–4), making it a feature of human nature.

The folk concepts of caring, love, and respect, and the intuition that we have a greater stake in the welfare of some individuals than others, appear to be underlain by an internal regulatory variable in the human mind–brain. By summarizing cue-based estimates of the fitness payoffs that the existence and choices of specific others afford to the actor—their genetic relatedness (5), their valuation of the actor’s welfare, their productivity, and their ability to intimidate the actor through aggression (see ref. 6)—this variable indicates the weight the actor attaches to the welfare of others when making decisions impacting both self and others (7). This variable, termed the Welfare Tradeoff Ratio of the actor toward individual i, regulates the actor’s choices by discounting (usually) the benefit derived by specific others when deciding whether to benefit them, as well as the cost incurred by specific others when deciding whether to benefit at their expense (8).

The evolution in humans of Welfare Tradeoff Ratios, and more generally of social valuation, led to evolutionarily recurrent challenges and opportunities relevant to how the actor values others and how others value the actor. It has been hypothesized that various social emotions are evolved solutions to such challenges and opportunities (9).

Evidence from comparative psychology (10, 11), developmental psychology (12, 13), biopsychology (14, 15), neuroscience (16, 17), social psychology (18, 19), experimental economics (20–22), and sociology (23) suggests that the human mind is equipped with machinery for strategically regulating the flow of self-relevant information to others. One of these mechanisms appears to defend the actor against reputational damage (3).

Revelations that an individual has become less productive, less knowledgeable, less generous, less formidable (through loss of strength or allies), or more exploitative, if they reach others’ minds, cause others to downregulate the weight they attach to the actor’s welfare (24). This leads them to incur fewer personal costs to help the actor and, reciprocally, to impose costs on the actor for lower personal benefit—to the detriment of the actor’s welfare. Being devalued by others is costly today and would likely have been even more costly ancestrally, when modern institutions and technologies that protect the individual against her neighbors’ devaluation—such as granaries, savings accounts, and impartial judiciaries—were unavailable. Data from contemporary and ancient humans suggest that disease, injury, and shortfalls compromised individuals’ ability to survive, especially if they lacked support from others (25, 26).

In sum, information-triggered devaluation would have been a major adaptive problem faced by our human ancestors. An adaptationist view of shame, therefore, is that it is the expression of a neurocognitive adaptation that was selected because it reliably solved the problem of information-triggered devaluation (4).

This view is not universal among emotion researchers, however. A prominent alternative theory—an attributional theory—holds that shame is better viewed as an “ugly” and “maladaptive” emotion (27–28) in the sense of being socially undesirable, because it leads to feelings of pain and inferiority (29, 30), evasions (31), and aggression (28).

According to the attributional theory, shame is an intrapersonal emotion—one that depends on how the actor makes causal attributions and evaluates herself—and is emphatically not an emotion that is aroused by concerns about being devalued by others (32). In particular, the attributional theory posits that shame is elicited when the actor attributes a negative outcome to stable, uncontrollable, or global aspects of the self (13, 33). For example, if you aspire to be knowledgeable (your identity goal), you would feel shame if you attributed your poor performance in a test to lack of intelligence (attribution: stable, uncontrollable, global). The result of such attributions is a view of the self as defective and inferior, followed by defensive maneuvers such as blaming others and aggression which, ironically, can create further feelings of inferiority (and further shame) (see ref. 34).

Feelings of shame are undeniably aversive, and some of the behaviors that it motivates are undesirable to victims and bystanders. Yet empirical findings on shame suggest that this emotion is improbably well-organized to serve the interests of the individual experiencing it when facing potential or actual devaluation.

In prospect, before committing an act that benefits the actor but costs others (e.g., theft), the shame system produces an internal signal in the format of feeling that conveys act-specific information about the likely devaluative threat. This anticipatory feeling of shame rises and falls in lockstep with the degree of devaluation expressed by audiences from one disgraceful act to the next (24). In this way, the anticipatory feeling of shame helps deter injurious choices by striking a balance between two competing risks: activating insufficiently (which leaves some of the devaluative threat unopposed) and activating excessively (which can lead to the insufficient pursuit of personal benefits). Shame’s tracking of audience devaluation is highly precise (35). It is also specific: Other negatively valenced emotions such as sadness and anxiety do not track audience devaluation (24). Notably, the anticipatory feeling of shame tracks the degree of devaluation expressed not only by local, contemporary audiences but also by humans in other places (including industrial and small-scale societies) and times (including ancient lawmakers) (24, 36–38). This suggests universality in both the structure and content of the shame system.

There is functionality not only in how the feeling of shame is modulated but also in the array of counterdevaluation measures that the shame system deploys. When the actor commits a disvalued act or possesses a disvalued characteristic and there is a risk of that information reaching others, people minimize disclosures (39), destroy incriminating information, and hide (18, 31). When people estimate the likelihood of information leakage to be high, they confess to at least secure some credit for honesty (40), possibly pretending the confession is motivated by genuine concern for others’ welfare.

When people learn that others have witnessed them committing an infraction, they often adopt a submissive stance which includes gaze avoidance, slumped posture, and reduced linguistic activity (41). This shame posture is phylogenetically ancient (42) and observed across cultures (43). It conveys acceptance of reduced standing and elicits less hostile responses from audiences compared to displays of other emotions or the absence of emotion displays (44). Displays of shame serve as learning opportunities for third parties: By observing people express shame, individuals gather information about what their fellow group members disapprove of and in turn become less inclined to engage in those acts (45).

Under shame, people apologize, deflect blame by making excuses or scapegoating others (46), appease (44), and aim to restore their lost social value when feasible (47), sometimes through cooperation (48). But sometimes people threaten or deploy aggression (28), which, in some contexts, can reduce or counter devaluation through the imposition of costs (49). Shame upregulates cortisol (50) and proinflammatory cytokines (14), perhaps to prepare the body for a worsened social landscape that includes injury and reduced social support. Underscoring that shame aims to minimize the devaluative threat, being devalued for false reasons elicits shame, even when one knows that one has not committed any infractions (51).

In sum, the theme that organizes cognition, motivation, physiology, and behavior in shame is evasion—evasion from others’ devaluation (52). This emotion appears designed to “structure interactions among different mechanisms so that they function particularly harmoniously when confronting commonly recurring (across generations) adaptive situations” (53, p. 73). According to the adaptationist hypothesis, shame functions to i) deter acts that would lead to excessive devaluation relative to the personal benefits gained from the act, ii) minimize the leakage of reputation-damaging information, and iii) reduce the cost of devaluation if it occurs (4).

To accomplish these functions effectively, the shame system appears to integrate at least two distinct inputs. The first is failure magnitude—the degree to which a failure would lead others to devalue the actor if they became aware of it. The second is failure visibility, such as the presence of observers—an index of the probability that reputation-damaging information will reach others’ minds. By hypothesis, these inputs are independent: an event may be highly visible but involve only mild failures or be less visible yet involve major failures. This suggests that the shame system responds to each variable independently of the other.

Here, we test two predictions derived from the hypothesis that shame is a defense against reputational damage. First, major failures will elicit more intense shame than mild failures. Second, failures will evoke more shame when they are visible to others than when they are private—because visibility increases the likelihood that reputation-damaging information will reach others.

There is some empirical support for these predictions. Experimental manipulations of failure elicit shame (47, 54), and failures on easy tasks—being more diagnostic of incompetence—elicit more shame than failures on difficult tasks (55). Experimental manipulations of visibility, too, boost shame (56). However, simultaneous manipulations of failure magnitude and failure visibility have, to our knowledge, not been conducted previously. Nor have these variables been examined across cultures. It has been claimed that emotions are conceptual constructions that are highly sensitive to culture (57). In particular, it has been argued that the cultural dimensions of individualism–collectivism (or Confucianism) (54, 58) and honor–nonhonor (59) shape the emotion of shame. In contrast with cultural constructionist views, the adaptationist hypothesis holds that the shame system is present in all groups and neurotypical individuals and that its fundamental structure—including how failure magnitude and failure visibility map onto shame intensity—is universal.

We tested these predictions in eight regions: six countries—the United States, the Netherlands, Portugal, Spain, Japan, and China—and two cultural regions within the United States: Southern states (honor; restricted to current residents of Alabama, Mississippi, Tennessee, Arkansas, or West Virginia) and Northern states (nonhonor; restricted to current residents of Rhode Island, Massachusetts, New Jersey, New York, or Connecticut). These eight regions vary considerably along the individualist–collectivist/Confucianism dimension, as well as in their honor–nonhonor orientation (54, 59–63). They also differ culturally in other respects (64). Despite these important cultural differences, an adaptationist account of shame predicts that the feeling of shame will everywhere be more intense when a failure is more visible and of greater magnitude—that is, when it elicits more devaluation from others.

Validation Study

As a first step, we conducted a study to validate our experimental manipulation of failure magnitude. Seventy participants from the United States indicated the subjective value they attach to ten abilities: five abilities presumptively valued mildly positively (e.g., drawing ability, color recognition) plus five abilities presumptively valued highly positively (e.g., intelligence, ability to communicate). All ten abilities were presented in random order. In a between-subjects design, participants were randomly assigned to rate either how much they personally valued each ability or how much they believed the average person in their community would value these abilities. The materials, data, and analysis from the validation study are archived in the OSF repository: https://osf.io/vs7gy.

Results

We conducted a 2 × 2 mixed-model ANOVA to examine the effects of ability value (high vs. mild value) and perspective (valued by self vs. by others) on participants’ ratings. There was a significant main effect of ability value, with presumptively high-value abilities rated as significantly more valuable than presumptively mild-value abilities, F(1, 68) = 197.93, P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.74, BF10 > 100. In contrast, there was no significant main effect of perspective, indicating that ratings did not differ between the participants’ own values and their beliefs about the values of the average person in their communities, F(1, 68) = 1.62, P = 0.207, ηp2 = 0.02, BF01 = 3.6. The interaction between perspective and ability value was also not significant, F(1, 68) = 0.12, P = 0.734, ηp2 = 0.00, BF01 = 4.0.

Discussion

The results confirmed that abilities presumed to be highly valued were rated by participants as more valuable than those presumed to be mildly valued, validating our manipulation of failure magnitude. All 10 abilities from this validation study (i.e., five mildly valued and five highly valued) were subsequently used in the main experiments, which we describe below.

Empirical Investigation

Participants read hypothetical scenarios in which they were described as experiencing failures. Based on the validation study reported above, failure magnitude was experimentally manipulated by depicting failures in abilities valued by others to a greater or lesser degree—such as intelligence or parenting versus color recognition or drawing—and thus likely to elicit correspondingly greater or lesser devaluation from others.

Failure visibility was manipulated by depicting the failures as either public—for example, low aptitude test scores announced aloud to the individual and 25 coworkers—or private—for example, low test scores seen only by the individual, with assurance that no one else would know.

The experiments featured a 2 (failure magnitude: mild failure vs. major failure) × 2 (failure visibility: private failure vs. public failure) between-subjects factorial design. Participants were randomly assigned to one of four conditions: i) mild failure in private; ii) major failure in private; iii) mild failure in public; iv) major failure in public. Participants in each condition read five scenarios of similar magnitude and visibility (e.g., five mild, private failures). For each scenario, participants rated how intensely they would feel shame if they were in that situation, using a scale from 1 (not at all) to 10 (extremely). We computed a mean shame intensity score for each participant by averaging their ratings across the five scenarios within their assigned condition, as well as condition-specific grand means.

The three experiments in the United States—the one with the general sample, as well as the ones conducted in Southern (honor) states and Northern (nonhonor) states—were preregistered. The other experiments included participant samples from the Netherlands, Portugal, Spain, Japan, and China.

Results

We conducted both frequentist and Bayesian analyses to assess the robustness of our findings, allowing us to evaluate both the statistical significance and the strength of evidence supporting the theoretical hypotheses. The null hypothesis (H0) predicted no differences in shame intensity between conditions, while the alternative hypothesis (H1) predicted that public and major failures would elicit greater shame than private and mild failures, respectively. Bayes factors (BF10) quantify evidence for H1 relative to H0, with values >100 indicating decisive evidence, 30 to 100 very strong evidence, 10 to 30 strong evidence, 3 to 10 moderate evidence, and 1 to 3 anecdotal evidence for H1, whereas values <1 indicate evidence for H0. Bayes factors used JASP’s default Cauchy priors, with r scale = 0.5 for fixed effects and r scale = 1.0 for random effects.

The general US sample was included for exploratory purposes and was preregistered accordingly to examine main effects of failure visibility and failure magnitude as predicted by the adaptationist theory and to test for an interaction between those variables. While the main effects emerged, the interaction did not (F(1, 137) = 0.11, p = 0.740, ηp2 = 0.00). Subsequent experiments in the other seven regions were confirmatory tests of the two main effects—note that two of these seven experiments (the US Southern states sample and the US Northern states sample) were preregistered and that neither preregistration included interaction effects. Nevertheless, we conducted exploratory analyses examining interactions in those seven samples. No significant interactions emerged. In sum, none of the eight experiments yielded statistically significant interaction effects, with Bayesian analyses providing anecdotal to moderate evidence for the null effect (BF01 = 1.3 to 3.8, equivalent to a BF10 of 0.25 to 0.78) (SI, Table S1).

It has been claimed that individuals from East Asian cultures engage in more intensive face-work—tactics to maintain one’s respectability in the eyes of others—than Western individuals do (65, 66). Some researchers have argued that people place greater weight on their beliefs about how others view them in the face cultures of East Asia, whereas in Anglo-American or Western dignity cultures, people are more autonomous and less guided by such considerations (65, 67). This perspective suggests that the public exposure of failures (relative to private failures) will exacerbate shame more strongly among East Asian participants than among Western participants. However, exploratory analyses did not support this prediction (SI Appendix, section B).

Discussion

Experimental data indicate that major failures—regarding abilities more highly valued by others, such as intelligence—elicit greater shame than mild failures, such as in color recognition. In addition, public failures—where the actor knows that others are aware of the failure—elicit greater shame than private failures, where the actor knows that others are not aware. The main effects of failure magnitude and failure visibility were observed in six culturally diverse countries—the United States, the Netherlands, Portugal, Spain, Japan, and China—and in two culturally distinct regions within the United States: Southern states (honor) and Northern states (nonhonor). Further, statistically significant simple effects of failure magnitude and failure visibility at each level of the other factor were observed in most cases.

If shame were truly a maladaptive emotion, one would expect it to be elicited in relation to the pattern of devaluation exhibited by others in counteradaptive fashion: mildly when the devaluative threat is high and intensely when the devaluative threat is low. Instead, the data show the opposite pattern: The intensity of shame closely tracked the severity of the devaluative threat—both failure magnitude and failure visibility. By doing so, the shame system minimizes harmful choices by balancing the risk of underactivation, which leaves some of the devaluative threat unopposed, against the risk of overactivation, which hinders the pursuit of personal benefits. The precise fit between the intensity of shame and the severity of the devaluative threat suggests that shame is an adaptation rather than maladaptive.

The fact that shame was elicited in the private failure conditions, where the participant is made aware of the failure but also told that no one else will know about it, raises the question of why this would be so under the hypothesis that shame is a defense against reputational damage. One answer is that the absence of cues of being observed is no guarantee that one is not being observed (today by electronic snoops, ancestrally by eavesdroppers or people in hiding). Consistent with this prudential hypothesis, theoretical, simulation, and experimental research suggests that the psychological architecture sets the conditional probability of being observed, given the absence of cues of being observed, to a low but nonzero value (21, 68). If the internal estimate of being observed in private is nonzero, the intensity of shame in private for a given failure will be correspondingly set to a nonzero value—as we observed. In addition, as noted above, shame appears to feature an anticipatory mode that is elicited not by being observed but by estimates of the costs one would incur if one were observed, and whose function is to forestall (or discontinue) the pursuit of a disgraceful act, or to prime counterdevaluation responses, in proportion to how much others would devalue one if they found out about it. The fact that mild private failures elicited more than zero shame (shame intensity ratings > 1) but less than major private failures is consistent with the adaptationist theory.

Shame is “maladaptive” and “ugly” if by those terms one means an aversive state or behavior that victims or third parties disapprove of, as some have suggested (27–28, 32). But those criteria are irrelevant to the question of whether something is an adaptation. Rather, the criterion for establishing an adaptation is whether there is evidence of special design for solving a problem or exploiting an opportunity ultimately relevant to reproduction (69). A useful analogy is physical pain. Pain, like shame, is aversive, but the threat is the injury or infection, not the pain itself. The pain system protects the individual from further tissue damage by directing attention to the likely source of harm, prompting withdrawal and avoidance, encouraging reduced movement to facilitate healing, and supporting learning to prevent future harm (70, 71). If the pain system were maladaptive, its elimination would yield reproductive benefits. In reality, however, eliminating the pain system imposes costs rather than benefits, as evidenced by individuals with congenital insensitivity to pain, who face elevated risks of injury and often die young (72). The data herein support the hypothesis that shame, though aversive like pain, incorporates special design features to minimize the threat posed by devaluation.

Another prominent theory holds—unlike the adaptationist theory and the intrapersonal–attributional theory—that there are no emotion programs in the brain. Rather, according to this theory of constructed emotion, emotional episodes emerge from the interaction of core affect (including valence and arousal) on the one hand, and culturally grounded concepts on the other (73). As Lisa Feldman Barrett put it, “your emotions are not built-in but made from more basic parts. They are not universal, but vary from culture to culture” (57, p. 24).

This way of thinking has influenced research on shame. For example, anthropologist Ruth Benedict (67) famously elaborated on the distinction between guilt cultures and shame cultures as relying on an internal sense of right and wrong (guilt) versus criticism by others (shame), exemplified by the United States and Japan, respectively. In line with this perspective, Michael Boiger and colleagues claimed that “shame is condoned in Japan and condemned in the United States” (74, p. 541, see also 75). A similar contrast has been proposed to exist between the English and Chinese languages: “in contrast to English, the organization of shame-related concepts in Chinese is extremely rich” (76, p. 383; see also 77), a pattern attributed to differences between American individualism and Chinese Confucianism. Comparable distinctions have been drawn between the honor–shame orientation of the Southern United States and the conscience–guilt orientation of the Northern United States (78), and between honor-based Mediterranean societies and more individualistic societies. For instance, based on research by anthropologist Julio Caro Baroja—“In conclusion we may say that in Spain the concepts of ‘honour’ and ‘shame’ have exerted great pressure on society at different periods” (79, p. 122)—and others, Patricia Rodriguez Mosquera and colleagues concluded that “the observed differences between Spain and the Netherlands with respect to pride, shame, and anger are consistent with predictions derived from the observation that honor-related values are relatively more important in Spain, whereas individualistic values are relatively more important in the Netherlands” (80, p. 843, emphasis added).

And yet, despite the fact that the six countries (the United States, the Netherlands, Portugal, Spain, Japan, and China) and the two US regions (Southern and Northern states) vary substantially along cultural dimensions previously argued to shape shame—including the individualist–collectivist/Confucianism dimension and the honor–nonhonor dimension (54, 59–63)—the effects of failure magnitude and failure visibility on shame activation were nonetheless strikingly similar everywhere. This supports the hypothesis that shame is part of human nature rather than culturally constructed. (And, as noted above, shame is not merely about negatively valenced affect, as other negatively valenced emotions are not tuned to the threat of audience devaluation; 10, Study 2).

We note, however, that some version of the hypothesis of shame as a culturally constructed emotion may be viable after all. Perhaps the regularities observed here were similarly culturally constructed everywhere in response to similar task demands. And perhaps other aspects of shame not studied here are constructed in culturally idiosyncratic fashion. At a minimum, however, the present data add to a growing body of research indicating that cross-cultural regularities in shame and other emotions are not uncommon. The time may be ripe to go from asking whether cultural similarities and differences exist in emotion (much research indicates that both do) to asking what types of similarities and differences one might find, at what levels of analysis (see ref. 81), and why.

The question of whether shame is functional can be answered in the affirmative or the negative depending on one’s frame of reference. If functionality is defined as not being aversive to the actor or not reducing other people’s payoffs, then shame is dysfunctional, as some have argued (27–28). But if functionality is defined as minimizing reputational damage to the actor, then shame is functional, as we have argued here. Whether shame varies across cultures, too, depends on the chosen frame of reference. If cultural variability is defined in terms of the manifestations or outputs of the shame system, then shame is culturally variable—for example, different populations vary in what they value and disvalue, and thus in the kinds of shortfalls that elicit shame (10, Study S2). However, if cultural variability is defined as differences in the underlying architecture—e.g., the widespread finding that shame intensity increases with the perceived devaluative threat—then the evidence for cultural variability is weaker, as shown here and elsewhere (24, 36–38).

Which frame should one adopt, then? There is an indefinitely large set of possible frames and a correspondingly large set of answers. Some answers will converge—because different theories may arrive at the same conclusion—while others will diverge. However, the choice of frame need not be arbitrary. Theories of adaptive function, and natural selection in particular, provide a principled guide. Natural selection is relevant because it is the only known naturalistic process capable of designing complex functionality. Thus, theories that focus on the probable adaptive functions of shame, while also considering by-products of adaptations and noise, constitute a promising frame for describing and explaining this emotion (1), including how and why it varies (82).

This research has limitations. The intensity of shame moves in lockstep with the devaluative threat—higher when the failure is major or public, and lower when the failure is mild or private. But whether more intense shame translates into more robust antidevaluation measures capable of countering greater devaluative threats, as the theory predicts, remains to be determined—although some evidence suggests that the answer is yes (31, 44). Whether the findings here generalize to other types of failures and to other populations and cultures, too, remains to be determined. Finally, one might expect that whereas higher failure magnitude boosts shame, and higher failure visibility does as well, their simultaneous presence at high levels could boost shame superadditively: Major failures revealed in public might be particularly shame-inducing. In our data, however, although both variables showed clear main effects, their interaction did not emerge. The adaptationist theory suggests that the way input variables combine to modulate the shame response will broadly mirror how those variables combine to determine the devaluative cost incurred by the actor. Yet whether failure magnitude and failure visibility actually combine superadditively to produce such costs is, to our knowledge, not presently known. Further research can profitably explore this question.

In conclusion, while much empirical evidence can be interpreted as indicating that shame is maladaptive or culturally constructed, an examination of its reproduction-promoting features suggests instead that shame is an adaptation that is part of human nature.

Method

Using a 2 × 2 between-subjects factorial design, we tested the effects of failure magnitude (mild vs. major) and failure visibility (private vs. public) on participants’ shame intensity across eight world regions—six countries and two cultural regions within the United States. In their assigned condition, participants read five hypothetical scenarios describing personal failures and rated the intensity of shame they would experience for each failure on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 10 (extremely). The materials, data, preregistrations, and analyses are archived in the OSF repository: https://osf.io/vs7gy.

Sample Sizes.

Experiment in the United States (general sample).

An a priori power analysis assuming 0.85 power and a small-to-medium effect size, conducted using G*Power, suggested a sample size of 400 participants (100 per cell). We stopped data collection at 160 participants (40 per cell) when the Bayes Factors reached our preregistered stopping criterion of BF = 5. This Bayesian stopping criterion was applied only to this initial exploratory US general sample and was not used in the subsequent confirmatory studies (i.e., the other seven regions). After excluding participants who spent fewer than 10 s on average reading each scenario, in accordance with our preregistered exclusion criteria, the final sample consisted of 141 participants (72 females, 68 males; mean age = 29.95, SD = 6.08). We preregistered the experimental design, hypotheses, sample size determination, data exclusion criteria, and planned analyses for the experiment in the United States (general sample): https://osf.io/vs7gy.

Experiment in the United States (Southern states and Northern states).

Participants were classified as being from a “Southern (honor) state” or “Northern (nonhonor) state” based on their current state of residence. Participants were eligible for inclusion as a “Southern (honor) state” participant if, at the time of the experiment, they were residing in Alabama, Mississippi, Tennessee, Arkansas, or West Virginia. Participants were eligible for inclusion as a “Northern (nonhonor) state” participant if they were residing in Rhode Island, Massachusetts, New Jersey, New York, or Connecticut. The final samples, after excluding participants who did not reside in the preregistered states, were as follows: Southern states: 83 participants (53 females, 30 males; mean age = 45.65, SD = 12.92); Northern states: 84 participants (39 females, 44 males, 1 of undisclosed sex; mean age = 44.23, SD = 13.96). These two US regional experiments were conducted as preregistered, confirmatory experiments, with hypotheses specified for the two predicted main effects (i.e., the effects of failure visibility and failure magnitude).

Experiments in the Netherlands, Portugal, Spain, Japan, and China.

Researchers were asked to collect data from “at least 20 participants per condition. That is, a total of (at least) 80 participants in the four conditions.” The final samples consisted of 272 participants from the Netherlands, 85 participants from Portugal, 94 participants from Spain, 130 participants from Japan, and 120 participants from China.

Demographic information for all samples is presented in SI Appendix, Tables S2 and S3.
