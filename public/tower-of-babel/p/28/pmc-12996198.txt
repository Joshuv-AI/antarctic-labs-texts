PHB2 ameliorates ferroptosis and aortic aneurysm/dissection through NEDD4L-dependent ubiquitination of NCOA4
Authors: Shengjun Xiong, Jie Lin, Ying An, Yuxin Du, Yiran E. Li, Zunhui Du, Rongjun Zou, Miyesaier Abudureyimu, Junbo Ge, Yingmei Zhang, Jun Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAortic aneurysm/dissection (AAD) is a catastrophic vascular emergency with limited therapeutic options and poorly understood molecular underpinnings. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death, emerged as a crucial driver of vascular degeneration although its pathological mechanisms in AAD remain largely undefined.MethodsWe integrated transcriptomic datasets to identify key dysregulated genes in AAD. PHB2 expression was examined by immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, and Western blotting in human tissues, murine models, and isolated vascular smooth muscle cells. Functional involvement of PHB2 was evaluated using VSMC-specific conditional knockout mice and AAV9-PHB2 overexpression in β-aminopropionitrile-evoked AAD model.ResultsTranscriptomic analysis revealed PHB2 as one of the most significantly downregulated genes in AAD, with selective suppression in VSMCs. Loss of PHB2 aggravated BAPN-induced aortic dilation, medial destruction, and elastic fiber fragmentation, whereas PHB2 overexpression preserved aortic wall integrity. RNAseq profiling implicated ferroptosis as the dominant pathway activated by PHB2 deficiency. Functionally, PHB2 overexpression mitigated Ang II-induced lipid ROS accumulation and Fe2+ overload, while PHB2 silencing aggravated ferroptosis. Mechanistically, rearranged subcellular localization of PHB2 promoted NCOA4 proteasomal degradation by interacting with NCOA4. PHB2 exhibited evident correlation with enhanced NEDD4L dimerization, enabling NEDD4L-mediated K48-linked ubiquitination of NCOA4 at Lys42, thus limiting ferritinophagy and suppressing ferroptosis. In vivo, PHB2 deficiency decreased NCOA4 ubiquitination and promoted ferroptosis, confirming the functional relevance of this pathway.ConclusionWe identify a previously unrecognized PHB2-NEDD4L-NCOA4 regulatory axis that restrains ferroptosis in VSMCs and protects against AAD progression. Targeting this pathway may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for the prevention and treatment of AAD.

Introduction

Aortic aneurysm/dissection (AAD) is a life-threatening cardiovascular emergency characterized by a tear in the aortic intima, allowing blood to enter the medial layer and form a false lumen [[1], [2], [3]]. Current treatment strategies rely predominantly on surgical repair and endovascular interventions, whereas effective pharmacological interventions remain extremely limited [4]. This therapeutic gap largely stems from an incomplete understanding of various molecular mechanisms underneath aortic wall instability. Pathologically, vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) dysfunction, extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, and inflammatory remodeling represent the fundamental drivers of AAD progression [5,6]. Under physiological conditions, VSMCs preserve vascular elasticity and tensile strength through balanced contractile and synthetic activities [7]. However, under pathological stimuli such as hypertension or angiotensin II (Ang II) exposure, VSMCs undergo phenotypic switching and various forms of programmed cell death [8,9]. Given that VSMC loss critically compromises the structural integrity of the aortic wall, identifying the dominant modes and upstream regulators of cell death in AAD has become an urgent research priority.

Given the growing recognition of regulated cell death in cardiovascular pathology, ferroptosis has recently emerged as a novel and compelling mechanism in vascular biology. Ferroptosis-a distinct form of programmed cell death-has attracted growing attention in cardiovascular research [[10], [11], [12]]. Unlike apoptosis or necrosis, ferroptosis is driven by iron-catalyzed lipid peroxidation, and accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [13]. Given the high-oxidative environment within the diseased aortic wall, ferroptosis is increasingly recognized as a potential contributor to vascular degeneration [14,15]. Among its key modulators, ferritinophagy-an autophagic process that selectively degrades ferritin to release ferrous ions-plays a central role in maintaining intracellular iron homeostasis [16,17]. Nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4) serves as the principal cargo receptor mediating this process by binding ferritin heavy chain (FTH1) and delivering it to lysosomes for degradation [18]. Excessive NCOA4-driven ferritinophagy enhances labile iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation, thereby promoting ferroptotic injury in VSMCs and compromising extracellular matrix integrity [[19], [20], [21]]. However, the upstream regulatory mechanisms controlling NCOA4 activity in VSMCs and their relevance to AAD pathogenesis remain elusive.

Given the emerging interplay between mitochondrial function and ferroptosis, recent studies have brought attention to prohibitin 2 (PHB2) as a potential upstream modulator of these processes. PHB2 is a highly conserved protein primarily localized to the inner mitochondrial membrane [22], where it governs mitochondrial quality control, dynamics, and mitophagy, to sustain cellular homeostasis [23]. Beyond its canonical mitochondrial roles, PHB2 also participates in transcriptional regulation, cell cycle control, apoptosis, and redox balance, acting as a versatile signaling mediator [[24], [25], [26]]. Increasing evidence suggests that PHB2 extends its regulatory function beyond mitochondria, influencing diverse cellular processes such as signal transduction and ferroptosis modulation [27,28]. Intriguingly, small molecules targeting PHB2 have been shown to suppress non-canonical ferroptosis [29], implying that PHB2 may serve as a key checkpoint linking mitochondrial integrity, iron metabolism, and ferroptotic signaling. Nevertheless, whether PHB2 interacts with NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy and how it influences ferroptotic stress in AAD remains largely unknown.

In this study, we sought to elucidate the role of PHB2 in the pathogenesis of AAD and to delineate its underlying molecular mechanisms with a particular focus on its potential regulation of NCOA4-driven ferritinophagy and ferroptotic injury in VSMCs. Expanding upon its established role as a mitochondrial inner membrane protein and mitophagy receptor, our findings position PHB2 as a pivotal hub bridging mitochondrial homeostasis and cytoplasmic ferroptotic signaling. Under conditions of oxidative stress, PHB2 appears to coordinate a tripartite nexus of redox signaling, lipid peroxidation susceptibility, and cell fate decisions, ultimately modulating cellular vulnerability to ferroptotic injury. By uncovering this PHB2-NCOA4 axis, we aim to provide novel mechanistic insights and identify potential therapeutic targets for preventing aortic wall degeneration.

Method

Experimental animal models

All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Institutes of Health, Publication) and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhongshan Hospital Fudan University (approval number: 202209028Z). In brief, adult male mice (3-10 weeks) were housed under controlled conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle and were allowed ad libitum access to food and sterilized water. PHB2flox/flox mice were generated using a CRISPR/Cas9-based genome editing approach by flanking exon 4 of the PHB2 gene with two LoxP sites. To achieve aortic VSMCs-specific conditional knockout mice, PHB2flox/flox mice were crossed with C57BL/6J TaglnCre mice (Cyagen Biosciences Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China). To achieve selective overexpression of PHB2 in VSMCs, we constructed a recombinant adeno-associated virus serotype 9 vector (rAAV9) encoding Flag-tagged PHB2 under the control of the SM22α promoter (rAAV9-SM22α-PHB2-Flag). The viral vector was packaged and purified by HAN Biosciences (Shanghai, China). One-week-old C57BL/6J mice were randomly assigned to receive a single intrathoracoabdominal injection of 2 × 1011 vector genomes of either rAAV9-SM22α-PHB2-Flag or an empty control vector (rAAV9-null). All experimental animals were male to minimize the potential confounding effects of sex hormones and because AAD occur more frequently in males. To establish the AAD model, C57BL/6J, PHB2flox/flox, and PHB2flox/flox; Tagln-Cre mice, as well as mice treated with either rAAV9-null or rAAV9-PHB2, were administered BAPN (1 g/kg per day; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) in drinking water for 4 weeks to induce AAD. The age at BAPN administration was carefully selected, as mice in the early phase of rapid postnatal growth exhibit increased susceptibility to elastic fiber degradation and subsequent AAD formation, thereby ensuring successful model establishment.

Ultrasound imaging of the thoracic aorta

Ultrasound imaging of the thoracic aorta was performed using a high-resolution Vevo 2100 (FUJIFILM VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada). Mice were anesthetized with 1-2% isoflurane in oxygen and placed in the supine position on a heated platform to maintain body temperature at 37 °C. Hair over the chest region was removed with depilatory cream before imaging. The parasternal long-axis view of the ascending and descending thoracic aorta was obtained in B-mode and M-mode for diameter measurements. End-diastolic and end-systolic aortic diameters were measured at the same anatomical location in at least three consecutive cardiac cycles, and the mean value was calculated for each mouse. Aortic dilation was defined as a diameter increase of ≥50% compared with control mice.

Histological examination and immunohistochemistry staining

Thoracic aortic tissues were harvested, rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C. After fixation, samples were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to assess overall aortic morphology and medial integrity. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated through graded alcohols, and stained with hematoxylin for 5 min followed by eosin for 2 min. After dehydration and mounting, images were captured using a Leica light microscope. The presence of elastic lamina disruption, medial dissection, and inflammatory infiltration was evaluated in a blinded manner.

Elastic Van Gieson (EVG) staining was performed to visualize elastic fibers and assess medial degeneration. Sections were stained sequentially with Weigert's resorcin-fuchsin solution for elastic fibers, differentiated in 95% ethanol, and counterstained with Van Gieson solution to visualize collagen.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was carried out to detect target protein expressions. After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval (10 mM citrate buffer, pH 6.0, 95 °C, 15 min), endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. Sections were then incubated with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min at room temperature to block nonspecific binding, followed by primary antibody incubation overnight at 4 °C. After washing, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature and visualized using a DAB chromogen kit (ZSGB-Bio, Beijing, China). Hematoxylin was used for counterstaining. Images were obtained with a Leica microscope, and the staining intensity and positive area were quantified using ImageJ software.

Constructions and infections of adenoviruses

To overexpress PHB2 in VSMCs, PHB2 exons were inserted into a pAdeno-MCMV-T2A vector to create the pAdeno-MCMV-Phb2-T2A vector (Obio Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). To knockdown target genes, siRNAs were deigned and synthesized (Genecefe Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuxi, Jiangsu, China) using sequences listed in Table S3. All constructs were verified using sequencing. Finally, these vectors were cloned into the AdMax System (MicroBix Biosystems, Mississauga, ON, Canada) following the manufacturer's instructions. The viral transfection was verified using Western blot.

Probes for mitochondrial detection

Mitochondrial morphology and membrane integrity were assessed using MitoTracker staining. Vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) were seeded onto glass-bottom confocal dishes and treated according to experimental conditions. After treatment, cells were incubated with MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Invitrogen, USA) diluted in prewarmed serum-free medium to a final concentration of 100 nM and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Following incubation, cells were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove excess dye and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL) for 5 min.

Measurement of ferrous iron and lipid peroxidation

Intracellular labile ferrous iron (Fe2+) levels were detected using the fluorescent probe FerroOrange (F374, Dojindo Laboratories) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) were seeded onto confocal glass dishes and subjected to the indicated treatments. After washing twice with prewarmed Hank's balanced salt solution (HBSS), cells were incubated with 1 μM FerroOrange working solution in HBSS at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Following incubation, cells were gently washed three times with HBSS to remove unbound dye and immediately imaged under a confocal fluorescence microscope (Leica TCS SP8) excitation wavelength of 543 nm.

Cells were sequentially incubated with the C11-BODIPY 581/591 fluorescent probe (1 μM, Invitrogen, C10445) and Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen, H1399) for 30 min. Lipid peroxidation levels were evaluated by calculating the fluorescence intensity ratio of GFP (excitation/emission: 484/510 nm) to RFP (581/610 nm), observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope (EVOS FL, AMG, USA) and quantified using ImageJ software. Additionally, fluorescence signals were detected in the 488 nm green channel using a multiparameter flow cytometer (A60-Universal, Apogee, UK), and data were further analyzed with FlowJo software (version 10.8.1).

Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay

To evaluate protein stability, VSMCs were treated with cycloheximide (CHX; MedChemExpress) at a final concentration of 100 μg/mL to inhibit de novo protein synthesis. Cells were harvested at the indicated time points (0, 1, 2, 4, and 8 h) after CHX administration and lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein were subjected to SDS PAGE followed by immunoblotting with the indicated primary antibodies. The relative protein levels were plotted and fitted to a one-phase exponential decay model to determine protein half-life.

MG132 treatment

To determine whether protein degradation was mediated by the ubiquitin proteasome pathway, cells were treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (10 μM; MedChemExpress) for 6 h before harvest. Whole-cell lysates were collected and analyzed by immunoblotting to assess the accumulation of the indicated proteins.

In vivo and in vitro ubiquitination assay

For in vivo ubiquitination assay, cells were co-transfected with PHB2-His, NCOA4-Flag, NEDD4L-HA, and Ub-Myc for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were incubated with MG132 (10 μM) for 4 h to block proteasome degradation. For in vitro ubiquitination assay, PHB2-His (1 μg), NCOA4-Flag (1 μg), NEDD4L-HA (1 μg), and Ub-Myc (2 μg) proteins which were expressed and purified in E. coli Rosetta (DE3) were incubated with E1 (50 nM) and E2 (500 nM) in a 30 μl ubiquitination assay buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.2, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM DTT, and 1 mM ATP) at 30 °C for 2 h. Subsequent immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting experiments were performed as described previously using anti-ubiquitin and anti-Myc antibodies.

Prediction of E3 ligase for NCOA4 ubiquitination

UbiBrowser (http://ubibrowser.ncpsb.org) is an integrated bioinformatics platform designed to predict and visualize the proteome-wide network of human E3 substrate interactions, drawing upon multiple types of heterogeneous biological evidence, including homologous E3 substrate interactions, enriched domain and GO term pairs, protein interaction network loops, and inferred E3 recognition motifs. Given its high sequence homology and evolutionary conservation, NCOA4 was used as a substrate to predict potential interacting E3 ligases via the UbiBrowser platform.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic protein Extraction

Nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins from mouse aortic tissues were isolated using a Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (P0028, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, freshly collected aortic tissues were rinsed in ice-cold PBS to remove residual blood, minced into small fragments, and homogenized on ice in Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagent A containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After incubation on ice for 10 min and addition of Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagent B, the homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 5 min at 4 °C to obtain the cytoplasmic fraction in the supernatant. The remaining pellet was resuspended in Nuclear Extraction Reagent supplemented with inhibitors, vortexed vigorously, and incubated on ice for 30 min with periodic mixing. The nuclear fraction was collected after centrifugation at 12,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0010, Beyotime).

Isolation of vascular smooth muscle cells, macrophages, and endothelial cells from mouse aorta

Thoracic aortas were carefully dissected from mice under sterile conditions and rinsed in ice-cold PBS to remove blood. After removal of adventitia and connective tissue, vessels were cut into small fragments and digested in an enzyme cocktail containing 1 mg/mL collagenase type I, 0.5 mg/mL elastase, and 1 mg/mL hyaluronidase (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 45-60 min with gentle agitation. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70-μm strainer and centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min. Cells were resuspended in PBS containing 0.5% BSA for immunomagnetic separation. CD31 MicroBeads (130-097-418, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) were used to isolate endothelial cells, followed by CD11b MicroBeads (130-049-601, Miltenyi Biotec) for macrophages, and the remaining CD31−CD11b− fraction was collected as vascular smooth muscle cells. The purity of each cell population was verified by immunofluorescence staining for α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA; 1:200, A5228, Sigma-Aldrich) for VSMCs, F4/80 (1:200, 70076, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) for macrophages, and CD31 (1:200, ab28364, Abcam, UK) for endothelial cells.

Culture of cell lines and plasmid transfection

VSMCs were maintained in F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin streptomycin. Plasmids encoding wild-type or mutant PHB2, NCOA4, and NEDD4L (Genecefe Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuxi, Jiangsu, China) were transfected into VSMCs using Lipofectamine 2000 (11668030; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) per the manufacturer's instruction. Cells were harvested 48 h following viral transfection for lysis. Human vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) were purchased from ScienCell Research Laboratories (Carlsbad, CA, USA; Cat# 6110). Homo sapiens, Female; RRID: CVCL_1G69. Cells were used between passages 3-8.

Proteomics

Quantitative proteomic analysis of mouse aortic tissues was conducted using TMTpro 16-plex labeling (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) combined with liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC-MS) (Shanghai Lu-Ming Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Database search was conducted using Proteome Discoverer 2.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and protein quantification was carried out. Proteins were retained for further analysis only if they met the criteria of a false discovery rate (FDR) < 1% and at least one unique peptide.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and mass spectrometry analysis

Mouse aortic tissues and cells were lysed in a buffer containing 0.5% NP-40, 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), and a protease inhibitor cocktail. Lysates were centrifuged and incubated overnight on a rotary shaker at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-Flag Magnetic Beads (M8823; Millipore, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), anti-HA Magnetic Beads (SAE0197; Millipore, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), anti-His Magnetic Beads (SAE0222; Millipore, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), rabbit anti-PHB2 (sc-133094; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), or rabbit anti-NCOA4 (ab183715; Abcam, Boston, MA, USA). Negative controls were prepared by incubating lysates with either mouse IgG (5415; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) or rabbit IgG (3900; Cell Signaling Technology). Protein G beads were then added to the immune complexes and incubated for 2h at 4 °C. The beads were washed 3-5 times and eluted for further analysis. To identify potential interactors of PHB2, Co-IP samples were separated using SDS-PAGE and were stained with Coomassie blue (P0017; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Stained bands were excised, and digested into peptides, prior to liquid chromatography mass spectrometry for protein identification (Shanghai Lu-Ming Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Immunofluorescence

AMCMs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with Triton X-100, and blocked with BSA. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies, mouse anti-PHB2 (66424-1-lg; Proteintech), rabbit anti-NCOA4 (ab183715; Abcam, Boston, MA, USA), and goat anti-SMC (ab140493; Abcam, Boston, MA, USA). After washing, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (4408; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) or Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibody (8889; Cell Signaling Technology) at 37 °C for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (P0131; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) before imaging.

Autophagy was evaluated using mt-keima (Hanheng Technology), a pH-sensitive, lyso-somal protease resistant fluorescent probe, with its excitation wavelength shifting from 430 to 590 nm in an acidic environment of lysosomes. Cells were transfected with mt-keima adenoviruses (multiplicity of infection = 20) for 48 h. Fluorescence images were captured using a laser confocal microscope (Leica) and analyzed using the ImageJ software.

Real-time quantitative PCR (real-time qPCR)

Total RNA from mouse cardiac tissues was isolated using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (RC112-01; Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. Reverse transcription was carried out with the PrimerScript RT Master Mix (RR036A; Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) to synthesize cDNA. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was then performed using the TB Green Premix Ex TaqII (RR820A; Takara Bio Inc.) on a CFX Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). GAPDH served as the internal reference, and relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The sequences of the gene-specific primers (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) are provided in Table S4.

Western blot analysis

After euthanasia, aortic tissues were rapidly exercised, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further analysis. Both aortic tissues and cultured cells were lysed in a RIPA buffer (P0013; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. Total protein lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes, followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C. After washing, membranes were incubated with the appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, and immunoreactive bands were detected using a ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Statistical analysis

Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Parametric tests were applied to normally distributed data, while nonparametric methods were used otherwise. Quantitative results are presented as mean ± SEM from at least three independent biological replicates. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software version 9.4.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test was used for normally distributed data, and the Mann-Whitney U test was applied otherwise. For multiple-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni correction was used for normally distributed data, whereas the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn's post hoc test was applied for non-normal data. In analyses involving two factors, two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction was used for normally distributed data, and the Scheirer-Ray-Hare test was performed for nonparametric data. For in vitro datasets with small sample sizes, each data point represented average observations from a large number of cells under identical conditions, and thus parametric analyses were considered appropriate. For in vivo or ex vivo samples with n < 6, nonparametric analyses were performed. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

Results

AAD pathogenesis correlates with loss of PHB2

We first analyzed the GSE147026 dataset from the GEO database, which provides differential gene expression profiles between human AAD and normal aortic tissues [30]. To complement this, we also examined the GSE153434 dataset which specifically focuses on ascending aorta tissue, the region most frequently afflicted in AAD, to yield site-specific transcriptional information [31]. As demonstrated in Fig. 1A, the intersection of the two datasets revealed a subset of co-expressed genes, which were analyzed in conjunction with our proteomics results. Subsequent enrichment analysis revealed that the major differences in cellular components were enriched in the mitochondria and the mitochondrial inner membrane (Fig. 1B), supporting PHB2 as a mitochondria-associated candidate deserving further investigation. To validate these bioinformatic findings, we performed immunohistochemical (Fig. 1C) and immunofluorescence (Fig. 1E) staining on aortic sections from patients with AAD and non-AAD controls. PHB2 expression was markedly reduced in the aortic media of AAD samples. Western blot analysis and quantification assessment revealed a significant decrease in both mRNA and protein levels of PHB2 in AAD tissues compared with controls (Fig. 1G–I). To further verify these observations in vivo, we established an AAD mouse model by administering 0.5% β-aminopropionitrile (BAPN) in drinking water to three-week-old mice (Fig. 1J) [32]. Reminiscent of human findings, PHB2 expression was also reduced in the aortic tissues of BAPN-treated mice (Fig. S1). As shown in Fig. S1A, PHB2-positive staining was prominently observed in the vehicle group, whereas BAPN challenge prompted a notable reduction in PHB2 staining. Quantitative analysis of IHC score confirmed that the PHB2 IHC score in the BAPN group was significantly lower than that of vehicle group (Fig. S1B). To further assess PHB2 expression, immunofluorescence staining was conducted. In the vehicle group, PHB2 exhibited potent green-fluorescent intensity, the response was markedly diminished in the BAPN group (Fig. S1C). Consistent with this finding, quantitative analysis of the mean fluorescence intensity revealed that PHB2 fluorescence was significantly decreased in BAPN group compared to vehicle group (Fig. S1D). Considering that the aortic wall mainly consists of endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells, and macrophages [33], we next examined PHB2 expression in each of these individual cell types. Western bot analysis revealed that PHB2 downregulation occurred specifically in VSMCs, (Fig. 1K–L), suggesting that VSMCs may be the primary cell population contributing to the loss of PHB2 during AAD development. Next, we established an in vitro model of AAD using Ang II. To discern the effect of Ang II on PHB2 expression in VSMCs, Western blot analysis was performed following time- and concentration-dependent manners. When VSMCs were treated with Ang II (1 μmol/L) for varying durations, PHB2 protein levels showed a gradual decrease over time (Fig. S4A). Ferroptosis is activated during AAD development

To examine the functional role of PHB2 in aortic dissection, we generated VSMC-specific PHB2 conditional knockout mice by crossbreeding PHB2flox/flox (PHB2fl/fl) mice with C57BL/6J TaglnCre mice. Following four weeks of 0.5% BAPN administration, PHB2CKO mice exhibited markedly more severe aortic dissection than their littermate controls (Fig. 2A), as evidenced by ultrasound imaging, which revealed significantly greater aortic dilation (Fig. 2B–C). During the 28-day BAPN treatment (30 mice per group), mortality due to aortic rupture was significantly higher in PHB2CKO mice (70%) compared with PHB2fl/fl mice (40%, Fig. S2A). Histological analysis further supported these findings: HE and EVG staining demonstrated extensive medial disruption and severe fiber fragmentation in PHB2-deficient aortas, with a markedly higher elastic fiber degradation score than controls (Fig. 2G–H). We next explored whether PHB2 overexpression mitigates BAPN-induced aortic injury. Mice were systemically administered AAV9-PHB2 or AAV9-NC prior to BAPN challenge. Gross morphological assessment revealed substantially less severe pathological changes in AAV9-PHB2-treated mice compared with controls (Fig. 2D). Consistently, the mortality rate following BAPN challenge was overtly less in AAV9-PHB2 mice (20%) than in AAV9-NC mice (36.3%; Fig. S2B). Echocardiographic evaluation further demonstrated a trend toward decreased maximal aortic diameter in AAV9-PHB2 mice relative to AAV9-NC mice (Fig. 2E–F), supporting a protective role of PHB2 in attenuating aortic injury. Histological analysis corroborated these findings, showing preserved medial architecture and continuous elastic fibers in AAV9-PHB2-treated aortas (Fig. 2I–J). To elucidate the potential mechanism underlying PHB2-mediated protection, we performed RNA sequencing on aortic vessels from BAPN-treated PHB2fl/flTaglncre and PHB2fl/fl mice. Among the top five significantly enriched pathways, ferroptosis emerged as the only specific cell death mechanism associated with AAD pathogenesis. PHB2 binds to NCOA4 in the cytoplasm of VSMCs

To investigate whether PHB2 regulates ferroptosis-related proteins, we performed mass spectrometry analysis of PHB2 interacting partners (Table S1). The results identified NCOA4 as a potential PHB2-binding protein. To validate this interaction, we performed co-IP assays. As shown in Fig. 3A, endogenous PHB2 and NCOA4 co-precipitated in VSMCs, confirming their physical association under basal conditions. This interaction was further substantiated by exogenous co-immunoprecipitation experiment (Fig. 3B). Subcellular fractionation revealed that PHB2 and NCOA4 co-localized within the cytoplasmic fraction of VSMCs (Fig. 3D–F), while immunofluorescence staining further verified their cytoplasmic co-localization (Fig. 3C), thereby establishing the spatial basis for their interaction. We then assessed the regulation of NCOA4 by Ang II in VSMCs. Under time-dependent Ang II exposure, NCOA4 protein levels were progressively elevated (Fig. S4C). For concentration-dependent Ang II challenge, NCOA4 expression was upregulated in concordance with the rising Ang II concentrations (Fig. S4D). Under Ang II challenge, subcellular localization of PHB2 protein was altered in VSMCs (Fig. 3G). PHB2 promotes ubiquitination degradation of NCOA4

To determine how PHB2 influences NCOA4 expression, we first examined NCOA4 protein levels by Western blot analysis. As shown in Fig. 4A–B, PHB2 overexpression markedly reduced NCOA4 protein abundance, whereas NCOA4 mRNA levels remained unchanged (Fig. 4C), suggesting that PHB2 regulates NCOA4 through a post-translational mechanism rather than transcriptional control. To further clarify the degradation pathway involved, we assessed the effects of proteasomal and lysosomal inhibitors. Treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 significantly restored NCOA4 protein levels in both control and PHB2-overexpressing cells (Fig. 4D), indicating that PHB2 promotes proteasomal degradation of NCOA4. In contrast, treatment with lysosomal inhibitors Pepstatin A and E64d did not alter NCOA4 level in either group, suggesting that PHB2-mediated NCOA4 degradation is independent of lysosomal autophagy. To further confirm the effect of PHB2 on NCOA4 stability, we performed cycloheximide chase assays. The results demonstrated that PHB2 overexpression accelerated NCOA4 turnover under both basal and Ang II-stimulated conditions (Fig. 4E–F), indicating that PHB2 functions as a key regulator of NCOA4 protein stability. To investigate whether ubiquitination is responsible for this degradation, we examined NCOA4 ubiquitination status. Silencing PHB2 markedly decreased NCOA4 ubiquitination (Fig. 4G), whereas PHB2 overexpression enhanced NCOA4 ubiquitination (Fig. 4H). PHB2 enhances NEDD4L dimerization

Given that PHB2 does not possess intrinsic ubiquitin-conjugating or E3 ligase activity, we explored the mechanism by which PHB2 promotes NCOA4 ubiquitination and degradation in VSMCs. In particular, we examined whether PHB2 exerts its effect through an E3 ligase-mediated process. Using an E3 ligase prediction database, we first identified candidate ubiquitin ligases for NCOA4 (Fig. 5A). Comparison of these predicted candidates with our PHB2 mass spectrometry dataset (Table S1) revealed NEDD4L as the only common E3 ligase. We subsequently validated this finding by co-IP, confirming the interaction between NEDD4L and NCOA4 (Fig. 5B–C), as well as between NEDD4L and PHB2 (Fig. 5D–E). Both endogenous and exogenous co-IP assays consistently demonstrated that NEDD4L physically associates with PHB2 and NCOA4. Furthermore, silencing NEDD4L significantly reduced PHB2-induced NCOA4 ubiquitination, providing compelling evidence that NEDD acts as the E3 ligase in the PHB2-mediated ubiquitination of NCOA4 (Fig. 5F). Subcellular fractionation and immunofluorescence analyses revealed that PHB2 and NEDD4L co-localized within the cytoplasm of VSMCs (Fig. 5G–H), consistent with the previously observed cytoplasmic localization of the PHB2 NCOA4 complex. Notably, E3 ligase activity of NEDD4 family members has been reported to depend on homodimerization. Immunofluorescence imaging demonstrated that PHB2 overexpression enhanced NEDD4L dimeric signal intensity (Fig. 5I). Consistently, Co-IP analysis verified the interaction between exogenously expressed HA-NEDD4L and Flag-NEDD4L, confirming NEDD4L homodimerization (Fig. 5J). Western blotting further showed that PHB2 overexpression increased NEDD4L dimer abundance relative to control (Fig. 5K). Previous studies have demonstrated that dimerization is intrinsically linked to the catalytic activation of NEDD4 family E3 ligases, as it facilitates efficient ubiquitin transfer and substrate recognition [34]. Consistent with this established mechanism, the enhanced NEDD4L dimerization observed in the presence of PHB2 correlates with elevated E3 ligase activity. Notably, total NEDD4L protein levels remained unchanged under AAD conditions (Fig. S6A), indicating that PHB2 regulates NEDD4L primarily through modulation of its enzymatic activity rather than its expression. NEDD4L catalyzes K48-linked polyubiquitin chains of NCOA4 at lysine 42

Having established that PHB2 regulates NEDD4L activity, we next investigated the specific domain of NEDD4L responsible for its interaction with NCOA4. As shown in Fig. 7A, deletion of the HECT domain abolished NEDD4L-mediated ubiquitination of NCOA4, indicating that the HECT catalytic domain is indispensable for this process. To further characterize the type of ubiquitin linkage involved, we constructed a panel of ubiquitin mutants representing distinct linkage types. The results demonstrated that NEDD4L primarily catalyzes Lys48 (K48)-linked polyubiquitination of NCOA4 (Fig. 7B). Consistently, PHB2 overexpression markedly enhanced K48-linked polyubiquitination of NCOA4 (Fig. 7C), in agreement with the canonical role of K48-linked ubiquitin chains in targeting substrates for proteasomal degradation. In vitro ubiquitination assays confirmed that under Ang II stimulation, PHB2 promotes NCOA4 degradation through NEDD4L-dependent K48-linked polyubiquitination (Fig. 7D). In vivo, NCOA4 K48-linked polyubiquitination was significantly reduced in PHB2-deficient BAPN-treated mice, further validating this mechanism (Fig. 7E). Moreover, graded overexpression of NEDD4L induced a dose-dependent increase in K48-linked polyubiquitination of NCOA4 (Fig. 7F). Discussion

In this study, we identified PHB2 as a critical regulator of AAD development through integrated transcriptomic analysis and functional validation. Our findings demonstrated for the first time that PHB2 protects VSMCs by mitigating ferroptosis during AAD progression. Conversely, PHB2 deficiency exacerbates ferroptotic injury both in vivo and in vitro, resulting in severe VSMC injury. Mechanistically, PHB2 directly interacted with the ferritinophagy receptor NCOA4 to promote its ubiquitination and subsequent degradation. Specifically, PHB2 facilitates the dimerization and activation of the E3 ligase NEDD4L, which in turn mediates the K48-linked polyubiquitination of NCOA4 at Lys42 (K42) site. This accelerated proteasomal degradation limits the release of free iron, thereby alleviating ferroptosis. Collectively, these findings suggest that targeting the PHB2 axis may offer a novel therapeutic strategy to prevent AAD onset and progression.

Our data further established a critical role for PHB2 in maintaining structural integrity of aortic wall. The cardioprotective role of PHB2 has been documented in various pathological settings such as heart failure, DOX cardiomyopathy, and vascular injury [[35], [36], [37]]. In our BAPN-induced AAD mouse model, VSMC-specific deletion of PHB2 markedly aggravated aortic dilatation and dissection, as shown by echocardiographic and histopathological analyses. HE and EVG staining denoted extensive medial disruption and fragmentation of elastic fibers in PHB2-deficient aortas. In contrast, AAV9-mediated PHB2 overexpression significantly attenuated aortic dilation and preserved wall architecture. Under BAPN challenge, thoracic aortas in the AAV9-PHB2 group displayed notably reduced damage compared with the AAV9-NC group, underscoring the therapeutic potential of PHB2 in AAD.

Traditionally characterized as a structural scaffold of the mitochondrial inner membrane, PHB2 is increasingly recognized as a dynamic rheostat of cellular redox homeostasis, representing an "underappreciated redox regulatory node." While its role as a protein chaperone for mitochondria and mitophagy receptor is well-documented, our findings reveal a functional versatility in sensing and responding to oxidative fluctuations. PHB2 serves as a pivotal bridge between mitochondrial oxidative stress and systemic cellular adaptations; it does not merely maintain architecture but actively orchestrates the antioxidant response by modulating mitochondrial protein turnover and metabolic flux. By integrating stress signals with the selective degradation of damaged organelles, PHB2 functions as a critical gatekeeper that prevents the escalation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) into irreversible cellular damage [38,39]. Given that mitochondrial dysfunction drives various cardiovascular pathologies, including aortic aneurysms and atherosclerosis [40,41], PHB2-mediated mitophagy is essential for vascular homeostasis. Indeed, impaired mitochondrial autophagy is linked to premature VSMC senescence and apoptosis, fueling vascular remodeling and disease progression [[42], [43], [44]]. Under pathological conditions, we observed a marked alteration in the subcellular distribution of PHB2, with enhanced PHB2 presence and signaling within the cytoplasmic compartment. Importantly, rather than implying an active, directional translocation, this observation reflects a context-dependent redistribution of PHB2 coinciding with oxidative stress and ferroptotic signaling. Furthermore, as PHB2 lacks intrinsic E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, it appears to function as a molecular scaffold that promotes NEDD4L dimerization, thereby indirectly enhancing its ligase activity and downstream substrate ubiquitination. By expanding the functional repertoire of PHB2 beyond its mitochondrial residence [[45], [46], [47], [48]], our work identifies a previously unrecognized mechanism connecting mitochondrial stress to iron-dependent cell death. This dual role preserving mitochondrial integrity while suppressing cytoplasmic ferroptosis positions PHB2 as a pivotal guardian of vascular stability.

At the mechanistic level, we delineated a tiered PHB2-NEDD4L-NCOA4 signaling axis that integrates ubiquitination dynamics with regulation of ferroptosis. As a typical HECT-type E3 ubiquitin ligase, the catalytic activity of NEDD4L is governed by its C-terminal HECT domain [49]. Through Co-IP and Western blot analysis, we confirmed that PHB2 specifically binds to the C2 domain of NEDD4L via its PHB domain, thereby facilitating NEDD4L dimerization. Our functional experiments demonstrate that PHB2 overexpression significantly increases NEDD4L dimer levels and enhances its capacity to ubiquitinate downstream substrates [50,51]. The catalytic efficiency of NEDD4L is profoundly regulated by its oligomeric state, which serves as a critical switch between dormant and active forms. Under basal conditions, NEDD4L primarily exists in an autoinhibited monomeric state, where the C2 domain interacts intramolecularly with the HECT domain, masking the active site and hindering E2 recruitment [52]. While early models suggested that Ca2+ binding primarily drives membrane translocation, recent structural and biochemical evidence reveals that self-association is a fundamental requirement for full E3 ligase function of NEDD4L. More importantly, while monomeric NEDD4L can perform monoubiquitination, the assembly of polyubiquitin chains - essential for substrate degradation such as ENaC -strictly requires NEDD4L oligomerization [53]. This oligomeric assembly allows for the efficient transfer of ubiquitin in trans, significantly enhancing the ligase's processivity. Thus, dimerization or higher-order oligomerization is not merely an auxiliary feature but a necessary condition for NEDD4L to achieve the catalytic threshold required for physiological substrate regulation. Importantly, our data indicate that total NEDD4L protein abundance remains largely unchanged in AAD, suggesting that its pathological contribution is governed by post-translational activation rather than expression-level alterations. In this context, PHB2 functions as a molecular scaffold that stabilizes NEDD4L's active conformation, indirectly amplifying its ubiquitination efficiency towards NCOA4.

Further analysis identified NCOA4 as the principal downstream effector of this pathway. As the primary cargo receptor for ferritinophagy, NCOA4 mediates ferritin degradation and the subsequent release of ferrous iron (Fe2+), which amplifies lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic injury [54,55]. We found that PHB2-induced activation of NEDD4L enhanced K48-linked polyubiquitination of NCOA4, preventing its pathological accumulation in VSMCs. Structurally, the K42 residue resides within the N-terminal region of NCOA4. This site does not overlap with the established ferritin-binding domain but instead corresponds to a predicted intrinsically disordered region. Such regions frequently serve as flexible regulatory platforms for E3 ligase recognition. This structural context aligns with our observation that K42-specific ubiquitination primarily dictates NCOA4 protein stability rather than directly disrupting ferritin binding, thereby fine-tuning ferritinophagy activity [56]. Under Ang II stimulation, PHB2 deficiency impairs NCOA4 degradation, leading to runaway ferritin breakdown, elevated intracellular ferrous iron levels, and ferroptotic activation. Collectively, these data define a hierarchical molecular cascade: PHB2 facilitates NEDD4L dimerization and activation; active NEDD4L ubiquitinates NCOA4 at Lys42 via K48 linkage; and the resulting NCOA4 degradation restores ferritin-iron homeostasis and preserving aortic wall integrity. This mechanism reveals a novel protective function of PHB2 in vascular homeostasis and identifies a targetable signaling axis for ferroptosis-associated vascular diseases.

From a clinical perspective, our results suggest that PHB2 holds promises as both a diagnostic biomarker and a therapeutic target for AAD. The consistent reduction of PHB2 expression in human AAD tissues highlights its potential utility in disease stratification. Nevertheless, several limitations warrant acknowledgement. First, while our mechanistic insights were primarily derived from murine models, the conservation of this axis in human VSMCs requires more extensive validation. Second, although we identified NCOA4 Lys42 as a critical ubiquitination site for regulating stability in vitro, its specific in vivo contribution to the development of aortic aneurysms and dissections remains to be fully clarified. Furthermore, the molecular mechanisms governing the redistribution of PHB2 from the mitochondria to the cytoplasm are not yet fully understood. This alteration in subcellular localization still awaits confirmation by high-resolution ultrastructural evidence (such as transmission electron microscopy), which will further strengthen this mechanistic interpretation. Moreover, while PHB2 deficiency clearly drives multiple ferroptotic phenotypes, our findings do not exclude the potential involvement of other regulated cell death pathways. Further studies are needed to delineate the relative contributions of distinct cell death programs in this pathological context. Finally, the translational potential of modulating the PHB2-NEDD4L-NCOA4 axis must be confirmed through rigorous preclinical and patient-derived organoid studies.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates for the first time that PHB2 protects aortic smooth muscle cells and delays aortic dissection progression by suppressing the ferritinophagy-ferroptosis axis. We show that PHB2 facilitates the NEDD4L-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of NCOA4, thereby maintaining iron homeostasis. The discovery of this PHB2-NEDD4L-NCOA4 signaling axis significantly enriches our mechanistic understanding of AAD pathogenesis and establishes a theoretical framework for ferroptosis-targeted vascular therapy. Future research should focus on elucidating the upstream regulators of PHB2 redistribution, validating its biomarker potential, and developing targeted strategies to exploit this pathway for the prevention and treatment of AAD [57,58].
