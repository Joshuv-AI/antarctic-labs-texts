Linking eye movements, pupil responses, and brain networks in early cognitive decline
Authors: Julius Opwonya, Boncho Ku, Frank van der Heide, Kun Ho Lee, Joong Il Kim, Jaeuk U Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly detection of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) requires biomarkers sensitive to pre-neurodegenerative dysfunction. Task-evoked ocular responses index arousal‑based gain and the stability of executive timing, but their relationship to brain structure, especially within AD‑signature cortex; regions that thin early in AD, remains poorly characterized. We tested whether ocular metrics map onto cortical thickness and subcortical volumes, and whether brain–ocular coupling differs between cognitively normal (CN) adults and those with mild cognitive impairment (MCI).MethodsParticipants with MCI (n = 212) and CN controls (n = 516) completed an interleaved prosaccade–antisaccade task; binocular pupil diameter and eye movements were time‑locked to cue and target onsets to derive pupil amplitude/variability and saccade metrics. Saccadic latency variability indexes loss of timing stability, revealing a thickness–stability inversion—positive in controls, absent or negative in MCI—that localizes to supramarginal gyrus within attention and executive‑control networks. Background

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) represents a transitional stage between normal aging and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) dementia, marked by cognitive decline that exceeds normative expectations but does not yet impair daily functioning [1]. Individuals with biomarker-confirmed MCI are at elevated risk for progression to AD dementia, yet early detection remains challenging [2]. Conventional neuropsychological assessments, while clinically useful, lack the temporal resolution and neurobiological specificity needed to capture subtle brain changes preceding overt cognitive decline [3].

Eye-tracking provides a powerful, non-invasive window into cognitive and neural processes. Saccadic eye movements offer true millisecond-level markers of sensorimotor and executive control, while task-evoked pupillary responses (TEPRs) reflect slower physiological dynamics that unfold over hundreds of milliseconds [4]. Although TEPRs lack the rapid temporal resolution of saccades, modern pupillometry systems sample pupil diameter at millisecond precision, enabling fine-grained modeling of neuromodulatory activity. These responses are shaped by distributed circuits—including the locus coeruleus–noradrenergic system, superior colliculus, and pretectal olivary nucleus (PON)—that integrate cortical and subcortical inputs to regulate attention, arousal, and cognitive effort [5, 6]. Importantly, disruptions within these circuits are increasingly recognized as early features of AD pathology, positioning ET as a promising tool for detecting subtle changes in executive and autonomic function [7, 8].

Despite this potential, few studies have examined ocular dynamics during paradigms that robustly engage executive control. The interleaved prosaccade–antisaccade (PS–AS) task is particularly informative, requiring rapid switching between reflexive and volitional responses and robustly engage frontoparietal-subcortical control circuits [8, 9].

This study addresses this gap by investigating whether individuals with MCI exhibit altered ocular dynamics during a PS–AS task and whether these alterations relate to structural integrity in these cortical and subcortical regions. Using generalized linear models and mixed effect models, we modeled the associations between cortical thickness and subcortical volumes and TEPRs/saccades, focusing on cortical regions implicated in early AD-related atrophy and subcortical regions [10, 11].

By linking high-temporal-resolution ocular dynamics to cortical thickness and subcortical volume changes, this study advances a novel biomarker framework for detecting early neuromodulatory and executive dysfunction in MCI. Our findings position ET as a scalable, non-invasive tool for probing distributed network dysfunction in prodromal AD.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study analyzed a subset of 832 older adults from the Gwangju Alzheimer’s and Related Dementias (GARD) cohort, a large-scale longitudinal platform in South Korea. Drawn from a prospectively followed, community-based subcohort, participants were free of dementia and capable of completing neurophysiological and oculomotor testing. The GARD cohort includes over 19,000 individuals aged 55–90 and integrates multimodal imaging, fluid biomarkers, cognitive phenotyping, and multi-omics profiling. This sampling strategy enriched for cognitively normal and MCI participants, enabling detailed analyses of early-stage Alzheimer’s disease phenotypes in an East Asian population.

All participants underwent a structured clinical evaluation, including the Seoul Neuropsychological Screening Battery–Second Edition (SNSB-II), a semi-structured neurological exam, and dementia staging via the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) scale [12] Cognitively normal individuals had a global CDR of 0, no subjective or informant-reported cognitive complaints, and normative performance across all SNSB-II domains. MCI was diagnosed per NIA-AA criteria, defined as a global CDR of 0.5 and a z-score < − 1.5 in at least one cognitive domain, based on Korean norms [13]. Global cognition was assessed using the Korean Mini-Mental State Examination (K-MMSE), embedded within the SNSB-II.

Participants met core GARD eligibility criteria, including a Hachinski Ischemic Score < 4, normal sensory acuity, MRI compatibility, and willingness to provide biospecimens and undergo longitudinal follow-up. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, recent psychiatric illness (e.g., major depression, bipolar disorder), substance use disorder within two years, high-risk alcohol use (> 5 units per occasion on > 4 occasions/week), recent use of cognition-impairing medications, and MRI-incompatible implants. Medication history was extracted from site-provided medical records summarizing reported drug use. However, specific drug composition, actual ingestion, and the use of medications known to influence pupil size—such as anticholinergics, adrenergic agonists/antagonists, antidepressants, sedatives, and opiates—were not independently verified. Participants with central nervous system disorders, substance use disorders, or long-term psychiatric medication use were excluded per protocol.

To minimize cerebrovascular confounds, all participants underwent high-resolution 3T structural MRI, processed using FreeSurfer v5.3 [14]. Additional MRI details are reported elsewhere [15]. Ocular screening ensured binocular acuity and excluded individuals with conditions likely to impair ET (e.g., glaucoma, macular degeneration). Eye-tracking calibration used a nine-point spatial grid; participants were excluded if mean gaze deviation exceeded 1° or if < 80% of calibration points were registered after two attempts.

Of 832 individuals screened, 25 were excluded for probable AD dementia and 79 for ophthalmologic or calibration issues. The final sample included 728 participants: 516 CN (mean age 77; 54% female) and 212 MCI (mean age 78; 49% female). All participants or their legal representatives provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Chonnam National University Hospital IRB (IRB No. CNUH-2019-279) and conducted under GARD data governance and quality assurance protocols (Table 1).

MRI acquisition

High-resolution structural MRI was acquired on 3-Tesla scanners using standardized GARD imaging protocols. T1-weighted images were collected using a sagittal 3D MPRAGE sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR), 2300 ms; echo time (TE), 2.98 ms; inversion time (TI), 900 ms; flip angle, 9°; voxel size, 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm³; matrix size, 256 × 256; and field of view (FOV), 256 mm. Additional scanner details and harmonization procedures have been reported previously [15].

Image processing

Cortical surface reconstruction and subcortical segmentation were performed using FreeSurfer v5.3. Cortical thickness estimates were derived from the Desikan–Killiany (DK) atlas, averaged across hemispheres, and grouped into global anatomical categories (frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital, insular, and cingulate cortex). Subcortical structures included lateral ventricles, thalamus, caudate, putamen, globus pallidus, hippocampus, amygdala, and nucleus accumbens; all volumes were expressed in mm³ and log-transformed to reduce skewness. Intracranial volume (ICV) was extracted from FreeSurfer’s aseg output. Details of region groupings and Alzheimer’s disease cortical signature composites are provided in Supplementary Table 1.1.

To approximate the Alzheimer’s disease cortical signature described by Dickerson and colleagues, composite regions of interest (ROIs) were constructed by averaging DK parcels spatially overlapping the original fsaverage clusters [10]. The medial temporal composite comprised entorhinal, parahippocampal, and fusiform cortices, and the inferior frontal composite included rostral middle frontal, caudal middle frontal, pars opercularis, pars orbitalis, and pars triangularis. Additional AD-signature parcels (inferior temporal, inferior parietal/angular, supramarginal, superior frontal, superior parietal, and precuneus) were extracted directly from DK labels. This approach preserves the hierarchical organization and signal characteristics of the original disease-relevant maps while stabilizing regional estimates for statistical modelling.

Eye-tracking acquisition

Eye-tracking data were recorded using the Tobii Pro Spectrum system (Tobii AB, Sweden) at a sampling rate of 300 Hz. Participants were seated in a dimly lit room with ambient luminance maintained at approximately 30 cd/m². Stimuli were presented on a 23.8-inch LCD monitor (FlexScan EV2451, EIZO; resolution: 1920 × 1080 pixels; refresh rate: 59–61 Hz; digital scanning frequency: HDMI: 15–68 kHz horizontal / 49–51 Hz or 59–61 Hz vertical), positioned at a fixed viewing distance of 65 cm, subtending a horizontal visual angle of ~ 35°. Head position was stabilized using an adjustable chin-and-forehead rest to minimize movement artifacts.

A standard nine-point calibration and validation procedure was performed prior to the experimental session, with acceptable calibration defined as a mean error < 0.5° across targets. Participants who failed to meet this criterion after two attempts were excluded from further analysis.

The experimental task was a mixed-design prosaccade–antisaccade (PS–AS) paradigm comprising 30 sequential blocks (Fig. 1). Each block contained three randomized trials: two prosaccades and one antisaccade. Trials began with a central fixation cross (1.5°) presented for 500 ms, followed by a colored cue (1.5° square; green for PS, red for AS) displayed for 800 ms. A peripheral target (2° white-outlined square) then appeared at ± 10° horizontal eccentricity, preceded by a 200 ms temporal gap. In PS trials, participants were instructed to saccade toward the target; in AS trials, they were instructed to inhibit the reflexive response and saccade to the mirror location. Target direction (left/right) was counterbalanced across trials. Verbal instructions and a practice block were provided to ensure task comprehension. No inter-trial interval was included.

Interleaved prosaccade (PS) and antisaccade (AS) paradigm. Each trial began with a 500 ms central fixation on a black background, followed by an 800 ms colored cue indicating trial type (green = PS; red = AS). After a 200 ms gap, the target appeared at ± 10° from center and remained visible for 1500 ms. Participants executed a prosaccade toward the target or an antisaccade in the opposite direction based on the cue. Trials were randomized within blocks across a single session comprising 30 blocks (90 trials total: 60 PS, 30 AS). AS = antisaccade; PS = prosaccade

Visual stimuli were presented using Tobii Pro Lab software (version 1.118), and raw gaze and pupil data were exported for offline analysis. Saccadic eye movements were preprocessed in MATLAB R2019b (MathWorks, Natick, MA) using custom scripts. Saccades were identified using a velocity-based detection algorithm with a minimum threshold of 30°/s. Each trial was segmented from fixation onset to 1500 ms post-target onset.

Saccadic preprocessing and outcome measures

Saccadic preprocessing followed the validated pipeline established in our previous work using this identical prosaccade–antisaccade paradigm, ensuring continuity in trial structure, timing parameters, and quality-control thresholds [16]. Eye‑movement data were segmented from fixation onset to 1500 ms after target onset and processed in MATLAB R2019b using custom velocity‑based algorithms (minimum detection threshold: 30°/s). Trials with more than 25% signal loss were excluded, and participants contributing fewer than 50% valid trials were removed, consistent with the original criteria.

The saccadic outcomes were categorized into the predefined outcome classes previously characterized in detail: directionally correct saccades, anticipatory responses (< 80 ms), omissions (no saccade within 500 ms), uncorrected inhibition errors (reflexive prosaccade on antisaccade trials not followed by correction), corrected inhibition errors (a corrective antisaccade within 500 ms following an erroneous prosaccade), and mean gaze‑in‑AOI duration computed using a predefined area‑of‑interest algorithm.

The saccade temporal measures were comprehensively defined and evaluated in the prior publication using the same task timing and validity thresholds, and their established profiles provide the necessary context for interpreting the pupil‑based dynamics examined here [16]. Accordingly, the present study focuses on the neuromodulatory information encoded in pre‑saccadic ocular responses, which represent a distinct physiological domain not captured by previously published saccadic metrics.

For pupil analyses, saccade onset was defined as the first gaze sample departing the central fixation region by more than 1° and remaining outside this boundary for at least 12 ms. This spatially anchored onset rule allowed us to isolate a strictly pre‑saccadic window in which the eye remained near the optical axis, thereby minimizing geometric foreshortening and other gaze‑angle–related distortions. For each trial, pupil data were truncated at the sample immediately preceding saccade onset, and all post‑saccadic values were removed to prevent contamination from eye‑movement artifacts. Trials contributed to the pupil dataset only when gaze dispersion throughout the pre‑saccadic interval remained within a maximum variation of 1° and the fixation immediately preceding saccade initiation met a minimum duration of 80 ms. Anticipatory (< 80 ms), late (> 500 ms), omission, direction‑error, and uncorrected inhibition‑error trials were excluded according to the predefined taxonomy. These procedures ensured that all pupil measurements were acquired during stable central fixation under uniform luminance and near‑orthogonal eye–camera geometry, where pupil foreshortening artifacts are negligible.

Pupillometry preprocessing

Pupil data were preprocessed in R (v4.3.1) using the pupillometryR and GazeR packages, following established guidelines [17–19]. Left- and right-eye signals were processed independently. Blinks were identified using a velocity-based algorithm that detects abrupt discontinuities and monotonic eyelid trajectories. issing segments were interpolated using cubic splines, with gaps < 100 ms merged. Traces were smoothed using a two-frame moving average and downsampled from 300 Hz to 30 Hz to align with standard cognitive pupillometry resolution [20]. Trials with > 75% missing or interpolated data were excluded. Remaining traces were averaged across eyes to yield a composite pupil signal.

Because the analytic focus of this study was the pre‑saccadic component of task‑evoked pupil responses, the timing of the pupil window for each trial was defined directly by the saccade‑onset procedure described in Sect. 2.5. For each trial, the pupil analysis window for a given epoch began at epoch onset and terminated at the final sample preceding saccade departure, ensuring that all pupil values reflected pre‑saccadic, centrally positioned gaze. This alignment eliminated contributions from gaze‑angle–related geometric distortion and prevented contamination from movement‑related artifacts, maintaining gaze near the optical axis where foreshortening is negligible and ensuring stable luminance by enforcing central fixation on a uniform, low‑contrast field; these fixation‑validity requirements were identical to those applied in the saccadic preprocessing pipeline, thereby preserving methodological coherence across behavioral and pupil‑based analyses without introducing additional filters at the pupillometry stage [20, 21].

To account for inter-individual variability in pupil size, we computed trial‑wise Δpupil using the final 100 ms of fixation (FIXend) as baseline. We then summarized task‑evoked responses within two a priori epochs expressed in fixation‑locked time. The CUE epoch spanned 500–1,300 ms after fixation onset. The TARon epoch spanned 1,500–1,580 ms after fixation onset, corresponding to 0–80 ms after target onset. Epoch definitions were derived from prior work indexing task-switching, task-set preparation, and pre-response cognitive processing [7, 22]. A summary figure illustrating the analysis epochs and group-level TEPR patterns is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Statistical analysis

Continuous predictors were z-standardized across the pooled sample. Categorical predictors used treatment coding with the cognitively normal group as the reference. Cognitive scores (MMSE, SNSB-II) were excluded to avoid conditioning on variables used in diagnostic classification. All linear mixed-effects (LME) models were implemented in nlme (v3.1–164) [23], with allowing the residual variance to vary for each level of regions considering in this study.

Saccade metrics — global cortical thickness

Regional modulation of saccade–brain coupling independent of diagnosis

Linear mixed‑effects models showed no evidence of regional modulation for Mean latency, which remained non‑significant in both antisaccade (AS; FDR = 0.9012) and prosaccade (PS; FDR = 0.2083) conditions. In contrast, Latency SD demonstrated robust region‑dependent variation, emerging consistently across independent model sets for PS (FDR = 0.0306) and AS trials (FDR = 0.0047). Thus, timing variability, rather than response onset speed, is the saccade feature most sensitive to macroscale cortical structure.

Diagnostic differences in saccade–cortical thickness coupling

Across most cortical regions and saccade metrics, CN and MCI slopes were small with wide, overlapping confidence intervals, indicating minimal evidence of thickness–variability coupling and no reliable diagnostic effects. Against this largely null background, PS Latency SD showed a clear, directionally consistent inversion of structure–function coupling across a restricted set of cortical regions.

The frontal cortex showed positive coupling in CN (βCN = 0.015 [0.006–0.023]) and a negative trend in MCI (βMCI = − 0.003 [− 0.015–0.009]), yielding Δβ = −0.018, d = − 0.191, FDR = 0.0405. A similar pattern emerged in the cingulate cortex (βCN = 0.021 [0.009–0.032], βMCI = − 0.003 [− 0.020–0.014]; Δβ = −0.024, d = − 0.183, FDR = 0.0405) and was strongest in the insula (βCN = 0.022 [0.009–0.035], βMCI = − 0.013 [− 0.032–0.005]; Δβ = −0.036, d = − 0.240, FDR = 0.0136). All other cortical regions were non‑significant (FDR ≈ 0.09–0.91) (Table 2).

AD cortical signatures regions (saccade)

Regional variation in saccade–AD cortical coupling independent of diagnosis

Within AD‑signature cortex, PS Latency SD again showed clear region‑dependent modulation, with a significant region × metric interaction after correction (FDR = 0.0409). AS Latency SD and Latency Mean remained non‑significant (FDR = 0.8944–0.6670).

Diagnostic differences in saccade–AD cortical thickness coupling

Across AD‑signature cortex, most regions showed small, imprecise CN–MCI slope differences (|Δβ| ≤ 0.017; FDR = 0.2655–0.6972), indicating minimal evidence for diagnostic effects. Only the supramarginal gyrus demonstrated a reliable inversion of PS Latency SD coupling. CN participants showed a positive slope (βCN = 0.014 [0.003–0.024]) whereas MCI participants showed a negative slope (βMCI = − 0.012 [− 0.027–0.002]), producing Δβ = −0.026, d = − 0.230, FDR = 0.0280. Thus, diagnostic effects within AD‑signature cortex are restricted to the supramarginal gyrus, with all other regions showing slopes near zero and confidence intervals overlapping the null (Table 3).

Subcortical volume (saccade)

Regional variation in saccade–subcortical coupling independent of diagnosis

Across subcortical nuclei, only PS Latency SD showed significant region‑dependent modulation (FDR = 0.0306). AS Latency SD and Latency Mean were non‑significant (FDR = 0.8334–0.4933). Thus, subcortical contributions to saccade behavior appear limited to variability in PS trials, with no evidence of regional effects for mean or AS‑related timing variability.

Diagnostic differences in saccade–subcortical slopes

Across subcortical nuclei, CN and MCI slopes for PS Latency SD were uniformly small with broad, overlapping confidence intervals, yielding no FDR‑significant diagnostic effects. Lateral ventricle, thalamus, caudate, putamen, pallidum, hippocampus, and amygdala all showed |Δβ| ≤ 0.009 with FDR ≈ 0.92, indicating negligible diagnostic differences (Supplementary Table 2.0). A nominal effect was observed in the accumbens area (βCN = 0.006 [− 0.014–0.026], βMCI = − 0.035 [− 0.063–−0.007]; Δβ = −0.041, d = − 0.184, p = 0.0197), but this did not survive correction (FDR = 0.1572).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that task‑evoked ocular responses; pupil dilation dynamics and saccadic latency—index early network vulnerability in MCI, reflecting neuromodulatory and executive‑control processes rather than peripheral motor output. These signals map selectively onto cortical architecture, offering a translational window on prodromal Alzheimer’s‑related change.

Three empirical themes anchor our interpretation. First, ocular–cortical coupling was robust and diagnosis‑independent across structural tiers. Pupil amplitude and variability measures showed strong spatial differentiation across the cortex, both at cue and target epochs, whereas timing-based metrics were uniformly null. In parallel, saccadic Latency SD—but not mean latency—demonstrated reliable regional modulation, emerging across whole‑cortex, AD‑signature, and subcortical analyses. The preferential localization to variability rather than timing in both modalities suggests shared sensitivity to control‑related mechanisms governing neuromodulatory gain and temporal stability.

Crucially, the absence of spatial differentiation in pupil timing should not be conflated with the well‑established timing deficits in saccadic eye movements. In our prior work using the same paradigm, individuals with MCI exhibited prolonged saccadic latencies and greater latency variability [16]. Thus, pupil temporal features likely capture neuromodulatory/preparatory dynamics distinct from motor execution latencies. Together, pupil–cortical effects reflect amplitude dynamics, whereas saccade–cortical effects index temporal stability within executive circuits; these properties are complementary and not contingent on diagnosis.

Second, diagnostic differences were anatomically selective, small in absolute magnitude, and convergent across modalities.

For pupil measures, MCI showed localized increases in coupling within global cortical regions and AD‑signature regions. In whole‑cortex LME models, the groups showed opposite structure–variability slopes: CN exhibited negative slopes in temporal, parietal, and insular cortices, whereas MCI exhibited positive slopes, representing a reversal of the typical structure–function relationship for pupil amplitude and variability. Within AD‑signature cortex, this inversion was most consistent in the medial temporal cortex and the supramarginal gyrus, regions central to early AD‑related vulnerability.

For saccades, MCI showed negative coupling between cortical thickness and PS Latency SD across global cortical regions and within AD‑signature regions. In whole‑cortex LME models, the groups exhibited opposite structure–variability slopes: CN showed positive slopes in frontal, cingulate, and insular cortices, whereas MCI showed negative or near‑zero slopes, reversing the typical structure–function relationship for temporal stability. Within AD‑signature regions, this inversion was again most consistent in the supramarginal gyrus, aligning with its role in attentional selection and sensorimotor integration. Together, these findings indicate a breakdown of the structural scaffolding that normally stabilizes saccade initiation in MCI. These patterns suggest that healthy cortex exhibits “elastic” resource control—greater structural integrity stabilizes timing—whereas MCI reflects a rigid or degraded regime in which additional structural resources no longer confer temporal stability. These functional coupling changes overlap the AD cortical‑signature system: the medial temporal cortex, where coupling effects were most robust—undergoes ~ 14% cortical thinning in mild AD, with supramarginal/angular gyri showing ~ 8–10% [10, 11]. This profile aligns with control circuits for volitional saccades, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), frontal eye fields (FEF), supplementary eye fields (SEF), and posterior parietal areas for antisaccades versus more reflexive superior colliculus (SC) and posterior parietal cortex pathways for prosaccades [9, 24]. Consistent with prior work in healthy adults, pupil dilation indexes antisaccade preparation and correlates with reaction time [22]; here, MCI showed exaggerated yet delayed dilation and selective coupling increases within AD‑signature cortex, suggesting inefficient—possibly compensatory—neuromodulatory allocation. Patterns differ from basal ganglia–frontostriatal disorders (e.g., Parkinson’s disease), which show attenuated preparatory modulation and weaker dilation–latency coupling [25].

Third, subcortical effects were modest, limbic structures showed weak positive associations with pupil amplitude in MCI; lateral ventricles showed nominal negative variability coupling in both modalities; and a nucleus accumbens association did not survive multiple‑comparison correction—collectively indicating small, heterogeneous, and non‑unified subcortical influences.

Synthesizing across findings, a multimodal pattern emerges; variability-based ocular metrics provide the strongest physiological signature of early cognitive impairment. Pupil amplitude variability reflects neuromodulatory responsiveness within the LC–SC–PON axis, while saccadic Latency SD captures temporal precision within frontocingulo-insular control networks [6, 9]. Converging evidence indicates that task‑evoked pupil signals arise from coordinated activity across LC (phasic gain), SC (orienting), and PON/EWN (parasympathetic control) in interaction with cortex—supporting the interpretation that pupil metrics integrate distributed brainstem control with cortical inputs rather than reflecting a unitary driver. Both ocular metrics highlight vulnerability within temporoparietal, insular, and medial temporal systems—regions known to undergo early tau accumulation and network breakdown in prodromal AD [26, 27]. Collectively, these findings suggest that ocular markers capture the downstream consequences of disrupted neuromodulatory–cortical coordination rather than reflecting motor or purely autonomic deficits.

Mechanistically, the results align with models positioning the LC–NE system as a regulator of gain, network integration, and preparatory control [28]. Reduced or inefficient phasic responsiveness in MCI would compromise the ability to modulate cortical state in anticipation of demanding tasks, consistent with the attenuated peak dilation, reduced variability, and delayed constriction. Importantly, attributing pupil dynamics solely to LC oversimplifies the architecture: SC and PON/EWN also shape moment‑to‑moment pupil size. Baseline confounds should be minimized via residualized/normalized pupil measures to isolate phasic components most relevant to LC–SC–PON coupling. The modest effect sizes observed here (d ≈ 0.2) fit with early functional alterations accompanying or preceding neurodegeneration, consistent with compensatory recruitment or reduced resilience rather than widespread failure [29].

Ocular metrics are scalable, non‑invasive indices of systems‑level vulnerability with potential roles in risk stratification, early detection, and mechanistic phenotyping. Longitudinal, multimodal work incorporating molecular and functional imaging will be critical for determining prognostic value and refining mechanistic interpretation.

This study’s cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and the mixed-task structure—while ecologically valid, complicates trial-specific analyses. The fixed 30-block, 3-trial structure may have introduced predictability, potentially influencing anticipatory control; future designs should vary block length and trial ratios to reduce expectancy effects. A further design constraint is the absence of a dedicated inter‑trial interval (ITI): trials occurred in rapid succession, so the pupil did not reliably return to a tonic baseline between trials. Although we used a 100 ms end‑of‑fixation baseline and excluded trials with excessive drift or blink‑related artifacts, these steps cannot fully separate tonic fluctuations from phasic task‑evoked responses; incorporating variable ITIs or longer return‑to‑baseline intervals would better isolate phasic TEPRs.

Signal‑processing choices impose additional constraints. Pupil traces were downsampled from 300 Hz to 30 Hz to align with standard cognitive‑pupillometry practice and to stabilize large‑cohort modeling; while this sampling is generally adequate for cognitively driven changes, it can attenuate rapid dynamics (e.g., brief changes in dilation slope or micro‑dilations). Moreover, summarizing responses within a priori epochs (CUE and target‑onset windows) enhances interpretability but forfeits continuous time‑course information (e.g., biphasic profiles, trial‑specific latency shifts). Time‑resolved approaches—growth‑curve modeling, continuous‑time GAMs, or deconvolution—should be adopted in future work to recover fine‑grained temporal structure at higher sampling rates.

The sample size, although sufficient for modeling, should be expanded to increase generalizability and capture within-group heterogeneity. Medication status was abstracted from clinical records; ingredient‑level verification and adherence could not be confirmed, leaving residual confounding possible for pupil outcomes. Longitudinal studies integrating fluid and imaging biomarkers will be essential to validate predictive utility and clarify temporal progression. Structural MRI metrics (cortical thickness, subcortical volumes) provided macroscopic proxies and do not index functional coupling or microstructure; combining eye‑tracking with fMRI, diffusion imaging, and neuromodulatory markers would yield a more mechanistic account of brain–ocular coupling. Finally, eye‑tracking is sensitive to environmental and technical factors, particularly lighting and calibration drift, underscoring the need for rigorous standardization and real‑time quality control for clinical deployment.

Conclusion

Task-evoked ocular responses during cognitively demanding paradigms capture distinct yet converging dimensions of network vulnerability across cortical and subcortical systems in mild cognitive impairment. The results show robust regional dependence of ocular–brain coupling, selective diagnostic increases in coupling within medial temporal and supramarginal cortices, and nominal limbic–ventricular subcortical trends, together indicating small, anatomically specific alterations in neuromodulatory–cortical coordination at prodromal stages. These ocular biomarkers reflect early disruptions in executive control and autonomic regulation and offer a mechanistically grounded window into the neurobiology of cognitive aging and AD. Their integration into multimodal diagnostic frameworks—alongside molecular, neuroimaging, and cognitive measures—holds promise for enhancing early detection, refining mechanistic models, and informing individualized interventions across the AD continuum.
