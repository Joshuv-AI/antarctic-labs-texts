Identification Procedures of Disaster-Injured Children in the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit After the 2023 Türkiye Earthquakes
Authors: Kübra İrday, İlknur Arslan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:In the literature, there is a disaster victim identification protocol published by Interpol for people who died during disasters. There are publications on earthquake-related demographic data, diagnosis, treatment, and mortality for disaster-injured persons (DIPs). However, there is no universally accepted definition or method for disaster-injured person identification (DIPI). Patients’ information was obtained from the electronic health records system. Information on identification procedures applied to children was obtained from the social services records.Results:“Procedure for children brought to the Adana City Training and Research Hospital who are able to provide a statement” and “Procedure for children brought to the hospital who, due to young age or adverse health conditions, are unable to provide a statement” were applied for identification. Identification was made with visual assessment in 71 children and DNA analysis in 13 children.Conclusion:Identification is a complex process involving a physician, social services specialist, law enforcement, and prosecutor. Physicians must be knowledgeable about identification. To ensure a more systematic and legal identification, procedures and algorithms should be determined before the disaster. The hope is that procedures that are shared in this article will be helpful to centers.

Introduction

Disasters are events that, whether of natural or human origin, cause social, environmental, and economic devastation, resulting in harm, death, and injury to many people. Disasters were classified by Interpol as open and closed disasters. Open disasters are issues due to which no one knows how many people have died. These often concern natural disasters such as earthquakes, floods, and large terrorist attacks. Closed disasters are issues where it is very likely that the number of casualties will be immediately established. Often, these are disasters where prior registration of the persons concerned has already taken place, such as aircraft accidents. An open disaster such as an earthquake may result in a large number of death and injury.1,2 Those who lose their lives during a disaster should be identified with dignity and returned to their families. Disaster victim identification (DVI) carries not only humanitarian dimensions but also legal, sociological, and economic aspects. It is essential for the issuance of death certificates, the conduct of burial procedures, identification in accordance with the Criminal Procedure Code 86, removal from the population registry, and for legal processes such as inheritance distribution, divorce, insurance, and bank credit. Moreover, it is necessary for clarifying homicide cases in which the deceased’s identity features have been deliberately erased, as well as for conducting funeral ceremonies that align with the deceased’s religious and cultural traditions.3,4

Interpol Working Group publishes the “Guide to DVI,” which is the unique globally accepted standard protocol. First produced in 1984, it is updated every 5 years and was recently published in 2023.5 Disaster victim identification methods are classified as primary and secondary methods. Primary methods, such as fingerprint analysis, dental examinations, and DNA profiling, are accepted as valid, reliable, and definitive means of identification. Secondary methods are not conclusive but serve as complementary to the primary methods. The findings obtained are useful in narrowing down the population. For example, determining the victim’s sex can decrease the number of individuals by half. Other clues beneficial in secondary identification include personal medical information, clothing, uniforms, tattoos, birthmarks, and skin scars. When visual identification by the victims’ relatives is not possible and does not yield results, or in cases of uncertainty, fingerprint analysis, dental examinations, and DNA profiling should be performed.6-8

On February 6, 2023, at 04:17 and 13:24 2 earthquakes measuring 7.7 and 7.6 on the Richter scale, respectively, occurred along the East Anatolian Fault, with epicenters in Pazarcık-Kahramanmaraş and Elbistan-Kahramanmaraş. While the aftershock activity of these earthquakes continued, another earthquake with a magnitude of 6.4 on the Richter scale struck Yayladağı-Hatay on February 20, 2023. According to 2022 population data, the earthquakes affected 11 countries (Kahramanmaraş, Hatay, Adıyaman, Gaziantep, Malatya, Kilis, Diyarbakır, Adana, Osmaniye, Şanlıurfa, and Elazığ) home to 14 013 196 people. In what has been described as the disaster of the century, these earthquakes resulted in the loss of 50 783 lives and injured 115 353 individuals.9,10

After the earthquake, a lot of patients were transported to the hospital by air and land.11 Since most patients did not have an identity or relatives, temporary patient files and temporary medical records were created for evaluation, intervention, treatment, and operation. Children were transferred from the emergency room to the operating room, child services, or pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) using these temporary files (Figure 1). In such a chaotic environment, the identification of children is a psychological, social, legal, and economic necessity. Legal procedures were needed, but a guide or algorithm for disaster-injured person’s identification (DIPI) could not be reached. Previous studies conducted generally focused on demographic data, diagnostic and treatment methods, and factors affecting mortality and morbidity. Identification procedures were developed with social services workers inspired by the interpol DVI protocols.

The aim of this study is to present the procedures and results that were implemented in the PICU during identification after the 2023 Türkiye earthquakes, to define the concepts of “disaster-injured person” (DIP) and “disaster-injured person identification,” and to compare the characteristics of patients according to the diagnostic methods used, as well as to evaluate the relationship of these characteristics with clinical outcomes.

Materials and Methods

This study was designed as cross-sectional research. The aim of the study was to compare the characteristics of patients according to the diagnostic methods used and to evaluate the relationship of these characteristics with clinical outcomes. The study included children aged 1-216 months, including foreign nationals, who were admitted to the 70-bed tertiary PICU of Adana City Training and Research Hospital between February 6 and 28, 2023. Patient information such as age, sex, trauma region, and clinical outcome was obtained from the electronic health record system. Data on the identification procedures applied to the children were retrieved from the social services unit records.

Inclusion Criteria

Children who were brought to the emergency room due to injury during the earthquake and were admitted to the PICU with a temporary medical registration because they did not have identification underwent an identification procedure.

Exclusion Criteria

Patients referred from external centers or brought to the emergency room due to diseases not related to earthquakes such as pneumonia, epilepsy, or traffic accident.

Patients who were being monitored in departments other than the PICU, such as cardiovascular surgery, neurosurgery, or adult intensive care units.

Patients whose identities were determined, and permanent files were opened.

As in all forensic cases, the prosecutor in the morgue decided what to do with children who were admitted to the PICU and died before identification could be made. These patients who died before identification could be made were excluded from the study.

Approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee for Scientific Research of Adana City Training and Research Hospital (Date: March 6, 2025, meeting number:11, decision no:395). Since the study was retrospective, the ethics committee waived the informed consent.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 25.0 (IBM SPSS Corp.; Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (25th-75th percentile). The normality of distribution was assessed separately for each group using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since none of the main continuous variables (age, length of stay in the PICU, length of hospital stays) followed a normal distribution, comparisons between 2 groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups were made using the Pearson chi-square test; however, when expected cell counts were below 5, the Fisher’s exact test or the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test was applied. In particular, the Fisher’s exact test was used for variables such as the Glasgow Coma Score, and the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test was used for comparisons involving multicategory variables, such as clinical outcome. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P value of < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of n = 141 children were admitted to the PICU between February 6 and 28, 2023, when the study was conducted. Patients who were followed up for non-earthquake-related diseases (n = 48) were excluded. Children who already had identification (n = 8) and 1 child who died before identification were excluded from the study. Finally, 84 children who had undergone the identification procedure were included in the study (Figure 2).

Forty-four (52%) of the children were male, and the median age was 114.5 (58.5-161.5) months. Crush syndrome (n = 50) due to extremity (n = 36) and general body trauma (n = 21) was frequently observed. Fifty-six (66.6%) patients were hospitalized in the first 3 days. Sixty-four patients had a Glasgow Coma Score of ≥9 and were conscious and communicable. Twenty-four patients (28.6%) were admitted and intubated and received mechanical ventilation. Identification was made with visual assessment in 71 (84.5%) children and DNA analysis in 13 (15.5%) children. Nation, accepted unit, length of stay in PICU, length of stay in hospital, clinical outcome, and mortality information of children are shown in Table 1.

There was no statistically significant difference between identification method and sex, nation, accepted unit, trauma region, Glasgow Coma Score, crush syndrome, mechanical ventilation, and clinical outcome. There was a statistically significant difference between identification method and age, length of stay in PICU, and length of stay in hospital. The age of patients identified through DNA analysis (36(16.5-52) months) was found to be lower than that of patients who underwent visual assessment (123 (65-168) months) (P < .01). Visual assessment was performed on patients who were referred to an external institution (n = 12) and died (n = 10). Length of stay in PICU (124 (18.5-43) days) and length of stay in hospital (47 (36-49.5) days) of patients who underwent DNA analysis were found to be significantly longer than those who underwent visual assessment (6 (4-8) days and 11 (5-18) days).

Determination of Unidentified Patients

There are no clear definitions in the literature for patients who are injured during disasters. The events are described using various terms such as disaster, accident, calamity, or natural disaster, and the affected individuals are referred to by various names including victims, injured persons, casualties, or patients. In this study, an unidentified person who was removed from the earthquake region, the debris field, and transferred to the healthcare center was defined as “disaster-injured person (DIP),” and the identification process was defined as “disaster-injured person identification (DIPI)” (Table 2).

Disaster-injured persons who were unable to communicate or whose information did not match after interviews with the child and patient relatives were reported to the child handover commission. Upon the child handover commission’s decision, a forensic medicine specialist accompanied by a social services specialist came to the PICU and collected DNA samples. Protocols were designated with the child handover commission, namely, the “Procedure for children brought to our hospital who are able to provide a statement” and the “Procedure for children brought to our hospital who, due to young age or adverse health conditions, are unable to provide a statement” for identification (Figure 6).

On the day following the earthquake, a large number of family members seeking injured children contacted the authors via social media, various phone applications, the hospital phone, personal phones, or even by visiting the PICU. At this stage, to serve as a point of contact for family members, a “Crisis Coordination Center” was established within the emergency service, which was the most accessible location. Announcements were posted at the hospital entrances and in visible areas, and posts were shared on official and personal social media accounts (Figure 7). From patient relatives who applied to the crisis coordination center information regarding the DIP, such as age, gender, region of residence, birthmarks, tattoos, and other distinguishing features, was collected. The information obtained was then compared with the records of children with unknown identities being treated at the PICU, and a matching process was undertaken.

Discussion

The 2023 Türkiye earthquakes were extreme disasters. Since the occurrence of 2 devastating earthquakes in succession on the same day is a rare natural event, the resulting situation was described as the disaster of the century, which caused extensive and severe destruction over a wide area. The first earthquake struck during the night while people were asleep at home, causing buildings to collapse, trapping individuals under the debris, and leading to an increase in both injuries and fatalities.10,12 As a result of injuries sustained by individuals familiar with one another, such as neighbors and relatives, a large number of unidentified patients had to be transferred to distant provinces. Studies have demonstrated the importance of identification for these patients. Unidentified patients classified under a red triage code are at higher risk regarding the need for hemodialysis, emergency surgery, and mortality. In disaster medicine, DIPI is defined as a critical component in managing injured individuals, encompassing emergency medical care, dialysis, surgery, advanced treatments, and the organization of transfer systems. It has been reported that in cases where DIPs are not identified, patient safety and the quality of healthcare, as well as financial issues and coordination among systems, become significantly more challenging.13,14

After the 2023 Türkiye earthquakes, 84 DIP with a median age of 114.5 months (interquartile range = 48.5-161.5) were admitted to the PICU (Table 1). In this study, no difference between the identification method and the Glasgow Coma Score, trauma region, clinical outcome, or presence of crush syndrome was found, but a significant relationship between age and identification method was found. Based on the results, age is an important factor for identification methods, procedures, and algorithms. Furthermore, when comparing patient characteristics according to the identification methods, it was observed that patients identified through DNA analysis were significantly younger and had longer lengths of stay in both the PICU and hospital compared with those identified by visual assessment. These findings highlight that the method of identification is not only a technical process but is also associated with clinical severity and prognosis, which should be considered in future disaster preparedness planning.

The most comprehensive and detailed document on identification was the DVI protocol published by Interpol. According to the DVI protocol, a 4-phase approach should be applied: scene examination, postmortem data, antemortem data, and reconciliation.5,7 The procedures were created inspired by the interpol DVI protocol and recommendations. Firstly, a crisis coordination center was established to interview people who came to the hospital to search for their relatives. This team not only served as a bridge between the PICU and patients’ relatives but also helped reduce crowding in front of the PICU.

The children in the PICU were severely traumatized, injured, and frightened. They needed to be reunited with their parents immediately. The day after the earthquake, the child handover commission was established, and 2 separate procedures were created depending on the age and medical conditions.

Procedure for children brought to the hospital who can provide a statement was as follows: The medical findings of DIPs brought to the hospital after the earthquake were recorded in the hospital information management system. Based on the statement provided by the child, their relatives were contacted, and those who could be reached were invited to the hospital. Prior to any interview with the child, information regarding the child was gathered from the accompanying relatives; following verification, these individuals were provided with details about the child, and a secure meeting was arranged.

This procedure is applicable for DIP who are conscious, capable of communication, and able to provide meaningful responses to verbal prompts. It is a visual assessment-based identification method. For DIP who were not Turkish citizens, the same procedure applied with the assistance of authorized interpreters. Considering that children after the earthquake were frightened, agitated, and in pain, a social services specialist and/or the responsible physician asked questions regarding the child’s age, family, school, teachers, neighborhood, and telephone number. Based on the information obtained, relatives were invited to the hospital. For relatives arriving at the PICU, questions regarding the child were asked in a meeting room where the social services specialist and/or the responsible physician and a security officer were present. If the information provided by the child and the relatives matched, details regarding the child’s medical condition were shared, and the relatives were reunited with the children under the supervision of the social services specialist and/or the responsible physician and a security officer (Figure 6).

Using this method, identification was achieved for 71 DIPs in the PICU. A higher proportion of children were medically and developmentally capable of communication, and able to recall and share personal and environmental information. In cases where, at the referral center, the contact numbers were written on the bodies of the injured children, this made it possible to reach parents and make identification fast (Figure 8).

Procedure for children brought to the hospital who, due to young age or adverse health conditions, are unable to provide a statement as follows: The medical findings of DIP brought to the hospital after the earthquake were recorded in the hospital information management system. In cases where relatives could not be reached based on the available information and findings, this procedure may be used for children who are intubated due to injury, who are not conscious, or who, due to young age, are unable to communicate and provide meaningful responses to verbal prompts. To speed up the process and not to increase the trauma of the family, especially in children with high mortality, the case should be reported to the child handover commission along with the assessment results immediately, and the commission and prosecutor’s decision should be applied. Among the primary identification methods, such as fingerprint analysis, dental examinations, and DNA analysis, DNA analysis is the most appropriate method for these children. For genetic profiling, DNA can be obtained from body fluids such as blood, urine, saliva, and sperm; from soft tissues such as muscle, tendon, and mucosa; as well as from hard tissues such as bone, cartilage, and teeth.3 In the DIPs, samples for DNA analysis were obtained from peripheral blood. During this process, obtaining the necessary permission documents, keeping records, collecting the samples, and adhering to chain of custody rules are essential for ensuring the accuracy and reliability of the results.

In this country, DVI is carried out by the Criminal Department of the Gendarmerie of the General Command of the Ministry of Interior, the Criminal Department of the General Directorate of Security, and the Forensic Medicine Institute of the Ministry of Justice. In the Turkish legal system, the investigation of disaster events is the component of the Public Prosecutor’s Office as they are considered judicial events.7 For identification to be carried out according to the law, physicians must report the situation to the relevant authorities. The procedures that were applied after the earthquake are shown in algorithm in Figure 6. Children who die before identification can be made were considered judicial cases, and the decision belonged to the prosecutor. While identification procedures are ongoing, patients who die are reported to the child handover commission and prosecutor. While identification procedures are ongoing, the discharge of patients whose medical treatment has been completed is decided in collaboration with the child handover commission. After the identification process is completed, patients whose medical treatment has been completed are discharged after the child handover commission was notified (Figure 6).

Disasters and natural events occur periodically around the world and are overcome through rescue and recovery interventions, including adequate healthcare systems. However, in recent years, during the natural and economic crises, it has become clear that self-sufficiency rates are low and unpreparedness rates are high in European countries. Floods, earthquakes, droughts, and hurricanes have caused the deaths of hundreds of children in the last 20 years. An earthquake is an unpredictable, unexpected open disaster. As in the 2023 Türkiye earthquakes, disasters can cause large numbers of deaths and injuries requiring complicated medical and surgical treatments. Children carry a much higher risk of death and injury compared to adults. The most vulnerable always bear the greatest risk. During disasters, the most negative and direct impacts are observed among children. The events of recent years have warned administrators about the importance of crisis preparedness. Disaster preparedness, response, and recovery capacity depend on multidisciplinary, well-planned, coordinated, and reliable methodologies. Meeting the needs of children in future crises is possible with disaster management planning and emergency management strategies that build on past experiences.15-17

Limitations

This study has some limitations. This retrospective study only includes information about PICU, not the other intensive cares in general, such as general, anesthesia, neurosurgery. Some data have been overlooked due to the difficulty of keeping records in such a complex and chaotic situation. The morbidity and mortality status of intubated patients who were referred to external institutions after identification are not known. However, because patient data did not influence the procedures, this did not affect the results. The procedures cannot be compared due to insufficient information in the literature.

An earthquake can lead to considerable number of deaths and injuries. Identifying critically ill children to receive healthcare after disasters is a social, economic, and legal necessity. Physicians must be knowledgeable about identification and report cases to the authorities. Social services must be involved in the process. The assessment and investigation are conducted by law enforcement. The prosecutor makes the final decision. After all, identification is a complex, multifactorial, multidisciplinary process involving various institutions. To ensure a more systematic and legal identification, procedures and algorithms should be determined before the disaster. The hope is that the procedures that were shared in this article will be helpful to centers preparing for disaster.
