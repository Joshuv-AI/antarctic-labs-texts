Extracellular biogenic nanoscale mitochondria reprogram the wound microenvironment via ROS scavenging independent of cellular uptake
Authors: Fang Lin, Jing Liu, Yue Ding, Kexin Ma, Qingshu Meng, Xiaohui Zhou, Qingliu Zhang, Hao Hu, Zhongmin Liu, Xiaoting Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondria are nanoscale organelles essential for cellular metabolism and redox regulation, making them a compelling target for regenerative therapeutics. Analysis of wound-edge tissues from pediatric patients with chronic non-healing ulcers revealed marked metabolic insufficiency and impaired regenerative signaling, underscoring an unmet clinical need for mitochondrial-based interventions. Here, we show that topically applied mesenchymal stem cell-derived mitochondria (MSC-mt), functioning as naturally derived nanoscale organelles, markedly accelerate wound closure in a murine full-thickness skin injury model. MSC-mt enhanced angiogenesis, collagen deposition, and fibroblast survival while reducing oxidative stress and apoptosis. Mechanistically, their cytoprotective effects occur primarily through extracellular scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS), independent of cellular internalization. Excessive immobilization of MSC-mt within a thermosensitive hydrogel compromised their efficacy, emphasizing the importance of mitochondrial mobility and microenvironmental access. Under high oxidative stress, internalized MSC-mt activated PINK1–Parkin–mediated mitophagy, indicating a context-dependent intracellular quality-control response. These findings position MSC-mt as a cell-free, organelle-level nano-therapeutic that operates through a dual extracellular–intracellular mechanism and emphasize the importance of delivery strategies that preserve mitochondrial functionality and spatial freedom.Keywords: Mesenchymal stem cells, Mitochondrial transplantation, ROS scavenging, Mitophagy, Wound healing

Introduction

The skin is the largest organ of the human body and serves as a vital barrier against environmental insults. Chronic wounds, including pressure ulcers, diabetic foot ulcers, and venous leg ulcers, are particularly difficult to manage, with slow healing, high recurrence rates, and significant physical, psychological, and economic burdens. The global scar treatment market is poised for remarkable growth, with its value estimated at USD 19.1 billion in 2025 and projected to reach USD 43.5 billion by 2035 [1]. This accelerating demand is further intensified by three converging global trends: the rising prevalence of chronic diseases, the acceleration of population aging, and the resurgence of trauma-related injuries caused by accidents, burns, and armed conflicts. Together, these factors continue to strain existing wound care systems and highlight the need for innovative regenerative approaches.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have emerged as a promising therapeutic approach for wound healing due to their immunomodulatory properties, paracrine signaling, and capacity to promote tissue regeneration. However, direct transplantation of MSCs is associated with several limitations, including poor cell engraftment and survival at target sites, pulmonary embolism, and inconsistent efficacy in complex wound environments [2]. These concerns have prompted growing interest in cell-free alternatives that retain the therapeutic benefits of MSCs while offering improved safety and scalability.

In previous study, we reported that topical application of MSC-conditioned medium significantly accelerated wound healing in a mouse full-thickness skin injury model [3]. Among the active components secreted by MSCs, extracellular mitochondria have recently attracted attention for their role in tissue repair and cellular protection [4]. For instance, transfer of MSC-derived mitochondria (MSC-mt) enhances osteogenesis [5,6], promotes brown adipocyte differentiation [7], and supports cardiac recovery in doxorubicin-induced heart failure model [8]. We reported that myocardial injection of MSC-mt into the infarcted heart significantly improved cardiac function, outperforming mitochondria from fibroblasts, suggesting an intrinsic therapeutic advantage of MSC-mt [9]. Nonetheless, the therapeutic potential of directly applying MSC-mt to cutaneous wounds remains largely unexplored.

In this study, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of topically applied MSC-mt in a murine full-thickness skin wound model and explored the underlying mechanisms. We found that simple surface administration of MSC-mt was sufficient to accelerate wound closure and enhance tissue regeneration. Unexpectedly, we found that MSC-mt–mediated cytoprotection did not require cellular internalization; in fact, reduced uptake was often associated with greater benefit, pointing to an extracellular mode of action. These findings challenge the prevailing assumption that mitochondrial internalization is necessary for therapeutic benefit, and instead highlight the potential of MSC-mt to promote tissue repair via extracellular mechanisms, thereby informing the design of more effective and less invasive delivery strategies.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and mitochondrial isolation

Human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived mesenchymal stem cells (iMSCs) were used for mitochondrial isolation. iMSCs were cultured in low-glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Servicebio, Cat# G4520) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Servicebio, Cat# G8003) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS, Servicebio, Cat# G4003) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 as described previously [3]. Cells at passages 4 to 10 were used in all experiments.

For mitochondrial extraction, iMSCs were expanded in 15 cm culture dishes until reaching >80% confluency. Cells were then harvested and cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen at a density of two dishes per cryovial. For each isolation, one vial of frozen cells was rapidly thawed, washed once with ice-cold PBS, and pelleted by centrifugation. Mitochondria were isolated using a commercial mitochondria isolation kit for cultured cells (Beyotime, Cat# C3601) according to the manufacturer's protocol, with minor modifications. Briefly, the cell pellet was resuspended in 1 mL mitochondrial isolation buffer and homogenized using a 5 mL glass Dounce homogenizer with ∼50 strokes on ice. This typically yielded a Trypan Blue exclusion rate of approximately 50%, indicating effective plasma membrane disruption with preserved mitochondrial integrity. The mitochondrial fraction was obtained through differential centrifugation and resuspended in the mitochondrial storage buffer provided with the kit. All steps were performed on ice to maintain mitochondrial activity. Mitochondrial concentration was determined using the Bradford protein assay (Beyotime, Cat# P0006C), with standard curves prepared using the mitochondrial storage buffer as the diluent. On average, ∼50 μg of mitochondria were obtained from one vial of frozen iMSCs. For in vitro experiments, the working concentration was standardized to 37.5 μg MSC-mt per 10 cm dish of recipient cells.

Tissue sample collection

Human skin tissues were collected from pediatric patients admitted to Beijing Children's Hospital, Capital Medical University. Normal skin tissues were obtained from surplus skin excised during polydactyly correction surgeries. Chronic non-healing wound tissues were obtained from debrided wound edges of pediatric patients with burn- or trauma-induced wounds that failed to progress through normal healing phases. All tissue samples were collected with informed consent from the patients' legal guardians and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Children's Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval No. [2024]-Y-053-D).

Cytokine quantification from clinical samples

Debrided wound-edge tissues and control skin samples were lysed in RIPA buffer (CST, Cat#9806). Total protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher, Cat#23235) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cytokine levels, including TNF-α, EGF, FGF, and TIMP1, were measured using a cytometric bead array (CBA) kit (BioLegend), and data were acquired on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). Cytokine concentrations were normalized to total protein content.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

For nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), iMSCs were first thawed and labeled with MitoTracker Green FM (CST, Cat# 9074) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Labeled cells were then subjected to mitochondrial isolation as described above. Following the final centrifugation step, the mitochondrial pellet was resuspended and fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA) prior to NTA measurement. Fluorescent NTA was performed using the ZetaView system (Particle Metrix, Germany) equipped with a 488 nm laser for fluorescence excitation. The mitochondrial sample was diluted 25-fold in PBS to reach an optimal particle concentration range. Measurements were carried out under standard settings, and each sample was analyzed across multiple positions within the chamber. Particle size distribution and mean particle diameter were calculated using the accompanying ZetaView software.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Freshly isolated MSC-mt were immediately fixed in electron microscopy fixative (Servicebio, Cat# G1102) following the final centrifugation step. For analysis of intracellular mitochondria, L929 fibroblasts subjected to the indicated treatments (H2O2 alone or H2O2 + MSC-mt) were harvested and fixed in the same fixative. Samples were processed for conventional transmission electron microscopy, including dehydration, resin embedding, and ultrathin sectioning (60–80 nm). TEM was performed using a HITACHI HT7800 transmission electron microscope. Representative images were acquired at appropriate magnifications to visualize the intact double-membrane structure and morphology integrity. For quantitative analysis, mitochondria were evaluated from ≥10 cells per condition per experiment across three independent experiments. For each cell, 3∼5 randomly selected cytoplasmic fields were imaged at the same magnification. Mitochondrial morphology was quantified in a blinded manner, and fragmented mitochondria were defined as having a Feret max/Feret min ratio <1.5.

Assessment of mitochondrial membrane potential (TMRE assay) of isolated MSC-mt

The mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) of isolated MSC-mt was assessed using a TMRE Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (Beyotime, Cat# C2001S). Mitochondria were resuspended in the provided assay buffer and incubated with TMRE working solution at 37 °C for 30 min, according to the manufacturer's protocol. To assess the functional responsiveness of the isolated mitochondria, a subset of MSC-mt was treated with 10 μM carbonyl cyanide 3-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP) for 20 min prior to TMRE staining. Fluorescence was measured using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter), and data were analyzed with FlowJo software. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of TMRE was used to quantify relative mitochondrial membrane potential.

Citrate synthase (CS) activity assay

CS activity of freshly isolated MSC-mt (and heat-inactivated MSC-mt as control) was quantified using a commercial citrate synthase activity kit (Solarbio, Cat# BC1060) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, freshly isolated MSC-mt were divided into two portions: one portion was used directly as functional mitochondria, whereas the other portion was heat-inactivated by incubation at 95 °C for 5 min to generate an enzymatically inactive control. Mitochondrial samples were incubated according to the manufacturer's instructions, and the increase in absorbance at 412 nm, reflecting the formation of 5-thio-2-nitrobenzoic acid (TNB), was recorded over time and used to calculate CS activity according to the kit formula. The protein concentration of mitochondrial preparations was determined using the Bradford assay (Beyotime, Cat# P0006C), and all samples were normalized by protein content for subsequent experiments.

In vivo wound healing model and mitochondrial treatment

Male BALB/c mice (8–10 weeks old) were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and housed under standard conditions. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tongji University (Protocol No. TJBB04723101). A full-thickness excisional wound model was established as previously described [10]. Briefly, after anesthesia, a 15 mm circular full-thickness skin wound was created on the dorsal surface of each mouse. Mice were randomly assigned to following groups: (1) Control (100 μL PBS); (2) mtL (50 μg MSC-mt in 100 μL PBS); (3) mtH (100 μg MSC-mt in 100 μL PBS); (4) gel (50 μL PBS + 50 μL hydrogel); (5) 10% gel + mtH (100 μg MSC-mt resuspended in 90 μL PBS, then mixed with 10 μL hydrogel); (6) 50% gel + mtH (100 μg MSC-mt resuspended in 50 μL PBS, then mixed with 50 μL hydrogel). All treatments were administered by topical application to the wound bed immediately after injury. Mice were monitored daily for general condition, wound status, and signs of infection or distress. Wound closure was documented by photographing the wounds on post-wounding days (PWD) 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8. Wound areas were quantified using ImageJ software in a blinded manner by investigators unaware of treatment allocation. On PWD8, mice were euthanized using an overdose of anesthesia, and wound tissues were harvested for subsequent analysis.

Histological evaluation of tissue morphology and collagen deposition

Wound tissues were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to assess overall tissue morphology and inflammatory cell infiltration. Masson's trichrome staining was used to evaluate collagen deposition. All staining procedures were performed according to standard protocols provided by the reagent manufacturer (Servicebio, Cat#G1076 for H&E; Servicebio, Cat#G1006 for Masson's trichrome staining). Collagen deposition was quantified using ImageJ software by calculating the proportion of blue-stained area relative to the total tissue area in Masson-stained sections. Histological quantification was performed in a blinded manner using ImageJ by investigators unaware of group allocation.

In vivo fluorescence imaging

To track the in vivo distribution of MSC-mt at the wound site, iMSCs were labeled with MitoTracker Deep Red FM (Thermo Fisher, Cat# A66440) for 30 min at 37 °C prior to mitochondrial isolation. The labeled mitochondria were then topically applied to full-thickness wounds. Fluorescence imaging was performed using the IVIS Lumina Series III imaging system (PerkinElmer) at 1 h, 1 day, 4 days, and 7 days post-wounding. Fluorescence intensity at the wound site was quantified using Living Image software (PerkinElmer) by defining a region of interest (ROI) over the wound area.

Laser speckle contrast imaging

Blood perfusion at the wound site was assessed on PWD7 using a laser speckle contrast imaging system (SimOpto, China). Mice were anesthetized during the imaging process, and blood flow maps were generated and analyzed according to the manufacturer's protocol using automated analysis settings. Perfusion analysis was performed in a blinded manner with respect to treatment groups.

Tissue biochemical assays

Peri-wound tissue samples were collected on post-wounding day 8 and processed for biochemical analysis. For ATP measurement, tissues were homogenized in the lysis buffer provided with the ATP assay kit (Beyotime, Cat# S0026), and ATP levels were determined according to the manufacturer's instructions. For malondialdehyde (MDA) quantification, tissues were homogenized in RIPA buffer, and MDA levels were measured using a lipid peroxidation MDA assay kit (Beyotime, Cat# S0131S). In both assays, total protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay (Thermo Fisher, Cat#23235), and all results were normalized to protein content to ensure comparability across samples. Pro-inflammatory cytokine levels (including IL-10, TNF, IL-12p70, MCP-1, IFN-γ, and IL-6) were measured using a cytometric bead array (CBA) kit (BD Biosciences, Cat# 552364), with protein extracts from peri-wound tissues used as input samples. Data were acquired by flow cytometry and analyzed using FCAP Array software.

Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded wound tissue sections (4 μm thick) were used for immunostaining. For immunofluorescence, antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0), followed by blocking with 5% BSA. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD31 (Servicebio, Cat# GB120005, 1:200), Vimentin (CST, Cat# 5741, 1:200), and 8-hydroxyguanosine (8-OHG, Rockland, Cat# 200-301-A99, 1:200). After washing, sections were incubated with appropriate Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies and counterstained with DAPI. TUNEL staining was performed using a commercial kit (Servicebio, Cat# G1502) according to the manufacturer's protocol. For immunohistochemistry, Col1a1 expression was evaluated using a rabbit anti-Col1a1 antibody (CST, Cat# 72026, 1:200), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and DAB chromogen development. Images were acquired using a fluorescence or brightfield microscope (Leica), and quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software in a blinded manner with respect to treatment groups. Marker expression was evaluated either as fluorescence intensity, positive area fraction, or double-positive cell counts per field, depending on the staining type.

Compound and inhibitor treatments

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 1000 μM) was used to induce oxidative stress. Additional chemical treatments included the following: rotenone (500 nM; MCE, Cat# HY-B1756), PX-12 (10 μM; MCE, Cat# HY-13734), Dynasore (5 μM; MCE, Cat# HY-15304), MNS (1 μM; MCE, Cat# HY-78263), EIPA (20 μM; MCE, Cat# HY-101840), LY294002 (50 μM; MCE, Cat# HY-10108), and chloroquine (50 μM; MCE, Cat# HY-17589A). All inhibitors were prepared in DMSO and diluted to working concentrations in culture medium, with vehicle controls included accordingly. For hypoxia treatment, cells were incubated in sealed anaerobic bags (GENbag Anaer, bioMérieux, Cat# 45534) following the manufacturer's instructions. According to the product specifications, residual oxygen concentration drops below 1% within approximately 1 h.

Flow cytometry analysis

Flow cytometry was used to assess apoptosis, mitochondrial function, and senescence. All staining procedures were performed according to the manufacturers' instructions. Data were acquired using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed with FlowJo software. Apoptosis was performed using combinations of fluorescently labeled Annexin V and dead cell dyes. Specifically, cells were stained with either Annexin V–APC (Thermo Fisher, Cat# A35110) or Annexin V–PB450 (Thermo Fisher, Cat# A35122), in combination with propidium iodide (PI; Thermo Fisher, Cat# 00-6990-50) or DAPI (BD, Cat# 564907). Based on fluorescence profiles, cells were classified into four populations: live cells (Annexin V−/PI− or Annexin V−/DAPI−), early apoptotic cells (Annexin V+/PI− or Annexin V+/DAPI−), late apoptotic or necrotic cells (Annexin V+/PI+ or Annexin V+/DAPI+), and dead cells (Annexin V−/PI+ or Annexin V−/DAPI+). Mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) levels were measured using MitoSOX™ Red (Yeasen, Cat# 40778ES50) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was assessed using the TMRE assay kit (Beyotime, Cat# C2001S). Cellular senescence was evaluated with the CellEvent™ Senescence Green Flow Cytometry Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# C10841). Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) were detected using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe (Beyotime, Cat# S0033S). Mitophagy activity was analyzed using the Mitophagy Detection Kit (Dojindo, Cat# MD01) following the manufacturer's protocol. In selected experiments, mitophagy was analyzed in parallel with mitochondrial depolarization using a dual-staining strategy combining the Mitophagy Detection Kit with the mitochondrial depolarization probe mitoPeDPP (Dojindo, Cat# M466), allowing simultaneous evaluation of mitophagy activation and damaged mitochondria at the single-cell level. For analyses of mtROS, mitochondrial membrane potential, senescence and mitophagy, only viable cells (defined as PI− or DAPI− populations) were included in the analysis. Data were reported as percentages of positive cells or normalized MFI values.

Confocal microscopy for mitochondrial transfer

To visualize mitochondrial transfer from MSCs to recipient cells, L929 were pre-labeled with CM-DiI (Thermo Fisher, Cat#C7000) following the manufacturer's instructions. MSCs were pre-stained with MitoTracker Green (CST, Cat#9074) prior to mitochondrial isolation. The labeled MSC-mt were then co-cultured with CM-DiI–labeled L929 cells for 16 h. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and counterstained with DAPI. Confocal imaging was performed using a laser scanning confocal microscope (NIKON Eclipse Ti) equipped with the Eclipse C2 system (Nikon). Representative images were acquired and processed using Nikon imaging software.

Measurement of hydrogen peroxide

Cell culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 10,000×g for 10 min. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) levels in the clarified supernatants were measured using a commercial assay kit (Beyotime, Cat# S0038) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance was recorded at 560 nm using a microplate reader, and H2O2 concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve generated with known concentrations of H2O2.

Mitophagy fluorescence imaging

To assess mitophagy and mitochondrial depolarization in live cells, MSC-mt were pre-labeled with MitoTracker Deep Red (Yeasen, Cat# 40743ES50) and co-cultured with L929 under the indicated experimental conditions. Following co-culture, mitophagy was detected using a mitophagy detection kit (Dojindo, Cat# MD01), while depolarized mitochondria were simultaneously labeled with MitoPeDPP (Dojindo, Cat# M466), according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Cell nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342. Fluorescence images were acquired using a Leica DMi8 inverted fluorescence microscope.

Colony formation assay

For colony formation analysis, 500 L929 cells were seeded per well in six-well plates and cultured under indicated conditions. On day 8, colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 30 min at room temperature. After washing and drying, plates were imaged, and colonies were counted using ImageJ software. Clusters containing ≥50 cells were defined as colonies.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8)

L929 fibroblasts were seeded into 96-well plates (1000 cells/well) and allowed to adhere overnight, followed by co-culture with hydrogel alone or hydrogel-embedded MSC-mt. After 24 h, cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (Dojindo, Cat# 341-07761). To avoid interference from the hydrogel matrix, culture supernatants were transferred to a new 96-well plate before measuring absorbance at 450 nm. Cell viability was expressed relative to untreated controls. All experiments were independently repeated three times.

Assessment of mitochondrial release from hydrogel

Mitochondria were labeled by incubating MSCs with MitoTracker Green (CST, Cat# 9074) for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by extensive washing with PBS to remove excess dye. Labeled mitochondria (MSC-mt) were then isolated as previously decribed, resuspended in PBS, and mixed with thermosensitive hydrogel (Horizon Medical, China) at final gel volume fractions of 10%, 20%, and 50%. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 3 h. After incubation, 200 μL of supernatant was collected and transferred to a black opaque 96-well plate. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader (Synergy) with excitation/emission settings of 485/528 nm to assess mitochondrial release.

siRNA-mediated knockdown of PINK1

To investigate the role of PINK1 in MSC-mt–mediated mitophagy, small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting murine PINK1 (siPINK1) was synthesized by Shanghai Generay Co., Ltd. For knockdown in MSCs, cells at ∼60% confluence were transfected with siPINK1 (final concentration: 50 nM) using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 13778030) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Scrambled siRNA (siRNA-NC) was used as a negative control. Transfection efficiency and PINK1 knockdown were confirmed by qPCR and Western blot 48 h post-transfection, prior to mitochondrial isolation. For knockdown in L929 cells, a similar protocol was applied. Cells were transfected with siPINK1 or siRNA-NC for 48 h, followed by oxidative stress induction (400 μM H2O2) and subsequent co-culture with freshly isolated MSC-mt for functional assays.

qPCR and PCR array analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells and peri-wound tissue samples using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat# 74014), following the manufacturer's protocol. Reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara, Cat# RR036A). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was conducted using SYBR Green chemistry on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Primer sequences used for target genes are listed in Table 1. Gene expression levels were analyzed using the 2ˆ–ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the internal control. For pathway-level gene profiling, the Mouse Wound Healing RT2 Profiler PCR Array (Qiagen, Cat# PAMM-121ZA) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions. Data were analyzed using the Qiagen online data analysis portal, and fold changes were calculated using the ΔΔCt method.

Western blotting

For whole-cell protein extraction, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (CST, Cat#9806) supplemented with protease/phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (CST, Cat#5872). For mitochondrial protein extraction, the mitochondrial pellet obtained from the final step of the isolation procedure was resuspended and lysed in mitochondrial lysis buffer provided in the isolation kit (Beyotime, Cat# C3601), freshly supplemented with PMSF, according to the manufacturer's instructions. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay (Thermo Fisher, Cat#23235). Equal amounts of protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-PINK1 (CST, Cat# 6946), anti-NIX (CST, Cat# 12396), anti-TOM20 (CST, Cat# 42406), anti-USP30 (Proteintech, Cat# 15402-1-AP), Total OXPHOS Rodent WB Antibody Cocktail (Abcam, Cat# ab110413), anti-phospho-AKT (Ser473) (CST, Cat# 4060), and anti-β-actin (CST, Cat# 4967). After incubation with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, signals were developed using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Thermo Fisher, Cat#34580) and imaged with a chemiluminescence detection system (Tanon).

Protein array

Total cellular protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer, and protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay. Protein profiling was performed using the Mouse Apoptosis Signaling Pathway Array C1 (RayBiotech, Cat# AAM-APOSIG-1-4) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 350 μg protein was incubated with the antibody array membrane overnight at 4 °C. After washing and incubation with detection antibodies and chemiluminescent reagents, signals were visualized using the ImageQuant LAS 4000 Scanner (GE Healthcare). Quantification of signal intensity was performed using the accompanying analysis software provided with the scanner.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.2.1, San Diego, CA). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc correction was used. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Metabolic and regenerative dysregulation in chronic non-healing wound-edge tissues

We analyzed debrided wound-edge tissues from chronic, non-healing pediatric ulcers and compared them with surplus normal skin excised during polydactyly repair procedures. Biochemical assays revealed that ATP and SOD levels were significantly decreased in non-healing wound tissues compared with normal skin (Fig. 1A), indicating impaired mitochondrial function and antioxidant defense. In parallel, non-healing wound tissues exhibited significantly reduced levels of regenerative and pro-survival factors including Timp1, EGF, and FGF, along with a marked increase in the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α (Fig. 1B). H&E staining revealed that the epidermis in normal skin was well-defined and intact, while the non-healing wound edges displayed a thinned or disrupted epidermal layer, with an absence of skin appendages such as hair follicles and sebaceous glands (Fig. 1C). Immunofluorescence analysis further showed significantly reduced expression of vimentin in non-healing wound tissues compared to normal controls (Fig. 1D). These data suggest that the microenvironment of non-healing wound-edge is characterized by metabolic stress, persistent inflammation, and a deficiency in pro-regenerative signals, which collectively impair fibroblast activity and tissue regeneration.

Topically applied MSC-derived mitochondria accelerate wound healing in a murine skin injury model

We next applied MSC-mt topically in a murine full-thickness wound model to evaluate their ability to restore redox balance and promote tissue repair. Prior to therapeutic application, we characterized MSC-mt with structural and functional assays. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) revealed that the isolated MSC-mt exhibited a relatively uniform size distribution, with an average diameter of 438.5 ± 199.3 nm (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further confirmed the presence of intact, double-membrane structures typical of functional mitochondria (Supplementary Fig. 1B). To assess the bioenergetic status of the isolated mitochondria, we measured mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) using TMRE staining followed by flow cytometry. Compared with mitochondria treated with the uncoupler CCCP, MSC-mt displayed significantly higher TMRE fluorescence intensity (Supplementary Fig. 1C), indicating preserved membrane potential and mitochondrial activity. Freshly isolated MSC-mt retained robust citrate synthase (CS) activity, whereas heat-inactivated MSC-mt showed minimal activity, confirming the enzymatic integrity of isolated MSC-mt (Supplementary Fig. 1D). Western blot analysis demonstrated the enrichment of key mitochondrial components in the isolated fractions, including OXPHOS complex proteins, the mitochondrial deubiquitinase USP30, and the outer membrane protein Tom20 (Supplementary Fig. 1E). Notably, PINK1 was detected on isolated MSC-mt, indicating that a proportion of the mitochondrial population retains mitophagy-associated signaling components (Supplementary Fig. 1E).

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of topically applied MSC-mt in promoting cutaneous wound healing, a full-thickness excisional wound model was established in mice, followed by surface administration of either low-dose (mtL) or high-dose (mtH) MSC-mt (Supplementary Fig. 1F). Wound closure was monitored and quantified over time. While the mtL group exhibited a trend toward improved wound healing compared to the control group, the mtH group showed a significantly enhanced wound closure rate (Fig. 1E–G). H&E staining showed that the wound area in the mtH group was markedly smaller than that in the control group on post-wounding day 8 (PWD8), as indicated by green outlines and arrows (Supplementary Fig. 1G). In the low-magnification view, black boxes highlight the central wound area, where epithelial coverage was observed in the mtH group (marked by red lines), but not in the control group. Blue boxes show higher magnification images of the wound region, revealing extensive lymphocyte infiltration in the control group, which was substantially reduced in the mtH group (Supplementary Fig. 1G). Consistent with the reduced inflammatory cell infiltration observed histologically, cytometric bead array (CBA) analysis revealed significantly lower levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-12p70, MCP-1, IFN-γ, and IL-6, in the mtH group compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 1H). Masson's trichrome staining on PWD8 further demonstrated enhanced collagen deposition in the mtH group compared to the control group (Fig. 1H–I). Together, these results indicate that topical mitochondrial treatment significantly accelerates wound healing in vivo.

Topical MSC-mt application alleviates oxidative stress and promotes tissue regeneration during wound healing

To further investigate the mechanism underlying MSC-mt–mediated wound healing, a series of biochemical and histological analyses were conducted on wound tissues. In vivo imaging showed that fluorescently labeled MSC-mt remained at the wound site for up to 4 days following topical application, whereas fluorescence signals were barely detectable by day 7 (Fig. 2A). On PWD8, the mtH-treated group exhibited a significant increase in ATP levels in peri-wound tissues compared with the control group, suggesting enhanced local metabolic activity (Fig. 2B). Conversely, malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, an indicator of lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress, were markedly reduced in mtH-treated wounds. Notably, this reduction was already evident at an early time point (PWD1; Supplementary Fig. 1I) and was sustained through PWD8 (Fig. 2C), indicating that MSC-mt rapidly attenuate oxidative damage in the wound microenvironment and maintain a lower oxidative burden during the healing process. Improved wound perfusion was confirmed in the mtH group by laser speckle contrast imaging (Fig. 2D), accompanied by enhanced neovascularization evidenced by increased CD31 expression (Fig. 2E) and upregulation of angiogenesis-related genes, including Ang, Ang2, CD31, and Hbegf (Fig. 2F). These vascular changes are known to provide essential support for fibroblast function and matrix production during tissue regeneration. In line with this, collagen deposition, a key process largely mediated by dermal fibroblasts, was significantly elevated in mtH-treated wounds, as demonstrated by increased Col1a1 expression (Fig. 2G and H). To directly assess fibroblast status, we performed double immunofluorescence staining for Vimentin (fibroblast marker) and TUNEL. The number of Vimentin+/TUNEL+ double-positive cells was significantly reduced in the mtH group, indicating that MSC-mt treatment effectively inhibited fibroblast apoptosis (Fig. 2I and J). Furthermore, co-staining for Vimentin and 8-hydroxyguanosine (8-OHG) revealed decreased oxidative DNA damage in fibroblasts following mtH treatment (Fig. 2K and L). Collectively, these results indicate that topical application of MSC-mt reduces oxidative stress, improves tissue perfusion, and promotes fibroblast survival and function.

MSC-mt protect fibroblasts from H2O2-induced oxidative damage and functional decline

Given the central role of fibroblasts in wound healing [11,12] and their impaired function observed in clinical non-healing wounds (Fig. 1B and D), along with the enhanced fibroblast survival following MSC-mt treatment in vivo (Fig. 2G–L), we next investigated the direct cellular effects of MSC-mt on fibroblasts under oxidative stress. To this end, we established an in vitro model using skin fibroblasts exposed to H2O2, a commonly used inducer of oxidative injury, to simulate the wound microenvironment. To establish an effective MSC-mt dose, skin fibroblasts under H2O2 were co-cultured with MSC-mt for 24 h across a gradient. ATP showed a bell-shaped response with a maximum at 0.67 μg/cm2, while higher doses (1.33–2.66 μg/cm2) reduced ATP (Supplementary Fig. 2A), indicating an optimal window; thus, 0.67 μg/cm2 was used for subsequent assays. Using this dose, MSC-mt elevated cellular ATP across a broad H2O2 range (125–1000 μM) at 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 2B), indicating robust bioenergetic support even under severe oxidative stress. We then quantified apoptosis by Annexin V/PI at defined time points following H2O2 challenge. MSC-mt treatment significantly reduced H2O2-induced apoptosis and cell death, whereas mitochondria pretreated with rotenone [(mt(R)], a mitochondrial respiratory chain inhibitor, exhibited weakened protective effects (Fig. 3A and B). To confirm that rotenone treatment indeed impaired mitochondrial function, we performed Western blot analysis, which revealed that mt(R) displayed decreased expression of OXPHOS components, PINK1, and TOM20 (Supplementary Fig. 2C). Consistent with the flow cytometry results, brightfield imaging at 90 and 180 min post-treatment showed marked cell shrinkage and morphological deterioration in the H2O2 group, which were alleviated by MSC-mt; this effect was notably attenuated when rotenone-treated mitochondria were used (Supplementary Fig. 2D). To assess mitochondrial function, mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) levels were measured using MitoSOX staining in live cells (gated by DAPI−) after 180 min of H2O2 exposure. MSC-mt co-treatment markedly reduced mtROS accumulation compared to H2O2 alone, while rotenone-treated MSC-mt failed to exert a similar antioxidative effect (Fig. 3C and D). In parallel, MSC-mt preserved ΔΨm under oxidative stress conditions, whereas rotenone-pretreated MSC-mt had diminished efficacy in preventing ΔΨm loss (Fig. 3E and F). H2O2 treatment significantly increased β-galactosidase activity, indicative of stress-induced premature senescence, which was markedly suppressed by MSC-mt. This anti-senescence effect was again impaired in the mt(R) group (Fig. 3G and H). Consistent with these findings, colony formation assays showed that H2O2 exposure severely impaired the clonogenic potential of fibroblasts, whereas MSC-mt restored colony-forming ability. This restorative effect was significantly attenuated by rotenone pretreatment (Fig. 3I). To explore underlying transcriptional changes, a wound healing PCR array was performed following 180 min of H2O2 stimulation. MSC-mt partially reversed the expression changes of H2O2-responsive genes (Fig. 3J), suggesting a broad regulatory impact at the gene level. In addition, a mouse apoptosis protein array revealed that several key anti-apoptotic proteins, including p-AKT (Ser473), p-BAD (Ser112), p-ERK (T202), and p-IκBα (S32), were downregulated upon H2O2 stimulation, but partially restored by MSC-mt treatment. Again, this restorative effect was reduced when using rotenone-pretreated mitochondria (Fig. 3K). Western blot confirmed a consistent trend of p-AKT (Ser473) downregulation following H2O2 stimulation, which was alleviated by MSC-mt but not by rotenone-treated MSC-mt (Fig. 3L). Together, these data demonstrate that MSC-mt confer robust protection against H2O2-induced oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, senescence, and apoptosis in fibroblasts, and that the protective effects rely on the bioenergetic integrity of the mitochondria.

Association between MSC-mt transfer and fibroblast survival

To explore the relationship between mitochondrial transfer and cell fate, we first examined the mechanisms by which MSC-mt are internalized by recipient cells. Previous studies have proposed several uptake pathways, including macropinocytosis, clathrin-mediated endocytosis, and dynamin-dependent endocytosis [4,13]. Based on these mechanisms, we selected a panel of pharmacological inhibitors targeting the corresponding pathways: EIPA and LY294002 (macropinocytosis), chloroquine (CQ; clathrin-mediated endocytosis), Dynasore (Dyn; dynamin-dependent endocytosis), and MNS, an inhibitor of Src and Syk kinases involved in membrane dynamics and vesicle trafficking. Flow cytometry analysis showed that only Dyn treatment significantly reduced mitochondrial transfer in recipient cells after 16 h of co-culture (Fig. 4A). Consistently, confocal imaging revealed a marked reduction in intracellular MitoTracker Green-labeled MSC-mt signal in CM-DiI–labeled L929 cells following Dyn treatment (Supplementary Fig. 3A), suggesting that dynamin-dependent processes play a primary role in MSC-mt internalization in L929 cells. Unexpectedly, apoptosis analysis showed that the proportion of viable cells (Annexin V−/PI− population) was increased in the Dyn-treated group, despite reduced MSC-mt uptake (Fig. 4B). Correlation analysis between mitochondrial transfer rate (MitoTracker Deep Red+ cells from Fig. 4A) and cell viability (Fig. 4B) revealed a significant inverse relationship (Fig. 4C). To further characterize this relationship, apoptotic rates were analyzed separately in mt transfer+ (MitoTracker Deep Red+) and mt transfer− (MitoTracker Deep Red−) subpopulations. Across all treatment groups, the mt transfer− population consistently exhibited a significantly higher proportion of viable cells (Fig. 4D), indicating that mitochondrial internalization was preferentially enriched in a cell subset with intrinsically lower survival capacity. To confirm these findings in a more physiologically relevant model, we repeated the experiments using mouse primary skin fibroblasts. Apoptosis was assessed after co-culture with MSC-mt for 90 and 180 min. MSC-mt treatment alleviated H2O2-induced apoptosis in fibroblasts, while mt(R) showed weakened protection. Notably, Dyn treatment further enhanced the protective effect of MSC-mt, resulting in reduced apoptosis at both time points (Fig. 4E and F). Flow cytometry confirmed that Dyn significantly inhibited mitochondrial transfer in skin fibroblasts (Fig. 4G). When comparing apoptosis levels in mt transfer+ and mt transfer− fibroblast populations (gated based on Fig. 4G), we again observed that mt transfer− cells had a significantly higher survival rate across all conditions (Fig. 4H and I). To further assess whether the cytoprotective effects of MSC-mt are consistent across different oxidative stress models and modulated by mitochondrial integrity or uptake, we conducted additional experiments using two established inducers of oxidative damage: PX-12, a thioredoxin pathway inhibitor, and rotenone, a mitochondrial complex I inhibitor. In both models, MSC-mt significantly reduced fibroblast apoptosis, an effect that was attenuated by rotenone pretreatment but consistently enhanced by co-treatment with Dyn (Supplementary Fig. 3B). MitoSOX staining revealed that MSC-mt effectively reduced mtROS accumulation under rotenone stress, and this effect was further potentiated by Dyn treatment (Supplementary Fig. 3C). Collectively, these results reveal a reproducible inverse association between MSC-mt transfer and cell survival, with mitochondrial internalization being preferentially observed in a subpopulation of cells exhibiting reduced viability.

Physical or pharmacological blockade of mitochondrial internalization enhances the cytoprotective effects of MSC-mt

To further determine whether the protective effects of MSC-mt require internalization by recipient cells, we employed both pharmacological and physical approaches to restrict mitochondrial entry under oxidative stress conditions. As illustrated in the experimental design (Fig. 5A), L929 cells were treated with H2O2 and co-cultured with MSC-mt either directly or with mitochondrial entry blocked by Dynasore (Dyn), or physically separated using Transwell chambers. In the H2O2+(mt) group, mitochondria were placed in the upper chamber while cells and H2O2 were in the lower chamber; in the (H2O2+mt) group, both H2O2 and MSC-mt were placed in the upper chamber. Given that H2O2 (molecular weight ∼34 Da) can freely diffuse through the 400 nm Transwell membrane, but most mitochondria are larger than 400 nm (Supplementary Fig. 1A), this setup should effectively exclude direct mitochondrial-cell contact or internalization. Flow cytometry analysis showed that direct MSC-mt treatment significantly increased the proportion of live cells following H2O2 exposure, while Dyn, H2O2+(mt), and (H2O2+mt) groups demonstrated even higher survival rates, with the (H2O2+mt) group showing the most pronounced protective effect (Fig. 5B and C). Correspondingly, MSC-mt signals in recipient cells were markedly reduced by Dyn and were nearly undetectable in both Transwell conditions (Fig. 5D), confirming effective blockade of mitochondrial internalization. Interestingly, when apoptosis was analyzed within mt transfer+ and mt transfer− subpopulations, mt transfer− cells consistently exhibited higher viability than mt transfer+ cells, further supporting the notion that internalization may not be necessary for protection (Fig. 5E). Consistent with these findings, MitoSOX staining revealed that MSC-mt treatment reduced H2O2-induced mitochondrial ROS accumulation, but this effect was even greater in the Dyn and Transwell groups, particularly in the (H2O2+mt) group (Fig. 5F and G). To investigate whether MSC-mt could directly scavenge extracellular ROS, we measured H2O2 concentrations in the culture supernatant. At 30 min, the (H2O2+mt) group showed a significant reduction in H2O2 levels (Fig. 5H). By 60 min, both the direct MSC-mt group and the (H2O2+mt) group exhibited significantly lower H2O2 concentrations (Fig. 5I), suggesting that MSC-mt can directly neutralize extracellular H2O2, independent of cellular uptake. Taken together, these results demonstrate that blocking mitochondrial entry not only fails to reduce the cytoprotective effects of MSC-mt, but may even enhance them, likely through extracellular ROS scavenging mechanisms.

Physical immobilization of MSC-mt in thermosensitive hydrogel impairs their cytoprotective function

To investigate whether restricting the spatial mobility of MSC-mt affects their functionality, we employed a clinically approved thermosensitive hydrogel (“intelligel”, Horizon International (Beijing) Medical Devices Co., Ltd, NMPA registration number (Beijing): 20202140315), composed primarily of hydroxybutyl chitosan, glycerol, and purified water, which transitions from a liquid state at 4 °C to a solid state at 37 °C. (Fig. 6A). Transmission electron microscopy confirmed that the overall morphology of mitochondria remained intact after suspension in the gel matrix (Fig. 6B). CCK-8 assays revealed no detectable cytotoxicity of hydrogel or hydrogel-embedded MSC-mt in L929, indicating good biocompatibility (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Release assays with MitoTracker Green–labeled MSC-mt showed that 20% and 50% gels significantly reduced mitochondrial release, whereas 10% gel did not, indicating a diffusion threshold (Fig. 6C). In vivo, we topically applied free mitochondria (mtH), 10% gel + mt, or 50% gel + mt to full-thickness mouse skin wounds. The 10% gel + mt group significantly accelerated wound closure and modestly outperformed free mtH (Fig. 6D–F). By contrast, 50% gel + mtH lagged behind both 10% gel + mtH and mtH, with larger residual wound areas and slower closure throughout the observation period (Fig. 6D–F). On PWD7, laser speckle contrast imaging showed comparable perfusion in 10% gel + mtH and mtH, whereas 50% gel + mtH was significantly lower (Fig. 6G). H&E sections revealed more continuous epithelial coverage and fewer inflammatory infiltrates in 10% gel + mt and mtH, while 50% gel + mtH displayed wider wound gaps and denser inflammatory cells (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Masson's trichrome staining showed greater edge collagen deposition in 10% gel + mtH and mtH compared with 50% gel + mtH (Supplementary Fig. 4C–D). In vivo imaging further showed that 50% gel + mtH increased early (1 h) mitochondrial signal at the wound site, but this advantage dissipated by days 1, 4, and 7 (Fig. 6H), suggesting that high-concentration hydrogel transiently traps mitochondria locally while limiting their subsequent dispersion into surrounding tissue dispersion into surrounding tissue. To explore the mechanism underlying this loss of function, we simulated oxidative stress in vitro by treating L929 cells with H2O2 (400 μM) and co-culturing them with either free or 50% gel-embedded MSC-mt. H2O2 quantification in the culture supernatant revealed that ROS clearance was markedly reduced in the gel + mt group compared to the free mt group, with residual H2O2 levels comparable to those observed in the H2O2+(mt) group, where mitochondria were physically separated from cells using a Transwell insert (Fig. 6I). Flow cytometry analysis showed that intracellular mitochondrial signal was significantly reduced in the gel + mt group (Fig. 6J and K). DCFH-DA staining demonstrated that while free MSC-mt effectively attenuated H2O2-induced ROS accumulation in L929 cells, this effect was abolished when mitochondria were embedded in gel (Fig. 6L and M). Together, these results reinforce the concept that MSC-mt function as metabolically active ROS scavengers whose cytoprotective effects depend on sufficient spatial freedom to access and neutralize oxidative stress in the extracellular environment.

MSC-mt internalization is associated with enhanced mitophagy under oxidative stress

While the preceding results demonstrate that MSC-mt exert cytoprotective and antioxidative effects independent of cellular uptake, the intracellular consequences of mitochondrial internalization under oxidative stress remain to be clarified. Given previous reports that exogenous mitochondria may influence mitophagy [14,15], we investigated whether internalized MSC-mt activate mitophagy in recipient cells under oxidative stress. Flow cytometric analysis of fluorescently labeled MSC-mt co-cultured with L929 cells revealed that H2O2 stimulation alone induced mitophagy, and this response was further enhanced by MSC-mt treatment. Notably, mitophagy activity was significantly higher in the mt transfer+ population than in mt transfer− cells (Fig. 7A and B), indicating a close association between mitochondrial internalization and mitophagy activation. To further characterize the molecular features of these populations, mt transfer+ and mt transfer− cells were isolated by flow sorting and analyzed by Western blotting. The mt transfer+ fraction exhibited significantly higher levels of phosphorylated PINK1 (S228) and Parkin compared with mt transfer− cells, whereas total p62 and phosphorylated p62 at S349 and S403 were markedly reduced (Fig. 7C and D). These changes are consistent with an actively engaged and functionally executed PINK1–Parkin–mediated mitophagy program accompanied by effective p62 consumption. In line with our earlier apoptosis analyses, the mt transfer+ population also showed reduced expression of the pro-survival signaling molecule pAKT (Fig. 7C and D). To dissect the respective contributions of mitochondrial-derived and recipient-derived PINK1, we first validated PINK1 knockdown efficiency in both donor MSCs and recipient L929 cells. Transfection of MSCs with siPINK1 markedly reduced PINK1 expression at the protein level prior to mitochondrial isolation (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Similarly, siRNA-mediated knockdown of PINK1 in L929 cells resulted in a substantial reduction of PINK1 expression, as confirmed by qPCR and immunoblotting analyses (Supplementary Fig. 5B–C). When PINK1-deficient MSC-mt (siPINK1-mt) were co-cultured with H2O2-treated L929 cells, mitophagy activation was significantly attenuated across the total population as well as within both mt transfer+ and mt transfer− subpopulations (Fig. 7E). In contrast, knockdown of PINK1 in recipient L929 cells did not abolish MSC-mt–induced mitophagy, which remained particularly prominent in the mt transfer+ fraction (Supplementary Fig. 5D), indicating that mitochondrial-derived PINK1 plays a dominant role in triggering mitophagy under oxidative stress. To determine whether mitochondrial internalization represents a passive process or a stress-responsive event, we quantified MSC-mt transfer across a gradient of H2O2 concentrations. Mitochondrial internalization remained low at 50–250 μM H2O2 but increased markedly at 500 μM and 1000 μM. Flow cytometric analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between H2O2 concentration and intracellular MSC-mt signal (Supplementary Fig. 5E–F), indicating that mitochondrial transfer preferentially enriched in cells experiencing more severe oxidative injury. Consistent with these findings, TEM revealed an increased number of mitochondria with fragmented morphology in the H2O2 + MSC-mt group (Supplementary Fig. 5G). To further dissect the relationship between mitochondrial damage and mitophagy, we assessed depolarized mitochondria using mitoPeDPP staining in parallel with mitophagy measurements. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that H2O2 treatment markedly increased mitoPeDPP signal, indicating accumulation of damaged mitochondria, whereas MSC-mt co-culture significantly reduced mitoPeDPP levels while further enhancing mitophagy (Fig. 7F). Flow cytometric quantification confirmed these observations. Compared with H2O2 alone, MSC-mt treatment significantly increased the proportion and mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of mitophagy-positive cells, while concomitantly reducing the proportion and MFI of mitoPeDPP-positive cells (Fig. 7G–J). Importantly, the proportion of cells double-positive for mitoPeDPP and mitophagy was comparable between the H2O2 and H2O2+MSC-mt groups, whereas MSC-mt treatment selectively shifted cells toward a mitophagy-positive, mitoPeDPP-negative phenotype. Stratification by mitochondrial transfer status further revealed that mitophagy activity was significantly enriched in mt transfer+ cells, while mitoPeDPP signals were also higher in mt transfer+ cells than in mt transfer− cells, indicating that mitochondrial internalization preferentially occurred in cells harboring a higher burden of damaged mitochondria. Collectively, these results indicate that MSC-mt internalization under oxidative stress is selectively enriched in a subpopulation of cells with elevated mitochondrial damage and active mitophagy. This pattern supports the interpretation that mitochondrial uptake reflects a damage-responsive, compensatory process associated with PINK1–Parkin–mediated mitochondrial quality control.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that topically applied MSC-mt significantly improved wound healing in a murine full-thickness skin injury model. MSC-mt treatment enhanced wound closure, promoted angiogenesis and collagen deposition, and protected dermal fibroblasts from oxidative stress and apoptosis. Mechanistically, our findings revealed a novel and counterintuitive insight: mitochondrial internalization by recipient cells was not essential for cytoprotection. Instead, MSC-mt primarily act extracellularly, with spatial accessibility emerging as a key determinant of their therapeutic function. Nevertheless, when internalization did occur under severe oxidative stress, MSC-mt preferentially activated the PINK1–Parkin-mediated mitophagy pathway, indicating a context-dependent intracellular quality-control response driven largely by mitochondrial-derived PINK1. Collectively, these results uncover an underappreciated extracellular mode of action for MSC-mt and underscore the necessity for therapeutic delivery strategies that preserve mitochondrial bioactivity and spatial accessibility.

Wound healing is a highly metabolically demanding process, with mitochondria playing a central role in cellular energy supply and redox homeostasis. Literature highlights the involvement of mitochondria in various aspects of wound repair and chronic wound pathogenesis, including the regulation of cellular metabolism, apoptosis, and oxidative stress signaling [16]. Consistent with this, our analysis of pediatric non-healing wound-edge tissues revealed marked reductions in ATP and SOD levels, accompanied by decreased regenerative growth factors and elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines. The downregulation of TIMP1 and vimentin further suggests compromised fibroblast function and impaired matrix remodeling at the wound edge. Similar features of mitochondrial dysfunction, metabolic insufficiency, and persistent oxidative stress have been reported in other chronic wound contexts, including diabetic and age-associated ulcers, supporting mitochondrial dysregulation as a shared vulnerability across etiologically diverse non-healing wounds [[16], [17], [18]].

Given the central role of mitochondrial dysfunction in chronic wound pathogenesis, restoring mitochondrial homeostasis represents a promising therapeutic strategy. Mitochondrial transplantation, traditionally aimed at replenishing cellular bioenergetics via uptake by recipient cells, has shown potential in mitigating oxidative stress and cellular dysfunction in injured tissues [13,19]. Unlike whole-cell transplantation, which carries risks such as immune rejection, tumorigenicity, and pulmonary embolism, isolated mitochondria represent a safer and more controllable alternative, avoiding many of the limitations associated with cell therapy [20]. Both local and systemic mitochondrial transplantation have shown therapeutic benefits across various animal models, and as of January 2024, eight actively recruiting clinical trials are listed on ClinicalTrials.gov investigating mitochondrial transplantation for conditions such as myocardial ischemia, cerebral ischemia, and inflammatory myopathies [4]. The limited efficiency of mitochondrial uptake by recipient cells has long been recognized as a major obstacle to fully harnessing the therapeutic potential of mitochondrial transplantation, prompting extensive efforts such as surface modification, carrier-assisted delivery, and recipient cell preconditioning to enhance internalization efficiency [15,21,22]. However, our study highlights the potential of MSC-mt as an effective cell-free therapeutic agent that does not require cellular internalization to exert protective effects. In our murine skin wound model, simple topical application of MSC-mt, without the need for injection or systemic delivery, was sufficient to significantly accelerate wound healing. This approach minimizes invasiveness, enables localized and repeatable administration, and simplifies storage and dosing, thereby enhancing its translational potential for chronic wound therapy.

Fibroblasts play a fundamental role in skin wound healing by orchestrating extracellular matrix production, wound contraction, and crosstalk with immune and endothelial cells [23]. Mitochondrial ROS levels in fibroblasts modulate the expression of key nuclear genes involved in wound healing and oxidative stress, highlighting the potential of targeting mitochondrial ROS regulation as a natural redox-based approach for promoting repair in chronic wounds [24]. Prompted by our clinical observation of compromised fibroblast activity and matrix remodeling in chronic non-healing wound-edge tissues, we investigated the impact of MSC-mt on fibroblast function. In vivo, MSC-mt treatment significantly attenuated fibroblast apoptosis and oxidative DNA damage. In vitro, MSC-mt preserved fibroblast viability, mitochondrial function, and proliferative capacity, while suppressing senescence and restoring key survival signaling pathways. These protective effects were markedly reduced when MSC-mt were pretreated with rotenone, underscoring the importance of their bioenergetic competence. These findings differ from a recent report, which showed that dysfunctional mitochondria lacking mtDNA remained capable of supporting endothelial repair, suggesting that functional respiration was not essential and that mitochondria may act as signaling triggers rather than metabolic organelles [14]. This discrepancy likely reflects cell type–specific demands: endothelial cells mainly rely on mitochondria as signaling triggers for processes like angiogenesis, whereas fibroblasts in wounded tissue face intense oxidative stress and depend on functionally intact mitochondria to scavenge ROS, sustain ΔΨm, and deliver metabolic and anti-apoptotic support.

A central and unexpected finding of our study is that MSC-mt exert their cytoprotective effects largely through extracellular mechanisms, rather than relying on cellular internalization. Several lines of evidence support this view. First, pharmacological inhibition of mitochondrial transfer with Dynasore or physical blockade using Transwell chambers did not attenuate, but instead enhanced, the protective effects of MSC-mt against oxidative injury. Second, cells that internalized MSC-mt (mt transfer+) consistently exhibited greater oxidative damage and reduced viability, indicating that mitochondrial transfer preferentially occurs in a more severely injured subpopulation rather than being required for cytoprotection. Third, MSC-mt significantly reduced extracellular H2O2 levels within minutes of co-culture, even in the absence of direct contact with recipient cells. Fourth, excessive immobilization of MSC-mt in a thermosensitive hydrogel blunted their efficacy in a concentration-dependent manner. At high gel density (50%), mitochondrial release into the wound milieu was markedly restricted, resulting in impaired H2O2 clearance, sustained intracellular ROS accumulation, and diminished cytoprotective effects in vivo. Notably, a permissive low-density (10%) hydrogel preserved mitochondrial mobility and modestly enhanced wound healing compared with freely applied mitochondria, supporting a threshold-dependent relationship in which partial confinement enables local enrichment and prolonged extracellular presence without restricting access to spatially distributed oxidative stress. Accordingly, these findings provide for biomaterial-assisted mitochondrial delivery, suggesting that effective systems should preserve sufficient extracellular mobility while enabling controlled local confinement. Together, these findings indicate that MSC-mt act as metabolically intact and spatially dynamic extracellular ROS scavengers whose cytoprotective efficacy depends on sufficient extracellular mobility. Consistent with this interpretation, loss of cytoprotection following rotenone pretreatment supports a requirement for mitochondrial functional integrity, while the specific molecular contributors to extracellular ROS scavenging remain to be defined.

While MSC-mt exert robust cytoprotective effects primarily through extracellular ROS scavenging, we further show that mitochondrial internalization engages a distinct intracellular program under severe oxidative stress. Specifically, MSC-mt transfer was selectively enriched in cells exhibiting higher oxidative burden, and these mt transfer+ cells displayed markedly enhanced PINK1–Parkin–dependent mitophagy. This response was characterized by increased PINK1 phosphorylation, Parkin accumulation, and effective consumption of the autophagy receptor p62, indicating a fully executed mitophagic flux rather than passive mitochondrial accumulation. Importantly, genetic dissection revealed that this mitophagy response was predominantly driven by mitochondrial-derived PINK1. Knockdown of PINK1 in donor MSCs significantly attenuated mitophagy induction, whereas depletion of PINK1 in recipient cells did not abolish MSC-mt–mediated mitophagy, particularly within the mt transfer+ population. These findings suggest that MSC-mt not only serve as substrates for quality control but also actively supply signaling components that initiate the mitophagic cascade in stressed recipient cells. Notably, mt transfer+ cells exhibited higher mitophagy activity yet reduced pro-survival signaling, consistent with their greater mitochondrial damage burden. This observation supports a model in which mitochondrial internalization represents an adaptive, damage-responsive response preferentially engaged by severely injured cells attempting to restore mitochondrial quality. In contrast, cells with lower oxidative stress (mt transfer−) primarily benefited from extracellular ROS buffering by MSC-mt and did not require intracellular mitochondrial engagement. Together, these results delineate a dual-tier response to MSC-mt treatment, whereby extracellular mitochondrial activity provides broad cytoprotection, while selective mitochondrial internalization triggers PINK1–Parkin–mediated mitophagy as a secondary, cell-intrinsic quality control mechanism.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although we incorporated human wound-edge tissues to strengthen clinical relevance, these samples were derived from pediatric patients at a single center and represent heterogeneous trauma- or burn-related non-healing wounds; thus, cohort size and etiologic diversity limit generalization to other major chronic wound settings such as diabetic foot ulcers, venous leg ulcers, or pressure injuries. Future studies should validate the metabolic and cytokine signatures, as well as therapeutic responsiveness, in larger multi-center cohorts and in clinically representative animal models. Second, while transfer-independence is supported by convergent pharmacological (Dynasore), physical (Transwell), and diffusion-restriction (hydrogel immobilization) approaches, each carries intrinsic limitations. Pharmacological inhibitors may have off-target effects, and fluorescence-based readouts of mitochondrial “transfer” or intracellular signal do not fully resolve bona fide cytosolic internalization from membrane association or vesicular sequestration. Likewise, diffusion-restriction strategies such as hydrogel immobilization may introduce carrier-dependent physical constraints, underscoring the value of more granular carrier-only controls. More definitive in vivo tracking and ultrastructural localization strategies, together with genetic perturbations of uptake pathways, will further strengthen causal inference. Third, while extracellular ROS scavenging by MSC-mt is supported at a functional level, the specific molecular contributors were not resolved. The relative roles of individual mitochondrial antioxidant enzymes or oxidative phosphorylation–associated redox processes remain to be defined. Fourth, although we identify a stress-contingent mitophagy program triggered upon mitochondrial internalization, the downstream consequences of this response (beneficial quality control versus excessive mitophagy) likely depend on injury severity, timing, and cell state; the boundary conditions governing this switch remain to be systematically mapped. Finally, important translational considerations remain to be addressed, including mitochondrial manufacturing consistency, stability over clinically relevant storage and handling windows, optimal dosing regimens, and long-term safety. In particular, although extracellular MSC-mt administration did not elicit overt inflammatory exacerbation in the models examined here, the potential for innate immune sensing of extracellular mitochondria, context-dependent inflammatory signaling, and effects associated with repeated or prolonged exposure were not systematically evaluated and warrant dedicated investigation in future preclinical studies.

Conclusion

Our data reveal a two-tiered mode of action for mesenchymal-stem-cell–derived mitochondria in cutaneous repair. Extracellularly, freely diffusing MSC-mt act as potent “ROS sponges,” rapidly buffering oxidative stress and creating a pro-healing microenvironment—an effect that is lost when mitochondrial mobility is constrained. Intracellularly, selective transfer by severely injured cells triggers PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy, providing a secondary, damage-contingent repair pathway. Together, these findings challenge the prevailing notion that therapeutic benefit requires high mitochondrial internalization and instead emphasize dispersion‐focused delivery strategies that preserve mitochondrial bioactivity and spatial freedom as a promising, cell-free approach for chronic wound management. Accordingly, any biomaterial co-delivery should be engineered to preserve mitochondrial mobility and extracellular access, prioritizing dispersion and controlled release over immobilization.
