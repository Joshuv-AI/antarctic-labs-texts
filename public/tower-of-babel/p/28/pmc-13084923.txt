Emotional well-being at diagnosis may indicate future mental health risk in patients with juvenile idiopathic arthritis: data from the German inception cohort ICON
Authors: Prasad T Oommen, Jens Klotsche, Rainer Berendes, Frank Dressler, Ivan Foeldvari, Dirk Foell, Johannes-Peter Haas, Gerd Horneff, Anton Hospach, Tilmann Kallinich, Jasmin Kuemmerle-Deschner, Frank Weller-Heinemann, Daniel Windschall, Kirsten Minden, Claudia Sengler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo determine the prevalence of depressive and anxiety symptoms among young people 7 years and 9 years after inclusion in the multicentre, prospective inception cohort (ICON) of newly diagnosed patients with juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) in Germany, and to identify factors associated with mental health problems at study inclusion and in the course of the disease.MethodsPatients and controls (healthy peers, eg, friends of the same age and sex) from the ICON cohort (both ≥13 years) were assessed for mental health using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 and the Generalised Anxiety Disorder Scale-7 at 7-year and 9-year follow-ups. Demographic and clinical characteristics, treatments and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) (Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL)) were documented at baseline and follow-up visits. Cross-sectional (analysis of variance or χ² tests at 7-year/9-year follow-up) and longitudinal analyses (generalised linear mixed models for PedsQL in follow-up from baseline) were conducted, respectively.ResultsA total of 344 patients (age 18.7±3.5 years, disease duration 9.2±1.8 years, 42% polyarthritis) and 224 controls (age 18.2±3.6 years) were evaluated. Moderate to severe symptoms of depression and anxiety were present in 13% and 10% of patients, respectively, compared with 7% and 2% of controls. Patients with moderate to severe psychological distress did not exhibit significantly higher physician-reported disease activity at inclusion and follow-up but reported worse patient-reported outcomes. These patients already showed reduced emotional functioning 3 months after diagnosis (p<0.001) and reported lower physical and emotional functioning (p<0.001) after the first year of specialised care compared with those without relevant mental health problems at long-term follow-up.ConclusionPoor emotional functioning at the start of care for JIA may be an indicator of future mental health issues. Therefore, HRQoL should be routinely assessed at treatment initiation to identify at-risk patients early and provide targeted support.Keywords: Arthritis, Juvenile; Epidemiology; Patient Reported Outcome Measures; Psychology; Outcome Assessment, Health Care

Introduction

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is the most common chronic inflammatory rheumatic disease in children and adolescents, with onset before age 16. It encompasses a heterogeneous group of disorders,1 primarily affecting the joints but potentially involving extra-articular organs such as eyes and skin. New therapeutic concepts and the availability of biological and targeted synthetic disease modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) have substantially improved the management and outcomes of JIA during the past two decades.2 3 Non-pharmacological therapies, including physiotherapy and occupational therapy, psychosocial support and physical activity, complement pharmacological treatment.4

Despite these advancements, JIA remains a chronic condition, requiring ongoing medical care and monitoring to prevent disease-related damage and manage potential short-term and long-term drug side effects. Children and adolescents with chronic rheumatic diseases still often experience a variety of burdens, such as pain or functional impairments, and their social lives can be affected by time-consuming therapies and doctor’s appointments.

Adolescents face additional challenges during the transition from paediatric to adult rheumatology care involving developmental, emotional, medical and structural factors. A poor transition process may lead to disease flare-ups and loss of formerly stable remission,5 considering that at least half of patients with JIA still have active disease in adulthood, and the majority of these patients require treatment with DMARDs.6

These challenges, along with the burden of chronic illness, contribute to the perception that young people with JIA experience higher rates of depression and anxiety than their healthy peers. However, studies dealing with psychosocial health of patients with JIA from the past 20 years show different, sometimes conflicting, results.7

The aim of our analysis was to determine the prevalence of depressive and anxiety symptoms in patients with JIA compared with control subjects who both had been regularly evaluated for their health since diagnosis or for at least 7 years in the inception cohort of newly diagnosed patients with JIA (ICON) and to assess correlating factors.

Methods

Patients

ICON is a prospective, multicentre observational study that has been conducted at 11 paediatric rheumatology centres in Germany from 2010 to 2021. Children and adolescents diagnosed with JIA in the previous 12 months were eligible for inclusion (for further details on ICON, see Sengler et al8).

A comparison group of healthy peers, such as same-age and same-sex friends from day care or school, was also simultaneously recruited for ICON from the start and surveyed annually.

Children (from the age of 8 years) and their parents gave their informed consent to participate in the ICON study.

Measurements

After inclusion in ICON, follow-up examinations were carried out every 3 months in the first year and every 6 months thereafter. In addition to demographic parameters (age, sex), clinical parameters, such as JIA category according to the criteria of the International League of Associations for Rheumatology (ILAR, 1), age at disease onset, diagnosis and laboratory parameters (eg, antinuclear antibodies (ANA), rheumatoid factor, human leukocyte antigen-B27 (HLA-B27)), at each visit, number of active joints, physician’s global assessment of disease activity (PhGA) on a 21-point Numerical Rating Scale (NRS; 0–10, 0=inactive) and information on therapy were documented, and also patient-reported outcomes, such as the patient’s global assessment of overall well-being (PGA, NRS 0–10, 0=best), pain (NRS; 0–10, 0=no pain), fatigue (NRS; 0–10, 0=no fatigue) and functional limitations (Childhood Health Assessment Questionnaire (scores 0–3, 0=no functional limitations for patients <18 years) and Health Assessment Questionnaire (scores 0–3, 0=no functional limitations, for patients >18 years),9,11 were recorded. Furthermore, patients and healthy peers aged 8 years up to the age of 17 years assessed their health-related quality of life (HRQoL) at each visit using the German version of the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) generic core scales 4.0.12 13 This questionnaire consists of 23 items across four subscales: physical functioning, emotional functioning, social functioning and school functioning. Responses are recorded on a 4-point Likert scale (1=best function (never), 4=worst function (always)) and subsequently reversely scored and linearly transformed to a 0–100 scale, with higher scores indicating better HRQoL.

The socioeconomic status (SES) was determined using an established German multidimensional aggregated index.14 The calculation of this index had to be modified because the parents’ work status had not been collected in the ICON study. Therefore, it was based only on parental education level (including schooling and vocational training) and net household income. For details, we refer to the work of Listing et al.15 The lower and upper quartiles of the sum of education and income score were used as cut-off points to define low, middle and high SES.

During the ICON cohort follow-up, additional questionnaires—including the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)16 17 and the Generalised Anxiety Disorder Scale-7 (GAD-718)—were administered. We chose to conduct the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 questionnaires at the follow-up 7 years and 9 years after inclusion in ICON because, first, patients and controls must be at least 13 years old for these tests, and second, we assumed that we might be able to see a possible impact of chronic illness on mental health more clearly after a longer course of the disease. So, these instruments were first applied to participants on reaching 13 years of age and completing either the 7-year or 9-year follow-up. If a participant completed the questionnaires at both time points, data from the most recent follow-up were used for analysis. PHQ-9 and GAD-7 data for each patient were always taken from the same time point for the analysis presented here.

Patient Health Questionnaire-9

The PHQ-9 comprises nine items aligned with the diagnostic criteria for depression as outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders. Developed as a screening tool for routine use in somatic-medical settings,16 17 it evaluates the frequency of depressive symptoms experienced by respondents over the preceding 2 weeks. Responses are scored as follows: 0 (‘Not at all’), 1 (‘On some days’), 2 (‘On more than half of the days’) and 3 (‘Almost every day’). The total score, ranging from 0 to 27, is interpreted as follows: 1–4=minimal depressive symptomatology, 5–9=mild, 10–14=moderate, 15–19=moderately severe and 20–27 severe depressive symptomatology.

Generalised Anxiety Disorder Scale-7

The GAD-7 was developed to identify individuals with generalised anxiety disorder and to assess the severity of anxiety symptoms.18 19 It consists of seven questions that evaluate the frequency of the seven core symptoms of generalised anxiety disorder by the respondent over the past 2 weeks. Responses are categorised as follows: ‘not at all’, ‘on some days’, ‘on more than half of the days’ and ‘almost every day’, corresponding to numerical values of 0–3. The total score ranges from 0 to 21, with the following interpretations: 0–4: minimal anxiety symptoms, 5–9: mild, 10–14: moderate, 15–21: severe anxiety symptoms.

Statistics

Descriptive statistics (absolute/relative frequencies for categorical variables and mean/SD for continuously distributed variables) were used to describe the distributions of mental health assessment (eg, PHQ-9 and GAD-7), sociodemographic variables, disease activity parameters reported by the paediatric rheumatologist, patient-reported outcomes such as pain or PedsQL and treatment in patients and controls at the time of mental health assessment as well as at study enrolment. Patients and controls included in this study were compared with those who did not meet the inclusion criteria based on sociodemographic and clinical parameters at ICON enrolment. Two-sample t-tests and χ2 tests (categorical variables) were used in order to compare the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores between patients and controls (not matched) as well as sociodemographic characteristics in patients and controls and clinical characteristics in patients with moderate to severe versus no to mild symptoms. We also analysed whether there were differences in the scores of the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 completed by patients and controls before the start of the COVID-19 pandemic (before 1 March 2020) compared with the scores of these questionnaires from 1 March 2020 onwards. Furthermore, we compared the longitudinal course of the scores for the PedsQL 4.0 subscales ‘emotional functioning’ and ‘physical functioning’ of both patients and healthy controls from inclusion in ICON to administration of the PHQ-9 7/9 years later in order to test whether the mean values differ across time between patients and controls by generalised linear mixed models. This analysis was performed for patients with a PHQ-9 score <10, patients with a PHQ-9 score ≥10, control subjects with a PHQ-9 score <10 and control subjects with a PHQ-9 score ≥10.

Generalised linear mixed models were applied to estimate the course of PedsQL emotional and physical functioning for patients and controls, the number of active joints (for patients only) and the PGA (for patients only), including all available intermediate visits from baseline to assessment of PHQ-9 7/9 years later (factors time) and PHQ-9 ≥10 versus PHQ-9 <10. All models were adjusted for ILAR category, age and sex. The resulting marginal means were estimated to obtain population-level estimates and 95% CIs for the parameters of interest and to test for differences between patients and controls as well as for PHQ-9 ≥10 versus PHQ-9 <10 at each follow-up.

Receiver operating characteristics curves were used to determine the optimal cut-off for PedsQL emotional functioning at baseline that best discriminated between PHQ-9 scores <10 and ≥10, balancing sensitivity and specificity.

Missing values were not imputed, because of the low rate of missing values in considered parameters at baseline and 7-year follow-up and 9-year follow-up (each <5%).

Statistical analyses were performed with use of SAS V.9.33 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

Results

Patients and controls

Of all participants included in ICON, 344 patients and 224 controls (88% and 79% of eligible participants, respectively, figure 1) completed the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 questionnaires 7 years or 9 years after inclusion. These patients and controls did not significantly differ in sociodemographic and clinical characteristics from those who did not meet the inclusion criteria. Patient and control characteristics are summarised in table 1. Compared with the overall ICON cohort,15 the study sample included a slightly higher proportion of patients with enthesitis-related arthritis (16.6% vs 10.5%) and a lower proportion of patients with oligoarthritis (38.7% vs 46.3%).

Mental health of patients and controls at the 7-year or 9-year follow-up

Overall, 13.4% of patients showed moderate to severe symptoms of depression, a proportion twice as high as in controls (6.7%). Moderate to severe anxiety symptoms were seen in 10.2% of patients, compared with only 2.2% of controls (figure 2, online supplemental table 1). A total of 8.1% of patients had both moderate to severe symptoms of depression and anxiety. The correlations of PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores were 0.85 in patients and 0.76 in controls.

HRQoL in patients and controls over time and its association with mental health at follow-up

At enrolment in the ICON study, patients had significantly lower HRQoL than the control group (PedsQL score 77.1±15.4 vs 91.4±10.5, p<0.001), which included both physical (73.9±20.4 vs 94.9±9.5, p<0.001) and emotional functioning (75.8±21.0 vs 87.1±14.3, p<0.001). In patients, the most substantial improvements in HRQoL occurred within the first year of care (online supplemental table 4 and figure 3).

Patients who later exhibited moderate to severe depressive symptoms showed no significant difference in physical functioning at ICON inclusion compared with patients without these symptoms (69.3±19.3 vs 74.7±20.5, p=0.114), and the same was true for the two control groups (95.2±9.4 vs 91.3±9.8, p=0.122).

Patients with moderate to severe depressive symptoms at follow-up already showed significantly lower emotional functioning at inclusion in the ICON study (66.1±25.5) than patients with PHQ-9 scores <10 (77.5±19.8, p≤0.001), but the same applied to the two control groups (79.3±17.4 vs 87.8±13.9, p=0.035, online supplemental table 4).

Longitudinal analysis showed the estimated change in physical and emotional functioning in patients and controls, stratified by PHQ-9 ≥10 vs <10 at follow-up (figure 3).

While patients with later PHQ-9 scores <10 showed significant improvement in their perception of physical functioning within the first years of specialised care, eventually reaching a level comparable to healthy peers, patients with significant mental health problems reported less improvement, with lower scores at 1 year follow-up and minimal change thereafter (figure 3a,b).

Both patient groups (PHQ-9 <10, PHQ-9 ≥10) improved from baseline to the 1 year follow-up to a similar extent (with 12 and 9.6 points mean difference) in the PedsQL Scale ‘emotional functioning’ and achieved values that no longer differed significantly from or were even better than those of the corresponding controls (figure 3c,d). The two control groups (PHQ-9 <10, PHQ-9 ≥10) showed no relevant change in the assessment of this subscale between the two time points.

From the 1 year follow-up onwards, the patients (PHQ-9 <10 vs PHQ-9 ≥10) no longer differed from the corresponding controls (PHQ-9 <10 vs PHQ-9 ≥10) on the emotional functioning scale (figure 3c,d). A decrease in the score on the emotional functioning scale with age was found in participants (both patients and controls) with mental health problems (figure 3d).

Regarding active joint count, a significant difference between patients with and without mental health issues was observed only at enrolment (figure 3e). Physician-assessed disease activity showed no significant difference between patients with later PHQ-9 scores ≥10 and <10 at any time point (figure 3f).

Mental health prediction

The areas under the curves, the ability of emotional functioning at baseline to distinguish between patients with no to minimal depressive/anxiety symptoms and moderate to severe depressive/anxiety symptoms at follow-up, were 0.63/0.67 and 0.67 for both conditions, respectively, corresponding to a PedsQL cut-off value of 64.

Discussion

In our prospective ICON study, 13% of adolescents and young adults exhibited moderate to severe symptoms of depression, while 10% reported moderate to severe anxiety symptoms—approximately twice the prevalence observed in controls (7% and 2%, respectively). A meta-analysis on psychiatric comorbidities in patients with rheumatic diseases reported anxiety disorders in 13% and depression in 20%, though variations in age, gender and clinical parameters, such as disease duration across studies, complicate direct comparisons.20 A recent German study using the Beck Depression Inventory-Fast Screen Questionnaire found depressive symptoms in 13% and diagnosed depression in 9.5% of patients with JIA.21

Several studies indicate that children and adolescents with JIA experience higher rates of anxiety and depression compared with their healthy peers, but at levels similar to those with other chronic childhood diseases.7 22 23 However, a recent study by Hanns et al found no significant differences in depressive or anxiety symptoms between patients and healthy controls,24 aligning with earlier studies.25 26 These discrepancies may stem from variations in demographic factors (age, gender and sample size), clinical characteristics (JIA category, disease duration and activity) or differences in assessment tools and cut-off values.

Chronic illnesses profoundly affect daily life and mental well-being. This impact may be even more pronounced when a chronic disease develops during adolescence, a period characterised by evolving social interactions, participation and autonomy. Our finding that moderate to severe depressive or anxiety symptoms were more common in individuals with older age at disease onset aligns with this context. Additionally, depressive symptoms were more frequent in individuals aged 18 and older, as well as in female participants—both in patients and controls—consistent with trends observed in healthy young people27,29 and those with rheumatic diseases.30

In our study, patients with moderate to severe symptoms of depression or anxiety in late adolescence or early adulthood did not differ from those without these symptoms in terms of JIA category, ANA status and HLA-B27 status. Likewise, no significant associations were found between PHQ-9 or GAD-7 scores and drug therapy or SES. Instead, patient-reported outcomes clearly correlated with PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores at follow-up, while physician-reported outcomes, such as disease activity score and active joint count, showed only partial correlation. These findings are in line with previous research, which indicates that anxiety and depression in patients with JIA are more closely linked to pain and functional impairment than to traditional disease measures. For example, Ding et al found that self-reported functional status correlated with depression and anxiety, whereas inflammatory markers, physician-assessed disease activity and active joint count did not26. Similarly, Hanns et al, in a longitudinal study of the British Childhood Arthritis Prospective Study, reported that 14.7% of patients had depressive symptoms30. These symptoms were associated with pain and disability at baseline, predicted worsening pain and disability over time, but did not predict disease activity. In a cross-sectional study, Hanns et al also found a significant association between depression or anxiety symptoms and functional limitations and pain, but not with inflammatory markers or the number of active joints24. These findings indicate that patient-reported outcomes—such as overall well-being, pain and functional ability—reflect not only disease activity or damage but also mental health status.

Reinfjell et al postulated that the PedsQL may be an interesting measure of depressive symptoms in adolescents. In their study assessing the PedsQL in comparison to the Mood and Feelings Questionnaire in 425 Norwegian adolescents, they were able to show that individuals who reported higher levels of depressive symptoms had significantly lower scores on the HRQoL as measured by the PedsQL, even when adjusted for gender, age, the number of stressful life events and the level of social anxiety symptoms.31

In our study, at inclusion in ICON, both patient groups had a reduced score in emotional functioning compared with their control groups: Within the first year in ICON, both JIA patient groups (PHQ-9 <10, PHQ-9 ≥10) improved in the score of the PedsQL subscale ‘emotional functioning’ to the level of their respective control groups. This suggests a therapy effect within the first year in specialised care.

Patients who experienced moderate to severe depressive symptoms in late adolescence or young adulthood already had significantly lower emotional functioning at enrolment or shortly after their JIA diagnosis. This suggests that early emotional difficulties may serve as an indicator of future mental health challenges. However, it is worth noting that an emotional functioning score below 64 on the PedsQL at baseline demonstrated only poor to fair predictive value for subsequent moderate to severe depressive symptoms.

Moreover, patients who later developed mental health problems showed less improvement in perceived physical functioning during the first year, despite reductions in disease activity and the number of active joints. Notably, their physician-reported disease activity over the entire observation period was not significantly higher than that of patients without mental health issues. These findings suggest that disease severity alone does not fully explain the development of depressive symptoms in JIA. Rather, lower emotional well-being at disease onset may serve as an early marker of long-term psychological vulnerability. This is further supported by data from the healthy control participants who also went on to experience mental health issues. Like the affected patients with JIA, they reported significantly lower emotional functioning at baseline and followed a similar trajectory over time. However, a higher proportion of patients than controls in the ICON cohort exhibited poorer emotional functioning at enrolment, as indicated by lower scores on the PedsQL ‘emotional functioning’ subscale. The factors contributing to this reduced emotional well-being at study entry remain unclear and warrant further investigation. It can be assumed that the diagnosis itself had already affected the mental health of some patients with JIA.

This study has several limitations. First, not all patients in the ICON cohort could be assessed for depressive or anxiety symptoms due to age restrictions. The older age of participants in this study led to a slightly different JIA spectrum compared with the full cohort. Second, HRQoL was assessed using the PedsQL only up to age 17, limiting longitudinal insight for older participants. Third, psychological stress may be underreported, as it is often difficult to admit. Most participants completed the questionnaire in the clinic, with only a minority doing so at home, which may have influenced responses. We calculated a cut-off value for PedsQL emotional functioning to differentiate between patients with no to minimal depressive/anxiety symptoms and moderate to severe depressive/anxiety symptoms at follow-up in our sample. The validity of this cut-off should be confirmed in other cohorts.

Strengths of this study include longitudinal observation of a large number of incident JIA cases, the use of a control group and access to over 7 years of prospectively recorded HRQoL data. This enabled robust comparisons and long-term monitoring of mental health development in young people.

Summary

Adolescents with JIA reported more frequent symptoms of depression and anxiety than their healthy peers. These symptoms were more prevalent among female participants, and their occurrence increased with age. Patient-reported outcomes—such as functional limitations, pain and fatigue—were significantly associated with depressive and anxiety symptoms, whereas physician-reported disease activity and treatments showed no such associations. Notably, both patients and controls who later developed mental health problems already exhibited reduced emotional functioning at the time of ICON inclusion.

In conclusion, this study suggests that impaired emotional functioning may be an early indicator of emerging mental health problems, independent of the severity of somatic illness as assessed by a physician. It seems important to identify patients who need mental health support at an early stage, and ideally, care centres should have a support structure in place for patients who show abnormal results in screening tests, so that tailored treatment options can be offered in a suitable time frame. Therefore, routine assessment of HRQoL, including mental well-being, should be an integral part of rheumatological care, alongside physical examination. Incorporating HRQoL evaluations into standard JIA management, particularly at disease onset and at the 1-year follow-up, can facilitate early identification of at-risk patients and support timely psychological interventions, potentially improving long-term outcomes.
