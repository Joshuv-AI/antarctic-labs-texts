Experience with the mobile health application ‘mSaada’ to facilitate home-based human papillomavirus testing in Western Kenya
Authors: Nguyen Thao Thi Nguyen, Melat Woldetensae, Sahil Choudhri, Evans Obuto, Raphael Onyango, Christina Makarushka, John A. Gallis, Joao Vissoci, Megan J. Huchko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCervical cancer screening using human papillomavirus (HPV) DNA testing is an essential component of prevention efforts. Countries with limited health care infrastructure need innovative strategies to make HPV-based screening feasible. Our team developed a mobile phone application, mSaada, to facilitate counseling, data collection, and client follow-up for HPV-based cervical cancer screening in western Kenya.MethodsCommunity health promoters (CHPs) working in Kisumu County underwent training in mSaada-assisted HPV-based screening before offering testing via self-collection during routinely scheduled home visits. After each visit, CHPs filled out a questionnaire to capture factors interrupting the home visits, including technology challenges. HPV results and follow-up information were provided directly to women by text or home visit and put into their clinic records. Treatment with thermal ablation was offered at their local health facilities, free of charge. All CHPs completed an mSaada usability survey and focus group discussion at completion of the pilot.ResultsTwenty-two CHPs performed screening between October 2023 and June 2024. Of the 1118 women approached, 964 (86%) agreed to screen. Within 3 months after the screening period ended, 116 out of 218 women (53%) who tested positive for HPV completed follow-up treatment. Device or mSaada issues were minimal, with the most common being charging problems, slow mSaada performance, and quick response (QR) code scanner malfunctions. Usability scores indicated high satisfaction with mSaada (mean 4.4/5).ConclusionsmSaada was a useful tool to facilitate HPV-based screening during home visits. Background

Cervical cancer is a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality among women globally, with over 600,000 cases diagnosed each year—88% of which occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1]. In Kenya, where cervical cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer among reproductive-aged women, the lifetime risk of developing the disease is 3.7% [2]. Although highly preventable through human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination and screening, access to these services remains limited in many resource-constrained settings.

Self-collection for HPV testing has emerged as a safe, effective, and acceptable screening option and is now widely adopted in Kenya [3, 4]. National guidelines updated in 2018 and 2024 recommend HPV-based screening where feasible due to its improved performance over visual inspection with acetic acid or cytology [5]. However, barriers persist—particularly in rural areas—including limited awareness, logistical challenges in accessing care, and a lack of systems to support follow-up after testing [6, 7]. These challenges hinder completion of the full screening cascade: screening, receiving and understanding results, and accessing appropriate follow-up services.

Because point-of-care HPV testing is not available, most programs require women to return for results and follow-up, which can create delays and missed care opportunities. Self-collection enables screening to occur outside of clinical settings, but it also requires systems that can support counseling, specimen tracking, and timely communication of results. Community health workers (CHWs) are increasingly involved in delivering these services but often lack adequate tools or training to support screening effectively [8].

Mobile health (mHealth) tools can help address these gaps by improving data capture, enhancing client communication, and supporting healthcare providers in the field [9]. Evidence from several LMICs has demonstrated that mHealth interventions can increase screening uptake, improve the timeliness of follow-up, and reduce documentation errors [10–12]. For example, text message reminders to CHWs in Tanzania have improved visit timeliness, while multimedia job aids have helped reduce clinical errors among CHWs in Colombia [11]. Text messaging is also a feasible and acceptable method for communicating lab results, as shown in studies with HIV-positive clients in Uganda, though concerns around privacy and message clarity remain important considerations [12].

Building on this evidence, we implemented an mHealth solution tailored for HPV-based cervical cancer screening in western Kenya. The application, named mSaada—from the Swahili word meaning “to support,” with the “m” indicating that it is a mobile health tool—was adapted based on a prototype our team previously developed [13] and refined through participatory design and pilot testing to support community-based HPV screening in western Kenya. It was designed for use by community health promoters (CHPs)—lay health workers in Kenya’s public health system—to facilitate counseling, specimen tracking, and follow-up for clients undergoing HPV screening. In this model, CHPs served as the primary users of the app, while their clients were the end beneficiaries of the services it supported. To address critical needs in the screening cascade, mSaada was built with the following core functions: (1) counseling support for CHPs, (2) specimen tracking, (3) automated text result notifications, and (4) a case management system to track and follow up with clients needing results or treatment scheduling. To ensure that mSaada is context-appropriate and usable by the target population, we sought input from key stakeholders to assess user experience and identify support needs for successful implementation [14].

We conducted a pilot study to assess the functionality and user experience of mSaada within a home-based screening model in order to inform a large-scale cluster-randomized trial. The primary objectives of the pilot were to evaluate the app’s ability to support HPV-based screening through CHPs while identifying technical and implementation challenges that could inform future refinements. To evaluate mSaada’s performance across diverse contexts, we selected one peri-urban and one rural site in western Kenya—settings that differ in infrastructure, connectivity, and access to health services, especially where transportation poses significant barriers. Insights gained from this pilot will guide ongoing improvements to ensure the platform meets the needs and capabilities of target communities ahead of larger-scale implementation and evaluation.

Methods

Study design

This was a 9-month pilot study of mSaada-supported home-based HPV screening offered by CHPs from community units (CUs) linked to two health facilities in Kisumu County, Kenya. The study aimed to evaluate the feasibility, functionality, and usability of the mSaada app in supporting CHPs to provide HPV-based screening, client counseling, and follow-up care in community settings. Between October 2023 and June 2024, 22 CHPs were trained in mSaada use and processes for counseling and testing for HPV. After loading mSaada onto government-provided smartphones, CHPs conducted home visits to offer screening to eligible women across two CUs—one rural and one peri-urban. During these visits, CHPs provided education about HPV and cervical cancer screening, supplied vaginal self-collection devices (cytology brushes collected in a dry container for Aptima HPV testing [Atila BioSystems, Mountain View, CA, USA]), and guided clients in performing self-sampling at home. Screening activities were temporarily paused for 3 weeks across December and January due to the holidays. The pilot was designed as a real-world implementation to inform app refinement and future scale-up.

Description of mSaada application

The mSaada application used in this study was based on a previously developed prototype [13], which included core features for client data collection and counseling support. In collaboration with key stakeholders, including women in the community and CHPs, the prototype was refined and translated into a functional mobile application using CommCare (Dimagi, Cambridge, MA, USA), an adaptable case management program designed for use by CHPs.

Figure 1 illustrates the key functions of the mSaada platform and the workflow across its four user groups: CHPs, clients, health facilities, and the laboratory. CHPs were the primary users of the mobile app and used it to identify eligible women (Step 1), provide counseling (Step 2), and collect patient data during home visits (Step 3). If a client self-collected a specimen, they could choose to receive their result via text message, home visit, or clinic visit. If they chose to receive results by text message, they immediately received a confirmation text including a contact number for questions. CHPs then delivered the specimen to the health facility, where it was tracked electronically (Step 4). Research assistants then collected specimens on a bi-weekly basis to be run at a sub-county hospital on the Ampfireä platform (Atila Biosystems, Menlo Park, California, USA), which processes HPV tests using a simplified, rapid, and low-cost isothermal amplification method with real-time fluorescent detection. HPV results were uploaded into mSaada (Step 5), which automatically generated text notifications and enabled CHPs to follow up with patients (Step 6). In addition to CHP-facing workflows, mSaada also includes modules for research assistants, lab personnel, and treatment providers, enabling comprehensive case tracking and documentation. The app was also configured to generate standard Ministry of Health reports for each facility.

The use of CommCare allowed additional functionality for electronic specimen tracking, automated result notification, improved case management and report generation, and robust data security. The final integrated digital platform provides support for clients, CHPs, and the health system, and facilitates accurate tracking of HPV specimens, results, and follow-up, improving successful completion of the cervical cancer screening cascade as outlined in Fig. 1.

To facilitate uninterrupted use of mSaada, we contracted with Safaricom, the largest telecom company in Kenya, to “whitelist” mSaada, which supported the use of the CommCare app without data bundles on individual phones. This reduced the burden of reloading pre-paid data bundles and the potential diversion of data credit. We also leveraged an existing country-specific gateway through CommCare to facilitate automated text messages to notify clients of their screening results and send follow-up reminders.

Study setting and participant selection

This study was conducted in Kisumu County in Western Kenya, an area with a population of 1.14 million and 215 government health facilities, ranging from basic dispensaries to a referral hospital in the county capital city, Kisumu. mSaada was implemented in CUs linked to two health facilities in Kisumu County, one rural and one peri-urban CU. CUs generally consist of one supervisor and ten CHPs, each of whom is assigned to 100 households in the community. Each CU is linked to a health facility, which can support up to 6–7 CUs. CHPs’ main worksite is within their local villages, where they offer health messaging and basic preventative health measures at the household and community level. Residents are referred to the health facility for any services that require a health care provider.

In the cervical cancer screening model, HPV-based screening was offered in the home. Eligibility criteria for HPV screening included women living with HIV aged 25 years or older, and women not living with HIV aged 30 to 65 years. Women who had a prior hysterectomy were not eligible for screening. Women who reported being pregnant were advised to defer screening if they were within 3 months of their due date and were offered the option to reschedule. Women who were menstruating were still offered screening; if the sample was heavily contaminated with blood and yielded an indeterminate result, CHPs repeated the test at a later visit.

The two facilities/CUs selected for the mSaada pilot had been among the Ministry of Health pilot sites using the new electronic Community Health Information System (eCHIS) for household data entry by CHPs using government-provided smart phones during home visits. All CHPs at these facilities had undergone training and had functional smart phones with internet capability. mSaada was downloaded onto the CHP smartphones prior to training. Twenty-two CHPs and two CHP supervisors in the two sites were trained in HPV-based screening, counseling, and use of mSaada. Group training, including didactic content, role plays, and simulations, took place over two days, followed by 2–4 days of individually mentored home visits. CHPs were considered ready to start screening once a research team member determined them to be independently capable of registering a client, facilitating HPV testing, and using mSaada for data collection. In addition, we carried out refresher training for healthcare providers in both facilities providing treatment for clients with a positive HPV result. These providers were also given mSaada accounts and training on the mSaada treatment documentation module.

Data collection and measurements

CHPs incorporated counseling and offered HPV testing via self-collection during routine home visits. After each visit, CHPs filled out a 7-question workflow questionnaire (Supplementary Material 1: Workflow questionnaire) to identify any barriers to making client contact, technical problems with the mobile device and mSaada, and issues with obtaining cervical cancer screening. At the end of the pilot, all CHPs completed an mSaada usability survey (Supplementary material 2: mSaada usability survey). This survey was adapted to our project based on the previously validated mHealth app usability questionnaire (MAUQ) to be appropriate for our app and study context [15]. The survey consisted of 20 questions on a 5-point Likert scale for a total score of 0 to 100 to assess ease of use, functionality, organization, content, and overall satisfaction.

Following administration of the usability surveys, two in-person focus group discussions (FGDs) were convened at the health facilities, one for each CU, with all CHPs participating. Only CHPs and research assistants were present during the FGDs, and discussions were audio-recorded. The FGD guides were designed to elicit deeper insight into CHP experience with mSaada-assisted counseling and probed for positive and negative experiences, limitations, and recommended adaptations (Supplementary Material 3: Focus group discussion guide). FGDs were conducted in English and Dhluo and then later translated into English transcripts for analysis. The peri-urban FGD lasted approximately three hours and the rural FGD approximately two hours. Because all CHPs in the study participated, and the sample was small and well defined, data were considered sufficient to capture the range of perspectives in this pilot, although formal data saturation was not assessed. Transcripts were not returned to participants for comment or correction.

Qualitative data were collected by trained research assistants under the oversight of senior author, Megan Huchko, MD, MPH. Dr. Huchko is a physician in obstetrics and gynecology and Director of the Center for Global Reproductive Health at Duke University. She completed a fellowship in reproductive infectious disease and has extensive experience in cervical cancer prevention and global women’s health research, including long-term collaboration with clinics in western Kenya. Dr. Huchko did not have direct contact with study participants, and the research assistants had a professional working relationship with the community health providers through the study but no prior personal relationships (Supplementary Material 4: COREQ checklist) [16].

Data analysis

Data were stored on the cloud-based CommCare platform and exported to Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp, Redmond, WA, USA) to facilitate further analysis. Statistical analysis was conducted using RStudio version 1.2.5033 (RStudio, Boston, MA, USA). Descriptive statistics were used for client demographics, preferred method of result notification, HPV result, and follow-up with treatment. Exploratory data analysis was performed to analyze the daily workflow questionnaires and the mSaada usability survey. CommCare metadata properties were used to track time spent on surveys. Because of the small pilot sample size, rather than emphasizing statistical significance, we primarily examined observational data from both CUs and compared differences between CUs that were meaningfully large and that could affect future implementation and follow-up patterns.

Qualitative data from the FGDs were analyzed using thematic analysis [17]. Two members of the research team reviewed the transcripts and independently identified common themes related to user experience, challenges, and suggestions for improvement. These initial themes were then discussed and organized into broader, overarching themes that captured key patterns across the data. A hierarchical coding tree was not formally constructed. This iterative and collaborative process ensured that the main insights from CHPs regarding mSaada use were thoroughly captured.

Results

Screening

A total of 1,118 women were approached for screening during home visits. The average age was 40.0 years (SD = 9.8). Of all women approached, 18.9% reported living with HIV, 22.4% had prior cervical cancer screening, and 0.2% had received treatment for cervical dysplasia (Table 1). More women were approached for screening in the rural CU (n = 586, 52%) compared to the peri-urban CU (n = 532, 48%), with an average of 53 women per CHP compared to 48, respectively. The first full months of the pilot had the highest number of women approached for screening: October 2023 (219 clients), November 2023 (153 clients), and February 2024 (126 clients). The month with the least number of clients approached was June 2024 (40 clients). Of the total women approached, 964 (86%) agreed to discuss screening and be registered in mSaada, and 819 (85%) were screened for HPV. Regarding modes of result notification, 581 (75%) opted to receive their results via text message, 184 (24%) via home visit, and 7 (1%) by returning to the clinic. There was incomplete data for 47 clients (41 in the peri-urban CU and 6 in the rural CU) who did not have a preferred method of result notification documented. This missing data was due to an omission in the screening form (that has since been corrected), which did not ask for the preferred notification method if clients deferred screening during the initial registration encounter. Preferences for notification differed by CU, with a greater proportion of women in the peri-urban CU requesting results by text message compared to the rural CU (88% versus 63%). With respect to rates of successful result notifications, we found that 86% of women who requested their results via text message successfully received their result. Of those who requested a home visit, only 16% had a documented home visit encounter for result notification. Among those who chose to receive their results in clinic, 100% had a documented clinic encounter during which their results were disclosed (Table 2).

There were 218 (27%) women who tested positive for HPV. By September 30, 2024 (3 months after screening was completed), 116 of the 218 women who tested positive for HPV (53%) had followed up in clinic for treatment (Fig. 2). All women who followed up for treatment were treated with thermal ablation at their local health facility. The average time to treatment was 56 days (SD = 39). CHP characteristics

Demographic data were collected from 21 of the 22 CHPs who participated in the pilot—10 from the peri-urban CU and 11 from the rural CU. All but one identified as female, and all had served as CHPs for more than 3 years. The average age was 46.5 years. Most (18/21) were married or cohabitating, with one reporting being single and two widowed. Regarding education, four had completed primary school, one had completed secondary school, and the remainder had attended some secondary school without completion. Nearly all CHPs (18/21) had previously been screened for cervical cancer (Table 1).

Workflow and usability

CHPs took an average of 46 min to complete the registration form with clients, which generally included all the time between the initiation of counseling and completion of self-collection. There were low rates of device- or app-related issues reported on the workflow surveys. The most common device-related issues reported were problems with phone charging (n = 11) and phone not turning on or continuing to turn off (n = 10). The most common app-related issues were app loading slowly or freezing (n = 9) followed by quick response (QR) code scanner (used to scan specimen labels) not functioning properly (n = 5). Other app-related issues reported were difficulty editing or submitting client information (n = 3), difficulty opening app (n = 2), and client response (to a question in mSaada) not included in the list of options (n = 1) (Table 3). Usability scores were high, with a mean of 4.4 on a scale of 1–5, suggesting overall satisfaction with mSaada functionality and usability (Supplementary Material 2: mSaada usability survey). The scoring breakdown for the 5 usability domains assessed is as follows: satisfaction (4.57), learnability (4.41), efficiency (4.34), ease of use (4.58), and preventing errors (3.8). Four key indicators demonstrated strong CHP endorsement, with over 90% agreeing or strongly agreeing regarding user comfort, ease of use, sustained adoption, and enhanced workflow efficiency (Fig. 3). Key challenges identified during the FGDs included client misunderstanding of the screening process and app-related issues. A leading concern among CHPs was that, even after education, many women feared a positive HPV result meant they had cancer. They explained that the in-app scanner did not always capture the correct code or would not scan at all, forcing manual barcode entry. CHPs also expressed concern about delayed or missed text message notifications. They reported that some of their clients share phones, so they felt that there was a risk of others reading the message and deleting it. Lastly, CHPs had difficulty remembering to initiate follow-up, since they do not use mSaada outside of fieldwork and rarely check the app for updates.

Discussion

This pilot study demonstrated that mSaada, a mobile health application designed to support CHP-delivered HPV screening, was feasible, usable, and effective in a real-world setting. Like other mHealth tools implemented in low-resource settings, mSaada helped improve service delivery through task-shifting, real-time data capture, specimen tracking, and electronic follow-up mechanisms. Consistent with findings from prior mHealth interventions implemented in LMICs [10–14, 18–20], its success depended on adequate training, equipment readiness, and context-specific refinements.

CHPs were able to independently use mSaada in the field after training and mentorship, and uptake of screening was high: 86% of clients approached for screening ultimately agreed to participate. Among those screened, 27% tested positive for HPV, and 53% of clients who screened positive followed up at a clinic and completed treatment. This treatment completion rate exceeds national averages for HPV-positive women in Kenya (below 40%) [21], but still highlights a need to strengthen follow-up systems and care linkage to close the cervical cancer screening cascade.

Offering multiple notification options—including text messages, home visits, and clinic visits—ensured that clients with limited mobile access could still receive their results and helped address privacy concerns associated with receiving sensitive information via text message [12, 22]. This approach also aligns with emerging data showing that women and marginalized groups in Kenya are increasingly likely to access health information via text message over alternative modalities [23]. Despite pre-screening counseling, CHPs reported that some clients misinterpreted a positive result as a cancer diagnosis, resulting in emotional distress—similar to findings from Uganda, where women described anxiety after receiving test results via text message [12]. To address this, we plan to revise the text message language to clearly state that HPV positivity does not mean a cancer diagnosis and create brochures reinforcing key messages for both clients and their partners.

Documentation of home visits for result notification was often missing, although follow-up for treatment still occurred—suggesting a gap in recordkeeping rather than service delivery. In the next study phase, we will emphasize accurate documentation and implement automated dashboards to identify CHPs who need support following up with clients. To support more timely care, we will also expand automated text reminders to both clients and CHPs and simplify data entry during home visits. A study in Tanzania found that text message reminders to CHWs improved visit timeliness and reduced overdue follow-ups [11], suggesting this strategy may similarly strengthen our implementation.

Technical issues were more often related to the mobile devices themselves—such as low battery or failure to power on—rather than the mSaada application, reflecting common challenges in mHealth programs in Kenya [18]. The most frequent app-specific issue was difficulty scanning QR codes. As one CHP noted, “the barcode frustrated me most.” This challenge mirrors findings from a mass oral cholera vaccination campaign in Haiti, where incorrect barcode scanning was mitigated by adding numeric constraints in the form [19]. In mSaada, we incorporated a validation parameter to minimize erroneous entries and will expand training on barcode scanning in future iterations. Ensuring access to backup devices and power banks at the CU level may further improve readiness in the field.

To ensure the app worked effectively in areas with limited internet access—a frequent challenge in LMICs [20]—mSaada leveraged CommCare’s capability to collect data offline and sync it later when a network connection became available. To minimize data costs and ensure reliable access, we partnered with Kenya’s largest telecom provider to whitelist mSaada, allowing CHPs to use the app without relying on their personal mobile data. This helped address another common challenge in mHealth deployments where prepaid phone credit is shared or depleted for personal use [20]. In future phases, we will reinforce training on syncing data once reconnected to the network and integrate troubleshooting scenarios into the curriculum to prepare CHPs for common app errors in the field.

This real-world study of a mobile app designed to support HPV-based screening through government CUs had strengths and limitations. mSaada’s success was aided by the presence of the eCHIS, which provided CHPs with smartphones and training in their use for entry of health data, which may not be the case in other settings. In the absence of eCHIS or similar systems, successful deployment of mSaada would require greater investment in technology, training, and infrastructure. It is also important to note that our usability findings are based on a small sample of 22 CHPs, which may limit generalizability. Furthermore, our assessment of usability was limited to CHP feedback; we did not conduct direct observations out of concern that it might interfere with women’s privacy during home visits. However, we used multiple complementary strategies—including post-visit surveys, usability questionnaires, and FGDs—to capture CHP perspectives. In future phases, we will incorporate clients’ perspectives to more fully understand the client experience with mSaada-supported screening.

Conclusions

This pilot study found mSaada to be a feasible and user-friendly digital tool for facilitating home-based HPV screening by CHPs in western Kenya. Key implementation facilitators included offline functionality, automated result notification, and integration with Kenya’s community health system. Lessons learned from this pilot will guide refinements to both the application and implementation strategy. As we prepare for a larger cluster-randomized trial, next steps include updating app content and workflows, strengthening CHP training, and ensuring equipment readiness to optimize screening and follow-up in community settings.
