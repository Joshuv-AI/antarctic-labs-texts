Clinico-Epidemiological Trends of Leprosy at a Tertiary Care Centre of South Rajasthan: A 10-Year Retrospective Study
Authors: Vaishali Masatkar, Manju Meena, Rini Makhija, Chesta Agarwal, Manisha Balai
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Leprosy is a major public health problem in India, which affects many people every year and has significantly high new case detection rates in certain districts and blocks. According to the National Leprosy Elimination Program (NLEP) report of March 2019, there were a total of 1.14 lakhs new cases of leprosy in India with an Annual New Case Detection Rate (ANCDR) of 8.1 per 100,000 population.Aim:The present study aimed at determining the epidemiological and clinical profile of leprosy patients from a tertiary level teaching hospital in South Rajasthan.Materials and Methods:A retrospective, record-based study was carried out on patients registered in the leprosy clinic of a tertiary level teaching hospital of South Rajasthan during a period of ten years (2010-2019). Data regarding the demographic profile, clinical features, treatment given, and complications were analysed.Results:A total of 1372 patients registered in the leprosy clinic during a period of 10 years were evaluated. There were 1007 (73.4%) males and 365 (26.6%) females with a male-female ratio of 2.76:1. The majority of patients 671 (48.9%) belonged to the middle age group (21-40 years). Multibacillary leprosy was the most common clinical type seen in 1109 patients (80.8%). Borderline leprosy was the most common morphological type, determined in 625 (45.6%) patients, of which BL (21.0%) was the most common group. 16.7% patients presented with leprosy reaction at the time of initial diagnosis. Type 1 reaction was seen in 91(6.6%) and type 2 reaction in 138 (10.1%) patients. Grade I disability was observed in 347 (25.3%) patients whereas grade II disability was seen in 456 (33.2%) patients. 54 patients (3.9%) had a definite history of contact in the family. 28 patients defaulted during the study period. Yearly trend of epidemiological and operational parameters was observed, and the parameters were compared with the national data.Conclusion:Persistent high proportion of multibacillary cases and grade 2 disability indicates that leprosy continues to be a significant health care problem despite statistical elimination. There is a clear need to strengthen early detection, treatment and regular follow-up of these cases in both high and low endemic settings.

Introduction

Leprosy is a chronic infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium leprae, a slow-growing mycobacterium, manifesting as damage to the skin and peripheral nerve. India contributes about 80% of the global leprosy caseload.[1] The National Leprosy Control Programme was launched in 1954 in India and converted to a National Leprosy Elimination Programme (NLEP) in 1983 intending to eliminate leprosy.[2] India has achieved the goal of elimination of leprosy in December 2005.[3] However, the new case detection rate, an important statistical indicator in leprosy control programmes, has not shown a significant decline despite all efforts. Understanding the epidemiological profile is thus an essential prerequisite to address public health needs in the country and to enable efficient programme planning and management.

This study aimed to find the trend in presentations of leprosy patients at a leprosy centre and to interpret these data to different epidemiological variables such as age, sex, type of disease and deformity.

Materials and Methods

Study design and participants

This is a single-centre hospital-based retrospective record-based study. All the leprosy patients registered in the leprosy clinic of a tertiary-level teaching hospital in South Rajasthan during a period of 10 years (January 2010–December 2019) were included in the study. In total, 1372 patients were identified; their clinical records and photographs were reviewed and analysed.

Case definition

Any patient with one or more of the following cardinal signs was diagnosed with leprosy:

Hypopigmented or erythematous skin lesion(s) with either definite loss or impairment of sensation.Peripheral nerve involvement is demonstrated by definite thickening with sensory impairment.Slit skin smear examination positive for acid-fast bacilli.

This was supplemented by a skin biopsy wherever needed.

The two main classification systems used in this study were as follows: Ridley–Jopling system[4] and the NLEP classification.[5] The Ridley–Jopling’s system aids in categorising the different types of leprosy along a spectrum: tuberculoid leprosy (TT), borderline tuberculoid (BT), borderline borderline (BB), borderline lepromatous (BL) and lepromatous leprosy (LL).

The NLEP classification was used for therapeutic purposes, and multidrug therapy was provided to patients based on paucibacillary (PB) and multibacillary (MB) disease regimen. A PB case is defined as one to five skin lesions with definite sensory impairment and/or only one nerve trunk involvement. Single nerve trunk thickening (mononeuritic) without skin involvement is also categorised as PB leprosy. An MB case is defined as six or more skin lesions with definite sensory impairment and/or two or more nerve trunk involvement. Two or more nerve trunk involvement without skin involvement is also categorised as MB leprosy.

Disability

The patients were categorised as having a grade 0, grade 1 and grade 2 disability as per the World Health Organization (WHO) disability grading system.[6] A patient manifesting no sensory or motor nerve function impairment or loss of vision due to leprosy was considered having grade 0 disability. Grade 1 disability was diagnosed whenever a patient presented with sensory nerve function impairment affecting hands or feet without motor nerve function impairment affecting these sites (due to leprosy) or visual impairment better than 6/60 due to leprosy. Grade 2 disability was diagnosed when there was motor nerve function impairment or visible deformity of hands or feet or visual impairment <6/60 due to leprosy.

Relapse

The Guide to Leprosy Control (WHO 1988)[7] definition was used for determining relapse in leprosy. It defines relapse as ‘A patient who successfully completes an adequate course of multidrug therapy (MDT), but who subsequently develops new signs and symptoms of the disease, either during the surveillance period (2 years for PB and 5 years for MB leprosy) or thereafter’.

Study variables

Epidemiologic variables

Sex, age, residence and history of contact with leprosy patient(s) were recorded.

Clinical variables

Operational classification—PB/MB as per NLEP.

Clinical classification according to Ridley–Jopling (TT, BT, BB, BL and LL) and histoid leprosy and pure neural leprosy.

The number and laterality of thickened peripheral nerves were recorded.

Regarding leprosy reactions, only those cases of reactions were added to the database in which the initial presentation at the time of enrolment in the study was a reaction. However, reactions developing later during the follow-up period were not added to the database.

Laboratory

Slit skin smear in all the patients. A skin biopsy was performed for confirmation of diagnosis in selective cases; however, they were not analysed in this study.

Statistical analysis

Our data were compared with state and national trends. Data were managed and analysed in Microsoft Excel, and SPSS software was used for statistical analysis.

Ethical commitee approval taken on 9.7.20.

Results

A total of 1372 cases of leprosy were registered during the study period of 10 years (January 2010 to December 2019). There were 1007 (73.4%) males and 365 (26.6%) females with a male-to-female ratio of 2.76:1.

Clinical parameters of the study population

Age and sex distribution of leprosy patients

Most of our patients belonged to rural areas (1047; 76.3%) with a rural-to-urban ratio of 3.22:1. Considering the proximity, most of the patients in this study were native to Rajasthan with more than half belonging to Udaipur.

The duration of the disease ranged from 2 days to 15 years. Only 54 patients (3.9%) had a definite history of contact with leprosy patients in their households, and the most common presentation was lepromatous leprosy.

Clinical spectrum

Of all, 1109 (80.8%) patients were classified and treated as multibacillary leprosy.

Of the 1372 patients, 1326 presented with primary cutaneous manifestations, whereas 46 presented with primary neurological complaints. A total of 625 (45.6%) patients were suffering from borderline leprosy, of which BL (21.0%) was the most common group, whereas 602 (43.9%) patients had LL.

Leprosy reactions

A total of 229 (16.7%) patients presented with leprosy reaction at the time of the initial diagnosis. Type 1 reaction was seen in 91 (6.6%) and type 2 reaction in 138 (10.1%) patients. The patient spectrum most affected by type 1 reaction was borderline (BT + BB + BL) comprising 50.5% of all type 1 reactions, whereas patients with LL were most frequently encountered with type 2 reaction (85.5%) [Figure 2].

Distribution of leprosy reactions according to clinical types of leprosy

The majority of patients who suffered from leprosy reactions suffered from multibacillary leprosy.

Neural involvement and deformity

Forty-six patients presented with pure neuritic leprosy with no skin lesions of which 31 patients had mononeuritic, whereas 15 patients had polyneuritic leprosy. Of these 46 patients with pure neuritic leprosy, 22 (47.83%) patients had deformities.

The ulnar nerve was the most common nerve affected (n = 1094, 79.74%), followed by the radial cutaneous nerve (932, 67.93%) and common peroneal nerve (803, 58.53%). Other peripheral nerve thickening observed clinically was supra-orbital, greater auricular and pretibial nerve. 118 patients had a single nerve involvement.

A total of 803 (58.5%) patients suffered from various physical disabilities. Grade 1 disability was observed in 347 (25.3%) patients, whereas grade 2 disability was seen in 456 (33.2%) patients.

The most common grade 2 disability encountered was trophic ulcers affecting 191 (13.9%) patients. The claw hand was the most frequent paralytic deformity noticed in 126 patients (9.2%). Lagophthalmos was seen in six patients. Five patients had foot drop, and four patients had facial palsy.

Relapse and defaulters

During the study period, five patients presented with a relapse of the disease after completion of the full course of MDT. The MDT-MB blister pack was restarted in these patients. Twenty-eight defaulters were identified during the study period, and all these patients were categorised as multibacillary at the initial presentation.

Discussion

Knowledge and understanding of the epidemiological profile are essential prerequisites to assess and address public health needs in the country and to enable efficient programme planning and management. An impressive decline in the leprosy prevalence rate (PR) in all endemic districts of India is seen in the post-MDT era.[8] Recent data analyses showed that 34 states/Union Territories have already achieved the level of elimination, that is PR less than 1 case per 10,000 population. One state (Chhattisgarh) and one UT (Dadra and Nagar Haveli) have remained with PR between 2 and 5 per 10,000 population. Five other states/UT viz. Odisha, Bihar, Chandigarh, Goa and Lakshadweep wherein elimination was achieved earlier have shown a slight increase in PR in the current year.

In the present study, a total of 1372 cases of leprosy were registered during a ten-year study period. Males outnumbered the female patients with M:F ratio of 2.76:1. Many previous studies revealed a higher male prevalence of 1.7:1,[9] 2.5:1,[10] 3.5:1[11] and 2.1:1.[12] Leprosy is more prevalent in males possibly because males are more exposed to the infection in comparison with females and have greater access to health services. Women in India mostly work indoors, which makes them less prone to infection. However, if women stay indoors most of the time, their health problems go undetected.[10111213]

In the current study, the majority of the patients (48.9%) belonged to the age group of 21–40 years, corroborating with the observations made in earlier studies.[91213141516] This group of patients has self-motivation for health-seeking behaviour and is detected more as compared to other age groups. Increased incidence in this age group indicates vulnerability because of greater mobility and increased opportunity for contact in a big population. More importantly, disease occurrence in leprosy is often related to age at detection rather than age at the onset of disease. Nevertheless, leprosy in young patients points towards the endemicity of the disease.

Childhood leprosy is an important indicator of the efficiency of NLEP. A higher proportion of leprosy among children reflects a high level of disease transmission in a given population. In the present study, childhood cases contributed 2.4% to the leprosy burden, which is lower than the national average (8.7%). Data analysis from the same tertiary care centre showed a total of 32 cases (2.3%) of childhood leprosy over the study period of 2006–2015 with a gradual decline in cases in 2009, followed by an increase in 2013–14.[17] The incidence of childhood leprosy recorded in tertiary care hospitals ranged from 5.1% to 11.43%, which is higher than ours.[18] This could be due to the low endemicity of leprosy in Rajasthan in general, with childhood leprosy contributing to 1.73% of all cases.

A higher number of rural cases in our study were also supported by other studies.[11141619] In rural India, the culture of living together in joint families along with frequent social gatherings promotes the transmission of infection. Additionally, illiteracy, poor health services and communication gaps lead to under-reporting of rural cases. Also, in the context of Udaipur, the prevalence of rural leprosy is commensurate with the size of its rural population.

Our study observed that 3.94% of patients had a history of contact with leprosy cases in their households. Most of these have LL, but the pole of their contacts was unknown. Previous studies have reported the percentage of household contacts as 10.34% by Gupta R et al.,[14] 6.19% by Relhan et al.,[15] 4.8% by Kadam et al.,[19] 9.2% by Chhabra et al.[20] and 5.9% by Jindal et al.[21] Family contacts increase the risk of leprosy transmission by up to nine times, which necessitates screening of family members of leprosy patients.[14]

Most of the cases were multibacillary, that is 1109 (80.8%), as the majority of patients presented with lepromatous or borderline disease. The high proportion of MB cases in our study may indicate the existence of inaccessible areas of the population harbouring leprosy patients for a long time. Adil M et al.[13] (73.4%), Gupta R et al.[14] (80.17%), Relhan et al.[15] (66.71%), Mathan R et al.[22] (58.3%) and Tiwary PK et al.[23] (80.57%) also reported higher cases of multibacillary leprosy. The reason behind the higher proportion of MB cases can be attributed to the chronic nature of the disease itself; hence, patients report being in the advanced stages of the disease. Thus, the higher proportion of multibacillary cases is an indicator of delayed diagnosis. The number of MB cases is a key epidemiological indicator of programme performance, with MB leprosy cases attributing to greater infectiousness and disease transmission. Early case recognition and management by keeping a high index of suspicion by the healthcare professional, health education and provision of adequate healthcare services to periphery levels are imperative to bringing the caseload of MB cases down.

Borderline leprosy (BT + BB + BL) was the most common morphological group followed by LL. The increased population of borderline and LL indicates either an immunologically depressed population or a delay in approach to treatment. The smaller number of patients in TT and IL may be due to misdiagnosis or spontaneous regression with good cell-mediated immunity (CMI). Late presentation of tuberculoid cases automatically leads to the detection of more patients in the BT and BB groups, thus increasing the borderline spectrum. Histoid leprosy was observed in 3.2% of patients. Gupta R et al.,[14] Jindal N et al.[21] and Kaur et al.[24] reported the incidence of histoid cases as 3.87%, 7.4% and 1.8%, respectively. The detection of histoid leprosy cases requires expertise, and since the bacillary load is very high in these patients, they become potential reservoir of infection in the community.[25]

229 (16.7%) patients presented with leprosy reaction at the time of diagnosis. All of them belonged to the multibacillary group. Type 1 reaction was present in 91 (6.6%) and type 2 reaction in 138 (10.1%) patients. A variable incidence of leprosy reaction ranging from 9.6 to 37.4% has been reported by other studies.[8920222627] Majority of the studies observed a more frequent occurrence of type 2 reaction.[92728]

A higher incidence of type 2 reaction can be explained based on a greater proportion of LL cases. This is because the patients with type 1 reaction are more prone to deformities, whereas the patients with type 2 reaction are more prone to systemic complications.[14] Early recognition and proper management (indoor/outdoor) of reactional leprosy are essential irrespective of the type of reaction to prevent disabilities and systemic complications.

Pure neuritic leprosy was observed in 46 (3.3%) patients, including 31 mononeuritic and 15 polyneuritic types. The occurrence of pure neuritic leprosy observed in other studies ranges from 1.3 to 18%.[913202227] Thakkar et al.[9] also made the observation that of the 18% of patients who presented with primary neural leprosy at the time of the initial diagnosis, 31.1% developed cutaneous lesions later during the disease. This raises the possibility that neuritic symptoms are probably the earliest symptoms of leprosy preceding the skin lesions; hence, these patients must be carefully monitored for the long term.

91.4% of patients had multiple nerve involvement and the ulnar nerve was the most commonly affected nerve, as reported by previous studies.[1220]

In our study, 25.3% of patients were noted to have a grade 1 disability, while 33.2% had a grade 2 disability. Similar observations were made by Singal et al.[8] (37% of patients with grade 2 disabilities) and Chhabra et al.[20] (30.79% of patients with grade 2 disabilities). A lower incidence in the range of 10–20% is reported by other studies.[9212227] This discordance can be attributed to overall differences in the proportion of multibacillary cases, leprosy reactions, disease awareness, healthcare accessibility and the social stigma resulting in poor care and neglect. Disability often goes unnoticed till it progresses to obvious deformity and disfigurement. Early diagnosis, initiation and completion of full treatment are the most effective means of reducing nerve function impairment. Recognising early signs of nerve damage and anticipating impending neural damage will go a long way in reducing disability by the introduction of prophylactic treatment and supportive or preventive measures. More effective implementation of health education and treatment programmes should further reduce the scourge of leprosy in the community.

Twenty-eight patients (0.2%) defaulted from the treatment, and five patients relapsed after completion of the full course of MDT. Since patients were not followed actively for the development of relapse, these data cannot be accurately interpreted.

We made an attempt to follow the trend of significant epidemiological and operational parameters over the 10-year study period. Total leprosy cases reported to our institute per year were between 111 and 160. Studies from Gujarat[29303132] and Maharashtra[33] have reported a decreasing trend in MB cases over the years, but we did not observe that in our study. The proportion of MB cases was consistently higher than PB cases contributing to 70–88% of the total cases. A lower proportion of female patients ranging from 22.2 to 33.3% was recorded over the years, and similarly, the percentage of childhood leprosy remained lower than 4.6%. No child case was reported in 2011, followed by an increase to 2% in 2012 and 4% in 2013. This may indicate the under-reporting of child cases. Grade 2 disability % remained high fluctuating between 25 and 40.8%. Different studies have reported different findings in reporting grade 2 disability cases in India.[29303435] Overall higher MB cases and grade 2 disability % point towards low disease awareness and late case detection. There are other variables at play including the geographic location contributing to the footfall of the mainly tribal population in the hospital, healthcare accessibility and availability of preventive measures.

We also tried to compare the data obtained from the institute to the state and national NLEP data. The PR (0.16) and the annual new case detection rate (ANCDR) (1.43) in the current study were lower than the national average (PR = 0.66 and ANCDR = 10.2). However, the proportion of multibacillary cases is 60% higher than the national average (81% vs 50%). Both the female % and childhood leprosy % are lower than the national average of 26.6% vs 39.2% and 2.4% vs 8.7%, respectively. The most alarming observation is a significantly higher percentage of patients presenting with grade 2 disability of 33.2% vs 3.9% (national). This indicates that the cases are being detected late, and there is a failure of outreach services. Field studies will probably show a higher percentage that the programme has failed to reach.

India entered the post-elimination era in 2005, and since then, global strategies have been implemented as part of NLEP to further reduce the disease burden aiming to achieve the elimination of leprosy at the district level during the XII Plan (2012–2017). A newer initiative—Leprosy Case Detection Campaign (LCDC)—was launched in 2016 during which it was observed that the trend of two important indicators of NLEP, that is ANCDR and prevalence rate (PR), was almost static for 2006–2007. Also, the percentage of grade 2 disability among new cases detected increased from 3.10% (2010–2011) to 4.61% (2014–2015).

The high MB proportion and proportion of new cases detected with grade 2 disability indicate ongoing leprosy transmission in the population. The large percentage of the rural population suggests that leprosy eradication programme and awareness campaigns need to be more vigorously implemented to target these segments.

Conclusion

Although leprosy has been eliminated statistically at the global and national levels, a persistent high proportion of MB cases, leprosy reactions and new cases presenting with disabilities and deformities in our study raise the concern that the reality at ground level is different. Udaipur is a low-endemic region with lower than national PR, ANCDR and childhood leprosy %; however, it still contributes to more severe cases, which has not shown a decline over a decade. Thus, effective and sustained measures such as awareness about the disease, facilities for investigations and unhindered provision of therapy are needed, not just at tertiary care centres but also at the community level. Implementation of newer strategies including house-to-house visits by field-level workers and intensive Information, Education and Communication (IEC) activities is needed to target susceptible groups to achieve complete eradication of this disease from society. The study highlights the need for the continuation of targeted leprosy control activities and active case detection.
