Binding of Fusobacterium nucleatum autotransporter adhesin CbpF to human CEACAM1 and CEACAM5: A Velcro model for bacterium adhesion
Authors: Fan Shen, Linjie Li, Dongchun Yang, Zhexiao Tang, Lu Zhang, Kefang Liu, Wenzhe Hou, Zhuozhuang Lu, Jing-Yuan Fang, Jianxun Qi, Xin Zhao, George F. Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceFusobacterium nucleatum is associated with the initiation, metastasis, and immune evasion of colorectal cancer (CRC). Its adhesin CbpF promotes CRC progression by binding to host receptors CEACAM1 and CEACAM5. In this study, we resolve the structures of CbpF in complex with either human CEACAM1 or CEACAM5. We identify the Q78 residue in both receptors as molecular determinant for CbpF’s selective binding. Furthermore, we propose a Velcro-like bacterium–host adhesion model, wherein flexible bacterial proteins interdigitate with host receptor through multiple binding sites to stabilize interactions. This work provides a molecular-level explanation for plasticity of bacterial adhesion, deepening our understanding of pathogen–host interactions and laying foundation for developing anti-tumor colonization therapies. Notably, the Velcro model may fit other flexible bacterum–host adhesion interactions.

Cell junctions are essential structures in multicellular organisms that mediate cell adhesion and coordinate intercellular communication (1, 2). These junctions primarily comprise membrane proteins, cytoskeletal components, and extracellular matrix elements (3). Three major types are recognized: tight junctions, anchoring junctions (including desmosomes, hemidesmosomes, adherens junctions, and focal adhesions), and gap junctions (1). These specialized contact systems demonstrate tissue-specific localization patterns, enabling precise adaptation to organ-specific physiological requirements (1).

The ability to breach host barriers and establish stable adhesion to host cells is a critical determinant for successful colonization of bacteria (4, 5). Pathogenic bacteria have evolved a variety of molecular structures to achieve tight adhesion, including polysaccharides, fimbriae, and protein-based adhesins (4). Among these, adhesins exhibit selective binding to extracellular matrix components and host cell receptors, mediating close pathogen–host contact through specific molecular recognition (4). This bacterium–host interaction is dynamically regulated throughout infections. Under mechanical stress, bacteria, particularly rod-shaped species, demonstrate variable adhesion tightness to host cells (6). However, the regulatory mechanisms governing the dynamic adjustment of adhesion strength at the bacterium–host interface remain poorly characterized.

Polymorphic microbiomes have been recognized as a novel cancer hallmark due to the critical roles of microorganisms in cancer initiation, progression, and therapeutic response (7). The gut microbiota, comprising over 1,000 microbial species, play pivotal roles in intestinal physiology and pathology (8–10). Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum), an anaerobic bacterium, significantly enriches in the gut microbiota of colorectal cancer (CRC) patients, functions as a tumor-promoting agent through intestinal colonization, epithelial invasion, and immune modulation in CRC pathogenesis (11–13).

The trimeric autotransporter adhesin (TAA) family comprises virulence factors widely distributed in Gram-negative bacteria (14). These proteins consist of an N-terminal signal peptide, a passenger domain displayed on the bacterial surface and a C-terminal transmembrane autotransporter domain (14). Prominent examples include Yersinia enterocolitica YadA, Haemophilus influenzae Hia, Moraxella catarrhalis UspA, Salmonella enterica SadA, Burkholderia pseudomallei BpaA, Bartonella BadA, and the diffusely adhering Escherichia coli Eib proteins (15). These TAAs mediate critical pathogenic processes by facilitating host cell adhesion, invasion, and biofilm formation (15). CbpF has been identified as a TAA of F. nucleatum that mediates host cell attachment and suppresses antitumor immunity via binding to two members of the carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecule (CEACAM) family, CEACAM1 and CEACAM5 (16–19).

The extracellular immunoglobulin V-set (IgV) domain represents the extensively characterized functional module of CEACAM1 and CEACAM5 (20). This domain mediates homotypic (CEACAM1-CEACAM1, CEACAM5-CEACAM5) or heterotypic (CEACAM1-CEACAM5/6) interactions, thereby orchestrating intercellular adhesion, and engages diverse microbial adhesins to facilitate bacterium–host attachment (21–27). CEACAM1, which contains an intracellular immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif (ITIM), shares mechanistic parallels with canonical immune checkpoint molecules such as TIGIT and PD-1 in executing immunosuppressive functions (28). Furthermore, both CEACAM1 and CEACAM5 are canonical tumor-associated antigen highly expressed in a wide range of malignancies and strongly associated with tumorigenesis, invasion, and metastasis (29). Mechanistic characterization of the CbpF-CEACAM1/CEACAM5 interaction is crucial for developing targeted therapies against bacterial-facilitated tumorigenesis. Meanwhile, it might offer a new way for antimicrobial developments as antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is an ever-growing challenge to the human health (30–32).

During the preparation of the manuscript, a structural study of F. nucleatum 25586 (Fn25586) CbpF in complex with CEACAM1 revealed how a unique lateral loop extension engages the IgV domain of CEACAM1 (19). In this study, we determined the high-resolution structures of Fn23726 CbpF in complex with both CEACAM1 and CEACAM5, identifying the Q78 residue in CEACAM1/CEACAM5 as a critical residue for CbpF binding. Notably, Fn23726 CbpF has an additional β-roll at the N terminus, which slightly affects CbpF’s binding to CEACAM1. Furthermore, we uncovered a bacterium–host interaction mechanism termed the “Velcro-like model”. This model proposes that highly flexible pathogen adhesins (probably the physiological microbiota adhere to the intestinal cells with the same mechanism) interdigitate with host cell receptors via different binding mechanisms to regulate adhesion strength. Collectively, these findings enhance our understanding of F. nucleatum colonization in tumors and highlight potential therapeutic targets for disrupting bacterial-driven oncogenesis.

Results

F. nucleatum CbpF Specifically Binds to Both Human CEACAM1 and CEACAM5.

The recombinant ectodomain of Fn23726 CbpF was expressed in E. coli. Based on the elution peak positions of size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) molecular standards and SDS-PAGE analysis, the molecular weights of the CbpF monomer and trimer were 35 kDa and 105 kDa, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). To evaluate the binding capacities of CbpF to human CEACAM proteins, we expressed CEACAM1 and CEACAM3-CEACAM8 on HEK293T cells surface and assessed the binding capacity using flow cytometry (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Flow cytometry revealed specific binding to CEACAM1 (9.3%)-expressing and CEACAM5 (11.5%)-expressing cells, whereas no detectable binding occurred with other CEACAM family members (CEACAM3, CEACAM4, CEACAM6, CEACAM7, or CEACAM8) (Fig. 1A).

CEACAM1, a single-pass transmembrane protein, contains two cytoplasmic ITIM domains and an extracellular segment with one IgV and three IgC domains (Fig. 1B). CEACAM5 is GPI-anchored and features an extracellular segment with one IgV and six IgC domains (Fig. 1B). These proteins harbor 20 and 28 N-linked glycosylation sites, respectively (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, the IgV domains of human CEACAM1 and CEACAM5 involved in the interaction with CbpF were expressed in E. coli. Both CEACAM1 and CEACAM5 formed stable complexes with CbpF in solution, as demonstrated by distinct elution profiles and sedimentation coefficients (Fig. 1 C and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis using a 1:1 binding model revealed comparable binding affinities between CbpF and CEACAM5 (KD = 9.90 × 10−7 M) versus CEACAM1 (KD = 9.19 × 10−7 M) (Fig. 1 E and F). Notably, as CbpF exists predominantly in trimeric conformation, the calculated KD values based on a 1:1 stoichiometry may not fully reflect binding dynamics. Nevertheless, these results consistently demonstrate that CbpF exhibits similar binding abilities toward both CEACAM1 and CEACAM5. On the other hand, as trimeric CbpF was immobilized on the chip with flow through either CEACAM1 or CEACAM5, the experiments could be regarded as a 1:1 interaction.

Overall Structure of F. nucleatum CbpF.

A recent study has reported the structure of Fn25586 CbpF in complex with CEACAM1, revealing that CbpF exhibits no conformational differences before and after binding to CEACAM1 (19). Their research identified that H92, N99, and four residues (E142, N144, Q146, and Q148) of the EFNGQYQ loop of Fn25586 CbpF mainly interact with CEACAM1 (Fig. 2A). Sequence analysis identified that Fn23726 CbpF in our study shares 93.93% amino acid identity with Fn25586 CbpF (Fig. 2A). Notably, Fn23726 CbpF contains a distinctive insertion (SAFGYKNIANGIEG) and exhibits 30 additional amino acid substitutions (Fig. 2A).

To evaluate the molecular mechanism of Fn23726 CbpF in complex with either CEACAM1 or CEACAM5, we determined cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of both Fn23726 CbpF/CEACAM1 and Fn23726 CbpF/CEACAM5 complexes at resolutions of 2.11 Å and 2.40 Å, respectively (SI Appendix, Figs. S4 and S5 and Table S1). Both complexes adopt a scorpion-like architecture measuring 125 Å in length and 72 Å in width (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Fn23726 CbpF assembles as a trimer with each protomer binding one CEACAM1/CEACAM5 molecule, forming symmetrical 3:3 binding mode (SI Appendix, Fig. S6).

Structure analysis of the Fn23726 CbpF trimer (spanning residues S24-L286) of the complex revealed a classical lollipop-shaped architecture measuring 125 Å in length and 45 Å in width, organized into distinct head, neck, and stalk domains (Fig. 2B). Each monomer comprises 15 β-strands and four α-helices (Fig. 2B). The head domain (~70 Å long) is composed of the first 14 β-strands (Fig. 2B). The neck region initiates at the 15th β-strand, navigates through a large flexible loop and transitions into the stalk domain beginning at V243 (Fig. 2B). The stalk domain adopts a unique architecture featuring four discontinuous α-helices (α1–α4) (Fig. 2B). The hydrophobic cavity predominantly stabilized by F residues from three monomers, maintains the trimeric assembly through hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 2B). When comparing the structures of Fn23726 CbpF to Fn25586 CbpF, the former (~70 Å) has a longer head region than the latter (~64 Å), including an additional β-roll at the N terminus (Fig. 2C). The overall structures of the two CbpFs exhibit a RMSD of 0.926 Å (Fig. 2D).

The structural arrangement of CbpF closely resembles TAA in other bacteria, such as YadA, EibD, and UspA (SI Appendix, Fig. S7) (19, 33–35). Key structural distinctions between CbpF and these TAAs lie in β-roll number and trimeric hydrophobic core residues within the head domain (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). I, L, and V residues observed in YadA, UspA, and EibD are less predominant in CbpF (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Furthermore, whereas the stalk domains of YadA, UspA, and EibD adopt canonical coiled-coil configurations, CbpF features discontinuous α-helices (SI Appendix, Fig. S7).

The Differences Between Fn23726 CbpF and Fn25586 CbpF in Their Interactions with CEACAM1.

The binding of CbpF to CEACAM1 is localized into two distinct patches (Fig. 3 A and B). In the Fn23726 CbpF/CEACAM1 complex, patch 1 features six hydrogen bonds, while in the Fn25586 CbpF/CEACAM1 complex, patch 1 forms five hydrogen bonds (Fig. 3 A and B). Within patch 1, residues N157 (N144) and Q159 (Q146) of both Fn23726 and Fn25586 CbpF form hydrogen bonds with Y68, Q78, and T90 of CEACAM1 (Fig. 3 A and B). Additionally, E142 of Fn25586 CbpF forms a hydrogen bond with T90 of CEACAM1, an interaction absence in the Fn23726 CbpF complex (Fig. 3 A–C). N157 of Fn23726 CbpF also forms two hydrogen bonds with S66, which are not observed in Fn25586 CbpF/CEACAM1 (Fig. 3 A, B, and D). Q161 of Fn23726 CbpF forms a hydrogen bond with Q78, while its Fn25586 CbpF counterpart Q148 bonds with T90 (Fig. 3 A, B, and E). In patch 2, H105 (H92) of CbpF binds S127 of CEACAM1 through a hydrogen bond (Fig. 3 A and B). However, in the Fn23726 CbpF/CEACAM1 complex, R98 forms a hydrogen bond with D128, while in Fn25586 CbpF, this residue is replaced by H85, which cannot form this hydrogen bond (Fig. 3 A, B, and F). Instead, N99 of Fn25586 CbpF forms a hydrogen bond with D128 (Fig. 3 A, B, and F). Van der Waals (Vdw) contacts were calculated and revealed 137 contacts for Fn23726 CbpF with CEACAM1 versus 176 for Fn25586 (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 and Table S2). The patch 1 interaction residues on CbpF are conserved, while patch 2 residues exhibit diversity (Fig. 3G and SI Appendix, Fig. S8 and Table S2).

Sequence alignment of the IgV domain of CEACAM1 and CEACAM5 revealed 89.52% identity. Crucially, residues involved in hydrogen bonding within the Fn23726 CbpF/CEACAM1 complex are conserved in the Fn23726 CbpF/CEACAM5 complex (Figs. 3A and 4A). The 0.225 Å RMSD between these complexes demonstrates high structural similarity (Fig. 4B).

The total Vdw contacts between CEACAM5 and Fn23726 CbpF are 121, slightly fewer than those 137 contacts of CEACAM1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 and Table S3). In patch 1, there are two residues differences between CEACAM1 (A83 and Q123) and CEACAM5 (V83 and H123) (Fig. 4 C–E and SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). Additionally, CEACAM1 contains two residues (F63 and D74) that interact with Q140 and Q159 of CbpF, respectively (Fig. 4 C and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). In patch 2, CEACAM5 forms additional contacts with CbpF (L129-S111, T86-F132, and L129-H105) comparing with CEACAM1 (Fig. 4 C and G and SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). Nevertheless, the key CbpF binding sites remain similar between CEACAM1 and CEACAM5, indicating that different residues contribute little to the binding affinity. Instead, it is the collective binding contribution with less amino acid specificities. Notably, the three N-linked glycosylation sites (N104, N111, and N115) in the IgV domain of CEACAM1 and the two in CEACAM5 (N104 and N115) do not participate in CbpF interactions.

The Key Residue Q78 Mediates the CbpF-CEACAM1/CEACAM5 Interaction.

To delineate the molecular basis of CbpF-CEACAM1/CEACAM5 specificity, we performed sequence alignment and conservation analysis of key residues on the interface across CEACAM family members, including CEACAM1, CEACAM3, CEACAM5, CEACAM6, CEACAM7, and CEACAM8 (Fig. 5 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Among the 22 residues crucial for CbpF binding, a comparison with CEACAM1 shows that CEACAM3 and CEACAM5 each exhibit 2 substitutions, while CEACAM6 contains 3 substitutions (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S10A). In contrast, CEACAM4, CEACAM7, and CEACAM8 display 10 to 16 substitutions (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S10A). Six residues (F63, S66, Q78, T90, S127, and D128) forming the hydrogen bond are entirely absent in CEACAM4 and CEACAM7, while CEACAM8 retains only T90, which precludes their binding to CbpF (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S10A). Compared to CEACAM1, CEACAM5 (A83V and Q123H) and CEACAM3 (Q78L and A83V) show two substitutions each, whereas CEACAM6 carries an additional F63I mutation than CEACAM3 (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S10A).

The preserved binding affinity of CbpF to CEACAM5 and CEACAM1 suggests minimal effects by A83V or Q123H substitution (Fig. 1 D and E). These observations indicate that Q78 plays a critical role in mediating CEACAM1/CEACAM5 binding to CbpF (Fig. 5 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Using our CbpF/CEACAM1 and CbpF/CEACAM5 complex structures, we modeled the interactions of CEACAM6 (PDB ID code 4WHC) and CEACAM8 (PDB ID code 4Y88) with CbpF. Structural analysis revealed that L78 in CEACAM6 disrupts the hydrogen bond with the N157 residue of CbpF, whereas R78 in CEACAM8 retains potential for this interaction (Fig. 5 C and D). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations demonstrated that the molecular mechanics generalized Born surface area (MMGBSA) free energies of the CbpF/CEACAM1-Q78L and CbpF/CEACAM1-Q78R are −78.20 kcal/mol and −91.24 kcal/mol, which are higher than CbpF/CEACAM1 (−103.04 kcal/mol) (Table 1). Similarly, the values of MMGBSA binding free energies of the CbpF/CEACAM5-Q78L and CbpF/CEACAM5-Q78R complexes are 25.40 and 30.45 kcal/mol higher than that of the CbpF/CEACAM5 (Table 1).

To validate the functional role of Q78, we generated single mutants by substituting Q78 in the IgV domain of CEACAM1/CEACAM5 with L78 or R78. Additionally, double mutants were constructed through simultaneously replacing R77 with S78 and Q78 with L78. Mutational analysis revealed abolished binding signals for both Q78L and Q78R variants of CEACAM1 and CEACAM5, confirming Q78 as an essential binding residue (Fig. 5E). These results demonstrate that Q78 in CEACAM1/CEACAM5 determines CbpF binding specificity.

The Velcro-Like Binding Model of CbpF to Human CEACAM1 and CEACAM5.

During cryo-EM data processing, we observed a minor particle population exhibiting an antiparallel binding mode besides the predominant 3:3 binding stoichiometry (Fig. 6A). A survey of available structures revealed that the cis-dimerization of CEACAM1-IgV (PDB ID code 2GK2) involves in the ABED face, while trans-dimerization (PDB ID code 4QXW) is mediated by the GFCC’C’’ face (Fig. 6B) (36, 37). The weaker ABED-face binding affinity favors trans-dimers in solution (38). Gel filtration further confirmed dimeric states of CEACAM1-IgV and CEACAM5-IgV, showing distinct elution profiles relative to monomeric CEACAM8-IgV (SI Appendix, Fig. S11) (39). Analysis of CEACAM1-IgV dimer interfaces revealed steric clashes between the trans-dimer binding surface and the CbpF-binding site (Fig. 6 C and E). Conversely, the cis-dimer binding surface occupies the opposite face (Fig. 6 D and E). Structural comparison shows a single cis-dimer accommodates two CbpF trimers, matching our observed particle configuration (Fig. 6 A and F). Given CbpF’s membrane localization on F. nucleatum and its flexible stalk, this arrangement likely represents a physiological binding state (Fig. 6F).

Based on these findings, we propose two mechanistic models for F. nucleatum and host cell interface formation (Fig. 6G). 1) Two CbpF trimers bind two monomers within a CEACAM1/CEACAM5 cis-dimer (Fig. 6G). Residual binding sites on CbpF may recruit additional CEACAM1/CEACAM5 monomers or cis-dimers (Fig. 6G). Under this condition, the F. nucleatum membrane displayed reduced intermembrane distance and tight membrane apposition with the host cell membrane (Fig. 6G). 2) A single CbpF trimer engages three CEACAM1/CEACAM5 monomers or cis-dimers (Fig. 6G). In physiological contexts, structural heterogeneity exists where CbpF trimers engage 1 to 2 CEACAM1/5 monomers or dimers, enabling F. nucleatum to dynamically modulate binding avidity at the host–bacterium membrane interface (Fig. 6G). The interaction between cell-surface CEACAM1/CEACAM5 and CbpF on F. nucleatum mimics the Velcro mechanism (Fig. 6H). The bacterial outer membrane with adhesins corresponds to the loop components, while the host cell membrane and its receptors array resemble the hook elements (Fig. 6H). Adhesion occurs through loop-hook coupling (Fig. 6H). Under physiological shear forces, interactions reversibly weaken or dissociate, establishing a dynamic adhesion-separation cycle at the host–microbe interface (Fig. 6H).

Discussion

CbpF orchestrates dual pathological functions via CEACAM1/CEACAM5-mediated tumor niche colonization and CEACAM1-dependent immunosuppression. Mechanistically, CEACAM1/CEACAM5 overexpression on CRC cells establishes a high-affinity binding platform for CbpF, driving F. nucleatum enrichment within tumor tissues. This bacterial accumulation may activate Wnt/β-catenin signaling via FadA adhesin and its receptor E-cadherin to promote tumor cell proliferation (40). During Coxsackievirus A10 (CV-A10) infection, the virus hijacks the host receptor KRM1 to suppress β-catenin accumulation and block Wnt/β-catenin signaling transduction, resulting in Onychomadesis (41, 42). These paradigms establish that pathogen-specific receptor exploitation critically shapes disease phenotypes by modulating signaling pathways. Concurrently, CEACAM1 exploits its ITIMs to recruit SHP-1/SHP-2 phosphatases, which dephosphorylates key T cell activation nodes, thereby inhibiting antitumor immunity (20). While the Fn25586 CbpF/CEACAM1 complex structure was recently reported (19), our study reveals differences between Fn23726 CbpF and Fn25586 CbpF in both overall structures and CEACAM1-bound. Additionally, we also determined the CbpF/CEACAM5 complex structure.

The CEACAM family comprises CEACAM1, CEACAM3-CEACAM8, CEACAM16, and CEACAM18-CEACAM21, encoded by 12 distinct genes clustered on chromosome 19q13.1-13.2. These proteins exhibit high structural conservation in the N-terminal IgV domain, although each member performs unique function (29). CEACAM1, CEACAM3, CEACAM5, and CEACAM6 demonstrate bacterial binding capacity, while CbpF specifically targets CEACAM1/CEACAM5 through Q78-mediated receptor selectivity (16, 18, 24). In contrast to the ubiquitous tissue distribution of CEACAM1, CEACAM3 displays granulocyte-specific expression, whereas CEACAM5 and CEACAM6 are overexpressed in neoplastic cells (43, 44). The intracellular immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif (ITAM) of CEACAM3 mediates Syk kinase activation via phosphotyrosine signaling, driving phagosome maturation for enhanced microbial clearance (44). CbpF fails to bind Q78L-mutated CEACAM3, indicating F. nucleatum evolved this selective evasion strategy to bypass granulocyte-mediated immunity. However, this molecular adaptation simultaneously restricts its capacity for CEACAM6 engagement, highlighting an evolutionary trade-off. Such receptor selectivity arises from structural complementarity at host–pathogen interfaces and evolutionary pressures for immune system evasion.

Furthermore, we propose a Velcro-like model for bacterium–host interactions that bacterial proteins analogous to the highly flexible loops interact with host membrane proteins acting as the hooks. These extended loops establish multivalent contacts through diverse binding modalities, thereby controlling adhesion strength to host cells. Beyond the CbpF-CEACAM1/CEACAM5 axis, F. nucleatum employs distinct adhesins for tumor niche colonization, Fap2 targets Gal-GalNAc, RadD engages CD147, and FadA binds E-cadherin (40, 45, 46). Notably, these adhesins also converge on immune checkpoint pathways. CbpF, Fap2, and RadD interact with inhibitory receptors CEACAM1, TIGIT, and Siglec7 on T cells and NK cells, synergistically suppressing cytotoxic immune responses to potentiate tumor immune evasion (47, 48). This multireceptor engagement mechanism, regulating bacterial and cellular membrane binding through combinatorial interactions, deepens our understanding of the Velcro model. Moreover, this multimodal pathogenesis underscores the therapeutic potential of multivalent anti-adhesin antibodies designed to simultaneously block bacterial colonization and immune escape mechanisms (49, 50).

In summary, our study employs structural biology to unravel how F. nucleatum CbpF exploits CEACAM1 and CEACAM5, two same family proteins, to achieve tumor colonization and immune evasion. We identified two druggable targets, the EFNGQYQ loop on CbpF and Q78 residue on CEACAM1/CEACAM5. Additionally, we proposed a Velcro-like interaction model revealing the molecular mechanism of bacterial adhesion to host cells. This model provides a generalizable conception for understanding complex host–microbe interactions, while offering mechanistic insights into developing therapeutic strategies targeting bacterium–host interface in oncology.

Materials and Methods

Cell Lines.

HEK293T cells were from ATCC and cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS).

Plasmid Construction.

The constructions of eukaryotic expression plasmids were used the homologous recombination method. Briefly, the coding DNA sequences of CEACAM1 (NM_001712), CEACAM3 (NM_001815), CEACAM4 (NM_001817), CEACAM5 (NM_004363.6), CEACAM6 (NM_002483), CEACAM7 (NM_006890), and CEACAM8 (NM_001816) were amplified and added a Strep-tag II at the C terminus using the PrimeSTAR Max DNA Polymerase (Takara) and then inserted into EcoRI and XhoI enzyme sites of the linearized pCAGGS vector via homologous recombination.

For protein expression plasmid in prokaryote, the recombined ecto-domain (residues 21 to 295 and residues 346 to 368) of Fn23726 CbpF (GenBank: AVQ22222.1), with a TEV cleavage site, twin-Strep-tag II and 6 × His-tag added at the C terminus, was cloned into the pET21a vector. The constructs of human CEACAM1 and CEACAM5 have been described previously (10, 11). The IgV domain of human CEACAM1 (residues 35 to 141) and CEACAM5 (residues 35 to 144), which were fused with a TEV cleavage site and twin-Strep-tag II at the C terminus, were synthesized and cloned into the pET21a vector. All these sequences were codon-optimized for E. coli and inserted into NdeI and XhoI enzyme sites, respectively. The constructions of CEACAM1-IgV and CEACAM5-IgV mutants prokaryotic expression vectors were constructed by site-directed mutagenesis though homologous recombination.

Protein Production and Purification.

Recombinant proteins of CbpF, CEACAM1, CEACAM5, and corresponding mutants were expressed in E. coli strain BL21 (DE3). Bacteria were cultured at 37 °C with LB medium, induced with 1 mM IPTG at 16 °C for 12 h. Bacteria were harvested via centrifugation and lysed with TBS buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl and 150 mM NaCl, pH 8.0) via ultrasonic. After the sample was centrifuged to remove sediment, the supernatant was loaded on Strep-Tactin affinity column (GE Healthcare Life Sciences), and target proteins were eluted using 50 mM D-Biotin dissolved in TBS. Target proteins were subsequently purified by gel filtration using the Superose 6 100/300 GL column (GE Healthcare Life Sciences). The purity and molecular weight of the purified proteins were analyzed by SDS-PAGE. To obtain the complex of CbpF bound to CEACAM1 or CEACAM5, CbpF and CEACAM1 or CEACAM5 were mixed at a molar ratio of 1:12 and incubated at 4 °C for about 12 h in vitro. The complex was purified by gel filtration using Superose 6 100/300 GL column and analyzed by SDS-PAGE. To analyze the dimerization state, the IgV domains of CEACAM1, CEACAM5, and CEACAM8 were separately loaded onto a Superdex 200 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare Life Sciences), and the resulting chromatograms were overlaid.

Measurement of CbpF Binding to CEACAM by Flow Cytometry.

1 mg/mL CbpF protein was labeled with 50 uL 1 mg/ml FITC (Yeasen Biotechnology) for 8 h at 4 °C, followed by exchange with PBS buffer (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4 and 2 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.4) to remove excess FITC through centrifugation via a concentration tube. The expression vectors of CEACAM1 and CEACAM3-CEACAM8 were transfected into HEK293T cells using PEI transfection reagent. After 24 h of culture, 0.1 mg/ml FITC-labeled CbpF was coincubated with cells grown for 2 h at 37 °C. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS and then filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer. Flow cytometry was performed on BD FACSCanto II, and data were analyzed by FlowJo software.

Analytical Ultracentrifugation (AUC) Assay.

Sedimentation velocity experiments were performed using ProteomeLab XL-A analytical ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter). A volume of 400 μL protein sample and corresponding TBS buffer were loaded into a dedicated centrifugal cell. As centrifugation proceeds, optical detection systems continuously track molecular movements. Radial scans were collected at 280 nm with 4 min intervals. Scans were collected at 280 nm, with 3 min elapsed between each scan. Data were analyzed using the continuous sedimentation coefficient distribution c(s) model in the SEDFIT program.

SPR Assay.

The binding kinetics and affinities of CbpF to CEACAM1 or CEACAM5 were analyzed using Biacore 8 K biosensor (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) with SA chip at 25 °C with PBST buffer (PBS buffer with 0.005% Tween-20, pH 7.4). CbpF protein was labeled with 10 mM EZ-Link Sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotin reagent with a molar ratio of 1:20 for 2 h at 4 °C in PBS buffer. After exchange with PBST buffer to remove excess biotin reagent, biotinylated CbpF was coupled on SA chip as immobile phase. CEACAM1, CEACAM5 (with concentrations of 0.1875, 0.375, 0.75, 1.5, and 3 μM) and all point mutant proteins (with concentrations of 2.5, 5, 10, 20, and 40 μM) were injected into the chip. The kinetic parameters were analyzed using Biacore insight evaluation software (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) using 1:1 binding model. Briefly, in the experimental setup, two flow cells were employed. Flow cell 1 served as the reference channel without any immobilized ligand, while flow cell 2 contained immobilized CbpF as the experimental channel. The experimental curve was obtained by subtracting both the reference channel signal and zero-concentration analyte signals from the experimental channel data. A 1:1 binding model was then used to generate the fitted curve and calculate the binding kinetics parameters.

Cryo-EM Sample Preparation and Data Collection.

For cryo-EM sample preparation, 4 uL purified CbpF/CEACAM1 and CbpF/CEACAM5 complex at a concentration of 0.06 mg/mL was placed on the 300 and 400 mesh Au grid covered with porous carbon film and graphene oxide (Quantifoil) using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher) at 4 °C and 100% humidity. After adsorbing and blotting with a filter paper for 3 s, grids were plunged into liquid ethane and then stored in liquid nitrogen. Cryo-EM data were collected using a 300-kV Titan Krios transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher) equipped with a Falcon4i direct electron detector. Movies were recorded in superresolution counting mode, and detailed data collection information is seen in SI Appendix, Table S1.

Image Processing.

The data were processed using the cryoSPARC platform (51). Generally, MotionCor2 (52) was used to generate micrographs from the movies, followed by the CTF estimation (53), particles picked out and extracted and 2D classification. We selected serval particle stacks of 2D classification for 3D reconstruction via ab initial reconstruction (C3 symmetry) and heterogeneous refinement to generate 5 classes of volumes (54, 55). The best class was used for nonuniform refinement (55), Global/local CTF refinement, and DeepEMhancer sharpening (56). The resolution was determined by the FCS cut-off at 0.143 (57). The detailed data processing flow is shown in SI Appendix, Figs. S4 and S5.

Model Building, Refinement, and Validation.

The crystal structure of CEACAM1 (PDB ID code 4QXW)/CEACAM5 (PDB ID code 2QSQ) and the predicted structure of CbpF via Alphafold3 (58) were used as the model to fit the density map using UCSF ChimeraX (59). The model was then manually refined in Coot 0.9.8.92 (60). and autorefined using Phenix-1.20.1 real space refine program (61).The stereochemical quality of all models was assessed using MolProbity of Phenix. The statistics of model refinement and validation are summarized in SI Appendix, Table S1.

Structure Analysis and Visualization.

Vdw contacts were analyzed with the Contact program in CCP4 suite at a cutoff of 4.5 Å and hydrogen bonds with the PISA online server at a cutoff of 3.5 Å. All density maps were visualized with ChimeraX, and the atomic model representations were generated and analyzed with ChimeraX (59) or PyMOL (https://pymol.org/2/).

MD Simulations.

MD simulations were performed using the AMBER24 package with the AMBER ff14SB force field for protein parameterization (62). The structures of the mutant complexes were modeled based on the CbpF/CEACAM1 or CbpF/CEACAM5 complex. All systems were solvated in a cuboid box using the TIP3P water model, with a 12 Å buffer extending from the protein boundary to ensure sufficient solvent coverage (63). Long-range electrostatic interactions were handled using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method, while short-range Vdw forces were truncated at 10 Å (64). Chloride ions were added to neutralize the system charge. The system was minimized in three steps. The first step restrained the complex structure with a force of 100 kcal·mol−1·Å−2, allowing only the solvent to equilibrate; The second step restrained only the protein backbone atoms (C, CA, and N) with the same force; The third step relaxed all atoms. This process used the steepest descent algorithm for 5,000 steps, followed by 5,000 steps of the conjugate gradient algorithm. Subsequently, the system was gradually heated to 300 K over 100 ps using the V-rescale temperature coupling method under the canonical ensemble (NVT). A pressure of 1 bar was maintained via the Berendsen pressure coupling method during a 1 ns equilibration. Following these steps, a 50 ns production MD simulation was performed under NPT conditions with the Nosé-Hoover thermostat. The SHAKE algorithm was applied to constrain hydrogen bonds, enabling a 2 fs time step (65). Trajectory snapshots were saved every 10 ps for analysis. The last 20 ns of each MD trajectory were used to ensure conformational sampling from equilibrated states. Binding free energies (ΔEBinding) were calculated using the MMGBSA method (66).
