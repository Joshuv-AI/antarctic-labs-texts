Malaria among febrile patients during a yellow fever outbreak in Ghana: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Lidiwan Mensah, Anisa Abdulai, Gloria Amegatcher, Deborah Pratt, Evans Aduhene, Magdalene Sarah Nketia Ofori, Abdul Rahim Mohammed Sabtiu, Patience Lartekai Adams, Prince Ketorwoley, Christopher Mfum Owusu-Asenso, Nana Aba Setorwu Eyeson, Kwamena William Coleman Sagoe, Joseph Humphrey Kofi Bonney, Yaw Asare Afrane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBetween October 2021 and February 2022, there was an outbreak of yellow fever viral disease that spread within several districts in the northern part of Ghana. Malaria is the leading cause of death in most sub-Saharan countries including Ghana. It is important that surveillance is focused on malaria among febrile patients during a yellow fever outbreak in Ghana. This study investigated infection with malaria among febrile patients during a yellow fever outbreak in Ghana.MethodsThe study was a cross-sectional study conducted in two yellow fever outbreak sites (Wenchi and Damongo) and two non-outbreak sites (Kumbungu and Tamale). A total of 498 febrile patients from healthcare facilities were recruited in the rainy and dry seasons. A structured questionnaire was administered to collect patients’ demographic information. Venous blood was collected from consented study participants for malaria parasite detection via microscopy and nested Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR). Total RNA was extracted from serum samples for the detection of yellow fever virus using Reverse Transcription-PCR (RT-PCR).ResultsNone of the patients tested positive for yellow fever virus by RT-PCR. Out of a total of 498 febrile participants, 129/498 (25.90%) tested positive by PCR for P. falciparum parasites and 378 were examined for malaria microscopy with 54/378 (14.29%) testing positive to P. falciparum parasite detection. While there were 54/410 (13.17%) cases of P. falciparum infections via microscopy in the rainy season there was no positive infection in the dry season 0/88 (0%). However, the difference in malaria prevalence via PCR in the rainy and dry seasons was not significant (P > 0.05).ConclusionNucleic acid for yellow fever was not detected among all the febrile patients. High malaria prevalence was found in participants in the study sites, affecting preschool and the school-aged children the most. This suggests an underscoring urgent need for malaria interventions to mitigate high transmission within communities especially among school-based populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12936-026-06000-0.Keywords: Malaria, Yellow fever, Microscopy, PCR, Ghana, Outbreak

Introduction

Approximately 263 million malaria cases were recorded in 2023 in countries where malaria is endemic, with an estimated 6,552,000 cases recorded in Ghana according to the World Health Organization [1].

In Ghana, yellow fever exhibits different cyclical and seasonal patterns. Ghana has experienced yellow fever outbreaks in past years. This is evident as there has been yellow fever outbreaks in the country in 2011–2012, 2015–2016 and 2021–22 [2, 3]. Historic data estimates the occurrence of yellow fever outbreak every 5 years, particularly among unvaccinated people in the northern regions of Ghana [4]. Transmission of yellow fever virus to humans takes place in three cycles, namely; sylvatic, intermediate and urban transmission [5]. Early outbreaks were predominantly urbans cycles where vaccination coverage against yellow fever was very low, while the dynamics drifted to sylvatic/intermediate cycles after many people in the country got vaccinated [6]. In a recent outbreak that started from October 2021 to February 2022, a total of 202 suspected cases with 70 confirmed and 35 deaths were reported with a case-fatality rate of 50% [7]. The outbreak was reported in several districts in four regions in Ghana (Savannah, Upper West, Bono and Oti regions) [8]. The recent outbreak started in the Sahel Savannah zone of Ghana (West and North Gonja districts), where malaria prevalence is very high [9]. As part of outbreak response, the WHO supported vaccination campaigns that targeted thousands of Ghanaians aged 9 months to 60 years and included molecular and serological diagnostics in controlling the outbreak [7].

Symptoms of malaria includes fever, chills and headaches and general body aches, and these mimic the intial symptoms of yellow fever [10]. Due to this overlap in symptoms, yellow fever could initially be misdiagnosed as malaria, and vice-versa, which could affect treatment or patient management. This study therefore sought to understand this phenomenon among febrile patients during a yellow fever outbreak in Ghana.

Methods

Study design and study site description

This was a cross-sectional study that covered the major climatic seasons in Ghana (April to October 2022). Samples from the dry season were obtained from April to May 2022, and that for the rainy season September to October 2022. The study was conducted in four districts: Wenchi (7.7420° N, 2.1008° W) and Damongo (9.3634° N, 1.6761° W) which had facilities that recorded cases during the outbreak whilst Kumbungu (9° 22′ 33.34" N 0° 42′ 29.67" W) and Tamale (9° 33′ 45.2" N 1° 01′ 54.6" W) had no facilities recording any cases during the outbreak (Fig. 1). The outbreak sites were selected to assess continual transmission of yellow fever infections within the communities while the non-outbreak zones were selected to assess whether the yellow fever virus has spread to non-outbreak communities. Damongo, Kumbungu and Tamale are in the Sahel Savannah zone of Ghana, which is characterized by a unimodal rainfall pattern from May to November and a long dry season from December to April. The mean annual temperature ranging from 28 to 42 °C. Wenchi is in the forest zone of Ghana. The forest zone has a tropical rainforest climate, with an annual average temperature of 26.4 °C.

Map of study sites

Study site

The study was carried out in two yellow fever outbreak and two non-outbreak sites. The outbreak sites were Wenchi and Damongo while the non-outbreak sites included Tamale and Kumbungu. The facilities included Tamale Central Hospital, Kumbungu King's Medical Centre, Wenchi Methodist Hospital, and West Gonja District Hospital. Tamale Central Hospital is a public healthcare facility in the Northern region which serves over 100, 000 residents in the Tamale Metropolis and its environs. Kings Medical Centre (KMC) is the only hospital in the Kumbungu district of the Northern region of Ghana. It provides basic health care to the more than 170,000 residents of the Kumbungu and neighboring districts. Wenchi Methodist Hospital is a 238-bed facility in the Wenchi Municipality. The hospital serves as a referral center for 19 health facilities within the Wenchi Municipality and others including Tain and Banda districts. The West Gonja Hospital is in Damongo, West Gonja District in the Northern Region of Ghana. The hospital is a 100-bed facility and that serves the over 41,000 residents in the West Gonja District.

Study population

The study consisted of febrile patients seeking treatment at healthcare facilities located in the selected study sites. Febrile patients of any age who visited the healthcare facilities in the selected study sites and provided informed consent or child assent to participate were included in the study. Febrile participants were defined as individuals with a body temperature equal to or higher than 37.5 °C. Individuals with a body temperature below 37.5 °C were not included in this study. Additionally, febrile patients who were too ill or weak during the data or sample collection period, were excluded from the study.

Sample size and sampling technique

A minimum sample size of 280 was calculated using the Cochran formula n = z2 pq/d2 at 19.8% prevalence of malaria from Bam and Anane [11], 95% confidence interval (CI), and 5% margin of error. To cater for the non-completeness rate, 15% of the calculated sample size was added. Therefore, 498 febrile participants were recruited for this study to increase the statistical power of the study. Participants were randomly selected for this study.

Sample and data collection procedure

Approximately 3 ml of venous blood samples were drawn from each eligible participant after consent and or assent was obtained. The blood samples were divided into two parts: one portion was collected into serum separator tubes for the detection of RNA of yellow fever virus. The remaining portion was used to prepare blood spots and thick and thin blood smears for malaria diagnosis. Dried blood spots (DBS) was prepared by dropping 50 μl of blood onto strips of Whatman™ #3 filter paper. The DBS were then air-dried and placed into zip-lock bags containing silica gel. Also, 100ul of the blood was used to prepare thick and thin blood smears on microscope slides using previously described protocols [12]. The serum samples were transported to and analyzed at the Virology Department of the Noguchi Memorial Institute for Medical Research (NMIMR) for the detection of yellow fever virus. Paper blots and thin and thick blood films were sent to and analyzed at the Virology Unit at Department of Medical Microbiology, Korle-bu Teaching hospital, University of Ghana. A structured questionnaire was used to collect demographic and other information including, age, gender, address, occupation, vaccination status, travel history and current clinical symptoms (Additional file 1).

Molecular detection of yellow fever virus

Total RNA was extracted and purified from the serum samples of patients using the QIAmp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. A one-step reverse transcriptase real-time PCR reaction was used for the molecular detection of yellow fever virus using well-described protocols [13].

Detection of P. falciparum by microscopy

The thick and thin smears were processed and stained with Giemsa [14]. Parasites in the thick smears were counted against 200 leukocytes when the slide was positive; otherwise, the whole slide was carefully scanned before being declared negative. Parasite densities were converted to the number of parasites per microliter of blood, assuming a leukocyte count of 8000 cells/μl. Parasites in thick smears were also counted against 200 leukocytes. Stained slides were examined by two independent microscopists to ensure quality.

Detection of P. falciparum using nested polymerase chain reaction

Extracted DNA from the dried blood spots on the Whatman filter papers was carried out with the use of the Chelex extraction protocol as previously described [15]. The extracted DNA was subjected to nested nucleic acid amplifications to detect P. falciparum infections using well-described protocols [16]. P. falciparum genus-specific primers, rPLU5 and rPLU6 were used for the first step of the nested PCR to amplify a 1100 bp PCR product of the 18S rRNA. P. falciparum species-specific primers, rFAL1 and rFAL2 in the second nested reaction to amplify a 205 bp product indicated a P. -falciparum-infection.

Data analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to assess the occurrence, patterns, and socio-demographic characteristics of study participants. Additionally, chi-square tests were carried out at a 95% confidence level to explore the significant differences between socio-demographic factors and malaria infection among the study participants. The parasite prevalence of each population was calculated as the percentage of positive cases over the total examined.

Laboratory results for each participant were entered into Microsoft Excel 2019 and analyzed using SPSS version 25.0. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 with a 95%CI, and chi-square tests were used to assess associations between categorical variables.

Ethical consideration

A written informed consent was obtained from all study participants above 18 years before recruitment into the study. For patients below 18 years, a written consent was obtained from their legal guardians. In addition, assent was obtained from the minors through signing of an assent form (older minors) or a verbal assent (for younger minors). Recruitment of study participants and blood sample collections were done at two time points in the study sites: in the dry season (April to May 2022) and the rainy season (September to October, 2022).

Results

Characteristics of the study participants

A total of 498 febrile participants were included in the study during both dry and rainy seasons. The study consisted of a higher proportion of female participants (313, 62.9%) compared to male participants (185, 37.1%). Most of the participants were older than 15 years (321, 64.5). The highest number of participants were recruited from Kumbungu (140, 28.1%) while the least number from Wenchi (101, 20.3%). A higher number of participants were recruited in the rainy season (410, 82.3%) compared to the dry season (88, 17.7%). A high proportion of the febrile patients in the study (293, 58.8%) had been vaccinated against yellow fever (Table 1).

Carriage of yellow fever virus

All 498 febrile participants screened for the presence of yellow fever virus tested negative by RT-PCR. The positive and negative controls used for quality control and each test run came up clearly as valid with a CT value of 34.99 as the positive and the negative was undetermined. Any sample with CT value greater than 40 was considered as negative for yellow fever virus.

Malaria infections within the communities

Seasonal variations in P. falciparum infections across the study sites

Of a total of 498 febrile participants, (129/498, 25.90%) tested positive for P. falciparum using PCR. Again, using PCR for detection of P. falciparum parasites, there were (17/101, 16.83%) cases in Wenchi, (32/137, 23.36%) cases in Damongo, (23/120, 19.17%) cases in Tamale and the highest (57/140, 40.71%) cases in Kumbungu. The highest P. falciparum prevalence by PCR during the rainy season was observed in participants aged under 5 (27/66, 40.91%) and 5 to 9 (12/33, 36.36%) while the older age groups, 10 to 15 and above 15 had P. falciparum parasite prevalence of (9/29, 31.03%) and (65/282, 23.05%) respectively. The differences in parasite prevalence by PCR across the different age groups of study participants was statistically significant (χ2 = 10.921 df = 4, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2).

Seasonal variations in P. falciparum infections across the study sites

A total of 378/498 febrile participants were tested for P. falciparum using microscopy, (that is, all participants except those from Tamale). Samples from Tamale (120) were not examined for P. falciparum using microscopy due to logistic reasons. A total of (54/378, 14.29%) were positive to P. falciparum parasite detection using microscopy. Also, using microscopy for detection of P. falciparum parasites, there were (2/101, 1.98%) cases in Wenchi, (21/137, 15.33%) cases in Damongo and the highest (31/140, 22.14%) cases in Kumbungu (Fig. 3a). Using microscopy, the highest P. falciparum parasite prevalence was observed in participants aged 10–15 years (6/34, 17.65%) (Fig. 3b). P. falciparum prevalence via microscopy was significantly higher in the rainy season, (54/410, 13.17%) compared to the dry season, (0/88, 0%) (P < 0.001). However, the differences in parasite prevalence by microscopy was not significant (P > 0.05).

Discussion

Febrile illness is a common source of morbidity and mortality in Ghana and Africa. Etiologic agents of these febrile illnesses include P. falciparum parasite that causes malaria, Samonella typhi that causes typhoid fever, Streptococcus pneumoniae that causes pneumonia and yellow fever virus that causes yellow fever [17].

The consideration of only malaria and yellow fever in the study was because of recent outbreak in febrile patients seeking healthcare in health facilities located in two yellow fever outbreak districts (Wenchi and Damongo). All patients tested negative for yellow fever virus by RT-PCR. It is likely that we did not detect any positive yellow fever cases due to of our small sample size, high vaccination coverage, time of sample collection and diagnostic technique. Moreover, it is likely transmission may have been interrupted and/or there could have been an extensive protection against the viral infection due to naturally acquired immunity or vaccination. It is documented that yellow fever virus has a short viremic phase from symptomatology to detection, hence the time between sample collection and detection by RT-PCR assay might have been an issue [18]. In Ghana, historic data estimates the occurrence of yellow fever outbreak every 5 years, particularly among unvaccinated wanderers in the northern regions of Ghana. This is evident as there has been yellow fever outbreaks in Ghana in 2011–2012, 2015–2016 and 2021–22 [19]. Early outbreaks were predominantly urbans cycles where vaccination coverage against the disease was very low, while the dynamics drifted to sylvatic/intermediate cycles after many people in the country got vaccinated [20]. A similar studies in West Africa further reports that laboratory-confirmed yellow fever cases are rarely identified during inter-epidemic periods despite ongoing risk [21].

Malaria percentage positivity using PCR was higher in the rainy season compared to the dry season. This is expected as during rainy season there is an increased breeding habitats for the female anopheles’ mosquitoes [22], which subsequently leads to high malaria cases. However, the difference was not statistically significant. Study site, age group and season of sample collection were significantly associated with the carriage of P. falciparum parasites in this study. A high malaria percentage prevalence was observed in the age group 10 to 15 years using PCR in the dry season. This suggests a possible shift of the burden of malaria from children under 5 years to the older age groups [23, 24]. The higher risk of malaria infection during the dry season among children aged 5–9 years compared to children under 5 years is similar to other studies [25, 26].

In Ghana, fever is frequently presumed to be malaria, increasing the risk of misdiagnosis of other infections, including yellow fever. In order to prevent misdiagnosis of fever, there should be an improved laboratory-confirmed diagnostic capacity of febrile illnesses. In general, an increased vaccination coverage for yellow fever is a pivotal public health action needed to better control and prevent yellow fever. The expansion of integrated vector control measures such as the widespread use of insecticide-treated nets and indoor residual spraying together with timely is critical for reducing transmission and controlling malaria.

Limitations of study

The absence of detected yellow fever virus-positive cases in this study may, in part, be related to the diagnostic approach used. The incorporation of serological methods such as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) could potentially improve case detection, as these assays detect antibodies produced in response to yellow fever virus infection. In contrast to reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), which is most effective during the viremic phase of infection, ELISA may identify evidence of recent or past exposure after viremia has resolved, thereby providing a wider diagnostic window.

Conclusion

The study found no carriage of yellow fever infections in febrile patients seeking healthcare in selected health facilities. However, malaria prevalence was high in participants within the study sites. Preschool and the school-age groups had the highest malaria prevalence. This study may contribute to the evidence of interventions deployment targeting school children as the current intervention only targets pre-school children.
