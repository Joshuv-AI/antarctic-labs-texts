The Antipsychotic Aripiprazole Induces Cytotoxicity in Bladder Cancer Cells While Preserving Urothelial and Bladder Function
Authors: Liam A O'Callaghan, Katie Powell, Russ Chess‐Williams, Catherine McDermott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAripiprazole (ARI), an atypical antipsychotic, has demonstrated anticancer activity in several malignancies and may be a candidate for drug repurposing as an intravesical therapy for bladder cancer, particularly non‐muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC). This study evaluated whether brief, intravesical‐like ARI exposure could induce cytotoxic effects in bladder cancer cells while preserving normal bladder structure and function. RT4 and T24 bladder cancer cells, together with non‐malignant UROtsa urothelial cells, were exposed to ARI (1–300 μM) for 30 min or 2 h, and viability was assessed at 24, 48 and 72 h. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation was measured in RT4 and T24 cells after 2 h pretreatment, while caspase‐3 activity and stress‐associated protein expression were examined in T24 cells. In parallel, an ex vivo porcine bladder model was used to assess the effects of luminal ARI pretreatment (300 μM, 2 h) on urothelial thickness, ATP and acetylcholine release, detrusor contractility, β‐adrenergic relaxation, and nerve‐evoked responses. ARI reduced viability in a concentration‐dependent manner in RT4, T24 and UROtsa cells, with greater cytotoxicity after 2 h pretreatment. In bladder cancer cells, ROS increased only at higher concentrations, whereas ARI increased caspase‐3 activity at lower concentrations and altered multiple stress‐related proteins in T24 cells. In porcine bladder, ARI preserved urothelial structure and mediator release, maintained detrusor and neurogenic function, and enhanced the inhibitory influence attributed to urothelium‐derived inhibitory factor (UDIF). Collectively, these findings identify ARI as a mechanistically active yet bladder‐sparing candidate for intravesical repurposing and support its further evaluation as a potential therapy for bladder cancer.Keywords: anticancer, antipsychotic, apoptosis, aripiprazole, drug repurposing, intravesical therapy, urology

Introduction

Bladder cancer is a common malignancy worldwide and is characterized by a high risk of recurrence and progression [1, 2], necessitating repeated intravesical treatments and lifelong cystoscopic surveillance. Intravesical therapy involves the direct instillation of therapeutic agents into the bladder through a catheter. Current intravesical therapies such as mitomycin C (MMC) and Bacillus Calmette‐Guérin (BCG) reduce recurrence but are frequently limited by local toxicity [3, 4, 5, 6]. Both agents can compromise urothelial integrity, provoke irritative symptoms, and impair bladder compliance, leading to treatment discontinuation in a substantial proportion of patients [7, 8, 9, 10]. There is an unmet need for therapeutic strategies that provide effective local tumour control while preserving bladder structure and function, particularly in patients who are intolerant or refractory to current regimens.

Drug repurposing offers a pragmatic approach to address this gap, enabling accelerated translational trajectories by leveraging existing clinical safety, pharmacokinetic, and drug–drug interaction data. Aripiprazole (ARI), an atypical antipsychotic and partial D2 receptor agonist [11], displays complex pharmacology extending beyond dopaminergic modulation. Several preclinical studies have demonstrated that ARI can induce cytotoxicity or radiosensitisation in diverse cancer cell types, often through caspase activation, modulation of stress‐activated protein kinases such as JNK and p38, and suppression of antioxidant defenses [12]. However, systemic administration of ARI is limited by dose‐dependent adverse effects, including metabolic disturbances [13] and extrapyramidal symptoms [14]. These toxicities restrict its feasibility for chronic or high‐dose use in oncology settings. Consequently, local intravesical delivery represents an attractive strategy to achieve high luminal drug concentrations while minimizing systemic exposure and associated side effects.

During intravesical therapy, the bladder mucosa is directly exposed to luminal drug delivery. The mucosa comprises the urothelium, underlying lamina propria, and a muscularis mucosae in species including pig and human, together with interstitial cell populations implicated in mucosal signaling and spontaneous activity [15]. Within this compartment, the urothelium is a highly specialized epithelial layer that regulates permeability and protects the underlying lamina propria and detrusor muscle [16]. Disruption of this barrier alters sensory signaling and detrusor function, processes that also underlie inflammatory bladder disorders such as interstitial cystitis, where urothelial dysfunction and heightened afferent activity contribute to chronic pain and urgency [17]. Beyond its barrier role, the mucosa regulates bladder excitability through the release of paracrine mediators, including a diffusible urothelium‐derived inhibitory factor (UDIF) that attenuates cholinergic contractions [18]. These functions are critical to maintaining bladder homeostasis during luminal drug exposure.

The present study investigates the effects of short‐duration exposure to ARI on both bladder cancer cell biology and bladder tissue function. The capacity of ARI to induce cytotoxic and stress‐related mechanisms was examined in RT4 (low‐grade) and T24 (high‐grade) bladder cancer cell lines, while its impact on urothelial structure and mediator‐dependent regulation of detrusor activity was evaluated using an ex vivo porcine bladder model. Integrating mechanistic analyses in relevant cancer cell models with tissue‐level functional assessments provides a framework for evaluating ARI's potential as a bladder‐sparing therapeutic agent and for elucidating its underlying cellular mechanisms of action.

Methods

Cell Culture and Treatments

Human bladder cancer cell lines T24 (high‐grade, invasive; RRID:CVCL_0554) and RT4 (low‐grade, non‐muscle invasive; RRID:CVCL_0036) were obtained from CellBank Australia. The immortalized non‐malignant urothelial cell line UROtsa (RRID:CVCL_0571) was provided by Dr. Scott Garrett (University of North Dakota, USA) [19]. All cell lines were authenticated by the supplier or source using short tandem repeat profiling and were screened for mycoplasma contamination using a Lonza MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza, LT07‐318), with negative results prior to experimentation.

T24 and RT4 cells were maintained in McCoy's 5A medium (GlutaMAX, phenol red) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS), 50 U/mL penicillin, and 50 μg/mL streptomycin. UROtsa cells were cultured in DMEM‐low glucose supplemented with 5% FBS, 1 mg/mL glucose, and antibiotics. All cells were grown at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator and sub‐cultured at 75%–90% confluence. Cells were seeded into 96‐well plates at optimized densities (T24: 0.3 × 104, RT4: 0.9 × 104, UROtsa: 1 × 104 per well) and allowed to attach for 24 h. ARI (ThermoFisher Scientific, 457990010) was prepared as a 100 mM DMSO stock stored at −20°C (≤ 30 days) and diluted into culture medium immediately before use, with final vehicle concentrations ≤ 0.3% (v/v) DMSO. Cells were exposed to ARI (1–300 μM) for either 30 min or 2 h, reflecting intravesical instillation times, then washed and incubated in fresh drug‐free medium. Viability and mechanistic assays were conducted at 24, 48, and 72 h post‐treatment.

Viability and Cytotoxicity Assays

Cell viability was assessed in RT4, T24 and UROtsa cells 24–72 h following ARI pretreatment by incubating cells with a 44 μM resazurin solution (Merck, R7017) prepared in culture medium. Fluorescence (Ex/Em = 530/590 nm), reflecting metabolic reduction of resazurin and therefore viable cell number, was measured using a FLUOstar Omega microplate reader. In parallel, methylene blue staining was performed at 72 h as a complementary endpoint to assess adherent cell biomass. Following the resazurin assay, cells were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for at least 30 min, stained with 0.1% methylene blue (Merck, M9140) in 0.01 M borate buffer (pH 8.5) for 30 min at room temperature, washed to remove unbound dye, and air‐dried. Bound dye was then eluted with developing buffer (1:1 ethanol and 0.1 M HCl), and absorbance was measured at 650 nm using a FLUOstar Omega microplate reader.

ROS Generation

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) production was measured in RT4 and T24 cells using the DCFH‐DA assay (Merck, 6883). Following 2 h ARI pretreatment, cells were washed with 1× PBS and incubated in fresh culture medium for a further 24, 48 or 72 h. At each timepoint, cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH‐DA for 40 min at 37°C in the dark, and fluorescence was measured using a FLUOstar Omega (Ex/Em = 530/590 nm). Values were normalized to vehicle controls and corrected for viable cell number using resazurin data.

Caspase‐3 Activity

Caspase‐3 activity was assessed in T24 cells using a fluorometric assay according to the manufacturer's instructions (Merck, MAK457) 24 h following ARI pretreatment (10 or 30 μM, 2 h). Immediately prior to assay, culture medium was aspirated and working reagent containing caspase‐3 substrate Ac‐DEVD‐AFC, and DTT was added directly to each well, enabling simultaneous cell lysis and caspase activity measurement. Following incubation, fluorescence was read at Ex/Em = 400/490 nm using a FLUOstar Omega microplate reader. Caspase‐3 activity was normalized to resazurin viability values from matched wells to account for differences in viable cell number.

Human Cell Stress Protein Array

The Proteome Profiler Human Cell Stress Array Kit (R&D Systems, ARY018) was used to examine relative changes in stress‐associated proteins in T24 bladder cancer cells following ARI pretreatment. T24 cells were exposed to ARI (30 μM, 2 h) or vehicle control, and total protein was extracted 24 h later using the kit lysis buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Merck, P2714) and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (1 mM; ThermoFisher Scientific, 36978). Lysates were clarified by centrifugation (14 000 × g , 10 min, 4°C), and protein concentration was determined by Bradford assay using bovine serum albumin standards (ThermoFisher Scientific, 23209). For each membrane, 100 μg of total protein was incubated overnight at 4°C with the Human Cell Stress Array, which enables the simultaneous detection of multiple cell stress‐related proteins in a single sample. The proteins assessed included ADAMTS1, Bcl‐2, Carbonic Anhydrase IX (CA9), Cited‐2, COX‐2, Cytochrome c, Dkk‐4, FABP1/L‐FABP, HIF‐1α, HIF‐2α, HSP60, HSP70, IDO, NF‐κB1, p21/CIP1, p27/Kip1, p‐HSP27 (S78/S82), p‐JNK Pan (T183/Y185), p‐p38α (T180/Y182), p‐p53 (S46), PON1, PON2, PON3, SIRT2, SOD2, and Thioredoxin‐1. Following incubation, membranes were washed, incubated with the supplied biotinylated detection antibody cocktail, and visualized using chemiluminescent substrate according to the manufacturer's instructions. Signal intensity was captured using a Bio‐Rad ChemiDoc MP imaging system, and relative protein expression was quantified by densitometric analysis of each spot using ImageJ software (v2.16.0). Results from ARI‐pretreated and vehicle control samples were compared by analyzing the signal intensity of each array spot to determine relative changes between conditions.

Porcine Bladder Tissue Studies

Bladders from female Landrace pigs (~6 months) were obtained from a local abattoir and transported on ice in Krebs‐bicarbonate solution (mM: NaCl 118.4, NaHCO3 24.9, KCl 4.7, CaCl2 1.9, MgSO4 2.4, KH2PO4 1.15, C6H12O6 11.7). Domes were opened longitudinally and mounted in modified Ussing chambers for luminal pretreatment with ARI (300 μM, 2 h) or vehicle control [20]. After exposure, tissues were washed and dissected into full‐thickness bladder strips (intact), urothelium/lamina propria (urothelium/LP), or detrusor muscle.

Urothelial Mediator Release and Histology

After tissues were mounted in organ baths and allowed to equilibrate for 1 h, samples of the bathing solution were collected to measure basal mediator release. ATP release was quantified using a luciferin‐luciferase assay (Invitrogen, A22066), and ACh release was measured with the Amplex ACh/AChE assay (Invitrogen, A12217; Ex/Em = 530/590 nm). Histological analysis was performed on paraffin‐embedded cross‐sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Urothelial thickness was measured at multiple points using ImageJ (v2.16.0) and averaged for each treatment group.

Organ Bath Studies

Following a 2 h pretreatment with ARI or vehicle control, tissue strips (10 × 5 mm) were mounted in organ baths maintained at 37°C in Krebs solution aerated with 5% CO2 in O2 and connected to isometric force transducers (ADInstruments). After 1 h equilibration, responses to carbamylcholine chloride (carbachol, 10−8–10−3 M; Merck, C4382), ATP (1 mM; Merck, A2383), and isoprenaline (1 μM; Merck, I5627) were recorded, washing to baseline between drug tests. Potassium chloride (KCl, 60 mM) was used to assess receptor‐independent contractility via membrane depolarisation. Electrical field stimulation (EFS; 20 V, 0.1 ms pulse width, 1–40 Hz) was applied to detrusor strips in the absence and presence of atropine (1 μM; Merck, A0132) to block muscarinic cholinergic transmission, α,β‐methyladenosine 5′‐triphosphate lithium salt (α,β‐mATP, 10 μM; Merck, M6517) to desensitize P2X purinergic receptors, and tetrodotoxin (TTX, 1 μM; Merck, 554412) to inhibit voltage‐gated sodium channel‐dependent neurotransmission, thereby determining the relative cholinergic and purinergic contributions to nerve‐evoked contractions.

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For in vitro assays incorporating both ARI concentration and post‐treatment time (24, 48 and 72 h), including resazurin viability and ROS measurements, data were analyzed by two‐way ANOVA. Dunnett's multiple comparisons test was used to compare each ARI concentration with the corresponding untreated control within each timepoint, and Tukey's multiple comparisons test was used to compare post‐treatment timepoints within each concentration. Methylene blue viability data and caspase‐3 activity data were analyzed by one‐way ANOVA with Dunnett's post hoc test. Human Cell Stress Protein Array data were analyzed using two‐tailed unpaired t‐tests. For porcine tissue studies, paired comparisons between control and ARI‐treated tissues, including basal ATP release, basal ACh release, urothelial thickness, UDIF (% suppression of detrusor responses by intact bladder), EFS responses, atropine‐induced inhibition of EFS, and other matched functional endpoints, were analyzed using two‐tailed paired t‐tests, as tissues were obtained from the same bladders. Concentration‐response curves to carbachol were fitted by nonlinear regression, and between‐group differences in fitted parameters (pEC50, maximal response, and Hill slope) were assessed using the extra sum‐of‐squares F test.

Results

Cell Studies

Brief ARI Exposure Reduces Viability in Bladder Cells

ARI reduced resazurin‐derived cell viability in a concentration‐dependent manner in RT4, T24 and UROtsa cells following both 30 min and 2 h pretreatment (Figure 1A–F). After 30 min pretreatment, RT4 cells (Figure 1A) appeared the most sensitive, with the greatest reduction in viability observed at 300 μM after 72 h. In T24 cells (Figure 1C), viability was significantly reduced at 100 and 300 μM at 24 and 48 h, whereas only 300 μM remained significant at 72 h. UROtsa cells (Figure 1E) were comparatively resistant after 30 min exposure, with a significant effect observed only at 300 μM at all post‐treatment timepoints.

Extending pretreatment to 2 h enhanced the cytotoxic effect of ARI in all three cell lines. In RT4 cells (Figure 1B), 100 and 300 μM significantly reduced viability at 24, 48 and 72 h. In T24 cells (Figure 1D), 100 and 300 μM significantly reduced viability at all timepoints, with 30 μM also reducing viability at 48 h. In UROtsa cells (Figure 1F), 2 h pretreatment produced significant reductions in viability from 30 μM onwards, with significant loss of viability at 100 and 300 μM at all timepoints. Overall, prolonging pretreatment from 30 min to 2 h increased the cytotoxic effect of ARI, with near‐complete loss of viability at 300 μM in all cell lines after 2 h pretreatment. Methylene blue staining at 72 h provided an independent measure of adherent cell biomass and confirmed the concentration‐dependent loss of viable cell mass observed in the resazurin assay (Figure S1).

ARI Induces ROS Formation, Activates Caspase‐3, and Alters Stress‐Response Protein Expression in Bladder Cancer Cells

Following 2 h ARI pretreatment (1–300 μM), ROS formation was assessed in T24 and RT4 bladder cancer cells at 24, 48 and 72 h. As RT4 cells showed a similar concentration‐dependent ROS response to T24 cells, only T24 data are presented in the main figure (Figure 2A), with RT4 data shown in Figure S2. In T24 cells, ARI induced a concentration‐dependent increase in ROS formation that varied across post‐treatment timepoints. Significant increases relative to the corresponding untreated control were observed only at 100 and 300 μM at 24, 48 and 72 h (all p < 0.001), whereas no significant changes were detected at concentrations ≤ 30 μM. Comparisons between post‐treatment timepoints showed that, at 100 μM, ROS formation differed between 24 and 48 h, while at 300 μM, ROS formation differed between 24 and 48 h and between 48 and 72 h. These findings indicate that ARI‐induced ROS formation was restricted to higher concentrations and changed over time.

To assess apoptotic signaling, caspase‐3 activity was measured in T24 cells 24 h after 2 h ARI exposure (10 or 30 μM). Both concentrations significantly increased caspase‐3 activity compared with untreated controls (Figure 2B), with mean activity rising 1.3‐fold at 10 μM (**p = 0.0022) and 1.5‐fold at 30 μM (***p = 0.0002). Notably, caspase‐3 activation occurred at 10–30 μM, whereas significant DCF‐detectable ROS increases were only observed at ≥ 100 μM, indicating that apoptotic signaling can be engaged at sub‐oxidative stress concentrations and that ROS may become prominent only at higher exposures or later stages of cell injury.

To explore broader stress‐associated protein changes, T24 cells were treated with ARI (30 μM, 2 h) and profiled 24 h post‐treatment using a Human Cell Stress Protein Array (Figure 2C). ARI significantly altered the expression of a subset of stress‐related proteins, including downregulation of Bcl‐2, cytochrome C, p‐HSP27, HSP60, IDO, p‐JNK Pan, NFκB1, p‐p38α, p‐p53, PON1‐3, thioredoxin‐1, and SOD2 (all p < 0.05 vs. untreated control). In contrast, no significant changes were observed for ADAMTS1, CA9, Cited‐2, COX‐2, Dkk‐4, FABP‐1, HIF‐1α, HIF‐2α, HSP70, p21/CIP1, p27, or SIRT2 (Figure S3).

Bladder Tissue Studies

ARI Does Not Alter Mediator Release or Urothelial Width

To assess whether ARI altered urothelial signaling following luminal pretreatment, basal ATP and ACh release were measured from urothelium/LP strips mounted in organ baths. After a 1 h equilibration period, basal ATP and ACh concentrations measured in the organ bath did not differ significantly between ARI‐pretreated and vehicle control tissues (Figure 3A,B).

Basal ATP (A) and ACh (B) release from porcine urothelium/LP tissue strips measured in organ baths following a 2 h ARI pretreatment and a 1 h equilibration period. No statistically significant differences were observed between ARI‐treated and vehicle control tissues (n = 4, paired t‐tests, p > 0.05). In a separate experiment, the effects of ARI on urothelial structural integrity were assessed using intact, full‐thickness bladder wall strips. Urothelial thickness was preserved following ARI pretreatment, with mean widths of 43.4 ± 2.2 μm in control tissues and 41.7 ± 1.6 μm in ARI‐treated tissues (paired t‐test, p > 0.05; Figure 3C).

ARI Enhances Urothelial Inhibitory Activity and Preserves Neurogenic Contractions

To assess whether luminal ARI exposure altered carbachol‐induced contractions or the inhibitory influence of the urothelium/LP on detrusor responses (UDIF), contractile responses of intact bladder and detrusor‐only tissue strips were compared. In control tissues, the urothelium suppressed detrusor contractility by 38.7% ± 11.4%; following ARI pretreatment, this suppression significantly increased to 59.8% ± 10.6%, indicating an enhanced inhibitory influence arising from the mucosal compartment on detrusor contractile responses (p = 0.033; Figure 4A,B).

Importantly, ARI pretreatment did not alter broader physiological responses. Depolarisation‐induced contractions (60 mM KCl), purinergic contractions evoked by exogenous ATP (1 mM) or α,β‐mATP (10 μM), β‐adrenergic relaxations to isoprenaline (1 μM), and spontaneous phasic activity were unchanged between ARI‐treated and control tissues (p > 0.05). Neurogenic responses were also preserved. EFS (1–40 Hz) of detrusor strips produced frequency‐dependent contractions of similar amplitude in both groups (Figure 4D). Atropine (1 μM) caused substantial inhibition of responses to EFS at all frequencies, confirming ACh as the predominant excitatory neurotransmitter. The degree of atropine‐induced inhibition did not differ between groups (Table 2). Subsequent addition of α,β‐mATP (10 μM) to desensitise detrusor to purinergic receptors produced minimal further inhibition, indicating similar purinergic contributions (data not shown). Together, these data indicate that ARI pretreatment does not affect direct purinergic contractility or cholinergic/purinergic neuroeffector transmission in porcine bladder.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that brief exposure to ARI even at high concentrations preserves urothelial structure and has minimal impact on bladder function in normal bladder tissues, while exhibiting cytotoxic actions in bladder cancer cell lines, activating multiple anti‐tumor pathways. These findings support the potential for ARI as a repurposed intravesical agent that combines functional tolerability with mechanistic potency.

Cytotoxic Mechanisms of ARI

Brief ARI exposure induced delayed, concentration‐dependent cytotoxicity in bladder cancer cells, with caspase‐3 activation detectable at 10–30 μM and significant DCF‐detectable ROS elevation emerging only at ≥ 100 μM. This concentration separation suggests that ARI can engage apoptotic signaling at lower exposures through ROS‐independent or ROS‐below‐detection pathways, while overt oxidative stress becomes more evident at higher exposures and/or later stages of injury. Accordingly, rather than positioning ROS as the sole upstream trigger, our data support a model in which ARI disrupts mitochondrial and stress‐survival networks early, with amplified redox imbalance contributing to terminal injury. Similar ROS induction has been reported in neuronal [24], lung [25], and hepatic cells [26], indicating that oxidative stress is a reproducible component of ARI toxicity, but the temporal and concentration dependence may vary by cell type and assay sensitivity.

At higher ARI concentrations, the rise in ROS may reflect disruption of mitochondrial redox homeostasis (e.g., altered NAD+/NADH balance and increased electron leakage at respiratory complexes) together with progressive exhaustion of antioxidant buffering capacity [27, 28]. In this setting, sustained ROS could contribute to terminal injury and shift the response from adaptive stress signaling towards cell death. While caspase‐3 activation at 10–30 μM indicates engagement of apoptotic signaling, the magnitude of viability loss at higher ARI concentrations suggests that caspase‐dependent apoptosis alone is unlikely to account for the full cytotoxic phenotype. Consistent with this, methylene blue staining at 72 h confirmed a significant, concentration‐dependent reduction in adherent biomass. Together, these findings raise the possibility that additional caspase‐independent processes and/or secondary necrosis contribute at higher exposures, potentially in the setting of severe mitochondrial dysfunction and redox stress. Further phenotyping (e.g., Annexin V/PI profiling and inhibitor‐based rescue approaches) will be required to resolve the dominant mode(s) of cell death across the exposure range.

Consistent with impaired redox homeostasis, ARI pretreatment was associated with selective changes in stress‐ and survival‐related proteins on the Human Cell Stress Array. Specifically, ARI reduced array signal for Bcl‐2, cytochrome c, SOD2, thioredoxin‐1, p‐p38α, p‐HSP27, HSP60, paraoxonase isoforms (PON1‐3), IDO, NFκB1, p‐JNK, and p‐p53. Reduced Bcl‐2 signal is consistent with attenuation of anti‐apoptotic buffering at the mitochondria, potentially lowering the threshold for mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilisation and downstream caspase activation [29, 30, 31, 32]. Similar Bcl‐2 suppression has been reported in MCF‐7 and pancreatic cancer models [33, 34]. Likewise, reduced SOD2 and Trx1 signals are consistent with diminished antioxidant capacity [35, 36, 37], and reduced paraoxonase isoforms may further compromise mitochondrial and lipid membrane resilience. Collectively, these coordinated changes are consistent with a shift towards vulnerability to oxidative injury, aligning with redox‐linked cytotoxic mechanisms described in other contexts (e.g., cisplatin models in which Trx1 loss is associated with mitochondrial dysfunction) [38, 39].

In parallel, reduced p‐p38α, p‐HSP27, and HSP60 signals are consistent with attenuation of MAPK‐linked stress signaling and chaperone‐mediated protein quality control [40, 41, 42], pathways implicated in proliferation and stress tolerance in bladder cancer. Suppression of phospho‐p38α is notable given its role in stabilizing transcripts encoding inflammatory mediators and stress‐responsive genes [43]. Reduced IDO signal may also have implications for tumor–immune interactions via altered tryptophan metabolism and immunomodulatory signaling [44, 45]. Importantly, these membrane arrays are semi‐quantitative and were performed at a single concentration/time point with modest sample size. Validation of key targets (e.g., Bcl‐2, SOD2, Trx1) using independent methods (e.g., immunoblotting) and time−/concentration‐response profiling will be an important next step to confirm the extent and kinetics of these signaling changes.

Bladder Tolerability and Mucosal Effects

Tolerability of intravesical chemotherapy is an important consideration when investigating new treatment approaches, as drugs associated with greater side effects are more likely to be discontinued, impacting outcomes for patients [17]. Urothelial preservation is central to the tolerability of intravesical treatments [46]. Chemical agents that disrupt the mucosa or reduce epithelial thickness can increase afferent firing, cause urgency and pain, and compromise bladder compliance [47]. Intravesical MMC, an alkylating agent commonly used to treat bladder cancer, significantly thins the urothelium and impairs mediator release, consistent with its known clinical irritative profile [48, 49]. By contrast, ARI exposure did not alter mediator release or urothelial histology and left detrusor contractility, β‐adrenergic relaxation, and nerve‐evoked responses unchanged. These findings are consistent with limited acute tissue disruption and an absence of neuromuscular toxicity following short‐term exposure. ARI's physicochemical properties, together with pharmacokinetic studies of intravesical drugs showing restricted urothelial penetration during short dwell times, may have contributed to this preservation [50, 51, 52], although drug penetration was not measured in the present study and this interpretation should therefore be viewed cautiously.

Limited penetration may partly explain the apparent discrepancy between the in vitro and ex vivo findings, whereby ARI produced concentration‐dependent cytotoxicity in non‐malignant UROtsa cells while intact porcine urothelium remained unaffected. However, differences in the timing of outcome assessment between the models are also likely to have contributed. Although drug exposure duration was matched, cell viability was assessed 24–72 h after ARI treatment, allowing delayed cytotoxic effects to emerge, whereas tissue function and histology were assessed immediately after exposure in an acute ex vivo model. The urothelial glycosaminoglycan (GAG) layer, tight junctions, and multilayered urothelial architecture may also reduce diffusion into deeper cell layers [53, 54, 55]. Future in vivo studies assessing bladder function and tissue integrity at later timepoints after intravesical ARI exposure would help clarify the relative contribution of delayed toxicity and restricted tissue penetration.

The enhancement of the inhibitory effect attributed to UDIF following ARI pretreatment is mechanistically relevant. UDIF provides an endogenous inhibitory brake on cholinergic detrusor contractions, mediated by a non‐classical, diffusible factor that is independent of nitric oxide and prostanoids [18, 56, 57]. In the present study, ARI increased the inhibitory influence of the mucosa on detrusor responses in intact tissue strips, consistent with enhanced UDIF‐mediated inhibition. Although ARI also produced an upward trend in carbachol‐evoked responses in isolated urothelium/LP strips, this effect was not statistically significant. These findings are not contradictory, because urothelium/LP contractility reflects the behavior of the isolated mucosal compartment, whereas UDIF reflects inhibitory mucosa‐to‐detrusor signaling [18, 58, 59]. Accordingly, mechanisms that alter mucosal mediator release or intercompartmental signaling could modify UDIF‐related inhibition without necessarily changing the contractile properties of the isolated urothelium/LP preparation.

Two pharmacological mechanisms are hypothesized. First, given ARI's partial 5‐HT1A agonism and 5‐HT2A antagonism [11, 60], ARI could modulate mucosal serotonergic signaling in a manner that influences urothelium‐detrusor crosstalk and inhibitory signaling output, rather than directly altering isolated mucosal contractility. Serotonergic pathways regulate bladder mucosal function across species [61, 62, 63, 64], making this a plausible candidate pathway. Second, ARI has been reported to inhibit TRPM7 in other cellular contexts, and TRPM7 is overexpressed in bladder cancer and implicated in Src‐Akt‐JNK signaling and stretch‐evoked ATP release [65, 66, 67, 68, 69, 70, 71]. TRPM7 modulation could influence mucosal mechanotransduction and downstream inhibitory signaling, which may contribute to altered UDIF‐related inhibition without a significant change in isolated urothelium/LP contractile responses. These mechanisms remain speculative in the present study and should be directly tested in future work using selective serotonergic antagonists/agonists during UDIF assays and/or TRPM7 inhibition/knockdown approaches, alongside measurement of stretch‐evoked mediator release and sensory readouts.

Clinically, an increased mucosal inhibitory influence on detrusor output could in principle mitigate detrusor overactivity during intravesical instillation [63, 64], however, because increased mucosal activity has been linked to urgency phenotypes in some contexts, potential irritant risk warrants consideration [15, 72, 73]. In the present acute ex vivo model, ARI did not increase basal ATP or ACh release and did not enhance spontaneous phasic activity or neurogenic contractions, arguing against a broad excitatory/irritant effect under these conditions. Nonetheless, dedicated sensory readouts (e.g., afferent recordings, and in vivo cystometry with repeated exposure) will be required to define irritancy and tolerability more fully.

These findings, supported by extensive preclinical literature, provide a strong rationale for intravesical repurposing of ARI. Its induction of tumouricidal stress pathways and preservation of bladder function aligns with the clinical objectives of NMIBC therapy. Formulation strategies that prolong mucosal contact, such as thermosensitive gels [74] or mucoadhesive carriers [75, 76], could enhance dwell time and retention. Beyond preclinical development, recent expert commentary has highlighted the critical lack of clinical data on ARI in oncology and proposed structured retrospective and prospective strategies to address this gap, including large‐scale cohort studies using pharmacovigilance databases and biomarker‐linked phase I/II trials [77]. Such approaches could accelerate the clinical evaluation of ARI by leveraging real‐world evidence to guide trial design and patient selection. Given its established clinical safety profile, ARI represents a promising candidate for translational development, provided preclinical efficacy and tolerability are confirmed.

Summary

Brief intravesical‐like exposure to ARI preserved urothelial structure and key aspects of bladder function in porcine tissues while inducing concentration‐dependent cytotoxicity in bladder cancer cells. Complementary mechanistic findings supported engagement of apoptotic and stress‐associated pathways in malignant cells. Collectively, these findings support further investigation of ARI as a potential intravesical therapy for NMIBC, while highlighting the need for additional preclinical studies to clarify its mechanisms and therapeutic potential.
