Dermal fibroblasts attenuate osteoarthritis by restoring synovial fibroblast homeostasis
Authors: Lei Shi, Tong Xing, Kexin Liu, Kewei Rong, Hongsheng Li, Yin Li, Lizhi Jiang, Tingxian Guo, Chen Chen, Tobias Winkler, An Qin, Jie Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundManaging early-to-mid-stage knee osteoarthritis (OA) remains an unmet clinical need. Synovitis driven by disrupted synovial fibroblast lineage homeostasis is a central pathological driver, but current anti-inflammatory treatments fail to correct this underlying dysfunction.MethodsFibroblast lineage alterations and phenotypic similarities between dermal fibroblasts (DFbs) and synovial fibroblasts were characterized via synovial single-cell sequencing, histology and transcriptomics. Labeled DFbs were injected into rat knees, with their retention and in vivo fate traced by single-cell RNA sequencing. Therapeutic efficacy was assessed in a rat OA model via intra-articular DFb injection. Paracrine mechanisms were investigated using co-culture systems and quantitative proteomics.ResultsOA synovium exhibits disrupted fibroblast lineage homeostasis, marked by a prominent pro-inflammatory phenotype in synovial fibroblasts. DFbs shared remarkable phenotypic and transcriptomic similarity with healthy synovial fibroblasts and exhibited intrinsic resistance to inflammatory stimulation. Intra-articularly injected DFbs engrafted specifically in the synovium and persisted for over 3 weeks, producing sustained therapeutic benefits for at least 2 months. Mechanistically, DFbs alleviated cytokine-induced inflammatory responses through paracrine secretion of apolipoprotein D (APOD).ConclusionsThis study validates a novel lineage-specific cell therapy for OA that targets synovial fibroblast dysfunction. DFb-based therapy offers a promising disease-modifying strategy for early-to-mid-stage OA, with potential for clinical translation.The Translational Potential of this ArticleThis article's translational potential lies in validating DFb as a safe and effective cell therapy for rat knee OA, targeting the pathology of disrupted fibroblast lineage homeostasis to alleviate synovitis and cartilage degeneration. It provides a clinically feasible disease-modifying strategy that fills the unmet need for durable OA treatments, laying the groundwork for advancing cell-based therapies into clinical trials.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Synovial fibroblasts, Dermal fibroblasts, Synovitis, Cell therapy, Apolipoprotein D

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is one of the leading causes of disability globally, with approximately 528 million patients worldwide [1]. Knee OA is the most prevalent form of OA, affecting an estimated 365 million individuals worldwide [2]. Beyond the epidemiological burden, knee OA inflicts profound patient-centric harm: progressive joint pain, restricted mobility and increase comorbidity risks, and impose substantial socioeconomic costs via lost productivity and healthcare utilization [3].

The synovium—a connective tissue lining the inner surface of the joint capsule—emerges as a critical regulator of OA progression. It comprises two layers: the sublining layer (SL) and the lining layer (LL). Physiologically, it is an important communication channel for transporting nutrients, removing debris and waste, and regulating immunity and joint inflammation. Joint homeostasis is maintained by tissue-resident synovial fibroblasts and other synoviocytes [4]. Pathologically, synovitis (synovial inflammation) drives OA worsening by disrupting this balance [5]. Synovitis is characterised by proliferation of synovial fibroblasts, macrophage recruitment, and ultimately hyperplasia, hypertrophy and fibrosis [6]. These changes induced the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and increased synovial vascular permeability, ultimately disrupting the synovial microenvironment, accelerating knee OA progression [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]], making synovitis a prime therapeutic target. Yet, preclinical and clinical attempts to target synovial inflammation have yielded inconsistent results [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]], partially due to their failure to reverse the underlying dysfunction of synovial fibroblasts.

Fibroblasts, long regarded as merely passive structural cells, are now recognized as central orchestrators of chronic inflammatory diseases [[19], [20], [21], [22]], with their regulatory roles reshaping our understanding of inflammatory disorders. In both OA [11,23,24] and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [25], pathogenic activation of synovial fibroblasts—marked by pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic phenotypes—has been extensively validated as a key driver of disease progression, firmly establishing dysregulated fibroblast lineages as a core pathological target in arthritis. Recent cross-tissue single-cell transcriptomic studies have further revolutionized fibroblast biology by defining a conserved “universal fibroblast” population across mammalian tissues. A landmark study by Buechler et al. further defined the molecular features of universal fibroblasts: human universal fibroblasts are characterized by high expression of a conserved core gene set, including DPT, IGFBP6, C3, APOD, SMOC2, FBLN5, and MFAP4 [26], and the expression of these universal fibroblast markers shows a strong inverse correlation with pathogenic activation [19,26]. This provides a potential strategy for treating chronic inflammatory diseases by targeting specific fibroblast subsets and/or lineages [27,28]. Notably, a resting sublining synovial fibroblast subset critical for synovial regeneration shares striking transcriptomic similarity to both unwounded dermal fibroblasts (DFbs) and universal fibroblasts [25]. Furthermore, intravenous injection of DFbs has been shown to attenuate autoimmune arthritis [29]. Collectively, these advances raise a pivotal question for OA whether exogenous DFbs that share similarity with homeostatic synovial fibroblasts can restore disrupted fibroblast lineage homeostasis in OA, thereby reversing synovitis and slowing disease progression.

In this study, we systematically investigated the therapeutic efficacy of intra-articular DFb injection in a rat anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) model of OA, and explored the underlying mechanisms through multi-omics analyses and functional validation experiments (Scheme 1).

Materials and methods

Human specimens

Ethical approval was granted by the institutional ethics review board of Shanghai Ninth People’ s Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Approval No: SH9H-2021-T401, SH9H-2023-T186). All participants provided full consent.

Healthy synovium was collected from three patients: one male and two females. The average age of the patients was 21.6 ± 3.5 years (18–25 years). Patients enrolled required arthroscopic surgery for reasons such as patellar dislocation or meniscal injuries. OA synovium was collected from eight female patients. The average age of the patients was 74.8 ± 3.3 years (69–80 years). All patients required total knee replacement (TKA) due to end-stage knee OA. Prepuce tissues were collected from three male patients. The average age of the patients was 9.3 ± 3.8 years (5–12 years). The patients underwent traditional circumcision. All tissue samples were collected from patients undergoing surgery at the Shanghai Ninth People’ s Hospital.

Animal studies

Animal studies were approved by the laboratory animal ethics committee of Shanghai Ninth People’ s Hospital (Approval No: SH9H-2024-A1393 and SH9H-2025-A8). In accordance with animal housing criteria, male C57BL/6 mice and male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were group-housed at 23 °C to 25 °C with a 12-h light-dark cycle and were allowed to drink and eat freely.

In this study, 6-week-old male SD rats were utilized as experimental subjects. A validated knee OA model was established via ACLT, selected for its high reproducibility and low intergroup variability [30,31]. 18 SD rats were randomly allocated into three experimental groups: Sham Group (n = 6): Underwent sham surgery without ACLT; ACLT + PBS Group (n = 6): Received intra-articular PBS injections into the right knee post-ACLT; ACLT + DFb Group (n = 6): Administered intra-articular DFb suspensions into the right knee post-ACLT.

Body weight was monitored at 2-week intervals throughout the study period. Gait analysis was conducted at 8 weeks post-surgery. Terminal endpoints were reached at 8 weeks postoperatively, with euthanasia performed via cervical dislocation by trained personnel. Knee joints were harvested for micro-computed tomography analysis and histopathological assessment of synovium and articular cartilage.

The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis

Data source and quality control (QC)

For human data, this study utilized the GSE216651 dataset, processed using the R-based single-cell analysis tool Seurat (v5.1.0). The dataset includes scRNA-seq data derived from the infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP) and synovium of three healthy donors and three OA patients. Initially, scRNA-seq data from the three healthy donors were integrated and subjected to rigorous QC. For rat synovium (GSE327818), a total of nine samples were included in this study. Raw sequencing data in FASTQ format were processed using the DNBelab C Series single-cell RNA analysis software (dnbc4tools, v2.1.3) to generate the initial gene expression matrix. Subsequent analyses were performed with Seurat. Doublets were identified and removed using Scrublet (v0.2.3), and low-quality cells were filtered out.

Data normalization and integration

Each sample was independently subjected to data normalization (NormalizeData) and highly variable feature selection (FindVariableFeatures, vst method, nfeatures = 2000), and cell cycle scoring was performed using the built-in cell cycle gene set in Seurat (CellCycleScoring). Subsequently, multi-sample data were integrated using the FindIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData functions to generate an integrated expression matrix. Z-score normalization was performed using the ScaleData function, with the effect of mitochondrial gene proportion regressed out, and principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using the first 30 principal components. Based on the first 30 principal components, a UMAP plot was generated using RunUMAP, a KNN graph was constructed using FindNeighbors, and finally cell clustering was completed using FindClusters.

Cell annotation

Differential expression analysis for each subcluster was performed using the FindAllMarkers function. Genes with an adjusted P-value <0.05, |log2FC| > 0.25, and expressed in at least 25% of cells within the subcluster were defined as significant marker genes. Cell type annotation was performed for each subcluster by integrating the top marker genes with the Cell Taxonomy and CellMarker 2.0 databases.

Activity assessment of apoptosis-related gene sets

Apoptosis-related genes were extracted from the GO term GO:0006915 (apoptotic process) and the KEGG pathway rno04210 (Apoptosis), and the union of these genes was taken to construct an apoptosis-related gene signature. The activity score of this gene signature in fibroblasts was calculated using AUCell, and pairwise comparisons of apoptotic activity among fibroblast subpopulations were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Pseudotime analysis and cell-cell communication

Pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed on fibroblast subpopulations using Slingshot (v2.7.0) based on the UMAP dimensionality reduction results to infer potential differentiation trajectories and developmental directions among subpopulations. Cell-cell communication networks between GFP + fibroblast subpopulations and macrophage subpopulations were analyzed using CellChat (v2.1.2) based on the built-in ligand-receptor database, with the minimum cell number threshold set to 10 and other parameters left as default. The communication strength was visualized using the netVisual_circle function.

Proportion analysis of cell subpopulations

The relative abundance of fibroblast subpopulations and macrophage subpopulations in each sample was calculated and visualized using stacked bar plots with ggplot2. Using the Sham group as a reference, the Pearson correlation coefficient between the proportion of each subpopulation and the group assignment was calculated using the cor.test function to assess the significance of changes in cell subpopulation proportions across different groups.

Bulk RNA sequencing analysis

RNA samples were sent to ShenZhen BGI Genomics Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China) for bulk RNA sequencing analysis. Then, complementary DNA library preparation and sequencing, raw reads, DEGs identification were conducted according to the BGISEQ platform's standard protocol.

Volcano plots, correlation analysis, GO enrichment analysis, GSEA, and Venn diagrams were analyzed and plotted using https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn (last accessed on 10 Dec 2024), an online platform for data analysis and visualization.

Knee OA induction-ACLT surgery

A knee OA model was established by ACLT surgery on 6-week-old rats. After anaesthesia, a longitudinal incision was made along the medial edge of the patellar ligament. The subcutaneous tissues were then bluntly separated layer by layer in order to expose the articular cavity, after which the anterior cruciate ligament was severed with ophthalmic scissors. The success rate of the ACLT was then ascertained by visual inspection and performing the anterior drawer test. Subsequent to this, the joint cavity was flushed with a sterile saline solution, and the joint cavity and skin were sutured. The incision was then closed. Postoperative antibiotics were administered for a period of 3 days to prevent infection.

For rats (n = 12) used in the study of cell residence in the knee joint, a bilateral injection of 25 μL (20, 000, 000 cells/mL) lentivirus-transfected DFbs (n = 4) or GFP DFbs (n = 8) was administered in the second week following the ACLT surgery. The remaining rats (n = 18) were randomly divided into the following three groups: (1) sham group; (2) ACLT + PBS group; (3) ACLT + DFb group. Two weeks following the surgical procedure, rats were given intra-articular injections of 25 μL PBS or 25 μL (20, 000, 000 cells/mL) DFbs. At the 2-month postoperative timepoint, animals were humanely sacrificed.

In vivo bioluminescence

Luciferase activity was assessed on days 1 to 21 post-injection using the IVIS Spectrum system (PerkinElmer, USA). In brief, D-luciferin (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was diluted in PBS with a concentration of 20 mg/mL. Rats were injected intraperitoneally with 1 mL of D-luciferin followed by being allowed to move freely for 15 min, followed by gas anaesthesia for in vivo imaging. Photon emissions from the knee, head and abdomen were collected using 1 min exposure time. The bioluminescence signal was superimposed over a photograph of the rats, and the flux within a circular region of interest was quantified using the Living Image software (PerkinElmer).

Multiplex immunofluorescence

Tissue slides were deparaffinised with xylene and rehydrated through a graded series of ethanol solutions (100%, 85% and 75%). After antigen retrieval via citric acid, three primary antibodies were used, including COL2A1 (Servicebio, China), Vimentin (Servicebio, China) and eGFP (ProteinTech, China). Following the addition of the primary antibody, the slices should be incubated overnight at 4 °C, in conditions that ensure protection from light. Subsequently, the tissue was covered with a drop of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody of the corresponding species to the primary antibody and incubated for 50 min at room temperature. Heat-induced epitope retrieval was used to eliminate all antibodies, including primary and secondary antibodies, following each staining cycle. Finally, the nuclei were restained with DAPI. A tissue section digital scanner (Pannoramic MIDI, 3DHISTECH, Hungary) was used to obtain multispectral images.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned, deparaffinised in xylene and rehydrated through a graded series of ethanol solutions (100%, 85% and 75%). Human tissues were then subjected to incubation with antibodies against IL-6 (ProteinTech, China), Vimentin (Servicebio, China) and CXCL1 (ProteinTech, China). Rat tissues were then subjected to incubation with antibodies against MMP3 (ProteinTech, China), MMP13 (ProteinTech, China), NOS2 (ProteinTech, China), COL2A1 (Servicebio, China), IL-6 (ProteinTech, China) and COX2 (Abcam, UK). Following this, the samples were rinsed thrice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody for 50 min at room temperature. Thereafter, the samples were washed again and stained for DAPI for 10 min at room temperature. A tissue section digital scanner (Pannoramic MIDI, 3DHISTECH, Hungary) was used to obtain multispectral images.

The positive cell rate and area of positive regions were analyzed using ImageJ (version 1.54g, USA).

Immunofluorescence staining

The samples were washed 3 times with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by treatment with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 20 min. Subsequently, the cells were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin at 37 °C for 1 h, washed with PBS, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody against GFP and IL-6 (1:200). Next, the cells were washed 3 times with PBS and incubated with Alexa Fluor®488- labeled secondary antibody (1:500) at room temperature for 1 h. They were then counterstained with DAPI. Finally, observations were performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM; Leica DMi 8, Germany).

The positive cell rate and mean fluorescence intensity were analyzed using ImageJ (version 1.54g, USA).

Histomorphometric analysis

Human synovial tissue and rat major organ specimens were fixed in 4% PFA (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) for 1 week. The tissue sections were stained with Hematoxylin & Eosin (H&E) in accordance with the standard laboratory protocols. The histopathological score of synovitis was performed in accordance with the established protocol described in previous literature [32].

Rat knee specimens were fixed in 4% PFA (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) for 1 week and decalcified in 10% ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) for approximately 8 weeks. The tissue sections were stained with Safranin O-Fast Green (SO/FG) and H&E in accordance with the standard laboratory protocols. Total joint cartilage degeneration sum was determined according to the Osteoarthritis Research Society International score system (OARSI Criteria).

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT)

Rat knee specimens were scanned by micro-CT (NMC-200, PINGSENG, China). 3D Slicer software (version 5.6.2) was used to reconstruct and create three-dimensional images of the micro-CT scans. Osteophytes were measured and osteophyte score was determined according to the Grading of Osteoarthritic Cartilage Damage (OARSI Criteria).

Gait analysis

The walking patterns of the rats were recorded using a CatWalk device (Noldus Information Technology Inc, Netherlands) to measure gait parameters. A measurable run was defined as the ability of the animals to take three or more consecutive steps without interruption while traversing the walkway, and average values were subsequently calculated for analysis. Intensity thresholds were adjusted and rat paw prints were automatically categorised using CatWalk software (ver. 10.6). All steps taken by the rats between these two points were recorded.

Isolation of primary synovial fibroblasts and DFbs

The synovial tissue/prepuce tissue was cut into 1 mm diameter pieces with sterilised ophthalmic scissors, while it was later digested in 0.5 mg/mL collagenase I (Biosharp, China) and DNase I (Roche, Switzerland) for 3 h. The isolated cell suspension was passed through a 100 μm sieve and then centrifuged to remove the supernatant. Synovial fibroblasts and DFbs were cultured in DMEM with 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Hyclone, USA). Synovial fibroblasts and DFbs (Vimentin positive) were identified at the 3rd passage (P3), and synovial fibroblasts at P3 with a purity over 90% were used for the function and mechanism experiments.

Isolation of primary chondrocytes

The joint cartilage tissue was cut into 1 mm diameter pieces with sterilised ophthalmic scissors. Then cartilage tissue was digested in 0.5 mg/mL collagenase II (Biosharp, China) and DNase I (Roche, Switzerland) for 2 h. The isolated cell suspension was passed through a 100 μm sieve and then centrifuged to remove the supernatant. Chondrocytes were cultured in DMEM/F12 containing 10 ng/mL ITS (OriCell, China), 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 10% fetal bovine serum.

CCK-8 cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, NCM Biotech, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates in 100 μL of complete medium. After overnight adherence, cells were treated with or without IL-1β on day 1. At the indicated time points, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and plates were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured. The relative proliferation rate was normalized to the absorbance value of day 1 for each group. Six biological replicates were performed for each experimental group.

Annexin V/PI immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometry apoptosis assay

Apoptotic cells were visualized by fluorescence staining using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Briefly, DFbs and FLS were seeded in glass-bottom confocal dish. After overnight adherence, cells were treated with or without IL-1β for 24 h. The staining procedure was performed following the manufacturer's instructions.

For flow cytometry apoptosis assay, DFbs and FLS were seeded into 6-well plates. After overnight adherence, cells were treated with or without IL-1β. Following 24 h of stimulation, cells were harvested by EDTA-free trypsinization, and subsequent staining was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. The positive rates of Annexin V and PI were detected by flow cytometry.

Lentiviral transfections

The lentiviruses were purchased from OBiO Technology (Shanghai, China). DFbs were transfected with lentivirus (pASLenti-pA-Luc2-CMV-EF1-GdGreen-P2A-Puro-WPRE) for 48 h followed by a stable transfected cell selection using puromycin. Following puromycin screening, flow cytometry was utilized to detect cellular GdGreen positivity rate, and the final cells employed for the experiment exhibited a GdGreen positivity rate of greater than 99%.

APOD overexpression and knockdown

Primary DFbs were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected with APOD-overexpressing plasmid or APOD-targeting siRNA (Tsingke Biotechnology, China) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Three independent small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting rat Apod and a non-targeting negative control siRNA (siNC) were synthesized and purified. The sequences (5' → 3′) are as follows:

si-Apod-1: Sense 5′-GGUACGAAAUCGAGAAGAU(dT) (dT)-3′, Antisense 5′-AUCUUCUCGAUUUCGUACC(dT) (dT)-3′

si-Apod-2: Sense 5′-CCACGGAUUACGAGAGCUA(dT) (dT)-3′, Antisense 5′-UAGCUCUCGUAAUCCGUGG(dT) (dT)-3′

si-Apod-3: Sense 5′-GGAGAACGGAAACAUCAAA(dT) (dT)-3′, Antisense 5′-UUUGAUGUUUCCGUUCUCC(dT) (dT)-3′

Empty pcDNA3.1(+) vector and non-targeting siRNA were used as negative controls. Transfection efficiency was verified at 48 h post-transfection by RT-qPCR and Western blot.

Co-culture assay

Synovial fibroblasts or chondrocytes were resuspended at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/well (2 mL) and then seeded in the lower cell chamber (0.4 μm pore membrane, Corning, USA). The upper chamber was inoculated with DFbs, 1 × 105 per well, in 6-well plates and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 and humidified incubator. 24 h after seeding plates, cells were treated with IL-1β at a final concentration of 10 ng/mL. After 48 h incubation, the lower and upper cells were harvested.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis

The total RNA was extracted from each sample using the TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Then, cDNA was obtained from the RNA template using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit. Subsequently, the real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay was performed on QuantStudio 6 Flex (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) by using the SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, China). Specifically, a total volume of 10 μL liquid (comprising 0.5 μL of mixed forward and reverse primer, 4.5 μL of diluted cDNA, and 5 μL of SYBR Green Master Mix) was added to each well of the 384-well PCR plate. To determine the specificity of the amplification product, melting curves were used. The primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from cells using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against APOD (1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), iNOS (1:1000, ProteinTech, China), IL-6 (1:1000, ProteinTech, China), MMP9 (1:1000, ProteinTech, China), and β-actin (1:5000, ProteinTech, China). The secondary fluorescence antibody was then incubated in the dark at room temperature for 1 h after 3 times TBST wash. After another 3 times TBST wash, the Odyssey v3.0 image scanning was used to develop the result of reactivity (Li Cor. Inc., USA).

Data-independent acquisition (DIA) based quantitative proteomic analysis of DFb supernatant

Foreskin-derived fibroblasts were isolated from two healthy donors and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 7.5% human platelet lysate (HPL). Following expansion to passage 3 (P3), when cell confluency reached 80–90%, the culture medium was aspirated and replaced with an equal volume of fresh DMEM. The conditioned supernatant generated over a 3-day incubation period was harvested and subjected to proteomic sequencing to characterize the protein composition of the fibroblast-derived secretome. Vanquish™ Neo UHPLC -Astral LC/MS DIA method was used to generate the raw data.

GO enrichment analysis and Venn diagrams were analyzed and plotted using https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn (last accessed on 10 Dec 2024).

Cell high-density culture and Alcian blue staining

To assess chondrogenic differentiation, chondrocytes were suspended in incomplete DMEM at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells per 10 μL. The cell suspension was seeded as micromasses at the bottom of a 24-well plate, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h to allow cell adhesion. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of DMEM supplemented with 10 ng/mL ITS and 2% FBS was added to each well. The culture medium was refreshed every 48 h, and after 9 days of cultivation, the micromasses were stained with Alcian blue at room temperature.

Statistical analysis

Student's t‐test and one-way ANOVA were applied to assess the statistical significance. P-values <0.05 were considered significant. At least three samples were included in each statistical analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0.0, USA).

Results

Chronic inflammation and senescence of synovial fibroblasts in knee OA synovium

To define the pathological landscape of synovial fibroblasts in knee osteoarthritis (OA), we first re-analyzed a publicly available synovial single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset (GSE216651) from healthy donors and patients with end-stage OA. The single-cell expression matrix consisted of 5 cell subpopulations, namely fibroblast cells, endothelial cells (ECs), mural cells, myeloid cells, and lymphoid cells (Fig. S1A–C). Notably, fibroblasts constituted the predominant synoviocyte population relative to other subpopulations (Fig. S1D).

To further investigate fibroblast lineage dysfunction in OA, immune cells and vessel cells were removed from the merged dataset [33]. Unsupervised clustering partitioned the remaining fibroblast lineage into four clusters, and previously reported markers were used to annotate cell identities (Fig. 1A and B) [33]. Synovial sublining layer fibroblasts (SL Fb) were identified as a cluster with high expression of CHI3L1, RARRES1, CXCL12, BGN and GLIS3, whereas synovial lining layer fibroblasts (LL Fb) displayed elevated expression of PRG4, CLIC5, HTRA1, HAS1, and PDPN. Quantitative analysis revealed a profound remodeling of fibroblast subset composition in OA synovium, marked by a significant expansion of the SL Fb population relative to healthy controls (Fig. 1A–C). Differential expression gene (DEG) analysis demonstrated that OA SL Fb exhibited robust upregulation of pro-inflammatory and senescence-associated transcripts (Fig. 1D), with KEGG enrichment analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirming enrichment of inflammatory signaling, invasive pathways, and senescence-related gene [34] signatures (Fig. 1E and F). Parallel profiling of LL Fb identified a comparable pro-inflammatory and senescent transcriptional program (Fig. S1E and F), indicating global fibroblast lineage dysfunction in OA.

B) Dot plot showing the marker genes for each annotated fibroblast lineage subcluster. C) Stacked bar chart showing the relative proportion of each fibroblast lineage subcluster across different samples. D) Volcano plot depicting the DEGs in the SL Fb subpopulation between OA and healthy donors. E) KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs between the healthy SL Fb and the OA SL Fb. F) GSEA highlighting the enrichment of pathways related to immune responses and aging. G) H&E staining of adjacent tissue indicates the synovial lining layer (LL) and sublining layer (SL). H) Synovitis score of human synovium. I) Cell density in the sublining layer. J) Immunofluorescence staining of IL-6 and CXCL1 in the human synovium. K) Quantification of IL-6+ and CXCL1+ cells in the sublining layer. Data were presented as mean ± SD. Results were analyzed by t‐test.

To validate these transcriptomic findings in clinical samples, we analyzed synovial tissues from 3 healthy donors and 8 end-stage OA patients. The histopathological score of synovitis was performed in accordance with the established protocol described in previous literature [32]. H&E staining of OA synovium revealed hallmark pathological changes: thickening of the SL layer, increased cellularity, and presence of multinucleated cells—features absent in healthy synovium (Fig. 1G–I). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed that OA synovium contained significantly more IL-6+ and CXCL1+ cells (key markers of pro-inflammatory fibroblasts [[35], [36], [37]] in the SL layer compared to healthy controls (Fig. 1J and K).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that knee OA synovium is characterized by pathological expansion of pro-inflammatory, senescent SL Fb, coupled with loss of homeostatic fibroblast gene expression and disrupted lineage balance. This profound dysregulation of synovial fibroblast homeostasis represents a core pathological driver of OA progression, providing a direct rationale for exploring cell-based strategies to restore fibroblast lineage equilibrium.

Dermal fibroblasts recapitulated the homeostatic phenotype and transcriptome of healthy synovial fibroblasts

Given the pathological dysregulation of synovial fibroblast lineage homeostasis in OA, we next evaluated whether DFbs could serve as a candidate cellular reagent to restore this balance, by comparing their phenotypic and transcriptomic features with healthy synovial fibroblasts and OA synovial fibroblasts (OA FLS). Primary healthy synovial fibroblasts and OA FLS were isolated and expanded to passage 3 (P3) for characterization. Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining for Vimentin—an established mesenchymal/fibroblast marker [38]—confirmed >99% purity for all two cell populations (Fig. 2A). Morphological assessment revealed that OA FLS displayed a flattened, irregular shape indicative of cellular senescence, whereas both healthy synovial fibroblasts and DFbs retained a characteristic spindle-shaped morphology typical of quiescent, homeostatic fibroblasts (Fig. 2A–D).

Dermal fibroblasts recapitulated the homeostatic phenotype and transcriptome of healthy synovial fibroblasts. A) Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemical staining for Vimentin confirm the identity of synovial fibroblasts. B-D) The morphology of DFbs and healthy synovial fibroblasts. Results were analyzed by t‐test. E) Correlation plot of the transcriptomes of three cell types (DFbs, healthy synovial fibroblasts (HF), and OA synovial fibroblasts (OAFILB)). F) Intersection of DEGs between OA SL Fb and healthy SL Fb, and DEGs between DFb and healthy synovial fibroblasts. G) GSEA of gene sets associated with knee OA progression. H) Clustered heatmap of partial OA-associated genes in three cell groups. I) RT-qPCR analysis of IL6, CXCL1, CXCL3 and BMP2. n = 3. Data were presented as mean ± SD. Results were analyzed by one-way ANOVA.

Bulk RNA-sequencing was performed to quantify transcriptomic similarities across the three cell populations. Pearson correlation analysis showed a strong positive correlation between DFbs and healthy synovial fibroblasts, whereas both populations diverged markedly from OA FLS (Fig. 2E). To refine this comparison, we defined "OA-associated genes" as DEGs between OA FLS and healthy synovial fibroblasts. Intersection analysis of DEGs between (DFb vs healthy synovial fibroblasts) and OA-associated genes showed only ∼5% overlap (Fig. 2F), indicating that DFb and healthy synovial fibroblast differ minimally in genes linked to OA pathogenesis. GSEA further revealed no significant upregulation of inflammatory or OA-related pathways in DFbs compared with healthy synovial fibroblasts (Fig. 2G, Fig. S2). Clustering analysis of OA signature genes corroborated this finding, with DFbs and healthy synovial fibroblasts grouping into a distinct homeostatic cluster separate from pro-inflammatory OA FLS (Fig. 2H). RT-qPCR validation of partial key genes (IL6, CXCL1, CXCL3, BMP2) confirmed the transcriptomic consistency (Fig. 2I).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that DFbs faithfully recapitulate the morphological phenotype and homeostatic transcriptomic profile of healthy synovial fibroblasts, while lacking the pro-inflammatory and pathological transcriptional signature of OA FLS. This high degree of lineage and functional similarity identifies DFbs as a promising candidate to restore disrupted synovial fibroblast homeostasis in OA.

DFbs exhibited blunted proliferative and inflammatory responses to IL-1β stimulation relative to synovial fibroblasts

To delineate the divergent biological behaviors of DFbs and FLS in the inflammatory OA microenvironment, we assessed their cellular and molecular responses to IL-1β, one of the core pro-inflammatory cytokines driving synovitis and OA progression. We first examined proliferative activity under continuous IL-1β challenge: FLS displayed a time-dependent significant increase in proliferation, whereas the proliferation rate of DFbs remained unaltered with no significant difference between treated and control groups (Fig. 3A). We next evaluated apoptotic profiles under the same inflammatory stimulus. Annexin V-FITC/PI staining and flow cytometry analyses yielded consistent results, showing that neither DFbs nor FLS underwent significant apoptosis following 10 ng/mL IL-1β stimulation for 24 h (Fig. 3B–E).

We further compared the inflammatory responsiveness of the two cell types by quantifying the expression of canonical pro-inflammatory mediators implicated in OA pathogenesis. RT-qPCR results revealed that DFbs exhibited a markedly blunted inflammatory response to IL-1β, as evidenced by significantly weaker upregulation of Il6, Cxcl1, Nos2 and Ptgs2 compared with FLS (Fig. 3F).

These findings demonstrate that DFbs are partial resistant to IL-1β-induced proliferative and inflammatory activation, in contrast to the robust pro-inflammatory reprogramming of FLS. This stability under inflammatory stress supports the suitability of DFbs for therapeutic use in the hostile OA joint microenvironment.

DFbs engrafted in rat synovium with persistent survival

Having established the homeostatic phenotype of DFbs in vitro, we next evaluated their in vivo engraftment, tissue tropism and persistence, which are key prerequisites for therapeutic efficacy in OA. We generated two genetically labeled DFb lines: DFbs stably expressing GdGreen and luciferase via lentiviral transduction, and DFbs isolated from SD-CAG-EGFP transgenic rats; flow cytometry and luminescence assays confirmed high labeling efficiency (Fig. S3A–C).

Labeled DFbs were intra-articularly injected into rat knee joints two weeks after ACLT surgery, with the contralateral intact knee (left) as control. In vivo bioluminescence imaging showed that luciferase signals were detectable in injected knees for over 3 weeks, with a modest decline in the first week and a more pronounced reduction between days 7–10 (Fig. 4A–C). Notably, DFbs persisted longer in non-inflamed intact knees than in ACLT-injured OA knees, suggesting that synovial inflammation may moderate cell retention (Fig. 4B and C).

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining of coronal and sagittal knee joint sections (day 3 and 7 post-injection) confirmed specific DFb engraftment: GFP + DFb (green) localized predominantly to the synovial lining and sublining layers, co-localizing with Vimentin + synovial tissue (violet) but not COL2A1+ cartilage (yellow; Fig. 4D). No GFP + cells were detected in articular cartilage, bone, or other joint tissues, indicating intrinsic synovial tropism of DFb.

Safety assessment revealed no bioluminescence signals in extra-articular organs including the head and abdomen throughout the observation period (Fig. 4E). Histopathological examination of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) and GFP immunofluorescence showed normal tissue morphology and no off-target DFb engraftment, confirming the favorable biosafety profile of intra-articular DFb delivery (Fig. 4F).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that intra-articularly injected DFbs exhibit specific synovial engraftment, long-term in vivo persistence and excellent systemic safety, providing a critical cellular basis for sustained therapeutic modulation of the OA synovial microenvironment.

Single-cell transcriptomic dissection of the in vivo fate and functional heterogeneity of exogenous DFbs following intra-articular injection

To define the adaptive responses and biological behavior of exogenous DFbs upon engraftment into the OA joint microenvironment, we performed scRNA-seq on synovial tissues harvested at 5 days after intra-articular injection of GFP-labeled DFbs into rat ACLT knees (Fig. 5A).

Unsupervised clustering and cell type annotation of the entire single-cell transcriptomic dataset were first performed using canonical lineage-specific marker genes. Cells isolated from synovial tissues were classified into 7 major cell populations, including fibroblasts, macrophages, endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, T cells, neurons, and erythroblasts (Fig. 5B and C). We subsequently extracted the fibroblast population for downstream subclustering analysis, identified by the pan-fibroblast marker Col1a1 (Fig. 5D). Unsupervised subclustering of fibroblasts identified 8 transcriptionally distinct subpopulations, which were annotated based on their signature marker genes: fibroblast progenitor cells (FPCs, Sfrp2+ Dpt+), Sfrp4+ fibroblasts, sublining layer fibroblasts (SL fibro, Acan + Bgn+), lining layer fibroblasts (LL fibro, Prg4+ Has1+), and two exogenous GFP + fibroblast subclusters (Fib6 and Fib7, GFP+) (Fig. 5E–H). Pseudotime trajectory analysis further revealed two distinct differentiation branches originating from the Fib8 (FPC) subcluster (Fig. S4A–C), supporting the hierarchical lineage relationship and the validity of our fibroblast subpopulation annotation. Quantification of the relative proportion of each fibroblast subpopulation across experimental groups showed that the abundance of SL fibroblasts was significantly reduced in the DFb-treated group compared with the PBS-treated group (Fig. 5G).

Visualization of GFP expression confirmed the localization of exogenous DFbs within the synovial fibroblast landscape (Fig. 5I). KEGG pathway enrichment showed that the GFP + Fib6 subset was enriched for hypoxia, oxidative stress and apoptotic signaling (Fig. 5J–S4D). Correspondingly, apoptosis gene signature scoring demonstrated that exogenous DFb subsets exhibited higher apoptotic activity than most endogenous synovial fibroblast populations (Fig. 5K). Notably, a fraction of GFP + DFbs retained high expression of the proliferation marker Mki67, indicating concurrent proliferation and apoptosis within the transplanted cell pool. This transcriptomic observation was further validated by multiplex immunofluorescence staining of rat synovial tissues (Fig. S5).

Cell–cell communication analysis further uncovered extensive bidirectional crosstalk between exogenous DFbs and synovial macrophages (Fig. S6). Our results revealed that exogenous DFbs actively communicated with synovial macrophages via multiple signaling axes, including CSF and TGF-β signaling pathways, while macrophages predominantly exerted regulatory effects on fibroblasts through the TNF signaling pathway (Fig. S6I). This bidirectional crosstalk may underlie the therapeutic effect of DFbs in modulating the OA synovial microenvironment.

Together, these single-cell transcriptomic data delineate the complex in vivo adaptive responses of engrafted DFbs, including stress-induced apoptosis, residual proliferation and dynamic intercellular crosstalk with resident synovial cells. These findings provide a high-resolution mechanistic framework for understanding how exogenous DFbs remodel the OA synovial microenvironment.

Intra-articular injection of DFbs ameliorated canonical pathological gait phenotype and synovitis

To determine the therapeutic efficacy of DFb in OA, we administered intra-articular DFb suspensions (or PBS as control) to rats 2 weeks after ACLT, with sham-operated rats as normal controls (Fig. 6A). We monitored phenotypic and histological outcomes over 8 weeks to assess OA progression and synovial inflammation.

B) Changes in body weight over weeks in each group of rats. C) Representative paw step images of rats in three groups (RF: right front limb, LF: left front limb, RH: right hind limb, LH: left hind limb). D) Quantitative analysis of max contact area, swing speed, and duty cycle at 8 weeks postoperatively (n = 6). Data were presented as mean ± SEM. Results were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. Data were presented as mean ± SD. Results were analyzed by one-way ANOVA.

Body weight dynamics reflected disease-related behavioral changes: ACLT + PBS rats gained significantly more weight than sham controls, likely due to pain-induced reduced mobility. In contrast, ACLT + DFb rats exhibited an intermediate weight profile (Fig. 6B). Gait assessment is a well-validated behavioral assay that comprehensively reflects the integrated impact of multiple OA-related pathological changes on limb function. In rodent knee OA models, the canonical pathological gait phenotype is pain-induced limping, characterized by significantly reduced paw contact area, shortened stance phase duration, decreased duty cycle, and slowed walking velocity [39,40]. Our study demonstrated significant improvements in key parameters of the affected hind limb in DFb-treated rats, including improved swing speed and duty cycle, indicating restored locomotor function (Fig. 6C and D). Theoretically, when the affected limb develops pain-induced limping, the contralateral healthy limb undergoes compensatory gait adjustments to redistribute weight-bearing; however, these compensatory changes are often subtle and do not reach statistical significance in most preclinical studies, a finding that has been consistently reported in the OA literature [41,42].

Histopathological evaluation of synovial tissues showed severe synovial hyperplasia, thickening, and cellular infiltration in ACLT + PBS rats, along with significantly elevated synovitis scores. In contrast, DFb treatment markedly attenuated these pathological changes and reduced synovitis severity (Fig. 6E and F). Immunofluorescent staining further confirmed that DFb administration significantly decreased the proportion of IL-6+ pro-inflammatory cells, verifying the in vivo anti-inflammatory effects of DFbs (Fig. 6G and H).

Intra-articular injection of DFbs preserved articular cartilage structure and homeostasis

Given the alleviation of synovitis and locomotor deficits by DFb treatment, a subsequent investigation was conducted to evaluate whether the intra-articular injection of DFbs could attenuate articular cartilage degeneration.

Histopathological analysis of knee joints using H&E and SO/FG staining revealed severe cartilage damage in ACLT + PBS rats: reduced proteoglycan retention (SO/FG staining) and loss of cartilage thickness (Fig. 7A). In contrast, ACLT + DFb rats exhibited moderate attenuation of these changes, with preserved cartilage structure and increased proteoglycan deposition (Fig. 7A). Consistent with this, the OARSI score was significantly lower in ACLT + DFb rats than in ACLT + PBS, though not fully restored to sham levels (Fig. 7C). Following micro-CT 3D reconstruction, a reduction in the number and size of osteophytes was observed in the ACLT + DFb group relative to the ACLT + PBS group, with an osteophyte score intermediate between sham and ACLT + PBS groups (Fig. 7B–C).

Intra-articular injection of DFbs preserved articular cartilage structure and homeostasis. A) H&E staining and SO/FG staining of articular cartilage. B) Representative micro-CT 3D images and coronal images of knee joints of rats at 8 weeks after ACLT. C) Grading of osteoarthritic cartilage damage (OARSI Criteria). D) Immunofluorescence staining of MMP3, MMP13, iNOS and COL2A1. E) Quantification of positive cells and positive area in the cartilage. n = 6. Data were presented as mean ± SD. As indicated by the existing literature, MMP3, MMP13 have been demonstrated to reflect the catabolic state of articular cartilage, while COL2A1 reflects the anabolic state of articular cartilage [43,44]. In addition, iNOS has been shown to reflect articular cartilage inflammation [45,46]. The ACLT + DFb group exhibited a reduced proportion of MMP3-, MMP13-, and iNOS-positive cells relative to the ACLT + PBS group, while the COL2A1+ area was larger (Fig. 7D and E).

DFbs improve the inflammatory phenotype of synovial fibroblasts and chondrocytes via paracrine secretion

The effects of DFbs on both synovial fibroblasts and chondrocytes in vitro were further validated using the co-culture system (Fig. 8A).

Synovial fibroblasts were divided into three groups: normal culture conditions (Control), stimulation with IL-1β (FI), and co-culture with DFb following IL-1β stimulation (DFI). PCA and volcano plots revealed distinct transcriptomic profiles among groups, with FI cells showing robust pro-inflammatory phenotype (Fig. 8B–S7A-C). GO enrichment analysis of DEGs between DFI and FI groups, combined with GSEA of inflammation-related gene sets demonstrated downregulation of pathways linked to inflammatory response and cell adhesion (Fig. 8C and D). Consistent with this phenotypic reversal, pro-inflammatory markers including Il6 and Cxcl1, as well as other inflammation-related genes, were significantly downregulated in the DFI group relative to the FI group (Fig. 8E). Immunofluorescence staining validated reduced IL-6 protein expression in DFI cells, with lower mean fluorescence intensity (Fig. 8F–H).

Chondrocytes were similarly divided into three experimental groups: normal culture conditions (Control), stimulation with IL-1β (CI), and co-culture with DFb following IL-1β stimulation (DCI). The volcano plot (Fig. 8I) showed that the expression of multiple inflammation-related genes (e.g., Ptgs2, Cxcl1, Nos2) in the co-culture group was significantly downregulated. To identify the genes with reversed expression following co-culture, intersection analysis was performed between the DEGs of IL-1β-stimulated chondrocytes relative to healthy chondrocytes and the DEGs after co-culture (Fig. 8J). Enrichment analysis revealed that these overlapping genes were primarily enriched in gene sets associated with inflammatory response and extracellular matrix (ECM) (Fig. 8K). Both the clustering heatmap and subsequent RT-qPCR validation showed that following co-culture, inflammation-related gene expression was downregulated, while chondrocyte ECM-related gene expression was upregulated (Fig. 8L and M). Alcian blue staining indicated that IL-1β-induced impairment of chondrogenesis was partially restored following co-culture with DFbs (Fig. 8N). Consistent results were obtained using primary mouse chondrocytes (Fig. S8), which further validated the robustness of these conclusions.

Collectively, these data demonstrate that DFbs secrete paracrine factors that suppress the pro-inflammatory phenotype of IL-1β-activated synovial fibroblasts, reverse IL-1β-induced inflammatory reprogramming of chondrocytes, and restore chondrocyte ECM anabolic function. This paracrine regulation provides a mechanistic basis for the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of DFb therapy against OA.

Intersection analysis of DFb supernatant proteins with human secreted proteins and secreted immunoglobulins identified 594 high-confidence DFb-secreted proteins (Fig. 9B). Functional annotation revealed that these proteins primarily include various growth factors, signaling ligands, and ECM components (Fig. 9C).

To narrow down candidate mediators, we intersected these secreted proteins with downregulated DEGs from OA synovial fibroblasts (both in vivo and in vitro), yielding 12 potential therapeutic proteins (Fig. 9D). Among these candidates, apolipoprotein D (APOD) stood out: transcriptomic analysis showed APOD was significantly downregulated in OA synovial fibroblasts but highly expressed in DFbs (Fig. 9E–G). Validation via the Human Protein Atlas database confirmed high APOD expression in fibroblasts across tissues, supporting its cell-type specificity (Fig. S9A).

Previous studies have demonstrated that APOD can inhibit inflammatory responses by downregulating the expression of cyclooxygenase 2 (COX2) [47], and multiple studies have reported its protective effects in knee joint tissues [48,49] (Fig. S10). To validate the functional role of APOD, we performed gain- and loss-of-function experiments in primary DFbs. For overexpression, DFbs were transfected with an APOD-overexpressing plasmid; for knockdown, three independent siRNAs targeting rat Apod (si-Apod-1, si-Apod-2, si-Apod-3) were designed and synthesized. RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses revealed that all three Apod-targeting siRNAs exhibited comparable and robust knockdown efficiency, with no significant differences among si-Apod-1, -2, and -3. Accordingly, si-Apod-1 was selected for all subsequent loss-of-function experiments. Empty vector and non-targeting siRNA (siNC) were used as negative controls, and transfection efficiency was verified at 48 h post-transfection (Fig. S9B and C). Our results showed that overexpression of APOD in DFbs significantly downregulated the expression of pro-inflammatory genes (including Nos2, Il6 and Ptgs2) in FLS, and markedly reduced the expression of matrix catabolic genes (including Mmp9 and Mmp13) in chondrocytes, compared with the empty vector control group (Fig. 9I–K). While siRNA-mediated knockdown of APOD markedly abrogated these effects (Fig. 9H–J). Consistent with the mRNA-level findings, Western blot analysis further confirmed these results at the protein level, showing that APOD overexpression enhanced, while APOD knockdown diminished, the inhibitory effects of DFbs on the protein expression of pro-inflammatory mediators (iNOS, IL-6) and matrix catabolic enzyme (MMP9) (Fig. 9L and M). Notably, recombinant APOD protein alone showed only moderate therapeutic efficacy, which was significantly weaker than that of empty vector DFb co-culture group. These results demonstrate that APOD is a key but not the sole paracrine mediator of DFb action. Other candidate factors identified in our proteomic analysis, including PDGFA, IGF2, and IL1RN, likely act synergistically with APOD to exert the full therapeutic effect of DFbs.

Consistent with in vitro findings, immunofluorescent staining of rat synovial tissues showed that DFb treatment significantly reduced COX2+ cells, further verifying the anti-inflammatory role of APOD in vivo (Fig. S9D and E).

Collectively, these results identify APOD as a critical paracrine effector secreted by DFbs, which inhibits FLS inflammatory activation and chondrocyte catabolic reprogramming to ameliorate OA pathogenesis. Our data also support that the full therapeutic efficacy of DFbs relies on the synergistic action of APOD with other secretome components.

Discussion

OA remains a globally prevalent disabling disease, with synovitis-driven disruption of synovial fibroblast homeostasis emerging as a central pathological driver. Here, we propose and validate a novel cell therapeutic paradigm for OA: intra-articular injection of DFbs to restore disrupted synovial fibroblast lineage homeostasis. Through comprehensive in vitro and in vivo experiments, we demonstrate that DFbs, which share remarkable phenotypic and transcriptomic similarity with healthy synovial fibroblasts, engraft specifically in the synovium, suppress synovial inflammation, protect articular cartilage, and improve gait function in a rat OA model. Mechanistically, we identify APOD as a key paracrine mediator of DFb action. These findings establish a new mechanistic framework for OA treatment and provide a clinically feasible strategy for targeting fibroblast lineage dysfunction in chronic inflammatory joint diseases.

A key insight of this study is that exogenous DFbs, which retain the core transcriptional signature of universal fibroblasts [25,50,51], can act as "functional replacements" to rebalance the synovial fibroblast pool. We demonstrate that DFbs retain the core transcriptional signature of universal fibroblasts and exhibit near-identical phenotypic and transcriptomic profiles to healthy synovial fibroblasts, while lacking the pro-inflammatory and senescent signatures that define OA FLS. Critically, DFbs display intrinsic resistance to inflammatory stimulation: unlike FLS, they do not undergo hyperproliferation or robust inflammatory activation upon IL-1β challenge. This intrinsic resistance to inflammation is a critical advantage of DFbs, as it allows them to maintain their homeostatic phenotype in the hostile OA joint microenvironment. By suppressing the expansion of pathogenic sublining synovial fibroblasts and reversing their pro-inflammatory activation, DFbs restore two core dimensions of lineage homeostasis: normalized subset composition and quiescent transcriptional function. This lineage-specific mechanism distinguishes DFb therapy from broad anti-inflammatory approaches and directly targets the root pathological defect in OA synovium.

The therapeutic success of intra-articular cell therapy depends on targeted engraftment, durable bioactivity and favorable safety—all of which were fulfilled by DFbs in this study. In vivo tracking revealed that intra-articularly injected DFbs specifically localize to the synovial lining and sublining layers without off-target migration to cartilage, bone or distant organs, confirming inherent synovial tropism. Notably, we observed that DFbs persisted in the joint cavity for at least 3 weeks. While inflammation accelerated the clearance of a portion of injected DFbs, the remaining cells were sufficient to exert therapeutic effects that persisted for at least 2 months post-injection. This observation supports a 'hit-and-run' mechanism of action for DFb therapy: transiently engrafted DFbs secrete bioactive factors that remodel the synovial microenvironment and restore fibroblast lineage homeostasis, leading to long-lasting disease modification even after the majority of transplanted cells have been cleared. This mechanism is particularly advantageous for OA treatment, as it avoids the risks associated with long-term cell engraftment while providing sustained therapeutic benefits.

High-resolution single-cell transcriptomic profiling further decoded the in vivo fate and functional heterogeneity of transplanted DFbs, revealing a dynamic adaptive response to the OA joint microenvironment. Exogenous DFbs exhibited concurrent stress-induced apoptosis and residual proliferation, reflecting exposure to hypoxia, nutrient deprivation and inflammatory stress in the joint cavity. Importantly, cell–cell communication analysis uncovered bidirectional crosstalk between DFbs and synovial macrophages: DFbs modulated macrophage function via CSF and TGF-β signaling, while macrophages regulated DFb survival through TNF signaling. This multicellular crosstalk amplifies the synovial microenvironment remodeling effects of DFbs, extending therapeutic activity beyond direct fibroblast lineage modulation.

Proteomic and functional validation identified APOD as a critical paracrine mediator of DFb-mediated therapeutic effects. APOD is a lipocalin family protein with well-documented antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [[47], [48], [49]], and we found that it is significantly downregulated in OA synovial fibroblasts but highly expressed in DFbs. Our gain- and loss-of-function experiments demonstrated that APOD secreted by DFbs inhibits the inflammatory activation of synovial fibroblasts and the catabolic phenotype of chondrocytes. Importantly, we also showed that recombinant APOD alone cannot fully replicate the therapeutic efficacy of intact DFbs, and that APOD-knockdown DFbs still retain partial anti-inflammatory and chondroprotective effects. These findings indicate that the therapeutic effects of DFbs are mediated by the synergistic action of a 'secretome cocktail' containing multiple bioactive factors", with APOD serving as a critical but not sole component. Other candidate factors identified in our proteomic analysis, including PDGFA, IGF2, and IL1RN [[52], [53], [54], [55]], likely contribute to the overall therapeutic effect by suppressing inflammation and promoting tissue repair.

Notably, this study provides new perspective on MSC-based OA therapy. MSCs are known to engraft in the synovium and on the articular cartilage surface [56,57] and exert immunomodulatory effects via paracrine secretion [[57], [58], [59], [60], [61], [62], [63], [64], [65]], but their capacity to target fibroblast lineage imbalance remains understudied. Our study provides a fibroblast-centric framework to re-evaluate MSC therapy and positions DFbs as a pragmatic, lineage-specific alternative for OA treatment. Furthermore, DFb offer unique advantages: their tissue accessibility (e.g., prepuce, skin biopsies), lineage specificity, and favorable safety profile (no systemic migration or organ toxicity) make them a pragmatic alternative or complement to MSCs. A reliable manufacturing process and standardized regulatory pathway for allogeneic fibroblast therapy have been developed and validated [66].

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration in future research. First, the clinical cohort exhibited imbalanced age and sex distribution between healthy and OA donors, a common constraint in OA research due to the difficulty of obtaining age-matched healthy synovial tissue [67,68]. Future studies with balanced cohorts will refine mechanistic conclusions. Second, the long-term durability of DFb therapy beyond 2 months remains to be determined; extended observation up to 6 months will clarify sustained disease-modifying effects. Third, we did not perform head-to-head comparison between DFbs and MSCs in the same OA model, which is needed to define relative therapeutic advantages and clinical indications. Finally, the downstream signaling pathways of APOD in synovial fibroblasts and chondrocytes remain incompletely characterized; mechanistic studies will uncover actionable targets for APOD-based small-molecule or gene therapy.

In conclusion, this study establishes a novel cell therapeutic paradigm for OA based on restoring synovial fibroblast lineage homeostasis. We demonstrate that intra-articular injection of DFbs, which share remarkable similarity with healthy synovial fibroblasts, effectively alleviates synovitis and cartilage degeneration in a rat model of OA. Mechanistically, DFbs act through paracrine secretion of multiple bioactive factors, with APOD serving as one of the key mediators. These findings advance our understanding of fibroblast biology in OA and provide a rationale for the clinical development of DFb-based therapy as a disease-modifying treatment for this prevalent and debilitating disease.

Conclusion

In summary, this study proposes a novel cell-based therapeutic strategy: restoring synovial fibroblast homeostasis via cell therapy to interrupt pathological synovium-cartilage crosstalk in OA. Empirical validation through rigorous in vitro and in vivo experiments confirms the feasibility of this approach. By bridging a critical gap in cell-based therapies for knee OA-associated synovitis, our findings offer a novel therapeutic option for early to mid-stage patients. This therapeutic paradigm holds promise for delaying disease progression and reducing the necessity for knee joint replacement in this patient population.
