The implementation of Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) in a Brazilian Public Hospital: a systematic review of challenges and perspectives in oncology
Authors: Stêphanie Rocha Vieira Elexias, Bruno A. Lopes, Rodolfo Acatauassú, Louisy Sanches dos Santos, Thiago Castro, Mariana Chantre-Justino, Thaís Porto Amadeu, Fabricio Borges Carrerette, Maria Helena Ornellas, Flavia Barata Ribeiro Pinto, Livia Loureiro, Adriana Huertas-Vazquez, Fabio Santiago
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNext-generation sequencing (NGS) technology has revolutionized oncology by detecting novel and rare somatic cancer mutations and identifying individuals with pathogenic germline variants, both of which can facilitate personalized medicine. By integrating NGS into routine diagnosis in Brazil, patients may benefit from improved treatment responses, however, several barriers to the implementation of NGS in Brazil exist. This review provides practical insights into how NGS can be integrated into diagnostics in public hospitals.MethodsA comprehensive systematic review was conducted following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta Analysis (PRISMA). PubMed, Medline, and Cochrane databases were queried for publications written in English, French, Portuguese, or Spanish from 2014 to February 2025. The research was conducted using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), including terms such as NGS, public hospital, diagnostic, implementation and Brazilian Public Health System (SUS).ResultsOf the 7804 articles identified, 1233 were analyzed with only 10 meeting inclusion criteria. Current literature on NGS is largely centered in the Global North, with limited contributions from low- and middle-income countries. Key challenges included lack of access to molecular profiling and inadequate medical education (27% each), high costs of NGS (23%), data interpretation challenges (15%), and need for additional cost-benefit analyses (8%). Of note, only one paper conducted a cost-effectiveness analysis, demonstrating the utility of NGS in treating cancer.ConclusionsThis systematic review highlights key gaps on NGS implementation in Brazil. There is a need for improved education for healthcare professionals and students to effectively incorporate NGS into clinical oncology practice, as well as strategies to lower costs of sequencing and systematic data interpretation pipelines. Despite these challenges, Brazil has the potential to lead NGS implementation in Latin America. Our experiences at a public hospital may be used as a model to implement NGS through meticulous procurement management, leveraging expertise from basic research, and integrating AI-driven data analysis platforms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12920-026-02326-5.

Background

The use of and advancements of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology in clinical practice has transformed the practice of oncology [1]. Recently, NGS has been utilized in clinical oncology to enhance clinical management, as detection of both germline pathogenic variants and somatic cancer mutations allow for the use of personalized medicine (PM) approaches [2]. NGS represents a substantial improvement in both diagnostics and selection of therapeutics by replacing traditional single-gene testing (e.g. Sanger sequencing) with high-throughput techniques that simultaneously assess multiple genomic alterations.

The Instituto Nacional de Cancer estimated that 704,000 new cancer cases would be detected in 2024 and to be the leading cause of death in Brazil by 2028 [3]. Brazil has a population of approximately 220 million people with White, Black, Indigenous and Asian ancestries; currently, multiracial people represent the largest ethnic group (45.3%) [4]. Given this diversity, NGS is fundamental in enabling personalized medicine approaches to be used on an individualized basis, considering the heterogeneity of the population in prevention, early detection, and treatment selection, aiming to reduce the burden of cancer in Brazil [5]. Although the Sistema Único de Saúde (SUS; the Brazilian Unified Health System, ) provides all Brazilians with access to the public healthcare system, inadequate financial support and cooperation between public and private sectors and regional differences in care can affect clinical services, with disparate access to genetic testing in both the public and private healthcare systems [5]. We use Pillar oncoReveal Multi-Cancer and oncoReveal™ Myeloid panels to stratify patient samples from the Brazilian Unified Health System (SUS) that are acquired through public bidding. To our knowledge, our laboratory at the Pedro Ernesto University Hospital in Rio de Janeiro is the first and only public hospital with the ability to offer both hereditary and somatic molecular testing to patients with cancer (Fig. 1).

Given the need for more widespread molecular testing across Brazil, this study presents a systematic review of the literature on the installation of molecular laboratories and implementation of NGS technology for diagnostic purposes, highlighting key challenges involved in this process, including technical limitations, data analysis complexities and cost. In addition, strategies to facilitate increased incorporation of NGS into clinical practice, including the use of targeted panels for myeloid carcinomas and for BRCA1/2, and a 52-gene panel for solid malignancies are proposed based on our experience in implementing NGS into the diagnostic workflow.

Methods

A systematic review of the literature was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta Analysis (PRISMA) [6] (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). On February 1, 2025, a database search was performed using PubMed Central, Medline and Cochrane Library. The database search was performed using the following combinations of Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) terms: (1) NGS, public hospital/diagnostic; (2) NGS, implementation/diagnostic; (3) NGS, laboratory/Sistema Único de Saúde (SUS; the Brazilian Unified Health System); (4) next-generation sequencing, diagnostic/SUS; (5) next-generation sequencing, diagnostic/Brazilian Unified Health System; (6) next-generation sequencing, diagnostic/Unified Health System; (7) next-generation sequencing, laboratory/Unified Health System; (8) next-generation sequencing, laboratory/Brazilian Unified Health System; (9) next-generation sequencing, implementation/diagnostic; (10) next-generation sequencing, Public hospital/diagnostic; (11) next-generation sequencing, diagnostic/Brazilian Public Health System; and (12) next-generation sequencing, laboratory/Brazilian Public Health System. The following inclusion criteria were applied for article selection: (1) indexed from 2014 to February 1, 2025; (2) included letters to the editor, case reports, case presentation, case series, and original research report; (3) manuscripts detailing the implementation of NGS technology for the diagnosis of oncological patients in developing countries, with a particular emphasis on Brazil and Latin America; (4) was written in English, French, Portuguese, or Spanish. Search results were merged with EndNote 21 Reference Manager (The EndNote Team, Clarivate, Philadelphia, PA), and duplicate records were removed, and the results were peer-reviewed Our PICO framework includes P: cancer patients in developing countries, with a focus on Brazil and Latin America, treated in public hospitals or public health systems; I: Implementation of NGS as a diagnostic or molecular characterization tool; C: Conventional diagnostic methods or absence of comparative intervention; O: Feasibility, diagnostic impact, clinical improvements, cost-effectiveness, and implementation challenges. When available, the full texts of articles were obtained and reviewed for eligibility. Disagreements regarding eligibility were resolved by further discussion with three senior researchers (FS, BAL and MHO). Our study was registered in Prospero with the number CRD420251241108.

Results

Our comprehensive search identified a total of 7,804 articles, of which 1,233 were analyzed. Ultimately, only eight articles met the criteria for inclusion in this systematic review, and an additional two articles were added from citation searching, as depicted in Fig. 2. The majority of research on NGS technology originated from developed countries, with a notable lack of reports from developing nations detailing experiences and efforts in establishing laboratories for NGS-based oncological diagnostics. Table 1 presents a summary of the articles that met criteria, highlighting the country of origin, implementation details, challenges encountered, and the funding sources. The predominant challenges reported were “Barriers to the implementation of molecular profiling” and “Medical education and/or staff training,” each accounting for 27% of the total challenges, followed by “High cost of sequencer and materials” (23%), “Challenges in data interpretation” (15%), and “Cost-benefit analysis of genetic testing” (8%) (Fig. 3).

Barriers to the implementation of molecular profiling in Brazil

Araujo et al., evaluated barriers to molecular profiling in patients with non-squamous cell lung cancer (NSCLC) in Brazil and noted that, although critical for providing personalized therapy, access to molecular testing and targeted therapies has been hindered by social and economic barriers globally, including inadequate infrastructure, technical knowledge gaps, high costs, and slow adoption of new drugs [7]. In low- and middle-income countries, personalized therapy is often inaccessible, especially in public healthcare settings. For instance, routine biomarker tests for NSCLC, especially evaluation of EGFR mutations or ALK rearrangements, have been limited to hospitals in populous cities; in Brazil, approximately half of the patients with NSCLC underwent EGFR testing, however, the majority of these tests were performed in private healthcare facilities [8, 9]. In addition, the high costs of new molecular tests and therapies can further exacerbate healthcare inequities [8]. Araujo et al. noted that in Brazil, barriers to molecular profiling include not only limited infrastructure, reimbursement and physician and patient education but also frequently insufficient tissue samples for testing [7].

Moraes & Stuchi-Maria-Engler (2023) explored the rapid integration of PM into clinical practice, particularly in oncology [10]. The authors presented several challenges to widespread implementation of NGS, including technical, ethical, and regulatory barriers, as well as the need for a robust bioinformatics infrastructure and specialized personnel to handle the complex data generated by these technologies. Despite these challenges, Brazil has been classified as having a favorable infrastructure to promote widespread PM approaches [11]. For example, the established Health Technology Assessment (HTA) process and number of qualified personnel in Brazil can facilitate implementation of molecular profiling into oncology practice; however, the country as a whole has not the committed budgetary resources needed, thus those treated in the public sector do not have widespread access to genetic resources. Moraes & Stuchi-Maria-Engler suggest that with strategic resource management and strong political commitment, Brazil can overcome these barriers to genetic testing and advocate for the development of comprehensive national guidelines, enhanced medical education, collaborative efforts among government bodies, healthcare professionals and the pharmaceutical industry to address financial and logistical challenges and the creation of a national genomic database to support the integration of NGS technologies [10].

Another challenge to implementing NGS in Brazil is the difficulty in correlating genetic variants with different phenotypes, given the high miscegenation of the Brazilian population when many large genetic databases are highly enriched in populations of European, American and Asian origin [12, 13]. The effective application of NGS methodologies in Brazil thus requires specialized training, complemented by comprehensive knowledge in biology, genetics, and the molecular mechanisms of carcinogenesis, allowing for the analysis and comparison of DNA/RNA sequences, with the aim of identifying mutations and genetic variants, in addition to correlating gene expression with disease. The shortage of professionals with the necessary knowledge to perform post-sequencing analysis, including identifying errors in the sequencing reads, assessing the quality of the sequencing run, detecting possible contamination in the samples, ensuring adequate coverage in the sequencing regions, verifying the successful assembly of the genome, and ensuring that results are accurately input into the analysis software [14], is a significant barrier to the implementation of molecular profiling in Brazil. In addition, investing in educational programs to train health professionals in utilizing genomic technologies is crucial [15, 16]. Physicians, nurses and other health professionals must be equipped to interpret genomic data effectively and integrate this knowledge into the diagnostic and treatment processes to ultimately enhance patient care.

Other challenges to widespread use of NGS in oncology care include identification of genetic panels with the optimal size and content to improve diagnosis, prognosis and treatment selection for the diverse Brazilian patient population while not significantly inflating costs [17, 18]. Beyond the cost of the panels themselves is the cost of reagents and infrastructure.

Cost-benefit analysis of genetic testing: exome analysis or targeted panels

In 2016, Helmy et al.. described significant challenges faced by developing countries in implementing genome sequencing technologies [19]. Establishment of a NGS facility can cost between U$100,000 and U$700,000 in the developed world with up to five times higher costs in low- compared to high-income countries attributable to factors such as cost of analysis, shipment, and infrastructure [20, 21], which is prohibitively expensive for many institutions in these regions. In addition, the choice between exome analysis and targeted gene panels requires a careful evaluation of cost and benefits [22]. Exome analysis, which sequences the entire protein-coding regions of the genome, offers a comprehensive understanding of genetic mutations, making it valuable for complex cancer cases with unclear genetic origins. However, this approach is associated with higher costs and generates extensive data that require substantial computational resources and specialized expertise for interpretation—resources that may be limited within the Brazilian healthcare system.

Although the availability of whole genome (WGS), exome, or transcriptome sequencing has been increasing, targeted gene sequencing remains the method of choice in clinical laboratories for cancer diagnosis [23]. This approach ensures optimal sequencing quality, including read depth and coverage, variant characterization, reporting, cost-effectiveness, and turnaround time. Small NGS panels, comprising fewer than 50 genes, can be applied to specific cancers such as acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) or breast cancer. However, larger NGS panels are commonly used in academic hospitals and commercial laboratories to address a wider range of cancers. Targeted gene panels focus on specific genes known to be implicated in certain cancers, making this method generally more cost-effective and faster than panels with hundreds of genes or WGS [24]. By sequencing fewer genes, data interpretation is simplified, providing precise and actionable insights, especially when clinical indications suggest a specific genetic etiology. Given the budgetary constraints often encountered in the Brazilian healthcare system, targeted panels represent a practical option, facilitating the efficient use of limited resources. However, this approach might fail to detect mutations beyond the selected genes, potentially overlooking critical genetic information. Ultimately, the choice between comprehensive and targeted sequencing depends on the clinical context, specific diagnostic requirements, and the available resources within Brazil’s healthcare infrastructure.

A single publication that performed a cost-effectiveness analysis of implementation of genetic testing in Brazil was identified in our literature search. Lopes David et al.. (2023) conducted a cost-benefit analysis of genetic testing for patients with gastrointestinal tumors, evaluating the efficacy of four NGS panels: two broad panels and two targeted panels [25]. The first broad panel, “FoundationOne,” analyses 324 genes [26], while “GS Infinity” covers 463 genes [27]. The targeted panels include “GS Focus GIST” [28] and “Fleury GIST” [29] which both interrogate two genes. The authors also compared the costs of the drug Glivec (Novartis) and its generic imatinib mesylate and considered costs for both public and private healthcare sectors. Overall, the authors found that the costs of preventing an event through genomic profiling before treatment were cheaper than treatment costs for all scenarios except for the use of the large FoundationOne assay in the public sector.

Challenge in data interpretation and staff training

The NGS platforms can generate high-quality and accurate data at a massive scale. However, the sheer volume of data produced poses significant challenges in data processing and analysis [2]. This has driven the development of advanced bioinformatics systems, rapid data processing technologies, and extensive data storage solutions, which can be costly to implement. In Brazil, while many institutions can adopt NGS technology, they often lack the computational resources and skilled personnel required for accurate data analysis and interpretation. The shortage of expertise and limited access to essential genomic data manipulation tools further impede progress in utilizing NGS technologies.

Helmy et al.. (2016) and Rocha et al.. (2022) reported that developing countries with higher research and development (R&D) spending, such as China, India, and Brazil, are making significant contributions to the field of NGS data interpretation, although gaps in bioinformatics expertise remains a critical issue [16, 19]. Solutions to this barrier include increasing research funding, establishing centralized sequencing facilities, fostering international collaborations, and creating effective training programs to address these challenges.

Medical education in the requesting of NGS tests

Sadik et al.., 2022 explored the critical need for medical education to effectively integrate NGS and PM into clinical practice, noting that a significant number of patients do not receive optimal personalized treatments due to gaps in clinical practice, including limited understanding of biomarker strategies and inappropriate use of testing results [30]. For example, 64% of patients with advanced NSCLC who were eligible for precision oncology therapies did not benefit from them, attributable to operational inefficiencies and lack of awareness among healthcare providers. Thus, there is an urgent need for comprehensive medical education programs to enhance the understanding and application of NGS and personalized medicine among clinicians, ensuring that patients receive the most appropriate and effective treatments available.

Ashton-Prolla et al.. (2015) also described the challenges faced by doctors when deciding between WGS or targeted sequencing [12]. While sequencing the entire genome might seem comprehensive, it results in a large amount of data which can be time-consuming to analyze and may fail to provide definitive answers about the disease. Therefore, targeted panels may be the more effective approach in resource-limited settings.

Since 2014, the inclusion of genetics in the medical curriculum by the Ministry of Education has marked a significant advancement, enabling laboratories to collaborate with a diverse range of health professionals. Another important development is the implementation of genetic counselling services led exclusively by medical professionals [12]. Existing counselling services consist of multidisciplinary teams focused on specific diseases, providing valuable support to both doctors and patients. For example, a bioinformatician may play a crucial role analyzing sequencing data, identifying genetic alterations relevant to diseases and possible adverse reactions to medications, assessing the patient’s eligibility for clinical trials based on their genetic alterations, and guide doctors in selecting the most effective treatment.

Implementing such educational initiatives in Brazil is crucial. The adoption of advanced technologies for cancer diagnosis and treatment has been significantly delayed in Brazil, with disparities in access to molecular testing and targeted therapies between the public and private sectors [5]. By fostering a robust educational framework that includes training in NGS and PM, Brazil can bridge these gaps, improve diagnostic accuracy, and optimize treatment outcomes. This approach will not only enhance the capabilities of healthcare professionals but also ensure that the benefits of personalized medicine are accessible to all patients, thereby contributing to a more equitable and effective healthcare system.

Discussion

The adoption of NGS technologies in Brazil faces significant hurdles, including complex regulatory requirements, high costs, and the need for specialized personnel and robust bioinformatics infrastructure. Consequently, the implementation of NGS technology into routine oncology practice presents a challenge for most Brazilian public hospitals and regional oncology treatment centers. Based on our experiences, we offer suggestions to overcome barriers to the implementation of molecular profiling, improve medical education, facilitate data interpretation and reduce sequencing costs.

To address these challenges, we established meticulous organization and management strategies for procurement through public bidding processes, which not only reduced costs but also guaranteed full compliance with local regulations. Furthermore, we leveraged our long-standing expertise in NGS from basic research, successfully transferring this knowledge to the diagnostic laboratory environment. This transition was supported by integrating professionals with strong backgrounds in genomics, molecular biology, and bioinformatics, ensuring that the technical and analytical demands of NGS were adequately met.

Equally important, we invested in capacity building and continuous medical education, enabling oncologists and pathologists to interpret molecular profiling data more effectively and apply it to patient care. Collaborative efforts with multidisciplinary team, including clinicians, researchers, and hospital administrator, were essential to creating a sustainable model for NGS integration.

In addition to the above steps we utilized to overcome barriers to the development of NGS facilities, we also integrated the study of NGS into the curricula of the Biology, Nursing, Medicine and Nutrition faculties. This includes comprehensive theoretical lectures and guided tours of the NGS laboratory. Recognizing the critical need for proficiency in interpreting NGS data, we have also established a dedicated training week for resident physicians and hospital staff. This initiative aims to enhance their understanding of NGS technology and its pivotal role in Oncology.

We integrated advanced data analysis platforms that utilize artificial intelligence, minimizing the need for extensive bioinformatics expertise and enhancing the accuracy of diagnostic reports To address these limitations, our laboratory adopted the Illumina Connected Insights-Research platform [31, 32]. This customizable platform integrates over 55 external knowledge sources, such as JAX-CKB and OncoKB, and supports a wide range of DNA and RNA assays This platform serves as a solution to the shortage of bioinformaticians in Brazil by automating the interpretation of genetic variants and streamlines the user experience from data upload to report generation, significantly reducing the time required for variant interpretation and enabling Brazilian laboratories to scale their operations efficiently. By offering powerful tools for automating user-defined workflows and providing comprehensive visualization features, Connected Insights-Research enhances operational efficiency and accuracy in genomic data interpretation.

High prices of medications have brought budget difficulties to fund treatments and undermined efforts to achieve sophisticated diagnoses. Integrated work between government and non-government bodies, professional society representatives, healthcare professionals, and the pharmaceutical industry could help to obtain a viable solution.

While private hospitals often have more flexibility to address these costs, the public system depends on funds from bidding processes, research funding or direct government support. To overcome these barriers, forming partnerships with research institutions may be beneficial. These collaborations offer access to advanced technologies, specialized technical knowledge and data, accelerating the development and adaptation of genetic sequencing tools into the Brazilian health care system. International partnerships may provide new opportunities for funding and expand access to global scientific knowledge, facilitating the integration of cutting-edge technologies into the SUS.

Conclusions

Through these combined efforts, we demonstrated that the introduction of NGS in a resource-limited setting is feasible when supported by strategic planning, cross-disciplinary collaboration, and the adaptation of research expertise to clinical practice. This experience highlights a replicable model that could inform similar initiatives across Latin America and other low- and middle-income countries. In conclusion, implementation of molecular profiling into routine oncology care is critical to reduce the increasing cancer burden in Brazil. Strategies such as those that have been successful in our laboratory may serve as a reference for other sites. In addition, future studies are needed to evaluate long-term clinical outcomes, assess cost-effectiveness, and develop scalable frameworks that can further expand access to precision oncology.
