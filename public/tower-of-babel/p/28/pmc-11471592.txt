The relationship between Fruit and vegetable intake and frailty: Data from NHANES 2007–2018
Authors: Kaikai Hu, Liuyin Jin, Yang Yang, Linman Wu, Guomin Xie, Xiping Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have indicated a certain relationship between the intake of fruits and vegetables and frailty. However, there is currently a lack of research on the association between fruit and vegetable intake and frailty.ObjectiveThis study aims to explore the potential correlation between fruit and vegetable intake and frailty. Additionally, we seek to investigate the relationship between fruit intake and frailty across different demographic characteristics and subgroups.MethodsInitially, participant information from the NHANES database (2007–2018) was extracted, including age, gender, race, education level, and other relevant factors. Subsequently, a comprehensive adjustment of multiple variables was carried out using a logistic regression model to analyze the relationship between vegetable and fruit intake and frailty. We also conducted subgroup analyses for variables showing significant differences in the regression model, exploring the relationship between vegetable and fruit intake and frailty in different demographic subgroups. Finally, we employed Restricted Cubic Splines (RCS) to explore the relationship between vegetable and fruit intake and frailty.ResultsIn our study of 13,935 participants, 2224 were classified as frail, while 11,711 were non-frail. Significant differences were observed between the frail and non-frail groups in variables such as gender, race, marital status, education level, and BMI. Weighted logistic regression analysis revealed a significant association between the intake of fruits (including f_other, f_whole, f_juice, f_total) and vegetables (including v_redor_total, v_total) and a reduced risk of frailty (p for trend <0.05, OR < 1). Notably, in the f_other group, both the medium and high intake groups had a significantly lower risk of frailty compared to the low intake group (ORs of 0.70 and 0.68, p < 0.001, respectively). Subgroup analysis indicated significant interactions between fruit and vegetable intake and frailty across different gender, income, alcohol intake, education level, and racial groups (p for interaction <0.05). Restricted cubic spline analysis showed that while an increase in fruit and vegetable intake reduced the risk of frailty within a certain range, the risk gradually increased beyond that range.ConclusionThis study provides preliminary evidence supporting a close association between vegetable and fruit intake and frailty, laying the foundation for future in-depth investigations into the dietary links with frailty. These findings also offer new nutritional intervention strategies for frailty prevention, emphasizing the crucial role of diet in maintaining overall health. However, further clinical and epidemiological research is needed to validate these associations and provide detailed explanations for the specific mechanisms through which fruit and vegetable intake impact frailty.Keywords: Fruit, Vegetable, Frailty, Juice, Red and orange vegetable

Introduction

Frailty is commonly described as a persistent decline in both physical and mental states, manifested by symptoms such as reduced physical strength, fatigue, diminished mobility, and a decline in overall quality of life [1].Frailty is a comprehensive concept involving multiple aspects, including muscle strength, cognitive function, and immune system health [2].Frailty scores are based on the assessment of multidimensional indicators, aiming to quantify an individual's overall health status [3].These indicators may include, but are not limited to, gait stability, grip strength, cognitive function, and levels of physical activity. By utilizing frailty scores, we can more comprehensively and objectively measure the degree of frailty in individuals [4].

In terms of dietary habits, the intake of fruits and vegetables has long been considered a key factor in maintaining health [5].Inadequate intake of fruits and vegetables is a major modifiable dietary risk factor contributing to increased mortality, both from infectious and non-communicable diseases [6,7].Concurrently, increased intake of fruits and vegetables is independently associated with reduced risks of diabetes, cancer, mortality, and improvements in cognitive function [[8], [9], [10]]. The consumption of fruits and vegetables plays a crucial role in supporting our mental and physical health, with reasonable intake associated with a lower risk of depression and anxiety [11,12].Particularly for children, increasing the variety of fruits and vegetables is crucial in supporting growth and development, as well as establishing healthy dietary habits into adulthood [13].Fruits and vegetables are rich in vitamins, minerals, antioxidants, and other essential nutrients, crucial for normal bodily functions and maintaining the immune system [14].Numerous studies have demonstrated a positive correlation between increased intake of fruits and vegetables and a reduced risk of chronic diseases, improved quality of life, and a slowing of frailty progression [15].However, research on the relationship between fruit and vegetable intake and frailty risk remains relatively limited.

To further explore the potential relationship between fruit and vegetable intake and frailty, this study opts to utilize the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database. This database offers a rich source of representative data spanning various age groups, genders, and ethnicities in the United States. Through an analysis of the NHANES database, our objective is to explore the correlation between fruit and vegetable intake and the risk of frailty. The aim is to establish a scientific foundation for the future development of targeted health intervention measures.

Method

Study population

NHANES is a comprehensive and nationally representative cross-sectional study conducted regularly since the 1960s, primarily aimed at assessing the health and nutritional status of children and adults in the United States. To ensure the national representativeness of the study population, NHANES employs a complex and multi-stage stratified sampling design. The study adheres to the principles of the Helsinki Declaration and has obtained approval from the Institutional Review Board of the CDC National Center for Health Statistics.

We gathered epidemiological data from 75,402 individuals aged 18 and above who participated in NHANES from 2007 to 2018. Subsequently, we refined this dataset by initially excluding participants with missing values for vegetable and fruit intake records (n = 26,784). Following that, we excluded individuals with missing values for marital status, gender, education level, poverty ratio, physical activity, alcohol consumption, smoking, BMI, and other variables (n = 34,683). Ultimately, we obtained a dataset of 13935 individuals.

Assessment of vegetable and Fruit intake

In the database, there were two dietary questionnaires, with the first 24-h recall conducted face-to-face by trained food recall data collectors in the Mobile Examination Center (MEC). The second recall was conducted via phone 3–10 days later. In our statistical analysis, we retained only the dietary intake data from participants who completed both recall surveys. Therefore, this analysis used the average vegetable and fruit intake over two days to reduce bias.

Based on the 2017–2018 Food Patterns Equivalent Database (FPED) [16], we defined different types of vegetables. Dark green vegetables included spinach, basil, beets, bitter melon leaves, and broccoli, among others. Red and orange vegetables included carrots, red peppers, and red or orange sweet peppers. Starchy vegetables comprised pumpkin, sweet potatoes (prepared in various ways such as fried, boiled, baked, or as chips), and other starchy vegetables like immature peas, lima beans, corn, breadfruit, burdock, and cassava. Other vegetables included artichokes, asparagus, avocados, bamboo shoots, beets, and total vegetable intake. For fruit intake, data were sourced from the FPED, providing information on food portion sizes and edible portions. In this study, fruit intake data were obtained from dietary interviews conducted as part of the FPED, recording the fruit intake of each participant over two days and averaging it for daily intake. Fruit intake included whole fruits, sliced fruits, juices, and the total sum of all fruits. The assessment of vegetable and fruit intake was standardized to cups, representing a standardized measure of vegetable and fruit intake. Equivalent weights were determined by summing similar types of fruits and rounding them to the nearest 0 or 5 g. This standardized measurement method is commonly used for food intake assessments and research purposes. We categorized vegetable and fruit intake into high, medium, and low groups based on percentiles, corresponding to Q3, Q2, and Q1 [15].

Assessment of frailty

Frailty was assessed using a frailty index model comprising 49 items across seven main categories: cognition (1 item), dependency (16 items), depression (7 items), comorbidities (13 items), hospital and nursing (5 items), anthropometrics (1 item), and laboratory results (6 items). Scores for specific frailty items were summed and divided by the number of frailty items, resulting in a numerical value between 0 and 1, representing the severity of frailty. A score of 0 indicated no frailty, while a score of 1 indicated complete frailty. This method allowed us to convert continuous variables into categorical variables for further analysis(see stable1)l. Regarding the frailty index, we divided participants into two groups: those with a score >0.21 were classified as the frailty group, while those with a score ≤0.21 were classified as the non-frailty group [17,18].

Assessment of covariates

In the initial interview, demographic and health-related data, including age, gender, race/ethnicity, education level, marital status, poverty income ratio (PIR), smoking, alcohol consumption, and total metabolic equivalents of task (MET) for weekly physical activity, were collected through a questionnaire. In the NHANES database, alcohol consumption was described as the average number of drinks per day over the past 12 months on days when the participant drank alcohol. Therefore, we defined "high intake" as drinks per day greater than or equal to 3 for females or greater than or equal to 4 for males, "middle intake" as drinks per day greater than or equal to 2 for females or greater than or equal to 3 for males, and the remaining as "light intake". For physical activity, we extracted the total MET equivalents for the week, categorized as "Low Exercise Group" for MET less than 600, "High Exercise Group" for MET greater than 1200, and "Moderate Exercise Group" for values in between. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.13), including the "NHANESR" and "survey" packages for data cleaning and analysis. In the baseline analysis, we presented continuous variables as weighted means ± standard deviations and conducted one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare differences between groups. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, with group comparisons performed using the chi-square test. Subsequently, we employed logistic regression models to analyze the relationship between fruit and vegetable intake and frailty. Model 1 was the most basic model, involving logistic regression for fruit and vegetable intake and frailty. Model 2 adjusted for variables such as age, race, marital status, education level, PIR, and BMI on top of Model 1. Model 3, building on Model 2, further incorporated smoking, physical activity, and alcohol consumption. To explore the relationship between fruit and vegetable intake and frailty in different subgroups based on age, education level, and other factors, we utilized the stratum_model to construct logistic regression analyses, calculating odds ratios (OR) and corresponding 95 % confidence intervals (CI). Given the potential for nonlinear relationships between variables, we utilized weighted Restricted Cubic Splines (RCS) analysis from the "rms" package to explore the correlation between fruit and vegetable intake and frailty. For subgroups with interactions, we conducted additional RCS analyses. In weighted logistic regression models, a significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Confidence intervals not including the value 1 were deemed statistically significant [21].

Results

Participant characteristics

In our study, we observed a total of 2224 participants classified as frail and 11,711 participants defined as non-frail. Except for the absence of differences between the frail and non-frail groups in the starch intake category, significant differences were observed in all other variables between these two groups. In the gender category, females reported a higher prevalence of frailty compared to males. Regarding race, 45 % of the Non-Hispanic White group reported frailty. Among participants with frailty, 44 % were married. In terms of education level, 78 % of participants with frailty had educational backgrounds in College, GED, or Some college. Additionally, 81 % of frail participants were distributed in the BMI >28 group. In terms of fruit, juice, dark green vegetables, and deep red vegetables intake, frail individuals exhibited higher starch vegetable intake and lower fruit and other vegetable intake compared to non-frail individuals, as shown in Table 2.

Logistic regression on Fruit and vegetable intake and frailty

To further investigate the potential correlation between vegetable and fruit intake and frailty, we conducted weighted logistic regression adjusting for comprehensive variables including age, gender, shimrace, education level, weekly MET for physical activity, smoking status, alcohol consumption, BMI, PIR, and others. We categorized vegetable and fruit intake into low, medium, and high intake groups based on percentiles, corresponding to Q1, Q2, and Q3 in the charts. After comprehensive adjustments, we found significant differences in frailty associated with the intake of four types of fruits (f_other, f_whole, f_juice, f_total) and two types of vegetables (v_redor_total, v_total) (p for trend <0.05). Moreover, these fruit and vegetable categories were associated with a reduced risk of frailty (OR < 1). Notably, in the f_other group (referring to the consumption of whole or granular fruits but excluding oranges, melons, and berries), the medium intake group had a lower odds ratio compared to the low intake group [0.70 (0.58, 0.84), p < 0.001], and the high intake group had a lower odds ratio compared to the low intake group [0.68 (0.57, 0.81), p < 0.0001]. In both comparisons, the OR values were lower than those in the f_whole, f_juice, f_total, v_redor_total, and v_total groups, indicating that consuming whole or granular fruits excluding oranges, melons, and berries may better reduce the risk of frailty. Subgroup analysis of Fruit and vegetable intake and frailty

To further explore the relationship between frailty and the intake of four fruit categories (f_total, f_other, f_whole, f_juice) and two vegetable categories (v_redor_total, v_total) across different age groups, genders, incomes, and other subgroups, and to identify subgroup effects, examine the correlation and interaction between fruit and vegetable intake and frailty, we conducted subgroup analyses on these six variables. Among all fruit intake categories, we found significant differences in frailty rates associated with total fruit intake in subgroups defined by gender, income (low income), alcohol intake (light and high intake), education (less than 9th grade and some college), race (Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American), marital status (married and never married), BMI, and age groups (20–39 years and 40–59 years) (p for trend <0.05). Additionally, the occurrence of frailty was consistently reduced in the medium and high intake groups compared to the low intake group. Discussion

Frailty is associated with increased vulnerability to stressors, and its prevalence is rising with the aging population [2,22].Clinically, various definitions of frailty exist, with the frailty phenotype proposed by Fried et al. [23].and the frailty index (FI) introduced by Mitnitski et al. [24,25]. FI being widely used. The FI accumulates health risks over the life course, making it applicable across a broad age range [26,27].Higher FI values are linked to numerous adverse health outcomes, including disability, mobility limitations, and chronic diseases [24].Consuming fruits and vegetables has been shown to reduce the risk of obesity, chronic diseases, improve mental health, and enhance gut health [9,17,25].Multiple studies indicate a negative correlation between the intake of plant-based diets, including fruits and vegetables, and the risk of frailty, consistent with the findings on dietary diversity23and intake of healthy plant-based foods (including whole grains, fruits, and vegetables, excluding unhealthy plant-based foods such as juices and potatoes) [28,29].Our logistic regression results suggest that the intake of starchy vegetables may increase the risk of frailty, with an OR of 1.03 for moderate intake compared to low intake and an OR of 1.03 for high intake compared to low intake. In contrast, increased intake of other vegetables and fruits is associated with a reduced risk of frailty, aligning with previous research [30,31].

There is a close relationship between BMI, plant-based foods, and frailty, with a higher likelihood of frailty in individuals with higher BMI. Research indicates that, compared to the normal BMI group, the combined RR range for frailty risk is 1.45 (95 % CI 1.10–1.90, I2 = 83.3 %; P < 0.01) in the overweight group and 1.40 (95 % CI 1.17–1.67, I2 = 86.1 %; P < 0.01) in the underweight group [32]. In our study, we found that regardless of the BMI group, increasing vegetable and fruit intake significantly reduces the incidence of frailty, especially in the underweight group. In daily life, consuming fruits as whole pieces or in their entirety is a viable option, and juicing is also a common practice [33].Research suggests that when fruits are consumed as whole pieces, they are absorbed more slowly in the intestine. In contrast, juicing leads to a large amount of fructose reaching the colon, promoting fat synthesis in the liver and potentially impacting appetite suppression [34]. Therefore, consuming whole fruits may reduce the risk of obesity and type 2 diabetes [8,[34], [35], [36]].Consistent with these findings, our study suggests that consuming whole fruits is more effective in lowering the risk of frailty compared to fruit juice consumption. Red and orange vegetables, including carrots, red peppers, and sweet peppers, are rich in nutrients such as vitamins, minerals, and bioactive compounds known as phytonutrients. These phytonutrients contribute to health improvement through their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antifungal, antiallergenic, chemical protective, neuroprotective, lipid-lowering, and/or blood pressure-lowering properties [22],In our study, an increased intake of these red and orange vegetables within a certain range significantly reduced the risk of frailty.

There is a negative correlation between vegetable and fruit intake and the risk of frailty, underscoring the crucial role of moderate vegetable and fruit consumption in both physical and mental health [7,[11], [12], [13]]. In the absence of a plant-based diet rich in vegetables and fruits, individuals may lack the intake of essential nutrients. A systematic review indicated that deficiencies in micronutrients (including vitamin D, carotenoids, vitamin B12, E, or C) and macronutrients (including protein intake) are associated with an increased risk of frailty [14,37,38]. However, with the exception of vitamin B12, most nutrients are primarily found in plant-based foods, emphasizing the importance of paying attention to plant-based foods [39].Additionally, fruit juices are more readily available than whole fruits in daily life, but increased fruit intake may raise dietary costs, These factors may limit people's fruit consumption [33,40]. Based on our study results and in conjunction with previous literature on the relationship between fruits, vegetables, and health, it is advisable to consider consuming whole fruits instead of fruit juices and increase the intake of red or orange vegetables while reducing the intake of starchy vegetables. Public awareness of the importance of vegetable and fruit intake for overall health can be enhanced through educational and promotional activities, dietary guidelines, and nutritional recommendations. Increasing access to fresh fruits and vegetables in schools and workplaces, establishing sustainable supply chains for fruits and vegetables, promoting agricultural sustainability and organic farming, and ensuring the affordability and accessibility of chosen options can collectively contribute to a healthier population.

One limitation of this study is its observational design, which constrains our ability to establish a definitive causal link between vegetable and fruit intake and frailty risk. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported dietary data introduces potential recall bias, which may affect the accuracy and reliability of the results. The generalizability of the findings may also be limited due to the specific demographic and geographic characteristics of the study sample, which could influence the applicability of the results to broader populations. While we accounted for several confounding variables, unmeasured factors such as genetic predispositions or other lifestyle habits might still impact the observed associations. Moreover, the relatively short follow-up period may not fully capture the long-term effects of dietary habits on frailty risk, highlighting the need for extended studies. Therefore, future intervention studies and mechanistic research are essential to further clarify the relationship between fruit and vegetable consumption and frailty, thereby providing stronger evidence to guide more precise nutritional intervention strategies.
