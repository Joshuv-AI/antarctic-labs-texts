Saliva as a potential and non-invasive approach to identify upregulated genes associated with comorbidities of T1DM: a brief report
Authors: Bashair M. Mussa, Ankita Srivastava, Reeja Rajan, Thenmozhi Venkatachalam, Abeer Al-Habshi, Elamin Abdelgadir, Alaaeldin Bashier, Fatheya Al Awadi, Khadija Hafidh, Rifat Hamoudi, Salah Abusnana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOne of the main challenges in Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) research is the sample size of the participants and the invasive process of collecting an adequate number of blood samples from young patients with T1DM. Therefore, it is of great interest to investigate the possibility of using saliva as a non-invasive tool to investigate the genetic factors that are associated with T1DM comorbidities. The present study aims to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in saliva samples of T1DM patients with various comorbidities using transcriptomic profiling.MethodsA total of 56 participants were recruited from the University Hospital Sharjah, Dubai Hospital and Rashid Hospital, United Arab Emirates. Participants were divided into various groups: Control Group: healthy Emirati (n = 13), Group 1: patients with T1DM without any comorbidities (G1; n = 14), Group 2: patients with T1DM and hyperlipidemia (G2; n = 10), Group 3: patients with T1DM and neuropathy (G3; n = 5), Group 4: patients with T1DM and ketoacidosis (G4; n = 6), Group 5: patients with T1DM and hypothyroidism (G5; n = 6) and Group 6: patients with T1DM and polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS, G6; n = 5). Saliva samples and blood were collected from all participants and RNA was extracted for transcriptomic analysis.ResultsThe transcriptomic analysis of saliva samples of T1DM patients showed several DEGs that were associated with T1DM comorbidities. Interestingly, the number of upregulated genes in blood (n = 350) and saliva (n = 353) was comparable in the PCOS group (G6). In addition, the number of DEGs was more than double in saliva (n = 270) compared to blood (n = 118) samples in the ketoacidosis group (G4). Ten common upregulated genes between saliva and blood were identified in T1DM patients with hyperlipidemia (G2): LYPD5, TCF15, PRKY, CYHR1, CCDC173, CHRNA5, MIAT, HBA2, RAD54L, and TSHZ3. In addition, two common upregulated genes were identified in the neuropathy group (G3): KCTD19, FAM209B. Moreover, the following upregulated genes were observed in ketoacidosis (G4), hypothyroidism (G5) and PCOS (G6): (NCKAP5, ADAD2, C20orf144), (ZFY, KCTD19, P2RY14, RPS60) and (C4orf19, RSAD2, MEGF10, CMPK2, CHRNA5, TSHZ3, CCDC77, CLEC12A, RTP4), respectively.ConclusionsThe present findings demonstrated that the number of upregulated genes can be comparable between saliva and blood of T1DM patients with comorbidities, and in some groups, the number of upregulated genes was higher in the saliva compared to blood, emphasizing that saliva can be a potential non-invasive tool to identify DEGs in T1DM comorbidities.

Background

Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) is one of the most challenging endocrine disorders, which is diagnosed mainly in children and young adults with a high prevalence of associated comorbidities [1, 2]. A dramatic increase in the incidence and prevalence of T1DM has been recorded around the world, and this directly influences the development of a wide range of comorbidities [1]. The latter include hyperlipidemia, neuropathy, ketoacidosis, hypothyroidism, and polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) [3, 4]. Although several studies have attempted to elucidate the underlying causes of these comorbidities, there are still some gaps in understanding the key factors that contribute to the development of T1DM comorbidities [5]. A large body of evidence has supported the role of genetic factors in the development of T1DM and its associated comorbidities [5–7]. However, no previous studies have investigated the differential expression of upregulated genes in T1DM comorbidities using saliva samples [5]. Our previous study, which was conducted using blood samples solely, has investigated the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in T1DM comorbidities [4]. However, withdrawing blood is an invasive procedure, especially in children and young adults; therefore, it is of great interest to conduct a follow-up study to investigate the possibility of using saliva as a non-invasive tool to study the genetic factors that are associated with T1DM comorbidities. Recently, using saliva as a non-invasive research tool has received increasing attention, as the collection of biopsies or blood samples is associated with a higher risk of infections and discomfort, especially in the young population, in addition to the time-consuming procedures [8].

Studying the heterogeneity of T1DM comorbidities represents an important research objective as it facilitates novel insights into the pathogenesis of these comorbidities. In addition, it provides a better understanding of the findings from sub-groups of patients with T1DM who experience a wide range of comorbidities and other sub-groups who have no experience of any comorbidities [3]. Investigating the DEGs in different sub-groups of T1DM patients with and without comorbidities can shed light on the genetic elements that are involved in the onset and development of T1DM comorbidities [8].

Our previous studies using blood samples have established transcriptomic profiles for five clusters of comorbidities including hyperlipidemia, neuropathy, ketoacidosis, hypothyroidism, and PCOS [4]. Interestingly, all five groups have seven common genes including SPINK9, TRDN, PVRL4, MYO3A, PDLIM1, KIAA1614, and GRP which were upregulated in T1DM patients with comorbidities compared to T1DM patients without comorbidities [4]. These genes were identified in the blood samples of T1DM patients; however, collecting a sufficient amount of blood samples is a challenging process which had negative impact on the total sample size [8].

Recent reports have highlighted saliva-derived transcriptomics as non-invasive and advanced techniques which hold promise for screening, early detection, diagnosis of a wide range of diseases including gastric cancer [9], oral cancer [10] and head and neck cancer [11]. In addition, saliva transcriptomic biomarkers were used to detect type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [12] however, none of the previous studies investigated T1DM comorbidities. Therefore, the present study is designed (i) to investigate the possibility of using saliva as a non-invasive tool to study the genes that are associated with T1DM comorbidities and (ii) to identify and compare the common upregulated genes between saliva and blood samples of patients with T1DM and its associated comorbidities.

Methods

Study population

A total of 56 Emirati participants (UAE citizens) recruited from University Hospital Sharjah (UHS), Dubai Hospital (DH) and Rashid Hospital (RH) were included in the present cross-sectional study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) age: > 18 years, (ii) BMI: < 25 kg/m2, (iii) Emirati participants, and (iv) confirmed diagnosis of T1DM and its associated comorbidities.

The study was approved by the ethical committees of the University of Sharjah (UOS, REC-17–08-08–01), UHS (UHS, 09042018), DH (DSREC-09/2018-13) and RH (DSREC-07/2019-05) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were requested to sign an informed-consent form written in their native language that was approved by the above-mentioned ethical committees. This study is a follow-up investigation from our previous work, which focused solely on blood samples in the same cohort (4). Based on the clinical characteristics and associated comorbidities, the participants were divided into the following groups: Control Group—healthy participants without T1DM (n = 13), Group 1—patients with T1DM without any comorbidities (G1; n = 14), Group 2—patients with T1DM and hyperlipidemia (G2; n = 10), Group 3—patients with T1DM and neuropathy (G3; n = 5), Group 4—patients with T1DM and ketoacidosis (G4; n = 6), Group 5—patients with T1DM and hypothyroidism (G5; n = 6) and Group 6—patients with T1DM and PCOS (G6; n = 5) (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics of the participants included the following variables: age, gender, age at diagnosis of T1DM, height, weight, BMI, HbA1c, and the lipid profile. Sample collection and processing

Both blood and saliva samples were collected from all participants. For saliva collection, subjects were requested to donate two samples (5 ml each) over 10 min using the passive drool technique, which involves moving their jaws in a chewing motion for a few seconds and then gently pooling the saliva into collection tubes containing RNA later® (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) [13]. Subsequently, RNA was extracted from these samples using the Qiazol® method (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. From the same participants, about 8 ml of blood was collected and processed as described in our previous study [4]. The processed samples were appropriately stored at − 20 ℃ until further use for sequencing analysis.

Sample variation was minimized during collection by standardizing the collection protocol across all time points and individuals, ensuring consistency in timing and storage conditions. For downstream analysis, normalization of raw counts using the DESeq2 package was performed, which applies size factor normalization to account for library depth and compositional differences. Given the limited number of saliva samples, stringent FDR thresholds were not applied in order to retain potentially biologically relevant genes that might otherwise be overlooked due to statistical stringency.

Whole transcriptome sequencing

About ~ 30 ng of Turbo DNase treated saliva and blood RNA was used to synthesize cDNA using SuperScript VILO cDNA Synthesis kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) followed by amplification with Ion Ampliseq gene expression core panel primers. Amplicons of ~ 200 bp were obtained by enzymatic shearing using FuPa reagent. The generated library was purified using Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, USA) and then quantified using Ion Taqman library quantitation kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, USA). The libraries were further diluted to 100 pM and amplified using emulsion PCR on Ion One Touch2 instruments (OT2) following manufacturer’s instruction. Finally, the template libraries were sequenced using the Ion S5 XL Semiconductor sequencer using Ion 540 Chip.

Bioinformatics analysis

The obtained RNA-seq data were analyzed using Ion Torrent Software Suite version 5.4. The Torrent Mapping Alignment Program (TMAP) was used for alignment by employing BWA-short, BWA-long, SSAHA, Super-maximal Exact Matching and Smith–Waterman algorithm for optimal mapping [14–18]. The samtools view –c –F 4 –L bed file, bam file was used to perform raw read counts. The number of expressed transcripts was confirmed after fragments per kilobase million (FPKM) normalization. Analysis for DEGs was performed using R/Bioconductor package DESeq2 with raw read counts from RNASeq and AmpliSeq wherein the regularized logarithm method was used for read count normalization. Exclusion criteria included genes with < 10 normalized read counts. The cut-off for log2 fold change was set to 2 based on which genes with positive values were considered upregulated while those with negative values were considered as downregulated. The LIMMA package was used to carry out the determination of genes [19].

All reagents used for whole transcriptome sequencing and analysis were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA, unless mentioned otherwise.

Discussion

Health and economic burden of T1DM highlights the importance of investigating the factors that lead to the onset and development of its associated comorbidities [20, 21]. One of the main elements that contribute to the pathogenesis of T1DM is the heterogeneity of the disease observed in patients suffering from a variety of comorbidities [22]. Our previous study which used blood samples solely has demonstrated the involvement of several challenges that can be resolved by non-invasive tools including saliva [4, 8]. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate potential genes that are associated with T1DM comorbidities using saliva samples. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to use saliva samples to identify genes in different groups of T1DM comorbidities in UAE population. In the present study, we identified almost 800 DEGs in saliva of T1DM patients with various comorbidities including hyperlipidemia (61), neuropathy (14), ketoacidosis (270), hypothyroidism (87) and PCOS (353) genes, respectively.

In agreement with the present study, previous studies in children with T1DM using saliva proteome have provided new insights for the pathogenesis of T1DM supporting the hypothesis that saliva can be considered a potential and non-invasive tool for T1DM research [23]. In addition, saliva can provide comprehensive details about the clinical parameters of T1DM patients. This is supported by the significant difference that was found between the number of differentially expressed proteins that were identified in the saliva of T1DM patients with good glycemic control compared to the poorly controlled patients [23]. Although genomic studies have found that the percentage of amplifiable human DNA is much lower in saliva compared to blood, the quality of DNA extracted from saliva is comparable to that extracted from blood [23]. In agreement with the previous studies, the outcomes of the present study have shown that the number of genes in T1DM patients with PCOS was comparable in saliva and blood of T1DM patients. However, in the ketoacidosis group, the number of genes was much higher in saliva compared to blood. This could be explained by the fact that the present study was conducted using transcriptomic analysis, whereas previous studies have focused more on genomic analysis [24].

One of the significant findings of the present study is that ten genes were upregulated in T1DM patients with hyperlipidemia, and this includes LYPD5, TCF15, PRKY, CYHR1, CCDC173, CHRNA5, MIAT, HBA2, RAD54L, and TSHZ3. Interestingly, previous reports have highlighted the association of some of these genes with differential pathological conditions. The following subsections will discuss the plausible interpretations of these findings in the context of the previous studies.

TCF15

The involvement of different signaling pathways in diabetic cardiomyopathy have demonstrated that TCF15 is associated with fatty acid uptake in cardiac myocytes [25]. This supports the present finding of upregulation of TCF15 in hyperlipidemia in T1DM and emphasizes the importance of investigating hyperlipidemia in this group of patients as it is a strong risk factor for diabetic cardiomyopathy. Moreover, the incidence of cardiovascular complications is greatly increased with significant mortality rates in T1DM, which has a direct correlation with dyslipidemia [26].

CYHR1

Although there was no prior evidence for differential expression of CYHR1 in T1DM, it is shown to be associated with a biologically active component of tea, offering a protective role in T2DM-related pathogenicity [27]. Given this previous finding, it is possible to suggest the same protective role in T1DM; however, more structured studies are warranted to support this suggestion.

CHRNA5

Overexpression of CHRNA5 is observed in mouse models of psoriasis, chronic pulmonary obstructive condition, and T2DM-related hepatocellular carcinoma [28–30]. It seems that CHRNA5 is associated with several inflammatory conditions, and this points towards its plausible role in T1DM-related inflammation.

MIAT

An important signaling pathway involving the MIAT gene transcript is found regulated by long-noncoding RNAs associated with renal tubular injury in response to hyperglycemia, which is one of the main features of T1DM [31]. Upregulation of MIAT in the present study could be indicative of T1DM-related hyperglycemia.

TSHZ3

An interesting correlation was reported between TSHZ3 dysregulation and hypertension, which is known to increase the risk factor of myocardial infarction in diabetes [32]. In agreement with this finding, our present results show upregulation of TSHZ3 in the hyperlipidemia groups, suggesting the involvement of this gene in cardiovascular complications in T1DM.

The second highest number of upregulated genes were found in T1DM with PCOS, which includes C4orf19, RSAD2, MEGF10, CMPK2, CHRNA5, TSHZ3, CCDC77, CLEC12A, and RTP4. The following subsections will discuss the plausible interpretations of these findings in the context of the previous studies.

RSAD2

Previous reports have revealed the role of RSAD2 in increasing the inflammation and subsequent degeneration of pancreatic β-cells in response to viral infection in these cells [33]. The role of inflammation is evident in the pathogenesis of PCOS, and a wide range of inflammatory markers have been identified in significant levels in PCOS patients [34].

CMPK2

Altered expression of the mitochondrial CMPK2 is involved in the enhanced production of reactive oxygen species and proinflammatory genes [35]. Interestingly, the upregulation of this gene in the saliva samples of the T1DM patients in the present study suggests a plausible correlation between chronic inflammation and T1DM.

CLEC12A

Previously it was found that the expression of CLEC12A decreased in autoimmune inflammatory responses in mouse models [36]. On the contrary, its expression was increased in T1DM-related PCOS in the present study and this could be attributed to the fine difference in pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases and PCOS.

RTP4

A recent study by Dai et al. has demonstrated that increased expression of RTP4 exacerbated HSV-1 infection by enhancing its binding to corneal epithelial cells of T2DM mice [37]. This study used transcriptomic analysis corneal epithelium to confirm a correlation between a hyperactive viral response and upregulation of RTP4 in T2DM mice [37]. Despite the fact that the present study was focused on T1DM, this can provide a possible explanation for upregulation of RTP4 in the PCOS group.

KCTD19 and FAM209B

Pertaining to neuropathy as comorbidities of T1DM, two genes were found to be upregulated in this group of T1DM patients: KCTD19 and FAM209B. KCTD19 is a member of the KCTD gene family, which is involved in neurodevelopment [38]. Its upregulation in the present study points towards a protective role in neurogenesis. However, further research is warranted to understand the exact mechanistic involvement of KCTD19 in T1DM comorbidities. FAM209B belongs to the FAM 3 gene family, which regulates hepatic gluconeogenesis and lipogenesis in T2DM [39]. The present study is the first to report the association between FAM209B and T1DM-related neuropathy.

NCKAP5 and ADAD2

Investigation of the upregulated genes in the ketoacidosis group revealed that NCKAP5 and ADAD2 were both upregulated. Previous studies have demonstrated that NCKAP5 was involved in the glycemic dysregulation and metabolism while ADAD2 showed a significant relationship with fasting plasma glucose levels [40, 41]. These findings corroborate with their upregulation in T1DM-associated ketoacidosis in the present study.

RPS6 and P2RY14

Another novel finding of the present study is the upregulation of RPS6 in T1DM associated with hypothyroidism. Previous studies have demonstrated the involvement of this gene in diabetes-induced renal hypertrophy [42]. The relationship between the latter and hypothyroidism is well-documented, and several studies have discussed this previously [43, 44]. Given the present finding, it can be speculated that the presence of hypothyroidism in T1DM patients may indicate the presence of other endocrine complications. Furthermore, P2RY14 plays an important role in maintaining glucose and lipid homeostasis and, interestingly, the stimulation of the receptors of this gene on adipocytes are known to decrease glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity [45]. This indicates similar possible outcomes in T1DM-related hypothyroidism.

Due to the uniqueness of the population cohort involved in the present study and the absence of a reference genome for this nationality, no relevant information was obtained for the following upregulated genes: HOXC9, LYPD5, PRKY, CCDC173, HBA2, RAD54L, C20orf144, ZFY, C4orf`9, MEGF10, RSAD2, and CCDC77. This further highlights the novelty and importance of the above-mentioned genes to open avenues for prospective future studies with the hope of identifying their therapeutic potential.

Limitations and future directions

Recently, saliva has received increasing attention as a patient-friendly, non-invasive alternative to blood sample collection. However, in the present study, we faced some challenges related to the quantity of RNA that can be extracted from the saliva samples and the stability of the samples prior to and after the processing protocols. In addition, the small size of the studied population impacted the number of T1DM patients in each group, which challenged the interpretation of some findings. Moreover, the cross-sectional nature of the study did not allow for a longitudinal and comprehensive assessment of T1DM comorbidities. Future studies would require collecting larger quantities of saliva samples per patient, followed by immediate processing and adequate storage to ensure higher quality and quantity of extracted RNA. Moreover, future studies will include further validation of the DEGs using different techniques, such as q-PCR.

Conclusion

The present findings demonstrated that the number of upregulated genes can be comparable between saliva and blood of T1DM patients with comorbidities, and in some groups, the number of upregulated genes was higher in the saliva compared to blood, emphasizing that saliva can be a potential non-invasive tool to identify DEGs in T1DM comorbidities. Upregulation of the identified genes that were associated with latter led to the prediction of mechanistic processes that may be involved in the development of these comorbidities. However, longitudinal investigation in a larger sample size is warranted.
