ROI-guided virtual narrow band imaging for laryngeal cancer screening
Authors: Jie-Lin Huang, Ji-Qing Zhu, Yu-Da Zhao, Li-Juan Li, Mei-Ling Wang, Xiu-Shan Luo, Wen-Ting Zhen, Hao-Jie Zhang, Jian-Hui Wang, Yong-Hui Zhang, Lin Liu, Yan Zhang, Ya Liu, Xiao-Guang Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWhite light laryngoscopy is widely available but can miss subtle vascular changes associated with early laryngeal neoplasia. We developed a region-of-interest (ROI)-guided cycle-consistent generative adversarial network (CycleGAN) that translates white light imaging (WLI) images into virtual narrow band imaging (NBI) images, while emphasizing expert-annotated lesion regions. The framework was trained and evaluated on 775 weakly paired WLI-NBI image pairs and assessed with technical image quality metrics, subjective reader scoring, a visual Turing test, and a multi-center reader study involving 12 otolaryngologists from nine institutions. The model preserved lesion-region structure better than global background structure and improved overall reader accuracy compared with WLI alone (81.5% versus 73.4%). Assistance mainly increased sensitivity for junior readers and specificity for senior readers. These findings support virtual NBI as a potential software-based adjunct where optical NBI is unavailable, while requiring further external and small-lesion validation.Subject areas: health sciences, medicine, medical tests, medical imaging, applied sciences, omputer science, computing methodology, artificial intelligence, artificial intelligence applications

Introduction

Laryngeal cancer is among the most common head and neck malignancies.1 Early detection of precursor lesions and carcinoma in situ (CIS) is critical as early-stage intervention greatly increases the 5-year survival rate and preserves laryngeal function.2 However, conventional white light imaging (WLI), the standard screening tool in primary care, often lacks sufficient contrast to distinguish superficial mucosal changes and subtle neo-angiogenesis. Therefore, early-stage lesions often go unnoticed or are misreported as benign inflammation during routine examinations, resulting in a delay in treatment.

To overcome the limitations of WLI, narrow band imaging (NBI), which is a powerful optical enhancement technology, was introduced. Through specific wavelengths of 415 and 540 nm, which hemoglobin absorbs, NBI highlights the intrapapillary capillary loops (IPCLs), indicating early malignancy.3,4,5 Despite its diagnostic superiority, NBI systems are expensive and require proprietary hardware, which restrict their availability primarily to tertiary medical centers.6 In resource-limited settings and primary ENT clinics, WLI remains the only available modality. Therefore, a software-based solution capable of converting standard WLI images into high-fidelity “virtual NBI” (Vir-NBI) images would democratize access to advanced diagnostics.

Recent advances in generative artificial intelligence (AI) have enabled virtual chromoendoscopy and other forms of medical image synthesis. However, most prior WLI-to-NBI studies have relied on unpaired image-translation frameworks that mainly optimize global style transfer, rather than lesion-level structural preservation.7,8 In laryngeal imaging, this limitation is clinically important because diagnostic interpretation depends heavily on subtle vascular morphology and lesion boundaries. A model that produces visually plausible images but alters intrapapillary capillary loop patterns or lesion contours could, therefore, mislead clinical assessment.9,10 In addition, most previous studies have focused on technical image quality metrics, with limited evidence that synthetic images improve diagnostic performance for real readers.11,12,13

To address these gaps, we developed a region-of-interest (ROI)-guided Vir-NBI framework trained on a weakly paired WLI-NBI dataset. Rather than claiming absolute safety, our design aims to reduce the risk of structure distortion in clinically relevant lesion regions by imposing a stronger reconstruction constraint within expert-annotated ROIs. We then evaluated the framework through a multi-center reader study to determine whether this lesion-aware image synthesis strategy was associated with improved diagnostic performance across different levels of clinical experience (Figure 1).

Results

Baseline characteristics and dataset composition

Following the application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 775 paired WLI-NBI images were included in the study. The group consisted of 326 pairs of malignant/precancerous lesions and 449 benign/normal tissues. The subjects were divided into a training set (n = 543), a validation set (n = 78), and an independent test set (n = 154). Critically, this partitioning was performed strictly at the patient level to prevent data leakage and ensure that the test set serves as a robust proxy for internal validation. The clinical and pathological characteristics at baseline were well matched in the training and test sets (Table 1).

Technical validation of the Vir-NBI system

The independent test dataset was able to accurately assess the proposed AI system. Qualitative samples of the generated images are shown in Figure 2. The Vir-NBI images managed to suppress the red tint of the background mucosa while enhancing the contrast of the microvascular structures (IPCLs). These images have high visual similarity with real NBI.

Qualitative comparison of image generation across laryngeal lesion types

Global structural similarity index measure (SSIM) was 0.811 ± 0.049, whereas ROI-SSIM within lesion regions was 0.843 ± 0.042, suggesting better structural fidelity in clinically relevant areas. The reconstruction peak signal to noise ratio (PSNR) was 34.80 dB and reconstruction SSIM was 0.949 ± 0.010. In addition, lesion-region reconstruction error was lower than that of the baseline model, with a 21.9% reduction in mean error (Figures 3A and 3B). The Fréchet inception distance (FID) was 129.22. Together, these findings suggest that the ROI-guided framework improved structural fidelity within lesion regions relative to the baseline model; however, these technical metrics should be interpreted as indirect evidence of structure preservation, rather than definitive proof of clinical safety.

Subjective image quality and visual Turing test

Twelve otolaryngologists assessed the generated images subjectively (Table 2). The mean overall realism score for the generated Vir-NBI images was 4.34 ± 0.72 on a 5-point scale. Diagnostic performance

Vir-NBI assistance was associated with improved diagnostic performance overall, with mean accuracy increasing from 73.4% (95% CI: 67.7%–78.5%) under WLI alone to 81.5% (95% CI: 76.7%–85.7%) under WLI plus Vir-NBI, and AUC increasing from 0.756 (95% CI: 0.695–0.814) to 0.836 (95% CI: 0.785–0.882) (Table 3; Figure 4). In repeated-measures analyses accounting for clustering by reader and case, Vir-NBI was associated with an absolute 8.1-percentage point increase in overall accuracy (95% CI: 6.5–9.7; p < 0.001), a 7.4-percentage point increase in sensitivity (95% CI: 3.5–11.2; p < 0.001), and a 9.2-percentage point increase in specificity (95% CI: 4.9–13.4; p < 0.001).

The magnitude of benefit differed by the readers’ experience. Among junior readers, the principal gain was in sensitivity, which increased from 64.7% (95% CI: 55.2%–74.1%) to 75.8% (95% CI: 66.7%–84.2%), with a fixed-effects estimate of an 11.1-percentage point increase (95% CI: 7.6–14.6; p < 0.001). Accuracy in junior readers also improved from 68.3% (95% CI: 61.7%–74.9%) to 78.5% (95% CI: 72.7%–84.4%), and AUC increased from 0.712 (95% CI: 0.634–0.790) to 0.804 (95% CI 0.736–0.861). Among senior readers, the most prominent gain was in specificity, which increased from 66.7% (95% CI: 52.9%–80.1%) to 76.2% (95% CI: 65.7%–86.4%), corresponding to a fixed-effects estimate of a 9.6-percentage point increase (95% CI: 1.8–17.4; p = 0.016). Senior readers’ sensitivity increased more modestly from 86.4% (95% CI: 75.4%–94.3%) to 90.0% (95% CI: 83.4%–95.8%), and this change was not statistically significant in the repeated-measures analysis (absolute increase: 3.6 percentage points; 95% CI: −2.1 to 9.4; p = 0.218). These findings suggest that Vir-NBI may support different but clinically relevant decision-making needs across levels of expertise.

Safety-oriented misclassification analysis

To provide a more safety-oriented assessment beyond aggregate performance metrics, we compared reader-case pairs that improved or worsened after Vir-NBI assistance. Overall, Vir-NBI corrected substantially more false-negative decisions than it introduced (63 corrected vs. 10 introduced; exact McNemar p < 0.001), and it also corrected more false-positive decisions than it introduced (52 corrected vs. 8 introduced; exact McNemar p < 0.001) (Figure 5). This pattern was especially prominent among junior readers for false negatives (44 corrected vs. 4 introduced; p < 0.001), whereas among senior readers, the clearest gain was in false-positive reduction (28 corrected vs. 5 introduced; p < 0.001). These results support the interpretation that Vir-NBI improved the diagnostic performance primarily by reducing clinically relevant error types, rather than by merely shifting decision thresholds in a nonspecific manner. Lesion-type subgroup analysis

To further characterize where Vir-NBI assistance provided the greatest clinical benefit, we performed a lesion-type subgroup analysis using the case-level pathological diagnoses of the 100-case diagnostic cohort. Because each pathology subgroup contained a single outcome class, subgroup analyses focused on pathology-specific sensitivity for malignant subgroups and pathology-specific specificity for benign subgroups, rather than AUC. Among malignant lesions, Vir-NBI improved sensitivity for both severe dysplasia and laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC) in junior and senior readers. In severe dysplasia, sensitivity increased from 83.3% to 95.2% in junior readers (exact McNemar p = 0.002) and from 73.8% to 86.9% in senior readers (p < 0.001). For LSCC, sensitivity increased from 68.6% to 75.6% in junior readers (p = 0.013) and from 62.8% to 70.5% in senior readers (p = 0.017). These results indicate that Vir-NBI was associated with sensitivity gains in both experience groups, although baseline performance differed across subgroups.

Among benign lesions, the most notable gains were seen in lesion types that are commonly difficult to distinguish from early malignancy under WLI. In junior readers, specificity improved from 54.6% to 66.7% for polyps (p < 0.001), from 75.6% to 88.5% for hyperplasia (p = 0.002), and from 58.3% to 75.0% for mild dysplasia (p = 0.031). In senior readers, baseline specificity was generally higher, and the direction of change was similar, but the absolute gains were smaller and did not consistently reach statistical significance, likely reflecting both ceiling effects and limited sample size in several pathology subgroups. These subgroup findings support the interpretation that Vir-NBI assistance was associated with different but complementary patterns of benefit across lesion categories and reader experience levels, rather than a uniform advantage of one reader group over the other.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated a clinically oriented deep learning framework to transform the standard WLI laryngoscopic images into high-fidelity Vir-NBI. Through the application of unique weakly paired dataset strategy and ROI-driven attention, our model exhibited significantly high anatomical fidelity in clinically relevant lesions. In our multi-center reader study, Vir-NBI assistance addressed two different but complementary clinical challenges. Inexperienced physicians showed a significant increase in sensitivity, helping them overcome the diagnostic gap, while experts were able to improve specificity, thereby offering an all-inclusive solution for early laryngeal cancer screening.

Recent developments in medical image synthesis, notably gastric Vir-NBI generation, have indeed demonstrated the capability to perform style transfer. Yet, these works have mostly remained limited to technical verification using metrics such as SSIM and FID.14,15 Nonetheless, pixel-level fidelity does not necessarily equal diagnostic value. The all-important question is whether these images, which are visually realistic, can in fact assist in clinical decision-making. Our study overcomes this translational gap, showing that ROI-driven synthesis can lead to real, role-specific clinical effects.

For junior readers, the main benefit was improved sensitivity. This pattern is clinically relevant because subtle vascular abnormalities are difficult to detect under WLI alone and typically require substantial experience in NBI interpretation.16,17,18 By enhancing lesion contrast and microvascular visibility, Vir-NBI may help reduce under-recognition of early malignant change in less experienced readers.

For senior readers, the principal benefit was improved specificity rather than a large additional gain in sensitivity. This finding suggests that enhanced vascular contrast may also support the distinction between early malignant lesions and benign mimics in cases that are already detectable under WLI, thereby potentially reducing unnecessary biopsy or overtreatment.19

Our technical strategy was designed to address a recognized limitation of unpaired image translation: visually plausible style transfer, by itself, does not guarantee preservation of diagnostically important local structure.9,20,21 Within this clinically constrained setting, standard cycle-consistent generative adversarial network (CycleGAN) was an appropriate baseline because exact pixel-level WLI-NBI pairing is difficult in the larynx owing to respiration, swallowing, scope distance, and viewing-angle changes. We, therefore, combined a weakly paired training strategy with ROI-weighted loss and consensus lesion masks to place stronger reconstruction constraints on clinically relevant regions. Higher ROI-SSIM than global SSIM, lower lesion-region reconstruction error, favorable reader ratings for vascular visibility and mucosal detail, and the safety-oriented misclassification analysis all support improved structural fidelity within lesion regions. The lack of a significant monotonic correlation between case-level ROI-SSIM and expert perceptual scores (Table S2) further suggests that technical ROI metrics and reader-based assessments should be interpreted as complementary rather than interchangeable evidence. At the same time, these metrics remain indirect, and the ROI-guided mechanism should be regarded as a safety-oriented structural constraint rather than a guaranteed safeguard against hallucination-related error.

From a translational perspective, this approach may be particularly relevant in settings where optical NBI hardware is unavailable. Because many primary hospitals still rely on standard WLI, a software-based enhancement strategy could expand access to vascular-pattern information without requiring dedicated processors.22 Broader benchmarking against other virtual chromoendoscopy frameworks remains an important direction for future work, and a comparative positioning summary is provided in Table S1.

We developed an ROI-guided Vir-NBI framework for laryngeal imaging and found that it was associated with improved reader performance in a multi-center reader study, with larger gains in sensitivity among junior readers and in specificity among senior readers. The ROI-guided design also improved lesion-region structural fidelity relative to the baseline model. These findings support the potential of Vir-NBI as an assistive image-enhancement approach in settings where optical NBI is not readily available. However, current evidence should be interpreted with caution because external image-based validation, safety-oriented assessment, and evaluation in very small lesions remain needed before broader clinical adoption.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the image dataset was derived from a single tertiary center; although the reader study included otolaryngologists from nine institutions, this reader-level diversity does not replace external image-based validation. Second, lesions occupying less than 30% of the image field were excluded to support training stability; so, performance in very small or extremely subtle lesions requires additional testing. Third, the weakly paired design reflects real laryngeal endoscopy conditions but cannot provide exact pixel-level WLI-NBI alignment. Fourth, sex and age distributions are reported in Table 1, but sex- or gender-specific effect modification was not a prespecified analysis.

Experimental model and study participant details

Clinical image dataset and case selection

This retrospective study was conducted at the Cancer Hospital and Institute of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. Paired endoscopic images were collected from patients undergoing laryngoscopy between January 2023 and December 2025. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Cancer Center/Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College (Approval No. 22/454-3656), and the requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB because of the retrospective study design. Patient age and sex distributions are reported in Table 1.

Strict quality control criteria were followed to build a high-quality dataset. The inclusion criteria were: (1) clear visualization of IPCL with sufficient vascular contrast, free from motion blur or equipment artifacts; (2) uniform and moderate illumination, avoiding overexposure or underexposure that could obscure vascular patterns; (3) the lesion area occupying 30% of the image field to ensure sufficient diagnostic information; and (4) availability of a definitive histopathological diagnosis as the gold standard.

The exclusion criteria included: (1) technical artifacts like heavy blurring, error of focus, and device-related noise; (2) significant biological interference, defined as hemorrhage covering 30% or secretions/necrotic tissue covering 20% of the field of view; (3) inappropriate imaging parameters (like lighting) causing the IPCL to be poorly contrasted; and (4) indeterminate pathological diagnosis or ambiguous cases.

The final dataset was partitioned into a training set, a validation set, and an independent test set. Crucially, partitioning was performed at the patient level to prevent data leakage and ensure rigorous evaluation.

Clinical trial registration: not applicable; this work was not a prospective clinical trial.

Method details

Weakly paired WLI-NBI dataset construction and ROI annotation

Because exact pixel-level alignment between WLI and NBI images is clinically difficult to achieve in the larynx owing to physiological motion, we constructed a weakly paired dataset by selecting WLI and NBI images from the same lesion with the closest available view, angle, and magnification. Lesion ROIs were manually delineated on the training images using LabelMe by three senior otolaryngologists, each with more than 10 years of experience in NBI interpretation and an annual volume exceeding 1,500 endoscopic examinations. The three experts annotated the lesions independently, and discrepant annotations were resolved by consensus review before model training. The final consensus masks were then used to guide lesion-aware training and to emphasize structural preservation within diagnostically relevant regions.

ROI-guided CycleGAN architecture and training

We developed a deep learning framework based on Cycle-Consistent Generative Adversarial Network (CycleGAN) to translate WLI images into Vir-NBI while prioritizing preservation of lesion-region structure. To benchmark the performance of our approach, we used the standard CycleGAN architecture as the baseline model. The ROI-guided framework addresses a clinically relevant limitation of standard generative models: global image-level optimization can dilute subtle lesion features or create visually plausible artifacts outside diagnostically relevant regions. We therefore incorporated an ROI-guided attention mechanism based on expert-annotated lesion masks to increase the training weight assigned to clinically relevant areas. The training objective combined visual realism with lesion-aware structural preservation, including adversarial loss, cycle-consistency loss, identity loss, and ROI-weighted reconstruction loss.

Objective image-quality assessment

Generation quality was evaluated quantitatively on the independent test dataset using complementary technical metrics. Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) was used to assess distribution-level similarity between Vir-NBI and real NBI images. Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) was measured globally and within expert-defined lesion ROIs to assess structural fidelity during domain translation. Cycle-reconstruction SSIM and PSNR between the original WLI and reconstructed WLI were used to assess mapping stability. These technical metrics provide indirect evidence of image fidelity and were interpreted together with reader quality scoring and diagnostic-performance analyses.

Reader study design

To assess clinical utility, we conducted a multi-center reader study involving 12 otolaryngologists from 9 institutions, including 6 junior readers with less than 5 years of experience and 6 senior readers with more than 15 years of experience. Clinical validation was organized into three experiments using separate datasets. For the diagnostic experiment, readers evaluated 100 test cases in a two-session crossover design with a prespecified 2-week washout period. Case order was randomized independently for each reader and for each session, and readers were blinded to the histopathological diagnosis during all assessments. In Session A, cases were interpreted using WLI alone. In Session B, the same cases were interpreted using WLI together with Vir-NBI assistance. Because the assisted session explicitly displayed both WLI and Vir-NBI images, blinding to assistance condition was not possible by design. For each case, readers provided a binary diagnosis and a confidence score on a 5-point scale, with histopathology used as the reference standard. Details of the simulation-based power analysis are provided in Methods S1.

Subjective image-quality evaluation and visual turing test

For subjective image-quality evaluation, readers scored 60 independent cases on vascular visibility, mucosal detail, overall realism, and artifact-free appearance using a 5-point scale. Vascular visibility and mucosal detail were treated as clinically relevant surrogates of IPCL interpretability and lesion-structure readability. In a separate visual Turing test, readers classified 100 randomized unpaired images, including 50 authentic NBI images and 50 Vir-NBI images, as either real or AI-generated. In the visual Turing test, readers were necessarily blinded to image authenticity by the task design.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All quantitative image-quality metrics are reported as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise specified. For the reader study, diagnostic performance was summarized overall and by reader experience level for WLI alone and WLI plus Vir-NBI assistance. To account for the crossed multi-reader, multi-case repeated-measures structure of the diagnostic dataset, we fitted linear probability models with reader fixed effects, case fixed effects, and two-way cluster-robust standard errors by reader and case. These models were used to estimate the absolute effect of Vir-NBI assistance on accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. In addition, 95% confidence intervals for accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC were obtained using two-way cluster bootstrap resampling over readers and cases. Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. A simulation-based power analysis based on the observed crossed reader-case structure was performed to evaluate whether the design with 100 test cases interpreted by 12 readers would provide adequate power to detect a clinically meaningful 10-percentage-point improvement in overall accuracy.

For lesion-type subgroup analyses, pathology-specific sensitivity or specificity was compared between WLI alone and WLI plus Vir-NBI using exact McNemar tests on paired reader-case decisions. For exploratory technical-clinical linkage analysis, Spearman correlation was used to assess associations between case-level ROI-SSIM and mean expert quality scores because expert scores were ordinal Likert ratings.

Data Availability Statement

Data

The clinical image datasets used in this study are not publicly available due to patient privacy regulations but are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request and institutional approval.

Code

The source code for the ROI-guided CycleGAN framework, along with the inference scripts and pretrained weights used in this study, has been deposited on GitHub (https://github.com/lin521045/roi-guided-cyclegan-larynx) and archived in Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20094409). It is publicly available as of the date of publication.

Additional information

Any additional information required to reanalyze the data reported in this paper is available from the lead contact upon request.
