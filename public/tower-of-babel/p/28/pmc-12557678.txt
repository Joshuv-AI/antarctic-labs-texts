Feasibility of intraoperative Doppler ultrasound for intersegmental plane identification in pulmonary segmentectomy: a retrospective preliminary validation study
Authors: Jialiang Liu, Yuhui Gong, Lingyan Jiang, Giulio Maurizi, Junji Ichinose, Stefano Bongiolatti, Shiyu Shen, Haitao Ma, Haitao Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe intraoperative identification of intersegmental planes remains a technical challenge in pulmonary segmentectomy. Although indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence imaging provides reliable visualization, its clinical adoption is limited by transient effects and high costs. This observational study aimed to evaluate the concordance between Doppler ultrasound and ICG fluorescence in the demarcation of intersegmental planes.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, we analyzed 12 consecutive patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic segmentectomy at The Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (May 2020 to January 2021). Intraoperative Doppler ultrasound was applied to detect intersegmental planes via the assessment of the arterial blood flow differences, with subsequent ICG fluorescence imaging serving as the reference standard. All procedures were performed by senior thoracic surgeons with >10 years of experience in video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) segmentectomy. Doppler ultrasound was performed using preset vascular mode with optimized parameters (gain 15–20%, frequency 5.1 MHz, depth 0–1 cm). Inter- and intra-observer variability were not assessed, which represents a methodological limitation.ResultsThe cohort comprised 12 patients (8 males and 4 females; mean age 60.42±13.27 years). A close concordance was observed between the Doppler-identified intersegmental planes and the ICG fluorescence delineation across the majority of cases (11 out of 12, corresponding to a 91.7% concordance rate). Moreover, no procedural complications associated with either modality were noted.ConclusionsDoppler ultrasound demonstrated the potential to a potentially cost-saving and dye-free alternative to ICG fluorescence for intersegmental plane identification. However, these preliminary findings should be validated in larger prospective cohorts to address limitations of single-center design, small sample size, and operator-dependent ultrasound technique.Keywords: Indocyanine green (ICG), pulmonary segmentectomy, intraoperative Doppler ultrasound, observational study, case series

Highlight box

Key findings

• With reference to indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence, intraoperative Doppler ultrasound delineated intersegmental planes in 11 out of 12 patients (91.7%), with a mean boundary difference of 0.62±0.28 cm; importantly, no modality-related complications occurred.

What is known and what is new?

• ICG fluorescence is widely used in clinical practice for lung segment boundary mapping. However, it is constrained by factors such as transient signal duration, cost considerations, and dye-related concerns. Doppler ultrasound is extensively used for real-time vascular mapping in hepatic and renal surgical procedures.

• This groundbreaking study is the first to validate the use of Doppler ultrasound against ICG in thoracoscopic pulmonary segmentectomy. Doppler ultrasound demonstrated a high degree of concordance with ICG, offering continuous, repeatable visualization without the need for contrast agents.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Doppler ultrasound may be a cost-effective and safe alternative to ICG fluorescence, particularly in resource-constrained settings. It allows surgeons to iteratively assess and redefine segmental boundaries with precision.

• Larger, multicenter trials are needed to further confirm the efficacy of Doppler ultrasound, refine scanning protocols, and achieve technical advancements (such as contrast-enhanced ultrasound and artificial-driven noise reduction) to facilitate its widespread clinical adoption and integration.

Introduction

For stage I non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), pulmonary segmentectomy has demonstrated comparable long-term survival to that of lobectomy while providing superior preservation of pulmonary function and adequate margins (≥2 cm or ≥ tumor diameter) (1). Compared to wedge resection, pulmonary segmentectomy also enables a more adequate segmental, hilar and mediastinal lymph node dissection, reducing recurrence risk and improving staging accuracy.

A critical technical challenge in thoracoscopic pulmonary segmentectomy remains the precise identification of intersegmental planes. Currently used methods, such as intravenous indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence imaging, provide rapid and reproducible boundaries but are limited by short duration (typically <2 minutes), high equipment costs, and dye diffusion artifacts. Doppler ultrasound, widely used in hepatic and renal surgeries for real-time vascular mapping, offers potential advantages in cost-effectiveness, repeatability (2,3), and stable visualization. However, its feasibility in thoracic surgery—particularly for intersegmental plane detection—remains underexplored.

This retrospective cohort study aimed to evaluate the consistency and practicality of intraoperative Doppler ultrasound in identifying intersegmental planes during pulmonary segmentectomy, with ICG fluorescence imaging serving as the reference standard. We present this article in accordance with the AME Case Series and STROBE reporting checklists (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1549/rc).

Methods

This single-center retrospective cohort study included 12 patients who underwent thoracoscopic pulmonary segmentectomy between May 2020 and January 2021. Eligible participants have early-stage NSCLC (stage IA, tumor diameter ≤2 cm) confirmed by postoperative pathology, met the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) criteria for pulmonary segmentectomy, and underwent preoperative three-dimensional CT reconstruction (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine data) for surgical planning. Exclusion criteria included prior lung surgery, iodine allergy (contraindication to ICG), and incomplete lung collapse during surgery. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. All patients provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (No. 241004).

Under general anesthesia, one-lung ventilation was achieved with a double-lumen endotracheal tube. Then, make a 1 cm incision from the seventh or eighth intercostal space on one side of the chest, at the axillary midline, and insert a trocar to serve as an observation port. Next, create a 3 cm incision from the fourth or fifth intercostal space on the axillary midline for the operating port. After complete lung collapse, the target segment’s arteries, veins, and bronchi were dissected and divided to ensure adequate hemodynamic contrast. No significant signal loss was observed under these conditions. Intraoperative Doppler ultrasound was conducted with an S8 portable digital color Doppler ultrasound system (SonoScape, Shenzhen, China) with an LAP7 probe (The LAP7 convex array laparoscopic probe by SonoScape, China, features a center frequency of 7.5 MHz and a scanning width of 38 mm), which was applied to the lung surface in color Doppler mode to identify the intersegmental plane, with the blood flow signals between the preserved segment (vascular-rich area) and the target segment (vascular-defect area) being contrasted. The Doppler ultrasound probe was applied to the completely deflated lung, and ICG was also injected under conditions where the lung was kept deflated. Doppler ultrasound was performed in vascular mode using optimized parameters (gain 15–20%, frequency 5.1 MHz, depth 0–1 cm). The probe was gently applied to the deflated lung surface with minimal pressure. The boundary was marked with an electric hook. For validation, 25 mg of ICG dissolved in 10 mL of sterile water was administered intravenously (0.15 mL/kg). The near-infrared fluorescence mode of the thoracoscope was activated 10–15 seconds after the injection to visualize the intersegmental plane. Consistency between Doppler and ICG boundaries was considered to be a distance difference <1 cm. The intersegmental plane was ultimately divided along the fluorescent boundary with a linear stapler or ultrasonic scalpel, with intraoperative frozen sections confirming surgical margins as per the NCCN guidelines (≥2 cm or ≥ tumor diameter) (1).

Primary outcomes included the consistency of intersegmental plane identification between Doppler ultrasound and ICG fluorescence. Secondary outcomes comprised the time to boundary appearance (from ICG injection to clear visualization) and boundary duration (from clear visualization to fluorescence diffusion). The duration of fluorescence visualization was recorded only for the clear phase, as this was the period of reliable boundary delineation.

Due to the small sample size, subgroup analyses (e.g., complex vs. simple segmentectomies, upper vs. lower lobes) could not be performed, which limits the granularity of our findings.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and data recording was performed via SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation.

Intraoperative Doppler ultrasound successfully identified intersegmental planes in 11 of 12 cases (91.7%), with boundaries showing strong agreement to those of ICG fluorescence imaging (mean boundary distance difference: 0.62±0.28 cm). In the case of disagreement [left upper lingular segment resection (LS4+5)], Doppler ultrasound failed to provide clear visualization due to the thin lung tissue and cardiac motion artifacts, necessitating reliance on ICG fluorescence.

Discussion

The precise identification of intersegmental planes continues to be a significant challenge in thoracoscopic pulmonary segmentectomy and has implications for both oncological efficacy and functional preservation (6,7). This study demonstrated that intraoperative Doppler ultrasound can achieve strong agreement with ICG fluorescence imaging (mean boundary difference: 0.62±0.28 cm; 11/12 cases), which is the current gold standard, while offering distinct advantages in cost-effectiveness, real-time repeatability, and stable visualization. However, its performance is limited by anatomical factors of lung tissue and the constraints of ultrasound technology, which restrict its broader clinical applications. The operator-dependency of Doppler ultrasound remains a limitation. Standardized scanning protocols and reproducibility assessments will be necessary in future prospective studies.

Comparative advantages over ICG fluorescence

ICG fluorescence imaging, despite its rapid boundary visualization (12.67±2.06 seconds’ postinjection), suffers from transient efficacy (67.83±11.23 seconds) and practical limitations. The brief duration of fluorescence boundaries, caused by rapid dye diffusion and surgical field contamination from ICG-containing blood exudate, often necessitates repeated injections. However, these are impractical due to the prolonged metabolic clearance, which can take 20 to 30 minutes. Furthermore, fluorescence thoracoscopy systems are prohibitively expensive for many institutions, particularly in resource-limited settings. In contrast, Doppler ultrasound circumvents these issues by providing continuous, real-time visualization, without being dependent on dye. Its repeatability allows surgeons to refine boundary markings iteratively, a critical advantage in complex anatomies. These findings align with prior studies highlighting Doppler’s utility in hepatic and renal surgeries (8-11), which reported that real-time vascular mapping enhances precision. Doppler ultrasound may represent a cost-saving alternative in the long term, particularly in institutions with existing Doppler infrastructure, as it does not require consumables like contrast agents. However, the initial capital investment and operational costs should be considered.

Principle of action and technical limitations

Doppler ultrasound identifies intersegmental planes by detecting hemodynamic contrasts: the ligation of target segment arteries creates vascular-defect zones, while preserved segments exhibit robust blood flow signals. This principle, rooted in the Doppler effect’s sensitivity to moving scatterers (e.g., erythrocytes), has been validated in the localization of solid pulmonary nodules (12-14). However, lung-specific challenges, such as residual alveolar gas and thin parenchyma, attenuate ultrasound signals. The applicability of Doppler ultrasound may be limited in certain anatomical locations, particularly when thin lung parenchyma or cardiac motion artifacts are present. In such cases, the technique may not provide clear delineation of the intersegmental plane, as observed in the lingular segment resection (LS4+5). Further studies are needed to identify specific anatomical conditions that influence Doppler ultrasound performance. Similar limitations have been reported in thoracic ultrasound studies, in which the air-tissue interfaces and cardiac interference were found to degrade image clarity. Moreover, in cases where venous division is not feasible, Doppler ultrasound may be limited and cannot fully replace ICG fluorescence. Optimizing ultrasound parameters (e.g., adjusting gain, frequency) partially mitigated noise but could not eliminate subjectivity in boundary interpretation, as surgeons rely on screen-based flow-echo contrasts rather than direct visual cues. Early recognition of a poor Doppler signal should prompt immediate conversion to ICG fluorescence as a rescue strategy, ensuring safe and accurate boundary identification.

Clinical implications and future directions

Beyond intersegmental plane identification, Doppler ultrasound may have potential applications in mapping pulmonary vasculature, identifying lymph nodes, and improving surgical precision in other thoracic procedures (15-18). Doppler ultrasound offers real-time, continuous feedback and does not require contrast agents, making it an attractive alternative, especially in settings where ICG fluorescence is not feasible or cost-effective. It provides a safer and more flexible option for surgeons, particularly in patients with renal impairment or iodine allergies. However, its success is contingent upon operator expertise and favorable anatomical conditions (e.g., thick parenchyma and minimal cardiac interference). A potential limitation of this study is the sequential, non-blinded procedure, which may introduce observer bias. Future studies could benefit from a randomized sequence or independent, blinded review of the recordings to address this concern. Further innovations, such as contrast-enhanced ultrasound with microbubble agents, could amplify blood flow signals in challenging regions (19). Moreover, the integration of artificial intelligence for noise reduction and automated boundary detection may decrease subjectivity (20,21).

Study limitations

This study has several limitations which should be acknowledged. First, the small cohort (n=12) and single-center design limit the generalizability of the findings. Due to the small sample size, we were unable to perform subgroup analyses or provide confidence intervals/interobserver agreement statistics. These analyses will be critical in future multicenter prospective studies to validate our findings. Second, the retrospective nature of the analysis precluded the use of standardized protocols for ultrasound parameter optimization. Third, while the 1 cm consistency threshold is pragmatic, the study lacks histological validation of the intersegmental planes identified by Doppler ultrasound and ICG fluorescence, which should be addressed in future studies. Third, long-term outcomes (e.g., recurrence rates and pulmonary function) were not assessed, leaving the clinical impact of boundary accuracy unresolved. Forth, patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) were not included in this study. Since emphysematous changes and alveolar gas retention may interfere with Doppler signal acquisition, the applicability of this technique in such patients remains uncertain and requires further investigation. Finally, the absence of randomized direct comparisons between Doppler and ICG, as well as against alternative intersegmental plane identification techniques like inflation/deflation, hinders broader methodological conclusions, despite our primary aim being the validation of Doppler against the clinical gold standard.

Conclusions

Intraoperative Doppler ultrasound serves as a promising adjunct for identifying intersegmental planes, offering practical advantages over ICG fluorescence. Although technical improvements are necessary for mitigating signal attenuation in thin lung areas and reducing operator dependency, its affordability and real-time capabilities make it a valuable tool for enhancing precision in pulmonary segmentectomy.
