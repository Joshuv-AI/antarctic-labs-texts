Upcycling Coir Fiber into Polydopamine-Enabled Adsorbents for Efficient Cu(II)/Cd(II) Removal and Effluent Safety
Authors: Shangpeng Liu, Wenji Wang, Dadong Wang, Jian Lu, Yibo Li, Mingchen Lv, Min Sun, Zhen Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heavy metal pollution remains a global environmental challenge, calling for sustainable and low-cost sorbents. Here, we upcycle coir fiber into a bioinspired adsorbent by depositing a polydopamine (PDA) coating (PDA/Coir fiber) for efficient Cu(II) and Cd(II) removal from water while improving effluent safety. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Fourier transform infrared (FTIR), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) confirm successful PDA functionalization and associated structural changes. Compared with pristine coir fiber, PDA/Coir fiber shows 6.84-fold and 12.86-fold higher adsorption capacities for Cu(II) and Cd(II), respectively. Adsorption is well described by the Langmuir isotherm, indicating monolayer-dominated binding. Kinetic analysis shows that the adsorption of both ions follows the pseudo-second-order model. The fiber also exhibits good reusability over four adsorption–desorption cycles. Importantly, cytotoxicity assays of the treated solutions show substantially reduced biotoxicity after metal removal. Overall, PDA/Coir fiber offers a sustainable and low-cost platform for heavy metal removal by integrating efficient adsorption capabilities and safety implications.

1. Introduction

Heavy metal pollution from industrial activities, mining, and urban runoff presents a significant and multifaceted environmental and health risk [1,2,3,4]. These noxious contaminants, including both nonessential (e.g., Cd2+) and essential ions (e.g., Cu2+), can cause severe ecological and health impacts when introduced into aquatic ecosystems and drinking water sources [5,6,7,8]. A major concern is their persistence, which results in their accumulation in ecosystems and food chains [9,10]. Through bioaccumulation, heavy metals accumulate in organisms over time, posing severe health threats to both humans and wildlife [11,12]. Such exposure can lead to debilitating health issues, ranging from neurological damage to organ failure and certain types of cancer [13,14]. Thus, there is a pressing need for effective, cost-efficient, and environmentally friendly strategies to combat heavy metal pollution, driving the development of innovative materials and techniques.

Adsorption technology is widely regarded as a practical option for heavy metal removal because of its high efficiency, high adsorption capacity, and simple process [15]. Conventional adsorbents typically include carbon-based materials (such as activated carbon) [16], inorganic materials (such as zeolites) [17], and polymeric materials (such as ion-exchange resins) [18]. However, the preparation and regeneration of these materials are often costly and energy-intensive. Advanced porous materials and hybrid nanomaterials, such as metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) [19], covalent organic frameworks (COFs) [20], nanofibers [21], graphene-based aerogels [22], and MXene-based composites [23], can provide high capacity and selectivity. However, they often require complex synthesis, and it is difficult for these materials to translate to industrial applicability in the short term. In addition, the biosafety of the adsorbent after heavy metal adsorption was rarely evaluated in previous work, but is essential for application, particularly when used in settings where human or environmental exposure is possible (Table 1). Accordingly, low-cost and scalable biomass fibers for efficient and safe heavy metal removal are of growing interest.

Coir fiber, derived from coconut husks, is an abundant, renewable, and natural material celebrated for its versatility and eco-friendly attributes [28]. Therefore, coir fiber is used in various fields, including the production of ropes, horticultural products, mats, filtration media, and reinforcing materials [29,30]. However, pristine coir fiber shows limited binding affinity for heavy metal removal [31,32], which restricts its performance in handling heavily contaminated wastewater and complex conditions [33,34]. In recent years, polydopamine (PDA)-based composites have gained attention in heavy metal removal [35,36]. PDA coating provides a facile and effective technique, and introduces catechol ligands for metal coordination [37,38]. In 2023, Bertagnolli et al. modified the surface of a commercially available open-cell melamine foam using PDA (me@PDA absorbent). The me@PDA absorbent exhibited efficient adsorption for Pb(II) [39]. Moreover, Zhang et al. designed an engineered sub-10 nm nanocomposite ZrO2 nanocrystals over a PDA layer. The hybrid adsorbents exhibited superior selectivity and good filtration performances (>99.5% removal) in Pb removal [40]. However, these PDA-based composites often suffer from high manufacturing costs or difficulties in solid–liquid separation. And they are difficult to scale for industrial use. More importantly, the PDA coating exhibits biocompatibility and has been widely applied in biocompatibility modification [41]. Therefore, we hypothesize that PDA-modified coir fibers not only exhibit enhanced adsorption efficiency for heavy metal ion removal but also possess good biocompatibility.

In this study, natural coir fiber was selected as a low-cost and scalable scaffold for heavy metal removal. Dopamine was polymerized in situ on the coir fiber surface under mildly alkaline conditions to generate a PDA coating and boost its adsorption capacity (Figure 1a). The resulting PDA coating forms a stable, conformal layer on the high-surface-area coir fibers and introduces catechol binding sites that promote metal–ion coordination while maintaining the overall safety and biodegradability of the materials (Figure 1b). We systematically evaluated the adsorption kinetics, the isotherms, and the reusability of PDA/Coir fiber. Moreover, cytotoxicity assessments of the treated solution showcased that dopamine-modified coir fibers not only retained their biocompatibility but also significantly reduced the systemic toxicity of the effluent by efficiently adsorbing heavy metal ions. These results highlight the potential of PDA-coated coir fiber as a sustainable and low-cost solution with practical promise for heavy metal removal.

2. Methods

2.1. Materials

Coir fibers were applied by mutual industries (Part# 17685-1-48). Copper chloride and cadmium chloride were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin biochemical technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. Dopamine hydrochloride was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA. The cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) was purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China. Human skin epithelial cells (HSEC) and human-derived normal L02 cells were supplied by Sunncell Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China. The phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solutions were purchased from Shanghai yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China.

2.2. Surface Modification of Natural Coir Fibers by Polydopamine (PDA)

The double-distilled water (DDW) was used to remove impurities and allowed to air dry before the specific experimental operation, establishing the control group. Subsequently, 1 g of coir fiber underwent an alkaline treatment in 50 mL phosphate buffer with a pH of 8.5 for 24 h of magnetic stirring. In addition, the same preparation process was used, and 100 mg of dopamine was added during incubation to prepare the in situ polydopamine coating layer. Then, the coir fiber with modification was purified via DDW and air drying. The final products were named as PDA/Coir.

2.3. Characterization of Materials

The morphology of the materials was observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using the Zeiss Sigma 300 VP instrument (ZEISS, Jena, Germany). The chemical structure was determined using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR, Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany) spectroscopy in the range of 4000–500 cm−1. Thermal stability was characterized using thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA). The test was conducted on a TG209F3 instrument at a heating rate of 10 °C/min from 25 °C to 800 °C in a nitrogen flow. The chemical structure and states of the materials were characterized by the X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS). Measurements were conducted using an ESCALAB 250Xi X-ray Photoelectron Spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The distribution of Cu2+ and Cd2+ ions in the PDA/Coir fiber was characterized by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) with a representative SEM image after adsorption experiments.

2.4. Adsorption Experiments

For the removal experiments of heavy metal ions, copper chloride (CuCl2) and cadmium chloride (CdCl2) were selected as reagents. CuCl2 and CdCl2 were dissolved in the water solutions with an initial concentration of 100 mg/L. The pH of the solution was adjusted with 0.1 M NaOH and 0.1 M HCl. Then, 100 mg of each dried fiber (original fibrous form) was individually incubated in the above solutions (pH = 6.0, 100 mL) and continuously stirred for 12 h to ensure equilibrium. Following incubation, these fibers were separated from the solution, and these solutions were filtered using a 0.45 μm membrane filter (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Finally, the residual metal ion content in the supernatant was analyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, PerkinElmer NexION 300X, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA).

The adsorption capacity of each fiber at equilibrium was calculated on the basis of the following Equation:qe= C0−Ce×VM where C0 is the initial concentration of the heavy metal solution (mg/L), Ce is the concentration at equilibrium (mg/L), M is the weight of the fiber, and V is the volume of the solution (L).

For the adsorption kinetic study, the contact time varied from 5 to 360 min, and the initial concentration of metal ions was fixed at 100 mg/L. Each reaction beaker contained 50 mg of the PDA/Coir fiber and 100 mL of heavy metal ion solution, which were continuously stirred at pH = 6 and 25 °C. The kinetic curves were fitted using the pseudo-first-order kinetic model and the pseudo-second-order kinetic model.

Pseudo-first-order kinetic model:qt= qe(1−e−k1t)

Pseudo-second-order kinetic model:qt= k2qe2t1+k2qet where t (min) represents the contact time, and qt (mg/g) and qe (mg/g) are the adsorption capacities at time t and at equilibrium, respectively.

To analyze the adsorption isotherms, the PDA/Coir fiber (100 mg) was placed into the metal ion solution (100 mL) containing Cd2+ and Cu2+. The concentration of Cu2+ was varied at 20~200 mg/L, and the concentration of Cd2+ was varied at 10~150 mg/L. The adsorption experiments were performed at 25 °C, a solution pH of 6, and a shaking time of 12 h. Adsorption isotherm curve fitting was performed using the Origin 2022 software. All the adsorption experiments were carried out in triplicate.

For regeneration experiments, the PDA/Coir fiber (100 mg) was placed into the metal ion solution (100 mg/L, 100 mL) containing Cd2+ and Cu2+. The adsorption experiments were performed at 25 °C, a solution pH of 6, and a shaking time of 12 h. The post-adsorption fiber was collected and dried. Then, the PDA/Coir fiber with metal ions was immersed in 50 mL of 0.5 M EDTA-2Na solution to desorb the metal ions. After desorption, the fiber was thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and used in the next adsorption cycle. All experiments were performed in triplicate. The removal efficiency (%) was calculated using the equation:Removal efficiency= C0−CeC0×100% where C0 is the initial concentration of the heavy metal solution (mg/L), and Ce is the concentration at equilibrium (mg/L).

2.5. Cell Experiment

The cells were cultured using Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution. L02 cells are seeded in culture vessels at a recommended density of approximately 1 × 104 cells/cm2 and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment. CCK-8 assay was utilized to assess the biocompatibility of the coir fiber, Coir/Alkali fiber, and PDA/Coir fiber. Briefly, approximately 1 × 104 cells per well with DMEM medium were seeded into 96-well plates. After being cultured for 24 h, the DMEM medium was replaced with 100 μg/mL of the coir fiber for another 24 h. Then, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well according to the corresponding manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbance of each sample was measured using a microplate reader at 450 nm. Cell viability was calculated according to the formula:cell viability=ODsampleODcontrol× 100%

Additionally, the Cd2+ and Cu2+ solutions after adsorption with the coir fiber, Coir/Alkali fiber, or PDA/Coir fiber were mixed at equal volume, then 20 μL of heavy metal ion solution was added into the culture wells for 24 h incubation, and related cytotoxicity was detected with the same method.

3. Results and Discussion

3.1. Preparation and Characterization of PDA/Coir Fiber

The coir fibers underwent a rigorous purification process involving washing with ultrapure water, followed by natural air drying in a controlled environment. These fibers were designated as the Coir group. Additionally, another set of coir fibers underwent an alkaline treatment to augment their surface adsorption properties, referred to as the Coir/Alkali group. This treatment involved immersing the coir fibers in a pH 8.5 phosphate buffer solution for 24 h, followed by thorough washing with ultrapure water and subsequent natural drying. To further enhance the heavy metal ion adsorption capabilities of the coir fibers, in situ polydopamine (PDA) modification was meticulously performed. During the fiber’s alkaline treatment, dopamine monomers were introduced, and alkaline-triggered polymerization was employed to generate polydopamine coatings (termed the PDA/Coir group). This process was followed by the same purification and air-drying procedures. The outcomes of these treatments are depicted in Figure 2a, where a noticeable transition from yellow to black in the PDA/Coir group indicates the successful dopamine polymerization on the fibers’ surface.

Detailed morphological examinations were conducted, starting with inverted microscope observations, as displayed in Figure 2b. This revealed evident pores following alkali treatment, with a thinning of the fibers. However, the internal structure was effectively restored after the in situ formation of dopamine coatings. Complementing this, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to analyze the carriers’ microscopic morphology, yielding the results shown in Figure 2c. Although SEM images revealed that alkali treatment notably induced fiber bifurcation, the limitation was effectively remedied by the in situ deposition in the PDA/Coir group. Collectively, these characterizations substantiate the successful preparation of PDA coating on the coir fiber, holding significant promise for bolstering their heavy metal adsorption capacity.

Additionally, the functional groups on coir and PDA/Coir fiber surfaces were characterized using an ATR-FTIR spectrometer. Figure 3 displays the FTIR spectra of the coir fiber and PDA/Coir fiber. The spectrum of the coir fiber exhibited characteristic bands at around 3320 cm−1 (O-H stretching of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin), 2915 cm−1 (C-H stretching of aliphatic -CH2/-CH3), 1735 cm−1 (C=O stretching of ester and carboxyl groups in hemicellulose), 1230 cm−1 (C-O stretching), and 1028 cm−1 (C-O-C stretching of polysaccharide backbone) [42,43]. After PDA modification, the carbonyl peak at 1735 cm−1 disappeared or merged into the adjacent PDA bands. The peak intensity at 1510 cm−1 and 1600 cm−1 increased significantly, which corresponds to the overlap of the aromatic N-H and C=C bending vibrations of the aromatic structure in PDA. Notably, a new peak appeared at 872 cm−1 in the spectrum of the PDA/Coir fiber, which is assigned to the bending vibrations of the aromatic rings in the PDA structure. The new distinct peak appeared at 2847 cm−1. The peak originates from the aliphatic ethyl chains of dopamine molecular structure [27,44].

Moreover, the high-resolution C 1s of the PDA/Coir fiber was characterized to confirm the successful deposition of the PDA layer on the fiber surface (Figure 4). In the high-resolution C 1s spectrum of the coir fiber, the peak at 284.8 eV is assigned to the C-C bond (77.82%), forming the carbon backbone. The peak at 286.4 eV corresponds to the C-O bond (15.39%), which originates from the cellulose and lignin in the coir fiber. After PDA modification, the relative content of the C-C component decreased to 46.72%, indicating the effective coverage of the fiber surface by the PDA layer. More importantly, the peak at 286.4 eV increased significantly to 36.66%, which is attributed to the overlap of the C-O signals with newly introduced C-N bonds from the PDA coating. Meanwhile, the proportion of C=O rose to 16.6%, providing direct evidence for the presence of quinone structure formed during dopamine polymerization [45]. Collectively, the above results confirmed that the PDA modification on the surface of the coir fiber was successful.

Furthermore, the thermal stability and degradation of the coir and PDA/Coir fibers were evaluated by TGA and DTG, as shown in Figure 5a,b. Both samples display multistep mass loss profiles. The first minor weight loss occurring below 200 °C is attributed to the evaporation of physically adsorbed and bound water. In this region, the mass loss of the PDA/Coir fiber (5.40%) is slightly higher than that of the coir fiber (≈5.23%), which is consistent with the increased content of hydrophilic functional groups introduced by the PDA coating.

The main degradation step for coir and PDA/Coir fibers takes place in the range of 250–350 °C, corresponding to the thermal decomposition of hemicellulose and cellulose, including depolymerization, dehydration of the sugar rings, and cleavage of glycosidic and ether linkages in the lignocellulosic matrix. A broader, slower mass loss extending up to 350–700 °C is associated with the gradual breakdown of lignin and the formation of char. Furthermore, the DTG curve of the coir fiber exhibits a single dominant peak at 355 °C with a total weight loss of ≈73.68% in the principal degradation stage. After PDA coating, the overall degradation pattern of the PDA/Coir fiber remains similar, but notable differences can be observed. The PDA/Coir fiber shows a shoulder peak in the DTG curve around 288.33 °C, which can be assigned to the decomposition of the PDA layer. Moreover, the main DTG peak shifts from 355 °C (coir fiber) to 330 °C (PDA/Coir fiber), and the mass loss in the major degradation region changes from 73.68% to 65.59%, indicating that the PDA coating participates in and slightly modifies the thermo-oxidative degradation process of the lignocellulosic substrate. Importantly, the residual mass at 800 °C increases from 21.10% (coir fiber) to 29.01% (PDA/Coir fiber), reflecting the higher char-forming tendency of the aromatic PDA layer. These changes in degradation profile, DTG peak shape/position, and final char yield, while preserving the characteristic thermal signature of the coir fiber, demonstrate that PDA has been successfully deposited on the fiber surface and alters its thermal stability through the formation of an additional carbonaceous coating.

3.2. Adsorption Performance of PDA/Coir Fiber

Next, we performed preliminary adsorption screening of coir and PDA/Coir fibers under identical conditions. As shown in Figure 6a,b, the Coir/Alkali fiber exhibited improved heavy metal adsorption compared to the control group. This enhancement is likely attributed to the increased surface roughness and the exposure of more negatively charged functional sites for metal ion complexation and ion exchange induced by the alkaline treatment. Encouragingly, the PDA/Coir fiber displayed 6.84-fold and 12.86-fold higher adsorption capacities for Cu2+ and Cd2+, respectively, than the untreated coir fiber. Particularly, the adsorption capacity for Cd2+ increased from 4.98 mg/g (Coir fiber) to 64.15 mg/g (PDA/Coir fiber), highlighting its potential for heavy metal removal from wastewater. In addition, EDS mapping reveals a uniform distribution of the adsorbed metals across the fiber surface (Figure 7a,b). The excellent adsorption performance of the PDA/Coir fiber is primarily attributed to the PDA coating on the fiber surface. The introduction of the PDA layer provides abundant active functional groups, particularly the amines and catechol. These groups facilitate strong interactions with metal ions through coordination and hydrogen bonding, thereby significantly improving the adsorption capacity of the PDA/Coir fiber.

Moreover, the XPS was used to elucidate the elemental composition and metal state of the PDA/Coir fiber after Cu2+ and Cd2+ adsorption. As shown in Figure 8a and Figure 9a, Cu 2p and Cd 3d peaks appear in addition to C 1s, O 1s, and N 1s in the PDA/Coir fiber, corroborating the successful adsorption of Cu2+ and Cd2+ onto the PDA/Coir fiber. The high-resolution Cd 3d spectra show a well-resolved Cd2+ 3d5/2 (405.44 eV) and Cd2+ 3d3/2 (412.16 eV) doublet, consistent with Cd(II) (Figure 8e). Likewise, the high-resolution Cu 2p spectra exhibit the Cu2+ 2p3/2 and Cu2+ 2p1/2 doublet with pronounced shake-up satellites, which is diagnostic of Cu(II) (Figure 9e) [46]. In the high-resolution O 1s spectrum, the peaks at 531.4 eV and at 532.4 eV originate from the cellulose components of the coir fiber, assigned to C=O and C-O, respectively [47]. Notably, O 1s fitting curves require an additional Metal–O (Cu–O at 530.54 eV; Cd–O at 530.21 eV) component in Figure 8d and Figure 9d, supporting coordination between the metal ions and oxygen-containing PDA groups (catechol/quinone).

We further investigated the adsorption mechanism of the PDA/Coir fiber via the high-resolution O 1s spectrum. As shown in Figure 10a, the surface oxygen was primarily composed of C-O (67.71%) and C=O (32.29%) groups before adsorption. After the Cu(II) adsorption, the proportion of C-O notably decreased to 45.75%, and a new peak attributed to Cu-O bonds (20.65%) appeared (Figure 10b). These results show that the phenolic hydroxyl groups in the PDA coating were consumed during the adsorption process. This suggests that Cu(II) was captured mainly through a complexation mechanism, where the catechol groups formed coordinate bonds with the metal ions. Taken together, these XPS results provide direct evidence that Cu2+ and Cd2+ are effectively adsorbed on the surface of the PDA/Coir fiber through surface complexation with PDA functional groups.

The adsorption kinetics of Cd2+ and Cu2+ on the PDA/Coir fiber were fitted using the pseudo-first-order kinetic model and pseudo-second-order kinetic model (Figure 11). The fitted parameters are summarized in Table 2. As shown in Figure 11 and Table 2, the R2 constants of the pseudo-second-order model (Cd2+: 0.9495; Cu2+: 0.9975) are higher than those of the pseudo-first-order kinetic model (Cd2+: 0.8735; Cu2+: 0.9609), which indicates that the pseudo-second-order model exhibits a better fit for Cd2+ and Cu2+ ions. K values represent the rate of active-site occupation by the adsorbates and can be used to describe the kinetics of chemisorption. For both ions, these curves show a rapid initial uptake followed by a slower approach to equilibrium, which is typical for fibrous sorbents. The Cu2+ reaches equilibrium faster than Cd2+, consistent with the larger rate constants (K1 = 0.0789 (Cu2+) and 0.0241 (Cd2+), K2 = 0.0009 (Cu2+) and 0.0004 (Cd2+)). This result aligns with stronger and faster coordination between Cu2+ and the catechol groups in PDA. Moreover, qe represents the maximum adsorption capacity when adsorption reaches equilibrium. The fitted theoretical equilibrium adsorption amount from the pseudo-second-order model is also closer to the experimental equilibrium adsorption amount. In conclusion, the kinetic results indicate that the pseudo-second-order model provides a more reliable description of Cd2+ and Cu2+ on the PDA/Coir fiber.

The adsorption isotherms of Cu2+ and Cd2+ on the PDA/Coir fiber are presented in Figure 12. For both metal ions, the adsorption capacity (qe) increases steeply with rising equilibrium concentration (Ce) in the low-concentration range, followed by a gradual approach to a plateau at higher concentration, indicating saturation of the available adsorption sites. The maximum uptake capacities estimated from the isotherms reach 119.92 mg/g (Cu2+) and 76.32 mg/g (Cd2+), demonstrating that the PDA/Coir fiber can efficiently remove Cu2+ and Cd2+ from aqueous solutions, even at relatively low concentrations.

To further elucidate the adsorption mechanism, the equilibrium data were fitted using the Langmuir and the Freundlich isotherm models. In both cases, the Langmuir model provides a superior fit to the experimental data (Table 3), with higher correlation coefficients (R2 = 0.9607 (Cu2+); R2 = 0.9159 (Cd2+)) compared to the Freundlich model (R2 = 0.7207 (Cu2+), R2 = 0.7001 (Cd2+)). This predominance of the Langmuir description suggests that Cu2+ and Cd2+ are mainly adsorbed as a monolayer on a finite number of energetically equivalent sites on the PDA/Coir fiber surface, consistent with specific complexation of metal ions with catechol and amine functionalities in the PDA coating. However, it is noteworthy that the fitting is not entirely perfect, indicating that the adsorption mechanism is more complex than the assumption of a homogeneous surface. The deviation can be attributed to the chemical and structural heterogeneity of the PDA/Coir fiber surface. Unlike ideal adsorbents, the PDA/Coir fiber possesses multiple types of active adsorption sites, including the functional groups of PDA (such as amines and catechol) and residual hydroxyl and carboxyl groups from the natural lignin and cellulose structure in the coir fiber. Therefore, while the Langmuir model is useful, the adsorption process likely involves multiple types of adsorption sites with different adsorption energies. The slight deviation in the isotherm fitting reflects this inherent energetic heterogeneity and confirms that the removal of heavy metals is driven by a combination of chemical chelation, electrostatic attraction, and surface complexation mechanisms. Furthermore, we evaluated the reusability of the PDA/Coir fiber over adsorption–desorption cycles for Cu2+ and Cd2+ (Figure 13). The PDA/Coir fiber still had good adsorption performance after four cycles, and the removal rates of Cu2+ and Cd2+ ions reached about 77% and 82%, respectively. Therefore, the PDA/Coir fiber is a reusable adsorbent with practical regeneration potential for repeated heavy metal removal.

3.3. Biocompatibility

To assess the biocompatibility of coir fibers, human skin epithelial cells (HSECs) were utilized to examine cellular activity. The procedure involved culturing HSECs until they reached the logarithmic growth phase using complete medium, followed by trypsin digestion to obtain a cell suspension. These cells were then inoculated into 96-well plates at a density of 104 cells per well and subsequently incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. After incubation, the coir fiber was individually introduced for an additional 24 h incubation period. In this process, 20 μL of PBS solution served as a control and was established as 100% cell viability. As shown in Figure 14a, the cell viability remained consistently above 95% both before and after modification, confirming the sustained biocompatibility of the coir fiber and suggesting its suitability for practical applications.

In the context of assessing the capacity of coconut husk fiber to reduce the biotoxicity of effluent following heavy metal adsorption, human-derived normal (L02) cells were used as a cellular model. This model provides a realistic simulation of the actual conditions of the effluent post-uptake. This was achieved by mixing the heavy metal solutions after adsorption and subsequently measuring the cytotoxicity levels produced by the remaining heavy metals using the CCK-8 kit after 24 h of incubation. The findings showcased in Figure 14b revealed a significant difference. After coir and Coir/Alkali fiber adsorption treatments, the heavy metal solution retained high cytotoxicity, with cell mortality exceeding 75%. Conversely, in the heavy metal solution after treatment with the PDA/Coir fiber, more than 90% of the cells survived, aligning with its remarkable heavy metal scavenging capability. This data underscores the pivotal role of PDA-modification in efficiently reducing the cytotoxicity of effluent after heavy metal adsorption, further underscoring the suitability of the coir fiber for practical applications.

4. Conclusions

In summary, this study utilized natural coconut shell fibers as an effective medium for wastewater treatment. The in situ polydopamine (PDA) deposition process significantly enhanced the adsorption capability for heavy metal ions. Comprehensive characterization confirmed the successful coating of PDA on the surface of fibers, leading to improved adsorption of copper and cadmium ions, as demonstrated by the adsorption experiments. Furthermore, reusability tests showed that the PDA/Coir fiber adsorbent maintains stability and effectiveness over multiple cycles, confirming its potential for long-term use. Moreover, cellular modeling verified the biocompatibility of the PDA/Coir fiber, both before and after treatment, and demonstrated its remarkable ability to reduce the toxicity of residual heavy metal ions after adsorption. These results not only highlight the dual functionality of the PDA/Coir fiber but also emphasize its potential for practical applications in wastewater treatment, offering a sustainable and efficient solution for heavy metal removal.

Outlook: Although this work shows a promising result, there are some limitations that need to be addressed in future research. Firstly, future studies need to explore the energy distribution and multi-site adsorption characteristics of the adsorption process, providing further insight into the interactions between metal ions and functional groups. Secondly, while the PDA/Coir fiber exhibits significant adsorption ability for copper and cadmium ions, its long-term stability and performance on various heavy metal ions need to be further evaluated, particularly in actual wastewater systems. Moreover, the reusability of the material in practical, large-scale applications should be optimized to enhance its sustainability. Future studies should also focus on the economic evaluation of whether this strategy is cost-effective for mass production compared to the commercial adsorbents. Finally, while the biocompatibility of the material was confirmed, more in vivo studies should evaluate its safety and effectiveness in environmental applications.
