A new animal model of cardiorenal syndrome could be established by inducing heart failure through coronary artery ligation in spontaneously hypertensive rats
Authors: Biye Zhou, Jinbao Zhao, Dong Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In rats with unilateral nephrectomy and cardiac dysfunction, renal function deteriorates at an accelerated rate, as evidenced by increased proteinuria. Whether myocardial infarct-induced heart failure (HF) exacerbates renal injury in hypertensive rats with mild renal injury has not been reported. Rats underwent either coronary ligation or sham surgery. Thirty spontaneously hypertensive rats (SHRs) aged 8 weeks were randomly divided into two groups. Group 1 was the sham group, in which the rats underwent thoracotomy without ligation of the coronary artery. Group 2 underwent coronary artery ligation. The rats in group 2 underwent coronary artery ligation on week 0. The experiment lasted 12 weeks. Urine was collected in metabolic cages over a 24-h period. Urine was collected from the rats 2 days before the end of the experiment, and the ratio of urinary protein to urinary creatinine was measured in the clinical laboratory. All rats were examined by echocardiogram one day before the end of the experiment. On the last day of the experiment, blood was collected and sent to the laboratory for analysis. Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining were performed on heart and kidney sections. The ejection fraction in group 2 was lower than that in group 1 (P < 0.001). The urinary albumin to creatinine ratio in group 2 was greater than that in group 1 (P < 0.001). The urea and creatinine levels in group 1 were significantly lower than those in group 2 (P < 0.01). The levels of brain natriuretic peptide (BNP), neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) and cystatin C were greater in the second group than in the first group (P < 0.05). The interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels in group 2 were significantly greater than those in group 1 (P < 0.001). The malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in Group 2 were greater than those in Group 1 (P < 0.01). The glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) levels in Group 2 were lower than those in Group 1 (P < 0.05). The level of angiotensin II (AT-II) in group 1 was lower than that in group 2 (P < 0.001). Cardiac dysfunction secondary to myocardial infarction could induce cardiorenal interactions in SHRs. It could be interpreted by the activation of oxidative stress, changes in inflammation and alteration of renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease and renal disease are closely related and form a vicious cycle known as cardiorenal syndrome (CRS)1. The renal function decline rate is faster in patients with hypertension than in those without hypertension2. The cause may be insufficient nephrons and congenital nephrons in hypertensive patients. The loss of renal units, which is caused by hypertensive glomerular injury associated with albuminuria, may also be a reason. It is not clear whether this could result in heart failure. Hypertension and albuminuria are both important treatment targets for kidney diseases. Although renin–angiotensin–aldosterone (RAAS) inhibitors, diuretics and beta blockers are used to reduce blood pressure in patients with heart failure, and they yield positive results, a decrease in blood pressure is often not the essential goal of treatment. The combined use of many antihypertensive drugs may eventually lead to a lower glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and higher mortality because it reduces renal perfusion pressure and damages RAAS activity, while the RAAS can maintain GFRs in patients with low renal blood flow3.

Hypertension is closely related to CRS and is very common clinically4. The heart and kidney of spontaneously hypertensive rats are vulnerable to injury5. Therefore, on the basis of existing renal damage, we performed coronary artery ligation on rats with spontaneous hypertension. We created an animal model of cardiorenal syndrome to elucidate the mechanism of cardiorenal syndrome.

Previous studies have shown that heart failure can lead to kidney dysfunction2,3,6–10. Hypertension can lead to structural and functional dysfunction of the heart and kidneys, but coronary artery ligation in hypertensive rats has not been reported to lead to severe renal dysfunction. Changes in hemodynamics, activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, metabolic disorders, inflammation11, oxidative stress12, etc., are important in the pathogenesis of cardiorenal syndrome.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval of the studies and informed consent

All experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of the People's Liberation Army General Hospital, and all methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All methods were reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Experimental materials

Experimental animals

Thirty spontaneously hypertensive male rats aged 8 weeks and weighing 130–160 g were used in this study. The rats were kept in the animal laboratory of PLA General Hospital at 17–21 °C. Unrestricted animal feed and water were provided by the Laboratory Animal Center of our hospital. Drinking water was provided ad libitum.

Drugs and indicator testing

Pathology of the heart and kidney of the rats was performed by the Department of Pathology, Chinese PLA General Hospital, and all blood parameters were determined by Beijing Zhongtong Lambo Laboratory13.

Animal model

Thirty 8-week-old SHRs were randomly divided into two groups. Group 1 was the sham group, in which rats were subjected to thoracotomy without ligation of the coronary artery and were orally administered saline by gavage. Group 2 was subjected to coronary artery ligation at week 0. The HF model was established by the left anterior descending artery (LAD) ligation method14. Before anaesthesia, all the rats were shaved, weighed, disinfected, and scrubbed with 75% ethanol from the neck and chest area and anaesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 3% phenobarbital sodium (30 mg/kg). The anaesthetized rats were placed in a supine position, and endotracheal intubation was performed. Ventilation was achieved by connecting the endotracheal tube to an HX-300S ventilator at a breathing rate of 80/min and tidal volume of 10 mL. The heart was exposed through a left thoracotomy via 3rd and 4th interspaced rib resection. After the pericardium was opened, the coronary anatomy was inspected to determine the proximal LAD coronary artery. The LAD was then transiently ligated with a 6–0 polypropylene suture. The incision was closed in layers, and a chest tube was placed to drain the chest and evacuate the pneumothorax. The chest tube was removed once the animal became ambulatory. HF, defined as ST-elevation or depression, was confirmed by ECG and BNP levels15. The entire experiment lasted 12 weeks. The 24-h mortality rate of the infarcted animals was 13.33%. At the end of the experiment, 13 rats in group 1 and 10 rats in group 2 survived.

Measurements of cardiac ultrasound and cardiac and renal function in rats

Before euthanizing, the urine of the rats was collected and sent to the laboratory for determination of the urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio. Urine was collected in metabolic cages over a 24-h period. One day before euthanasia, all rats were anaesthetized and examined by cardiac ultrasound. After euthanasia, blood was collected to measure creatinine and urea nitrogen. Serum IL-1β, IL-6, malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), angiotensin II (AT-II), B natriuretic peptide (BNP), neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), and cystatin C were also measured. The measurements were carried out according to the kit instructions13.

Pathological examination of the heart and kidneys of rats

The hearts and kidneys were removed immediately after euthanasia, cleaned in normal saline (NS), and then cut into approximately 3 mm thick tissue blocks, which were soaked in a 4% formaldehyde solution and fixed for more than 72 h. The heart and kidney samples were sent to the laboratory of the Nephrology Department of PLA General Hospital for paraffin embedding and staining. Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining were performed on heart and kidney sections, respectively. Histopathological observations were performed13.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the mean ± SEM. All the statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS V19.0 software program (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA). Differences between groups were analysed using Student’s t test. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Determination of urea and creatinine levels in rats

The urea levels in group 2 were significantly greater than those in group 1 by 18.04% (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3B). The creatinine levels in group 2 were significantly greater than those in group 1 by 25.49% (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3C).

Determination of inflammatory cytokine levels

Interleukin 1β is an important cytokine and peptide regulator produced mainly by mononuclear macrophages. Interleukin 6 is a multifunctional cytokine produced by mononuclear macrophages, lymphocytes, and nonlymphocyte cells. The IL-1β and IL-6 levels in group 2 were significantly greater than those in group 1 by 35.83% and 53.29%, respectively (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3D,E).

These findings suggest that inflammatory mediators are involved in the heart-kidney interaction disease model.

Determination of antioxidant MDA and GSH-Px levels

Malondialdehyde is one of the final products of membrane lipid peroxidation, and its content can be used as one of the indices to evaluate the severity of cell damage. Glutathione peroxidase can remove lipid peroxides induced by reactive oxygen species and protect the integrity of the cell membrane structure and function. The MDA levels in Group 2 were 30.10% greater than those in Group 1 (P < 0.01). The GSH-Px levels in Group 2 were 12.64% lower than those in Group 1 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3F,G).

Determination of AT-II levels

Angiotensin II (AT-II) binds to angiotensin receptors, causing corresponding physiological effects. It acts on vascular smooth muscle and contractile arterioles and increases arterial blood pressure. Furthermore, it is one of the most potent vasoconstrictor substances known. The data are presented as the mean ± SEM. The level of AT-II in group 2 was 84.10% greater than that in group 1 (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3H).

Determination of BNP, gelatinase-associated lipocalin, and cystatin C levels

As a quantitative marker of heart failure, BNP reflects not only left ventricular systolic dysfunction but also left ventricular diastolic dysfunction, valvular dysfunction and right ventricular dysfunction.

Neutrophil gelatinase–associated lipocalin (NGAL) is a sensitive biomarker of early kidney injury, and NGAL levels increase significantly within 2 h of nephrectomy16. Cystatin C, also known as cysteine protease inhibitor C, is a biomarker of the glomerular filtration rate.

BNP was 12.39% greater in group 2 than in group 2 (P < 0.05). NGAL was 31.14% greater in group 2 than in group 2 (P < 0.001). The prevalence of cystatin C was 19.38% greater in group 2 than in group 2 (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3I–K).

Discussion

In this study, we used SHRs to establish a cardiorenal syndrome model that is more suitable for specific clinical conditions. This novel animal model is highly similar to clinical practice. In patients with existing hypertension, myocardial infarction may further lead to renal structural and functional damage17. This injury may be related to the activation of the renin angiotensin aldosterone system17. Excessive activation of the renin angiotensin aldosterone system is harmful to both the heart and kidneys18. In the present study, the renin angiotensin aldosterone system was activated in the group 2. Cardiorenal syndrome occurs under the combined effects of hypertension, renin angiotensin aldosterone system overactivation, myocardial infarction, and heart failure. Oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and vascular inflammation could be therapeutic targets when the RAAS is activated by the accumulation of conventional cardiovascular risk factors. However, a strategy for CRS management has not yet been established1. Such management would better simulate the occurrence of cardiorenal syndrome in patients with hypertension and myocardial infarction.

To date, none of the known animal models can fully replicate the pathogenesis and clinical features of human cardiorenal syndrome19,20. Therefore, there is a need for a better animal model of cardiorenal interaction disease that is able to not only mimic the clinical features of cardiorenal syndrome, such as cardiac, renal, hemodynamic and neuroendocrine changes but also evaluate the therapeutic effect. Such a model should include combined renal and cardiac damage, characterized by progressive deterioration of cardiac and renal function21. For cardiac function, systolic dysfunction should be replicated because it leads to a reduction in cardiac output and subsequent reduction in renal blood flow (RBF), as confirmed by echocardiography or hemodynamic measurements. In addition, a model that demonstrates increased end-diastolic pressure and venous congestion is also required to further explore the latest findings from clinical observations. At the histological level, cardiac injury must have the following characteristics: hypertrophy and fibrosis, especially myocardial/capillary mismatch. For kidney injury, this model must have the following characteristics: progressive renal function reduction with increased creatinine levels, increased albumin excretion and a decreased GFR/creatinine clearance ratio. Glomerulosclerosis and interstitial fibrosis should also be documented. An important point in the exploration of new animal models is to reduce cardiac and renal function to the extent that it leads to significant dysfunction and still allows sufficient time to observe its progress. It is desirable that cardiorenal failure gradually develop so that more severe stages and preventive treatment measures can be studied. These findings will help researchers draw conclusions, and more focus can be placed on the pathogenesis and development of the different stages behind cardiorenal interaction disease22. There are a number of approaches that can induce cardiac and renal dysfunction in rodents, and these approaches can be combined to develop a novel model of cardiorenal interaction disease23. Each has advantages and disadvantages, but none of them has all the characteristics of the "ideal model" mentioned above24. In humans, the functions of affected organs gradually decrease, eventually leading to failure. The sudden onset of organ failure is a common defect in many common disease models and is not clinically consistent25. Another reason for the limited use of animal models based on surgical interventions is that they lead to a significant reduction in residual tissue, such as in models of subtotal nephrectomy or myocardial infarction26. This reduces potential drug targets and therapeutic effects, as well as the possibility of molecular analysis27. It is important to remember that differences in response to surgical interventions have been reported across sexes or organisms. The shortcomings of the available animal models described above may call into question the possibility of designing a perfect animal model of cardiorenal syndrome. However, heart and kidney failure are multifactorial diseases that arise from multiple underlying diseases and involve multiple regulatory mechanisms28. Given the complexity of the mechanisms involved in the individual and combined pathogenesis of heart and kidney failure, a reasonable understanding may require more than one animal model, and the choice of animal model depends on the problem being studied29. All potential new models and current models, including those described here, need to be investigated in more detail. In addition to the established factors involved in cardiorenal syndrome, new findings in cardiorenal interaction disorders, including increased venous congestion, anaemia, and renal tubular damage, need to be further investigated and represented in these models30. The use of hypertensive rats in a model of cardiorenal syndrome is worthy of further study25.

Conclusion

Cardiac dysfunction secondary to myocardial infarction could induce cardiorenal interactions in SHRs. It could be explained by the activation of oxidative stress, changes in inflammation and alteration of renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system.
