Maintenance of oral hygiene with beetroot rinse in gingivitis patients with oral lichen planus: A pilot randomized controlled trial
Authors: Snehlata Kaushik, Rameshwari Singhal, Samiksha Dubey, Vikram Khanna, Abbas Ali Mahdi, Prashant Gupta, Pavitra Rastogi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Oral lichen planus (OLP) is a chronic inflammatory mucocutaneous disorder frequently associated with painful gingival involvement, which can compromise effective plaque control. Chlorhexidine (CHX) is the gold standard chemical plaque control agent but may be poorly tolerated in OLP patients due to mucosal irritation. Natural alternatives such as beetroot juice, rich in bioactive compounds, may offer supportive benefits.Aim:The study aimed to compare the short-term antiplaque, antimicrobial, and salivary nitric oxide (NO) effects of a beetroot juice mouthrinse with chlorhexidine in gingivitis patients with and without OLP.Materials and Methods:This pilot randomized controlled study included 44 gingivitis patients, with OLP (Group 1) and without OLP (Group 2). Participants were randomly allocated to receive either beetroot juice rinse (Groups 1A and 2A) or once-daily 0.2% chlorhexidine mouthrinse (Groups 1B and 2B). All patients underwent scaling and root planing before intervention. The clinical and biochemical parameters were recorded at baseline, immediately after intervention, and at 1-week follow-up. Plaque index (PI), gingival index (GI), Streptococcus spp. colony-forming units (CFU), and salivary NO levels were evaluated.Results:At 1 week, significant reductions in PI and GI scores were observed within all treatment groups (P < 0.05). Streptococcus spp. CFU counts decreased following intervention in all groups; however, sustained reduction from day 1 to day 7 was more consistently observed in the chlorhexidine groups. Salivary NO levels showed a significant increase in the OLP + beetroot group, while reductions were observed in the chlorhexidine groups.Conclusion:Within the limitations of this short-term pilot study, beetroot juice rinse demonstrated preliminary antiplaque effects and was associated with changes in salivary NO levels. Chlorhexidine remains the gold standard for antimicrobial persistence. Beetroot rinse may be considered an exploratory, well-tolerated adjunct for oral hygiene maintenance in OLP patients, pending confirmation from larger, long-term studies.

INTRODUCTION

Oral lichen planus (OLP) is a chronic, idiopathic, immune-mediated mucocutaneous disorder, with a reported malignant transformation rate ranging from 0.3% to 10%.[1] Clinically, OLP most commonly affects the buccal mucosa, tongue, and gingiva, often presenting as bilateral and symmetrical lesions.[23] These multiple, bilaterally symmetrical lesions make oral hygiene practices difficult. Involvement of the gingiva – frequently termed desquamative gingivitis – can cause significant pain, burning sensation, and mucosal fragility, thereby compromising effective plaque control and routine oral hygiene practices.

Topical corticosteroids remain the first-line therapy for the symptomatic management of OLP.[4] However, their clinical utility is limited by variable patient response, formulation-dependent efficacy, cost, and potential adverse effects with prolonged use.[5] Consequently, adjunctive and supportive approaches aimed at improving oral hygiene and reducing the local inflammatory burden are particularly relevant in patients with OLP.

Evidence suggests a significant association between OLP and increased periodontal inflammation. A recent systematic review reported that patients with OLP exhibit greater severity of periodontal disease compared with healthy controls.[6] Gingival inflammation in these patients is multifactorial and closely linked to plaque accumulation, which plays a central role in the initiation and progression of gingivitis.[7] Mechanical and chemical plaque control remains the cornerstone of gingivitis management. Although gingivitis associated with OLP represents a polymicrobial biofilm-mediated condition, Streptococcus spp. was selected as a surrogate microbial marker in this pilot study because they constitute early plaque colonizers, play a key role in biofilm initiation, and allow standardized, culture-based quantification in the short-term exploratory trials.

Chlorhexidine (CHX) is considered the gold standard chemical plaque control agent due to its broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity.[8] However, its long-term use is associated with adverse effects, including tooth staining, altered taste, mucosal irritation, and epithelial desquamation – effects that may be particularly problematic in patients with mucosal disorders such as OLP. This highlights the need to explore alternative, better-tolerated adjunctive plaque control agents for this population.

Natural and plant-based oral rinses have gained increasing attention as potential adjuncts for long-term oral hygiene maintenance due to their perceived safety, accessibility, and additional biological benefits.[9] Agents such as aloe vera, neem, tulsi, and moringa have demonstrated antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties; however, existing evidence remains limited and heterogeneous, warranting further controlled investigations.[1011]

Red beetroot (Beta vulgaris rubra) was selected for the evaluation in the present pilot study because of its distinct biochemical profile and potential relevance to periodontal and mucosal health. Beetroot is rich in dietary nitrates, which are metabolized by oral bacteria into nitric oxide (NO). NO exhibits antimicrobial, vasodilatory, and immunomodulatory effects and may influence gingival inflammation and local tissue healing. In addition, beetroot contains bioactive phytochemicals, including polyphenols, flavonoids, carotenoids, ascorbic acid, and betalains, which possess antioxidant and antimicrobial properties against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria.[12] Flavonoids, in particular, are known to disrupt bacterial cell membranes, increasing permeability and inducing cell lysis.

Unlike many herbal rinses that primarily target antimicrobial activity, beetroot may exert combined effects by modulating oxidative stress, inflammatory pathways, and microbial load. These properties suggest potential utility as a supportive oral hygiene adjunct, particularly in conditions such as gingivitis and OLP, where chronic inflammation and mucosal sensitivity coexist.

Accordingly, the present pilot randomized controlled study aimed to explore the short-term antimicrobial, antioxidant, and antiplaque effects of a beetroot juice mouthrinse in patients with gingivitis with and without clinically diagnosed OLP and to compare these effects with those of once-daily CHX mouthrinse.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

Parallel arm, randomized, controlled prospective interventional study screened 136 patients aged 18–55 years from May 2022 to March 2023 (CTRI//2022/05/042882). The sample size of 11 in each group was calculated at 5% significance level with 95% confidence interval, assuming 90% power and the standard deviation (SD) of 0.241 in the reduction of Streptococcus sp. in the test group.[13] The sample size was calculated exclusively based on the primary microbial outcome (Streptococcus spp. colony-forming units [CFUs]). The study was not powered to detect statistically significant differences in clinical parameters such as plaque index (PI), gingival index (GI), or NO levels, which were therefore considered secondary and exploratory outcomes.

The inclusion criteria were healthy adult patients 18–55 years old, male or female, presenting with gingivitis (GI score 1–3) with and without clinically discernible, symptomatic lesions of OLP.[14] OLP was diagnosed by a trained examiner based on the clinical features of lesions described by Escudier et al. and gingival involvement.[15] Histopathological confirmation was not performed, as biopsy was avoided in symptomatic erosive lesions for ethical and patient comfort considerations. This may introduce a risk of diagnostic misclassification. The exclusion criteria were immunocompromised patients, malignant diseases, hepatitis C infection, diagnosed psychiatric disorders, patients on anti-inflammatory drugs or antibiotics, any systemic diseases, periodontal pockets or recession >3 mm, pregnant women, smokers, alcohol users, and drug abusers.

The Institutional Ethics Committee (IX-PGTSC-II B IMR-R/P6) approved the study, and informed written consent was obtained for all the patients enrolled in the study.

Study design and procedures

Forty-four gingivitis patients with and without OLP were randomly divided into test and control groups using a computerized table of random numbers. Group 1 consisted of 22 gingivitis patients with OLP, randomly divided into intervention test Group 1A (n = 11), receiving beetroot juice rinse and intervention control Group 1B (n = 11), receiving CHX rinse. Similarly, Group 2 (gingivitis patients without OLP) was randomly divided into 2A (Beetroot rinse, n = 11) and 2B (CHX, n = 11) [Figure 1].

CONSORT flow diagram showing participant recruitment, allocation, follow-up, and analysis according to CONSORT 2010 guidelines

Due to the distinct color, taste, and odor of beetroot juice, participant blinding was not feasible. The study, therefore, followed an open-label design. In addition, the investigator responsible for dispensing the mouthrinses also performed the clinical outcome assessments, which may introduce performance and detection bias.

Beetroot rinse (preparation and dosing)

Fresh beetroot (Beta vulgaris) was used to prepare the experimental rinse; however, no chemical standardization or quantitative analysis of active constituents such as dietary nitrates, betalains, or polyphenols was performed. Consequently, potential batch-to-batch variability in bioactive compound concentration could not be controlled.

A standardized preparation protocol was followed for each batch: 100 g of washed, peeled beetroot was juiced using a domestic centrifugal juicer to yield approximately 40–60 mL of juice. Participants were instructed to use 50 mL of freshly prepared beetroot juice as a 30-s rinse once daily, then expectorate. All juices were prepared within 30 min of use to minimize phytochemical degradation.

The clinical parameters recorded were PI and GI at baseline and a 1-week follow-up (day 7).[14] Plaque and saliva samples were collected at baseline, 30 min after the rinse (day 1), and 1-week follow-up (day 7). The severity of Lichen Planus Index (SLPI)[15] and dichotomous patient pain and discomfort were recorded at baseline, day 1, and day 7 on a Visual Analog Scale (VAS) by a blinded assessor unaware of the treatment protocol.

Scaling and Root Planing (SRP) was performed for all participants before intervention to ensure a uniform baseline and to minimize the confounding effect of preexisting plaque deposits. Baseline records and samples were taken before SRP. On the same appointment after SRP, patients in the intervention groups were asked to rinse with one tablespoon (15–20 ml) of freshly prepared beetroot juice for 30 s or with 0.2% CHX mouth rinse (Ciprofresh, Cipla Health, India). CHX gluconate mouthrinse was prescribed once daily rather than the conventional twice-daily regimen. This modified dosing protocol was adopted to improve patient compliance and reduce the risk of mucosal irritation in individuals with OLP. After a waiting time of 30 min, “after rinse,” records and samples were taken. Patients were recalled daily for 1 week and given freshly prepared beetroot juice/CHX to rinse for 30 s in the investigator’s presence. After a week, the final records and samples were taken.

Participants were followed for 7 days after intervention initiation. This short follow-up duration was selected to assess immediate antimicrobial and antiplaque effects; however, it does not permit evaluation of long-term clinical effectiveness, substantivity, or recurrence.

Saliva sampling and processing

The collection of saliva was done by drooling method. Patients were seated in the Coachman’s position, head slightly down, and asked not to swallow or move their tongue or lips during the saliva collection. The collection time was 5 min. The saliva samples were centrifuged immediately to remove cell debris (×3000 g for 10 min at 4°C). The supernatant was removed and stored in small aliquots at −80°C until the analysis.

NO concentration in saliva was measured using a NO Colorimetric Assay Kit (Elabscience, E-BC-K035-S) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. The detection of a reddish azo compound was linearly related to the concentration of NO and measured using a spectrophotometer at 550 nm wavelength.

Salivary NO levels were assessed as a biochemical marker associated with oxidative and inflammatory pathways. However, NO is a pleiotropic molecule and changes in its levels may reflect multiple biological processes, including nitrate metabolism, inflammatory activity, and host response. Therefore, NO values were interpreted as exploratory biochemical indicators rather than direct measures of antioxidant or therapeutic efficacy.

Plaque samples

Whole plaque samples were obtained from the buccal surface of the maxillary first molar using an excavator from mesial to distal. The samples were stored and transferred in a 2 ml saline solution test tube and diluted to 10−3 dilution. After dilution, it was planted in Streptococcus jelly medium (Himedia - Mitis Salivarius Agar Base) using the pour plate method and then incubated for 24 h at 37°C. The inoculation result of Streptococcus sp. was counted using a colony counter [Figure 2].

Representative culture plates showing postinoculation colony-forming units (CFU) of Streptococcus species

Microbiological assessment was limited to quantifying Streptococcus spp. using the culture-based CFU analysis. Although gingivitis and OLP-associated inflammation are polymicrobial in nature, Streptococcus spp. were selected as a representative early plaque colonizer and a pragmatic surrogate marker for biofilm modulation in this pilot study. Comprehensive microbiome analysis was beyond the scope of the present investigation.

Data analysis

All data were presented as mean ± SD. The difference in means between groups was measured using the Independent sample t-test and Mann–Whitney test. Paired t-test was used to compare results within the same group before and after rinsing with Beetroot juice and CHX. Repeated-measure ANOVA and Chi-square test were conducted to compare all groups’ mean. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d for continuous variables and Cramér’s V for categorical comparisons. To adjust for multiple comparisons involving biomarker expression levels across groups, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate at 5%. The study was designed as an exploratory pilot trial, and findings should be interpreted accordingly. Given the exploratory pilot nature of the study and the evaluation of multiple clinical, microbiological, and biochemical outcomes, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. This approach increases the potential risk of Type I error; therefore, statistically significant findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis, generating rather than confirmatory.

RESULTS

A total of 44 participants were enrolled, with 11 patients allocated to each group. All participants completed the study, and no dropouts were recorded. The groups were comparable at baseline with respect to age, gender distribution, and clinical parameters. The comparison of baseline OLP severity using the SLPI score between Groups 1A and 1B demonstrated no statistically significant differences at baseline (χ2 = 6.13, P = 0.167) or at day 7 (χ2 = 5.83, P = 0.786), indicating comparable OLP status between the intervention arms. No adverse effects were reported in both the groups.

Combined analysis (irrespective of oral lichen planus status)

When the data from both OLP and non-OLP participants were pooled [Table 1], PI and GI scores showed statistically significant reductions from baseline to day 7 within both intervention groups (beetroot and CHX). However, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two interventions at any time point.

Streptococcal CFU counts did not demonstrate statistically significant differences between the beetroot and CHX groups at baseline, day 1, or day 7. Similarly, no statistically significant changes were observed within either intervention group across the three time points.

Salivary NO levels demonstrated statistically significant differences between the two interventions at day 7 (P = 0.028). Salivary NO levels demonstrated divergent biochemical responses between interventions. Beetroot rinse was associated with increased NO levels at day 7, whereas CHX use was associated with a reduction. Given the pleiotropic role of NO in inflammation and host response, these findings should be interpreted as biochemical changes rather than indicators of therapeutic benefit.

VAS pain scores did not differ significantly between the two intervention groups at any time point. However, both groups showed statistically significant reductions in VAS scores from baseline to day 7.

DISCUSSION

The present pilot randomized controlled study evaluated the short-term adjunctive effects of a beetroot juice rinse following SRP in patients with gingivitis, with and without clinically diagnosed OLP. Given the chronic inflammatory nature of OLP and its frequent association with plaque-induced gingival inflammation, effective and well-tolerated plaque control strategies remain particularly relevant in this population.[11617]

The age range of 18–55 years was selected to minimize confounding influences related to systemic comorbidities, medication use, and age-related periodontal changes that could obscure short-term gingival outcomes. This restriction improves internal validity for a pilot investigation, although it limits generalizability to older populations with more complex periodontal profiles. All participants underwent SRP before randomization to standardize baseline plaque levels and ensure ethical periodontal care. While this approach ensured comparable starting conditions, it likely contributed substantially to the observed reductions in plaque and gingival indices across all the groups. Consequently, the independent adjunctive effects of the test and control rinses may have been attenuated, particularly within the short follow-up period.

CHX remains the gold standard in chemical plaque control due to its substantivity and broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity.[1819] Consistent with existing literature, both beetroot and CHX groups demonstrated significant within-group improvements in PI and GI.[202122] These findings should be interpreted primarily as the combined effect of SRP, reinforced oral hygiene practices, and short-term chemical plaque control rather than as definitive evidence of equivalence between interventions. Although beetroot contains bioactive compounds such as phenols and betalains with reported antioxidant and antimicrobial properties,[2324] the present study was not designed to establish mechanistic efficacy or long-term clinical superiority.

Microbiological analysis focused on Streptococcus spp. as a representative early colonizer and pragmatic surrogate marker in this pilot setting. While both interventions produced early reductions in Streptococcal CFU counts, sustained reductions from day 1 to day 7 were observed predominantly in the CHX groups, consistent with its well-documented substantivity.[13] In contrast, CFU levels in the beetroot groups remained relatively stable after the initial reduction, suggesting limited antimicrobial persistence. These findings support the interpretation that beetroot juice lacks substantivity comparable to CHX and should not be considered equivalent in antimicrobial durability.[252627] Freshly prepared beetroot juice was used without chemical standardization of nitrates, polyphenols, or betalains. Although preparation protocols were standardized to minimize variability, batch-to-batch differences in bioactive compound concentration cannot be excluded.[2829] Future studies employing chemically characterized and standardized formulations are necessary to improve reproducibility and dose–response interpretation.

Salivary nitrate/nitrite levels were assessed as exploratory biochemical markers of the dietary nitrate–nitrite–NO pathway. Beetroot use was associated with increased salivary nitrate/NO levels in some subgroups, whereas CHX use was associated with reductions, likely reflecting CHX-mediated suppression of nitrate-reducing oral bacteria.[30] However, NO is a pleiotropic mediator, and its elevation may reflect either physiological host-modulatory activity or inflammatory upregulation, depending on the context.[3132]

This study has several important limitations. The short duration (7 days), small sample size, lack of participant and assessor blinding, and once-daily rinse regimen limit the strength of inferences that can be drawn. The clinical diagnosis of OLP without routine histopathological confirmation may introduce the misclassification bias. In addition, subjective outcomes such as VAS are susceptible to performance and detection bias. Multiple outcomes were assessed without formal adjustment for multiple comparisons, increasing the risk of Type I error.

CONCLUSION

Within the limitations of this short-term, pilot, randomized controlled trial, both beetroot juice rinse and CHX demonstrated short-term improvements in plaque and gingival indices following SRP in patients with gingivitis, with and without OLP. CHX remains the reference standard due to its established antimicrobial substantivity and broader evidence base.

Beetroot juice exhibited transient antimicrobial effects and distinct biochemical changes in salivary nitrate/NO levels; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, given the complex biological role of NO and the absence of chemical standardization and long-term follow-up. While beetroot may offer advantages in terms of tolerability and natural origin, its antimicrobial activity and clinical reliability remain inferior to those of CHX, according to the present data.

Larger, longer-term, adequately powered studies using standardized beetroot formulations, comprehensive microbial profiling, and robust blinding protocols are required before beetroot-based rinses can be recommended as reliable adjuncts to conventional periodontal therapy. At present, CHX remains the gold standard for chemical plaque control, particularly in patients requiring sustained antimicrobial efficacy.
