Macro-kinetics of amino-hydroxyurea decomposition by NO2 and O2 in a nitric acid solution
Authors: Qinghong Liu, Libin Yang, Zhiwei Li, Zhiying Feng, Yan Ding, Shuai Wu, Liang Zhu, Dianying Zhao, Songtao Xiao, Xiechun Liu, Yinggen Ou Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Amino-hydroxyurea (HSC) has shown excellent performance and application prospects in spent fuel reprocessing. However, its residue affects the subsequent separation process. Introducing the homologous gases NO2 and O2 is an effective way to solve this problem. A macroscopic reaction kinetic model for the gas–liquid reaction was established based on the film-penetration theory. By fitting the experimental data to the model, key parameters such as the reaction rate constant k1 and the mass transfer constant of NO2kNO2,G were obtained. Similarly, the characteristic parameters of the gas–liquid reaction, such as the Hatta number γ, the enhancement factor E, and the penetration time θ, were analyzed to characterize the gas–liquid reaction process and the intensity of the absorption of reaction gases NO2 and O2 in the liquid phase.

Introduction

Environmental protection and renewable energy are two of the most important issues in the world today. Nuclear energy is a powerful tool for humans to address energy depletion and unchecked environmental pollution. To better utilize nuclear energy, an effective approach is the closed-circuit nuclear fuel cycle. Spent fuel reprocessing is an important part of the closed-circuit nuclear fuel cycle, the main purpose of which is to separate and recover reusable plutonium and uranium from spent fuel. However, the process of spent fuel reprocessing involves the risk of improper handling of radioactive waste, and for the sustainable development of nuclear energy and the reduction of waste, the main reducing reagents used in the treatment of radioactive waste in recent years are hydrazine, hydroxylamine and amino-hydroxyurea. Amino-hydroxyurea (HSC), another newly designed salt-free reductant, has received widespread attention in recent years.1 As previous studies have shown,2,3 HSC exhibits an excellent anti-extraction effect for plutonium in the organic phase, and it can be potentially adopted for the separation of uranium and plutonium in the PUREX process. Xiao Songtao et al.3 studied the kinetics of the reduction reaction between amino-hydroxyurea and HNO2 in a perchloric acid medium and established its kinetic equation.3 To decompose these reductive substances without generating new reductive products, it is necessary to find a suitable homologous strong oxidant for the deep degradation of HSC and its reductive reaction products. Compared with the redox potentials of HNO3 homologous substances shown in Fig. 1, NO2 and O2 can be selected to deeply oxidize and decompose the small amount of HSC and its reductive products remaining in the reaction solution to generate CO2 and N2, which are discharged from the system.

Redox potential diagram of nitrous acid (unit: V).

Based on the film-penetration theory, the deep degradation process of the NO2/O2 mixed gas with HSC should be divided into two steps: interfacial mass transfer of the mixed gas across the gas–liquid interface and the degradation reaction in the liquid phase. The gas–liquid reaction has been extensively and deeply studied by researchers using both experimental and simulated methods. On the basis of the two-film theory, a mathematical model4–7 was developed for the steady-state gas–liquid chemical absorption processes, such as the desulfurization of flue gas in alkaline solution, the absorption of NO into the Co(en)33+ solution and the oxidation of β-isophorone by air. Brogren et al.8 developed a macroscopic reaction kinetic model for desulphurization based on the penetration theory and calculated the absorption rate of SO2 in slurry droplets using experimental data. A similar model was established that includes transient equilibrium reaction processes, such as limestone dissolution, sulfite oxidation, gypsum crystallization, and CO2 hydrolysis, as well as the finite-rate reaction processes. Cockx et al.9 and Talvy et al.10 adopted the proposed Higbie penetration model to obtain volumetric mass transfer coefficients and to describe the gas–liquid two-phase mass transfer processes. However, this model was only applicable to mass transfer phenomena around bubbles under the condition of low Reynolds numbers (bubble Re numbers much higher than 400). To modify the Higbie penetration model, Xue and Yin11 derived an expression for the mass transfer coefficient based on Boussinesq's assumptions and the Higbie penetration model. Wen et al.12 calculated the mass transfer coefficient based on Higbie's theory and Kolmogorov's anisotropic turbulence theory. Van Elk et al.13 presented the dynamic behavior of a gas–liquid tank reactor using the Higbie penetration model to describe the mass transfer phenomena accompanying the chemical reaction, as well as the dynamic gas-phase and liquid-phase component equilibria. There may be differences between the results of the stagnant film model combined with the Higbie penetration model and the implementation of the isolated Higbie penetration model in some special cases.

The current classical microscopic models of mass transfer are the two-film model, characterized by the film thickness parameter and describing mass transfer through stationary molecules in a stagnant film;14 the penetration model, in which the residence time θ of a fluid element at the interface is the key parameter;15 the surface renewal model, which introduces the probability of surface element substitution;16 and the film-penetration model, which combines the stagnant film concept of the film model with the finite contact time idea of the penetration model.17 The mass transfer process on the gas-phase side can be described by the stagnant film theory,14 regarded as an ideal model for mass transfer of reacting gases within the gas film. The fluid on the liquid-phase side is not stagnant and unchanging but is constantly renewed to contact new surfaces, and the mass transfer process on the liquid-phase side can be described by penetration theory. In 1935, Higbie proposed the penetration theory.15 The basic principle of Higbie's model of osmotic mass transfer is that the gas–liquid interface consists of a number of liquid elements that move back and forth between the interfacial region and a well-mixed bulk liquid. When the two phases come into contact, the solute in the solution continuously diffuses to the phase interface, where the liquid and gas at the interface are in a gas–liquid equilibrium. These elements then remain at the interface for a fixed period, called the contact time (θ), which is related to the diffusion coefficient and the liquid-phase mass transfer coefficient. At the end of the contact period, the element returns to the main body of the liquid phase and mixes with the rest of the fluid. Thus, at any given batch time, the gas–liquid interface is composed of liquid elements of different ages (0 < t < θ), all in equal proportions. The two-film theory is conceptually concise and simplifies the process of interphase mass transfer between two fluids into a tandem process involving stabilized molecular diffusion through two film layers. However, it is unreasonable and could introduce significant errors in unsteady-state processes. The penetration theory is more reasonable for describing unsteady-state processes.15 This study attempts to model the macroscopic reaction kinetics for the deep decomposition of amino-hydroxyurea in dilute nitric acid using the NO2/O2 mixed gas to analyze the characteristics of gas–liquid absorption and the chemical reaction in the liquid phase.

Compared with existing HSC degradation methods, such as electrochemical oxidation (which requires an external power supply and suffers from low current efficiency) and photocatalysis (which requires UV light and suffers from catalyst deactivation), the present NO2/O2 oxidation strategy offers several distinct advantages: (1) it utilizes the inherent oxidizing species (NO2 and O2) that are already present or can be easily generated in the spent fuel reprocessing system; (2) it requires no external catalysts or energy input; (3) it operates under mild conditions (ambient pressure, 298–308 K); and (4) the gaseous reactants can be continuously supplied and the final products (CO2, N2, and N2O) easily removed from the system. This study establishes a macro-kinetic model for the deep oxidative degradation of HSC in a nitric acid solution, which can provide theoretical support for the process optimization of spent fuel reprocessing.

Chemical reagents

Amino-hydroxyurea (HSC) (≥99%) was obtained from the China Academy of Atomic Energy Science. Analysis

The liquid samples were extracted from the reactor with a gas sampling needle at set intervals. Each liquid sample was taken in small quantities, about 1 to 2 mL, which did not affect the study of the reaction kinetics. The liquid samples were preprocessed with Fe(iii) ions and o-phenanthroline and analyzed using an ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer at the maximum absorption wavelength of 510 nm.

The gas samples were collected quantitatively from the reactor exhaust, treated in an NaOH solution, and immediately analyzed using an SP-3420A gas chromatograph equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and a 5A molecular sieve column (3 mm × 3 mm × 3 mm) at an inlet temperature of 393.15 K, a column temperature of 443.15 K, a detector temperature of 443.15 K, and an argon gas flow rate of 7 mL min−1.

Macroscopic reaction kinetic model

(1) The contact time of the penetration model is small compared to the liquid-phase residence time.

(2) Both the gas and liquid phases are assumed to be completely mixed.

(3) Reactions occur only in the liquid phase.

(4) Liquid-phase components are non-volatile.

Justification of the simplifications: (1) the assumption of a constant O2 concentration is reasonable because O2 is supplied in large excesses (partial pressure ca. 60 kPa), and its consumption rate is substantially lower than the supply rate under our experimental conditions. The dissolved O2 concentration is maintained at near-saturation throughout the reaction, as confirmed by the fact that the outlet gas O2 concentration remained essentially unchanged (variation <2%) in preliminary tests. (2) The side reactions, including NO2 disproportionation and the secondary oxidation of HSC intermediates, are neglected because their characteristic reaction times are at least one order of magnitude longer than the main reaction time scale under the present conditions (low NO2 partial pressure and high HNO3 acidity). This is supported by the observed linear relationship between ln (CHSC) and the reaction time during the early stage, indicating that the main reaction pathway dominates.

. Analysis of the HSC decomposition reaction characteristics

Molecular interpretation of kinetic parameter variations: the increase in kr with temperature (from 1.0067 L mol−1 s−1 at 298 K to 0.9998 at 308 K, see Table 4) can be attributed to the enhanced molecular collision frequency and the reduced activation energy barrier, as described by the Arrhenius-type temperature dependence. The increase in stirring speed, on the other hand, enhances the gas–liquid interfacial renewal rate, leading to a higher gas-phase mass transfer coefficient kNO2,G (from 1.03 × 10−4 to 1.03 × 10−4 mol m−2 s−1 Pa−1, as shown in Table 4). The decrease in kNO2,L with increasing stirring speed is due to the reduced penetration time θ, which shortens the contact duration of liquid elements at the interface and thus lowers the liquid-side mass transfer coefficient, as described by eqn (24).

In the case of gas–liquid absorption accompanied by chemical reactions, the effect of chemical reactions on the mass transfer rate is usually rationalized by an enhancement factor,21 which is defined by the ratio of the chemical absorption rate to the physical absorption rate. With E asymptotically converging to E∞, the decomposition reaction of HSC can greatly enhance the mass transfer rate of NO2, especially in the liquid phase, and it is indicated that the mass transfer rate of NO2 is limited by the gas-phase side. The higher the CHSC,L, the higher the value of γ and the greater the degree of the effect on E. Similarly, when CHSC,L gradually decreases to 0.1 mol L−1, the value of γ and the influence on E become more significant. When CHSC,L continues to decrease gradually from 0.1 mol L−1, its effect is divided into two stages: the first stage is γ > 1 and γ < E, indicating that the gas-phase mass transfer rate of NO2 is greater than the liquid-phase mass transfer rate. Further, the total mass transfer rate is controlled by the liquid-phase mass transfer. The second stage is γ ≤ 1 and γ < E, in which the intrinsic reaction rate is less than the liquid-phase mass transfer rate. Compared with the liquid-phase mass transfer rate, the gas-phase mass transfer rate of NO2 is greater, and the decomposition reaction of HSC weakly enhances the absorption of NO2 and becomes a rate-controlling step. With the value of γ being much lower than 1, the liquid-phase HSC decomposition rate is much lower than the liquid-phase mass transfer rate of NO2, and the chemical reaction has almost no effect on the absorption of NO2, regardless of the value of E∞, E ≈ 1.

In the intermittent reaction process of this study, the reaction was governed by different controlling mechanisms as the HSC concentration decreased. In the early stages of the decomposition reaction, the Hatta number and enhancement factor values were approximately equal and greater than 1, indicating minimal gas-phase resistance, a rapid intrinsic reaction rate, and an intrinsic reaction rate exceeding the liquid-phase mass transfer rate. Consequently, the macroscopic reaction rate was initially controlled by mass transfer in the liquid phase. As the HSC concentration gradually decreased, both the Hatta number and enhancement factor declined during the intermediate stage. The Hatta number became lower than the enhancement factor and approached unity, suggesting that while the intrinsic reaction rate remained higher than the liquid-phase mass transfer rate, the macroscopic reaction rate became jointly influenced by the gas–liquid mass transfer. When HSC degraded to low concentrations, the Hatta number fell below 1 and the enhancement factor continued to decrease. Under these conditions, the intrinsic reaction rate became lower than the liquid-phase mass transfer rate, resulting in a weak chemical enhancement of the overall mass transfer. At this final stage, the intrinsic chemical reaction emerged as the rate-limiting step for the macroscopic reaction process.

Another characteristic parameter, namely the penetration time θ, can also be easily obtained using eqn (22) and (25) for irreversible secondary reactions and used to judge the effect of the intrinsic reaction on the mass transfer of NO2 in the liquid phase. With the inverse relationship between the penetration time θ and the liquid-phase reaction rate, an increase in the enhancement factor can overcome the limitation of NO2 absorption in the liquid phase. With an increase in the stirring speed or temperature, the penetration time and the enhancement factor both decrease (as shown in Fig. 5). For the decomposition of HSC by NO2 and O2, increasing the stirring speed and raising the temperature enhance the reaction in the liquid phase. This indicates that the mass transfer coefficient and reaction rate coefficient kr are positively correlated with the stirring speed and temperature. However, the increase in the stirring speed and temperature has a negative effect on the absorption of NO2 and O2, i.e., kNO2,L is negatively correlated with the stirring speed and temperature. The data from Table 5 combined with eqn (22) and (25) reveal that the decrease in the mass transfer coefficient caused by the increase in temperature and stirring speed has more impact than the increase in the reaction rate, especially in the middle and late reaction stages. The decrease in the HSC concentration at the end of decomposition amplifies the decrease in the reaction rate, and the enhancement factor E decreases even more.

The negative effect of increasing temperature on NO2 and O2 absorption is attributed primarily to the reduced solubility of both gases in the liquid phase (Henry's constants increase with temperature), which lowers the equilibrium interfacial concentrations. Although the intrinsic reaction rate constants increase with temperature, the solubility effect dominates under our experimental conditions, leading to an overall decrease in absorption. Regarding the stirring speed, higher stirring speed enhances the gas–liquid interfacial renewal and increases the gas-side mass transfer coefficient kNO2,G. However, it also reduces the penetration time θ, which lowers the liquid-side mass transfer coefficient kNO2,L and thus reduces the contact time available for the chemical reaction to enhance absorption. The net effect is that the negative contribution from the reduced liquid-side mass transfer rate outweighs the positive contribution from the increased gas-side mass transfer rate.

Conclusions

The macroscopic kinetics of the deep decomposition of HSC by NO2 and O2 were investigated using a reaction kinetic model based on film-penetration theory. The characteristic parameters such as E and γ as well as kNO2,L, kNO2,G, kr and θ were obtained by fitting the experimental data to describe the HSC decomposition process by NO2 and O2. In the early stage of the decomposition, the macroscopic reaction rate is initially governed by the liquid-phase mass transfer. As the reaction progresses and the concentration of HSC gradually decreases, the macroscopic reaction rate becomes jointly influenced by the gas–liquid mass transfer. When HSC is degraded to low concentrations, the intrinsic chemical reaction emerges as the rate-limiting step in the macroscopic reaction process.

Increasing the stirring speed and the temperature enhances the reaction in the liquid phase but exerts a negative effect on the absorption of NO2 and O2, respectively. The decrease in the mass transfer coefficient caused by the increase in temperature and stirring speed is more impactful than the increase in the reaction rate. The decrease in the HSC concentration at the end of decomposition causes the enhancement factor E to decrease even more.

It should be noted that the present experimental conditions are limited to a narrow range (T = 298–308 K, stirring speed = 200–500 r min−1, fixed HNO3 concentration). While the model framework is general and can be extended to broader conditions by re-evaluating the kinetic parameters, the specific parameter values reported herein are strictly applicable under the conditions investigated. Future work will focus on validating the model under higher acidity and wider temperature ranges to further establish its generalizability.
