A Cross-sectional Observational Study to Correlate the Trichoscopic Findings of Female Pattern Hair Loss with the Disease Severity and Underlying Histopathological Changes
Authors: Shreya Singh, Meena B. Makhecha, Kinjal Deepak Rambhia
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:The increasing prevalence of female pattern hair loss (FPHL) is an alarming concern. Trichoscopy is an important bedside diagnostic tool for the diagnosis of FPHL, but there are very few studies correlating it with disease severity or histopathological findings which would provide a better insight to the treating clinician. Hence, we decided to take up this study.Aim:This study aimed to study the trichoscopic findings of FPHL and correlate their relationship with disease severity and histopathological finding in our tertiary care hospital.Materials and Methods:Ninety females attending the dermatology outpatient department who were clinically diagnosed with FPHL were included in the study after informed consent and clearance from the institutional ethics committee. This was followed by a detailed history, clinical examination, and trichoscopic assessment. A trichoscopy-guided biopsy was performed from two sites. After the data collection, the statistical software used was IBM SPSS version 21.0 and Microsoft Office Excel 2007. P < 0.05 was accepted as indicative of statistical significance.Results:Anisotrichosis, increased interfollicular distance, and decreased hairs per follicular unit were universal. Brown peripilar sign had a positive association with inflammation (P < 0.0005) and white peripilar sign with duration of disease and fibrosis whereas dartboard sign had a significant association with inflammation as well as fibrosis.Conclusion:Trichoscopy helps in diagnosing the condition, assessing the severity, and monitoring the response to treatment. A knowledge about the features underlying specific trichoscopic signs such as inflammation and fibrosis would help the clinician decide the line of management.

INTRODUCTION

A significant increase in the number of patients with complaints of excessive hair loss has been reported in recent times. Female pattern hair loss (FPHL) is an increasingly common diagnosis in such cases. The hair loss in FPHL presents in specific patterns over the scalp such as widening of the central part with little or no recession of the frontal hairline or a bitemporal recession of the hairline.

Trichoscopy is an invaluable diagnostic tool in modern-day dermatology. Its magnification provides the clinician with detailed features of the scalp, which can be missed on unaided clinical examination. We aim to study whether the trichoscopic findings of FPHL can provide a clue about the severity of the disease and the underlying histopathological changes. We believe that this knowledge will empower the treating clinician to take decisions regarding the line of management.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This was a cross-sectional, descriptive study conducted over 1 year which included 90 females attending the dermatology outpatient department who were clinically diagnosed with FPHL. Subjects were recruited after taking clearance from the institutional ethics committee and obtaining their written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were pregnancy, lactation, overlap with other forms of alopecia, treatment for hair loss in the last 6 months, medical condition or therapy known to cause hair loss, and bleeding diathesis.

The demographic details of each subject were noted, followed by a detailed history including the age of onset, duration and progression of hair loss as well as the presence of any associated scalp symptoms. Clinical examination comprised noting down the extent of widening of mid-part (any value more than 1 cm was considered significant), pattern of recession of frontal hairline, signs of scalp inflammation (erythema or scaling), pattern of hair loss-basic and specific pattern (BASP) for classification, and Sinclair scale for calculating the severity.

A hair pull test was performed by holding a bundle of approximately 50–60 hairs from the affected area of the scalp. The bundle was held between the thumb, index finger, and middle finger close to the root and was pulled with gentle traction as the fingers slid down the hair shaft. Pulling out of more than two hairs was considered a positive test according to the latest guideline by McDonald et al.[1]

Severity was calculated according to the FPHL Severity Index [Table 1].[2]

Trichoscopy was performed with a Heine Delta 20T polarized handheld dermatoscope at ×10 [Figure 1]. Features such as interfollicular distance, hair diameter diversity (HDD), predominance of single-hair pilosebaceous units, empty follicles (EFs), white peripilar sign (WPPS), brown peripilar sign (BPPS), white dots (WDs), focal atrichia (FA), more than six short vellus hairs (SVHs) in the frontal scalp, erythema, and scaling were looked for and documented.

Heine delta 20 T polarized hand-held dermoscope

A trichoscopy-guided biopsy was performed from two sites with a 4-mm disposable punch along the direction of hair follicles [Figure 2]. One of the 4-mm tissues was sectioned horizontally 1 mm below the dermoepidermal junction (at the mid-isthmus level) and the other vertically in the midline making sure that the cut is made parallel to the direction of hair growth [Figure 3]. Samples were then sent for histopathological processing. Hematoxylin and eosin (H and E) stain was used for routine staining [Figure 4], and Giemsa stain was used to demonstrate mast cells. Slides thus prepared were evaluated by a single observer at increasing magnifications – ×4, ×10, and ×40.

The vertical section was evaluated for epidermal changes, levels of the bulb of hair follicles, location and character of the inflammatory infiltrate, state of the sebaceous lobules, fibrous streamers, and perifollicular fibrosis. In the horizontal section, the observer looked for HDD and counted the total number of hairs and follicular unit (FU) as well as the number of hairs per FU. The total number of terminal and vellus hairs was calculated and their ratio was obtained. For this purpose, the intermediate hairs were categorized as vellus hairs. Follicles were classified as vellus like using the criteria established by Whiting: the width of the Inner root sheath (IRS) equals or exceeds the diameter of the hair shaft or the hair shaft diameter is <30 μm.[3] The percentage of telogen hairs was calculated out of the total number of terminal hairs. The presence of any other unusual feature was noted and documented.

RESULTS

Demographic data

The youngest patient was 17 years old and the oldest was 65 years old (mean age: 34.7 ± 1.3 years). The mean duration of the disease was found to be 3.4 ± 0.5 years, and the mean age of onset of hair loss was 31.3 ± 1.3 years.

Pattern prevalence

Widening of the mid-partition of the scalp was found in 86 (95.6%). Twenty-seven (30%) were found to have recession of the frontal hairline. According to the BASP scale, the basic pattern prevalence as found in our study was 46.7% for M type, 35.6% for L type, and 17.7% for C type. We did not record any U pattern in our study.

Scalp examination

Signs of inflammation such as scaling or erythema were found in 49 (54.4%) patients. The hair pull test was positive only in 9 (10%) patients.

Severity score

The FPHL severity score was calculated, and out of 90, only 8 (8.9%) were found to have early FPHL (score: 5–9) whereas 82 (91.1%) had established/advanced FPHL (score: ≥10).

DISCUSSION

FPHL is becoming an increasingly prevalent condition in the present times due to a shift in the patients’ profile with respect to lifestyle modifications, cosmetic habits, hormonal disorders, increase in psychological stress levels, increasing awareness, and a peer-pressure-driven compulsion to look good. The increasing prevalence can also be attributed to the recent advances in diagnostic tools and criteria. Dermatologists today rely increasingly on trichoscopic findings in an attempt to avoid a scar due to biopsy in a noncicatricial alopecia. This makes the histopathological studies in FPHL scarce as compared to the clinical and trichoscopic features. Therefore, we decided to take up this study correlating the trichoscopic and histopathological findings in the same set of patients to gain an insight over and above the existing literature.

With this in mind, 90 females with patterned hair loss were enrolled in the study and the mean age of onset of hair loss was found to be 31.3 ± 1.3 years. Our study could not prove the biphasic age of onset of FPHL as reported previously.[4] The disparity might have arisen because our study is based on a population belonging to a lower socioeconomic stratum. This explains why the dependent population of the society does not seek consultation for a cosmetic issue such as hair loss. Moreover, elderly females are not very concerned about their appearance at an age when they are already battling multiple comorbidities.

Eighty-two (91.1%) had established/advanced FPHL (score: ≥10). This reflects that by the time, patients consult a doctor; their hair loss is already progressed, which calls for immediate measures to halt the progress of miniaturization.

Trichoscopy at ×10 revealed that anisotrichosis increased interfollicular distance and decreased hairs per FU were universal. The most common findings observed were the peripilar signs – white and brown. FA, EF, SVH, and WPPS correlated with increased severity of the disease. The high specificity of predominance of single-hair pilosebaceous units in frontal area as compared to the occiput makes it a reliable criterion to diagnose FPHL before the patterned balding becomes clinically evident. BPPS might be analogous to the phenomenon of postinflammatory hyperpigmentation a finding similar to the study done by Deloche et al.[5] In a study done by Zhang et al., a significant correlation was found between WPPS with the duration (P = 0.018) and stage of hair loss (P = 0.001) of FPHL. Hence, the authors presumed that WPPS can be considered a sign of underlying perifollicular fibroplasia in long-standing cases FPHL with poor prognostic implications.[6] In accordance with this, we report that WPPS correlated positively with the duration of hair fall (P = 0.038) and was significantly associated with perifollicular fibrosis on histopathology (P = 0.03) like finding of Hu et al. suggesting that it is a marker of advanced FPHL.[7]

DS comprised a whitish perifollicular halo suggesting perifollicular fibrosis and a peripheral brown ring indicating advancing inflammation, resembling a dartboard and hence the name. Thus, this sign can be considered a marker of underlying microinflammation and consequent perifollicular fibrosis with a poor prognosis.

FA was an increasingly common trichoscopic finding in those with a long duration of hair fall or advanced severity score as proven by previous studies and hence is another trichoscopic marker of poor prognosis.[6]

The number of kenogen phases increased proportionately with the vellus hair cycles which is a marker of advanced androgenetic alopecia (AGA) as mentioned by Guarrera and Rebora.[8] The finding of numerous EFs in our patients with a severity score ≥10 emphasizes that they are trichoscopic features of an advanced stage of FPHL which is in accordance with the work of Karadağ Köse and Güleç in 2012.[9]

We reported more than six vellus hairs per 20-fold magnification field in the frontal area in 90% of the patients compared to 80% of Herskovitz et al. in 2013 who recommended its use as an additional diagnostic criterion for FPHL, especially in early cases when the irreversible changes have not set in.[10]

Honeycomb pigment pattern seen in 54.4% of our patients had a positive correlation with the duration of sun exposure (P = 0.038) as proven by previous studies.[6] It is seen due to the increased pigmentation because of direct sun exposure of the bald areas of the scalp.

Features suggestive of seborrheic dermatitis like scalp erythema and greasy yellowish scales were noted in a significant number of our subjects (28.8%). [Figure 10] This was in concordance with a study done by Torkamani et al in which they found that the miniaturization of hair in AGA is often accompanied with sebaceous gland hyperplasia, resulting in an oily scalp along with inflammation in form of seborrheic dermatitis.[11] This may also explain the presence of Demodex mite in the histopathological section in 6 patients which was associated with an increased inflammatory infiltrate [Figure 11].[12]

Histopathological analysis was done on the horizontal as well as vertical sections taken from the affected scalp. The normal telogen count should be between 0% and 15% and a count of >20% is considered abnormal as it reflects the extent of miniaturization which was found in our study.[13] Identifying many more fibrous stelae in the same specimen indicate a large proportion of follicles shifting into catagen [Figure 12] and telogen phase which is the sign of follicular miniaturization. A mild, peri-infundibular chronic inflammation is very commonly observed in normal scalp specimens; however, a dense or deep inflammation which is unexpected or unusual should be regarded as abnormal. The degree of inflammation was graded as mild (mild perivascular infiltrate), moderate (dense perivascular infiltrate), or severe (dense infiltrate of inflammatory cells that may be located in a perivascular as well as periadnexal location). Inflammation was found in 66 (73.3%) of our cases [Figure 13, Figure 14 red arrow] which agrees with the findings of Sueki et al. stating that the lymphocytic inflammatory infiltrate observed around the lower portions of the follicular infundibulum in FPHL patients is a part of the pathogenesis and not merely a co-incidence.[14]

The cases that presented with an itchy scalp had eosinophils and/or mast cells in the inflammatory infiltrate which in turn had a significant positive association with fibrosis on histopathology of the scalp (P < 0.003) which is comparable to the finding of previous studies.[15] Antihistaminics cause a significant reduction in both the inflammatory cell infiltrate and prostaglandin D2 production from mast cells that ultimately leads to eosinophil recruitment. This was proven by a study done in 2018 where 1% topical cetirizine resulted in an increase in the total hair density, terminal hair density, as well as a decrease in HDD.[16]

Perifollicular fibrosis seen in 31.1% of the subjects is in concordance with previous studies that identified androgen receptors in the interfollicular dermal fibroblasts. They discovered that collagen synthesis from fibroblasts is stimulated by androgens and transforming growth factor-β produced by T-cells, eosinophils, or mast cells [Figure 15a-c]. It was thus concluded that perifollicular fibrosis may be another pathogenetic mechanism of AGA resulting in hair follicle miniaturization rather than being a mere manifestation.[17]

Horizontal section at 40X demonstrating the character of inflammatory infiltrate: a-Eosinophils in H&E stain (arrow); b-Lymphocytes in H&E stain ; c-Mast cells in Giemsa stain (arrow)

Identifying many more fibrous stelae in the same specimen indicates a large proportion of follicles shifting into catagen and telogen phase which is the sign of follicular miniaturization.

Sebaceous glands are preserved until the very late stages, and they appear larger compared to the size of miniaturized hair follicles. However, very late in the course of AGA, the entire pilosebaceous unit diminishes with the sebaceous glands and follicles being markedly smaller than in a normal scalp.[18] They were found to be decreased in 28.9% and increased in 5.6%. The mean severity score in patients with an increase and decrease in size of sebaceous glands on histopathology was 8.6 and 12.0, respectively. This implies that the sebaceous glands are enlarged in early stages of FPHL which might be due to the hyperandrogenic state or as a result of a relative decrease in the diameter of the hair follicle. On the contrary, in the advanced stages of the disease, the size of these glands gets diminished owing to the periappendageal chronic inflammation and fibrosis. This corresponds to the findings of previous studies by Ackerman and Guo.[19]

CONCLUSION

A trichoscopic evaluation is a must for all cases presenting to dermatologists with complaints of increased hair loss. Anisotrichosis and increased number of single-hair pilosebaceous units on the frontal scalp as compared to the occipital scalp are universal findings in FPHL. These can be used to diagnose early cases which are often misdiagnosed as telogen effluvium. BPPS, perifollicular scaling, or associated seborrheic dermatitis represents an underlying inflammatory infiltrate which needs to be addressed in order to halt the progression of the disease. Hence, topical agents such as mid-potent corticosteroids, antifungals, 5% permethrin, or 2% metronidazole can be used as and when required. WPPS, DS, FA, and EF are markers of an advanced stage, and patients should be counseled not to have unrealistic expectations from the treatment modalities offered. They can be offered other modalities such as cosmetic camouflage, scalp micropigmentation, or scalp prosthesis. The fact that almost one-third of our patients with advanced FPHL showed perifollicular fibrosis in histopathology is alarming. We feel that FPHL should be considered micro-cicatricial alopecia and measures should be taken early in the disease to control the inflammation which ultimately leads to fibrosis. Newer treatment modalities targeting steps in the pathogenesis can be used more frequently in the management of this entity to provide better treatment outcomes.
