Low-molecular-weight peptides from deer antler velvet: Antioxidant potential, biosafety, and synergistic interaction with nanomelanin
Authors: Nguyen Dinh Thang, Pham Hai Long, Nguyen Thu Uyen, Tran Hoang Thuy Duong, Ngo Thi Trang, Le Thi Hong Nhung, Nguyen Lai Thanh, Nguyen Thi Bich Loan, Pham Thi Luong Hang, Vu Van In, Vu Minh Hung, Jung-Hyun Lee, Min-Sik Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deer antler velvet (DAV) is a precious pharmaceutical source. Low-molecular-weight peptides (LMWPs) extracted from DAV have been reported as promising pharmaceutical ingredients with various potential applications across different health care areas. Components, molecular weights, and bioactivities of DAV’s peptides may vary depending on the production protocols. This study optimised an enzyme-based protocol for DAV protein digestion to obtain DAV peptides (as LMWPs form) and investigated bioactivities of the LMWPs. Different enzymes as single forms and/or combined forms were applied for digestion of DAV to create DAV peptides. Then, DAV peptides were mapped by proteomics analysis using LC/MS system. It indicated that sequential treatment with pepsin and trypsin yielded a distinct pool of LMWPs of below 10 kDa. Moreover, 8288 LMWPs from 1405 DAV’s proteins digested by combined pepsin and trypsin were mapped. This LMWPs sample exhibited strong antioxidant activity in the ABTS assay, with an antioxidant potential of 1 mg/mL, corresponding to that of 1.7 mg/mL Ascorbic acid. Interestingly, the mixture of LMWPs and nano-melanin exhibited synergistic antioxidant effects, resulting in a ~2-fold increase in relative antioxidant potential. In addition, LMWPs at concentrations ranging from 5 to 1000 µg/mL exhibited very low toxicity toward STO cells, and toward zebrafish embryos, with NOEC and LOEC values estimated at 193 mg/L and 289 mg/L, respectively. This study suggests an enzymatic method to digest DAV’s proteins to extract bioactive LMWPs, and implies possible combinations of the DAV’s LMWPs and nano-melanin to develop nutritional foods and/or pharmaceutical preparations for antioxidant applications.

INTRODUCTION

Deer antler is a valuable medicinal resource containing numerous bioactive proteins and peptides.[1234] Peptides extracted from deer antler velvet (DAV) can be useful in the treatment of various diseases.[2345678910111213141516] The hydrolysis process, with the enzymes breaking down proteins into small peptides, can enhance biological effects. However, the components and bioactivities of DAV peptides may vary depending on deer species, the part of the antler velvet used, and the hydrolysis method.[1415161718] In addition, the safety and toxicity of DAV peptides have not yet been thoroughly clarified, especially regarding the organism.

Melanin is a natural polymer that could be easily extracted and purified from the ink sacs of squid and/or octopus. Melanin particles at the nanoscale have been reported as highly promising biomaterials for ultraviolet (UV) absorption, heavy metal removal, antibacterial, antioxidant, and immune-promoting properties[192021222324] and can be applied in various aspects of life science. So far, the biological activities of the combination of nanomelanin and DAVs’ peptides have not been investigated.

In this study, we focused on developing protocol to extract low-molecule-wight peptides from antler velvet of Vietnam sika deer (Cervus nippon) and investigating the bioactive of these peptides in a single form or/and in the peptides/melanin combination form in vitro and in vivo using zebrafish (Danio rerio) AB strain, which is a widely used in vivo model for toxicity assessment.[2526]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Toxicity test on the organism

Toxicity testing was performed on zebrafish embryos according to the OECD guidelines for acute toxicity testing of chemicals in fish embryos.[27] The detailed protocol for the toxicity test on zebrafish embryos is presented in Supplemental Information 1.

MTT cytotoxic assay

The mouse embryonic fibroblast STO cell line was activated and cultured for 5 passages before using it in the cytotoxicity test. The cells were cultured in DMEM medium containing FBS (10%), penicillin (1%), and incubated at 37°C, 1 atm, and 5% carbon dioxide. The cytotoxic activity was evaluated using the MTT assay KIT (Invitrogen, USA; Cat. M6494).

Nanomelanin solution making

1.0 g of melanin powder extracted from the ink sacs was dissolved in 100 mL of 0.5 M NaOH for 3 h and 24 h under gentle shaking to obtain S1 and S2 nanomelanin solutions, respectively. The nanomelanin solution was then neutralized with HCl to reach pH 7.

Total protein extraction and enzymatic proteolysis

DAV was washed with distilled water several times. DAV was spliced into 5 mm-thick pieces and ground into small particles in liquid nitrogen. 20-gram DAV was placed into 100 mL deionized water (DAV/water ratio = 1/5). Enzyme at different concentrations of 1%, 2%, 3%, and 4% (w/w) under optimized pH conditions (pH: 2 for pepsin and pH: 8 for trypsin) was added for proteolysis to produce various protein/peptide pools [Figure 1]. The protein/peptide pools were then lyophilized to create the protein/peptide powders [Figure 2]. Concentrations of peptide and protein were measured using the Bradford method.

Tricine-SDS-PAGE

In this study, low-molecular-weight peptides (LMWPs) in the peptide pools were separated on Tricine-SDS-PAGE.[28] In the Tricine-SDS-PAGE, a tricine–glycine buffer system and a high concentration of polyacrylamide of 16% were applied to create a gel with higher cross-linkers.

Proteomics analysis

Proteomic analysis underwent four steps, including (1) peptide cleanup, (2) LC-MS/MS analysis, (3) database searching,[2930] and (4) bioinformatic analysis.[3031] The detailed protocols for these four steps are presented in Supplemental Information 2.

Antioxidant assays

ABTS – 2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)[32] and ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP)[33] were used as antioxidant assays. Ascorbic acid (Vitamin C) was used to build the standard curve. The peptide samples were diluted to 0.3 mg/mL. Protocols for antioxidant assays are presented in Supplemental Information 3.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated three times (n = 3). Data were presented as mean ± SD. The SD values were interpreted as error bars on the graphs. Student’s t-test and ANOVA were applied for different comparisons between experimental groups. Significant differences with P < 0.05 (*), <0.01 (**), and < 0.001 (***) were applied.

RESULTS

Optimizing the protocol for producing low-molecular-weight peptides

Using pepsin, trypsin, and a combination of pepsin and trypsin at different concentrations and incubation times, corresponding digested peptide pools were generated at about 10 mg/mL. The peptide distribution in peptide pools was examined by SDS-PAGE (16%).

Data showed that the longer incubation time (in the range of 1–14 h) and the higher concentration of pepsin are associated with higher degradation levels of DAV’s proteins. Notably, 1% pepsin did not generate 15–17 kDa peptides [Figure 3a], whereas these bands appeared after 12 h incubation with 2% pepsin [Figure 3b], and clearly visible after 5 h with 3% pepsin [Figure 3c]. Notably, all tested concentrations (1%–3% pepsin) produced LMWPs in the range of below 10–11 kDa [Figure 3a-c].

Total DAV proteins were digested by pepsin at different concentrations of 1% (a), 2% (b), and 3% (c) with various incubation times. Lane 1: 1 h; Lane 2: 3 h; Lane 3: 5 h; Lane 4: 7 h; Lane 5: 8.5 h; Lane 6: 10 h; Lane 7: 12 h; Lane 8: 14 h; M: protein marker

In another way, DAV’s proteins were digested with trypsin at different concentrations of 1%, 2%, 3%, and 4% for 1 h. At a concentration of 1%, although trypsin could degrade proteins into smaller fragments, a high percentage of proteins/peptides remained with high molecular weights [Figure 4a, lane 1]. Remarkably, trypsin at higher concentrations of 2%, 3%, and 4% efficiently degraded DAV’s proteins to create LMWPs (below 10 kDa). However, clear bands at 63–65 kDa were still observed in 2%–4% pepsin treatments [Figure 4a, lanes 2, 3, and 4, respectively].

Pepsin 3% + trypsin 2% for 15, 30, 45, and 60 min (Lanes: 1–4, respectively); and pepsin 3% + trypsin 3% for 15, 30, 45, and 60 min (Lanes: 5–8, respectively) (b). Interestingly, it revealed that the two-enzyme combination treatment produced LMWPs distributed in the region of below 10–11 kDa, with no bigger band appearing on the gel [Figure 4b]. Importantly, the 63–65 kDa bands that appeared in the case of trypsin-only treatment also vanished in the combination treatment.

Based on these results, three peptide pools were selected to use for further experiments including the following: (i) pool 1 containing peptides/proteins after treatment with pepsin 3% for 12 h (named as pepsin-treated peptides); (ii) pool 2 containing peptides/proteins after treatment with trypsin 2% for 1 h (named as trypsin-treated peptides); and (iii) pool 3 containing peptides/proteins after treatment with pepsin 3% for 12 h and then with trypsin 2% for 1 h (named as pepsin/trypsin-treated peptides). The total peptide/protein concentrations in these three pools were 9.366 mg/mL, 11.05 mg/mL, and 9.662 mg/mL, respectively.

Proteomics analysis of Vietnamese deer antler velvet

Using the proteomic sequence assembled by Wang et al.,[30] we mapped 14939, 5773, and 8288 LMWPs from the pepsin-treated, trypsin-treated, and pepsin/trypsin-treated peptide pools, respectively [Figure 5a]. The number of proteins identified from pepsin-digested samples was 1852, while it was 1749 proteins from trypsin-digested samples and 1405 from the samples digested with pepsin and trypsin [Figure 5b]. Moreover, 820 shared proteins among all these three conditions in a total of 2642 (184 + 133 + 820 + 268 + 473 + 323 + 441) proteins in the DVA’s sample were identified [Figure 6a]. To better understand the characteristics of proteins identified from antlers, we performed gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis across GO:BP, GO:CC, and GO:MF. GO:BP showed that the most enriched terms were associated with metabolic processes and proteolysis [Figure 6b]. GO:CC enrichment identified several proteins that belonged to the proteasome core complex, intermediate filament, collagen trimer, microtubule, and clathrin adaptor complex [Figure 6c]. Finally, GO:MF showed major functions related to essential life activities such as GTP binding, catalytic activity, nucleic acid binding, and oxidoreductase activity, which were shown to play a vital role in antler development [Figure 6d].[34]

UV absorption and antioxidant properties of nanomelanin

Nanomelanin solutions were created under different conditions. After incubation for 3 h and 24 h, nanomelanin solution S1 [Figure 7a] and S2 [Figure 7b] were created from melanin powder [Figure 7c]. The concentration of nanomelanin in S1 and S2 solutions was measured by spectrophotometry at 420 nm and was found to be 0.5 mg/mL and 3.2 mg/mL, respectively. The S2 solution was then lyophilized to make nanomelanin powder [Figure 7d]. The sizes of the nanomelanin particles in the S1 [Figure 7e] and S2 [Figure 7f] solutions were distributed in the range of 400–500 nm. Nanomelanin S1 [Figure 7g] and S2 [Figure 7h] solutions exhibited impressive UV-absorption capacities in a concentration-dependent manner. Remarkably, nanomelanin S2 solution exhibits quite high antioxidant activity on ABTS, 3.2 mg/mL equivalent to that of ascorbic acid (1.0 mg/mL) [Figure 7i] to inhibit 92% activity of ABTS [Figure 7j]. Based on this result, the S2 nanomelanin sample was used in subsequent experiments.

Antioxidant synergistic effect of nanomelanin and deer antler velvet’s peptides

Two antioxidant tests, the ABTS and FRAP assays, with ascorbic acid (Vitamin C) as a positive control, and results were expressed as ascorbic acid-equivalent antioxidant capacity [Supplementary Figure 1 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2]. In general, the higher the enzyme concentration and the longer the incubation time, the greater the antioxidant potential of DAV’s peptides. The data also showed that the antioxidant activity of trypsin-treated peptides was approximately 1.5-fold higher than that of pepsin-treated peptides in both the ABTS and FRAP methods. Interestingly, all peptide preparations showed much stronger antioxidant activity in the ABTS assay than in the FRAP assay. Particularly, on ABTS, 1 mg/mL pepsin-treated peptides, 1 mg/mL trypsin-treated peptides, and 1 mg/mL pepsin/trypsin-treated peptides were equivalent to the antioxidant activities of 1.8 mg/mL, 1.2 mg/mL, and 1.7 mg/mL ascorbic acid, respectively. On FRAP, 1 mg/mL pepsin-treated peptides, 1 mg/mL trypsin-treated peptides, and 1 mg/mL pepsin/trypsin-treated peptides were equivalent with antioxidant activities of 0.12 mg/mL, 0.08 mg/mL, and 0.10 mg/mL ascorbic acid, respectively.

Antioxidant activities FRAP of DAV’s peptides under various treated conditions using FRAP method

Impressively, mixtures of micro/nanomelanin with pepsin-treated peptides (named as MEL + P), or with trypsin-treated peptides (named as MEL + T), or with pepsin/trypsin-treated peptides (named as MEL + P/T) exhibited synergistic antioxidant effects, about a two-fold increase in antioxidant potential on ABTS. In contrast, the mixture of P and T did not have such an effect [Figure 8a]. On the other hand, both pepsin-treated peptides and trypsin-treated peptides showed about 10-fold lower antioxidant activities on FRAP compared with those on ABTS. Nevertheless, the antioxidant ability of nanomelanin on FRAP was only slightly lower than that on ABTS [Supplementary Tables 1 and 2]. Interestingly, the mixtures – MEL + T, MEL + P, and MEL + P/T also showed synergistic antioxidant effects in the FRAP assay, whereas the P + T mixture did not [Figure 8b].

Cytotoxicity of deer antler velvet’s peptides on STO cells

The MTT assay was used to assess cytotoxicity in STO cells. It revealed that pepsin-treated peptides [Figure 9a], trypsin-treated peptides [Figure 9b], and pepsin/trypsin-treated peptides [Figure 9c] exhibited very low toxicity to STO cultured cells. In the concentration range of 5–1000 µg/mL, none of the DAV’s peptide samples exhibited toxicity on the cells. The appearances of STO cells treated with pepsin/trypsin-treated peptide at various concentrations of 0 µg/mL [Figure 9d], 125 µg/mL [Figure 9e], 250 µg/mL [Figure 9f], 500 µg/mL [Figure 9g], and 1000 µg/mL [Figure 9h] were shown with normal morphology and uniform development on the culture dish.

Toxicity deer antler velvet’s peptides on zebrafish embryos

The effect of DAV’s peptides on the mortality of embryos depended on the exposure time. Particularly, DAV’s peptides at concentrations below 193 mg/L were safe to embryos without inducing death, malformation, and developmental rates of embryos [Figure 10a-c]. Normally, embryos started hatching at 48 h, and the hatching rate increased until 96 h. It also revealed that DAV’s peptides just affected the hatching rate of zebrafish embryos at a high concentration of 289 mg/L [Figure 10c]. The morphologies of the zebrafish embryos cultured in the media supplemented with DAV’s peptides at different concentrations were also observed [Figure 10d]. The results demonstrated that the malformation just occurred on the embryos treated with DAV’s peptide at a high concentration of 289 mg/L, with signs of abnormalities including spinal curvature and pericardial edema [Figure 10d]. The estimated NOEC and LOEC thresholds were 193 mg/L and 289 mg/L, respectively.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we used antler velvet from Vietnamese sika deer to extract peptides with pepsin, trypsin, and a pepsin-trypsin combination. GO data analysis provided not only general information about DAV’s protein composition but also specific information about sequences, molecular weights, and distributions of LMWPs extracted from DAV’s proteins by different methods. Our results demonstrated that the combined pepsin/trypsin had high efficiency in producing LMWPs under 10 kDa [Figure 3b]. Mapping the peptides from DAV’s peptides sample [Figure 4a] showed the number of LMWPs in pepsin-treated DAV’s samples to be 3-fold and 2-fold higher than those in trypsin-treated and pepsin/trypsin-treated DAV’s samples [Figure 4a]. On the other hand, the number of peptides in the pepsin-treated samples (1852) was almost equal to that in the trypsin-treated sample (1749) and about 1.3-fold higher than that in the pepsin/trypsin-treated samples (1405) [Figure 4b]. These results indicated that pepsin had more degrading sites on polypeptide chains compared with trypsin.

The peptides in the combined pepsin/trypsin-treated peptides pool and almost all the peptides in trypsin-treated peptide pools were LMWPs, while the percentage of LMWPs in the pepsin-treated peptide pool was low. These results might explain the greater antioxidant activity of the combined pepsin/trypsin-treated peptides and trypsin-treated peptides compared with pepsin-treated peptides. This result was consistent with those reported in previous studies.[1235] Practically, DAV’s peptides exhibited strong antioxidant potential on ABTS but low antioxidant potential on FRAP [Supplementary Tables 1 and 2]. This difference can be explained by differences in the mechanisms underlying antioxidant activity in the ABTS and FRAP assays. ABTS is used for radical scavenging, while FRAP is used for metal ion reduction. Interestingly, the antioxidant potentials of DAV’s peptides were promoted in the presence of nanomelanin [Figure 8]. In addition, it revealed that DAV’s peptides exhibited very low toxicities on cultured cells (STO cells) [Figure 9] and on zebrafish embryos [Figure 10].

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, a pool of LMWPs with a molecular weight under 10 kDa was produced from DAV using a combination of proteolytic enzymes of pepsin and trypsin. The obtained results suggest possible combinations of LMWPs and nanomelanin to develop various types of functional foods and pharmaceutical preparations with high antioxidant potential to reduce the effect of reactive oxygen species, slowing the aging process, and inhibiting the development of various diseases.
