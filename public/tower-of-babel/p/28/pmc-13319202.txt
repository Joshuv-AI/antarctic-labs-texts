Dynamics of IDH1/2 mutated clones in acute myeloid leukemia challenge their use as universal MRD markers
Authors: Giovanni Marconi, Lorenza Bandini, Agnese Patuelli, Valentina Robustelli, Claudia Venturi, Giorgia Simonetti, Jacopo Nanni, Gianluca Cristiano, Chiara Sartor, Michela Rondoni, Cristina Papayannidis, Francesco Lanza, Emanuela Ottaviani, Antonio Curti
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The interpretation of persistent IDH1/2 mutations in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) remains a matter of debate, particularly in the context of measurable residual disease (MRD) assessment. While IDH1 and IDH2 mutations are well-established therapeutic targets and are readily trackable with high analytical sensitivity, their biological position within clonal hierarchies raises uncertainty about whether their persistence reflects residual leukemia or survival of pre-leukemic clones [1–3]. This distinction has direct clinical implications, as misclassification of IDH1/2 persistence may lead to inappropriate therapeutic escalation or misinterpretation of remission depth.

To address this issue, we performed a longitudinal analysis of IDH1/2 mutational burden using droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), a highly sensitive and quantitative technique, across multiple disease stages in patients with IDH1/2-mutated AML; ddPCR assays were optimized and validated for each IDH1/2 variant analyzed, demonstrating excellent reproducibility (median precision 99.1%) and a practical sensitivity limit of 0.2% fractional abundance (FA). This sensitivity allowed reliable detection of low-level mutant alleles well below the threshold of conventional sequencing (Supplementary S1). Our aim was not to re-assess IDH1/2 as a universal MRD marker, but to provides a practical, biology-oriented interpretation of serial IDH1/2 results across different disease stages and treatment contexts, including a blast/IDH FA ratio to help distinguish leukemia-related persistence from CH-like persistence. Experiments were conducted according to relevant regulations; ethical approval for sample collection and analysis was obtained for all the participants in the ethical committee of Bologna (approval code: 012/2009/U/Tess, 01/2011/U/Tess, 10/2011/U/Tess and 253/2013/O/Tess).

We analyzed 106 serial bone marrow samples obtained from 23 adult patients with IDH1 or IDH2-mutated AML from a pool of 84 consecutive cases (consort diagram is shown in Supplementary S2, patients characteristics are summarized in Table 1). Samples were collected at diagnosis, during complete remission (CR), and at relapse or stable disease, with longitudinal sampling available for most patients and a median follow-up of 24 months (Supplementary S3).

At diagnosis, IDH1/2 mutations were detected in all evaluable samples and typically displayed high FA values (median 45.3%), consistent with their presence in founding or dominant leukemic clones. Similarly, at relapses or disease persistence, most samples showed FA values above 20%, reflecting re-expansion of the leukemic compartment (Fig. 1A). These findings confirm that IDH1/2 mutations robustly track overt disease burden at active disease stages, confirming previous reports [4, 5].

In contrast, the behavior of IDH1/2 mutations during complete remission was markedly heterogeneous. While most CR samples showed very low or undetectable IDH1/2 FA, a distinct subset retained high FA values comparable to those observed at diagnosis, despite the absence of morphologic disease. Unsupervised clustering revealed a bimodal distribution, separating patients with near-complete clearance of IDH1/2 mutations from those with persistent high-level positivity (Fig. 1 B).

Consistent with these observations, IDH1/2 positivity detected by ddPCR did not predict clinical outcome in our cohort. Importantly, this persistence was not associated with imminent relapses, inferior overall survival, or shorter remission duration (Supplementary S4), and we documented both cases of patients with stable CR despite high IDH1/2 FA and cases of sudden relapse after undetectable IDH1/2 (Fig. 1C). These data add to the ongoing debate on the prognostic value of persistent IDH1/2 mutations, as previous studies have yielded heterogeneous results [6–9]. In a recent large trial-based cohort of intensively treated IDH-mutated AML, Boertjes et al. reported that persistent IDH2, but not persistent IDH1, in complete remission was associated with increased relapse risk, particularly in patients without concomitant NPM1 mutations or FLT3-ITD [9]. These findings suggest that the utility of IDH-based MRD is context-dependent rather than uniform across all IDH-mutated AML. Our study addresses a different and complementary question: how serial IDH1/2 measurements obtained by ddPCR across diagnosis, remission, relapse, and different treatment contexts should be interpreted in routine practice. Rather than focusing only on relapse prediction at a remission landmark, we examined longitudinal clonal kinetics, concordance with an orthogonal MRD marker, and a practical marrow blast/IDH FA ratio that may help distinguish leukemia-related persistence from clonal hematopoiesis-like persistence. Notably, this work is intended to refine interpretation of persistent IDH1/2 positivity rather than to propose IDH1/2 as stand-alone universal MRD markers.

Furthermore, in patients harboring concurrent NPM1 mutations—a validated molecular MRD marker—IDH1/2 FA showed poor concordance with NPM1-based MRD status. Regardless of the FA threshold applied, IDH1/2 positivity failed to reliably identify true MRD-positive cases when NPM1 was used as the reference standard, underscoring the limited specificity of IDH1/2 persistence for residual leukemia (Fig. 1D, E). These findings support the concept that IDH1/2 mutations frequently persist in pre-leukemic or clonal hematopoiesis (CH) compartments [10–13], and strengthen the evidence of limited concordance already reported [9, 14]. This interpretation is biologically plausible, as IDH1/2 mutations can arise early during leukemogenesis and may be retained in hematopoietic stem or progenitor cells that survive cytotoxic therapy [5, 8, 15]. In this context, persistence of IDH1/2 mutations reflects clonal ancestry rather than active leukemia, explaining why high FA values may coexist with durable clinical remission.

Very low c values (<0.05) indicate disproportionately high IDH1/2 burden relative to blast count, consistent with CH-like persistence. Conversely, very high c values (>20) suggest subclonal IDH1/2 involvement that does not represent the bulk leukemia population (S5). Applied retrospectively, this approach helped identify patients with lifelong CH-like IDH1/2 persistence as well as rare cases of subclonal IDH1/2 mutations. While heuristic and requiring external validation, this ratio provides a pragmatic framework to contextualize IDH1/2 results in routine practice and can be translated to other mutations.

In our set, treatment modality further influenced IDH1/2 mutation kinetics. In patients achieving remission after intensive chemotherapy, leukemia-related IDH1/2 mutations typically cleared rapidly, mirroring the cytotoxic eradication of the dominant clone. In contrast, in patients treated with IDH inhibitors, mutation clearance was often delayed, even in the presence of clinical and morphologic remission (Supplementary S6). This pattern is consistent with differentiation-driven responses and metabolic reprogramming rather than immediate clonal elimination. These observations emphasize that the depth and timing of molecular clearance must be interpreted in the context of treatment mechanism and should not be uniformly equated with treatment failure. After allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation, all evaluable patients became IDH1/2-negative at early post-transplant timepoints, although relapse still occurred in some cases [16]. This highlights that the biological meaning of IDH1/2 persistence differs substantially in the post-transplant setting, where donor hematopoiesis, immune-mediated graft-versus-leukemia effects, and clonal selection reshape disease dynamics. Accordingly, our interpretive framework was not intended to be applied after transplantation.

Taking together, our data reinforce current European LeukemiaNet MRD recommendations, which emphasize that not all somatic mutations carry equal MRD informativeness [17]. While ddPCR is a powerful tool for sensitive detection of IDH1/2 mutations, persistence of these mutations alone should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of residual leukemia. Instead, IDH1/2 mutations should be viewed as biological reporters whose significance depends on clonal hierarchy, co-mutation pattern, blast burden, and treatment context.

From a clinical standpoint, these findings argue against using IDH1/2 positivity as a stand-alone trigger for therapeutic intervention in remission. Persistent high-level IDH1/2 positivity in the absence of other MRD signals should prompt cautious interpretation rather than automatic treatment escalation. Conversely, rapid clearance of IDH1/2 mutations in selected contexts may reflect effective targeting of leukemic clones but should be confirmed with orthogonal MRD methods.

In conclusion, IDH1/2 mutations are excellent therapeutic targets and valuable tools for tracking clonal architecture in AML, but they should not be considered universal or stand-alone MRD markers. A context-aware, biology-informed approach integrating molecular, morphologic, and treatment-related variables is essential to avoid misinterpretation. Prospective studies incorporating standardized MRD frameworks and treatment-specific kinetics will be required to define when, and in whom, IDH1/2-guided monitoring can safely inform clinical decision-making.
