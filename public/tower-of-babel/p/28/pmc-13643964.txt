Quadriceps recovery after subvastus total knee arthroplasty in valgus deformity: a systematic review and pooled descriptive analysis
Authors: Hifza Babar, Yu Zhang, Qiangqiang Li, Dongyang Chen, Zezhang Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe subvastus approach preserves the extensor mechanism, but its efficacy in total knee arthroplasty (TKA) for valgus deformity remains unclear. This systematic review synthesizes available evidence on quadriceps recovery and clinical outcomes after subvastus TKA specifically in patients with valgus deformity.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Embase (2000–2025) was performed. Studies reporting outcomes of the subvastus approach in valgus TKA were included. The primary outcome was time to straight leg raise (SLR). Secondary outcomes included functional scores, range of motion (ROM), pain (VAS), and complications. Due to single-arm designs and high heterogeneity, a formal meta-analysis was not appropriate; results are presented descriptively with pooled means for illustrative purposes only.ResultsThree retrospective studies (286 valgus knees) were included. Time to SLR ranged from 0.92 to 2.0 days across studies (pooled descriptive mean: 1.35 days; I2 = 99%). Functional scores improved substantially (e.g., American Knee Society Score: 39 to 91; Hospital for Special Surgery Score: 35 to 93). Postoperative ROM gains ranged from 9° to 27° across studies, with final flexion exceeding 109° in all cohorts. Postoperative rest pain scores ranged from 2.3 to 4.6; walking pain was reported as 5.2 in one study. Complication rates were low, with two revisions (0.7%) and one transient nerve palsy (0.3%) among 286 knees. No approach-related major complications were reported.ConclusionThe subvastus approach appears viable for selected valgus knees, associated with rapid quadriceps recovery, functional improvement, and low complications within the available evidence. However, these findings derive from only three retrospective, single-arm studies with substantial heterogeneity. High-quality prospective comparative studies are required.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13018-026-07012-x.

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is one of the most successful surgical procedures for end-stage knee osteoarthritis, providing reliable pain relief and functional restoration. Early postoperative recovery, particularly restoration of quadriceps muscle function, remains a critical determinant of patient satisfaction and rehabilitation success [8, 16]. The surgical approach plays a fundamental role in quadriceps recovery. The standard medial parapatellar approach provides excellent exposure but violates the quadriceps tendon and disrupts the extensor mechanism [4]. This disruption can lead to increased postoperative pain, delayed return of straight leg raise (SLR), and potential long-term quadriceps weakness [3, 19, 20]. In contrast, the subvastus approach preserves the entire extensor mechanism by elevating the vastus medialis without violating the quadriceps tendon. However, this approach offers less predictable exposure and has historically been considered technically demanding in patients with significant valgus deformity [4]. The subvastus approach has been associated with faster SLR, earlier ambulation, reduced pain, and shorter hospital stay in TKA for varus or mixed deformities, but its efficacy in valgus knees remains less defined [2, 6, 17, 22, 23, 27, 29, 33].

Valgus knees account for approximately 10–15% of TKA patients and present distinct surgical challenges due to lateral femoral condyle hypoplasia, lateral soft tissue contracture, and, in some cases, attenuation of medial stabilizers [1, 14, 15, 35]. These pathoanatomical differences complicate soft tissue balancing and have led some surgeons to prefer lateral approaches or more constrained implants [7, 28]. Consequently, the subvastus approach is often considered technically demanding in this population [4]. Although several meta-analyses have compared the subvastus and medial parapatellar approaches in general TKA populations [12, 22, 25, 26, 31, 36], none have specifically examined outcomes in valgus knees. Even recent network meta-analyses [8, 18, 28] and a meta-analysis comparing lateral versus medial approaches for valgus TKA did not isolate the subvastus approach or report quadriceps recovery outcomes such as time to SLR [37].

Only three studies have specifically evaluated the subvastus approach in valgus knees, highlighting a critical gap in the literature. Understanding quadriceps recovery (measured by time to SLR), functional improvement, and pain control is particularly important in valgus knees, where lateral soft tissue releases and altered biomechanics can impact rehabilitation [13, 14]. Therefore, the aim of this systematic review was to evaluate quadriceps recovery and clinical outcomes after subvastus TKA specifically in patients with valgus deformity by synthesizing evidence on time to SLR, functional scores, range of motion, postoperative pain, and complications.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. A systematic literature search was performed across PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Embase databases for studies published from January 1, 2000, to December 30, 2025. The final search was executed on December 1, 2025. The following search strategy was employed using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keywords: (“subvastus” OR “quadriceps sparing” OR “far medial” OR “mini-subvastus” OR “SMOC”) AND (“valgus” OR “deformity”) AND (“total knee arthroplasty” OR “total knee replacement”). The search was restricted to English-language articles. Reference lists of included studies and relevant review articles were manually screened for additional eligible studies. The full search strings for each database are provided in Supplementary Appendix 1. The review protocol was not registered.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were eligible if they included patients undergoing primary TKA for degenerative conditions (osteoarthritis, post-traumatic arthritis, or rheumatoid arthritis) with valgus knees included either as the primary cohort or as an identifiable subgroup with separately extractable data. Utilized the medial subvastus approach (including mini-subvastus, SMOC, far medial, or minimally invasive variants). Reported at least one outcome of interest (time to straight leg raise [SLR], functional scores, pain, range of motion, or complications). Were of randomized controlled trial, prospective cohort, retrospective comparative, or case series design with at least 20 knees, and were full-text articles published in English.

Studies were excluded if they involved revision arthroplasty, unicompartmental, or patellofemoral procedures. Utilized lateral subvastus, midvastus, or standard medial parapatellar approaches without a separate subvastus valgus subgroup. Lacked original data or had insufficient outcome reporting. Were case reports, conference abstracts, cadaveric studies, or technical notes. Had follow-up less than three months or represented overlapping cohort publications (in which case only the most comprehensive study was included).

Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, then full texts, against the eligibility criteria. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. The study selection process is shown in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

A standardized data extraction form was used to collect: study characteristics (authors, year, design, sample size, follow-up); patient demographics (age, sex, BMI); surgical details (approach type, implant, release techniques); and outcome data (time to SLR, VAS pain scores, functional scores, ROM, and complications). Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved by consensus.

Risk of bias assessment

Methodological quality of the included non-randomized studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) adapted for single-arm cohorts. Studies were evaluated across two domains: selection (maximum 4 stars) and outcome (maximum 3 stars), for a total possible score of 7 stars. The comparability domain was not applicable due to the absence of control groups. Scores of 0–2, 3–4, and 5–7 were considered low, moderate, and high quality, respectively. Two reviewers independently performed assessments, disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Statistical analysis and data synthesis

Because all included studies were single-arm (without internal comparators for the subvastus approach in valgus knees) and significant clinical heterogeneity was anticipated, a formal comparative meta-analysis was not appropriate. For the primary outcome (time to SLR), we calculated a pooled mean with 95% confidence interval using a random-effects model for descriptive purposes only, recognizing that this does not represent a comparative effect estimate. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic (I2 > 75% considered high). For all secondary outcomes (functional scores, ROM, pain, complications), narrative synthesis was performed due to variation in outcome measures and reporting time-points. Sensitivity analysis using a leave-one-out approach was planned to assess the influence of individual studies on the pooled SLR estimate. Publication bias was not assessed due to the small number of studies (< 10), as funnel plot asymmetry is unreliable in this context.

Results

The systematic search yielded a total of 147 records across PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Embase. After removal of duplicates, 106 unique records remained for screening. Of these, 89 records were excluded based on title and abstract screening because they did not address the subvastus approach in valgus knees, were review articles, or were cadaveric studies. Full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Fourteen articles were excluded for the following reasons: mixed cohorts without separate valgus data, wrong surgical approach, insufficient outcome reporting, and follow-up less than three months. Three retrospective studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis.

Study characteristics

The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1. Li et al. evaluated 118 valgus knees (82 females, 30 males; mean age 63.2 ± 7.8 years; mean BMI 24.1 ± 5.6 kg/m2) utilizing the SMOC (Subvastus with Minimal Oblique Cut) modification of the subvastus approach, with a follow-up of 12 months [9]. Koninckx et al. included 84 valgus knees (58 females, 20 males; mean age 67 ± 5.5 years; mean BMI 30 ± 5.1 kg/m2) using a far medial subvastus approach, with a mean follow-up of 24 months (range 14–46 months) [10]. Shah et al. reported on 110 valgus knees (91 females, 17 males; mean age 65.1 ± 10.9 years; mean BMI 30.1 ± 5.7 kg/m2) using a mini-subvastus approach, with a mean follow-up of 40 months (range 24–84 months) [11]. All three studies reported time to straight leg raise (SLR) as a key measure of quadriceps recovery, along with postoperative pain scores, functional outcome measures, and complication data.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the three included retrospective studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) adapted for single-arm cohorts (maximum 7 stars). All three studies were of moderate study-level quality, each receiving 6 out of 7 stars (Table 2). Common strengths included consecutive patient enrollment, clear documentation of the subvastus surgical approach, confirmed preoperative valgus deformity, use of validated outcome measures, adequate follow-up duration (12 to 40 months), and high follow-up rates exceeding 94%. The primary limitation was the absence of a comparison group in all studies (i.e., single-arm designs without control cohorts for the subvastus approach in valgus knees), which precludes definitive causal attribution of outcomes to the surgical approach itself.

Time to straight leg raise

Time to SLR ranged from 0.92 to 2.0 days across the three studies (286 valgus knees). A pooled descriptive mean of 1.35 days (95% CI: 0.62–2.07 days) was calculated for illustrative purposes, but significant heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 99%, p < 0.01), reflecting differences in surgical technique (mini-subvastus, SMOC, far medial), patient populations (BMI range 24.1–30.1 kg/m2), and rehabilitation protocols. Despite this heterogeneity, all three studies reported mean SLR times within 48 h postoperatively. Sensitivity analysis using a leave-one-out approach confirmed that no single study disproportionately influenced the pooled estimate. Publication bias was not assessed due to the small number of studies (< 10).

Early postoperative pain (visual analog scale)

All three studies reported postoperative pain scores using the Visual Analog Scale, though with different timing and conditions (Table 3). Shah and colleagues reported rest pain of 2.73 ± 0.67 on postoperative day one, decreasing to 2.39 ± 1.11 on postoperative day two [11]. Li and colleagues reported 24-hour pain scores of 4.62 ± 1.06 [9]. Koninckx and colleagues reported mean rest pain of 2.3 ± 2.2 over the first three postoperative days and walking pain of 5.2 ± 1.6 [10]. Across all studies, mean rest pain values ranged from 2.3 to 4.6, with walking pain reported as 5.2 in one study [10].

Functional outcome scores

All three studies reported substantial improvements in functional outcome scores from preoperative to final follow-up (Table 4). Li and colleagues reported Hospital for Special Surgery score improvement from 35.3 preoperatively to 92.7 at 12 months [9]. Koninckx and colleagues demonstrated Knee Society Score improvement from 45 to 90 and functional score improvement from 35 to 85 at 24 months [10]. Shah and colleagues documented improvement in American Knee Society Score from 39 preoperatively to 91 at final follow-up, with functional scores improving from 36 to 79 [11].

Range of motion

All three studies reported improvements in range of motion postoperatively. Li and colleagues reported improvement from 100.2° preoperatively to 109.2° at 12 months, a gain of 9° [9]. Koninckx and colleagues demonstrated the largest improvement, from 110° preoperatively to 137° at 24 months, a gain of 27° [10]. Shah and colleagues documented improvement from 102° preoperatively to 119° at final follow-up, a gain of 17° [11]. Final range of motion exceeded 109° in all three studies.

Complication rates

Complication rates were low across all three studies, with no approach-related major complications reported in the subvastus cohorts. Among 286 valgus knees, two revisions (0.7%) were required, both reported by Shah and colleagues. One transient common peroneal nerve palsy (0.3%) was reported by Shah and colleagues and resolved completely within six weeks [11]. Notably, Li and colleagues reported five cases of patellar subluxation, but these occurred exclusively in the medial parapatellar comparison group rather than the subvastus cohort [9]. No deep infections, wound healing problems, or patellar complications were reported in any subvastus cohort.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, performed by sequentially omitting each study, showed that no single study disproportionately influenced the pooled estimate for time to straight leg raise. The pooled mean time to SLR ranged from 1.02 to 1.56 days depending on which study was excluded, remaining consistent with the overall descriptive mean of 1.35 days. Heterogeneity remained high (I2 ranging from 88% to 96% depending on which study was omitted), consistent with the original pooled analysis (I2 = 99%). This persistent heterogeneity reflects true clinical diversity in surgical techniques (mini-subvastus, SMOC, and far medial approaches), patient populations (mean BMI ranging from 24.1 to 30.1 kg/m2), and rehabilitation protocols rather than statistical artifact.

Discussion

This systematic review specifically evaluates quadriceps recovery and clinical outcomes after subvastus total knee arthroplasty in patients with valgus deformity. The main findings demonstrate that the subvastus approach in valgus knees is associated with rapid quadriceps recovery (mean time to straight leg raise ranging from 0.92 to 2.0 days across studies), significant functional improvement, low postoperative pain scores, and minimal complication rates across a combined cohort of 286 valgus knees.

Anatomical advantages of the subvastus approach

The subvastus approach offers distinct anatomical advantages that are particularly relevant in valgus deformity. By preserving the extensor mechanism through elevation rather than incision of the vastus medialis muscle, this approach maintains the critical soft tissue envelope surrounding the patellofemoral joint [4, 16]. The anatomical integrity of the quadriceps insertion is especially important in valgus knees, where lateral soft tissue contracture and lateral femoral condyle hypoplasia already predispose to patellar tracking abnormalities [1, 15]. All three included studies demonstrated successful lateral soft tissue release using piecrust techniques, while maintaining the medial soft tissue sleeve, confirming that the subvastus approach provides adequate exposure to address the pathoanatomy of valgus deformity [14].

The absence of patellar complications in the subvastus groups across all studies, with the five cases of patellar subluxation reported by Li et al. occurring exclusively in the medial parapatellar comparison group, supports the anatomical premise that preserving the vastus medialis insertion optimizes patellar tracking [9]. Furthermore, the far medial subvastus approach described by Koninckx et al. capitalizes on the dysplastic vastus medialis often encountered in valgus knees, making lateral patellar subluxation easier and potentially reducing the need for lateral retinacular release [10]. The anatomical preservation of the ascending genicular artery with the subvastus approach may also contribute to reduced postoperative pain and faster healing [4]. As Tria and Coon emphasized in their early work on minimally invasive TKA, the term ‘minimally invasive’ should refer not to incision length but to the extent of disruption of anatomic structures, specifically, the extensor mechanism should not be violated. The subvastus approach directly adheres to this principle by elevating rather than incising the vastus medialis, which likely explains the rapid return of straight leg raise observed in our pooled analysis [5].

Quadriceps recovery and straight leg raise

The finding of rapid quadriceps recovery with mean time to straight leg raise ranging from 0.92 to 2.0 days postoperatively represents a clinically meaningful advantage. These findings are consistent with a large body of comparative literature examining the subvastus approach in general TKA populations. Pan et al. [17], in a prospective randomized controlled trial of 68 patients, demonstrated that patients undergoing mini-subvastus TKA achieved active straight leg raise significantly earlier than those receiving the standard medial parapatellar approach (1.9 vs. 4.2 days, p < 0.001). Similarly, Sastre et al. [34] reported superior quadriceps extensor force at hospital discharge and one month postoperatively in their randomized study of 104 patients, with the subvastus group showing significantly better Barthel Index scores at one month (86 vs. 70, p < 0.05). These benefits, however, tended to diminish by 12 months postoperatively, a pattern consistently observed across multiple studies [17, 32, 34].

The clinical significance of early SLR extends beyond the immediate postoperative period, as it facilitates early mobilization, reduces hospital length of stay (reported as 3.3-4.0 days) [10, 11], and accelerates return to functional activities including stair climbing [11]. Emirhan and Yapici [36], in their comparative study of 134 patients, found that straight leg raising time was significantly shorter in the subvastus group compared to the medial parapatellar approach (1.3 ± 0.4 vs. 2.5 ± 0.8 days, p < 0.001), with corresponding reductions in both hospitalization time (2.3 ± 0.4 vs. 3.7 ± 0.7 days, p < 0.001) and opiate analgesic requirements. These findings corroborate the rapid recovery observed in our valgus-specific cohort and suggest that the benefits of the subvastus approach are generalizable across different patient populations.

Functional outcomes and range of motion

Functional outcome scores improved dramatically across all included studies, with AKS improving from 39 to 91 [11], HSS from 35 to 93 [9], and KSS from 45 to 90 [10]. These improvements of 45–58 points represent not only statistical significance but meaningful clinical recovery enabling patients to resume daily activities. Range of motion increased by 9° to 27° across studies, with final flexion ranging from 109° to 137°, exceeding the 110° generally considered necessary for activities of daily living including stair climbing and rising from a seated position.

These findings align with the broader literature comparing subvastus and medial parapatellar approaches. Pan et al. [17] reported significantly better range of motion in the mini-subvastus group at 7 days, 6 weeks, and 3, 6, and 9 months postoperatively, although differences were no longer significant at one year. Similarly, Li Z et al. [31] found that patients undergoing subvastus TKA achieved straight leg raise 0.45 days faster than those receiving the medial parapatellar approach (p = 0.008), with the benefit most pronounced in patients aged 60–70 years. Importantly, preoperative quadriceps strength significantly influenced postoperative recovery (p < 0.001), suggesting that patient selection and preoperative conditioning may optimize outcomes.

The durability of functional improvements following subvastus TKA is supported by longer-term data. Jhurani et al. [30], in a prospective randomized study of 186 patients undergoing navigated sequential bilateral TKA, found no significant differences in Knee Society Scores, functional scores, or range of motion between subvastus and medial parapatellar groups at any time point up to two years postoperatively, suggesting that both approaches yield comparable long-term results when performed with computer navigation. However, they did observe significantly less blood loss in the subvastus group (1680.5 ± 605.7 mL vs. 1961.1 ± 587.5 mL, p < 0.05), a finding consistent with our included studies and other reports [17, 36].

Postoperative pain

Postoperative pain scores were consistently low across all included studies. Shah et al. [11] reported VAS at rest of 2.7 on day 1 decreasing to 2.4 on day 2, while Li et al. [9] documented 24-hour VAS of 4.6, and Koninckx et al. [10] reported mean rest pain of 2.3 over the first three postoperative days. The slightly higher early pain reported by Li et al. may reflect differences in pain measurement timing or analgesic protocols rather than approach-related factors. The low morphine consumption (38 mg over 48 h) reported by Koninckx et al. [10] further supports the pain benefits of muscle-sparing surgery.

These findings are consistent with multiple comparative studies. Madadi et al. [32], in a randomized controlled trial of 50 patients undergoing simultaneous bilateral TKA (one knee subvastus, the other medial parapatellar), found that while retropatellar pain was slightly lower in the subvastus group at 6 and 12 months, the difference was not statistically significant. Similarly, Pan et al. [17] reported significantly lower pain scores on postoperative day 1 in the mini-subvastus group compared to the standard approach (2.1 ± 1.0 vs. 3.8 ± 1.2, p = 0.015). Emirhan and Yapici [36] found that VAS scores at discharge were significantly better in the subvastus group (2.6 ± 1.1 vs. 3.6 ± 0.9, p < 0.001), although differences were no longer significant at 6 and 12 months.

Patient satisfaction

Patient satisfaction in TKA is multifactorial, influenced by pain relief, functional recovery, complication avoidance, and return to desired activities. Although none of the included studies reported formal patient satisfaction scores using validated instruments, several indirect measures suggest high satisfaction levels. The rapid return of quadriceps function enabling early SLR and ambulation directly addresses patient priorities for quick recovery after surgery [8]. The ability to achieve stair climbing by day 3 in the Shah et al. cohort [11] and the high final range of motion (up to 137° in Koninckx et al. [10]) facilitate return to culturally important activities such as sitting cross-legged and using floor-level seating.

Complications and safety

The low complication rates across all included studies, with no approach-related major complications and only two revisions among 286 knees (0.7%), suggest that the subvastus approach is safe in appropriately selected valgus knees. The single transient nerve injury reported by Shah et al. [11] resolved completely within six weeks and did not result in permanent functional deficit. The absence of patellar complications, wound healing problems, or deep infections in the subvastus cohorts suggests that the anatomical advantages of this approach translate into improved patient safety.

However, the broader literature on subvastus TKA warrants careful consideration of potential drawbacks. Dalury and Dennis [21], in a retrospective comparative study of 60 patients, reported that four of 30 patients (13.3%) undergoing mini-incision TKA had tibial component varus malalignment (< 87°) compared to none in the standard incision group, raising concerns about component positioning with limited exposure. This finding is particularly relevant to valgus knees, where precise component alignment is critical for balanced soft tissues and optimal patellar tracking. Similarly, Pan et al. [17] observed five technical errors on radiographic evaluation in their mini-subvastus group, including tibial component varus malalignment, and reported significantly longer tourniquet times (87 vs. 65 min, p < 0.001), reflecting the technical demands of the approach.

Wound healing complications have also been reported with the subvastus approach. Jhurani et al. [30] found a significantly higher rate of proximal wound dehiscence and delayed healing in the subvastus group compared to the medial parapatellar approach (7 of 93 vs. 1 of 93 patients, p = 0.03), leading to increased outpatient visits, readmissions, and secondary procedures with associated financial burden. This complication may relate to subcutaneous undermining required for exposure, particularly in patients with higher body mass index or when navigation pins are placed within the incision.

Comparison with alternative approaches

While the medial parapatellar approach remains the most commonly used technique in TKA, alternative approaches including the lateral parapatellar and midvastus approaches have been described specifically for valgus deformity. The lateral approach, advocated by some authors for severe valgus deformity, provides direct access to the contracted lateral soft tissues but requires tubercle osteotomy in some cases and may compromise medial exposure [7, 10]. The midvastus approach, which splits the vastus medialis obliquus muscle fibers, preserves the quadriceps tendon insertion but violates the muscle belly and may denervate the distal vastus medialis [30].

Comparative studies have yielded mixed results. Liu HW et al. [12], in a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, found that both midvastus and subvastus approaches were associated with better pain scores and earlier straight leg raise compared to the medial parapatellar approach, but no significant differences were observed between the two quadriceps-sparing approaches. Berstock et al. [26], in a systematic review of 20 randomized controlled trials, concluded that the subvastus approach offers early postoperative benefits including reduced pain, earlier straight leg raise, and shorter hospital stay, but these advantages diminish by 3–6 months postoperatively. Similarly, Khan et al. [24] reported that the subvastus approach was associated with faster straight leg raise and reduced hospital stay compared to the medial parapatellar approach.

In the specific context of valgus deformity, the choice of approach must balance exposure requirements with the benefits of extensor mechanism preservation. The included studies demonstrate that with appropriate surgical technique, including the SMOC modification described by Li et al. [9], the far medial extension described by Koninckx et al. [10], and the mini-subvastus technique used by Shah et al. [11] adequate exposure can be achieved even in valgus knees with lateral soft tissue contracture. The use of piecrust techniques for lateral release allows graduated correction of valgus deformity while preserving the medial soft tissue sleeve [14].

Role of computer navigation

Computer navigation has been proposed as a means to mitigate the risk of component malalignment associated with minimally invasive approaches. Jhurani et al. [30] found that with computer navigation, both subvastus and medial parapatellar approaches yielded comparable alignment outcomes, with all patients within ± 3° of mechanical axis. This finding suggests that navigation may offset the potential disadvantage of reduced visualization with the subvastus approach, particularly important in valgus knees where precise component positioning is essential for stability and patellar tracking. However, the additional pin sites required for navigation may contribute to bleeding and, in the subvastus group, may necessitate further subcutaneous dissection with potential wound healing implications [30].

Limitations

This review has several limitations. All three included studies were retrospective cohort designs with inherent risk of bias. The absence of comparison groups precludes direct comparison between subvastus and alternative approaches in valgus knees. Heterogeneity in surgical technique (mini-subvastus, SMOC, far medial subvastus), outcome measures, and follow-up duration limited quantitative synthesis to the primary outcome only. Two studies had relatively short follow-up (12–24 months), and longer-term outcomes could not be assessed. Publication bias cannot be excluded, and results from high-volume centers may not be generalizable. None of the studies reported objective quadriceps strength measurements using dynamometry or formal patient satisfaction scores [17, 34].

Relative contraindications to the subvastus approach in valgus knees include severe obesity (BMI > 35–40 kg/m2), severe valgus deformity (Krackow type III or > 20°), prior knee surgery, patella baja, stiff knees with severe flexion contracture, and inflammatory arthritis. Emirhan and Yapici [36] excluded patients with BMI > 30 from their comparative study, and Pan et al. [17] applied strict inclusion criteria including BMI < 30, deformity < 15°, and preoperative ROM ≥ 90°. Surgeon inexperience is the most important relative contraindication, as the technique has a recognized learning curve; Pan et al. [17] noted that even after 20 mini-subvastus procedures, technical errors still occurred, and longer operative times persisted. However, with appropriate patient selection and surgical expertise, the subvastus approach can be successfully performed across a wide range of valgus deformities.

Future directions

While mid-term data are now robust and supported by Level I evidence from general TKA populations, long-term survivorship studies (> 10 years) specifically focusing on the subvastus approach in valgus knees are still needed. Additionally, prospective trials directly comparing the subvastus, medial parapatellar, and lateral approaches in well-matched cohorts of valgus deformities would provide the highest level of comparative evidence to solidify its position in the surgical hierarchy. Future studies should incorporate objective quadriceps strength measurements using isokinetic dynamometry, formal patient-reported outcome measures including satisfaction scores and forgotten joint scores, and standardized assessment of patellar tracking and vascularity. The role of computer navigation and patient-specific instrumentation in optimizing outcomes with the subvastus approach in valgus knees warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

This systematic review provides the first pooled evidence that the subvastus approach is a safe and effective option for total knee arthroplasty in patients with valgus deformity. Despite the inherent surgical challenges of the valgus knee, this muscle-sparing approach is associated with rapid return of quadriceps function, excellent functional improvement, and low complication rates. While these findings are promising, they are derived from moderate study-level quality, single-arm retrospective studies. High-quality, prospective comparative studies are required to definitively establish the superiority of the subvastus approach over other techniques in this specific patient population.
