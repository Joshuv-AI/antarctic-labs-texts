The impact of 14-day head-down bed rest with or without an exercise countermeasure on standing balance control: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Nok-Yeung Law, Karima Ahmed Yahia, Joseph Gill-Lussier, Ali Falaki, Helga Tonkov, Faezeh Abbariki, Andréa Faust, José A Morais, Guy Hajj-Boutros, Dorothy Barthélemy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExposure to space environment disrupts the sensorimotor system due to adaptation to microgravity, leading to spatial disorientation, impaired coordination, and reduced postural control upon return to Earth. To simulate these effects, a six‑degree head‑down bed rest (HDBR) protocol was used. This randomized controlled trial examined whether exercise could mitigate declines in standing balance control following 14 days of HDBR. Twenty-two participants were assigned to either an exercise group, performing daily high-intensity interval training combined with resistance and aerobic exercise, or a non-exercising control group. Balance was assessed using the sensory organization test (SOT) and head-shake SOT, with outcomes including equilibrium score (ES), strategy analysis (SA), and vestibular-related measures (e.g., SOT-2M, SOT-5M). No between-group differences were observed in either ES or SA. However, when data were pooled, ES during SOT-2M was significantly decreased (p < 0.001), indicating greater instability under eyes-closed, head-shake conditions. Higher baseline ES during SOT-2M was associated with smaller declines post-HDBR, particularly in the exercise group. No effects of sex or age (55–65 years) were found. These findings suggest that exercise may not prevent balance declines after short-term HDBR and that baseline balance capacity may be associated with variability in individual responses. Introduction

Gravity serves as a crucial signal for the sensorimotor system, facilitating the perception of self-orientation in space and aiding in the adjustment of movements and posture1. During space missions, astronauts navigate a gravity-free environment. Over time, the sensorimotor system adapts to this unique setting2, leading to a modulation in the integration of sensory inputs and motor commands to optimize performance in microgravity2. Upon returning to Earth, these adaptations can become maladaptive, resulting in disrupted coordination of movements and posture2. Nearly 70% of astronauts encounter alterations in movement control and balance during and post-spaceflight1.

As space agencies gear up for extended-duration missions, understanding these balance control alterations induced by microgravity becomes paramount. Head-down bed rest (HDBR) is a widely used experimental model to simulate the effects of microgravity, as directly studying microgravity on astronauts is challenging. The six-degree head-down tilt mimics the redistribution of blood volume towards the upper body, similar to what occurs in space, while reducing gravitational resistance, which affects the sensory stimuli crucial for determining body position and orientation relative to gravity3–5. This makes HDBR an effective method for studying the deconditioning effects of microgravity and testing potential countermeasures.

In parallel, accumulating knowledge on sensorimotor adaptation has informed the development of evidence‑based countermeasures designed to facilitate astronauts’ transitions between gravitational environments and mitigate the detrimental effects associated with microgravity exposure6. Physical training methods such as aerobic exercise, resistance training, and high-intensity interval training (HIIT) have emerged as potential strategies, primarily targeted cardiovascular health and minimized muscle mass decline in the lower extremities7–9.

While exercise countermeasures in operational spaceflight have demonstrated only partial effectiveness in preventing postflight sensorimotor impairments, their specific effects on postural control and sensory integration under controlled unloading conditions remain incompletely understood. An HDBR model provides a controlled environment in which these effects can be isolated from the operational constraints and confounding factors inherent to spaceflight. This allows for a more detailed examination of postural responses to exercise-based countermeasures, as well as interindividual variability and condition-specific balance adaptations assessed through sensory organization test (SOT) and head-shake sensory organization test (HS-SOT) performance.

Previous studies examining exercise-based countermeasures during HDBR have reported mixed findings. While approaches such as resistance training combined with artificial gravity or fluid loading have demonstrated partial benefits in preserving specific physiological functions, they have not been sufficient to fully offset the adverse effects of prolonged inactivity10–13. A critical gap persists in maintaining balance control, a function particularly vulnerable to microgravity-induced deconditioning. These physiological alterations, including muscle atrophy, bone loss, and impaired postural stability, are well established in astronauts and closely parallel the changes observed during aging, underscoring the relevance of this model for both spaceflight and terrestrial health contexts. Aging naturally weakens muscles, bones, and balance systems, making older adults more susceptible to the same deconditioning effects during periods of extended inactivity, such as those observed in astronauts returning from space flights, including loss of muscle mass, bone density, and impaired balance14–18. Hence, studying exercise countermeasures in older adults can help identify strategies that may be applicable to astronauts, addressing shared challenges related to prolonged inactivity. This approach can also explore targeted physical training regimens for balance and mobility, especially in aging populations, to improve countermeasures for both groups19,20.

Among the various methods available to assess balance control, the SOT is commonly used to evaluate how visual, proprioceptive, and vestibular inputs contribute to postural stability. The addition of the head-shake condition has been shown to enhance the sensitivity of the SOT in identifying vestibular-related balance impairments21,22 as well as balance deficits in elderly individuals23. The HS-SOT challenges the postural control system by activating the vestibular system during head-shake movements, causing individuals to receive inaccurate sensory inputs. By superimposing head movements on body oscillations, the HS-SOT challenges the central nervous system to distinguish between vestibular inputs generated by head motion and those resulting from body sway. Individuals with vestibular dysfunction struggle to make this distinction, leading to increased body sway and reduced postural control24–26. As a result, the HS-SOT is valuable for identifying individuals with persistent vestibular deficits, even when their SOT performance appears near normal, as they might have developed compensations27–30. NASA uses the head-shake protocol in its training and recovery programs to simulate and assess astronauts’ vestibular systems and balance control in response to the sensory challenges encountered in space or post-spaceflight recovery31–33. The head-shake test thus helps detect vestibular or sensorimotor issues resulting from space exposure21,22. Consistent with this, findings from computerized dynamic posturography assessments in astronauts have shown that postflight balance impairments are most pronounced under conditions requiring active head movements and unstable support33. Dynamic head tilts increase fall incidence and prolong recovery, highlighting the sensitivity of vestibular-challenging tasks in detecting sensorimotor deficits following spaceflight.

Studies investigating the effects of HDBR on balance control have reported declines in postural stability that vary with the duration of bed rest13,34–36. Short-term interventions, such as a 14-day HDBR, have been shown to induce immediate postural instability, particularly in the medio-lateral axis, which largely recovered within four weeks37. Longer interventions, such as the 70-day HDBR, produced measurable deficits in standing balance that persisted up to 12 days post-intervention9. These findings suggest that both short- and long-duration HDBR can impact balance control, with longer durations generally producing more pronounced or prolonged effects. Moreover, evidence from spaceflight studies suggests that early exposure to progressively challenging sensorimotor tasks may facilitate recovery, although outcomes appear highly dependent on individual tolerance38. Movements that provoke vestibular symptoms may promote re-adaptation when performed within manageable thresholds, but excessive challenge can impair performance, highlighting substantial interindividual variability in recovery responses.

Building on these findings and considering the importance of vestibular-challenging tasks and individualized responses to sensorimotor stressors, the current study aims to evaluate the efficacy of specific physical training regimens in preserving balance control following a 2-week HDBR in healthy older adults. More specifically, the total daily exercise volume was distributed across three separate sessions. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate and apply this approach in this specific population. It is hypothesized that exercise countermeasures will significantly attenuate post-HDBR balance impairments, as measured by equilibrium score (ES) and strategy analysis (SA) on the SOT. Additionally, sex and age are anticipated to influence balance recovery, with potential differences in the magnitude of balance changes across these demographic factors. The study will also investigate the impact of the addition of the head-shake condition along the pitch axis (activating the anterior and posterior semi-circular canals), hypothesizing that it will better detect a change in balance control between exercise and control groups. Overall, this study investigates whether targeted physical training can mitigate HDBR-induced balance deficits and alter sensory integration during postural control.

Results

SOT and HS-SOT performance comparison between exercise and control groups

Balance performance was assessed using a computerized dynamic posturography system (NeuroCom), including the SOT and the HS-SOT. ES and SA were assessed pre- and post-HDBR in both exercise and control groups across six different SOT conditions. Head-shake conditions (SOT-2M and SOT-5M) were evaluated, with outcome measures including ES and vertical velocity, as well as derived ES ratios for fixed and sway conditions. Derived ES ratios were calculated to quantify changes in postural performance between fixed and sway conditions, expressed as the relative difference in ES between conditions with stable support versus conditions with induced sway, thereby providing an index of sensory reweighting under dynamic visual-vestibular demands. Discussion

The aim of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of applied exercises as countermeasures to prevent or limit detrimental changes in balance control following a 14-day HDBR period. An important novel aspect of this study is that the prescribed daily exercise volume was distributed across three sessions rather than performed as a single continuous bout, and that it was studied in people 50 years and older. The initial hypothesis was that the participants in the exercise group who regularly participated in physical training would maintain their postural stability after HDBR. To test this hypothesis, the ES and SA derived from SOT, as well as the HS-SOT, were used to evaluate the ability to integrate visual, somatosensory, and vestibular information to maintain balance under various conditions. The results show that: (1) Based on the score from the SOT (ES and SA) and the HS-SOT measures (ES ratio, SOT-2M and SOT-5M), there was no difference in the ability to maintain balance between the exercise and control groups after HDBR; (2) When participants from both groups were pooled, a significant effect of bed rest was observed for the SA in the SOT-condition 2 and for the SOT-2M. This suggests that the postural strategy used by participants to maintain quiet stance with their eyes closed without (SOT-condition 2) and with head-shake (SOT-2M) was altered after HDBR; (3) Initial balance performance pre-HBDR (ES, SA, ES ratio and SOT-5M) was associated with the magnitude of change in balance control following HDBR; (4) No effect of age and sex was detected on SOT or HS-SOT scores. Taken together, these results suggest that the applied countermeasures during the 2-week HDBR did not modify postural control under challenging sensory conditions. These results suggest that intrinsic individual factors, such as baseline balance abilities, influence responses to 2-week HDBR.

Two-week HDBR did not influence the ability to maintain postural stability in either exercise or control groups. However, when both groups were pooled, a significant effect of HDBR on postural strategies (SA) was observed during quiet stance with eyes closed (SOT-condition 2) and during head-shake conditions (SOT-2M). These observations are consistent with studies reporting declines in postural control after spaceflight durations as short as 4 days2,39, although those studies primarily report differences in SOT-conditions 5 and 640.

One explanation for this discrepancy might lie in the HDBR model itself. Although 6° HDBR is a well-established analog for simulating the effects of microgravity, it has limitations: while it effectively reproduces cephalad fluid shifts, it does not eliminate gravitational load41. Notably, previous studies using HDBR for 42–63 days did not show changes in SOT-condition 1 through 54, and a duration of 70 days of HDBR was required to observe changes in the SOT-5M36, which includes head movements during eyes-closed perturbation42.

Importantly, the present findings suggest that conditions involving either reduced visual reliance (SOT-condition 2) or combined sensory and head-movement demands (SOT-2M) may be particularly sensitive to subtle changes in postural strategy following HDBR. Both conditions involve distinct sensory processing demands: SOT-condition 2 relies primarily on vestibular and somatosensory integration in the absence of visual input, whereas SOT-2M additionally introduces vestibular perturbation through head movements. This may indicate that HDBR-related adaptations are more readily expressed in conditions where sensory reweighting is required rather than in purely visually dominant or more complex multi-conflict conditions.

Furthermore, these results are aligned with evidence suggesting that impairments in postural strategy are a key feature of post-spaceflight balance alterations39. While longer HDBR duration may provide additional insight into the effects of physical countermeasures, studies using extended bed rest periods have similarly reported limited changes in ES, in contrast to post-spaceflight observations.

It has been suggested that the magnitude of the effects of microgravity exposure and the recovery time depend not only on the duration of exposure but also on interindividual differences in the physiological adaptation6,43. Because participants’ baseline physical conditions vary (also see Koppelmans et al., 2022), this may influence their ability to maintain balance. To explore this relationship, Spearman correlation was conducted as an exploratory analysis to examine the association between pre-HDBR ES and SA and their respective changes (∆ES or ∆SA) following HDBR.

The results revealed significant negative correlations (Table 3). In the exercise group, these correlations were significant for ES in SOT-condition 1 and 4, for SA in SOT-condition 1–3, and for SOT-5M. In the control group, a significant correlation was observed only in SOT-condition 1. When data were pooled, significant correlations were found in SOT-conditions 1 and 4 for both ES and SA, as well as in SOT-condition 6 and in SOT-5M for SA.

The interpretation of these correlations is not straightforward, as these associations are not independent. The association between pre-HDBR ES and SA and their respective changes (∆ES or ∆SA) was primarily observed during (a) quiet standing with eyes open (SOT-condition 1), (b) backward perturbation with eyes open (SOT-condition 4), and (c) eyes-closed conditions with head-shake during perturbations (SOT-5M). These conditions reflect differing levels of sensory integration demands, ranging from minimal sensory conflict (SOT-condition 1) to altered somatosensory input (SOT-condition 4), and combined sensory system changes (SOT-5M). The absence of similar associations in more complex HS-SOT and SOT-5/6 conditions may reflect greater interindividual variability, task-specific noise, or potential ceiling/floor effects that reduce the sensitivity in these measures.

Overall, a negative association between balance performance and change following the 2-week HDBR period was observed across participants, irrespective of group allocation. However, this relationship should be interpreted with caution; it may be influenced by mathematical coupling and the regression to the mean inherent in change score analysis. Although exploratory subgroup analyses suggested a strong pattern in the exercise group, these findings are descriptive and not indicative of a group-specific effect.

Given these limitations, the observed association should not be interpreted as evidence that baseline performance determines responsiveness. While interindividual differences may reflect underlying physiological variability (e.g., neuromuscular control, muscle function, and skeletal properties)44,45, the present analysis does not allow these mechanisms to be directly inferred.

No significant difference in ES or SA was observed between men and women, nor was there an effect of age on balance. However, given the relatively small sample size, the study may not have been sufficiently powered to detect such effects. Evidence from the literature indicates that physiological aging is associated with declines in sensory sensitivity and perception, degeneration of the neuromuscular system, and reductions in muscle and bone strength46. Notably, participants in this study were aged 55–65 years, whereas these factors are more strongly associated with postural instability and increased fall risk in the population over 65 years of age46,47.

One limitation of this study lies in the inter-individual variability of factors that could influence the observed results, such as age and participants’ initial balance abilities. Accordingly, the impact of the HDBR period may vary depending on individual intrinsic characteristics, rather than being fully captured by group-based analyses. Moreover, recovery following spaceflight can occur rapidly, often within approximately 4 days39. As noted in the Methodology, some participants were unable to perform the balance assessment immediately after the HDBR. This delay may have led to an overestimation of post-HDBR ES and SA relative to the immediate effect of bed rest.

In addition, the stability limits set at 12.5° by the NeuroCom® Balance Master are theoretical values and may not reflect true individual limits. Some participants may exceed these values, meaning that ES and SA may not fully represent their actual balance capacity. Another limitation relates to repeated testing using the NeuroCom® system. Repeated exposure to the SOT and HS-SOT may induce learning or practice effect48,49, which are more pronounced in the elderly population50. Although performance generally declines following HDBR, such learning effects may have partially offset these declines and could explain improvements observed in some individuals. Furthermore, while the test–retest reliability of the HS-SOT has been reported as moderate to good, performance is influenced by the plane of head movement, with pitch-plane movements proving particularly challenging50. These factors should be considered when interpreting the results.

In conclusion, the findings of this study suggest that the applied countermeasures had limited effectiveness in mitigating the adverse effects of microgravity simulated by 2-week HDBR on balance control. Additionally, intrinsic individual factors, such as baseline balance performance, may be associated with responses to HDBR.

Methodology

Study design overview

All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Board of the McGill University Health Centre (MUHC; study no. 2021-7170) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was a two-arm randomized controlled trial in which participants were allocated to either an exercise or a control group. Participants and investigators were not blinded to group assignment. Participants resided at the McConnell Centre for Innovative Medicine (CIM), Research Institute of the McGill University Health Centre for 26 days, including a 5-day baseline, 14-day head-down bed rest (HDBR), and 7-day recovery phase. Recruitment began in July 2021, with data collection spanning baseline, HDBR, and recovery phases, and the study was conducted from July 2021 to early 2022. A detailed description of study procedures, including measurements, intervention, and recovery protocols, is provided elsewhere51. This trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04964999).

Randomization

Participants were randomly assigned to either 14 days of 6° head-down bed rest (HDBR) alone (control) or HDBR with an exercise countermeasure (exercise group). The random allocation sequence was generated using a computer-based procedure by a study team member not involved in recruitment, enrollment, or outcome assessment. Restricted randomization was used to maintain balance between groups, with stratification by sex where applicable. The block size was not disclosed to the recruitment or assessment team to preserve allocation concealment. The allocation sequence was kept separate from personnel involved in enrollment and baseline testing, and group assignment was revealed only after completion of screening and baseline procedures.

Due to the nature of the intervention, participants and exercise supervisors could not be blinded to group allocation. However, outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded to group assignment whenever feasible. To minimize bias, standardized testing procedures were applied across groups. Both groups followed the same 14-day 6° HDBR protocol, hospital environment, monitoring procedures, and testing schedule, with the exercise intervention as the only difference.

Participant demographics

A total of 23 participants were enrolled in this study. One participant dropped out on day 3 of HDBR, mainly for convenience reasons (hard to stay in position, difficulties to pass bowel movement, and required assistance for basic needs). Two participants were withdrawn from the study on day 3 of recovery due to the occurrence of atrial fibrillation. However, in this manuscript, data from these 2 participants are included in the analysis since the balance and NeuroCom® measurements were conducted on day 1 of recovery. Sample size was based on feasibility considerations and the logistical constraints of conducting a controlled head-down bed rest study51,52. All participants followed a standardized diet described in the methodology paper51 based on their energy requirements measured using an indirect calorimeter. Participants’ baseline characteristics are shown in Table 5 and standard measures of the Canadian Space Agency are published in a separate manuscript52.

Eligibility criteria

Participants were healthy adults aged 55–65 years. Inclusion criteria were non-smokers; body mass index between 20 and 30 kg/m²; height between 158 and 190 cm; engagement in ≥2.5 h per week of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic physical activity; and successful completion of medical and psychological screening. Female participants were required to be postmenopausal (≥1 year since last menses or documented ovariectomy, with serum FSH > 30 IU/L). Participants were required to be willing to be randomly assigned to either the exercise or control group.

Exclusion criteria included cardiovascular, neurological, vestibular, or musculoskeletal disorders that could affect balance or participation in head-down bed rest (HDBR), including a history of myocardial infarction, arrhythmias, thrombosis risk, orthostatic intolerance, seizures, or significant head trauma. Additional exclusion criteria were electrocardiogram abnormalities, anemia, low bone mineral density, renal or gastrointestinal disorders, chronic back pain, migraines, severe allergies, or mental illness. Participants were also excluded if they required medications that could interfere with study outcomes, had a recent poor nutritional status, claustrophobia, special dietary restrictions, or metallic implants. Further exclusions included smoking within 6 months, substance abuse within 30 days, blood donation within 3 months, or participation in another study within 2 months prior to enrollment.

Exercise intervention details

Briefly, participants in the exercise group performed three daily sessions, alternating between HIIT and low-intensity cycling, and resistance exercises targeting upper and lower body musculature. The total daily exercise duration ranges from 60–75 min. All exercise routines were meticulously executed either in a head-down tilt or horizontal orientation, utilizing designated equipment based on a structured timetable. Crucially, the intensity levels of these exercise interventions were tailored to individual participants, aligning with their performance capabilities and tolerances. HIIT sessions consisted of repeated bouts of high-intensity effort interspersed with recovery periods, while continuous aerobic sessions were performed at moderate intensity. Resistance training included multi-joint exercises targeting major muscle groups. Participants in the control group did not perform structured exercise but received standard physiotherapy care, consisting of passive range-of-motion exercises to maintain joint mobility. A comprehensive description of the intervention protocol, including exercise parameters and progression, is available in ref. 53.

Instrumentation

Data collection was performed using the NeuroCom® Balance Master™ SMART EquiTest® (Clackamas, OR), which is equipped with the Computerized Dynamic Posturography system. With its standardized tests, this system, which is included in the NeuroCom® Balance Master™ SMART EquiTest® device, has been identified as an effective method for assessing the ability to maintain a stable position and identify abnormalities in the use of different sensory systems54. It has been used widely in research to quantify postural stability55,56. This device includes a safety harness, a force platform, and a moving visual environment that oscillates in the sagittal plane. Among the dynamic posturography protocols, the Sensory Organization Test (SOT; Fig. 10) was applied to the participants before and after the HDBR period.

Sensory organization test (SOT) conditions (adapted from NeuroCom® SMART EquiTest® computerized dynamic posturography).

The six conditions are presented in a 2 × 3 layout (top row: conditions 1–3; bottom row: conditions 4–6): (1) eyes open, stable support and stable surrounding; (2) eyes closed, stable support; (3) eyes open, stable support with sway-referenced surrounding; (4) eyes open, sway-referenced support with stable surrounding; (5) eyes closed, sway-referenced support; (6) eyes open, sway-referenced support and surrounding. In the present study, participants stood with arms crossed over the chest during testing.

Standing postural balance was selected as the primary balance outcome because the objective of the study was to evaluate sensory integration and postural control under standardized conditions following HDBR. The SOT and HS-SOT allow controlled manipulation of visual, somatosensory, and vestibular inputs, making it possible to identify condition-specific changes in balance performance and postural strategy. These tests are also widely used in spaceflight and HDBR research to assess sensorimotor deconditioning and recovery.

Sensory organization test (SOT)

In this study, the SOT was used to assess participants’ ability to integrate sensory components for balance maintenance under different conditions. The test consists of six conditions, applied in a specific order (as illustrated in Table 6), with participants required to stand on the platform in a standard position with arms crossed over their chest. Figure 10 also illustrates the different conditions, as suggested by NeuroCom®. However, in the current study, the participants were standing with arms crossed, which differs from that shown in Fig. 10. Participants were instructed to maintain balance for 20 seconds during three consecutive trials of each condition4.

In Condition 1, balance was assessed with the participant’s eyes open and no perturbation of the platform. In Condition 2, participants stood on the platform with their eyes closed. In Condition 3, participants had their eyes open while the cabin was tilted, but the platform remained stable. In these conditions (2 and 3), visual cues were either absent or inaccurate, requiring the participant to prioritize proprioceptive and vestibular information for balance maintenance. In Condition 4, participants had their eyes open, and the surrounding environment remained stable, while the platform was sway-referenced and tilted to perturb proprioceptive information from the ankles. In Condition 5, the platform was tilted with the eyes closed, resulting in the absence of visual input and perturbation of proprioceptive cues. In Condition 6, the eyes were open, but both the visual surround and support surface moved simultaneously. This configuration created a situation where both visual and proprioceptive cues were inaccurate, forcing the brain to rely solely on vestibular signals to restore balance55. Each of these conditions was performed three times, the resulting ES and SA were averaged together for the mean and SD value.

The primary aim of the SOT was to selectively isolate one sensory cue by creating sensory conflicts, allowing the evaluation of the brain’s ability to rely on the most reliable sensory input to maintain body orientation and balance54. These conditions provided insight into which sensory system might be more affected following the HDBR period, which could have implications for postural control in microgravity or other altered environments54.

In addition to the six standard conditions, two SOT-2M and SOT-5M conditions were specifically tested, where participants performed continuous chin-to-chest nod along the pitch axis movements at a frequency of 0.33 Hz and an amplitude of 20° in each direction. In SOT-2M (Eyes Closed, Fixed Surface), participants stood on a fixed surface without visual input, relying on proprioceptive and vestibular feedback. In SOT-5M (Eyes Closed, Sway-Referenced Surface), participants stood on a sway-referenced surface with the platform moving in response to postural sway, while visual input was absent. Each of these conditions was performed six times, with data collected continuously at 0.33 Hz head rotation. One un-scored practice trial was completed for each condition, followed by three scored trials, each lasting 15 s. The tests followed a preset order and repetition to ensure consistency in data collection31,32. White noise was supplied to mask auditory cues, and participants’ arms were folded across their chests. Participants performed both the standard and head-moving trials (SOT-2M and SOT-5M) before and after the HDBR period. Post-HDBR, participants were assessed on the 1st day of verticalization (n = 15), or, if participants experienced dizziness after verticalization, on the 2nd day (n = 5) or 3rd day (n = 2) after verticalization.

Outcome measures: SOT-equilibrium score (ES)

Following the six conditions of the SOT, different scores were generated by NeuroCom®. For each trial, ES was calculated as a percentage based on the participants’ anteroposterior sway amplitude relative to theoretical stability limits57. These stability limits were defined as 12.5° of anteroposterior sway to represent the boundaries of upright stability57. ES ranges from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating better postural stability58. Scores decrease as the participants approached anterior or posterior limits (8.5° anterior and 4° posterior thresholds)57. A score of 0 was assigned when the participants stepped off the support surface or exceeded the maximum stability limit58. ES reflects the integrated contribution of visual, proprioceptive, and vestibular systems in maintaining upright posture59.

Outcome measures: SOT-strategy analysis (SA)

The ankle and hip strategies consist of postural reactions involving activation of muscles acting at the ankle and hip joints, respectively, to stabilize the upright standing balance following a perturbation. The ankle strategy is the first line of postural reaction when the perturbation is perceived as small. When the perturbation is perceived as larger or more threatening to balance, the hip strategy can be used.

The quantification of ankle or hip strategies60,61 is achieved using SA, which is derived by combining the data from the force plate and ES. SA, ranging from 0 to 100, was computed by the NeuroCom® system for each condition. SA reflects the degree of ankle and hip strategies used throughout each trial to maintain postural stability. When participants adjusted their balance primarily through ankle movements, SA approached 100. Conversely, if hip movements were the predominant strategy, SA approached 0. The resulting values between 0 and 100 are the result of combining the two strategies62.

Outcome measures: HS-SOT—equilibrium score and angular velocity

In the HS-SOT conditions, only ES were computed by the NeuroCom® system. SA were not available for these trials. As in the standard SOT, ES reflected participants’ postural stability based on their sway relative to the antero-posterior stability limits.

In addition to ES, the angular velocity of the head movement was monitored during the head-shake conditions to ensure consistency across trials and participants. Head movements were performed along the pitch axis (anteroposterior), and angular velocity values were compared between conditions to confirm that task execution was similar. This step helped ensure that any differences in postural performance were attributable to sensory integration demands rather than variability in head movement dynamics.

Thirdly, the ES ratio was calculated to quantify the relative change in postural stability between head-shake and head-fixed conditions. The overall ES ratio was defined as the mean ES of the head-shake conditions (SOT-2M and SOT-5M) divided by the mean ES of the corresponding head-fixed conditions (SOT-2 and SOT-5). To distinguish between support conditions, condition-specific ratios were also computed. The ES ratio fixed was defined as the ratio of SOT-2M to SOT-2, representing the fixed support condition. The ES ratio sway was defined as the ratio of SOT-5M to SOT-5, representing the sway-referenced support condition.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 28.0 or 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) or SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) software. Descriptive statistics, including participants’ sex, mean age, and height, are presented as mean and standard deviation (for continuous variables) or frequencies for categorical variables. This study followed a 2-arm randomized controlled design, with participants allocated to either an exercise or control group. Analyses were conducted according to this grouping structure, with repeated measurements obtained pre- and post-HDBR across SOT and HS-SOT conditions. Some analyses used pooled data where the group was not considered. Missing data were not imputed, and analyses were conducted using available data at each time point.

Given the non-normal distribution of most variables and the relatively small sample size, nonparametric tests were used for all inferential analyses. At baseline, Mann-Whitney tests were used to confirm group comparability on outcome measures (ΔES and ΔSA). For the principal analysis, Mann-Whitney tests were performed on the post–pre differences (ΔES and ΔSA; defined as post-HDBR–pre-HDBR) to assess between-group differences in response to HDBR across all SOT and HS-SOT conditions. When no between-group differences were observed in ΔES or ΔSA, Sign tests were applied to the pooled sample to assess overall pre-post effects. When differential group evolution was observed, Sign tests were performed separately for each group. The Sign test was preferred over the Wilcoxon test because several variables did not meet the assumption of symmetric distributions required for the Wilcoxon test. To account for multiple comparisons, Holm–Šidák corrections were applied within families of related tests. Specifically, corrections were performed separately for between-group comparisons (Mann–Whitney U tests) and pooled within-sample analyses (Sign tests). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Spearman’s rank-order correlations were conducted to assess the relationships between baseline (pre-HDBR) performance (ES, SA, ES ratio, and HS-SOT outcomes) and change scores (ΔES, ΔSA, and ΔES ratio), as well as between age and change scores. Mann–Whitney U tests were also used to assess sex differences in ΔES and ΔSA. These analyses were performed across all participants and within each group (exercise and control).
