Differences in health self-management behaviors among residents with illnesses across remote islands with different degrees of isolation from the mainland
Authors: Natsumi Yamakawa, Rieko Nakao, Mayumi Ohnishi, Ryoko Kawasaki, Satoko Kosaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective: Owing to geographic isolation, limited healthcare services, and aging populations, residents with illnesses living on highly isolated islands may engage more actively in health self-management. However, this assumption has not been empirically examined. The aim of this study was to compare health self-management behaviors among residents with illnesses according to the degree of island isolation.Materials and Methods: A questionnaire survey was conducted in clinics on four remote islands administered by Goto City, Nagasaki Prefecture, Japan, between July and August 2025. Participants consisted of 65 and 64 individuals from islands with low and high levels of isolation, respectively.Results: Preventive health behaviors differed according to the level of isolation. Attendance at lung and colorectal cancer screening was lower on highly isolated islands, likely reflecting reduced availability and accessibility. Illness coping behaviors also varied. Among residents of more isolated islands, fewer searched independently for health information before deciding whether to seek care, whereas a higher proportion reported not caring, even when feeling unwell.Conclusion: Residents on more isolated islands were less active in health self-management behaviors than those on less isolated islands, possibly because of limited availability of healthcare services and closer relationships with medical professionals. These findings suggest that isolation from the mainland influences residents’ health self-management and represents a distinct form of geographic disparity. Considering these behavioral differences is important when allocating medical resources and developing telemedicine strategies for island communities.Keywords: health self-management behavior, preventive health behavior, illness coping behavior, remote island, Japan

Introduction

With the growing burden of noncommunicable diseases, health self-management is shifting from a supplementary practice to a fundamental requirement for maintaining individual health and ensuring the sustainability of healthcare systems. However, several barriers hinder the adoption of effective self-management behaviors. The physical and social characteristics of neighborhood environments are influential. Geographic distance to primary healthcare facilities is critical in areas where transportation options are limited, and people who must travel longer distances to healthcare facilities are less likely to seek medical attention when needed1,2,3,4). Social context is also an important factor; an earlier study indicated that those who live alone are less likely to receive annual health checkups than those living with others5). These findings show that neighborhood environments substantially shape health self-management and highlight the need to consider locality when designing health policies and interventions.

Remote island residents face additional challenges related to geographic isolation and limited access to services, including healthcare. Compared with the more commonly examined urban–rural disparities (e.g., health disadvantages of rural populations6, 7)), the differences between remote islands and the mainland are often more pronounced. Emergency medical transport is difficult when treatment exceeds local capacity, and islands typically have limited medical resources compared with the mainland. These conditions may influence how residents manage their health-related behaviors. Previous studies have shown that island residents tend to eat regular meals, sleep sufficiently, and engage in higher levels of physical activity than mainland residents, thereby contributing to the prevention of noncommunicable diseases8, 9).

Even on remote islands, residents’ health-related behaviors may differ according to the degree of isolation from the mainland. In more isolated settings, access to healthcare resources is often further reduced, increasing the difficulty of responding to health emergencies. In Japan, such remote islands often have a high proportion of older adults with high rates of chronic conditions, indicating the increased importance of effective self-management in preventing health deterioration.

However, there have been no empirical studies on health self-management behaviors among residents with illnesses across islands with differing levels of isolation. This study aimed to identify differences in health self-management behaviors among residents with illnesses living on multiple remote islands in Japan. The findings will inform intervention planning and policy development to support the health and well-being of isolated island populations.

Materials and Methods

We conducted a questionnaire survey among residents of four remote islands administered by Goto City, Nagasaki Prefecture, Japan, who visited selected clinics between July and August 2025. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nagasaki University Graduate School of Biomedical Sciences (approval no. 25021303).

Study areas and participants

This study was conducted on Fukuejima, Hisakajima, Kabashima, and Saganoshima Islands. The Japanese classification of remote islands often distinguishes between primary remote islands (or Ichiji Ritō; hereafter PRIs), which have direct public transportation access to the mainland, and secondary remote islands (or Niji Ritō; hereafter SRIs), which require transit via a PRI to reach the mainland. Among the study areas, Fukuejima Island is a PRI, accessible from mainland Japan directly by public transportation, such as flights and sea routes. The other three islands are SRIs, accessible from mainland Japan only via Fukuejima Island. Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the islands.

We conducted a questionnaire survey among patients receiving medical care in clinics, focusing on island residents with illnesses. Of the 27 clinics on Fukuejima Island, two were selected based on geographic distribution and clinical departments. A researcher visited the two clinics on Fukuejima Island (PRI) and all clinics on Hisakajima, Kabashima, and Saganoshima Islands (SRIs) for 1 to 3 days each. After patients received an explanation of the study purpose and protocol, self-administered questionnaires were distributed to all patients aged ≥18 years. Individuals accompanying patients were excluded.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire included questions on participants’ basic demographic characteristics (age, gender, education, etc.), social network status, and health self-management behaviors. Social network status was assessed because it represents an important social dimension of neighborhood environments that varies across communities and is difficult to measure using secondary data. Participants reported their number of close relatives, friends, and neighbors, and their frequency of communication with them11).

Health self-management behaviors were divided into preventive health and illness coping behaviors, in accordance with a previous study12). Preventive health behaviors refer to actions taken for disease prevention and early detection in the absence of subjective symptoms, whereas illness coping behaviors refer to actions taken to manage and recover from illnesses when symptoms are perceived.

Regarding preventive health behaviors, participants were asked about their daily lifestyle practices and the frequency of attendance at health checkups (general checkups, lung cancer screening, and colorectal cancer screening). The Japanese government recommends annual checkups and screening for adults aged ≥40 years, and in Goto City, these services are provided without charge. Daily lifestyle items from an earlier study in Japan were used13), covering eight factors: sleep duration, breakfast habits, nutritional balance, snacking, salt intake, alcohol consumption, smoking, and exercise. Responses were scored from 0 to 8 by summing healthier behaviors: sleeping for 7–8 h per day, eating breakfast almost daily, considering nutritional balance, snacking less than daily, limiting salt intake, drinking alcohol less than daily, not smoking, and exercising regularly.

Illness coping behaviors were evaluated based on 13 items: five regarding actions when feeling unwell, four about decisions to visit a hospital or clinic, and four about treatment decision making. Twelve items were adopted from a study by Tamura and Miyazaki12), who reviewed earlier studies on medical consultation behavior in Japan. Additionally, one original item elicited responses regarding seeking medical care outside the islands. Respondents indicated their degree of agreement with each statement on a four-point scale ranging from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree”.

Statistical analysis

Data from Hisakajima, Kabashima, and Saganoshima Islands were combined into the SRI category. The proportions of responses were compared between the PRI and SRIs using Fisher’s exact test, excluding missing responses (“No response” in the tables) with pairwise deletion. The t-test was used to compare the mean values of lifestyle scores. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata IC 12 (StataCorp., College Station, TX, USA), and two-sided P-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Sixty-five individuals from the PRI (Fukuejima Island) and 64 from the SRIs (Hisakajima, Kabashima, and Saganoshima Islands) participated in this survey. Table 2 shows their basic characteristics, and Table 3 summarizes their social network status. Excluding missing responses (“No response” in the table), significant differences were observed between the PRI and SRIs in age (P=0.014), level of education (P=0.001), vehicle ownership (P<0.001), number of close friends (P=0.012), and frequency of communication with them (P=0.012). Compared with respondents from the PRI, those from the SRIs included a greater proportion of adults aged ≥80 years and a lower proportion of those with a higher level of education and private vehicle ownership. Discussion

To examine the differences in health self-management behaviors among island residents with illnesses based on the degree of isolation, we conducted a questionnaire survey focusing on preventive health and illness coping behaviors among medical care recipients in clinics on a PRI and SRIs administered by Goto City, Nagasaki Prefecture, Japan. There were no significant differences in preventive health behaviors related to daily lifestyle or attendance at general health checkups between the PRI and SRIs. However, annual participation in lung and colorectal cancer screening was higher on the PRI than on the SRIs. Regarding illness coping behaviors, significant differences were detected in two items. A smaller proportion of SRI respondents reported searching for relevant information before deciding whether to visit or contact a hospital or clinic. A smaller proportion also reported deciding on their treatment plan in consultation with a doctor. Conversely, a larger proportion reported not caring, even when feeling unwell.

Basic characteristics and social networks

The level of education was generally lower among respondents on the SRIs than on the PRI. More than half of the respondents on the SRIs had not attended or graduated from high school, likely because there are no high schools on these islands. In addition, a greater proportion of respondents on the SRIs did not own a vehicle, despite the absence of public transportation on Kabashima and Saganoshima Islands. Many residents likely rely on friends or neighbors with vehicles when they need to travel long distances. Consistent with this suggestion, SRI respondents reported having a larger number of close friends and communicating with them more frequently than PRI respondents. Although not statistically significant, 35.5% of respondents on the SRIs reported having more than 10 close neighbors, and 26.6% communicated with their neighbors almost daily. Both proportions were higher than those on the PRI. These findings suggest that social networks are broader and stronger on more isolated islands. Such networks may also include close relationships with medical professionals, which is discussed later.

Health self-management behaviors

Given the limited healthcare resources on the SRIs, particularly for medical emergencies, residents with illnesses may be expected to show more active engagement in preventive health behaviors. A previous study reported healthier lifestyle habits among island residents than among those living in urban settings, although the study included participants regardless of their illness status and examined only a single island (Ojikajima Island)8).

In our comparison of multiple islands with different degrees of isolation, we found several differences in health self-management behaviors, consistent with our original expectations. While no significant differences were observed in lifestyle factors according to isolation, the rates of annual lung and colorectal cancer screening participation were significantly lower on the SRIs than on the PRI. A national survey in 2022 reported screening attendance rates among adults aged ≥40 years of 45.0% for lung cancer and 41.5% for colorectal cancer14). Screening attendance in our study was lower than these national values, particularly on the SRIs. The observed difference between the PRI and SRIs was likely attributable to service availability. According to the official list of medical institutions providing cancer screening published by the local government15), lung cancer screening is unavailable on all three SRIs, and colorectal cancer screening is available only on Kabashima and Saganoshima Islands, not on Hisakajima Island. Some residents may not consider it worthwhile to travel to the PRI for these screening examinations. These findings suggest that residents with illnesses living in more isolated communities do not necessarily engage in preventive health behaviors, despite having limited access to healthcare resources.

Similarly, respondents on the SRIs showed lower rates of illness coping behaviors. More than 60% did not search for relevant information when feeling unwell before deciding whether to visit a hospital or clinic, and approximately 40% reported not caring, even when feeling unwell. In addition, the proportion of respondents who decided on their treatment plan in consultation with a doctor was lower on the SRIs than on the PRI, while a higher proportion left treatment decisions entirely to the doctor, although these differences were not statistically significant.

There are multiple possible explanations for this relatively low level of participation in health self-management behaviors. First, residents of the SRIs may have limited options due to restricted availability and accessibility of healthcare resources, even if they are concerned about their condition or possess information about their health. Such attitudes may have developed over time, as more than 75% of SRI respondents had lived on the same island for more than 20 years. Second, residents may rely heavily on local healthcare providers and therefore engage less actively in independent self-management. Doctors and nurses in clinics on SRIs often live in the same local community and maintain close relationships with residents. Although the quality of available medical equipment is lower on SRIs, the number of hospitals and clinics per capita and the number of healthcare workers per capita are higher than on the PRI, which may facilitate closer relationships between residents and providers. Indeed, 60.9% of the respondents on the SRIs answered “strongly agree” to the statement “When I feel unwell, I immediately contact or see a doctor”, while the proportion was 35.4% on the PRI. Nurses on the SRIs (Kabashima Island and Saganoshima Island) reported frequent contact about minor concerns. Such close relationships with healthcare providers have both benefits and drawbacks. For example, although they enable individualized and responsive care, frequent clinic visits may increase the burden on the healthcare system.

Limitations

We compared health self-management behaviors among recipients of medical care between a PRI (Fukuejima Island) and three SRIs (Hisakajima, Kabashima, and Saganoshima Islands) in Nagasaki Prefecture, Japan. Notably, the questionnaire items on illness coping behavior have not been validated. In addition, since we compared multiple variables, multiplicity cannot be ignored. Although we identified several differences between these areas, they may not have been attributable solely to the degree of isolation of the islands. For example, levels of education and vehicle ownership differed between the PRI and SRIs, and these factors may also influence health behaviors. While this study primarily focused on geographic differences in health behaviors, the lack of an adjusted model accounting for potential confounders, such as education or vehicle ownership, represents a limitation. These socioeconomic differences may themselves reflect varying degrees of isolation. Larger and more detailed studies are warranted to clarify the factors driving differences in health self-management behaviors. Although telemedicine is becoming increasingly common, it was not considered in this study. A pilot program for drone-based medication delivery is currently underway on Fukuejima and Hisakajima Islands; however, it is not available on Kabashima and Saganoshima Islands, where no pharmacies operate. Therefore, the use of online medical services is likely minimal in the study region, particularly on the SRIs. With the continued expansion of telemedicine, future studies should examine its potential effects on residents’ health self-management behaviors.

Conclusion

In this study, the differences in health self-management behaviors among island residents with illnesses based on the degree of isolation were examined. Preventive health behaviors differed in the use of cancer screening services, with lower rates of attendance for lung and colorectal cancer screening on the more isolated islands, likely due to reduced availability and accessibility. Illness coping behaviors also varied according to isolation. Residents on the more isolated islands were less likely to search for health information and were more likely not to care, even when feeling unwell. Overall, health self-management behaviors were less active on the more isolated islands, potentially reflecting limited healthcare resources and close relationships with local medical professionals. These findings suggest that geographic isolation influences how residents manage their health and should be recognized as a distinct form of geographic disparity. It is important to consider these behavioral differences when allocating medical resources and planning telemedicine services.
