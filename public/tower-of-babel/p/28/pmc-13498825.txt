Evaluating the Association of Surgical Setting With Costs and Complications in Ankle Arthrodesis: Medicare Claims Analysis
Authors: Hyunkyu Ko, Brook I Martin, Sean Parham, Devon Nixon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Ankle arthrodesis (AA) remains a reliable surgical treatment option for end-stage ankle arthritis in appropriately selected patients. Although traditionally performed in an inpatient (IP) setting, interest in these surgeries being done as an outpatient (OP) and ambulatory surgery center (ASC) settings has increased. We compared costs and post-operative complication rates for AA performed across these settings.Methods:Using Medicare fee-for-service claims (2016-2021), we retrospectively identified patients ≥65 years of age who underwent AA. Using subsequent inpatient and Part B (provider) claims, we evaluated postoperative complications within 1 year, defined by International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes, including infection, thromboembolic events, and device-related issues. We also evaluated total episode and post-acute care costs within 90 days. We tested for differences in perioperative safety indicators and costs through multivariate regressions, controlling for age, sex, race, and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), dementia, and the involvement of a surgical assistant. A propensity-matched analysis was performed to minimize selection by indication bias.Results:We included 7104 initial AA cases across surgical settings: 262 (3.7%) in ASC, 3047 (42.9%) in OP, and 3795 (53.4%) in IP. Compared with IP cases, OP and ASC surgeries were performed in younger, healthier (as assessed by CCI) patients and more frequently in male patients (P < .001). In multivariate analyses, IP cases had the highest infection rates (+7.2 percentage points compared with ASC, P = .001; +4.2 percentage points compared to OP, P = .016) and the highest 90-day total episode costs of $19 817 (95% CI: $18 137 to $21 497). Propensity-matched analyses were consistent with multivariable results, confirming the same directional associations but with modestly larger effect sizes.Conclusion:AA performed in ASC settings was associated with lower complication rates and substantially lower episode-of-care costs compared with IP and OP settings in this Medicare fee-for-service population, particularly among appropriately selected patients.Level of Evidence:Level III, retrospective comparative study.Keywords: ankle arthrodesis, surgical setting, postoperative complications, health care costs, Medicare claims

Introduction

Ankle arthrodesis (AA) is a surgical procedure that achieves tibiotalar joint fusion to manage pain and functional impairment associated with end-stage ankle arthritis.1-5 Traditionally performed in inpatient (IP) settings because of its complexity and need for postoperative monitoring, AA has increasingly transitioned to outpatient (OP) settings6,7 and ambulatory surgery centers (ASCs). 8 These settings differ in resources, perioperative protocols, and patient selection criteria, which may influence clinical outcomes and costs. Understanding these variations is essential for optimizing care and resource.9,10

Comparing complications and costs across surgical settings is challenging because of substantial variability in patient complexity.11,12 ASCs offer a cost-efficient option with same-day discharge. However, their suitability for AA may be limited by patient comorbidities and the need for postoperative monitoring.6,13 OP facilities also provide same-day discharge and lower costs but are generally reserved for less complex cases. 13 In contrast, IP settings accommodate patients with significant comorbidities and complex surgical needs, requiring specialized anesthesia, closer intraoperative monitoring, and extended postoperative care. 6 Although IP settings incur higher costs, they remain essential for achieving optimal outcomes in high-risk populations. Consequently, postoperative outcomes and costs are not directly comparable across settings because of differences in patient complexity.

Previous studies have examined differences across surgical settings, but most focused primarily on inpatient vs outpatient comparisons and were limited by small sample sizes (eg, n = 40 in Oh et al 14 ).15,16 To address this gap, we analyzed complications and costs associated with AA across ASC, OP, and IP settings using a large Medicare claims data set. We hypothesized that, after adjusting for observable patient and procedural characteristics, AA performed in ASCs would be associated with fewer postoperative complications and lower costs compared with other surgical settings. However, because patient characteristics likely differ across ASC, OP, and IP settings, residual confounding cannot be fully eliminated and should be considered when interpreting these associations.

Methods

We performed a retrospective analysis of Medicare claims from 2016 to 2021, including fee-for-service beneficiaries aged 65 years or older who underwent AA, identified through provider-submitted Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes 27870 (Open Ankle Arthrodesis) and 29899 (Arthroscopic Ankle Arthrodesis). Cases with co-existing procedural codes indicating revision were excluded to improve specificity of the cohort. Beneficiaries eligible for Medicare through End-Stage Renal Disease, Social Security Disability Insurance, or Medicare-HMO programs (eg, Medicare Advantage), as well as those with unclear surgical setting information due to incomplete linkage between Part B carrier claims and institutional (inpatient/outpatient) claims files, were excluded. Exclusion criteria are detailed in Figure 1.

Exclusion criteria flow diagram.

Independent Variable

Medicare differentiates ASCs, OP, and IP through distinct claims files. We first identified procedures using Part B carrier claims, which include an indicator for services performed in ASCs. These claims were then linked to inpatient and outpatient institutional claims using beneficiary identifiers and procedure dates to assign OP or IP setting when applicable. Using this 2-step approach, we grouped patients by procedure setting as ASC, IP, and OP to enable comparative analysis of postoperative outcomes across settings.

Dependent Variables

Postoperative complications were identified from inpatient and Part B claims within 90 and 365 days following discharge. Complications were defined using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), codes and included musculoskeletal complications (primarily postoperative pain and hematoma), infection, cerebrovascular events, vascular complications, pulmonary embolism (PE), deep vein thrombosis (DVT), and device related issues (Table A1). To ensure complete 365-day follow-up, patients treated during the final quarter of 2021 were excluded from 90-day analyses and patients treated in 2021 were excluded from 1-year analyses. Thus, differences in denominators across follow-up periods reflect follow-up eligibility rather than censoring during observation. The composite complication outcome captured the occurrence of any listed complication. All-cause readmissions were identified from inpatient claims, and mortality was assessed separately using validated date-of-death data from the Master Beneficiary Summary File and was not included in the composite outcome.

Cost outcomes were derived from Medicare payments aggregated from the index admission through 90 days post discharge to capture the full episode of care, including inpatient facility costs, hospital outpatient services, Part B professional fees, skilled nursing facility care, home health agency services, and durable medical equipment.

Covariates

Covariates included demographic, clinical, and provider characteristics, given their potential association with postoperative outcomes. Demographics included age (5-year increments), sex, and race, classified as Black (Black or African American), White (White or Caucasian), and Other (including Asian, Hispanic ethnicity, and Native American). Clinical variables included comorbidity, measured using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) derived from ICD-10 codes in Medicare Part B claims during a 90-day preoperative look-back period, reflecting comorbidities documented in recent health care encounters, dementia, osteoporosis, and osteoarthritis.17,18 Provider-related factors included provider type (orthopaedic surgeon or podiatrist) and presence of a surgical assistant. Because claims-based setting designations do not reflect actual length of hospitalization, and some IP stays were ≤1 day whereas some OP cases exceeded 1 day, length of stay (LOS) was included as a covariate (0, 1, 2, or ≥3 days) to account for hospitalization duration and care intensity that may influence cost outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

We examined unadjusted differences in complication rates and costs across surgical settings using Welch t tests for continuous outcomes and χ2 tests for categorical variables. Complications were analyzed using logistic regression, and costs using generalized linear models with a gamma distribution and log link to account for skewness. 19 All models adjusted for demographic (age, sex, race), clinical (CCI, dementia, osteoporosis, osteoarthritis), and provider factors (provider type, surgical assistant), with standard errors clustered at the hospital level. Results were reported as estimated marginal means or odds ratios, with statistical significance defined at α = 0.05. Analyses of individual complication outcomes were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, whereas the composite complication outcome represented the primary confirmatory endpoint. Year fixed effects accounted for temporal variation, including COVID-19. E-values were calculated to assess robustness to unmeasured confounding.

To mitigate potential selection bias, we performed propensity score–matched analyses to estimate the average treatment effects for IP and OP relative to ASCs. Propensity score matching addressed non-random treatment assignment in observational studies.20,21 We implemented 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching within the region of common support using the same covariates with replacement and a caliper of 0.25 SDs. 22 Covariate balance was assessed using density plots. We additionally performed a risk stratification analysis to estimate predicted 90-day complication risk and examined risk tertiles across settings (Appendix A).

As secondary analyses, we conducted a direct comparison between IP and OP settings using the same multivariable regression framework. We also performed a sensitivity analysis redefining IP and OP status based on observed LOS (OP = LOS 0-1 day; IP = LOS ≥2 days), with ASC cases unchanged. Because LOS defined the exposure in this analysis, it was not included as a covariate in these models. All primary complication and cost models were re-estimated under this classification (Appendix B).

A data use agreement was established with Medicare, and the University of Utah Institutional Review Board granted a waiver of human subjects review. All analyses were conducted using Stata/MP version 18.0 within the Virtual Research Data Center (https://resdac.org/cms-virtual-research-data-center-vrdc).

Results

Study Population

Descriptive statistics for Medicare beneficiaries undergoing AA by care setting (ASC, OP, IP) are presented in Table 1. Among 7104 patients, 262 (3.7%) were treated in ASCs, 3047 (42.9%) in OP settings, and 3795 (53.4%) in IP settings. Mean age differed modestly but significantly across settings (ASC: 72.4 years; OP/IP: 73.2 years; P < .001), with patients aged ≥80 years more common in IP (19.8%) than OP (13.1%) or ASC (9.2%). Differences were also observed in sex, race, and comorbidity. Male patients were more frequently treated in ASCs (58.4%), whereas females predominated in IP settings (54.4%). ASCs had the highest proportion of patients without comorbidities (46.9%), whereas IP cases more often involved ≥2 comorbidities (55.9%), higher dementia prevalence (9.2%), and longer length of stay (≥2 days: 74.9%). Substantial variation was also observed in clinical characteristics across settings, with osteoarthritis less common in IP (72.0%) and OP (85.1%) settings than in ASCs (96.2%) (P < .001), and osteoporosis more common in IP (7.2%) and OP (6.0%) settings than in ASCs (3.8%) (P = .027). Patients excluded due to missing surgical setting information had broadly similar baseline characteristics compared to the analytic cohort (Table A2).

In addition, IP settings were associated with substantially higher costs than ASC and OP settings. Adjusted 90-day total episode of care costs were $19 817 (95% CI: $18 137-$21 497) higher for IP and $7199 (95% CI: $5704-$8694) higher for OP relative to ASCs. Index admission costs were higher for IP compared with ASCs ($7895; 95% CI: $7228-$8562) and OP ($2856; 95% CI: $2236-$3476). Skilled nursing facility costs were $6974 higher for IP (95% CI: $6042-$7905) and $1016 higher for OP (95% CI: $710-$1322) compared with ASCs, whereas home health agency costs were $1654 higher for IP (95% CI: $1533-$1776) and $1100 higher for OP (95% CI: $999-$1201) relative to ASCs.

The E-values indicate that these findings were robust to potential unmeasured confounding (Figure A1).

Results From Propensity Score–Matched Models

To address potential selection bias, we conducted propensity score–matched analyses using 1:1 nearest neighbor matching, yielding matched cohorts of 261 patients per group for each pairwise comparison. Covariate balance improved across ASC, OP, and IP groups, as evidenced by greater overlap in propensity score distributions (Figure 2). Results from the matched analyses (Table 3, columns (c)-(d); Table 4, column (b)) were consistent with the primary multivariable models, with similar directions and generally larger effect sizes.

Complication patterns in the matched cohort mirrored those of the primary analyses. At 90 days, both OP and IP settings had higher overall complication rates than ASC, including higher rates of musculoskeletal complications, infection, DVT, and readmissions, whereas PE and device-related complications were elevated only in IP settings. At 1 year, infection and musculoskeletal complication rates remained significantly higher for IP compared with ASC. IP settings also continued to demonstrate the highest 90-day episode of care costs, driven by higher index admission, skilled nursing facility, and home health agency costs relative to ASC.

Risk Stratification Analysis

To further characterize patient selection across settings, we conducted a risk stratification analysis using preoperative characteristics to estimate 90-day complication risk. Predicted risk distributions varied substantially by setting (Table A3): 15.0% of IP patients were classified as low risk, whereas only 1.5% of ASC patients were classified as high risk. Additional results are provided in Appendix A.

Secondary and Sensitivity Analyses

We conducted additional analyses, including direct IP vs OP comparisons and an LOS-based reclassification sensitivity analysis, to assess robustness. IP vs OP comparisons confirmed higher complication rates and costs in IP settings, and LOS based reclassification did not materially change the primary findings. Detailed results are provided in Appendix B.

Discussion

Our analyses of a Medicare fee-for-service population revealed notable differences in complication rates across surgical settings. Even after adjusting for patient characteristics, including CCI, IP settings were associated with higher overall complication rates. This pattern is not unexpected, as patients treated in IP settings are often more medically complex and may carry higher perioperative risk that cannot be fully captured by administrative data. Although we adjusted for a broad set of observable characteristics to make comparisons across settings as fair as possible, unmeasured factors related to case complexity and perioperative risk likely remain and may be associated with the higher complication rates observed in IP settings.

The pattern of complications also differed across follow-up periods. At 90 days, multiple complications were more common in IP settings, whereas at 1 year, infection and musculoskeletal complications remained higher in IP settings. The propensity score–matched analysis showed similar directional patterns, indicating that findings are robust to model specifications. This consistency across analytic approaches suggests that observed differences may reflect unmeasured factors not captured in administrative data. However, given the relatively small ASC cohort, safety estimates for ASC should be interpreted with caution, as nonsignificant findings, particularly for rare outcomes and in propensity score–matched analyses, may reflect limited statistical power rather than equivalence between groups.

In addition to postoperative outcome differences, AA procedures performed in IP settings were associated with significantly higher 90-day episode of care costs compared with those performed in ASC and OP settings. These findings highlight the potential economic advantage of shifting appropriate AA cases to ASCs, which were associated with lower observed complication rates and substantially lower costs. Such cost savings may be particularly meaningful for health systems operating under value-based care models, where reducing expenditures without compromising quality is a central objective. The present study is specific to ankle arthrodesis and should not be assumed to be directly generalizable to other orthopaedic procedures. Nevertheless, these results may inform considerations of surgical setting selection and resource use, particularly for appropriately selected patients.

These findings highlight the need for careful patient selection when determining the appropriate setting for AA. Although AA performed in ASC settings was associated with lower observed complication rates, the IP setting remains appropriate for individuals who may benefit from additional monitoring and resources, including those with substantial comorbidities, limited physiologic reserve, complex deformities, or elevated perioperative risk. We observed higher postoperative complication rates in IP settings compared with ASCs, but these differences may reflect unmeasured patient factors, differences in case mix, or aspects of care delivery. Consistent with differences in case mix, the markedly higher prevalence of osteoarthritis among ASC patients suggests a more homogeneous and potentially less complex diagnostic population compared with IP settings, which may include a broader range of etiologies such as post-traumatic or inflammatory conditions. Our risk stratification analysis further contextualizes these findings by demonstrating overlap in baseline risk profiles across settings. Notably, a subset of patients treated in IP settings had predicted risk profiles similar to those treated in ASC or OP settings. Together, these findings reinforce the importance of thoughtful patient selection and suggest that risk-based approaches may help inform surgical setting decisions, underscoring the role of clinical judgment and individualized assessment.

Although this study employed rigorous statistical methods, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although this study used advanced statistical techniques to mitigate selection bias including generalized linear models, logistic regression models, and propensity score–matched models, these approaches can only adjust for observed variables. Important aspects of clinical complexity, symptom acuity or duration, functional status, socioeconomic status, and intraoperative decision making are not captured in administrative claims and therefore could not be incorporated. Furthermore, the 90-day preoperative look-back period may underestimate chronic disease burden, especially among ASC patients with fewer recent health care encounters, which should be considered when interpreting results. Consequently, residual confounding from unmeasured factors may persist. Analyses of individual complications should also be interpreted cautiously given multiple comparisons and are considered exploratory. Additionally, because ASC cases were identified using claims data and Part B indicators, some procedures may not have been captured, potentially underestimating ASC volume. In addition, approximately 10% of the cohort was excluded because of missing surgical setting information resulting from incomplete claims linkage, which may introduce selection bias, although the direction and magnitude of this bias cannot be determined from the available data. Furthermore, suppression of small cell counts in accordance with CMS data use policies (requiring suppression of cell counts containing fewer than 11 observations) may limit interpretability, particularly for rare complications. Also, our study focused solely on Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries who underwent AA, which may limit the generalizability. Consequently, the results may not be applicable to the broader population or other patient groups. CPT codes do not fully distinguish primary from revision or staged ankle arthrodesis procedures. Although cases with co-existing revision procedure codes were excluded, some revision or staged procedures not explicitly coded as such may remain in the cohort. Additionally, differential mortality across care settings introduces a competing risk for non-fatal 1-year outcomes. Patients in the IP cohort experienced higher mortality at 1-year compared with OP and ambulatory surgery center (ASC) cohorts, and those who die cannot subsequently experience non-fatal complications such as infection or device-related events. This may lead to underestimation of complication rates in higher-mortality groups and attenuation of between-group differences. We did not perform formal competing risk analyses (eg, Fine-Gray models), and these findings should be interpreted accordingly. Lastly, our study focused on 90-day and 1-year outcomes, which may not capture long-term effects. Although our findings provide valuable insights into short- and medium-term outcomes, longer follow-up periods may offer a more comprehensive understanding of the complication and cost differences in AA across settings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, AA performed in ASC settings was associated with lower complication rates and episode-of-care costs compared with IP and OP settings in this Medicare fee-for-service population, particularly among appropriately selected patients. These results should be interpreted in the context of the observational design, limited ASC sample size, and residual confounding. Further prospective studies incorporating patient-reported outcomes and cost-effectiveness are needed to better inform surgical setting selection.

Appendix A: Risk Stratification Analysis

Methods.

To evaluate overlap in baseline postoperative risk across surgical settings, we conducted an exploratory risk stratification analysis. A multivariable logistic regression model was fit to estimate the probability of a 90-day composite complication using preoperative patient characteristics only. Covariates included age category, sex, race, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), dementia, osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, provider type (orthopaedic surgeon or not), and presence of a surgical assistant. Surgical setting and length of stay were not included in the model.

Predicted probabilities from this model were used to classify patients into tertiles of baseline complication risk. The lowest tertile was defined as low risk, and the highest tertile was defined as high risk. We then examined the distribution of risk strata across IP, OP, and ASC settings and descriptively compared observed outcomes within risk strata.

Results

The risk stratification model demonstrated modest discrimination (c-statistic = 0.656), consistent with claims-based prediction models using preoperative variables.

The distribution of predicted 90-day complication risk across surgical settings is presented in Appendix Table A3. Among patients treated in ASC settings, 84.0% were classified as low risk and only 1.5% as high risk. In contrast, 50.6% of patients treated in IP settings were classified as high risk, whereas 15.0% were classified as low risk. Patients treated in OP settings demonstrated an intermediate distribution of baseline risk (51.0% low risk and 15.3% high risk).

These findings indicate substantial separation in baseline risk profiles across surgical settings, with ASC utilization largely concentrated among lower-risk patients and IP utilization concentrated among higher-risk patients. However, some overlap was observed, particularly among patients treated in IP classified as low risk (n = 569) and patients treated in OP classified as high risk (n = 466). Because this analysis is descriptive and observational, it does not imply that individual patients would have experienced equivalent outcomes if treated in alternative settings. Rather, it provides a descriptive assessment of baseline risk overlap that may inform future research on risk-based approaches to surgical setting selection.

Appendix B. Secondary and Sensitivity Analyses

Direct comparison between OP and IP.

To further assess differences between IP and OP settings, we directly compared IP and OP cases. Consistent with the primary analyses, IP cases demonstrated higher postoperative complication rates at 90 days, including any complication, infection, cerebrovascular and vascular events, deep vein thrombosis, device-related complications, mortality, and readmissions, with musculoskeletal complications as the primary exception (Table A4). At 1 year, IP cases continued to exhibit higher rates of most complications and mortality, whereas musculoskeletal complication rates were lower. Overall, these findings confirm that IP settings are associated with substantially greater costs and higher complication risks compared with OP settings. IP cases were also associated with significantly higher costs, including 90-day total episode-of-care, index admission, skilled nursing facility, and home health agency costs (Table A5).

Redefining IP and OP based on LOS.

To evaluate the robustness of our findings to potential misclassification of surgical setting in administrative claims, we also performed a sensitivity analysis redefining IP and OP status based on observed LOS, with OP defined as LOS 0-1 day and IP defined as LOS ≥2 days, while leaving ASC cases unchanged. Results from this LOS-based sensitivity analysis were consistent with the primary analyses. Reclassification based on LOS did not materially change the direction or statistical significance of the associations between surgical setting and postoperative complications (Table A6), or costs (Table A7).
