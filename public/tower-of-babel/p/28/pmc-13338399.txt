Durability and heavy-metal stabilization of electrolytic manganese residue–steel slag cementitious binders under water, sulfate and chloride exposure
Authors: Jinyuan Liu, Zhonghu Wu, Shaoyun Pu, Yao Wang, Zheng Feng, Ronghua Zhang, Bo Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrolytic manganese residue (EMR) and steel slag (SS) are major industrial byproducts with distinct chemical and environmental characteristics. EMR contains high levels of leachable NH4+ and Mn2+, whereas SS is mainly composed of Ca, Si, Fe and Mg bearing phases and may also contain trace metal-bearing components. To evaluate the durability and heavy-metal leaching behavior of electrolytic manganese residue–steel slag cementitious materials (ESM), binder specimens with different EMR/SS ratios were exposed to distilled water, sodium sulfate solution, and sodium chloride solution. Their compressive strength, strength erosion coefficient, leaching characteristics, and microstructural evolution were systematically investigated. The microstructure of the specimens was also characterized by XRD, FTIR, SEM and MIP. The results indicate that an appropriate EMR/SS ratio contributed to improved strength retention under the tested exposure conditions, and specimens with EMR contents ≤ 40% showed better erosion resistance than the EMR50% group. NaCl solution had the greatest negative effect, followed by Na2SO4 solution, distilled water had the least effect, and Friedel’s salt was formed by the erosion of NaCl solution. The decomposition of the amorphous network-like gel occurred, and the dissolution of soluble substances increased the pore size of the specimens, which was the main reason for the decrease in strength. The NH4+ concentration in the ESM leachate was below the detection limit. -Mn2+ was obtained mainly in the form of H2Mn8O16·2.4H2O with a curing rate greater than 99.99%. The leaching concentration of heavy metals was in accordance with the Class IV groundwater standard as stipulated in the Chinese standard (GB/T 14,848-2017). These findings suggest that EMR–SS–GGBS binders can achieve acceptable durability and effective heavy-metal immobilization under the tested accelerated exposure conditions when the EMR content is controlled at an appropriate level.

Introduction

Electrolytic manganese residue (EMR) is a byproduct of the electrolytic manganese metal (EMM) production process1,2. China is the world’s largest producer of EMM, with more than 98% of global EMM produced in China3. Approximately 160 million tonnes of EMR are produced in China, and this number will increase at a rate of more than 10 million tonnes per year4,5. High concentrations of NH4+ and heavy metal ions (Mn2+, Pb2+, Cu2+, Ni2+ and Zn2+) are present in EMR, posing a serious threat to the environment during long-term storage6. These harmful ions can easily migrate into the surrounding environment, affecting human health, plants and aquatic organisms7. The use of some or all of the EMR cement as a cementitious material not only achieves the efficient use of waste residues but also reduces the mining and waste of these resources8–10. Xu et al.11 reported that sulphate in EMR contributes to the depolymerization and hydration of blast furnace slag with increasing Ca(OH)2 content in the gel material. The gel material has a good curing effect on heavy metals in EMR. The main reason is that the heavy metals replace Si, and the heavy metals are sealed in the three-dimensional network structure of [SiO4] and [AlO4]. Liu et al.12 investigated the volcanic ash activity of EMR calcined in an electric furnace. The EMR calcined at 700–800 °C for 3 h had good volcanic ash activity, and the CaO released from the decomposition of anhydrite gypsum with poor crystallinity stimulated the volcanic ash activity of the EMR at this temperature. The EMR calcined at 800 °C had a good solidification effect on heavy metals such as Sr, Cr, Zn, As, Co, Cu, and Ni.

Steel slag (SS) is a byproduct of converting iron to steel in a converter or melting scrap iron to steel in an electric furnace13–15. Steel lag minerals include C3S, C2S, C4AF, C2F, the RO phase (CaO-FeO-MnO-MgO solid solution), free CaO and free MgO16. The volume stability of steel slag is mainly determined by the content of free CaO and free MgO17. The presence of C3S, C2S, C4AF and C2F improves the gelling properties of steel furnace slag17,18. The C3S content of steel slag is much lower than that of silicate cement; therefore, steel slag can be regarded as a weak silicate cement clinker19,20. The presence of NaOH accelerates the hydration of steel slag15,21. Fly ash can be used in conjunction with steel slag to make Portland steel slag fly ash cement (PSSFAC)22. These materials exhibit lower drying shrinkage and better corrosion resistance23. Ground steel slag also showed better gelling properties in the presence of alkaline activators due to the potentially high content of free CaO and MgO and was used in conjunction with blast furnace slag to eliminate stability problems22,24. Lu et al.25 found that alkali-activated BFS-steel-slag cement pastes consisting of moderate amounts of BFS and steel slag had a denser structure than pure alkali-activated BFS cement pastes . The appropriate replacement of BFS with steel slag will also reduce shrinkage and increase the abrasion resistance of cement paste and concrete26. Alkali-activated steel slag-blast furnace slag cement has excellent corrosion resistance27.

The erosion resistance of structures is inevitably a problem in engineering and construction. For example, due to the erosion of sulphate ions and chloride ions, the hardened body of cementitious materials is subjected not only to water erosion but also to the erosion of ions in water, which is a great test of its service ability28,29. Chloride-related deterioration and corrosion are widely recognized as major causes of durability loss in cement-based materials and reinforced concrete structures30. This not only accelerates structural deterioration and shortens service life but also increases life-cycle costs due to frequent monitoring and regular maintenance and repairs31. Therefore, it is important to develop cementitious materials with good erosion resistance and long service life32. Durability studies have been conducted on a number of alkali-inspired materials33. Recent studies on sustainable cementitious composites have also shown that durability performance is closely related to matrix densification, pore-structure refinement and resistance to ion ingress34 However, little research has been reported on the erosion resistance of EMR-prepared cementitious materials.

This paper investigated the erosion resistance and environmental safety of electrolytic manganese residue-steel slag cementitious material (ESM) prepared with different ratios of EMR and SS. The microstructure under different erosion environments was also studied in depth by SEM, XRD and FTIR. This research contributes to the reuse of EMR and SS as high value-added recycled materials that can be used to produce safe and durable cementitious materials.

Materials and methods

Raw materials

The raw materials used in this test included EMR, SS, and S95 grade ground blast furnace slag powder (GGBS). The EMR came from a manganese company in Tongren, Guizhou. The EMR at the site was black mud with a moisture content of 28.26% and a density of 1.14 g/cm3. The EMR was dried at 105 °C for 3 h in an air blast drying oven and then crushed using a disc crusher. After crushing, the sample was placed in a boiling furnace at 800 °C for 3 h12,35. After calcination, the EMR was crushed through a 100 mesh sieve. SS and GGBS were purchased from a processing plant in Zhengzhou, Henan Province. The alkali activator was a sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solid purchased from Tianchen Chemical Co., Ltd. NaOH is a white flaky solid that is analytically pure and has a purity of ≥ 98.0%. Erosion tests were carried out on the specimens using distilled water, sodium chloride (NaCl) solution and sodium sulphate (Na2SO4) solution. NaCl was purchased from Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. and was analytically pure (content ≥ 99.5%, pH 5.0–8.0). Na2SO4, which was analytically pure (content ≥ 99.0%, pH 5.0–8.0), was purchased from Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Anhydrous ethanol was used to terminate the hydration of the specimen. Anhydrous ethanol was purchased from Chongqing Chuandong Chemical (Group) Co., Ltd. and was analytically pure with a content ≥ 99.7%.

Design of the experimental plan and preparation of samples

Design of the experimental plan

EMR/SS-based cementitious materials (ESM) were prepared by fixing the GGBS content at 30 wt.% and varying the relative proportions of EMR and SS within the remaining 70 wt.%. This design was adopted for two reasons. First, GGBS was used as a stable source of reactive Ca–Si–Al components to ensure sufficient alkali-activated hydration and gel formation in the binder matrix. Second, fixing the GGBS content allowed the influence of the EMR/SS ratio to be isolated without introducing an additional variable associated with GGBS dosage. A 30 wt.% GGBS content was selected based on our previous study36, in which this proportion provided adequate mechanical performance while maintaining a high total utilization level of EMR and SS.

Distilled water was used to evaluate the stability of the binder matrix under aqueous leaching conditions, whereas Na2SO4 and NaCl solutions were used to represent typical sulfate- and chloride-rich aggressive environments. A concentration of 5 wt.% was adopted as an accelerated exposure condition to induce measurable deterioration within the 56-day testing period. This concentration is sufficiently aggressive to distinguish the durability response of different mixtures, while avoiding excessively high concentrations that may cause unrealistic damage mechanisms. Since the objective of this study was to compare the relative resistance of different EMR/SS ratios under representative aggressive media, only one concentration level was used for each salt solution.

Preparation of specimens

First, NaOH was weighed and dissolved in distilled water to prepare the alkali activator, which was sealed and left for 24 h. Second, according to the mixture proportions listed in Table 3, the raw material powders were weighed and poured into the NJ-160A cement net mortar mixer, and the three powders were first stirred dry at a slow speed for 3 min to fully mix the three powders and then poured into the configured alkali activator solution for automatic mixing. Third, after mixing was completed, the slurry was poured into a triple test mould of 40 mm × 40 mm × 160 mm, and the sample was poured according to the Chinese standard (GB/T 17,671-1999)39. After pouring, the test mould was placed into a standard curing box (temperature 20 ± 2 °C, relative humidity ≥ 95%) for 24 h and then demoulded. After demoulding, the specimens were numbered and placed back into the curing box for curing. After curing for 28 days, specimens with each EMR/SS ratio were divided into four exposure groups: distilled-water exposure, Na2SO4 exposure, NaCl exposure and an uneroded control group. The control specimens, corresponding to ESM-16 to ESM-20 in Table 3, were kept under standard curing conditions without immersion in any exposure solution and were used as reference specimens for calculating the strength erosion coefficient. At erosion times of 3, 14, 28 and 56 days, the specimens were removed from the solution for compressive strength and corresponding microscopic tests. Toxicity leaching test

The 28-day toxicity leaching of the EMR and ESM specimens was performed with reference to the Chinese standard (HJ/T 299-2007)36. A mixture of sulphuric acid and nitric acid at a mass ratio of 2:1 was dissolved in distilled water, and the pH was adjusted to 3.20 ± 0.05. Crushed samples were taken at the end of the compressive test, crushed and passed through a 9.5 mm sieve. The samples were mixed with the extract at a ratio of 1:10 (ml/g), placed in a horizontal thermostatic oscillator with an oscillation frequency of 110 ± 10 times/min and leached for 8 h at room temperature. The leachate was then left horizontally for 16 h, after which it was filtered through a 0.45 mm filter membrane. The Mn2+ and NH4+ concentrations in the leach solution were detected with reference to Chinese standards (GB 11,906–89 and HJ 535-2009)6,41.The leaching concentration measurement data of each group of 3 compressed test block fragments are automatically averaged and recorded. Leaching concentrations of heavy metal ions were determined using inductively coupled plasma‒optical emission spectrometry (ICP‒OES), and NH4+ concentrations were determined by nanoreagent spectrophotometry.

Microscopic characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was carried out using a German D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation, an operating current of 80 mA, an operating voltage of 60 kV, a scanning test range of 5–90° and a scanning speed of 5°/min. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed using a Zeiss Gemini 300 scanning electron microscope equipped with an OXFORD Xplore energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS). A representative specimen was selected for energy spectrum analysis (EDS) along with SEM examination. The mercury in pressure (MIP) test was carried out using a fully automated mercury in pressure (MIP) metre (AUTOPORE Iv 9510). After the compressive strength test, crushed specimens were taken for the MIP test. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed using a Nicolet 380 instrument. The FTIR spectra were collected in the range of 4000–400 cm−1.

Results and discussion

Strength properties

To maintain consistency with the mixture design in Table 3, the results are discussed according to EMR/SS ratio and exposure medium. The ESM codes in Table 3 define the complete specimen matrix, whereas the terms EMR10%, EMR20%, EMR30%, EMR40% and EMR50% are used in the following sections to represent the five EMR/SS ratios of 10/60, 20/50, 30/40, 40/30 and 50/20, respectively. This notation allows direct comparison of two factors: the effect of exposure medium at a fixed EMR/SS ratio and the effect of EMR/SS ratio under the same exposure condition.

Effect of distilled water erosion on strength

As defined in Sect. "Strength test", Cn is the strength erosion coefficient, calculated as the ratio of the compressive strength of an exposed specimen to that of the corresponding uneroded specimen at the same age. Therefore, Cn is dimensionless; Cn > 1 indicates apparent strength retention or increase relative to the reference specimen, whereas Cn < 1 indicates strength loss after exposure.

The compressive strength and erosion coefficient of the specimens under distilled water erosion are shown in Fig. 5. As shown in Fig. 5a, compared to the noneroded group, there was a decrease in intensity at 3 days, and after 3 days, the intensity started to increase. After 28 days, the strength of the specimens under erosive conditions was greater than that of the noneroded specimens for specimens with ≤ 40% EMR. This shows that the strength growth of specimens under distilled water erosion is greater than that of noneroded specimens. The strength of the EMR is 50%, which is close to that of the uneroded specimens; both of these values are greater than those of the eroded specimens at the other ratios. The EMR10%–EMR40% groups maintained relatively high strength retention under distilled-water exposure, whereas the EMR50% group showed a lower erosion coefficient at 56 days. This indicates that continued hydration of unreacted SS and EMR partially compensated for dissolution-induced degradation when the EMR content was not excessive. As shown in Fig. 5b, the strength erosion coefficients (Cn) were lower than 1 at 0–14 days except for that of EMR20% (Cn = 1.066). At 28–56 days, Cn was greater than 1 for all specimens except for the specimens with 50% EMR. At 56 days, Cn was 1.219, 1.209, 1.089, 1.183 and 0.964 for the specimens with 10%, 20%, 30%, 40% and 50% EMR, respectively. The larger the EMR content is, the smaller the compressive strength erosion factor.Fig. 5Compressive strength and erosion coefficient of specimens under distilled water erosion. Under distilled water erosion conditions, the specimen may be subjected to two effects. The first effect is negative. Ca(OH)2 and C–S–H gels with high Ca/Si ratios as well as other soluble substances were present in the hydration products of the specimens42. Under the erosion of distilled water, the soluble substances in the specimen will dissolve, resulting in an increase in internal porosity. C–S–H gels with high Ca/Si ratios require a highly alkaline environment to maintain their structural stability. Some of the alkaline material is dissolved in water, and a decrease in alkalinity leads to the decomposition of C–S–H gels43. The second effect is positive. Studies have shown that SS activity is low, hydration is very slow, and there is a possibility of long-term hydration15,17,19. Under distilled water erosion, water slowly intrudes into the interior of the specimen, supplying water to the unhydrated SS, which induces a continuous and slow hydration reaction between the SS and the partially unhydrated EMR, resulting in the production of gelling products that increase in strength. The above phenomenon may be a result of the interaction of these two effects.

Under Na2SO4 solution erosion, the specimen may be subjected to the following two effects. The first effect is negative. A negative effect under Na2SO4 solution erosion is also present under distilled water erosion, as described earlier. The presence of sulphate leads to the breaking of Si–O–Si and Al–Si chemical bonds in the structure of geopolymer-like cementitious materials, releasing Si4+ 44,45. This is strongly correlated with the alkalinity of the hardened specimen. The reaction of C–S–H gel with SO42− to form ettringite (AFt) will lead to expansion of the specimen volume, resulting in expansion stress, causing cracks inside the specimen and a decrease in the mechanical strength of the cementitious material44. The second effect is positive. A positive effect also existed under Na2SO4 solution erosion, as previously described for distilled water erosion. In addition, the C–S–H gel reacts with SO42− to produce AFt to fill the pore structure inside the hardened specimen46. A decrease in the porosity and increase in the compactness of the specimen increase the compressive strength. The above changes in strength may be the result of the interaction of these two effects.

Effect of sodium chloride solution erosion on strength

The compressive strength and erosion coefficients of the specimens eroded by NaCl solution are shown in Fig. 7. As shown in Fig. 7a, the strengths of EMR10%, EMR20%, EMR30% and EMR40% increased with increasing erosion age. The difference between the 56-day strength and that of the noneroded group was not significant. These results indicate relatively stable strength retention for the EMR10%–EMR40% groups under chloride exposure, although NaCl remained the most aggressive medium when the Cn trends were compared across the three exposure conditions47. The strength of the EMR50% group was less than that of the noneroded group throughout the erosion period. This indicates that it is less resistant to chloride erosion. At EMR contents ≤ 40%, the erosive effect of chloride ions on the strength is relatively small. At EMR contents ≥ 40%, the erosive effect of chloride ions on the strength is significant. As shown in Fig. 7b, the Cn of EMR10% was greater than 1 at 3 days and gradually decreased from 3 to 56 days to 0.877 at 56 days. Under NaCl solution erosion, the specimen may be subjected to the following two effects. The first effect is negative. Negative effects are also present under NaCl solution erosion, as described earlier, and under distilled water erosion. Chloride ions are present as crystals in the hardened body, which prevents the migration of ions in solution and weakens the hydration reaction33. Chlorine ions can react with CaO to form volume-expanding Friedel’s salt (C3A·CaCl2·10H2O), causing cracks inside the hardened specimen48. The second action is a positive action. A positive effect, such as that under distilled water erosion, is also present under NaCl solution erosion. Chlorine ions enter the specimen and are adsorbed on the gelling product or exist in the free state in the pore solution, where they can generate Friedel’s salt (C3A·CaCl2·10H2O) with C3A to increase the strength of the specimen49. Chloride ions can lower the zeta potential in solution, favouring the formation of geopolymers50. The reason for these changes in intensity may be the result of the interaction of these two effects.

Thus, the strength evolution should be divided into two stages. The first stage is characterized by short-term or intermediate-age densification, during which continued hydration and secondary product formation partially refine the pore structure. The second stage is dominated by long-term degradation, including alkaline leaching, gel decalcification, chloride/sulfate-induced reactions and the enlargement of capillary pores. This distinction explains why some specimens exhibited temporary strength retention, whereas the EMR50% group, especially under NaCl exposure, showed clear deterioration.

Conclusion

In this paper, the strength and heavy metal leaching characteristics of electrolytic manganese residue/steel slag-based slurry specimens subjected to erosion by distilled water, Na2SO4 solution and NaCl solution were investigated. The main conclusions were as follows:Increasing the SS proportion improved the strength retention of ESM under distilled-water, sulfate, and chloride exposure. At EMR contents ≤ 40%, the specimens showed better erosion resistance. Distilled water and Na2SO4 solution erosion positively contributed to the compressive strength of the specimens. The MIP results show that erosion causes decomposition of amorphous network-like gels, such as C–(A)S–H gels. At the same time, soluble substances dissolve. Both increase the cumulative pore volume of the specimen, decrease the volume of gel pores, and increase the volume of capillary pores and stomata, increasing the porosity of the specimen. The reduction in gel-pore volume and the increase in capillary pores weakened matrix compactness, thereby explaining the lower strength retention after chemical exposure. The consistency among XRD, FTIR, SEM and MIP results indicates that strength degradation was mainly governed by gel destabilization and pore coarsening In addition, the performance is more significant under the erosion of NaCl solution.
