The association of opioid prescribing continuity and the risk of opioid use disorder among older adults
Authors: Prachi Prajapati, Shadi Bazzazzadehgan, Yi Yang, Kaustuv Bhattacharya, Shishir Maharjan, John P. Bentley, Sujith Ramachandran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Lower opioid prescribing continuity is associated with a higher risk of opioid-related adverse events. This study aimed to evaluate the association between opioid prescribing continuity and risk of opioid use disorder (OUD).Methods:Older adults (≥ 65 years) on long-term opioid therapy (LTOT) were identified from 2012 to 2020 5 % Medicare claims data. The study outcome was OUD. Opioid prescribing continuity, measured using the continuity of care index (COCI), was treated as a time-varying predictor measured monthly from LTOT initiation until outcome/censoring. The association between COCI and risk of OUD was evaluated using an extended Cox regression model.Results:Of 153,570 patients, 7.61 % (n = 11,697) developed OUD after LTOT initiation. The mean (SD) age was 75.74 (8.42) years and 70.16 % (n = 107,743) were female. After adjusting for covariates, patients with medium COCI (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] = 1.76, 95 % CI 1.58 – 1.96; p < .001) and high COCI (aHR = 1.32, 95 % CI 1.22 – 1.44; p < .001) had higher OUD risk compared to those with low COCI. Patients with high COCI (aHR = 0.75, 95 % CI 0.69 – 0.82; p < .001) had significantly lower hazard of OUD than those with medium COCI.Conclusion:Greater opioid prescribing continuity may promote OUD detection, highlighting the importance of consistent care for better management of opioid-related conditions. Alternatively, highly fragmented care among patients with LTOT may lead to lower detection of OUD, despite patients potentially being at higher risk for OUD. Thus, it is important to build trustful patient-prescriber relationships for early OUD detection and management.

Introduction

Chronic pain, including both chronic non-cancer pain (CNCP) and cancer pain, poses a significant public health challenge in the United States (US), affecting more than 50 million adults (Rikard, 2023). It is estimated that 7.6 million American adults are on long-term opioid therapy (LTOT) for pain management (Groenewald et al., 2022). LTOT has been found to be associated with several gastrointestinal, respiratory, cardiovascular, and nervous system adverse events (Baldini et al., 2012). More recently, LTOT has been identified as a risk factor for opioid-related adverse events, especially opioid use disorder (OUD) and overdose (Dowell et al., 2022).

The prevalence of OUD in the US increased gradually from 2000 to 2015, followed by a continual minimal decrease from 2016 to 2019 (Keyes et al., 2022). In 2019, it was estimated that approximately 6.7–7.6 million US adults were living with OUD (Keyes et al., 2022). Concurrently, the proportion of admissions to treatment facilities attributed to opioids increased steadily over time (Arfken et al., 2020). As of 2022, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reported that nearly 4 % of all American adults require OUD treatment, indicating that the public health impact associated with OUD remains significant (Dowell et al., 2024). About 16.7 % of US adults on prescription opioids reported having OUD in 2015 (Han et al., 2017). Patients using prescription opioids regularly may be at an increased risk of OUD, which warrants appropriate therapeutic management to optimize outcomes associated with opioid treatment.

OUD identification among LTOT patients remains challenging due to evolving definitions and complexities distinguishing between LTOT-induced physical dependence and OUD itself (Boscarino et al., 2015; Volkow and McLellan, 2016). The exclusion of dependence and tolerance from the OUD diagnostic criteria in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) (Hasin et al., 2013), has further complicated efforts to study the LTOT-OUD relationship. Additionally, the removal of the X-waiver requirement for buprenorphine prescribing has rapidly altered OUD diagnosis practices in clinical settings (Waiver Elimination (Waiver Elimination (MAT Act) 2023). The X-waiver, eliminated in January 2023, previously required special training and certification for clinicians to prescribe buprenorphine. Its removal aimed to expand access to OUD treatment (Waiver Elimination (Waiver Elimination (MAT Act) 2023). The 2022 CDC Clinical Practice Guideline for Prescribing Opioids for Pain recommend that clinicians recognize signs of OUD, address patient concerns, and offer medication-assisted treatment for those diagnosed with OUD, while continuing to prioritize pain management (Dowell et al., 2022). These recommendations highlight the importance of continuity of care for appropriate management of patients at risk of OUD.

In general, it has been suggested that patients who receive care from a single provider experience better health outcomes and lower healthcare resource utilization (Van Walraven et al., 2010; Kajaria-Montag et al., 2024). It has also been reported that older patients and patients with multiple chronic conditions can benefit from continuity of care more than others (Kajaria-Montag et al., 2024). With respect to opioid therapy, various studies have previously examined the association between continuity of care and risk of opioid-related adverse events among patients on opioid treatment (Hallvik et al., 2018; Ramachandran et al., 2023). Ramachandran and colleagues (2023) found that higher opioid prescribing continuity was associated with fewer opioid-related adverse events, including opioid-induced respiratory depression, opioid-related overdose, and all-cause mortality. Cochran et al. (2014) showed that compared to non-OUD patients, OUD patients had prescriptions filled at more pharmacies. This study aimed to evaluate the association between opioid prescribing continuity, measured by the Continuity of Care Index (COCI), and the risk of OUD among older Medicare beneficiaries on LTOT. We hypothesized that higher opioid prescribing continuity would be associated with decreased risk of OUD.

Methods

Study design and data source

This cohort study utilized the 5 % national sample of Medicare administrative claims data from 2012 to 2020. The Medicare administrative claims database contains healthcare services records, including inpatient claims, outpatient claims, and pharmacy claims, which can be linked using an encrypted beneficiary identifier. Patients’ enrollment records and demographic characteristics were identified using a beneficiary summary file. The study was approved by the University of Mississippi Institutional Review Board (protocol #24x-220) and a data use agreement (DUA #RSCH-2023–59140) for the use of Medicare data was obtained from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services.

Study cohort

The study design is depicted in Fig. 1. Medicare beneficiaries who were on LTOT between July 1, 2012, and June 30, 2020, were included in the study. LTOT was defined, per previous literature, as the presence of at least three opioid prescription claims with at least 45 days of opioid possession in any 90-day period (Ramachandran et al., 2021; Salkar et al., 2022). Opioids that might be prescribed as part of medication-assisted treatment (MAT) for opioid use disorder, including methadone, buprenorphine, and naltrexone, were not considered in the LTOT operationalization. The first LTOT episode that met the operationalization criteria during the study period was considered as the cohort entry date for each beneficiary. The 181st day after the first episode of LTOT initiation was defined as the “index date” to allow adequate lag for the measurement of the key exposure variable of interest. Medicare beneficiaries were required to be at least 65 years old as of the index date and were required to be continuously enrolled in Medicare Parts A, B, and D but not Part C for at least 6 months prior to index date (baseline period). Beneficiaries were excluded from the study cohort if they had (1) hospice use during the baseline period, (2) OUD diagnosis any time before the index date, (3) substance use diagnosis during the baseline period, or (4) died between LTOT initiation and the index date. Eligible Medicare beneficiaries were followed from index date until the first occurrence of OUD (i.e., the study outcome) or until censoring for loss of continuous enrollment, start of Medicare Part C enrollment, start of hospice use, death, or the end of the study period (December 31, 2020), whichever came first.

Study outcome

OUD was the outcome of interest. OUD was defined as the presence of at least two outpatient claims or one inpatient claim with an International Classification of Diseases 9th (ICD-9) or 10th revision (ICD-10) diagnosis code for OUD. The first occurrence of OUD was set as “event date”. ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnosis codes used to identify OUD are available in Supplementary Table 1.

Key exposure variable of interest

Opioid prescribing continuity was assessed using the COCI formula proposed by Bice and Boxerman (1977), which captures “the extent to which a given individual’s total number of visits for an episode of illness or a specific time period are with a single [provider] or group of referred providers.” In the present study, COCI was considered as a time-varying variable and computed every month starting from index date, until the occurrence of the event or censoring, based on a prior 180-day COCI assessment period. COCI was calculated based on opioid prescriptions filled, and its value ranged from 0 (complete fragmentation of care) to 1 (perfect continuity of care). COCI values were categorized into three levels. Scores of less than 0.5 were assigned to the low COCI group, scores greater than or equal to 0.5 but less than 1 were assigned to the medium COCI group, and scores of 1 were assigned to the high COCI group.

Covariates

In the analysis, several sociodemographic, clinical characteristics were treated as covariates. Sociodemographic variables such as age, sex, race, state of residence, and enrollment in the low-income subsidy (LIS) program, were measured as of the index date from Medicare beneficiary summary files. In addition, clinical characteristics such as the presence of CNCP conditions, cancer, renal insufficiency, hepatic insufficiency, sleep apnea, other sleep disorders, mental illnesses, Parkinson’s disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and the Deyo-Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) were identified from inpatient, outpatient and home health claims during the baseline period. Eligible Medicare beneficiaries were also assessed for concomitant medications use for the following medications: benzodiazepines, antidepressants, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), anticonvulsants, and skeletal muscle relaxants. These medications were considered concomitant if they had at least 90 days of cumulative supply during the baseline period. For opioid prescriptions, average number of fills, total days of supply, average morphine milligram equivalents (MME), and formulation during the baseline period were also assessed.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics of the study cohort. Appropriate bivariate tests of significance were conducted to compare patients classified as having OUD to those who were not. For categorical variables, chi-square tests were conducted whereas for continuous variables independent sample t-tests were performed. Restricted mean survival time (RMST) was used to calculate to the average time to OUD diagnosis (Das et al., 2023; Guo and Liang, 2019). The association between time-varying COCI category and OUD risk was analyzed using extended Cox regression (i.e., medium and high COCI categories vs. low; high COCI category vs. medium), adjusted for covariates. This extension of the standard Cox proportional hazards model allows for the inclusion of time-varying predictors. Hazard ratios reported for the COCI categories represent a weighted average of the true hazard ratios over the entire follow-up period and should be interpreted accordingly.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the findings. First, different cut-off points for the categorization of COCI were explored to determine if varying these thresholds would significantly alter the results (COCI scenario 1 and COCI scenario 2). For COCI scenario 1, the cutoffs chosen were 0.34 and 1, where those with COCI scores less than 0.34 were assigned to the low COCI group, those with COCI scores greater than or equal to 0.34 but less than 1 were medium COCI group, and those with a score of 1 were assigned to the high COCI group. Similarly, the cutoffs chosen for COCI scenario 2 were 0.75 and 1. Second, the main analysis was replicated after the study cohort was stratified into four distinct and mutually exclusive subgroups based on their pain conditions during the baseline period: those with cancer-related pain, those with CNCP, those with both cancer and CNCP, and those with no identifiable pain conditions. Third, we excluded diagnosis codes for opioid dependence from the outcome definition to address potential misclassification due to providers’ cautious coding practices. All data management and analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (Cary, NC).

Results

Study cohort

Of 599,547 Medicare beneficiaries on LTOT between July 2012 to June 2020, 153,570 beneficiaries were included in the final eligible cohort, after applying all inclusion/exclusion criteria. The attrition table for final cohort is presented in Fig. 2. Of the final cohort, 7.61 % (n = 11,697) developed OUD during the follow-up period, with an RMST of 92.53 months with a standard error of 0.083.

The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the final cohort, those with and without OUD, are presented in Table 1. The mean (SD) age of the cohort was 75.74 (8.42) years. Of included Medicare beneficiaries 70.16 % (n = 107,743) were female, 81.45 % (n = 125,086) were White, 48.48 % (n = 74,456) resided in the Southern region, and 37.92 % (n = 58,232) were enrolled in LIS.

Fig. 3 presents an unadjusted histogram depicting the percentage of patients in each level of the COCI categories at various time points throughout the follow-up period. While not represented in the graph, the number of patients continuing to be followed up decreased over the duration of follow-up. Across the duration of follow-up, approximately 70 % of patients were in the high COCI category, ~10 % in the medium COCI category, and ~20 % in the low COCI group. Up to one year into follow-up, there was a gradual increase in the proportion of patients with low COCI. After one year, the proportion of patients in the low COCI group gradually decreased and those in high COCI group gradually increased.

Association between COCI and OUD

The unadjusted analysis of the study showed that compared to patients in the low COCI group, those in the medium COCI group (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.47, 95 % confidence interval [CI]: 2.22 – 2.75; p < .001) and the high COCI group (HR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.34 – 1.58; p < .001) had significantly higher risk of OUD. However, those in the high COCI group had significantly lower risk of OUD compared to patients in the medium COCI group (HR = 0.59, 95 % CI: 0.54 – 0.64; p < .001)

Table 2 shows the results of the adjusted Cox regression model predicting the hazard of OUD detection for the primary and sensitivity analyses. After adjusting for all covariates in the full study sample, the medium COCI group (adjusted HR [aHR] = 1.76, 95 % CI 1.58 – 1.96; p < .001) and the high COCI group (aHR = 1.32, 95 % CI 1.22 – 1.44; p < .001) had significantly higher OUD risk, compared to the low COCI group. Changing the reference category to the medium COCI group revealed that the high COCI group had a significantly lower risk of OUD than this group (aHR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.69 – 0.82; p < .001). Results for the full model of the primary analysis are reported in Supplementary Table 2.

Results of sensitivity analyses

To evaluate the robustness of the findings, three sensitivity analyses were performed. First, two alternative COCI categorizations were tested. In COCI Scenario 1, where cutoffs were set at 0.34 and 1, the results remained consistent with the primary analysis. In COCI Scenario 2, with adjusted cutoffs of 0.75 and 1, the medium COCI group continued to show a significantly increased hazard of OUD compared to the low COCI group. However, in this scenario, the high COCI group no longer exhibited a statistically significant difference from the low COCI group. These results are presented in Table 2 and Supplementary Table 3.

Second, a sensitivity analysis was conducted within subgroups defined by pain conditions. The association between medium and high COCI and an increased hazard of OUD remained significant among all subgroups defined by pain condition, consistent with the primary analysis. These findings are presented in Table 2 and Supplementary Table 4.

In the third sensitivity analysis as presented in Table 2 and Supplementary Table 5, where opioid dependence codes were excluded, 5706 OUD patients were identified. The results remained consistent with the primary analysis, supporting the robustness of our findings to potential misclassification due to providers’ cautious coding practices.

Discussion

LTOT is an important treatment option for patients with chronic pain. However, it is also a common risk factor for opioid-related adverse events like overdose and mortality (Ramachandran et al., 2021). Given the high prevalence of OUD in the US, it is important to examine the appropriate management of patients on LTOT who may be at high risk of such conditions. Based on previous research related to continuity of care, opioid prescribing continuity may be indicative of the appropriate management of LTOT. The findings of this study highlight that compared to patients with low opioid prescribing continuity, medium continuity was associated with a 76 % increase in the hazard of OUD, and high COCI was associated with a 32 % increase in the hazard of OUD. Additionally, compared to patients with medium opioid prescribing continuity, high COCI was associated with a 25 % decrease in the hazard of OUD.

Previous studies have shown that higher opioid prescribing continuity is associated with better health outcomes. For example, using administrative pharmacy claims data from the state of Oregon, Hallvik et al. (2018) found lower opioid prescribing continuity was associated with increased filling of high-risk opioid prescriptions and higher overdose-related hospitalizations. Additionally, a study by Ramachandran et al. (2023) among Medicare beneficiaries with chronic non-cancer pain highlighted that lower opioid prescribing continuity increased the odds of an opioid overdose, mortality, or respiratory depression by 50 %. To our best knowledge, no previous study has examined how opioid prescribing continuity affects risk of OUD after long-term opioid therapy initiation. In contrast to our hypothesis, this study found that patients with higher opioid prescribing continuity were at a greater risk of OUD detection. The nature of these results may be explained by the complexity of developing, diagnosing, and reporting of OUD in clinical settings.

The identification of OUD for patients on LTOT is likely a result of the interplay of several complex scenarios. First, a patient’s risk of developing OUD likely depends on characteristics of their opioid use (e.g., dose, frequency, trends in patterns of use) and the presentation of symptoms related to their pain condition. Second, the identification of symptoms of OUD depends on the level of engagement with their provider as well as the quality of management of opioid therapy. Finally, the provider’s willingness to record the diagnosis of OUD depends on their intent to start treatment, choice of therapy, patient willingness to accept treatment, as well as the provider’s credentials to prescribe buprenorphine (since the data used for the current study are before the X-waiver was exempted). Patients with low COCI, who receive fragmented care from several different providers, may be unlikely to receive appropriate screening or treatment for OUD symptoms regardless of their individual risk, leading to significant under-identification of OUD. In contrast, patients with medium COCI had a 76 % higher risk of OUD in this study relative to those with low COCI. While this finding may seem counterintuitive, it could be explained by greater opioid prescribing continuity, which, in turn, may lead to more frequent screening and identification of OUD symptoms. Notably, transitioning from medium to high COCI, representing nearly perfect continuity of care, was associated with a 25 % lower hazard of OUD. This suggests that even a modest improvement in care continuity may facilitate earlier detection and intervention, ultimately reducing the risk of OUD. In this context, it is also important to note that the detection of OUD is, in and of itself, not an adverse outcome as much as the occurrence of an opioid overdose. Rather, not screening for symptoms of OUD and lack of provision of appropriate treatment is an undesirable event, as it exposes patients to a high risk of opioid overdose (“The ASAM National Practice Guideline for the Treatment of Opioid Use Disorder,” 2020).

With the introduction of updated guidelines for prescribing opioids for pain, as well as the changes in US Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) requirements for buprenorphine prescribing, both clinicians and patients must manage LTOT based on more stringent recommendations (Dowell et al., 2022). While these recommendations aim to strengthen the safety of LTOT through dose monitoring, it is equally important to maintain care continuity for patients. Opioid prescribing continuity can help in identifying LTOT patients at risk of OUD and help them manage their symptoms so as to avoid adverse events. Thus, it is important for providers to build a healthy and trustful relationship with their patients and encourage care continuity.

The results of this study must be interpreted with some caution. First, while COCI was treated as a time-varying predictor (calculated monthly), it is likely that other variables affecting the risk of OUD also varied over time. This time-varying confounding includes the possibility of increased provider visits for increased symptoms of OUD, changes in access to healthcare services, as well as changes in dose or formulation of opioid treatment. Future studies should employ other designs that can account for this possibility. Second, there is evidence of misclassification associated with the diagnosis of OUD using administrative claims data because of the irregular application and limited accuracy of the ICD diagnosis codes used for identification of OUD (Goodman-Meza et al., 2024; Howell et al., 2021; Lagisetty et al., 2021). Studies using survey research, electronic health records, or other forms of data collection may be better equipped to overcome these biases. Third, opioid prescription claims in claims data may not accurately represent actual opioid consumption by beneficiaries, potentially introducing misclassification in the operationalization of LTOT. Fourth, there is a possibility of bias due to loss to follow-up. As Fig. 3 shows, the proportion of eligible individuals in the high COCI category is greater toward the tail end of the follow-up period. This may suggest that these individuals had longer observation period, increasing the likelihood of capturing an OUD diagnosis when compared to those in the low COCI category. Finally, the study only included older Medicare beneficiaries enrolled in Medicare Parts A, B, and D, somewhat limiting generalizability. Future studies should confirm the results of the association between COCI and OUD risk in other patient populations.

In conclusion, this study is the first to assess the relationship between opioid prescribing continuity and risk of OUD among older adults on LTOT. With the X-waiver being no longer required for prescribers to provide treatment of OUD, it is important that OUD be detected and reported as accurately as possible. Opioid prescribing continuity through better patient-provider relationships can not only help identify patients at high risk of OUD but also prevent more severe adverse outcomes through appropriate treatment.
