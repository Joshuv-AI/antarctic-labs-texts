Protocol for the design, conduct, and evaluation of prime editing in human pluripotent stem cells
Authors: Youjun Wu, Mega Sidharta, Aaron Zhong, Benjamin Persily, Mu Li, Ting Zhou
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryPrime editing introduces single-nucleotide polymorphism changes, small deletions, or insertions at a specific genome site without double-stranded DNA breaks or the need for the donor template. Here, we present a protocol to design, conduct, and evaluate prime editing in human pluripotent stem cells. We describe steps for pegRNA and nicking sgRNA design and cloning, the prime editing tool electroporation, and the efficiency evaluation using Miseq. We elaborate the process of GBA (N370S) mutation induction and correction as an example.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Li et al. (2022).1Subject areas: CRISPR, Stem Cells

Before you begin

The protocol described has four key steps. Step 1: pegRNA and additional nicking sgRNA design for PE3; Step 2: pegRNA and additional nicking sgRNA plasmid cloning; Step 3: PE tool electroporation; Step 4: prime editing efficiency evaluation. For the downstream workflow aimed at the generation of genetically mutated human pluripotent stem cell (hPSC) lines, including single-cell cloning generation, PCR and Sanger sequencing for mutation screening, refer to our previously published protocol2 or simply use the workflow that have established in your laboratory.

PE tools selection (About PE2, PE3, PEmax, PE4, PE5, pegRNA and ePegRNA)

Prime editing is a precise genome editing technology that utilizes an RNA-programmable nickase fused with reverse transcriptase (RT) and a prime editing guide RNA (pegRNA) to copy the genetic information from the extension of the pegRNA into the desired genomic site. In contract to CRISPR/Cas9 based HDR, prime editing does not need DNA double-strand break (DSB) and donor DNA template to create single base substitutions, as well as small insertions and deletions. Previous studies have also showed that prime editing significantly reduces off-target frequencies compare to CRISPR/Cas9 based gene editing, due to absence of DSB requirements.3

There are multiple prime editing systems that have been developed. The core prime editing system, PE2, consists of an engineered Moloney murine leukemia virus (M-MLV) RT fused to nCas9 (H840A) and a pegRNA containing primer-binding site (PBS) and a 3′ extension that contains editing information.3 The PE3 system includes an additional sgRNA to generate a nick on the complementary strand, improving editing efficiency.3 Optimization of architecture of either prime editor (PEmax) or pegRNA (epegRNA) also improves prime editing. PEmax is an improved prime editor with the codon optimization for both RT and Cas9.4 On the other hand, epegRNA is an engineered pegRNA, which includes an additional 3′ RNA structural motif to improve stability.5 PEmax in synergy with epegRNA demonstrates a further improvement of prime editing efficiency.4 Further systems, PE4 and PE5, were developed by transiently inhibiting the DNA mismatch repair (MMR) pathway using a dominant negative domain of MMR (MLH1dn), resulting in higher editing efficiency and lower editing byproducts (PE4 = PE2+MLH1dn; PE5 = PE3+MLH1dn).4 Our recent study showed co-delivering of p53DD, a dominant negative fragment of p53, boosts PE3 efficiency without compromising the genome-wide safety.1

The protocol below describes how to design, clone, and evaluate a pegRNA for efficient prime editing in hPSCs using PE3 with p53DD. The protocol can be applied to the other systems, such as ePegRNA, PEmax, PE5 or PE5max.

About hPSC culture

High quality hPSC (hESC lines/hiPSC lines) cultures, with normal karyotype and in which the absence of spontaneous differentiation is observed, should be used to perform the prime editing. Our hPSC lines are maintained on Matrigel-coated plate with StemFlex medium. We routinely passage hPSCs at a ratio of 1:6 every 3–4 days using 0.5 mM EDTA. hPSCs cultured in other widely used hPSC medium, such as mTeSR1 or mTeSR Plus (StemCell Technologies), or E8 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) can be also used for prime editing.

Step-by-step method details

pegRNA and additional nicking sgRNA design for PE3

Timing: 2 h

1.pegRNA design.a.For pegRNA design, we first use the Benchling CRISPR Design Tool (https://www.benchling.com/#) to find the best spacer sequence close to the editing locus. Once you log in your Benchling account, from the left navigation bar, choose Create>CRISPR>CRISPR Guides.b.After the target sequence is imported, Benchling will provide a list of guides targeting the region along with both on-target and off-target score based on algorithms for predicting on-target and off-target activities.c.Then we select the spacer with shortest distance to the editing site and the highest on-target and off-target prediction score. Generally, targeting protospacers more proximal to the desired targeting site yields higher editing efficiency. Therefore, we recommend starting with a protospacer that is positioned as close as possible to the targeting site.d.We then design the PBS and RT templates of pegRNAs using criteria previously published: PBS range from 8-15nt, RT template range from 10-20nt, the first base of the 3′ extensions is not C.3

2.Nicking sgRNA design for PE3.

Nicking sgRNA design for PE3.

For PE3, an additional nicking sgRNA was designed using Benchling CRISPR Design Tool to target to the complementary strand at ∼ 60–100 bp downstream from the pegRNA cutting site.

Alternatives: other web tools are also available for pegRNA design such as PrimeDesign,6 pegIT7 and PE-Designer.8

Scheme 1 shows the design of PE3 editing for the introduction of the N370S mutation in the GBA gene in hPSCs. For the GBA (N370S) mutation correction in the patient’s iPSC genome, the desired modification within the RT template is A.

Schematics of pegRNA design and PE tools for the introduction of the GBA (N370S) mutation in hPSCs

The pegRNA is composed of a protospacer sequence that targets the region to edit, a primer binding site (PBS) and a reverse transcript (RT) template that includes the edit (in this case, N370S in GBA). The PE3 tools include a pegRNA, a PE enzyme (Cas9-H840A) fused to a reverse transcriptase, illustrated as a blue oval here) and an additional nicking sgRNA targeting the targeting the complementary DNA strand, located 61 bp downstream from the pegRNA nicking site.

pegRNA plasmid is cloned using Golden-Gate cloning strategy that assembling BsaI digested pegRNA backbone (Component 1), and three annealed double-stranded DNA fragments, including pegRNA spacer sequence (Component 2), pegRNA scaffold (Component 3), and pegRNA extension sequence consisted of PBS and RT template (Component 4).

PE tool delivery- electroporation

Timing: 3–4 days

Timing: ∼1 h (for step 5)

This section describes how to deliver the PE tool into hPSCs for prime editing.

5.Electroporation.a.hPSCs preparation. Maintain hPSCs in StemFlex medium. Routinely passage hPSCs at a ratio of 1:6 every 3–4 days using 0.5 mM EDTA.b.hPSCs digestion for electroporation. When the hPSCs reach ∼70%–80% confluency, aspirate the medium, wash the cells once with DPBS, and then add 1 mL Accutase and incubate at 37°C for 10 min. Add 3 mL of medium and detach cells by slowly pipetting up and down. Collect the cells into a 15 mL conical tube and spin at 120 g for 3 min. Aspirate the supernatant, add 1 mL StemFlex medium and count the number of cells using the Nexcelom Bioscience Cellometer K2.c.Prepare cells and reagents for one smaller cuvette electroporation reaction using the 16-well Nucleocuvette Strip provided in the P3 Primary Cell 4D-Nucleofector X Kit S:i.0.5 × 106 cells.ii.Mix the reagents: 16.4 μL P3 Primary Cell Nucleofector Solution and 3.6 μL Supplement 1.iii.Plasmids: We simultaneously electroporated hPSCs with five different plasmids. These plasmids include a PE expressing plasmid, a pegRNA plasmid containing sequences for pegRNA spacer, scaffold and 3′ extension sequence (refer to Scheme 2 for detail), a nicking sgRNA plasmid, a p53DD expression plasmid to enhance the primer editing efficiency, and a puromycin resistance gene plasmid used for the enrichment of electroporated cells upon puromycin selection. Condition: 1 μg sgRNA plasmid (Lsg-GBA-1+2) + 1 μg pegRNA plasmid (pegRNA-GBA-10 or pegRNA-GBA-4) + 1 μg PE plasmid (pEF-PE2) + 1 μg PUC57-Puro + 1 μg p53DD plasmid (see Table 1).For our GBA induction lines, we electroporated: 1 μg pEF-PE2. 1 μg pegRNA-GBA-10. 1 μg PUC57-Puro. 1 μg Lsg-GBA-1+2. 1 μg p53DD.For our GBA correction lines, we electroporated: 1 μg pEF-PE2. 1 μg pegRNA-GBA-4. 1 μg PUC57-Puro. 1 μg Lsg-GBA-1+2. 1 μg p53DD.CRITICAL: The electroporation process as well as the P3 solution are toxic to the cells and should be performed as quickly as possible. The plasmids should be pre-mixed prior to the start of the electroporation. When the cells are pelleted, they should be resuspended in the complete P3 solution first (P3 Primary Cell Nucleofector Solution plus Supplement 1), then combined with the pre-mixed plasmids. Afterward, the cells+plasmid mixture should quickly be electroporated.d.Transfer the cell mixture to the Nucleocuvette Vessel using a P200 pipet.e.Transfer the cuvette to the 4D-Nucleofector and electroporate the cells, select “Primary Cell P3”, Pulse Code, and press “Start”.Note:Pulse Code for your hPSC lines can be optimized according to the protocol supplied with the reagents. “CB-150” is the best code for hESCs and hiPSCs in our testing.f.Using a P200 pipet, transfer the cell mixture from the cuvette to a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube containing 1 mL of StemFlex + CloneR medium. Rinse the cuvette once with a small amount of StemFlex + CloneR medium using the pipet and transfer to the 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube.g.Transfer the 1 mL of cells equally into two wells of freshly Matrigel-coated 48-wells (0.5 mL cells in each well). We use one well of cells for Miseq analysis while reserving the other well as a cell stock. Place the plate in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator.Note: Gently shake the plate back and forth and side to side to evenly distribute the cells. Avoid circular motions to prevent concentrating cells in the middle or around the edge of the well.h.At 24 h post-electroporation (Figures 2A and 2B), aspirate spent medium, then change medium by adding 0.5 mL StemFlex + Puromycin medium for each well.i.At 48 h post-electroporation (Figures 2C and 2D), wash one of the wells once with at least 0.5 mL DPBS. Aspirate the DPBS and add 40 μL of Sigma Lysis Solution for Blood. Lyse this well by scratching the bottom of the well horizontally, vertically, diagonally. Transfer the lysis mixture to a 0.2 mL PCR tube and incubate at 75°C for 15 min followed by a 4°C hold. Expected outcomes

We have successfully applied the prime editing protocol described in this study to generate various types of mutations, including point mutations, 3 bp and 34 bp insertions, as well as 1 bp and 2 bp deletions, in multiple hESCs and hiPSC lines. For each new editing project, we typically design two pairs of the PE3 tool sets (pegRNA and nicking sgRNA) and perform Miseq analysis to determine the more efficient tool set. The editing efficiency we have observed ranges between 2% to 30%.

In cases where the on-target efficiency is low, identifying correctly edited hPSC single-cell clones can be time-consuming and costly. To address this issue, we have previously found that p53DD significantly enhances editing efficiency. Consequently, we have now incorporated the co-electroporation of p53DD into this protocol. For instance, when attempting to correct the LMNA (c.1824 C>T) mutation in HGPS patient iPSCs using PE3 alone, we did not obtain any corrected clones out of ∼200 single-cell clones. However, with the addition of p53DD, we were able to obtain 3 corrected single-cell clones out of a total of 96, making this approach more feasible. Therefore, we highly recommend using p53DD for co-electroporation in cases with low efficiency.

Limitations

Transfection or electroporation of plasmids can be associated with a low level of genome integration. In our previous study on the application of p53DD, we found that 1 out of 39 edited clones had detectable integration of the p53DD vector into the genome.1 This suggests that an additional characterization step for p53DD integration may be necessary for clones edited with the p53DD plasmid. To identify potential integration events, PCR amplification of a segment of the p53DD construct using the primers P53DD-FP: GGGCGTAAACGCTTCGAGAT and P53DD-RP: CAAGGTCACCAGACAGAGATGCT can be performed. In the case of a single cell clone with p53DD integration, a positive PCR band will be observed.1 Alternatively, one may consider using p53DD mRNA for electroporation to avoid potential integration issues.

Although it is possible to obtain homozygous edited clones through prime editing, we primarily obtained heterozygous edited clones in our experiments. This may be due to the lower editing efficiency of prime editing compared to WT Cas9-based approaches, such as using CRISPR/Cas9 + ssODN for generating mutations. However, this also indicates that prime editing is highly complementary to CRISPR+ssODN in cases where heterozygous editing is desired. One could perform a second round of prime editing to obtain homozygous edits when necessary.

As a general concern for genome editing, aberrant karyotyping and differentiation variations could exist in some edited clones. Appropriate characterization of edited clones is highly recommended to ensure their quality and suitability for downstream applications.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Failure of pegRNA cloning (related to Step 3).

Potential solution

Ensure that you have digested the pegRNA vector completely and isolated the ∼2.2kb pegRNA vector backbone with good quality. Ensure that you have prepared the component 1, 2, 3, 4 as detailed in step 3. Ensure that you spread all the competent cells onto Carbenicillin LB plate.

Problem 2

Rampant cell death after electroporation (related to Step 5).

Potential solution

Double-check that the electroporation program is optimized for your cell line and media choice. Ensure that your plate has been coated with Matrigel for at least an hour. Ensure to use endotoxin-free plasmids.

Problem 3

All cells die after puromycin selection (related to Step 5).

Potential solution

This means the electroporation was suboptimal or failed, or the concentration of the puromycin is off. We use 0.5 μg/μL puromycin for selection at 24 h post-electroporation to enrich the electroporated cells. Make sure to add puromycin only for one day using the correct concentration.

Problem 4

Cells do not die after puromycin selection (related to Step 5).

Potential solution

This means the cell density is not optimal after electroporation. Puromycin selection is not effective when the cells are confluent. You may adjust the size of the culture plate for your cells to achieve an optimal density (see Figure 2). You should also ensure that your original cell line does not already have puromycin resistance.

Problem 5

Problem: PCR amplification of the insert yields no band or non-specific band (related to Step 6).

Potential solution

Optimize the PCR system or PCR program. If the results do not improve, design and synthesize other pairs of primers and try again. Ensure to include a positive control for the PCR itself.

Problem 6

Problem: Low efficiency of prime editing (related to Step 7).

Potential solution

Design other pairs of pegRNA and nicking sgRNA, and evaluate the efficiency using Miseq first to compare tool sets. Try ePegRNA. You can use the GBA (c. 1226 A>G, p. N370S) mutation induction tool sets descripted here as a positive control for your prime editing experiments.
