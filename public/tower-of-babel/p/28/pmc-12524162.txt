Talonavicular Fusion in the Treatment of Planovalgus Foot in Ambulatory Children With Cerebral Palsy
Authors: Ramin H Zargarbashi, Morteza Aghajani, Behnam Panjavi, Soroush Baghdadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlanovalgus is a common foot deformity in cerebral palsy (CP), and surgical correction may be warranted when it becomes symptomatic in daily activities, interferes with brace/shoe wear, or causes substantial lever-arm dysfunction. The purpose of this study was to report the outcomes of talonavicular fusion based on radiographic measures and instrumented gait analysis.MethodsIn an IRB-approved retrospective study, ambulatory children with CP (GMFCS II/III) who underwent talonavicular fusion for the treatment of planovalgus foot from 2018 to 2022 were included. Patients who had pre- and 1-year post-operative instrumented gait studies were selected from this cohort. At final follow-up, all radiographic measures showed significant improvement, including the AP talus-first metatarsal angle (pre-op 21.4° ± 4.8, post-op 5.5° ± 2.7, P < .001) and the lateral talus-first metatarsal angle (pre-op 22.9° ± 4.2, post-op 5.7° ± 2.0, P < .001). Spatio-temporal gait parameters did not show significant changes, but the GDI had a significant improvement. Overall, there were 3 adverse outcomes, none of which required a return to the operating room.ConclusionsWe found significant improvements in radiographic outcomes and a high rate of parent satisfaction at final follow-up. Gait parameters did not show significant changes, but there was a significant improvement in GDI. Introduction

Planovalgus is the most common foot deformity in cerebral palsy (CP), particularly among those with diplegic and quadriplegic involvement [1]. This deformity is characterized by equinus at the ankle, valgus alignment of the hindfoot, and external rotation of the calcaneus. The midfoot collapses, and the forefoot becomes abducted and supinated, leading to uncovering of the talar head medially and inferiorly [2,3]. Although frequently reported by families, the severity of planovalgus deformity as assessed clinically or radiographically does not always correlate with symptoms or functional impairment.

Despite the prevalence, there is no consensus on the treatment of planovalgus in CP and other neuromuscular conditions. Management is often guided by ambulatory status, the severity of symptoms such as pain or difficulty with brace/shoe wear, and the degree of lever-arm dysfunction. Non-operative management typically begins with orthotic modifications. Surgical intervention is considered when conservative measures fail [1]. Soft-tissue procedures, such as tendon lengthening or transfers, are commonly performed, but their long-term efficacy remains limited.

Non-fusion techniques, including calcaneal lengthening and triple-C (Calcaneo-Cuboid-Cuneiform) osteotomies, have been shown to provide excellent deformity correction with a low complication rate in typically developing children with symptomatic flatfoot [[4], [5], [6]]. However, the rate of complications and recurrence has been higher when used in neuromuscular conditions [7,8].

Talonavicular fusion has emerged as a promising option, potentially offering correction comparable to subtalar fusion with a technically less demanding approach. However, the literature on this technique remains limited, particularly regarding functional outcomes assessed by instrumented gait analysis.

Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the radiographic and functional outcomes of talonavicular fusion in ambulatory children with CP. A secondary objective was to assess associated complications and return to the operating room rates following the procedure.

Materials and methods

Study design & patient selection

This retrospective IRB-approved review included patients with CP treated with talonavicular fusion for planovalgus deformity between January 2018 and December 2022. Inclusion criteria were age under 18, ambulatory status (GMFCS II–III), and availability of pre-operative and one-year post-operative gait analysis and radiographs. Patients with prior foot surgery were excluded. All patients underwent talonavicular fusion as part of single-event multilevel surgery (SEMLS).

To be included in the present study, patients needed to have undergone pre-operative instrumented gait analysis and complete clinical and radiographic records, as well as one post-operative gait evaluation one year after surgery. In addition, patients needed to have one set of pre-operative foot radiographs, as well as post-operative radiographs taken at least 6 months post-operatively. Patients with previous surgeries on the foot were excluded.

Indications for surgery

Our Indications for surgery are mostly in line with the literature. A symptomatic planovalgus deformity interfering with activities and/or brace wear, and lever-arm dysfunction with worsening gait kinematics are the main indications for surgical correction. Clinical and radiographic severity are not considered indications for surgical intervention.

Patients at GMFCS level I are generally treated similarly to typically developing children. A calcaneal lengthening procedure, combined with medial imbrication and a possible gastrocnemius recession, is the typical procedure in this functional level. Fusion procedures are rarely done at this level and are reserved for multiply-operated, stiff, and painful feet.

Patients at GMFCS levels II-III who are community ambulators with or without braces/walking aid often undergo flatfoot surgery as a part of single-event multi-level surgery (SEMLS). These patients are initially treated with brace/shoe modification. A talonavicular fusion with medial imbrication and tibialis posterior advancement plus a gastrocnemius recession is our treatment of choice. This is done through a 5 cm medial incision extending from the naviculo-cuneiform joint proximally. The talonavicular joint is identified, opened, and denuded of cartilage. The spring ligament and tibialis posterior tendon are transected and prepared for advancement and imbrication. Additional procedures are completed at this time when indicated. Calcaneal sliding osteotomy, tendon transfer and/or lengthening, and first metatarsophalangeal joint fusion are the most common additional procedures. Finally, the talonavicular joint is provisionally pinned and assessed under fluoroscopy. Depending on the patient's age and surgeon preference, wires, staples, or headless screws are placed for final fixation. If at the end of this procedure there is hindfoot valgus on fluoroscopy and clinical exam, a medial displacement posterior calcaneal osteotomy is done and fixed with a large-bore K-wire. Capsular imbrication and tibialis posterior advancement is completed. A well-molded cast is applied, and the patient is non-weight bearing for 4 weeks. An AFO is typically molded after 4 weeks and is worn when weight-bearing for at least 6 months. The wires are removed in the clinic at the time of cast change. Staples and screws are only removed if they cause symptoms.

Study measurements

Basic demographic and clinical data were extracted from our charts. Standing anteroposterior and lateral foot radiographs were obtained pre-operatively and at 1 year post-operatively. Talus-first metatarsal and talocalcaneal angles on both views were measured by a fellowship-trained orthopaedic surgeon.

Instrumented gait analysis was obtained for all patients pre-operatively, and at least 1 year following surgery. All gait studies were done at the same lab, with three-dimensional gait data collected at 120Hz during walking barefoot at their self-selected speed along a 10-m walkway. Kinematic angles were extracted using Vicon Plug-in Gait (Oxford Metrics, Oxford, UK). Temporal gait parameters were collected and compared. Gait Deviation Index (GDI) was calculated for pre- and post-op studies. For the purpose of GDI, the same database of typically-developing children was used as our reference data.

Finally, parent satisfaction was assessed at the time of follow-up gait study using a 5-point Likert scale (completely satisfied, partially satisfied, neutral, partially dissatisfied, completely dissatisfied). Complications and return to OR were extracted from medical records.

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (Armonk, NY) with a 2-tailed P < .05 considered statistically significant. Paired-sample t-tests and Mann-Whitney U tests were used to compare means depending on normality, and χ 2 tests were used to compare categorical variables.

Results

Thirteen patients with 26 feet were included in this study, all receiving bilateral single-event surgery. The mean age at the time of surgery was 10.2 years (range, 7–15 years). Eight patients were male (61%). Mean BMI was 17.6 ± 3.28 kg/m2. Ten patients (77%) were classified as GMFCS level II, and 3 as GMFCS level III (23%). Final radiographic and gait studies were performed at a mean of 11.6 months after surgery (range, 10–16 months).

All feet had at least one concomitant procedure done as part of the SEMLS, including gastrocnemius recession (17 feet, 65%) or Achilles tendon lengthening (7 feet, 27%), posterior calcaneal displacement osteotomy (15feet, 58%), peroneus brevis lengthening (14 feet, 54%), medial and/or lateral hamstring lengthening (10 feet, 38%), MTP fusion (9 feet, 35%), and tibial derotational osteotomies (8 feet, 31%).

There were 3 (11.5%) adverse outcomes in the cohort. One patient with wound dehiscence, one with pin-tract infection, and one non-union of the fusion site. The patient with non-union had some collapse at the fusion site but was asymptomatic and not treated further. There were no returns to the operating room in the first post-operative year. Eight (61%) families were very satisfied with the outcomes, four (31%) were somewhat satisfied, and one (8%) was unsatisfied. In the latter group, one patient's hospital stay was complicated by pneumonia resulting in a 6-week stay.

Discussion

Planovalgus foot is one of the most common deformities in CP, and surgical correction may be warranted when it becomes symptomatic in daily activities, interferes with brace/shoe wear, or causes substantial lever-arm dysfunction. This study demonstrates that talonavicular fusion, performed as part of single-event multilevel surgery (SEMLS), results in significant radiographic correction and improved GDI in ambulatory children with CP. With a low complication rate and high parental satisfaction, these findings support talonavicular fusion as a viable surgical option for planovalgus deformity in GMFCS II–III patients.

Despite the prevalence of planovalgus in typically developing children as well as those with CP, we are still far from a consensus on natural history, management, and surgical technique [2,7,8]. In children with CP, our initial approach is shoe/brace adjustment, physical therapy, and tone management. If deformity is limiting patient's function despite non-operative management, surgery is offered, typically as a part of the SEMLS. Patients at GMFCS level I receive joint-sparing surgery, typically in the form of a calcaneal lengthening procedure, which is backed by the literature [2,8,9]. For patients who function as GMFCS levels II and III, however, the literature is not consistent, but calcaneal lengthening, isolated fusions (e.g., talonavicular, extra-articular or intra-articular subtalar), and triple fusion have been reported. The common theme in the limited literature on the topic is a high rate of clinical and radiographic improvement with all techniques, but the functional improvement, complications, and long-term outcomes vary substantially, as was found in a systematic review by MacInnes et al. [9].

In one of the few long-term comparative studies, Kadhim et al. reported 5-year outcomes of calcaneal lengthening versus subtalar fusion in CP [10]. While the choice of procedure was surgeon dependent, GMFCS I and II patients underwent calcaneal lengthening more often, and GMFCS III and IV underwent subtalar fusions. Both groups showed significant post-op improvements in alignment and pressure distribution. There were 12 feet (8.7%) that experienced recurrence, and interestingly, regardless of the initial procedure, most required a medial fusion procedure. However, looking more closely at complications, there were an additional 13 hardware-related problems. Moreover, there were 35 (25%) painful feet at final follow-up, more frequent in calcaneal lengthening than fusion. A follow-up study by Kadhim et al. looked at a subset of patients with a 10-year mean follow-up from the above cohort and reported that deformity correction was durable in both groups [2]. Interestingly, long-term foot pain was less common in the subtalar fusion group, although these patients tended to have lower activity levels. Nine feet needed additional surgery to correct the foot deformity, most commonly for a midfoot collapse.

Joint-sparing techniques, such as calcaneal lengthening osteotomy, popularized by Mosca [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]], are the technique of choice in typically developing children, but have been used in CP as well [7,[10], [11], [12], [13]]. While this technique attempts to correct the hindfoot valgus and midfoot break, the recurrence rate is high in the neuromuscular population. Cho et al. reported 5-year outcomes of calcaneal lengthening in CP patients with GMFCS I through IV in 77 feet [7]. They found that the rate of undercorrection, as defined by improvement of radiographic measures to normal values, was 79.4% and 85.3% for AP and lateral talus-first metatarsal angles, respectively. Luo et al. also reported on 30 planovalgus feet, and found a 43% undercorrection rate [8]. They also found that an AP talus-first metatarsal angle >24° was a predictor of a planovalgus deformity that is beyond the corrective capacity of calcaneal lengthening to correct [14].

Another notable procedure to correct planovalgus foot in CP is the Grice extra-articular fusion, which attempts to preserve the subtalar cartilage and thus growth potential while correcting hindfoot valgus. The literature is not conclusive, but overall satisfactory outcomes have been reported [15,16]. However, the main drawbacks are the high rate of pseudoarthrosis, and the fact that this is still a fusion technique. Additionally, the midfoot break may still become problematic later in life.

In our practice, there are advantages to talonavicular fusion in GMFCS II and III patients. Talonavicular fusion is technically less demanding and time-consuming than subtalar fusion, making it easier to fit into a SEMLS. Additionally, talonavicular fusion addresses the midfoot break, while not interfering with the subtalar cartilage and hindfoot height. Fixation can be achieved with lower profile implants, which is less likely to become symptomatic. The disadvantages of this technique are that it does not address the hindfoot valgus, which could cause lateral pain and also suboptimal gait biomechanics.

We found significant improvement in radiographic indices at 1 year follow-up, which is consistent with the literature. Ramírez-Barragán et al. reported on 49 planovalgus feet in patients with GMFCS III/IV CP with minimum 10-year follow-up [17]. All radiographic measures showed significant, sustained improvement. Additionally, all patients were able to wear an AFO, and 8 did not need a brace. Similar improvements were reported in other studies [18,19].

However, the correlation between radiographic improvement and functional outcomes is not as clear. In the current study, none of the spatio-temporal gait parameters showed a significant change at 1-year post-operatively. There was a trend toward increased walking speed, lower cadence, and higher single-limb support, which suggests improvement in the base of support when ambulating, but none reached statistical significance. Turriago et al. published the only other study in the literature reporting on instrumented gait analysis after talonavicular fusion, and reported similar results, with no change in spatio-temporal parameters [2,9,17,19]. However, we found a significant improvement in the GDI post-operatively, which has not been reported before.

Common complications of talonavicular fusion are hardware irritation and non-union of the talonavicular fusion site. The latter is reported at a rate of 10–14% in the literature, although most are asymptomatic and do not require further intervention [2,10,17,19]. We had one foot with radiographic non-union and talonavicular collapse, which was asymptomatic. While the reported cases are very limited, the common denominator seems to be temporary wire fixation. We have since moved to staple fixation in most talonavicular fusions.

This study has several limitations. It is retrospective in nature and includes a relatively small sample size from a single institution. The absence of a control or comparison group limits the ability to evaluate relative effectiveness versus other techniques. Importantly, validated patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) were not collected, which limits insight into the subjective impact of surgical intervention. Given the known discordance between radiographic and functional outcomes, future studies should incorporate PROMs to better assess clinical significance. Other notable limitations are the technique differences, e.g. fixation techniques, between surgeons which may influence outcomes. Finally, long-term follow-up is needed to determine whether the improvements are sustainable. Despite these limitations, this study describes our experience in the use of talonavicular fusion in ambulatory CP patients with symptomatic planovalgus deformity. The findings offer a meaningful contribution to the literature and suggest that midfoot-directed correction may have beneficial effects on overall gait mechanics. For surgeons performing SEMLS in this population, talonavicular fusion presents a technically straightforward solution that directly addresses the apex of the deformity while preserving subtalar joint function.

Conclusion

In this study, we reported the outcomes of talonavicular fusion as part of a SEMLS procedure in patients with GMFCS II/III cerebral palsy and found significant improvements in radiographic outcomes. Despite minimal changes in spatio-temporal parameters, the improvement in GDI suggests enhanced gait biomechanics following midfoot stabilization. The procedure was associated with a low complication rate and high parental satisfaction. Given its technical simplicity compared to subtalar or triple fusion, and its ability to directly address midfoot collapse while preserving hindfoot growth and motion, talonavicular fusion represents a viable surgical option for GMFCS II–III patients with symptomatic planovalgus deformity. Future prospective studies incorporating long-term outcomes and patient-reported measures are warranted to further define the role of talonavicular fusion in the surgical algorithm for planovalgus foot in CP.
