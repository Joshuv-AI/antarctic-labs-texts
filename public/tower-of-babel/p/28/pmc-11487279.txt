Intelligent guide RNA: dual toehold switches for modulating luciferase in the presence of trigger RNA
Authors: Mohammad Hashemabadi, Hossein Ali Sasan, Saman Hosseinkhani, Mojdeh Amandadi, Azadeh Samareh Gholami, Majid Sadeghizadeh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The CRISPR system finds extensive application in molecular biology, but its continuous activity can yield adverse effects. Leveraging programmable CRISPR/Cas9 function via nano-device mediation effectively mitigates these drawbacks. The integration of RNA-sensing platforms into CRISPR thus empowers it as a potent tool for processing internal cell data and modulating gene activity. Here, an intelligent guide RNA—a cis-repressed gRNA synthetic circuit enabling efficient recognition of specific trigger RNAs—is developed. This platform carries two toehold switches and includes an inhibited CrRNA sequence. In this system, the presence of cognate trigger RNA promotes precise binding to the first toehold site, initiating a cascade that releases CrRNA to target a reporter gene (luciferase) in this study. Decoupling the CrRNA segment from the trigger RNA enhances the potential of this genetic logic circuit to respond to specific cellular circumstances, offering promise as a synthetic biology platform.

Introduction

Genetic computing circuits hold significant potential for unraveling the intricate wiring of natural gene networks and driving advancements in cellular engineering1,2. This transformative process relies on the comprehensive characterization and precise assembly of biological components into a synthetic pathways that are specifically designed to redirect the regulation of gene expression3. Nucleic acids, distinguished by their capacity to utilize Watson-Crick base pairing, have emerged as the leading strategy in synthetic biology engineering4. They possess the unique ability to induce highly specific and adjustable alterations to spatial structure through ligand binding or the implementation of toehold-mediated strand displacement reactions, the essential driving force behind enzyme-free kinetics in nucleic acid systems5–7. By employing the toehold-mediated strand displacement reaction, nucleic acids enable the programmable and reversible switching of spatial structures between different functional states, which causes precise modulation of gene expression in living cells8,9.

Controlling gene expression is a pivotal aspect of synthetic biology, and the design of inducible elements for triggering strand displacement reactions plays a crucial part in achieving this goal10. Inspired by nature, scientists have engineered gene circuits based on riboswitches to manipulate gene expression11,12. These riboregulators contain an inducible component capable of recognizing metabolites or ligands13. Some recent studies have used circuits based on toehold strand displacement to detect cognate trigger RNA14,15. In both cases, a modification in the spatial arrangement of the riboswitch structure leads to the liberation of the ribosome’s binding site, facilitating the expression of the desired target gene. However, riboregulators are predominantly found in prokaryotes with limited evidence of their application in eukaryotes, and their transferability between species is also constrained16. Furthermore, the direct manipulation of naturally occurring RNAs often presents challenges and requires additional engineering of target RNAs. In light of these challenges, employing alternative techniques like Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR) gene editing system to overcome the limitations of riboswitches can offer a more effective and suitable approach16–18.

Artificial CRISPR technique have recently brought a revolutionary breakthrough in the regulation of gene expression levels in organisms. However, it is important to address the potential off-target effects associated with uncontrolled CRISPR systems19. These systems are comprised of two crucial components: the CRISPR-associated protein (Cas) and the guide RNA (gRNA). Extensive efforts have been made to achieve controlled gene expression by modulating the activity of the Cas9 protein through chemical or optical inputs. However, a common limitation of these approaches is that they regulate all target genes in a uniform manner. Another promising option entails the development of gRNAs that exhibit conditional behavior, responding to specific ligands or trigger RNAs. By introducing alterations in the secondary structure of the gRNA, distinct functional states (ON or OFF) of the CRISPR system can be achieved. In recent studies, the direct strand displacement of carefully designed guide RNAs (gRNAs) has unveiled the potential to program target gene expressions using trigger RNAs, enabling the conditional activation of CRISPR function20,21. These investigations highlight the significant potential for developing genetic circuits that integrate strand displacement with CRISPR technology. While these strategies implement switchable gRNA for Cas9 functions, the question of how these powerful molecular computers are applied to achieve autonomous control based on endogenous cellular information remains a challenging one.

Here we present an advanced conditional platform called intelligent guide RNA (IngRNA), which demonstrates the ability to detect specific triggers. In this platform, the Crispr RNA(CrRNA) sequence, one of the components of gRNA, is sequestered by flanking sequences to prevent its function from targeting genes of interest in the absence of the trigger RNA. The design includes a short linker sequence, with the trigger RNA site located upstream. A substantial portion of these specific sequences are intentionally inhibited by the complementary strand. The upper loop of the platform contains the first toehold, which provides the necessary binding force for the trigger RNA. The trigger RNA binding event initiates the opening of the second toehold, situated within the complementary strand of the trigger RNA binding site. The liberation of the second toehold from its obstructed state facilitates the connection of the 5′ side of the platform to it, resulting in the release of CrRNA from its restrained condition, thus enabling it to carry out its intended function. In this biological circuit, the targeting of the luciferase gene is investigated in the presence or absence of cognate trigger RNA in test tubes, bacteria, and mammalian cells.

Material and methods

Material

The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed using the Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase enzyme (ThermoScientific). All DNA cloning oligonucleotides, PCR primers, and synthesized sequences for IngRNA1, IngRNA2, and IngRNA2B were obtained from GeneScript (listed in Supplementary Table (S3.2–S3.5)). Sanger sequencing was performed with the Sanger Sequencing 3500 Dx Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems) to verify the integration of all sequences into the plasmid. Restriction enzymes and T5-Exo enzymes were purchased from ThermoScientific and NEB, respectively, and used according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Linearized DNA vector and corresponding inserts were purified using the Favorgen Gel Extraction Kit (specify type, e.g., for DNA) in the cloning experiments. Subsequently, ligation was performed using T4 DNA ligase (ThermoScientific). Genomic DNA and plasmid extraction were carried out using the Miniprep Kit (Favorgen). RT enzyme and SYBR™ Green PCR Master Mix were purchased from ThermoScientific, while the T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit was obtained from NEB.

Plasmid construction

Briefly, for expressing Cas9 in the pET28a plasmid (ZaverZistAzma, Iran, #394-2), the thrombin site and T7-Tag were removed to avoid potential interference with Cas9 expression (Supplementary Fig. S2.7b). Oligonucleotides WmP and KmP were annealed and subcloned between NcoI/NotI sites of pET28a, creating pETm. The bacterial codon-optimized Cas9 gene was amplified (FcP/RcP primers) and subcloned between NotI/SalI sites of pETm. For bacterial expression of the gRNAs, pTargetF (Addgene, Cat#:62226) and pCas (Addgene, Cat#:62225) plasmids were used (gifts from Sheng Yang). The pCL plasmid was generated by TEDA cloning, as described in a previous study22, and the RED recombinant system in pCas was substituted with the bacterial codon-optimized luciferase gene (Iranian firefly, GenBank: AY742225.1) (Supplementary Fig. S2.8). The process involved using the primers specified in (Supplementary Table S3.2). To align the gRNA targeting site with the firefly luciferase sequence used in eukaryotic cells, quick-change PCR with FqlwC/RqlwC primers was used to generate pCLw23 The same method (quick-change PCR) was employed with primers FqcD/RqcD and FqcH/RqcH to generate an inactivated Cas9 variant (pdCas9) (Supplementary Fig. S2.8). For plasmid pTw, two BbsI sites were introduced downstream of the j23119 promoter in pTargetF, followed by fragment insertion (Supplementary Fig. S2.7a). pX(Reg) and pX(Sc) plasmids were formed by inserting annealed oligonucleotides between the BbsI site in pX458 (Addgene, Cat#:48138) as described previously24. Similar methods were used to construct remaining plasmids. Detailed information can be found in Supplementary Table S3.1 and Supplementary Fig. S2.7 and S2.8. Supplementary Fig. S2.6 displays all plasmids, along with their annotations and the genes they contain.

In vitro DNA cleavage assay

The SpCas9 protein purification was carried out as previous study with the modification of employing the BL21(DE3) pLysS bacterial strain instead of Rosetta25. Initially, BL21(DE3) pLysS competent E. coli cells were transformed with the pETm-Cas9 plasmid and selected on LB agar plates containing 50 μg/mL kanamycin. A single colony was grown overnight at 37 °C with shaking in LB broth containing kanamycin. The culture was then diluted 1:200 into fresh LB media and incubated at 37 °C until OD600 reached 0.5. Protein expression was induced by adding IPTG to a final concentration of 0.3 mM, followed by incubation for 14 h with shaking at 16 °C. The cells were harvested by centrifugation and lysed. Subsequently, SpCas9 protein was purified using Ni-NTA and cation exchange chromatography.

Guide RNAs (gRNAs) and trigger RNA were transcribed in vitro using the T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (NEB) following the manufacturer’s protocol. After transcription, the RNA samples were treated with DNase I for 30 min to remove any contaminating DNA. gRNAs were then purified using the TRIzol reagent. The target double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) used in the cleavage reaction was amplified by PCR and purified using the PCR Purification Kit (Favorgen). In the in vitro cleavage reaction, 150 nM of the target DNA was incubated with 100 nM of the gRNA in the presence of 200 nM Cas9 protein, all in CutSmart buffer (NEB). The reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Finally, the reactions were stopped with 6X DNA loading buffer and analyzed by non-denaturing agarose gel electrophoresis.

Trigger RNA Expression induction in bacteria and Bioluminescence assay

To initiate luciferase and trigger RNA expression within bacterial cells along the conditioned platform, 10 ng of pTw plasmid was introduced into BL21(DE3) competent cells harboring the pCLw plasmid using heat shock. Transformants were selected on LB agar plates containing kanamycin (50 μg/mL) and spectinomycin (100 μg/mL). A single colony was confirmed to contain both plasmids by PCR reaction.

The culture was grown in liquid LB medium containing both antibiotics until reaching an OD600 of 0.5. To mitigate growth disruptions in bacteria without the pCLw plasmid and to ensure retention of pCLw plasmids in the absence of antibiotics, the complete removal of kanamycin was avoided. Instead, the kanamycin concentration in the culture medium was reduced to 6 μg/mL prior to triggering RNA induction. After attaining the desired cell density, the cells were centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 4 min, and the media were replaced with fresh LB containing only spectinomycin (50 μg/mL) and a reduced concentration of kanamycin (6 μg/mL). IPTG (0.08 mM final concentration) was then added to induce trigger RNA expression, leading to activation of the IngRNA platform. Following 30 min of trigger RNA induction, L-arabinose (10 mM final concentration) was added to induce luciferase expression. The culture was incubated for an additional 6 h at 30 °C with shaking at 180 rpm.

To quantify luciferase activity, an equivalent amount of culture (based on OD600) was lysed by sonication. The lysate was centrifuged at 14,500 rpm for 10 min. The 10 μl supernatant, after normalization using the Bradford assay26, was mixed with 10 μl of substrate solution containing 10 mM MgSO4, 2 mM D-Luciferin potassium salt, 4 mM ATP, and 50 mM Tris–HCl at pH 7.8. Luminescence was measured using a luminometer (Berthold Detection System, Germany). To explore the impact of the IngRNAs-dCas9 complex on luciferase expression in bacteria, the aforementioned procedures are replicated with one modification: the pCLw vector, containing the active Cas9 enzyme, is replaced by the pdCas9 vector, housing the inactive form of Cas9.

For the antibiotic sensitivity test, 10 ng of the pTw vector was introduced into BL21(DE3) competent cells containing the pCLw vector through heat shock. A 50 μl aliquot of the transformation mixture was plated on LB agar containing both spectinomycin (100 μg/mL) and kanamycin (50 μg/mL), while another 50 μl aliquot was plated on LB agar containing only spectinomycin (100 μg/mL). Plates were incubated for 16 h at 30 °C.

Exploration of the intelligent platform in HEK293T cells

To achieve Cas9 protein expression in eukaryotic cells, the pSpCas9(BB)-2A-GFP (PX458) which was a gift from Feng Zhang (Addgene plasmid # 48138) was utilized. The IngRNA2 sequence and control sequences were incorporated between the Bbs1 site. Furthermore, miR16 expression was enhanced by inserting the pre-miR16 sequence downstream of the CMV promoter, positioned between the BamH1 and EcoR1 cutting sites in pcDNA3.1(+) plasmid gift from Dr. Karim Rahimi. Additionally, the full GFP sequence in the pDB2 (Plasmid #18954) plasmid was substituted with luciferase gene sequence. Subsequently, the fragments were inserted into the designated plasmids, resulting in the creation of pX-IngRNA2, pCN-m16, and pDBL plasmids, respectively.

HEK293 cells were cultivated in 12-well plates with a seeding density of 120,000 cells per well. The growth medium used was DMEM with 10% FBS. After 20 h of plating, when the cells reached approximately 70% confluence, plasmids were transfected. To target the luciferase gene in the pDBL plasmid, each well was transfected with 400 ng of pX-IngRNA2 plasmid, 800 ng of pDBL plasmid, and 300 ng of pCN-m16 plasmid (for trigger expression) using 2.5 μL of Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were harvested 20 h post-transfection. After rinsing twice with ice-cold Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the cells were lysed with hypotonic lysis buffer containing 20 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.6), 1.5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 100 mM sucrose, and 1 mM PMSF, which preserved intact mitochondria. After three freeze-thaw cycles, insoluble material was removed by centrifugation at 13,000 × g and 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and its protein content was normalized for further luminescence signal analysis, as described previously27.

Determination of trigger RNA expression levels

To collect eukaryotic and bacterial RNA, cells were pelleted through centrifugation at 3000 g for 5 min and washed twice with PBS. Subsequently, total RNA was promptly extracted from the samples using TRIzol, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted total RNAs underwent quality assessment through agarose gel electrophoresis and were quantified using UV–Visible spectrophotometry, ensuring that the OD260/280 value fell within the range of 1.8 to 2.0. The DNase treatment was applied to remove any DNA contamination then store in −80 °C.

The expression of trigger RNA was quantified using the stem-loop method, Briefly, 1 µg of total RNA was combined with 100 nM stem-loop primer (Supplementary Table S3.4) and the RT Mix (containing dNTP (0.25 mM each), reaction buffer, random primers(200 nM), reverse transcriptase (0.5U), and RNase inhibitor (0.5U)). The reaction was performed at 25 °C for 5 min, followed by 50 °C for 30 min, and then the reverse transcriptase was inactivated at 85 °C for 5 min. The qPCR experiments were performed using the reaction mixture containing SYBR Green reagent and primers (100 nM) in (Supplementary Table S3.4). The U6 small nuclear RNA was used as the internal reference gene to normalize miR-16 expression levels The thermocycling conditions for miR-16 and U6 as references gene were as follows: Initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 38 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing at 60 °C for 1 min.

The expression of trigger RNA in BL21(DE3) bacterial strain was assessed using the same approach, and stable 16S ribosomal RNA was employed as a reference to normalize the trigger RNA expression. The reaction was conducted as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 38 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing at 58 °C for 1 min. The data analysis involved using the threshold cycle (Ct) values and applying the 2-ΔCT and log2 (2-ΔΔCT) formulas. The StepOne Software (RRID: SCR_014281) was employed for data analysis. The experiments were conducted using the primer pairs outlined in (Supplementary Table S3.4). The results are expressed as the means ± SEM from triplicate strain experiments.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were performed at least in triplicate, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test, with a p-value threshold of 0.05

Results

Intelligent-gRNA design

To control Cas9 activity, we implemented an OFF-to-ON circuit using a conditional gRNA. This gRNA guides the Cas9 nuclease to cleave the firefly luciferase gene between nucleotides 67 and 68. In the OFF-state of the circuit, CrRNA inhibition within the gRNA prevents Cas9 from cleaving the target DNA. However, when a cognate trigger RNA is present in the environment, it induces the ON state of the system, activating Cas9 and leading to the silencing of the luciferase signal (Fig. 1). The crystal structure of the Cas9-gRNA complex demonstrated that the elongated 5′ end of gRNA does not interfere with Cas9 function in targeting the gene of interest. To design the IngRNA1, we utilized NUPACK source code (http://www.nupack.org) to add 92 nucleotides to the 5′ end of the gRNA, for effective inhibiting of the initial 15 nucleotides (Fig. 1a). Within this design, we placed 5 nt upstream of CrRNA as a linker and positioned the trigger RNA site upstream as well. Eight nucleotides from this position were incorporated into the loop as the first toehold, as previous research has shown its effectiveness in initiating the strand displacement reaction30. The Supplementary Section S1 provides details on the design of the IngRNA strand. The NUPACK algorithm is tailored to forecast the stability and behavior of basic nucleic acid structures like hairpins or duplexes. However, it faces challenges when dealing with complex designs featuring sequences housing multiple binding sites. Especially for IngRNAs, where the second toehold site and its upstream sequence can form complementary base pairs with both the target strand and the end of the platform simultaneously, designing these sequences presents a challenge for the NUPACK algorithm. To address this limitation, three RNA strands named M, S and D were introduced for the design (Supplementary Fig. S2.1). The S strand inhibits CrRNA, as depicted in Supplementary Fig. S2.2. The presence of the T strand or trigger RNA next to the M.S complex exposes the second toehold site. In the presence of the D strand, the strand displacement process can be initiated, leading to the liberation of the CrRNA sequence from restraint. The various stages of this reaction in the tube are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S2.3 as the target test tube.

The NUPACK functions were executed at a designated temperature (37 °C), 1.0 M Na+, and 0 M Mg2+, using the free energy parameters from Serra and Turner31. The assumed strand concentrations were set at 10 nM. The resulting IngRNA designs were evaluated for their orthogonality and screened to verify their effective interaction with the trigger RNA, while also minimizing cross-talk. Following the design process, the assembly of the S, D, and M strands resulted in the formation of the complete IngRNA. NUPACK analysis of the individual strand in the presence of the RNA trigger revealed the liberation of CrRNA from restraint (Supplementary Movie 1). This led to the designation of the platform as IngRNA1, and Minimum Free Energy (MFE) structures changed from −80 kcal/mol to −107 kcal/mol upon binding of the cognate trigger RNA. This release of energy drives a spontaneous strand displacement reaction. During in vitro cleavage experiments, we observed that the IngRNA1 structure had the ability to open spontaneously even without the presence of the trigger RNA. To prevent this undesired event, we designed a second-generation of IngRNA1, denoted as IngRNA2, which fully suppress 20 nucleotides of the crRNA sequence (Fig. 1b). These versions demonstrate specific recognition abilities, targeting trigger RNA (A). This modification decreased the MFE structure of IngRNA2 by 13 kcal/mol compared to IngRNA1, enhancing its stability, and preventing spontaneous opening at in vitro. Furthermore, to showcase the versatility of the IngRNA2 framework for diverse trigger RNAs, we developed the IngRNA2B variant see Table S3.5, stemming from the original IngRNA2 design, tailored to precisely target trigger RNA(B) (Table S3.5). This step was taken to expand the assessment of the IngRNA2-dCas9 complex’s efficacy.

In Vitro conditional RNA-guided DNA cleavage

To evaluate the system’s efficiency, an in vitro cleavage assay was performed. The assay aimed to induce a cut at the position between nucleotides 67 and 68 of the luciferase gene, causing the 1860 base pair PCR product to be cleaved into two fragments of 1506 and 354 base pairs, respectively (Fig. 2a). we first optimized the cleavage time for the target using 100 nM regular gRNA. Based on our findings, a two-hour duration proved adequate for subsequent downstream experiments (Supplementary Fig. S2.4a). We then examined the impact of 100 nM IngRNA1 on DNA cleavage, both in the presence and absence of a DNA mimic trigger (500 nM). We observed that the efficiency of the strand displacement reaction was not significant after two hours in the presence of the DNA-RNA complex. Moreover, there was no obvious difference in the induction of cleavage when the DNA mimic was present (Supplementary Fig. S2.4b). Continuing our investigation, we subjected the RNA form of the trigger to a similar test. Although we achieved the desired result, we noticed unintended spontaneous activation of the system and cleavage of the target DNA in the absence of the cognate trigger RNA as observed in lane number 4 (Fig. 2b). The primary cause of the system’s spontaneous opening is attributed to the limited quantity of binding base pairs. To tackle the significant issue of spontaneous release of CrRNA blockage, we developed the second generation of IngRNA, referred to as IngRNA2, which fully inhibits all 20 nucleotides of CrRNA, unlike IngRNA1. In the absence of the trigger RNA, no undesired activity was observed (lane 6, Fig. 2b), while the presence of the trigger RNA demonstrated effective cleavage of the target DNA (lane 7, Fig. 2b). To gauge the sensitivity of the system, we tested different concentrations of the cognate trigger RNA under identical conditions. Assessing system efficiency in cleaving and eliminating luciferase gene-containing vectors in bacteria

In this study, we utilized Escherichia coli strain BL21(DE3) pLysS to investigate the gene circuit’s function in living samples. For this objective, we utilized two vectors: To explore various gRNAs, we employed a modified version of the pTargetF vector (pTargetF, Addgene plasmid #62226), named pTw, which includes two BbsI cleavage sites positioned downstream of the constitutive promoter j23119 (Supplementary Fig. S2.7a). Additionally, the modified pCas vector (pCas, Addgene plasmid #62225), referred to as pCLw in this study, has the luciferase gene replacing the genes associated with the lambda red recombination system. The luciferase gene is regulated by the arabinose-inducible araBAD promoter, while the expression of trigger RNA is controlled by the Trc (trp-lac) promoter, which can be induced by IPTG (Supplementary Fig. S2.8). When bacteria expressed IngRNA2 and were exposed to different IPTG concentrations, the resulting variations in cognate trigger RNA concentration subsequently influenced luciferase activity. Remarkably, at a final IPTG concentration of 0.08 mM and after 6 h of incubation (30 °C), the CRISPR system efficiently suppressed luciferase activity through targeted regulation (Fig. 3a). The reduction in basal expression of the luciferase reporter in the absence of IPTG is observed in Fig. 3a, indicated by the lower luciferase activity levels compared to the IPTG-induced conditions. We also considered the possibility of another cause, such as a potential leak in the Trc promoter responsible for trigger RNA expression32. To investigate this reduction further, we quantified the amount of trigger RNA expression in the absence of IPTG and under the same conditions using real-time PCR. After normalization, it was found that the expression of trigger RNA increased two-fold at 30 °C compared to cells with an empty vector that does not contain the trigger RNA gene. This suggests potential promoter leakage from the Trc promoter responsible for trigger RNA expression (Supplementary Fig. S2.5a).

The activation of IngRNA2 in the presence of trigger RNA led to the cleavage of the luciferase gene and subsequent removal of the pCLw vector. This rendered the bacteria susceptible to kanamycin, as confirmed by an antibiotic sensitivity test. After transforming bacterial cells with two vectors, pCLw and pTw, an IPTG concentration of 0.08 mM was added. The transformed cells were then incubated overnight on separate plates. One plate contained both spectinomycin and kanamycin, while the other contained only spectinomycin. The absence of colonies on the plate with both antibiotics indicates susceptibility to kanamycin due to the removal of the pCLw vector. However, under identical conditions on the plate with spectinomycin alone, the absence of the pCLw vector does not hinder bacterial growth. In this case, resistance to spectinomycin is solely attributed to the presence of the pTw vector, as evidenced by the presence of colonies on this plate. Additionally, when IPTG was not present to induce trigger RNA expression, we observed significantly fewer kanamycin-resistant bacterial colonies expressing IngRNA2 via the pTw(Ing2) plasmid compared to those expressing the scrambled control through the pTw(Sc) plasmid. This difference suggests that the Cas9-IngRNA2 complex might be undergoing undesired leaky cleavage activity in the absence of IPTG induction (Fig. 3b).

Spontaneous IngRNA2 unfolding and temperature effects on unintended luciferase targeting in bacteria

To compare the unintended effects of IngRNA1 and IngRNA2 on plasmid targeting, an antibiotic sensitivity test was conducted in the absence of IPTG. The results demonstrated that complete inhibition of CrRNA within the IngRNA2 platform led to reduced targeting of the luciferase gene. Consequently, colonies expressing IngRNA2 outnumbered those expressing IngRNA1 (Fig. 3c). In the previous steps, we observed a decrease in the basal expression of luciferase, possibly due to a leak in the promoter responsible for trigger RNA expression. However, the role of the IngRNA2 structure unfolding in reducing of luciferase expression remains uncertain (Fig. 4a). To gain deeper insights, we substituted the expression of the cognate trigger RNA with a non-cognate trigger RNA in bacteria and assessed the luciferase activity. Selective regulation of luciferase activity using dCas9 in bacterial cells

The activity of IngRNA2 leads to the cleavage of the pCLw plasmid, resulting in plasmid deletion and a significant disruption of trigger RNA expression. This unwanted effect arises because both the trigger RNA expression sequence and the luciferase gene sequence are present within the same plasmid (pCLw). Consequently, the cleavage of the luciferase gene also results in the loss of the trigger RNA, causing a depletion in the expression levels of Trigger RNA compared to IngRNA. To address this issue, we utilized a catalytically inactive form of Cas9, known as dCas9, which was created by introducing specific mutations into the active Cas9 form (Supplementary Fig. S2.8). By using dCas9, we were able to bind to the luciferase gene without causing cleavage, thus preventing the loss of the pCLw plasmid and maintaining consistent levels of both IngRNA2 and trigger RNA expression. This approach stabilized the system and ensured continuous trigger RNA expression.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully developed a cis-repressed gRNA synthetic circuit that leverages RNA nanotechnology principles to achieve efficient recognition of stimulatory RNA, leading to targeted gene of interest. One feature of the IngRNA structure is its decoupling of the trigger RNA binding site sequence from the CrRNA region. This separation fosters Orthogonality and eliminates sequence constraints for trigger RNA sequences. This design mirrors the approach seen in Ma et al. introduction of the multi-arm riboswitch for viral RNA detection. Both systems exhibit initial trigger RNA binding from the loop region of the structure34. However, the intelligent guide RNA presents advantages over riboswitch-based systems, especially in eukaryotes, where ribo-regulators face restricted applicability35. Additionally, it overcomes the limitations of earlier methods that depended on partial sequence similarity between two RNAs. Moreover, this study addresses a constraint observed in earlier research where distinct hairpins were required to liberate the CrRNA from inhibition. This method introduced here eliminates the necessity for these separate components to locate each other and engage in a series of reactions within the complex cellular milieu36. Consequently, it streamlines the process of targeting the intended gene.

Another extra benefit provided by this system is its initial OFF configuration (Fig. 1). This implies that at 20 °C, it almost remains inactive in suppressing the target gene until the trigger RNA is present (Fig. 4c). This effectively thwarts any undesired system activity until the RNA trigger comes into play. In situations involving ON state setups, there’s a potential for undesired system activity until the circuit responds to the stimulating RNA37. In a prior investigation conducted by Jin and colleagues,they demonstrated the compatibility of their designed conditional platforms (rgRNA and agRNA) with both RNA and DNA triggers38. However, in the current study, through in vitro experiments, it was revealed that the influence of a DNA trigger on the release of CrRNA from the blockade and the subsequent cleavage of the DNA target was relatively modest (Supplementary Fig. S2.4b). Conversely, altering the trigger type from DNA to RNA had a distinct and noticeable impact on cleavage (Fig. 2b). It may be suggested the enhanced effectiveness of RNA strands to bind RNA complements is primarily attributed to their precise base pairing capability and the presence of ribose sugar in their structure.

The leaky cleavage observed in the IngRNA1 structure (Fig. 2b) was also noticed in the A5 structure examined in Siu and Chen’s research (21). Although by optimizing the structure of IngRNA1 to IngRNA2 unintended cleavage in the absence of trigger RNA was prevented (Fig. 2b), the outcome was different within bacteria, where this undesired activity was detected (Fig. 4b). In comparison to the total inhibition observed for IngRNA2 in vitro without a trigger RNA, the activity of IngRNA2 in bacteria might be attributed to the extended bacterial growth period compared to the in-vitro incubation duration. The most likely reason for the spontaneous unfolding of the structure is the presence of a single nucleotide mismatch bulge, deliberately introduced to facilitate the strand displacement process or secondary structure instability (Fig. 4a)39,40. The insertion of a single nucleotide mismatch bulge in the structure may behave as a toehold, but its efficiency is considerably lower compared to an eight-nucleotide toehold30.

In this study, it was demonstrated that even without the presence of IPTG as an inducer, a minimal quantity of RNA trigger in the bacteria can be detected (Supplementary Fig. S2.5a). This leads to the unfolding of the IngRNA2 structure and the release of the CrRNA structure from its inhibited state. This phenomenon explains the leakage in expression observed in the absence of IPTG, and it is attributed to the Trc promoter41. The luciferase activity remained stable at lower temperatures (Fig. 4c), likely because the IngRNA structure exhibits greater stability in such conditions42. This stability enables precise control of the entire system’s activity at lower temperatures, and we anticipate similar results at higher temperatures. Henceforth, it is advisable for future experiments to either devise a circuit devoid of a single nucleotide mismatch bulge or enhance the IngRNA structure through directed mutagenesis, focusing on modifying flexible nucleotides that are independent of the trigger RNA binding site sequence and CrRNA sequence. Additionally, incorporating the rhaB promoter, which can be induced by L-Rhamnose, could be helpful to minimize trigger RNA leakage for downstream bacterial studies43. This approach aims to potentially enhance overall efficiency.

To evaluate platform performance in bacterial cells, we found that having both the target gene sequence and trigger RNA coding sequence on a single plasmid leads to plasmid deletion and lower expression levels of trigger RNA compared to IngRNA. To address this, we used an inactive form of Cas9, known as dCas9, which binds to DNA sequences without causing double-strand breaks, thus preventing cell toxicity and plasmid loss while regulating gene expression without genomic instability. The key difference between dCas9 and active Cas9 is that active Cas9 cleaves DNA, leading to gene disruption and potential cellular damage, whereas dCas9 acts as a steric hindrance to transcription44. This makes dCas9 suitable for applications requiring gene regulation without permanent DNA modification. Fig. 5 shows higher luciferase activity suppression compared to Fig. 6, indicating that dCas9, while effective, is less so than creating double-strand breaks because the gene remains intact and can still be transcribed at reduced levels. Additionally, dCas9’s efficiency can vary based on promoter strength, enhancer elements, and chromatin context, while active Cas9 ensures more robust repression. To demonstrate versatility, we developed IngRNA2B to target trigger RNA (B), showing that the active IngRNA2 platform combined with dCas9 binds to the luciferase gene, reducing luciferase expression by 2.8-fold with IngRNA2 and 3.7-fold with IngRNA2B in the presence of their respective trigger RNAs (Fig. 6a).

The adaptability of this logic circuit to mammalian cells was explored by employing miR-16 as the trigger RNA (A). While some research has indicated an increase in miR-16 levels following cisplatin treatment in cells45,46, and others have suggested that heightened miR-16 expression makes cells more vulnerable to cisplatin, potentially through Bcl-2 regulation47,48, our investigation revealed no significant change in miR-16 expression in Hek293 cells treated with 10 μM cisplatin. In contrast, Xie et al. observed approximately a two-fold rise in miR-16 expression in K562 cells and a notable increase in SH-SY5Y cells at the same cisplatin concentration46,49. Since cisplatin did not affect miR-16 expression in Hek293 cells, we enhanced miR-16 expression using the pcDNA3 plasmid to evaluate its effectiveness as an RNA trigger on the IngRNA2 platform, leading to a substantial increase in miR-16-expressing cells. Consequently, luciferase activity was effectively inhibited in the presence of miR-16, demonstrating successful targeted modulation in mammalian cells (Fig. 7a). In earlier research, Mr. Hanewich and colleagues demonstrated that elevating the levels of cognate RNA triggers results in OFF state of their Terminator Switch platform, rendering it incapable of binding to its target in HEK293. They utilized dCas9-VPR to amplify the dTomato reporter signal, thereby inhibiting the conditional gRNA through increased trigger RNA expression. Their findings revealed a fourfold decrease in the fluorescent protein signal in the presence of trigger RNA compared to its absence20. Additionally, Mr. Lin’s experiment employed a self-inhibitory gRNA (OFF-ON switch), demonstrating that elevated endogenous expression of miR-17 activates the switch, resulting in over a tenfold increase in the expression of the endogenous CXCR4 gene36. However, in our study, we employed the wild-type Cas9 variant to target the luciferase gene, which led to a 70-fold reduction in luciferase signal with a ninefold elevation in miR-16 expression acting as the trigger RNA.

In summary, based on the findings presented in this manuscript, a conditional platform integrating RNA nanotechnology and CRISPR technology has been developed, marking a pioneering endeavor. The IngRNA1 and IngRNA2 platforms demonstrated efficient recognition of trigger RNA and modulation of gene activity. This system holds significant potential as a robust tool in synthetic biology, diagnostic methods, and gene regulation across diverse cellular environments. Moreover, it shows considerable promise for inducing cell death by selectively targeting antiapoptotic genes in response to specific trigger molecules, thereby making it applicable in cancer therapy and gene therapy through the sensing of specific markers.
