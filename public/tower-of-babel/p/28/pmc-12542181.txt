Thermal water inhalation for allergic rhinitis and recurrent respiratory infections: a narrative review of the evidence
Authors: Attilio Varricchio, Giulia Brindisi, Francesco Paolo Brunese, Maria Daglia, Giulio Dinardo, Lorenzo Drago, Alessandra Gori, Cristiana Indolfi, Matteo Naso, Enrico Tondina, Chiara Trincianti, Antonio Varricchio, Anna Maria Zicari, Michele Miraglia del Giudice, Giorgio Ciprandi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAllergic rhinitis (AR) and recurrent respiratory infections (RRIs) are common conditions that significantly impact quality of life and healthcare systems. Thermal water inhalation therapies have emerged as a potential non-pharmacological option due to their immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory properties. This narrative review aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of thermal water inhalations in managing patients with AR and RRIs. The inclusion criteria were pertinent articles written in English and published in journals listed in PubMed. Six studies conducted in Italy, involving 548 participants (most of whom were children and adolescents), met the inclusion criteria. Significant improvements were observed in nasal mucociliary transport time, with reductions ranging from 2 to 8 min across treatment groups. Total Symptom Scores decreased significantly (one study reported a > 50% reduction). The frequency and duration of upper respiratory tract infections were reduced considerably in the treatment groups compared to the controls. However, results for nasal cytology were inconsistent, and heterogeneity in study designs limited comparability. The reviewed studies highlighted the potential of thermal water therapies to enhance mucociliary clearance, alleviate symptoms, and reduce reliance on other pharmacological treatments. On the other hand, these studies have a regional limitation and this review was not systematic. In conclusion, this narrative review may suggest that thermal water inhalations could be a promising option for managing patients with AR and RRIs, providing an alternative therapy with significant clinical benefits. Nevertheless, further high-quality, standardized, and international studies are necessary to confirm these findings and facilitate meta-analyses. These treatments could play a valuable role in reducing the burden of respiratory and allergic conditions.Keywords: Thermal water, Allergic rhinitis, Recurrent respiratory infections, Symptoms, Mucociliary clearance

Introduction

Recurrent respiratory infections (RRIs) and allergic rhinitis (AR) are highly prevalent conditions affecting children, adolescents, and adults, often imposing a significant burden on the quality of life and healthcare systems. Allergic rhinitis is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the nasal cavity, primarily triggered by a type 2 immunological reaction to environmental allergens, which leads to symptoms such as nasal obstruction, sneezing, and rhinorrhea [1]. It frequently coexists with other respiratory conditions, including asthma, highlighting a shared type 2 immunity [2] and potentially increasing susceptibility to the development of RRIs. On the other hand, RRIs, characterized by repeated infections, particularly in the upper respiratory tract, are commonly encountered in pediatric and general practice. These infections are often associated with environmental factors, immune system immaturity, and the formation of microbial biofilms [3].

Conventional management strategies for these conditions involve pharmacological therapies, mainly including antihistamines, corticosteroids, and antibiotics. However, the increasing prevalence of antimicrobial resistance and the limitations of symptom management highlight the need for alternative therapeutic approaches [4]. In this context, thermal water inhalation therapies, a form of crenotherapy, have gained attention for their potential to modulate immune responses, improve mucociliary clearance, and alleviate respiratory symptoms [5].

Thermal water inhalations, characterized by their chemical and physical properties, including sulfurous and mineral-rich compositions, have traditionally been used to treat respiratory and dermatological disorders. Emerging evidence suggests their efficacy in reducing the frequency and severity of RRIs and AR symptoms. Despite promising findings, no dedicated review has evaluated the role of thermal water inhalations specifically for these two conditions that are frequently associated. On the contrary, the Italian Society of Pediatric Allergy and Immunology (SIAIP) recently promoted reviews on non-pharmacological interventions in managing children with allergic rhinitis [6–8]. Therefore, the SIAIP promoted the present narrative review as a novelty in this clinical area. This narrative review also addresses this gap by critically appraising the existing literature on thermal water inhalation therapies for AR and RRIs. By synthesizing evidence from clinical studies, this review provides a comprehensive understanding of their therapeutic potential while identifying limitations and areas for further research.

Materials and methods

We conducted a narrative review, as the published literature was restricted and most studies were performed with non-rigorous methodology.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was performed across multiple databases, including the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), PubMed, and Scopus. The search was conducted in November 2024. The search terms “thermal waters,” “sulfurous waters,” “balneotherapy,” and “crenotherapy” were coupled with “allergic rhinitis” and “recurrent respiratory infections” using the Boolean operator “AND.”

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) enrolled patients diagnosed with allergic rhinitis or recurrent respiratory infections, encompassing both pediatric and adult populations without age restrictions; (2) employed thermal water therapeutic interventions, specifically inhalations, nasal irrigations, or aerosol administrations, utilizing sulfurous, bromo-iodine, or radioactive water compositions; (3) specified precise treatment protocols with defined durations; and (4) reported quantifiable clinical outcomes. Study designs for inclusion comprised randomized controlled trials, prospective clinical studies, and methodologically sound observational investigations.

Studies were excluded if they: (1) involved subjects with confounding comorbidities (immunodeficiencies, uncontrolled asthma, significant cardiovascular conditions); (2) lacked relevant respiratory health outcome measures; (3) were case reports, narrative reviews, or methodologically inadequate investigations; or (4) were published in languages other than English. Three authors (FPB, AV, and GC) revised the initially selected papers and identified the eligible studies to be included in the present review.

Study selection and data extraction

The literature search identified 153 initial articles. After removing duplicates and excluding non-clinical studies and irrelevant diseases, 67 studies remained for abstract review. Of these, 56 were excluded during the screening process. To assess eligibility, the remaining 11 studies underwent full-text review by two independent, blinded reviewers. Five studies were excluded due to irrelevant outcomes, poor study quality, or review articles (Fig. 1).

A narrative description of the included studies

Study characteristics

All six eligible studies in this narrative review were conducted in Italy across regions, including Comano, Naples, Merano, and Parma. The studies included 548 patients, with individual study sizes ranging from 30 to 140 participants. The study populations consisted of children and adults diagnosed with allergic rhinitis and recurrent upper respiratory infections.

The studies investigated various thermal water types, including Comano thermal water [9], Ischia hyper-mineral chloride-sodium water [10], bromo-iodine thermal water [11], sulfide thermal water [12], sulfurous thermal water [5], and radioactive oligomineral water from Merano [13]. Each water type offered distinct chemical and physical properties for treating respiratory and inflammatory conditions.

Treatment durations varied from 12 days to three months, with follow-up periods ranging from two weeks to six months. This flexibility demonstrates the adaptability of thermal water therapies for both short-term and long-term treatment approaches.

Results

The analysis revealed consistent benefits of thermal water therapies across several outcomes. A description of the common outcomes is presented in Table 2. Passali et al. observed significant improvements in nasal mucociliary transport time (NMTT), with a reduction from a mean of 13 to 12 min in the treatment group, compared to 14 to 13 min in the control group (p < 0.001) [13]. La Mantia et al. reported a significant decrease in NMTT from 12.3 ± 1.3 to 9.6 ± 0.7 min in the treatment group, while the control group showed a reduction from 11.9 ± 1.1 to 10.9 ± 0.9 min (p < 0.05) [11]. Salami et al. similarly found an improvement from 19.22 ± 2.23 to 11.15 ± 1.59 min in the treatment group, exceeding the control group’s improvement from 18.98 ± 2.21 to 17.63 ± 2.17 min (p = 0.05) [6].

Common outcomes across the studies

For upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs), Varricchio et al. found significant reductions in both groups, with a more pronounced improvement in the treatment group, from 8.2 ± 1.2 to 3.1 ± 1.6 episodes compared to 8.3 ± 1.3 to 6.3 ± 1.0 reduction in controls (p < 0.01; p < 0.001) [12]. Salami et al. reported fewer than two episodes in both groups (p = 0.05) [6], while La Mantia et al. found no significant differences between groups (p = n.s.) [11]. Regarding URTI duration, La Mantia et al. observed a significant decrease from 15.6 ± 4.8 to 10.4 ± 2.7 days in the treatment group, compared to a minimal reduction from 15.2 ± 3.5 to 14.8 ± 3.1 days in the control group (p < 0.05) [11]. Salami et al. reported episodes lasting fewer than 3 days in the treatment group, compared to 3–6 days in the controls (p = 0.05) [6].

Total Symptom Scores (TSS) showed significant improvements in two studies. Ciprandi et al. reported a decrease from 6 ± 2.59 to 3 ± 2.96 in the treatment group (p < 0.001) [9], while Miraglia Del Giudice et al. found a reduction to 206.5 ± 40.3 in the treatment group versus 316.2 ± 66.2 in controls (p < 0.001) [10]. Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores also improved markedly: Ciprandi et al. noted an increase from 2 ± 0 to 8 ± 1.63 in the treatment group (p < 0.001) [9], and Salami et al. observed a reduction from 7.9 ± 0.42 to 1.8 ± 0.19 in the treatment group, surpassing controls (p = 0.05) [6]. Note that these studies used inverse VAS scales: 0 meant maximum severity in Ciprandi et al., but no symptoms in Salami et al.

Nasal cytology results were partially inconsistent. Varricchio et al. found a significant reduction in neutrophil counts in the treatment group (2.2 ± 1.1 to 1.7 ± 1.1) but no significant change in controls (p < 0.001) [12]. Passali et al. found no statistically significant difference in neutrophil counts, even if the neutrophils’ mean diminished from 8.13 at baseline to 4.78 at the end of study [13].

Additional benefits included reduced school absences and antibiotic use in the treatment group (p < 0.05) [11], as well as improved airway obstruction and ostiomeatal complex blockage (p < 0.05) [12].

While the findings generally favored thermal water treatments, some variations emerged in control group outcomes and statistical significance across studies. These differences likely stem from variations in study designs, patient populations, and baseline conditions. Nevertheless, the consistent improvements in key outcomes support the therapeutic potential of thermal water treatments for respiratory and allergic conditions.

Discussion

This narrative review highlights the therapeutic potential of thermal water inhalation therapies for managing allergic rhinitis (AR) and recurrent respiratory infections (RRIs). Across six eligible studies, significant benefits were observed in key outcomes, including nasal mucociliary transport time (NMTT), total symptom scores (TSS), and the frequency and duration of upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs), as summarized in Fig. 2. These findings underscore the promise of thermal water inhalations as a complementary or alternative treatment for these prevalent conditions, which often pose challenges in conventional management.

The studies reviewed utilized various types of thermal waters, such as Comano, Ischia, Salso-bromo-iodine, and sulfurous waters. These therapies use unique chemical and physical properties to enhance nasal mucociliary clearance, reduce nasal inflammation, and improve immune responses. The significant improvements in clinical outcomes across multiple studies suggest that thermal water inhalations can lessen the burden of AR and RRIs, potentially addressing limitations in traditional pharmacological approaches, including side effects and antibiotic resistance.

The predominance of Italian studies in this review is not unexpected, given Italy’s long-standing tradition in thermal therapy. Rooted in the Roman Empire and supported by the country’s unique hydrogeological features, Italy has developed numerous thermal centers dedicated to preventing and treating respiratory diseases. Moreover, strict regulation by the Ministry of Health ensures the quality and therapeutic efficacy of thermal waters through precise classification systems, each linked to specific medical indications. These factors have fostered strong collaborations between universities and thermal centers, producing a wealth of research on the benefits of thermal therapies for respiratory conditions.

Thermal water inhalations have been shown to offer a range of benefits for respiratory health. Their anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects reduce key inflammatory markers, such as TNF-α and IL-6, while promoting anti-inflammatory cytokines like IL-10 [14–16]. This modulation alleviates chronic airway inflammation and supports immune resilience. Additionally, sulfur-containing waters enhance mucociliary clearance by breaking disulfide bonds in mucus, facilitating the removal of pathogens and reducing bacterial colonization [15, 17]. These therapies also restore oxidative balance, mitigating oxidative stress through increased antioxidant enzyme activity, normalizing mucus production, improving airway hydration, and reducing biofilm formation [12, 18].

In addition to the benefits described above for AR and RRIs, other advantages of thermal water inhalations for upper respiratory tract (URT) conditions have also been reported. These include improved nasal flow and relief from nasal obstruction, both of which are critical in enhancing overall respiratory function [13, 16, 17]. Notably, inhalations with sulfur-sodium-chloride thermal water in cases of chronic inflammation of the upper airways and middle ear (leading to rhinogenic deafness) were found to improve the quality of normoacoustic curves, decrease pathological ones, and consequently reduce auditory loss while enhancing hearing [19]. Moreover, these therapies have shown promise in restoring olfactory function, further contributing to their role in managing URT disorders [20]. On the other hand, it must be considered that some of the beneficial activities of thermal waters are not directly derived from the included studies, but also from external literature.

As the considered studies were conducted in Italy, the geographic concentration (considering population characteristics, climate, and other peculiar aspects) and setting could affect the generalizability of results.

Despite these promising findings, the review also revealed several limitations. First, the heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and treatment protocols and originating from a single nation (Italy) limit the generalization of results. Differences in outcome measures and follow-up durations further complicate direct comparisons between studies. For instance, while improvements in NMTT and TSS were consistent, nasal cytology results showed mixed significance, reflecting the need for standardized assessment methods.

Moreover, there has been a lack of clinical research in the past five years. In addition, the search strategy was restricted to English-language publications from PubMed-indexed journals so that this choice could introduce language or publication bias. Another critical limitation is the lack of robust statistical power in some studies due to small sample sizes.

Additionally, few studies employed randomization or blinding, increasing the risk of bias. Also, the inclusion criteria for interventions (thermal water composition, temperature, delivery method) were broad and different. As a result, this additional heterogeneity may affect the comparability of reported studies. Moreover, the small size of studies may reduce the real value of statistical outcomes.

It is essential to note that these limitations impacted the strength of the evidence and the generalizability of the results. In addition, these methodological shortcomings emphasize the need for future research to adopt standardized protocols and rigorous designs. Conducting larger randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with uniform outcome measures will allow for more reliable comparisons and facilitate meta-analyses to strengthen the evidence base.

Conclusions

Thermal water inhalations have demonstrated considerable potential in reducing the frequency and severity of AR and RRIs while minimizing the reliance on pharmacological interventions. By addressing core pathological mechanisms, including inflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired mucociliary clearance, and offering benefits such as improved nasal flow, reduced obstruction, and restored sensory functions like hearing and smell, these therapies present a compelling case for broader clinical integration. However, future studies should prioritize methodological rigor, larger sample sizes, and consistent follow-up periods to establish their therapeutic role fully. Standardizing outcome parameters, such as NMTT and TSS, will enable more meaningful comparisons and pave the way for integrating these therapies into evidence-based clinical practice. In addition, the overall evidence quality and the limitations that currently preclude broad clinical recommendations, such as small sample sizes, heterogeneity in interventions, and limited geographic representation, may preclude broad clinical recommendations.
