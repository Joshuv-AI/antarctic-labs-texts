Missing persons patterns from Mexico: evidence of a forensic emergency crisis
Authors: Mirsha Quinto-Sanchez, N Sofia Huerta-Pacheco
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe relatives of missing persons in Mexico have denounced the slowness with which a court prosecution file is created by the justice administration system. Theoretically, the search is immediate, but many cases must wait 72 h to build an investigation folder as a legal criterion. This standard has been copied from the UK and Australian police reports without adapting to the Mexican context. The analysis of disappearance reports between 2006 and 2018 shows that this timing criterion in Mexico is not supported. The analyzed database (the National Center for Planning, Analysis, and Information to Combat Crime, CENAPI) showed that in the 72-h range, only 34.53% of the people had been found alive or dead; figure far from 50% to 80% of Europe or Australia. This fact shows that those searching officers or the judicial bureaucracy can act as a factor that limits the search for missing persons. Additionally, there is a random pattern in the geospatial distribution of disappearance, with non-homogeneous frequencies per year. Results highlight the participation of families, the adoption of an evidence-based model, and the generation of geospatial forensic intelligence analysis to generate evidence-based public policies. The social demand of families to the government for not considering them takes relevance in forensic practice in Mexico, and the disappearance data support this assertion.

Introduction

Mexico is immersed in a forensic emergency of missing persons with a total of cases of 294 297 according to officially reported events in September 2023 [1], with 111 516 (37.9%) missing persons and 182 781 (62.1%) located persons from 1962 to the date. Unfortunately, this crisis has not been able to stop by generalizing and deepening its effects. A key point for effective results in the searches and location of missing persons are the criteria established to carry out search actions and legal procedures. The victims’ families have demanded a delay in the search actions, especially for creating a court prosecution file [2–4], some recommendations of human rights organizations have given evidence of this [5, 6]. Court prosecution file conformation is a decisive and determinant legal procedure in searching for missing persons because it allows access to relevant information such as call sheets, Global Position System (GPS) information, or banking operations.

Recently, the National Search Commission (CNB) has published the Homologated Search Protocol (PHB) [7] as a mandate of General Law on Forced Disappearance of Persons, Disappearance Committed by Individuals, and the National Search System (LGDP) [8]. This protocol establishes that the search for persons must be immediate, but only in cases of disappearance of minors, women, journalists, and human rights defenders. However, in most cases, and despite the high numbers of violence, legal actions must begin up to 72 h after the disappearance event [7]. These criteria slow down the search string in its initial stage, called “golden hours” (first 24 h) [9] predicting bad outcomes. In addition, this encourages the bureaucratic apparatus of the national justice system not to choose immediate actions as a work standard, but waiting, at the highest criteria, as the rule of action in forensic cases. Here the research question arises, why has a criterion of 72 h been taken for conformation of a court prosecution file, if the gold standard is the immediate life search?

Although it is not clear, it is inferred that this criterion has been inherited from public policy official reports and research in the UK or Australia [10, 11] was a significant proportion of missing persons return home safely before the first 24 h of the report. The National Policing Improvement Agency of the UK published Missing persons: data and analysis 2009/2010 report that gives evidence for percentages of missing persons from 55% to 80% of all those reported missing [11]. A similar pattern was observed in Australia, where two-thirds or more (>60%) of those reported missing in New South Wales, Queensland, and the Australian Capital Territory (ACT) were located in <48 h [10]. Additionally, another example, for older adults in Croatia, points out that within the 72-h interval there are 74% of the cases reported [12].

A logical prediction is that the socioeconomic contexts between Europe, Australia, and Mexico are so disruptive that taking the 72-h indicator seems risky. However, the LGDP and PHB protocol have copied that criterion and made it a public policy. For this reason, a preliminary investigation is necessary to characterize the pattern of missing persons in Mexico to develop public policy based on evidence in search matters. This research focuses on analyzing the pattern of timing reports of missing people in Mexico from a geospatial-temporal perspective. The null hypothesis is to find the same pattern of disappearance reported in the UK and Australian studies and that the PHB was established as public policy.

Materials and methods

Data sample

In this research, the public records of the National Center for Planning, Analysis, and Information to Combat Crime (CENAPI) of the former Office of the Attorney-General of the Republic, now FGR of the Government of Mexico, were used (Supplementary Table S1, data are available at https://github.com/nshuerta-ForenseUNAM/Missing_person_Mexico). They consisted of 99 129 records of missing persons from 1 January 2006 to 6 May 2018, with the information distributed in 75 variables that consider different aspects (dates, sociodemographic information, description, and others). Only 92.20% (n = 91 392) of the records and a subset of 18 variables were used, relating to the dates of traceability and their associated primary characteristics, such as location status, year of disappearance, sex, age, city, cause of death, and others.

The sample used (n = 91 392) is divided into 40.12% (nLost = 36 663) of persons with non-located status and 59.88% of persons located (nFind = 54 729), which, in turn, is subdivided into living (nAlive = 51 652; 94.38%) and dead (nLifeless = 3 077; 5.62%). The exclusion of 7.80% (nExclusion = 7 737) from the records was mainly because of the presence of inconsistencies identified in registration days (nInconsistency = 7 153), missing values (nNA = 441), as well as belonging to another place outside Mexico, or the absence of the place registration (nLocalization = 143).

Data processing

It is relevant to mention that the information analyzed here represents ~76.82% of the population reported in the same period by the National Commission of Search (CNB) of the Government of Mexico in its National Register of Missing and Unlocated Persons (RNPDNO) in the public version. Besides, the percentage information subdivided for both the analyzed sample and the CNB records is approximate (Supplementary Table S1).

The steps taken for the processing of the contained data in the sample were: (i) approval of dates in a single format (last sighting date, event date, report date, the location date, and date of entry), (ii) extraction and consideration of the reporting year as one that marks the traceability of events, (iii) differences by each period of time (hours elapsed) between each event (sighting–event, event–report, report–location, location–entry, and report–death), (iv) extraction of cases with different origins (California, Georgia, New York, Texas, and unspecified), (v) splitting the data set between located and non-located, (vi) cleaning of missing data (NA) by the event of each localizations’ difference (reporting–location hours), (vii) subdivision of the data set of those located in the living and lifeless, (viii) the inconsistency excluded in the record of days was because there presented differences in the times of the report date and location date in the subsets located alive and lifeless, for example, reporting date 28/02/10 0:00 and localization date 01/01/00 0:00, the localization date being earlier than the reporting date, and (ix) a subset of 91 392 case data and 17 variables was made from the filtered and processed information of the records, and new variables were assigned such as report year, report month, sighting–event hours, event–report hours, report–location hours, location–entry hours, amongst others.

In this process, it was observed that the states with the highest number of inconsistencies in found alive persons were Chihuahua (n = 709; 10.90% with more female 7.94%), Sinaloa (n = 694; 10.67% with more male 6.60%), and Tamaulipas (n = 970; 14.92% with more female 8.24%).

In the same way, it was identified that the records with the greatest inconsistencies in the people located lifeless are in the states of Sinaloa (n = 173; 26.57%) and Nayarit (n = 86; 13.21%) with more proportion of males.

Geospatial analysis

Five analyses were applied to know the geospatial pattern for years of the frequency of missing people (non-located), people found alive, and lifeless. (i) We created national mapping by year of the states’ predominant number of records of non-located, located alive, located lifeless, median of the hours of located alive, and median of the hours of located lifeless. (ii) We applied a multivariate statistical technique called “Profile Analysis for One Sample” that uses Hotelling’s T2 test. The profile analysis is utilized for both inter- and intra-individual interpretations of the variables and allows the quantification of the degree of similarity between them [13]. This analysis allowed us to identify if (i) the proportions of the 13 years (2006–2018) recorded are equal to the hypothesized value (which in this case is one) and (ii) it is checked whether the proportions of the years are equivalent. That is, states, on average, have the same number of people found (lives/lifeless/median hours alive/lifeless) during the analyzed period (all relationships are equal). (iii) Moran’s I is also calculated to know the geospatial behaviour of the data (counts), looking if the spatial distribution of entity values can be the result of random spatial processes. (iv) Grouping states by the number of records of the living and lifeless localizations and the creation of groups of states by median hours of report–location of the same. All of this was done through cluster analysis using Euclidean distances and a classification criterion by the Ward method for the minimum variance of all states for all years. It is essential to mention that the selected groups of each cluster analysis were validated with different indexes through the “NbClust” package of R [14, 15]. (v) Finally, the application of canonical correlations to distinguish the association between the median of reporting-location hours with the number of records of living and lifeless localizations by states and years.

Results

Characterization of the pattern of missing persons: the timing report

We observed a higher proportion of located people are alive (94.38%), where the largest number of people located is female (53.17%). In contrast, the located lifeless (5.62%) where the highest proportion belongs to the male sex (4.86%, Table 1). The most frequent cause of death was unspecified in all time intervals. In men, the coup de grace was the second most frequent cause of death, changing to decomposition (unintentional) in the period of 1 week to 1 month. In women, the pattern begins with coup de grace, decomposition, and asphyxia for 5-day interval.

The localization intervals analysis, which explores the pattern of hours and number of missing persons, reveals that just 16.57% of people were found alive within the first 24 h (Table 1), with different proportions between sexes (female = 9.41% and male = 7.17%) and presenting the highest frequency of location for the interval between 1 month and before 1 year (23.79%) whether they are located alive or lifeless.

Also, results show that the 72-h criterion adds up to only 34.53% of people located, 32.61% alive, and unfortunately, 1.91% deceased, and that up to until the interval from 1 week to 1 month (67.46%) values higher than 65% are obtained of location of people (Table 1).

The temporal and geospatial disappearance pattern

For the general geo-temporal analysis in three statuses: not located (Supplementary Table S2 and Figure 1), located alive (Supplementary Table S3 and Figure 2A), and located lifeless (Supplementary Table S4 and Figure 3A), the highest percentage of reported missing persons (non-located) is predominantly found in 2016 (12.73%) and 2017 (15.92%), with the highest proportion for all years in Tamaulipas (16.35%) and the Estado de Mexico (10.61%). Additionally, those located alive have a greater presence in 2013 (17.57%) and 2014 (17.64%), where Guanajuato (24.16%) and the Estado de Mexico (14.18%) have the maximal proportion from 2006 to 2018. For those located lifeless, the highest percentage occurs in Sinaloa (22.16%) and the Estado de Mexico (13.71%) with the highest number of records in 2013 (15.83%) and 2014 (14.69%).

As a complement, the profile analysis results (Supplementary Tables S7–S10 and Supplementary Figures S1–S4) indicate that the median number of hours and counts of the people located alive or lifeless is different between years and states, because the results indicate that both hypotheses should be rejected for the evaluated data. The first test rejects the null hypothesis that the ratio of the number or median hours of live or lifeless location is equal to 1 for each year. Whilst the second test rejects the null hypothesis that the relationship between the number of live or lifeless located persons or that the median time of the location of living or lifeless is the same for each state (i.e. the difference is 0). Note that not rejecting the first null hypothesis makes the second hypothesis test meaningless. Therefore, it is concluded that the average amount of at least one of these 13 years is different from the rest. Therefore, the patterns of missing persons change in a multiscale manner, which is another evidence for why their continuous characterization is necessary by year, state, or region.

The geospatial pattern of missing and living-located persons resulted in a random geospatial trend as evidenced by the Moran's I index (Figures 1 and 2A and Supplementary Tables S11 and S12). The number of people located alive has a heterogeneous trend in most states, except for Guanajuato and Estado de Mexico, which appear constant between the years. Also, the cluster analysis of located alive and lifeless data for counts and median hours shows the conformation of three and nine clusters for located alive persons (Supplementary Figures S5 and S6), having a group with 93.75% of the states (Figure 2B).

Furthermore, there is a contrast of the heterogeneity of the number of people located alive with the distribution of the median hours of location by state (see map of median hours by year in Supplementary Figure S7), in which nine clusters of states can be observed, the group of the lowest median hours of the location being that of larger size (59.37%, Figure 2B and Supplementary Table S5). In addition, three clusters were distinguished for people located lifeless (Supplementary Figures S6 and S8). Also, relevant findings were identified, such as that the state of Nayarit does not present records, as well the state with the highest number of people found lifeless in Sinaloa. Additionally, Coahuila generally presented a greater number of median hours of location given its low number of people located lifeless (Supplementary Figures S7–S9 and Supplementary Table S6).

The canonical correlations analysis allowed us to see if the number of people located (alive or lifeless) was related to the median hours of location (Figures 2B, Supplementary Figure S5, and S6). It was identified that the people located alive in Mexico City presented a positive association with the median hours of location, which means that the greater the number of people located alive, the higher the median hours of location will be. Moreover, negative correlations were observed in Chihuahua, Guanajuato, and Queretaro, a greater number of people were alive, and a larger number of people were found alive with a low median of hours (Supplementary Figures S10–S12). Concerning the people found lifeless, it was observed that there is a positive association between the number of people and the median hours in San Luis Potosi and Zacatecas, and a negative association in Coahuila (Supplementary Figure S6).

Discussion

Locating missing persons is a complex and multivariate process, where data analysis plays a determining role in planning actions that mitigate its effects. Disappearance patterns are a multifactorial problem that is intrinsic to human communities. However, when they imply a crime risk or violence, they move to a dimension of citizen security, where more research on missing persons from an integrative perspective is needed [16]. The search theory allows seek in the shortest possible time, the optimization of scarce resources to find a missing person [17] and that all search will be considered as an emergency [18]. Contrary to this idea, the bureaucratic apparatus of the justice administration system in Mexico has arbitrarily established a wait of 72 h to create an investigation folder in most of the cases.

As a response to this controversy, initial characterization of the patterns of incidence of missing persons is a key to the generation and implementation of evidence-based public policies, as we have shown here. Mexico and many other Latin American countries lack research into missing person reports since all these factors may affect the structure, process, and outcomes of missing person searches or investigations.

In general, our results show that the 72-h criterion adds up to only 34.53% of people located, 32.61% alive, and unfortunately, 1.91% deceased, with Sinaloa as the state with the worst percentage of lifeless people. It is apparent from the causes of death, violence is present in many cases, so in this context of violence, the immediate search is a priority. Mexico does not reach the patterns from the UK of Australia for the first 24 h. It reaches this until the interval from 1 week to 1 month (67.46%, Table 1). This is a different timing pattern from what was previously reported, that shows the patterns of disappearance intervals are population-specific or context-dependent. The perspective “only a very small minority will be the victim of serious crime” [19] that the UK and Australian institutions have could not be applied in Mexico owing to the percentage of missing people and its violent context dependency.

Missing persons’ relatives and social organizations have denounced this phenomenon since 2013 [5], alluding to the slowness with which the authorities respond to their cases [20, 21]. The families have established that the pattern in the bureaucracy is to delay the conformation of court prosecution file. The foregoing, even though the LGDP and the PHB have already established an immediate search criterion, but not for all cases. Given the violence figures that exist in Mexico, it is assumed that there is a greater probability of disappearance because of the data presented here. Then, it is a priority to reassign the 72-h criterion for the conformation of the court prosecution file. The interval time of this criterion should be shortened to 24 h or less, as in the cases of differentiated approach. Although the prosecutor’s offices are aware of the immediate search criteria, the evidence suggests that the generality of cases in the Mexican context delays the conformation of the court prosecution file. Morewitz [22] have argued that law enforcement personnel can make it difficult to respond to missing person reports. Also, legislation can affect the classification and investigation of missing person cases and, therefore, influence the prevalence of different types of missing persons [22, 23] and searching officials by establishing a non-evidence criterion for persecution attention to a case as we have evidenced here.

Another determining point is the visualization of the problem of missing persons as a holistic and geospatial perspective. Results have shown that disappearance trends change between states (regions), years, and between disappearance categories. States with the highest rates of violence and drug trafficking, such as Sinaloa, Tamaulipas, and Guanajuato, have the highest rates of disappearance reports, this is the basis for the design of state and national public policies, as required by the LGDP. This analytical perspective is new and has not been developed in other data, so it is important to note that it allows for the particularization of general trends in the phenomenon. In addition, it is essential to characterize the regional disappearance phenomena constantly since, with this source of data, local search officers or municipal administrations institutions would make decisions as accurately as possible. Geospatial data, canonical correlations, cluster, and profile analysis show this pattern.

Regional analysis would have to be conducted in the forensic intelligence units of the local search committees. Reports provide evidence of the missing persons’ patterns from urban to rural regions change in their frequency of reporting disappearances, for example, New South Wales, Queensland, and the ACT in Australia [10]. This should be a criterion of utmost importance in the design of public policies regarding the complexity of the search for missing people in Mexico.

Here we highlight the usefulness of the evidence-based analysis for the generation of public policies. Specifically, the evidence-based model with a perspective and philosophy of imparting justice that involves the use of research, evaluation, analysis, and scientific processes in law enforcement decision-making [24]. For this reason, high-quality information is the crucial point in shaping good evidence-based public policies. Professionals like search officials, police, public ministries, and relatives of missing persons need accurate and robust information, generated with the combination of statistics, research, evaluation, and analytics [25]. Thus, this could mitigate the forensic emergency that Mexico is experiencing, which also implies a significant estimated economic loss [26]. The annual budget for Local Search Commissions operations for 2021 amounts to 581 941 510.00 pesos [27] and is considered insufficient, with criminal index and the number of reported disappearances continue their upward trend.

Evidence-based public policies attempt to move away from actions based on judgement or expert intuitions [25, 28, 29], ensuring that an instruction or action has been performed in the best possible way given the scientific evidence about it. This contributes to general knowledge of legal and operational search criteria of missing, lost, and not located people.

The evidence-based model takes up these lawsuits or claims, and, as the results have shown, the 72-h criterion for the prosecution file is just a copy from other countries, without any support or adaptation within the Mexican context. Then, the integration of the missing persons’ relatives in the construction of public policies is determinant, not just with taking into consideration the voice of citizens or families, but rather as active elements of the decision-making model. This is a rector point in the Minnesota protocol [30], as well as others protocols [7, 8, 31]. The participation of families in decision-making for the forensic field is part of their rights and not just a condition. Therefore, there is a juxtaposition between evidence-based models, decision-making models, citizen participation, and the rights of families of the disappeared. According to the saying “Sin las familias, no.” (“Without the families, no.”), it surged from the social demand of families to the government for not considering them. This demand of the missing person’s relatives is vindicated by the CENAPI disappearance database and the analysis carried out here. Finally, the release of the actualized CNB database is a pending demand, as families have argued, limits scrutiny and improvement in search results as well as the evaluation of the most recent period’s trend.

Integrating all the results exposed here, we present large evidence for the arbitrarily establishment of the temporal (or timing) criterion for missing persons and conformation of a court prosecution file in Mexico. We recommend creating a court prosecution file immediately, and, in a prevailing way, in <24 h. Additionally, assessment of regional patterns will be of importance to search teams.

The geospatial distribution of missing persons is a random pattern, with non-homogeneous frequencies per year. The number of people located alive has a heterogeneous trend in most states. Also, it was identified that the people located alive in Mexico City presented a positive association with the median hours of location, which means that the greater the number of people located alive. The worst rate of lifeless persons appears for the state of Sinaloa.

Finally, we highlight the participation of families, the adoption of an evidence-based model, and the generation of geospatial forensic intelligence analysis to generate evidence-based public policies. The social demand of families to the government for not considering them takes relevance in forensic practice in Mexico, and the disappearance data support this assertion.
