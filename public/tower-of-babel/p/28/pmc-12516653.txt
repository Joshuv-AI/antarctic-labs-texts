Comorbidities in Mild WAS/XLT Require Lifelong Follow‐Up and Consideration of Definitive Treatment
Authors: Coralie Mallebranche, Charline Miot, Mickael Alligon, Despina Moshous, Benedicte Neven, Vincent Barlogis, Virginie Courteille, Bénédicte Bruno, Marie Ouachée‐Chardin, Dalila Adjaoud, Yves Delneste, Olivier Bouaziz, Capucine Picard, Alain Fischer, Nizar Mahlaoui, Isabelle Pellier, Members of the CEREDIH French PID Study Group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLoss of function mutations in the gene encoding WASP (Wiskott‐Aldrich syndrome protein) result in Wiskott‐Aldrich syndrome (WAS) and X‐linked thrombocytopenia‐XLT (WAS/XLT). The clinical severity of the disease can be assessed using the WAS clinical severity score. Typically, patients with a score of 3 or less at 2 years of age are considered to have mild WAS/XLT disease. However, patients with a low score in the first 2 years of life can still experience life‐threatening complications, and there are no agreed guidelines for the management of these patients with mild WAS/XLT. We analyzed data on WAS/XLT patients from the French National Reference Centre for Primary Immunodeficiencies registry. At 10 December 2021, data were available for 261 patients, 170 of whom had mild WAS/XLT. The median age of these patients at last follow‐up was 15.8 years (range 2.0–60.4). Overall survival at 40 years was 73% in mild WAS patients versus 65% in severe WAS patients (p = 0.43). In the mild WAS population, prior to Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation or gene therapy, 38.2% of patients progressed to a WAS severity score of 4–5 after the age of 2. Remarkably, no deaths were reported in 45 HSCTs performed since 2010, regardless of initial severity, type and HLA compatibility of the transplant, and age at diagnosis, with a median follow‐up of 3.2 years (0–11.5) after transplant. As complications can occur throughout life, our study supports definitive treatment for all patients when available, including those with mild forms.Keywords: hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, primary immunodeficiency, Wiskott‐Aldrich syndrome (WAS), X‐linked thrombocytopenia

Introduction

Loss of function Wiskott‐Aldrich syndrome protein (WASP) mutations result in a variety of clinical manifestations ranging from X‐linked thrombocytopenia (XLT) to the classic Wiskott‐Aldrich syndrome (WAS) phenotype, which can include infections, atopy, autoimmunity, inflammation, bleeding, aortic aneurysms, non‐malignant lymphoproliferations, and malignancies [1, 2, 3, 4]. The estimated prevalence of WAS is 1.2% in patients with identified primary immunodeficiencies [3], with an incidence of 1–9 per million live male births [1].

The clinical phenotype depends on the type and location of the mutation [5, 6], but a single mutation can cause a wide variety of symptoms. For an identical mutation, it is currently impossible to predict which patient will develop a severe complication and when. Severity can be reported using the clinical WAS severity score, based on publications by Ochs's team [7, 8]. Classically, patients who achieve a WAS severity score of 4 or 5 before the age of 2 years are considered to have severe disease. For these patients, allogeneic Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation (HSCT) is an excellent curative treatment with survival rates > 95% [9], including patients with very severe refractory thrombocytopenia for whom haploidentical HSCT has shown good results [10]. However, there is no clear guidance on the indications for definitive therapies in patients with a WAS severity score between 1 and 3. Previous studies have reported that these patients have an excellent long‐term survival rate, but a high probability of serious disease‐related complications, such as infections, malignancies, and autoimmune manifestations [11]. In these patients, the question of definitive therapy before the onset of complications is an important issue. HSCT is a potentially life‐threatening procedure but can prevent the occurrence of serious complications. In this study, we retrospectively evaluated the clinical phenotypes and evolution of patients with a severity score of 3 or less at 2 years of age, as well as the specific events that occurred during their lives, to determine the risk of complications and the benefit of offering preemptive HSCT treatment.

Patients and Methods

Study Population

Since 2005, the French National Reference Centre for Primary Immune Deficiencies (CEREDIH) runs the French National Registry for Pediatric and Adult Patients with Primary Immune Deficiencies (PID) [12]. Demographic, clinical, biological, genetic, and therapeutic information is collected retrospectively and prospectively by dedicated clinical researchers at the Necker‐Enfants Malades Hospital, the coordinating center. CEREDIH uses the secure online database registry platform of the European Society of Immunodeficiencies (ESID) for data collection. Informed consent was obtained for patient enrollment in accordance with French regulatory requirements and the Declaration of Helsinki.

We carried out a retrospective, nationwide analysis of the medical records of XLT/WAS patients. Data extraction from the CEREDIH database was performed on December 10, 2021 with a last update on August 4, 2022. Patients younger than 2 years of age at the time of the last follow‐up were excluded, except for patients who died of the disease before 2 years of age, who were included in the severe WAS group. Patients with no follow‐up data available after inclusion in the database were excluded.

Definitions and Endpoints

WAS gene sequence analysis was performed in patients as part of standard care after written informed consent was obtained [2, 3]. Mutations were reported according to the current nomenclature of the Human Genome Variation Society [4]. Variants reported in ClinVar were scored according to the ClinVar classification. Other variants were scored according to ACMG criteria using Franklin by Genoox [4, 5]. Genetic data were not available for some patients (especially older cases), but these patients had a compatible clinical presentation with decreased or absent WASp expression by Western blot or flow cytometry.

The severity of symptoms associated with WAS was assessed at 2 years of age using a WAS severity score from 1 to 5 [6, 7]. The score was calculated by the investigator based on clinical events reported in the registry to ensure consistency of scoring. Patients with a score ≤ 3 at 2 years of age were considered to have “mild WAS;” patients with a score ≥ 4 at 2 years of age were considered to have “severe WAS.” The following comorbidity parameters were collected: infection, bleeding, malignancy, nephropathy, and autoimmunity/inflammation. Infections considered severe were deep‐seated bacterial infections, meningitis, sepsis, opportunistic infections (viral and fungal), and all other life‐threatening infections. Bleeding was considered severe with a Buchanan score of 4 or 5 [8], and were only included in the severity score if it occurred before the age of 2 years (early‐onset severe WAS). For vasculitis, we considered severe episodes involving organs such as the brain, gastrointestinal tract, kidneys, and lungs and/or joints or severe skin involvement documented by either imaging and/or pathology. We did not consider splenectomy as a severe event if no severe etiology was reported to explain the splenectomy. For event occurrence analyses, we considered only the first event in each group (i.e., autoimmunity, vasculitis, lymphoma, severe infection, or glomerulopathy) for each patient. The number of events per patient could not be determined in our cohort based on the information available in the registry. We also collected data on HSCT or gene therapy (GT) and the complications associated with these treatments.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 3.6.1 (R Core Team (2018), “survival” package). Categorical and continuous variables were compared using the Fisher exact test and the Mann–Whitney test, respectively. The baseline for survival analyses was birth or age at HSCT.

We defined overall survival (OS) as the time from baseline to death from any cause, and event‐free survival (EFS) as the time from baseline to the first occurrence of the observed event. For post‐HSCT EFS, the following events were considered: acute Graft Versus Host Disease (GVHD) grade 3–4, chronic GVHD, autoimmunity, lymphoproliferation, conditioned second HSCT, unconditioned stem cell boost, donor lymphocyte infusion (DLI), splenectomy, or death. Alive patients were censored on December 10, 2021.

EFS estimations accommodate the competing risks of HSCT, gene therapy, and death. We emphasize that accounting for competing risks is critically important for this study because deceased patients can no longer be considered at risk for experiencing events [9]. Without incorporating competing risks into the analysis, the risk of developing the event of interest would be overestimated. OS and EFS curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier estimator, and comparisons between groups were performed using the log‐rank test. The risk of death was calculated using truncated data, with the truncation variable being the maximum between the age at diagnosis of WAS/XLT and the age of 2 years. Since the diagnosis of a PID is the condition for a patient to be included in the CEREDIH registry, and being at least 2 years old is one of the conditions for being diagnosed with mild WAS, we emphasize that accounting for truncation for death is crucial and that not considering it would lead to an underestimation of the risk of death [9].

Results

Patients' Characteristics

On December 10, 2021, 282 patients out of the 8229 enrolled in the CEREDIH registry were carriers of a loss‐of‐function mutation in the WAS gene (Figure 1), including eight female patients with biallelic mutations. These patients were followed at 29 different centers throughout the country. Twenty‐one patients were excluded due to lack of follow‐up data after inclusion or age < 2 years at last follow‐up. One hundred and seventy patients had a severity score ≤ 3 at the age of 2 years, allowing classification of these patients into the mild WAS group, with a median age at last report of 15.8 years (range: 2.0–60.4 years) (Table 1).

Survival

OS in the overall WAS cohort was 85.0% at 15 years (95% CI, 80.82%–90.62%), 75.0% at 30 years (95% CI, 67.82%–84.35%), 71.9% at 45 years (95% CI, 63.29%–83.15%), and 71.9% at 60 years (95% CI, 63.29%–83.15%) (Figure S2). OS in the mild WAS population was 93% at 10 years, 84% at 20 years, 73% at 30 years, and 73% at 40 years (Figure 2). OS in the severe WAS population was 88% at 10 years, 78% at 20 years, 73% at 30 years, and 65% at 40 years. In patients with mild WAS, OS was significantly better in patients with a missense mutation than in patients with a stop mutation (p = 0.003) or a splice mutation (p = 0.005). There was no difference in outcome between patients carrying either splice or stop mutations (Figure S3). Twenty‐one out of 170 patients in the mild WAS group died during follow‐up. Four patients died of severe infections, all of whom had undergone splenectomy prior to the fatal infection. Four patients died of hematologic malignancies (lymphoma), two of hemorrhagic syndromes (including 1 in a traffic accident, without 4 or 5 prior events), and one of cerebral vasculitis. Six patients died of post‐transplant complications. In four patients, the cause of death was not reported.

Incidence of Severe Events

Prior to HSCT or GT, 69 patients (38.2%) in the mild WAS population progressed to a score of 4 or 5 after the age of 2 years. The risk of progressing to a WAS severity score of 4–5 was 23% at 10 years and 61% at 40 years, with a median age at risk of occurrence of 24.7 years (Figure 3). Twenty‐six out of 170 mild WAS patients (15.3%) developed autoimmunity/inflammation such as autoimmune cytopenia, arthritis, psoriasis, or systemic lupus erythematosus. The risk of developing autoimmunity/inflammation was 9% at 10 years, 16% at 20 years, and 22% at 40 years (Table S1). Eighteen of 170 patients with mild WAS (10.6%) developed vasculitis. Vasculitic disease occurred preferentially in the first 20 years of life, with a risk of 5% at 10 years, 12% at 20 years, and 15% at 30 and 40 years. Six of the 170 patients with mild WAS (4.1%) developed IgA nephropathy, all before the age of 20. The risk of nephropathy was 2% at 10 years and increased to 6% after the age of 20. Severe infections and lymphomas occurred throughout life. Severe infections affected 8.8% of the study population, with a risk reaching 16% at the age of 40 years. 10.6% of the population developed lymphoma, with a risk of 12% at age 40. This risk increased to 34% at the age of 55 years, but this could be a specificity of the cohort due to the small risk set at that time. The risk of progression to a severity score of 4–5 also differed according to the type of mutation, with an increased risk in patients with a nonsense (p = 2.10−8) or a splice (p = 0.0006) mutation compared to patients with a missense mutation (Figure 4). Finally, looking at patients alive at 5 years of age, we observed that survival was not better in patients with a score of 4 or 5 complication occurring between ages 2 and 5 years age than in severe patients with complications before age 2 years age (Figure S4).

Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation

A total of 114 patients received definitive therapy (109 HSCT and 5 GT). Thirty‐one of the 170 patients in the mild group underwent HSCT, including 19 performed after the occurrence of a grade 4–5 complication (median age at transplantation: 11.1 years). The remaining 12 patients received preemptive HSCT before the occurrence of a severe event, at a median age of 6.2 years.

For the overall population, OS after HSCT was 83% at 5 years. There was no significant difference in survival between patients transplanted for severe WAS (OS 86% at 5 years) and patients transplanted for mild WAS (OS 75% at 5 years) (Figure S5). Twenty‐one patients died after HSCT, among them eight of the 31 patients transplanted for mild WAS (4 post‐transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD), 1 sepsis, 1 severe GVHD, 1 lymphoma relapse, MD: 1).

Remarkably, no deaths were reported for the 45 HSCT performed since 2010, regardless of initial severity (17 mild WAS and 28 severe WAS), type of transplant, and age at diagnosis, with a median post‐transplant follow‐up of 3.2 years (0–11.5). There was no significant difference in the distribution of donor types before and after 2010, with respectively: matched sibling donor (MSD): 23% versus 25%; matched unrelated donor: 22% versus 36%; mismatched unrelated donor: 15% versus 11%; haploidentical donor: 40% versus 27% (p = 0.34). After 2010, all patients received a conditioning regimen with Busulfan in combination with Fludarabine. Three also received Thiotepa. Nine of 12 patients who received haploidentical transplants had an in vivo T‐cell depletion with post transplantation cyclophosphamide. Twenty‐four received serotherapy, mainly in non‐MSD transplants.

Among patients allografted after 2010, the 3‐year EFS after HSCT was 76% [CI95: 0.64; 0.90] (Figure 5). Post‐transplant complications were: (i) acute GVHD, mainly affecting the skin; (ii) viral replications, occurring within 1 month after transplantation; (iii) chronic GVHD; and (iv) autoimmune complications, occurring later with several events beyond the first year after transplantation. Acute GVHD mainly resolved rapidly spontaneously or with conventional treatment. Two patients with acute GVHD progressed to chronic GVHD. Two patients with severe WAS experienced severe neurological disorders (lymphocytic meningoencephalitis and infantile spasm syndrome); one patient had a progressive recovery after treatment, while the other patient retained sequelae related to the pre‐existing condition. Remarkably, after 2010, there was no difference in the incidence of post‐transplant complications between patients who received transplantation before or after 5 years of age (Figure S6).

Discussion

As reported in previous studies [10, 11, 12], patients with mild forms of WAS/XLT have a better OS in early childhood than those classified in the severe group. However, this study shows that patients with a mild WAS/XLT phenotype still have a high risk of mortality and morbidity throughout their lives. In particular, 68 out of 170 patients initially classified as mild eventually developed a severe complication after the age of 2 years, arguing for lifelong monitoring of these patients. Autoimmunity, severe infections, hemorrhagic events and/or malignant complications may occur in the course of mild WAS/XLT, and some patients diagnosed with a mild form of WAS/XLT may score 4 or 5 during their lifetime [13]. As the French registry is a retrospective and prospective database with a good level of data quality, regular updates and long‐term follow‐up [14], we are able to describe the true course of events. The high quality and update of the database may also explain the higher number of incidences of vasculitis, arthritis, autoimmunity and tumor events that we reported compared to previous studies of mild WAS/XLT patients. Indeed, we identified a higher percentage of vasculitis and arthritis in our cohort (10.7%) compared to the historical cohort of Albert et al. (3.5%). Glomerulonephritis is a rare complication in severe WAS (3%) [15], which is also seen in mild WAS (5.2%) [13]. In our study, we reported a similar incidence of this condition. The prevalence of malignancy in our mild WAS/XLT cohort was higher (8.8%) than in a previous study (5.2%) [13], although we did not include post‐transplant EBV lymphoproliferation in the group of cancers, unlike previously published studies.

Strikingly, most of the patients in our study who reached a score of 4 or 5 did so before the age of 25, with the notable exception of lymphomas, which were reported throughout life. Albert et al. have also described the occurrence of various adverse events at any time of life, although the majority of them occurred in the first three decades. In addition, our Figure S4 shows that survival is not better in patients who reach a severity score of 4–5 between 2 and 5 years than in those with complications before 2 years of age. These results support early definitive treatment in WAS, but should be interpreted with caution because the number of mild patients in this analysis is small and only patients still alive at 5 years of age were included (severe patients who had an event before 2 years of age and are still alive at 5 years are more distant from the event than patients who had an event between 2 and 5 years of age, who are then at greater risk of death). However, the data strongly suggest that severe events occurring after 2 years are no less severe than those occurring before 2 years. Severity and vital risk are delayed but not absent in mild WAS/XLT patients [16]. Furthermore, the design of our study, in which only the first event of each type of complication is considered, underestimates the total number of complications presented by each patient, and thus the burden of the pathology. While it is now clear that the type of mutation [16] and the absence of WAS protein expression [6] are strong criteria for predicting “severe” WAS, it is much more difficult to predict risk factors for developing complications among mild patients. In our study, we described that patients with a missense mutation have better overall survival and a lower risk of progression to a score ≥ 4 than patients with a nonsense or splice mutation. These results are consistent with those of Albert et al. [16], where the survival was higher and the incidence of serious events was lower in patients with a class I mutation (missense in exon 1 + 2 or c.559 + 5G>A). A 105 of the 123 patients with missense mutation in our study have a mutation in exon 1 or 2, falling into the category of class I mutations. These mutations are associated with residual expression of WASp, which has previously been described as clinical forms with a better prognosis [17]. Genetic classification is currently the only validated predictive factor, but it is not sufficient to predict the natural history of the disease. Basic research to better understand the pathophysiology of complications, especially autoimmune and autoinflammatory complications, may allow us to anticipate their onset. As a first conclusion, patients with mild WAS/XLT have a high incidence of vasculitis, arthritis, autoimmunity, and cancer throughout their lives, especially during the first three decades. The frequency of complications is higher in our study than in historical cohorts, although it may still be underestimated. The mild/severe dichotomy and associated terminology seem outdated in light of these findings, and it would be more interesting to consider WAS patients as a continuum of possible phenotypes that we need to continue to understand and identify as accurately as possible.

The long‐term complications of mild WAS/XLT may argue for definitive curative treatment as HSCT or GT [18]. HSCT has been the only curative option for many decades, with excellent results reported with different types of transplants (matched sibling donors, matched unrelated donors, and, more recently, umbilical cord blood and haploidentical transplants). Transplantation outcome in our study was excellent, with platelet recovery in all patients allografted after 2010 and excellent engraftment.

Of the 45 patients, two patients experienced graft rejection during the first month (one haploidentical graft and one unrelated blood cord 5 of 6) (4%); the proportion of graft rejections was the same in the study by Burroughs et al. [11] (7 rejections/129; 5%). Engraftment was excellent in our study, with chimerism > 90% at D90 for all patients. However, the analyses performed did not distinguish between chimerism in different populations. Considering the CD3+ T cell population, our results are similar to those of the Burroughs study. Although we do not have precise data on myeloid chimerism, platelet counts are very satisfactory, suggesting adequate myeloid reconstitution. All patients received myeloablative conditioning with busulfan, which is associated with better myeloid engraftment in the literature [11]. After HSCT, the overall survival of patients with mild WAS/XLT is similar to that of patients with severe forms. Most importantly, the overall survival of children transplanted after 2010 is 100%. This excellent outcome of post‐2010 HSCT, also found in the publication of Elfeky et al. [19], suggests that this therapy may be a safe curative treatment for children with mild WAS/XLT. Albert et al. [10] reported that OS and cGVHD‐free survival were not significantly affected by conditioning regimen, donor type, or timing of HSCT but were better in younger patients (threshold age of 5 years) at the time of HSCT. In our cohort, EFS of patients transplanted before or after the age of 5 years showed no difference, subject to the small number of patients older than 5 years. The complications of post‐2010 transplants were acute and chronic GVHD, autoimmunity, and EBV lymphoproliferation as expected, and in most cases were reasonably well controlled with current therapies. Acute and chronic GVHD were reported in respectively 31% and 11% of patients transplanted after 2010, respectively, which is similar to other recent cohorts [10, 11, 19]. Improved transplantation procedures, supportive care, and earlier pre‐emptive transplantation (before the onset of complications due to the disease) probably partly explain this improved survival after transplantation. The follow‐up period for patients transplanted after 2010 is relatively short (3.2 years), but this seems sufficient to assess the majority of deaths attributable to allogeneic transplantation, as in the Elfeky study in which all deaths occurred within the first 2 years post‐transplant (median follow‐up 63 months) [19]. Interestingly, we also noted that, of the cohort of patients transplanted before 2010, four patients had died of severe infection very late after transplantation (7, 12, 23, 30 years). These four patients had undergone splenectomy before transplantation, which is more consistent with death secondary to splenectomy than secondary to transplantation. This observation is in line with Albert's data on the risks of splenectomy and the benefits of avoiding this approach in patients with WAS/XLT [13].

These results suggest that transplantation can be confidently discussed in all patients, including those with mild WAS/XLT, particularly with the aim of improving these patients' quality of life. However, the majority of patients in this cohort received myeloablative conditioning, with reduced toxicity in recent years. While overall survival and the rate of disease correction are excellent, long‐term follow‐up and the risk of complications such as malignancy and infertility are still lacking and need some caution. Gene therapy has reshaped the therapeutic landscape for WAS, offering a personalized, non‐allogeneic option that circumvents the risks of graft‐versus‐host disease (GVHD), graft rejection, and the need for a compatible donor. Recent lentiviral vector‐based trials have demonstrated consistent immune correction and reduced bleeding, with a favorable safety profile [20, 21, 22]. Nonetheless, GT requires conditioning and presents challenges such as variable platelet recovery and potential post‐treatment autoimmunity. The choice between HSCT and GT should thus be individualized based on disease severity, donor availability, and long‐term safety considerations. In conclusion, our findings support HSCT as a safe and effective option for WAS across all severities. However, as GT becomes more broadly available, with regulatory approval probably imminent in Europe, it could provide an ideal therapeutic alternative, particularly suited for patients with milder WAS phenotypes.

Crucial research goals in the coming years will be: (1) improve the identification of mild patients at risk for severe complications; (2) improve the efficacy and/or long‐term safety of definitive treatments such as gene therapy and stem cell transplantation. Nevertheless, we suggest that all patients with mild WAS/XLT should be carefully followed throughout their lives and considered for definitive treatment, even in the absence of a compatible sibling donor, in order to improve outcome and quality of life [12].
