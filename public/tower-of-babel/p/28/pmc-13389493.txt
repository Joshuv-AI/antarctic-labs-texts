Topology-dependent node dynamics under mechanical manipulation in moiré ferroelectrics
Authors: Sang Hwa Park, Nicolas Leconte, Huseung Lee, Jeonglyul Kim, Byunghyun Kim, Ayoung Yuk, Daesung Park, Jiyun Chang, Feng Ping Li, Kenji Watanabe, Takashi Taniguchi, Hyeonsik Cheong, Hyobin Yoo, Jeil Jung, Sang Mo Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVan der Waals moiré superlattices can host interfacial ferroelectric domains, whose arrangement is influenced by the topology of nodes. Here, we investigate the dynamic behavior and topological protection of node types in twisted WSe2 homobilayers under local mechanical perturbations. Dark-field transmission electron microscopy and Kelvin probe force microscopy reveal symmetric sixfold nodes as well as non-ideal nodes, including fourfold and eightfold types. Mechanical loading with an atomic force microscopy tip shows striking contrasts in node motion: symmetric sixfold nodes are dynamically robust, whereas an asymmetric sixfold node can be displaced and guided by the tip scan direction, as confirmed by quasi-static loading simulations. Furthermore, we observe a fourfold node that fractured due to its lack of topological protection. These findings establish a direct link between node topology and mechanical response, providing fundamental insights into moiré ferroelectricity and advancing our understanding of topological defects in two-dimensional van der Waals materials.Subject terms: Ferroelectrics and multiferroics, Two-dimensional materials, Two-dimensional materials

Introduction

Two-dimensional (2D) van der Waals (vdW) materials have garnered tremendous attention due to their extraordinary electronic, optical, and mechanical properties1–4. In particular, moiré superlattices formed by twisting two vdW materials at small angles θ have emerged as a rich platform for exploring correlated quantum phases such as unconventional superconductivity5–7 and charge density waves8–10. Recently, a novel type of ferroelectricity, termed sliding ferroelectricity11–13, has been experimentally discovered at the interfaces of hexagonal boron nitride (hBN)13–15 and transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs)16–18. This interfacial out-of-plane polarisation arises from inter-layer displacement-induced inversion symmetry breaking and can be switched through in-plane sliding by one inter-atomic distance19–21. Unlike Janus TMDs, where the out-of-plane dipole is structurally fixed and polarisation switching is not trivial, sliding ferroelectrics are not intrinsically constrained by crystal asymmetry and therefore enable reversible polarisation switching, offering a distinct functional advantage.

At small θ values, moiré superlattices undergo atomic reconstruction, resulting in the formation of commensurate triangular domains separated by incommensurate domain walls (DWs)22. This reconstruction originates from the competition between vdW interaction energy and interfacial elastic energy. As a result, in rhombohedrally stacked TMD bilayers (MX2, where M and X denote the metal and chalcogen atoms, respectively), the energetically favorable MX and XM stacking regions expand, whereas the unfavorable MM (or XX) stacking regions shrink23. In the ideal configuration, six equilateral MX and XM domains surround a central MM node at which three DWs intersect, a structure we refer to as a sixfold node24,25 (Fig. 1a).

These nodes and DWs can be understood as topological defects, which are localized irregularities in the order parameter field of physical systems25. Such defects arise when the continuous symmetry of the order parameter is spontaneously broken. Furthermore, they are topologically protected and thus cannot be eliminated by smooth, local deformations. However, topological protection does not preclude their dynamic responses to external stimuli. Recent studies have revealed contrasting behaviors of nodes and DWs. For example, operando transmission electron microscopy (TEM) under global gating showed continuous motion of DWs driven by the repeated expansion and contraction of MX (or XM) domains, whereas the central sixfold node exhibited negligible motion26. Moreover, a piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM) study on moiré superlattices formed on a curved substrate reported that the nodes can be displaced by sliding-induced disturbance27. These results demonstrate that, despite their topological protection, nodes can exhibit diverse responses depending on sample conditions, underscoring the need for systematic investigation.

Notably, in real samples fabricated by the tear-and-stack method, moiré superlattices often deviate from the ideal configuration. Various structural imperfections, such as cracks, folds, and bubbles, along with complex strain distributions, give rise to intricate DW networks and a variety of non-ideal nodes28–30. However, systematic investigations of these anomalous nodes remain lacking. Furthermore, topological defects govern a wide range of physical behaviors in solids, including dislocations that control ductility in crystalline metals31, vortices that determine the magnetic response of superconductors32, and DWs that mediate switching dynamics in ferroelectrics33,34. In moiré superlattices, the emergent functionalities (e.g., ferroelectric and moiré domain antiferroelectric) are governed by the node density, which in turn depends on the θ value26. While the distribution and density of nodes provide a static picture of moiré superlattices, their functional properties are ultimately governed by how these nodes respond to external perturbations. Especially, clarifying whether a node is topologically protected is crucial, since protection ensures robustness against perturbations while limiting tunability. Therefore, comprehensive understanding of both the dynamic responses of nodes and the extent of their topological protection is highly required.

In this study, we investigate the dynamic behavior and topological protection of various node types in twisted WSe2 (tWSe2) moiré superlattices under local mechanical perturbations. Dark field (DF) TEM reveals non-ideal nodes, including fourfold and eightfold nodes, which are classified by the number of surrounding MX/XM domains. Local perturbations applied using an atomic force microscopy (AFM) tip show contrasting behaviors: while typical symmetric sixfold nodes are dynamically robust, a highly asymmetric sixfold node is mobile and its motion can be manipulated by the AFM tip scan direction. This result is further supported by quasi-static loading simulations. In addition, one fourfold node undergoes fracture, reflecting its lack of topological protection. This work establishes a direct connection between node topology and mechanical response, broadens insight into moiré ferroelectricity, and enhances our understanding of topological defects in 2D vdW materials.

Results

DF-TEM analysis of node topology

To investigate the node topology with high spatial resolution, we performed DF-TEM measurements on a tWSe2 homobilayer sample. Figure 1b shows a DF-TEM image taken using the g=101¯0 Bragg peak of the sample. The white and black contrasts correspond to the MX (upward polarisation) and XM (downward) domains, respectively22,35. We note that DWs in tWSe2 can be regarded as partial dislocations from a crystallographic viewpoint25. The fundamental property of a dislocation is specified by its Burgers vector, which therefore serves as a key quantity to identify. The Burgers vector uniquely determines the nature of the dislocation, including its type, energetics, and role in lattice deformation. In this way, it provides a direct link between the DW structure and its topological character, offering an essential clue for evaluating whether a given node configuration is topologically protected or unprotected. Experimentally, the Burgers vector of a given dislocation can be determined by analysing and comparing three DF-TEM images obtained using three symmetry-equivalent second-order (g=112¯0) reflections (marked with the red, blue, and green dashed circles in the inset of Fig. 1b)24,26.

Our DF-TEM measurements identified diverse node configurations, ranging from typical sixfold nodes to non-ideal fourfold and eightfold nodes. Figure 1c, e, and g show enlarged DF-TEM images of the regions indicated by open squares in Fig. 1b. To clearly visualise DWs with different Burgers vectors, we overlaid three DF-TEM images acquired from three symmetry-equivalent second-order reflections (g=112¯0), assigning red, blue, and green colors scales to each image (Fig. 1d, f, and h). This composite colour mapping provides distinct contrast depending on the Burgers vector, thereby enabling straightforward identification of the DWs24,25. As shown in Fig. 1c, d, three DWs with different Burgers vectors intersect at typical symmetric sixfold nodes, which are regularly surrounded by six triangular domains. According to Ko et al. 26, the symmetric sixfold nodes in the moiré domain antiferroelectric state remain robust against external stimuli due to the topological nature of the dislocation network25. Unlike the symmetric sixfold case, we observed various non-ideal nodes near cracks or sample edges where the moiré superlattice was distorted. As marked by the orange arrow in Fig. 1g, we identified a fourfold node, i.e., the node enclosed by four MX/XM domains. We found that four DWs converge at this node rather than only two DWs intersecting; in Fig. 1h, two green DWs meet the node together with one red and one blue DW. Remarkably, an eightfold node was observed, as indicated by the yellow arrow in Fig. 1e. This exotic node corresponds to three red, three green, and two blue DWs converging at the node, yielding a total of eight. In addition to the non-ideal node configurations, DF-TEM images also show various intricacies in DW structures (see also Supplementary Fig. S1). As indicated by the pink arrow in Fig. 1e, the Burgers vector changes in the middle of a DW, i.e., from red to green (Fig. 1f). Highly asymmetric sixfold nodes are also frequently observed. For example, as marked by the green arrow, two DWs (green and blue) are located in close proximity, resulting in a thin, curved white (MX) elongated domain between them.

We speculate that the dynamic behavior of non-ideal nodes under local mechanical perturbations may differ from that of typical symmetric sixfold nodes, as motivated by these qualitative observations in DF-TEM images. From a mechanical perspective, dislocations can be modeled as elastic objects with an effective line tension, which resists deformation and provides a restoring force26,36,37. Similarly, a ferroelectric DW stores elastic energy when perturbed and tends to recover its equilibrium configuration once the perturbation is removed33,37,38. Therefore, depending on the number and arrangement of DWs at a node, the restoring force can differ, leading to distinct dynamic behaviors. In the following, we test this hypothesis by directly probing the responses of different node configurations under controlled AFM loading.

Robust versus movable sixfold nodes

We first focused on a typical symmetric sixfold node. Figure 2 shows triangular ferroelectric domains (blue and light blue) of varying sizes and their DW network, as measured by KPFM (see Methods). We selected a specific sixfold node, located at the center of the region outlined by an orange dashed hexagon in Fig. 2a, for detailed study. To apply a purely local mechanical force to the targeted node (yellow dashed region), we performed square-area contact scans using an electrically grounded AFM tip under a loading force of 60 nN, hereinafter referred to as the ‘mechanical scan’ (Supplementary Fig. S2). The inset of Fig. 2a illustrates the fast and slow scan directions used in this scan (detailed schematics are provided in Supplementary Fig. S3). Figure 2b shows the KPFM image after the mechanical scan. Despite the applied mechanical perturbations, the node remained stationary. Even when the loading force was increased to 120 nN and the slow scan direction was altered (i.e., from down to up) in a subsequent scan (inset of Fig. 2b), the node position remained unchanged, as shown in Fig. 2c. These observations indicate that the symmetric sixfold node is dynamically robust against mechanical perturbations. In combination with the DF-TEM results, this situation can be interpreted as a balance of the restoring forces of the three intersecting DWs, which drives the system back to its original position after external mechanical perturbations. We note that the topography images taken before and after the scans showed no detectable changes (Supplementary Fig. S4).

However, we found that another type of node can be manipulated solely by mechanical scanning. We selected a highly asymmetric node located at the center of the region outlined by a larger red dashed polygon in Fig. 2d, close to the previously studied sixfold node. At first glance, it appears to be a fourfold node, i.e., surrounded by four triangular domains. However, as discussed below, it is different from a true fourfold node. As shown in Fig. 2d–i, we performed consecutive mechanical scans on the node (yellow dashed region) under a loading force of 60 nN, followed by KPFM imaging. Specifically, from Fig. 2d to Fig. 2f, three square-area scans were performed with a−y^ slow scan direction, while from Fig. 2g to Fig. 2h, two mechanical scans were conducted with a+y^ slow scan direction. After completing all perturbations, the sixfold node exhibited a net displacement of approximately 400 nm compared to its initial position (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Interestingly, the node moved along the slow scan direction, indicating that the node position was mechanically manipulated. The coincidence of the node displacement with the slow scan direction implies that the node is mechanically guided by the scanning trajectory. This is attributed to a relative topographical elevation at the node (i.e., MM site) compared with the MX and XM sites13,39–41, which induces the MM site to be swept along by the AFM tip during scanning. Finally, we found that this mechanically movable node turned out to be a sixfold node as well, i.e., it is surrounded by six domains. However, a key difference is that one of the six domains is a thin, curved, and elongated shape (Supplementary Fig. S6), similar to the case indicated by the green arrow in the DF-TEM image (Fig. 1e). We note that such a thin domain is difficult to resolve in our KPFM imaging due to the spatial resolution limit (Supplementary Fig. S6, S7). Consequently, such a thin and curved domain can effectively reduce the restoring forces arising from the deformed DWs under mechanical perturbations, and thus the node does not return to its original position but can instead be manipulated by the AFM tip motion. A similar dynamic response guided by the slow scan direction was observed in another region of the same sample, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S8.

Quasi-static loading simulations

To elucidate the underlying mechanism behind the different mechanical responses of the robust and movable sixfold nodes, we performed quasi-static loading simulations (see Methods for details). To simulate AFM-induced deformations, we modeled the tWSe2 bilayers used in experiments using effective honeycomb layers with tunable intralayer stiffness suitable for simulating polar twisted 2D homo- and hetero-bilayers, including other materials such as hBN. Our simulations should thus be considered qualitatively consistent with the experimental observations. We considered two distinct stacking configurations: an ideal sixfold symmetric structure with a central MM stacking region (i.e., node) surrounded by six MX/XM triangular domains, and symmetry-broken variant where one of these triangles is compressed, mimicking an experimentally observed asymmetric structure (see Methods for a description on how to achieve this compression). The left panels of Fig. 3a illustrate these two systems before AFM-like loading, while the right panels display their states after the additional unconstrained energy minimisation.

The symmetric configuration remains largely unaffected, whereas the asymmetric configuration exhibits a displaced MM node. b Root-mean-square (RMS) atomic displacements within the force-applied region for the symmetric and asymmetric configurations. Larger displacements in the asymmetric case indicate that the MM node is more easily displaced under load. c Evolution of the system’s total energy during quasi-static AFM-like loading with a force of 0.2 eV Å−1 applied within a 50 Å radius. To assess the response of these two distinct geometries under AFM perturbation, we quantified the atomic displacements resulting from identical in-plane force amplitudes applied across different directions and stacking configurations. Specifically, we computed the root-mean-square (RMS) displacement after energy minimisation of atoms within the region under quasi-static loading. This scalar value captures the average magnitude of atomic motion induced by the perturbation and serves as a proxy for the restoring force described earlier: smaller RMS values indicate a stronger internal resistance to deformation. In this context, the restoring force can be interpreted as the internal force field that opposes the applied perturbation and limits atomic excursions from equilibrium. Using this metric, we demonstrate that the symmetric structure resists in-plane deformation more effectively than the strained, asymmetric configuration.

In Fig. 3b, we chose 150° as the force direction, defined counter-clockwise with respect to the positive x-axis direction. Since this orientation is not aligned with any high-symmetry stacking axis, it serves as a representative example of the generic response expected for arbitrary directions. For this direction, we found a pronounced difference in mechanical response between the two stacking configurations: the symmetric structure exhibited an RMS displacement of 0.9 Å, while the strained, asymmetric configuration underwent a much larger displacement of 6.7 Å. This contrast directly reflects the greater mechanical stiffness of the symmetric configuration and its enhanced ability to restore its original geometry under external perturbations. A full angular analysis across all directions is presented in the Supplementary Fig. S9, where we found that this trend is robust across most angles, except for a few special orientations near DW axes. These results collectively establish that the symmetric configuration exhibits stronger resistance to in-plane AFM perturbations than the asymmetric configuration. This aligns well with the experimental observations on the sixfold nodes: symmetric nodes remained immobile under AFM perturbation, whereas asymmetric ones were readily displaced.

To complement our displacement analysis, Fig. 3c shows the evolution of the system’s total energy during quasi-static loading and subsequent release. We first tracked the energy throughout the minimisation steps, from the moment the force is applied until it is removed. We then continued monitoring beyond the vertical dashed line, where the applied force is removed and the system is allowed to relax unconstrained. We focused on the same direction as in the RMS analysis. Comparing the symmetric and asymmetric stacking configurations revealed a pronounced contrast: in the quasi-static regime, the asymmetric system accumulated about 0.6 meV atom−1 of elastic energy, whereas the symmetric configuration showed an increase of less than 0.1 meV atom−1. In the unconstrained regime, both systems reduced their energies and nearly recovered their initial energy states. However, as shown in the right panels of Fig. 3a, their final structural configurations differed markedly: only the asymmetric structure exhibited a displacement of the node from its initial position. This behavior reflected its weaker restoring force, previously quantified by the RMS displacements in Fig. 3b, which permitted larger deformations under load and led to a displaced configuration upon release. By contrast, the stronger restoring force of the symmetric structure limited atomic excursions during loading, resulting in smaller energy increases and a return to its original geometry. The persistence of the displaced node in the asymmetric case underscored its mechanical softness and aligns with our experimental observations that such nodes were more readily manipulated by AFM perturbations.

Topologically unprotected fourfold node

Surprisingly, we observed that a certain non-ideal fourfold node fractured under mechanical perturbation. Figure 4a shows the original sample location where this unexpected phenomenon occurred. As outlined by the red dashed polygon, the node displays a fourfold configuration. We carefully examined whether this node was accompanied by a thin, elongated domain (similar to Fig. 2d–i), but found no clear evidence for its presence. We performed a mechanical scan (inset of Fig. 4a) on the node by applying a loading force of 60 nN over a 1 × 1 μm2 region (yellow dashed square). As shown in Fig. 4b, the fourfold node broke apart, leading to the merging of two light blue domains (marked by pink circles) into a larger single domain and the separation of two blue domains (green stars). Such annihilation of a fourfold node was also observed in another sample (Supplementary Fig. S10)

The bilayer border is traced with a yellow long-dashed line. The inset illustrates a mechanical scan (1 × 1 μm2) performed on the targeted fourfold node, marked by the yellow dashed region in (a). b A mechanical scan irreversibly annihilated the node, resulting in the merging of two light blue domains (pink circle) into a larger single domain and the separation of two blue domains (green stars).

This result indicates that this fourfold node was eliminated due to mechanical perturbations and is, therefore, topologically unprotected. Such a node cannot be classified as a topological defect; it is merely a junction point of DWs. The annihilation of the fourfold node implies that its energy state corresponds to a local minimum, indicating that it is metastable, and thus it transforms into a more stable domain configuration under a small mechanical perturbation. Considering the DF-TEM results, the three DWs with different Burgers vectors would be expected not to intersect at this fourfold node, but rather to meet without forming a true junction, resembling the structure shown in Fig. 1h. Therefore, although our AFM study cannot resolve individual Burgers vectors, we believe that the crossing of three DWs is essential for topological protection. Further investigations are required to fully clarify the mechanism of the irreversible annihilation of the fourfold node. As an initial step, we provide an energetic and mechanistic comparison between sixfold and fourfold nodes in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Figs. S11–S13).

Discussion

In summary, we investigated the dynamic behavior and topological protection of various node types in tWSe2 moiré superlattices under local mechanical perturbations. Using DF-TEM and KPFM, we identified not only typical symmetric sixfold nodes but also non-ideal node types, such as fourfold and unexpected eightfold nodes, and classified them according to the number of surrounding MX/XM domains and the arrangements of DWs associated with distinct Burgers vectors. AFM-based mechanical loading experiments revealed striking contrasts in node motion: while symmetric sixfold nodes are dynamically robust, a highly asymmetric sixfold node can be displaced and guided by the scan direction of the AFM tip. These findings, further supported by quasi-static loading simulations, demonstrate that node topology critically determines the mechanical response of moiré ferroelectrics. Furthermore, a fourfold node undergoes fracture due to its lack of topological protection, underscoring its fundamental distinction from topologically protected nodes. Our results establish a direct link between node topology and mechanical response, providing fundamental insights into moiré ferroelectricity, and advancing our understanding of topological defects in 2D sliding ferroelectrics.

Methods

Sample preparation

Flakes used for sample preparation were mechanically exfoliated onto a 285 nm SiO2/Si wafer using adhesive tape. The thicknesses of exfoliated hBN and WSe2 flakes were determined prior to stacking using optical contrast on a 285 nm SiO2/Si substrate42. In this method, the optical contrast arises from interference between reflections at the air/flakes/SiO2 interfaces and depends sensitively on the flake thickness and refractive index. Once a mono-layer WSe2 flake was unambiguously identified by optical contrast, the tear-and-stack procedure was subsequently applied to deliberately assemble a bilayer structure43,44. A poly(bisphenol A carbonate) (PC)/polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) stamp was used to pick up and transfer the flakes. To fabricate the tWSe2/hBN sample, hBN flake was first picked up. Using this flake, a portion of a mono-layer WSe2 flake was torn and picked up. The stage holding the wafer was then rotated to achieve a target twist angle, after which the remaining portion of the WSe2 flake was collected. The mean twist angle of the samples was approximately 0.22°, as determined by moiré periodicity analysis (Supplementary Fig. S14) and second-harmonic generation measurements (Supplementary Fig. S15). The final stack was transferred onto a SiO2/Si wafer without solvent cleaning to preserve the PC film. Because the stacking process starts from a pre-identified mono-layer, the resulting tWSe2 region in the final structure is intentionally and precisely controlled to consist of exactly two atomic layers. For the fabrication of the tWSe2/hBN TEM sample, tWSe2 and hBN flakes were sequentially picked up following the same procedure described above26,45. The resulting stack was transferred onto a 50 nm-thick SiN membrane.

Atomic force microscopy

KPFM imaging was performed on commercial AFM (NX10, Parksystems). The Au coated conductive probes (PPP-NCSTAu, Nanosensors) were used during the entire measurements. The KPFM contact potential difference between the conductive probe and sample surface was detected by an AC modulation voltage of +3 V. The scan speed was set to 0.4 Hz in order to avoid surface tearing during the mechanical scans.

Transmission electron microscopy

We performed selected-area electron diffraction and DF-TEM imaging using 200-kV field emission TEM (JEM-2100F, JEOL) equipped with a CMOS camera (OneView, Gatan). For dark-field (DF) imaging, an objective aperture (diameter: 0.77 nm-1) was positioned in the diffraction plane at a camera length of 200 mm to isolate selected Bragg reflections. Illumination was set to a broad-beam condition using a 250 μm condenser aperture, ensuring a quasi-parallel incident beam and minimising excitation-error broadening. The first-order Bragg peak (g=101¯0) was used, with a slight specimen tilt, to obtain domain contrast, while the second-order Bragg peaks (g=112¯0,g=12¯10,andg=21¯1¯0) were used to resolve the domain walls and identify their corresponding Burgers vectors. Three DF-TEM images were acquired using symmetry-equivalent second-order reflections (g=112¯0,g=12¯10,andg=21¯1¯0), and each image was assigned a distinct colour channel (red, green, or blue). By overlaying these colour-adjusted images, we obtained a composite DF-TEM map where the domain boundaries were visualised and colour-coded according to their Burgers vectors24,25,45.

Second harmonic generation

The SHG measurements were performed using a 1064-nm pulsed laser with the pulse width of 1.66 ps and the repetition rate of 40 MHz (FL-1064-PS, Changchun New Industries Optoelectronics Tech Co.). To prevent sample damage, a relatively low average power of 3.3 mW was used. A 50× objective lens (numerical aperture 0.8) was used to focus the laser beam on the sample. A pair of polarizers were used to set the polarizations of the incident and the second-harmonic light in parallel, and a λ/2 wave plate was used to rotate the polarisation directions with respect to the sample. A back-illuminated deep-depletion charge-coupled device (Andor iDus420 CCD (Oxford Instruments)), coupled with a grating monochromator, was used to detect the SHG signal.

Theoretical calculations

We performed our simulations using LAMMPS46, employing a quasi-static approach in which a constant-magnitude in-plane force is applied during energy minimisation to emulate the effect of an AFM tip. This force, equal to 0.2 eV Å−1, was directed within the plane of the flake and acts on atoms located inside a circular region of 50 Å radius centered on the MM node. To probe directional responses, the force is rotated in-plane from 0° to 360°. The quasi-static approach is reasonable for scanning speeds below 5 m s-1 (ref. 20). We also carried out benchmark MD calculations to confirm that the quasi-static approach is not too constraining and observe similar direction-dependent trends for both approaches. Prior to the application of this force, we constructed two distinct stacking configurations: a symmetric sixfold structure and an asymmetric variant. These were prepared by leveraging internal torques that naturally arise when positioning the hexagonal top-layer flake on an infinite substrate. For the symmetric configuration, all corner atoms of the flake were fixed, thereby locking it into a highly symmetric orientation. In contrast, the asymmetric configuration was obtained by releasing the bottom-right corner atom of the flake, allowing the flake to partially realign; this resulted in a compressed lower triangle that mimics the formation of asymmetric structures. This intrinsic construction avoids the complications of artificially imposing external constraints to modify node symmetry. Importantly, releasing additional corners does not yield a thinner node, since the internal torque acts uniformly on all corners in the same clockwise direction. Once the initial configurations are established, we carried out the quasi-static simulations, keeping all corner atoms fixed to prevent global drift. For each direction of applied force, we extracted the atomic displacements and total energy after relaxation. To assess structural stability, we also performed an additional energy minimisation step with the force removed, allowing us to quantify recovery and residual deformation. In terms of force-field modelling, intralayer bonding is described by a modified version of the Tersoff potential47, while inter-layer interactions are captured by the registry-dependent ILP potential48. Our tailored version of the Tersoff potential reduces the interaction strength between B and N atoms by a factor of 0.55, effectively softening the system. This allowed us to emulate the response of a system with a true twist angle of 0.01° using a computationally more tractable simulation cell with a twist of 0.43°.
