SNX-mediated biogenesis of a plant-unique vesicle derived from the multivesicular body
Authors: Yanbin Li, Ran Tao, Hai Zhang, Xiaokang Wen, Stephen King Pong Leung, Qing Qi, Xiaohui Zheng, Haoxuan Guo, Congxian Wu, Zhifei Fu, Xiaorong Huang, Wilson Chun Yu Lau, Liwen Jiang, Yong Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRetrograde transport is central to endomembrane homeostasis, yet the identity and origin of plant retrograde carriers remain unresolved. Prevailing models propose that plant vacuolar sorting receptors (VSRs) recycle either from multivesicular bodies (MVBs) to the trans-Golgi network (TGN) or from the TGN to the Golgi apparatus and/or endoplasmic reticulum (ER). However, the ultrastructural features of plant retrograde transport carriers remain largely unresolved. Here, we show that plant retrograde transport is likely mediated by a previously unrecognized class of MVB-derived spherical vesicles. Using correlative light and electron microscopy and three-dimensional electron tomography, we identify a distinct population of ~30–50 nm spherical vesicles adjacent to MVBs, including nascent vesicles budding from the MVB limiting membrane in Arabidopsis root cells. Immunogold labeling shows that these vesicles are enriched in retromer components and VSRs, suggesting that they possibly function as retrograde transport carriers. To investigate their biogenesis, we perform cryo-electron microscopy and liposome tubulation assays, showing that Arabidopsis SNX1 generates shorter membrane tubules than its mammalian counterpart, consistent with reduced membrane affinity linked to differences in the amphipathic helix. Notably, the SNX1-SNX2 heterodimer produces heterogeneous structures, including spherical vesicles, recapitulating in vivo observations. Lastly, knockdown of SNX1 or SNX2 results in vacuolar mislocalization and increased degradation of GFP-VSR2, and defects in SNX1 and VPS29 inhibit formation of spherical vesicles adjacent to MVBs, resulting in embryonic lethality before the globular stage. Together, these findings establish MVB-derived spherical vesicles as plant retrograde carriers and reveal a distinct SNX-mediated mechanism underlying their formation.Subject terms: Protein trafficking in plants, Plant physiology

Introduction

All eukaryotic cells possess a highly efficient endomembrane system that ensures the precise transport of biomacromolecules, such as proteins, to their designated locations, allowing them to carry out their functions effectively1. This system depends on tightly regulated anterograde and retrograde trafficking to deliver cargo and recycle components. Lysosomes in animal cells and vacuoles in plant and fungal cells function as central degradation compartments and play essential roles in nutrient recycling, signal transduction, membrane turnover, and the maintenance of cellular homeostasis. In animal cells, lysosome biogenesis requires the coordinated synthesis of lysosomal proteins and their trafficking through the endosomal system. Lysosomal hydrolases are synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), modified in the Golgi apparatus with mannose 6-phosphate tags, and recognized by mannose 6-P receptor (MPR). The hydrolase-MPR complexes are then packaged at the trans-Golgi network (TGN). Lysosomal proteins are delivered either directly from the TGN to lysosomes via endosomes or indirectly via the plasma membrane (PM) through endocytosis. Proteins such as receptors that are not destined for lysosomes are recycled back to the PM or Golgi. In endocytosis, internalized membrane proteins can be directed to lysosomes or retrieved into tubulo-vesicular carriers for recycling—either directly back to the PM (fast recycling), indirectly via the endocytic recycling endosomes (slow recycling), or, in some cases, back to the secretory pathway2. Defects in these transport processes are associated with various neurodegenerative disorders3–5. In plants, retrograde transport is thought to be closely related to numerous physiological functions, including embryo development, plant morphogenesis, gravity response, and disease resistance6–16. However, the subcellular localization and formation mechanisms of retrograde transport carriers involved in recycling vacuolar sorting receptors (VSRs) in plants remain elusive, leading to two prevailing hypotheses15,17. The classical hypothesis suggests that retrograde transport primarily mediates the retrieval of VSRs from multivesicular bodies (MVBs), also referred to as prevacuolar compartments (PVCs), back to the TGN18,19. In this model, the recognition between VSRs and cargo proteins occurs at the TGN. Upon completion of sorting, VSRs and cargo proteins separate within the lumen of the MVB, after which VSRs are retransferred to the TGN to participate in the next round of cargo protein sorting20,21. Conversely, a more recent hypothesis suggests that the recognition between VSRs and cargo proteins occurs early in the ER, with sorting completed in the TGN. Thus, retrograde transport mainly mediates the transport of VSRs from the TGN to the Golgi apparatus and/or the ER to engage in subsequent rounds of cargo protein sorting22,23. In addition to the ongoing controversy surrounding their subcellular localization, the ultrastructure and formation mechanisms of plant retrograde transport carriers remain elusive23–25.

In yeast, retrograde transport is mediated by the retromer complex, a pentamer composed of a membrane-binding SNX subcomplex, formed by the heterodimer Vps5p and Vps17p, and a cargo-recognition VPS subcomplex comprising Vps26p, Vps29p, and Vps35p26. In animals, SNX1 and SNX2 are homologous to Vps5p, whereas SNX5 and SNX6 share a common ancestor with Vps17p and are closely related. The SNX protein family commonly features two specialized functional domains: a BAR (Bin/amphiphysin/Rvs) domain, which enables membrane curvature, and a PX (phox homology) domain, which binds to phosphoinositides. SNX1 and SNX2 interact with phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PI(3)P) and phosphatidylinositol 3,5-bisphosphate (PtdIns(3,5)P2) through the PX domain, thereby directly anchoring to the membrane27. The VPS subcomplex consists of VPS26-VPS29-VPS35, where VPS35 interacts with VPS26 and VPS29 through its N-terminal and C-terminal domains, respectively, forming a horseshoe-shaped α-helix28. Unlike the stable heteropentameric retromer complex in yeast, the SNX-BAR heterodimers SNX1/2–SNX5/6 in animals can assemble into functionally distinct endosomal sorting complexes (ESCPE-1) that operate independently of the retromer core29. In Arabidopsis, homologs of the Vps5p and VPS proteins are present, comprising three genes encoding SNX (SNX1, SNX2a, and SNX2b), three encoding VPS35 (VPS35a, VPS35b, and VPS35c), two encoding VPS26 (VPS26a and VPS26b), and one encoding VPS29. SNX1 has been shown to be essential for the recycling of PIN2, BRI1, IRT1, PHT1, and SOS1 to the plasma membrane14,20,30–32. Recent studies indicate that the retrieval of VAMP727, a protein involved in vacuolar fusion in plants, from the vacuolar membrane depends on the SNX complex but not on the VPS complex33. In addition, VPS complex also participates in proper host invasion in rice blast34–36. Although the structure and function of retromer components in animals and yeast have been increasingly well characterized, the mechanism by which the retromer complex mediates retrograde transport in plants remains unclear29,37–40.

In this study, we combined correlative light and electron microscopy (CLEM), 3D electron tomography (ET), cryo-EM, and all-atom molecular dynamics simulations, along with cell biology and genetic approaches, to investigate plant retrograde transport in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, with the following major findings: (1) The CLEM, 3D ET, and immunogold transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analyses revealed distinct spherical vesicles (~30–50 nm in diameter) labeled by retromer markers and VSR proteins in Arabidopsis root cells, with nascent vesicles budding from MVBs in Arabidopsis root cells; (2) We applied cryo-EM to demonstrate that, when incubated with liposomes, Arabidopsis MVB-localized, BAR domain-containing SNX proteins generate significantly shorter tubules than mouse SNX1 in vitro; (3) The combined structural data and all-atom molecular dynamics simulations indicate that Arabidopsis SNX1 has a reduced membrane affinity compared to mouse SNX1, primarily due to differences in its AH domain, which in turn restricting the formation of longer tubular structures relative to those formed by mouse SNX1; (4) The Arabidopsis SNX1-SNX2 heterodimer produces a more heterogeneous mixture of irregular tubules and spherical vesicles when incubated with liposomes in vitro, closely mirroring the formation of the spherical vesicles near MVBs observed in vivo; and (5) We demonstrated that knockdown of SNX1 or SNX2 resulted in vacuolar mislocalization and increased degradation of GFP-VSR2 and impaired proper seed development and germination in plants. Disruptions in SNX1 and the core retromer component VPS29 impair formation of the spherical vesicles near MVBs, lead to the development of enlarged MVBs, and result in embryonic lethality before the globular stage. Taken together, our study provides evidence for the structural features and biogenesis of plant-unique vesicles derived from MVBs and mediated by SNX1 and SNX2.

Results

Plant retromer markers and VSRs localize to MVBs

To investigate plant retrograde transport, we first performed a systematic analysis of the subcellular localization of retromer markers and VSRs, which serve as the transport machinery and cargoes, respectively, for retrograde trafficking. Confocal imaging analysis of transgenic Arabidopsis root cells revealed that VSR1, the key retromer component VPS29 and SNX1, partially colocalized with MVBs labeled by RHA1/ARA7 (conventional members of the Arabidopsis RAB5 family) and RABG3f (a member of the RAB7 family) (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, these markers did not colocalize with the Golgi apparatus or the TGN (Supplementary Fig. 2). Similarly, in protoplasts, VSRs exclusively co-localizes with MVBs labeled by ARA7 and RABG3f, and do not overlap with other organelles such as the ER, Golgi apparatus, or TGN (Supplementary Fig. 3). Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that retrograde transport may mediate the retrieval of VSRs from MVBs. Furthermore, we show that VSRs specifically colocalize with ARA7 and RABG3f, but not with other Rab GTPases (Supplementary Fig. 4), suggesting that RHA1/ARA7 and RABG3f may play a role in plant retrograde transport.

CLEM, 3D ET analyses, and immuno-EM reveal a plant-unique population of spherical vesicles (~30–50 nm in diameter), with nascent vesicles budding from MVBs

Given that plant retrograde transport likely to mediate the retrieval of VSRs from MVBs, we utilized CLEM and 3D ET to systematically identify and analyze potential structures around MVBs. Using CLEM, we first mapped the positions of all MVBs in a 200-nanometer root slice (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Through a systematic 3D ET analysis, we identified previously uncharacterized spherical vesicle structures, approximately 30 to 50 nm in diameter, located near MVBs (Fig. 1a–f, Supplementary Figs. 5b–t and 6, and Supplementary Video 1). Occasionally, spherical vesicle structures are observed connected to MVBs (Fig. 1d, e, arrowhead), suggesting they may represent newly budded vesicles from the MVB membrane. In addition, we observed that the lumen of these spherical vesicles is more electron-transparent than the intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) of MVBs (Fig. 1f), indicating a lack of cytosolic cargo proteins. This preliminary observation suggests that these spherical vesicles are not plant secretory vesicles, which typically contain cargo proteins and exhibit higher electron density within their lumens under TEM, despite their similar sizes. Next, we applied immunogold labeling to identify the nature of these spherical vesicles. In wild-type plants, VSR antibodies predominantly labeled the spherical vesicle and the limiting membrane of the MVB (Fig. 1g, j and Supplementary Fig. 7a). Interestingly, in transgenic plants expressing VPS29-GFP and SNX1-GFP, GFP antibodies primarily labeled the limiting membrane of the MVB, with a specific enrichment at the budding vesicle regions (Fig. 1h, i, k, l and Supplementary Fig. 7b, c). Taken together, all these results indicate that the newly identified spherical vesicles, with diameters ranging from approximately 30–50 nm, may serve in plant retrograde transport and are distinct from the tubular recycling endosomes described in animal systems.

In liposome tubulation assays, Arabidopsis SNX1 induces the formation of shorter tubules compared to those generated by mouse SNX1

3D ET analysis revealed that spherical vesicles may mediate plant retrograde transport (Fig. 1), a mechanism notably distinct from the tubular recycling endosomes observed in animal and yeast cells26,41. However, the mechanism underlying this structural difference remains unclear. In animals and yeast, the formation of tubular recycling endosomes is primarily mediated by dimers of SNX1/2 and SNX5/6 (in animals) or Vps5p and Vps17p (in yeast)26. Thus, we speculate that SNX proteins are also likely involved in mediating the formation of spherical vesicles in plants. We begin with a detailed analysis of Arabidopsis SNX1 (AtSNX1). The protein-lipid overlay assay showed that AtSNX1 could bind to PI(3)P (Fig. 2a), consistent with its localization to MVBs, as MVB membranes are enriched in PI(3)P. Although AtSNX1 can bind to other phospholipids such as PI(4)P, it did not colocalize with PI(4)P reporters (Supplementary Fig. 8a), and its punctate localization remained largely unchanged following PI(4)P depletion induced by treatment with 50 μM phenylarsine oxide (PAO), a PI(4)K inhibitor (Supplementary Fig. 8b–f), supporting the notion that its in vivo function is primarily associated with PI(3)P-enriched compartments. We then generated liposomes containing the major phospholipids of organellar membranes along with PI(3)P. In liposome tubulation assays AtSNX1 can induce tubule formation upon the addition of PI(3)P, but not PI(4)P (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 8g, h). Quantitative liposome sedimentation assays showed that AtSNX1 binds more efficiently to liposomes containing PI(3)P than to liposomes either lacking PI(3)P or containing PI(4)P (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 8i). Interestingly, the tubules formed by AtSNX1 are significantly shorter than those generated by mouse SNX1 (MmSNX1) (Fig. 2b). To investigate the underlying cause, we further analyzed the structure of AtSNX1 on membrane tubules using cryo-EM. The cryo-EM image showed that the tubules were coated with AtSNX1 (Fig. 2d), with diameters ranging from approximately 30–50 nm (Fig. 2e). Two helical diffraction patterns were observed and indexed, referred to as Class I and Class II (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10, Supplementary Fig. 11a). We determined the cryo-EM structures of SNX1-coated tubules with a resolution of 7.2 Å for Class Ⅰ and 8.7 Å for Class Ⅱ, as assessed by Fourier Shell Correlation (FSC0.143) (Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Table 1). The map revealed that Class Ⅰ tubules contain seven dimers per complete helical turn, with a tubule diameter of around 33 nm (Fig. 2e). Observation of the spiral coating structure of Class I tubules revealed the presence of distinct interaction interfaces. The first interaction interface, termed “Interface Ⅰ,” occurs along the same spiral line, where two adjacent SNX1 dimers interact through contacts between the α2 helix and α3 helix of the BAR domain (Fig. 2f, lower left). Another major protein-protein interaction interface, referred to as “Interface Ⅱ,” occurs between adjacent helical rows, facilitating the crosslinking of these parallel rows. The PX domain of the SNX1 dimer interacts with the α2 helix of the BAR domain from another dimer located in an adjacent helical row (Fig. 2f, lower middle). The residues potentially involved in tubule formation at these two interfaces are also highlighted (Supplementary Fig. 12). We also identified an interaction interface between SNX1 dimers, termed “Interface Ⅲ”, which is formed through the interaction of α helices between two BAR domains (Fig. 2f, lower right). Compared to Class Ⅰ tubules, the Class Ⅱ tubules formed by AtSNX1 exhibit a distinct helical symmetry, with six dimers per complete helical turn and a tubule diameter of around 30 nm (Supplementary Fig. 11b). The monomer (asymmetric unit) and dimer configurations are largely similar between Class I and Class II (Supplementary Fig. 13a). When comparing the structures of plant SNX1 with those of their homologs, these proteins adopt a similar overall architecture and display a comparable radius of curvature (~100 Å) (Supplementary Fig. 13a). However, at the level of higher-order scaffold organization, although Class Ⅰ and Class Ⅱ SNX1 dimers exhibit analogous stacking arrangements, the intersection angles of adjacent dimers at Interface Ⅰ show distinct discrepancies (Supplementary Fig. 11c and Supplementary Fig. 13b). Relative to Class Ⅰ, Class Ⅱ displays a smaller intersection angle, which reduces the overall radius of curvature and consequently decreases the number of dimers required to form a single helical turn. In addition, among the plant SNX1 and its homologs, the mouse SNX1 forms the smallest interaction interface at Interface I via the BAR domains, while the fungi Vps5 forms the largest (Supplementary Fig. 13b). Notably, these differences in the homotypic BAR-BAR domain interactions do not affect the radius of curvature of the helical lattice. Taken together, our analysis shows that higher-order lattice packing plays an important role in regulating membrane curvature in plant SNX1.

The amphipathic helix (AH) of AtSNX1 is essential for membrane binding of AtSNX1

Consistent with the previously reported MmSNX1, we also observed a segment inserted into the membrane within the AtSNX1 dimer (Fig. 3a). Sequence alignment and helix prediction revealed that this segment forms an AH (Fig. 3b). Unlike MmSNX1, where the AH (280–297aa) is primarily located between the BAR (300–521aa) and PX (139–269aa) domains, the AH (157–176aa) of AtSNX1 is situated at the N-terminus of the BAR domain (160–402aa). The AH features polar or charged residues on one side, and hydrophobic residues on the opposite side (Fig. 3c). The non-polar side of the AH inserts into the membrane (Fig. 3c), displacing lipid molecules and inducing curvature, which is essential for curvature-driven protein assembly42. We also investigated the function of the AH in AtSNX1 through mutational analysis. Using membrane-binding assays, we found that replacing residues 157 to 176aa of the AH with a flexible linker (GGGSGGGSGGGS, AH-mutant) significantly reduced AtSNX1’s ability to bind liposomes, thereby impairing its membrane tubulation capacity (Fig. 3d–f). These findings demonstrate that the AH is critical for both membrane binding and deformation by AtSNX1.

All-atom molecular dynamics simulations reveal that AtSNX1 has a lower binding affinity to membranes compared to MmSNX1

The length of tubules generated by SNX1 proteins is influenced by several factors, particularly the membrane-binding affinity of SNX1, which is determined by its AH and directly impacts tubule stability and length37. To further compare the membrane-binding affinities of AtSNX1 and MmSNX1, we performed all-atom molecular dynamics simulations. Two simulations were conducted, one using AtSNX1 and the other using MmSNX1 proteins, each with a timescale of 200 ns. The protein-membrane binding simulations revealed that, for both AtSNX1 and MmSNX1, the interaction with the membrane involved a gradual decrease in the distance between the protein molecules and the membrane, along with the insertion of the AH domain into the membrane (Fig. 4a–f, Supplementary Figs. 14a–f and 15a, b, d, e, and Supplementary Videos 2 and 3). At 200 ns, the simulations showed that the AH domain of MmSNX1 (right) was fully inserted into the membrane, whereas the AH domain of AtSNX1 was only partially inserted (Fig. 4b, c, e, f, and Supplementary Figs. 14b, c, e, f and 15a, b, d, e).

To explore the underlying cause, we compared the cryo-EM structures of AtSNX1 and MmSNX1 and identified several potential factors: (1) differences in their AH tilt angles; notably, The AH domain of MmSNX1 aligns parallel to the membrane upon binding, suggesting a larger lateral contact area and a more stable interaction with the membrane, compared to that of AtSNX1 (Fig. 4g); (2) based on the pseudoatomic model built obtained by AlphaFold and flexible docking into our cryo-EM map, which is further supported by computational membrane interaction prediction, we found that the AH of AtSNX1 has three negatively charged residues (D) and two positively charged residues (K and R) on its polar side, giving it a net negative charge (Fig. 3c and Fig. 4h, left). In contrast, the polar side of the AH in MmSNX1 contains three positively charged residues (K) and one negatively charged residue (D), resulting in an overall positive charge (Fig. 4h, right)43. The cell membrane surface is negatively charged, leading us to speculate that the positively charged AH of MmSNX1 may insert more effectively into the membrane. In contrast, the negatively charged AH of AtSNX1 could experience repulsion from the membrane, potentially reducing its membrane-binding efficiency and impairing subsequent membrane deformation. To further validate these findings, we replaced the AH of MmSNX1 with that of AtSNX1 and evaluated its membrane remodeling ability. The liposome tubulation assay demonstrated that replacing the AH of MmSNX1 with that of AtSNX1 resulted in the formation of shorter tubules, similar to those produced by AtSNX1. This indicates that the AH domain is the key factor underlying the differences in membrane binding and remodeling abilities between MmSNX1 and AtSNX1, and therefore influences the stability and length of the formed tubules (Fig. 4i); (3) In addition, we calculated the interaction energy, Ei(interaction), defined as Ei = Ecoul (electrostatic interaction) + Evdw (van der Waals interaction), which typically indicates attractive forces in all-atom molecular dynamics simulations systems44. The results demonstrated that as the simulation time progressed, the interactions between AtSNX1/MmSNX1 and the membrane gradually strengthened, as indicated by the increasing absolute value of Ei (Supplementary Fig. 15c, f). Notably, the interaction between AtSNX1 and the membrane was relatively weaker than that of MmSNX1, indicating lower attractive forces between AtSNX1 and the membrane compared to those between MmSNX1 and the membrane (Supplementary Fig. 15c, f).

SNX1 and SNX2 jointly induce the formation of spherical vesicles in liposome tubulation assays

In Arabidopsis, six SNX-like proteins have been identified. Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) experiments revealed that SNX1 interacts not only with itself but also with SNX2 (Fig. 5a), indicating that SNX1 can form a heterodimer with SNX2 to mediate vesicle formation. Consistently, the protein-lipid overlay assay showed that SNX2a, like SNX1, binds to PI(3)P, which is essential for its membrane-binding ability (Fig. 5b, c). To investigate the combined effect of SNX1 and SNX2a on membrane deformation, we purified the SNX1-SNX2a complex (Fig. 5d). The liposome tubulation assay showed that SNX2a alone was unable to deform the liposomes. However, the tubules formed by the SNX1-SNX2a complex were more distorted than those formed by SNX1 alone, as revealed by negative-stain TEM (Fig. 5e). Detailed observation of the tubules using cryo-EM revealed that the SNX1-SNX2a heterodimer produces a more heterogeneous mix of irregular tubules and spherical vesicles in vitro (Fig. 5f), closely reflecting the formation of spherical vesicles near MVBs observed in vivo. This suggests that the combined action of SNX1 and SNX2 may play a key role in the formation of spherical vesicles near MVBs in plants. We attempted to analyze the structure of the SNX1-SNX2a complex on membrane tubules using cryo-EM. However, the pronounced irregularity of the spherical vesicles and tubules induced by the SNX1-SNX2a heterodimer hindered the application of iterative helical real-space reconstruction, making it impractical to obtain high-resolution structural information for the complex. To further investigate the membrane-binding properties of the SNX1-SNX2a complex, we performed all-atom molecular dynamics simulations. Protein-membrane binding simulations showed that the AH domain of the SNX1-SNX2a complex resists insertion into the membrane, as average AH‑membrane distance does not decrease over the course of the simulation (Supplementary Fig. 16 and Supplementary Video 4). We have thus proposed that SNX2a modulates the activity of SNX1 by altering the conformation or membrane interaction dynamics of the heterodimer, thereby fine-tuning the curvature generation process. These results indicate that the SNX1-SNX2a complex has an even lower membrane‑binding affinity than the Arabidopsis SNX1 homodimer, leading to the formation of shorter membrane tubules and spherical vesicles, as shown by liposome tubulation assays in vitro.

Knockdown of SNX1 or SNX2 resulted in vacuolar mislocalization and increased degradation of GFP-VSR2 and impaired proper seed development, and germination in plants

To investigate the functional roles of SNX1 and SNX2 proteins in retrograde transport, we first examined whether their suppression affects VSR targeting. Notably, RNAi-mediated suppression of SNX1 or SNX2 expression in protoplasts resulted in its mislocalization of GFP-VSR2 to the vacuole (Fig. 6a). Subsequently, we performed protein immunoblotting and semi-quantitative analysis, which revealed that, compared to the control group, the degradation rate of GFP-VSR2 was significantly increased in protoplasts transformed with RNAi vectors targeting SNX1 or SNX2 (Fig. 6b, c). Similarly, we found that suppression of the core retromer protein VPS29 or the RAB7 activator MON1 by RNAi, as well as expression of dominant-negative mutants of RAB5 and RAB7 (ARA7S24N and RABG3fT22N), misdirected GFP-VSR2 to the vacuole for degradation as demonstrated by confocal microscopy, protein immunoblotting, and semi-quantitative analysis (Supplementary Fig. 17). These results indicate that SNX1 and SNX2, along with RAB5 (ARA7), RAB7 (RABG3f), and the retromer complex protein VPS29, are required for proper VSR targeting in plant cells.

Since VSRs are crucial for directing cargo proteins to protein storage vacuoles (PSVs) during seed development and mediating the targeting of newly synthesized proteinases to PSVs for seed germination45, we observed significant defects in the seeds of the snx1 mutant and the snx1/snx2a/snx2b triple mutant, as well as GEF mutants of RAB5 and RAB7, such as vps9a-2 and mon1/ccz1ab. Taken together, SNX1 and SNX2, as well as RAB5 (ARA7), RAB7 (RABG3f), and the retromer complex are essential for proper VSR targeting, which play a critical role in seed development and germination in plants.

Defects in SNX1 and the core retromer complex protein VPS29 inhibit formation of spherical vesicles near MVBs, resulting in embryonic lethality before the globular stage

Although SNX1 and SNX2 proteins are essential for VSR targeting, single (snx1, snx2a, snx2b) and double mutants exhibit only mild growth defects (Supplementary Figs. 19a–e and 20a–c). The snx1/snx2a/snx2b triple mutant shows slightly more pronounced defects in overall growth but displays negligible phenotypic changes during embryonic development (Fig. 6h, i, and Supplementary Figs. 19f and 20d). We speculate that the core retromer proteins may partially compensate for the functions of SNX1 and SNX2 in plant retrograde transport. To fully disrupt plant retrograde transport, we generated double mutants by crossing the snx1 mutants with the core retromer complex mutant vps29. The snx1−/−vps29+/− mutant exhibited significant defects during the seedling stage. Within the siliques of snx1−/−vps29+/−, seeds showed a high abortion rate (approximately 10.56%, n = 3548), which deviated from the expected theoretical value of 25%. Reciprocal crosses between wild type (Col-0) and snx1−/−vps29+/− revealed that fertility was compromised in the male but not in the female (Supplementary Fig. 21a). To further investigate the underlying cause, we performed Alexander staining to assess pollen viability. After staining, normal pollen appeared dark purple, while abnormal pollen was light green. In wild type and snx1−/−vps29+/−, 96.7 ± 1.3 and 94.7 ± 0.9% of the pollen, respectively, stained normally (Supplementary Fig. 21b, c). Statistical analysis revealed a slight decrease in pollen viability in snx1−/−vps29+/− compared to WT, indicating that the SNX1/VPS29 mutation had a minor effect on pollen viability (Supplementary Fig. 21c). Further analysis revealed that 4 h after pollen germination in vitro, there was no significant difference in germination rate between WT and the snx1−/−vps29+/− mutant. However, significant shorter pollen tubes were observed in the snx1−/−vps29+/− mutant compared to WT (Supplementary Fig. 21b, c), indicating reduced competitive ability caused by the SNX1/VPS29 mutation. To determine the seed abortion period in snx1−/−vps29+/− mutant offspring, whole-mount clearing was performed. Wild-type seeds contained late heart-shaped embryos at 9 days after pollination (DAP). In contrast, embryos in snx1−/−vps29+/− seeds were arrested at the globular stage and exhibited abnormal cell division (Fig. 7a–d and Supplementary Fig. 20e, f). To further clarify the cause of embryo abnormalities, TEM analysis of embryos of snx1−/−vps29+/− was carried out. The results showed that embryos of snx1−/−vps29+/− exhibited enlarged MVBs and a reduced number of spherical vesicles near MVBs, compared to WT (Fig. 7e, f). In summary, defects in SNX1 and VPS29 inhibit formation of spherical vesicles near MVBs, resulting in embryonic lethality before the globular stage.

Discussion

3D ET reveals distinct spherical vesicles in Arabidopsis root cells

A longstanding question regarding plant retrograde transport concerns their origin, for which two distinct models have been proposed to date: (1) retrograde transport mediates the transport of VSRs from MVBs to the TGN, and (2) retrograde transport facilitates the transport of VSRs from the TGN to the Golgi and/or the ER. One of the primary reasons for this debate is the limited characterization of the ultrastructure of plant retrograde transport carriers, largely attributed to their small size and distinctive architecture. In this study, we first demonstrated that retromer markers and VSRs localize to MVBs, suggesting that retrograde transport likely originates from MVBs (Supplementary Fig. 1). Then, we applied CLEM and 3D ET to systematically identify and analyze potential structures associated with MVBs (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 5). Interestingly, numerous small vesicles (~30 to 50 nm in diameter) were observed near MVBs (Fig. 1a–f). In some cases, spherical vesicle structures were seen connected to MVBs (Fig. 1e). Furthermore, immunogold labeling identified these vesicles as possibly representing retrograde transport carriers using antibodies specific to retromer markers and VSRs (Fig. 1g–l). Therefore, we reasonably conclude that retrograde transport most likely originates from MVBs.

Although 3D ET has provided valuable ultrastructural information on spherical vesicles near MVBs, it remains important to directly visualize their dynamic behavior. However, due to their small size (~30–50 nm in diameter) and rapid movement, capturing their dynamics and transient interactions with other organelles using conventional confocal microscopy is extremely challenging. In future work, we aim to employ advanced live-cell super-resolution imaging techniques, such as lattice light-sheet microscopy or instant structured illumination microscopy (iSIM), to resolve these transient events more effectively and thereby gain deeper insights into the dynamics of vesicle-mediated trafficking.

Structural basis of formation of plant unique spherical vesicles by SNX proteins

The formation of tubular recycling endosomes in animals and yeast has been extensively studied both in vitro and in vivo, with multiple components—including Rab GTPases, the retromer complex, and other associated proteins—coordinating membrane remodeling and cargo sorting26,41. In contrast, plant cells appear to utilize a distinct mechanism that remains less well understood. In our study, we demonstrated that RHA1/ARA7, RABG3f, VPS29, SNX1, and VSR proteins colocalize in MVBs, suggesting that retrograde transport may originate from MVBs. Furthermore, RNAi-mediated suppression of SNX proteins, the core retromer component VPS29, or the RAB7 activator MON1, as well as the expression of dominant-negative mutants of RAB5 and RAB7 (ARA7S24N and RABG3fT22N), redirected GFP-VSR2 to the vacuole for degradation (Fig. 6a–c and Supplementary Fig. 17). These findings indicate that SNX1 and SNX2, together with RHA1/ARA7, RABG3f, and the retromer component VPS29, are required for proper VSR targeting in plant cells. Among these components, (i) RHA1/ARA7 is initially recruited to MVBs; (ii) RHA1/ARA7 then recruits the MON1–CCZ1 complex, which activates RABG3f45; and (iii) RABG3f then serves as a key coordinator, recruiting downstream effectors—including the retromer component VPS35—to initiate vesicle formation12.

Within the retromer complex, SNX proteins are primarily responsible for membrane remodeling and are thus essential for the vesicle budding and formation. AtSNX1 and MmSNX1 share only 28.6% amino acid sequence similarity. MmSNX1 does not interact with AtSNX1 or AtSNX2 in Y2H assays, unlike AtSNX1, which interacts with both AtSNX1 and AtSNX2 (Supplementary Fig. 22a). In vitro liposome tubulation assays further demonstrated that replacing the amphipathic helix (AH) of MmSNX1 with that of AtSNX1 led to the formation of shorter tubules, resembling those induced by AtSNX1 (Fig. 4i). These findings suggest that the primary difference between AtSNX1 and MmSNX1 lies in their AH domains, which lead to distinct membrane binding and remodeling behaviors due to structural and electrostatic differences (Figs. 3 and 4a–h). AtSNX1 exhibits lower membrane-binding affinity and consequently generates shorter tubules than MmSNX1 in vitro (Figs. 2b and 3). In vivo, AtSNX1 expression results in a punctate localization pattern, whereas MmSNX1 forms elongated tubular structures in Arabidopsis protoplasts, roots, and HeLa cells (Supplementary Fig. 22b–g). We propose that AtSNX1 drives the formation of spherical structures due to its limited ability to bind to and stabilize tubules, thereby preventing the formation of elongated tubular structures. Interestingly, expression of MmSNX1 fails to rescue the vacuolar degradation defect of VSRs caused by RNAi-mediated knockdown of AtSNX1 (Supplementary Fig. 22j). This finding indicates that, although MmSNX1 can assemble long, stable tubular retrieval vesicles in Arabidopsis protoplasts, such extended carriers are probably poorly suited to the spatial and physiological demands of plant cells. Another possibility is that tubulation can be stabilized through interactions with the cytoskeletal elements and motor proteins in animal cells—a mechanism well documented in animal cells and one that may underlie the structural differences we observe46,47. Consistent with this, disrupting microtubules with 50 μM oryzalin for 6 h significantly reduced the length of MmSNX1-labeled tubules in Arabidopsis roots (Supplementary Fig. 22g–i), supporting microtubule-dependent stabilization of tubulation. Moreover, two recent studies in Arabidopsis further underscore a potential functional connection between endosomal and autophagosomal dynamics and the cytoskeleton. One study demonstrated that CLASP interacts with SNX1 to link microtubules to auxin transport through PIN2 recycling48. Another study revealed that SNX proteins interact with ATG8 and CLASP to facilitate autophagosome movement along microtubules49. However, whether microtubules contribute to the vesicle formation from MVBs in Arabidopsis remains unclear and represents a promising direction for future research.

In addition to SNX1, the Arabidopsis genome also encodes unique SNX2 genes. SNX1 and SNX2 proteins can form a heterodimer in Arabidopsis, potentially collaborating in plant retrograde transport. Notably, when liposomes were incubated with both SNX1 and SNX2, spherical vesicles were frequently observed alongside tubular structures, closely resembling the formation of spherical vesicles near MVBs observed in vivo. However, the pronounced irregularity of the spherical vesicles and tubules induced by the SNX1-SNX2 heterodimer rendered the iterative helical real-space reconstruction approach impractical, preventing us from obtaining structural information about the SNX1-SNX2 heterodimer. To further explore the role of SNX2, we conducted all-atom molecular dynamics simulations, which revealed that the AH domain of the SNX1–SNX2a complex resists membrane insertion, as the average AH‑membrane distance does not decrease over the course of the simulation (Supplementary Fig. 16 and Supplementary Video 4). We propose that SNX2a modulates SNX1 activity by altering the conformation or membrane interaction dynamics of the heterodimer, thereby fine-tuning membrane curvature generation. Consequently, the SNX1-SNX2a complex exhibits lower membrane-binding affinity than the Arabidopsis SNX1 homodimer, resulting in the formation of shorter tubules and spherical vesicles in vitro, as demonstrated by liposome tubulation assays (Fig. 5e, f).

Although SNX1 and SNX2 appear to play a central role in plant retrograde transport, the snx1/snx2a/snx2b triple mutant exhibited only mild defects in plant growth and negligible phenotypic changes during embryonic development (Fig. 6h, i, and Supplementary Figs. 19f and 20d). These findings led us to hypothesize that the VPS subcomplex of the retromer may partially compensate for the loss of SNX function. Therefore, we generated double mutants by crossing snx1 with the core retromer mutant vps29. The resulting snx1−/−vps29+/− plants exhibited pronounced defects during seedling development. Ultrastructural analysis by TEM further revealed that embryos of snx1−/−vps29+/− contained enlarged MVBs and a markedly reduced number of spherical vesicles in the vicinity of MVBs compared with wild type (Fig. 7e, f). Collectively, these results suggest that the VPS subcomplex of the retromer may provide functional redundancy or engage compensatory mechanisms in response to the loss of SNX function. However, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, and alternative explanations cannot be excluded.

Furthermore, the biogenesis of retrograde transport carriers is undoubtedly more complex and likely involves more factors within the cell. Future studies using cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) combined with cryo-focused ion beam (cryo-FIB) milling and cryo-lift-out procedures on various mutant samples will provide deeper insights into the factors and mechanisms underlying plant retrograde transport.

The nature of the spherical vesicles observed near MVBs in plant cells

Although we identified a plant-specific vesicle population that appears to originate from MVBs, is labeled by retromer and VSR proteins, and whose formation depends on retromer components, additional evidence is required to demonstrate that these vesicles traffic to the TGN and function as bona fide retrieval carriers. An additional unresolved issue concerns the relationship between SNX function and VSR recycling. While knockdown of SNX1 or SNX2 resulted in vacuolar mislocalization and enhanced degradation of GFP–VSR2, it remains unclear whether VSRs constitute direct cargo of SNX proteins. Previous studies have shown that Arabidopsis VSR1 can bind VPS35 as revealed by immunoprecipitation assays15, and that in yeast, the VSR homolog, Vps10p receptor, interacts directly with retromer via Vps35p50. However, the molecular relationship between SNX proteins and VPS35 in Arabidopsis, and whether SNXs directly participate in VSR recognition, remains unresolved33. Alternatively, the observed phenotype may arise from indirect or secondary consequences of SNX perturbation. For example, loss of SNX function could broadly disrupt endosomal membrane organization, phosphoinositide composition, or retromer recruitment, thereby indirectly impairing VSR recycling20,51. Furthermore, impaired recycling of other regulatory factors or cargo adapters may secondarily compromise VSR localization, rather than VSRs being directly recognized by SNX proteins themselves. In addition, degradation of membrane proteins residing on the limiting membrane of MVBs, such as VSRs, generally requires their sorting into intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) through ESCRT-dependent mechanisms52. However, the mechanism by which VSRs become missorted into ILVs in the absence of SNX function remains unknown. Addressing these questions will be important for elucidating the precise molecular pathway underlying VSR recycling and retromer-mediated trafficking in plant cells.

What is the biological significance of plant-specific spherical vesicles in retrograde transport?

Within the cell, the structure of organelles is inherently optimized to enhance their functions53. Spherical vesicles in plant retrograde transport are uniquely suited to their cellular environment. For example, in mature plant cells, the vacuole typically occupies nearly 90% of the cell volume54, leaving limited space for other organelles to move freely and transport rapidly, particularly for large or tubular organelles. Despite this, vacuolar transport of cargo occurs intermittently in plant cells, and receptors like VSRs must be efficiently recycled for subsequent rounds of cargo sorting. Therefore, plants have evolved unique tiny spherical vesicles that are compact and well-suited for navigating the constrained cytoplasmic space between the large central vacuole and the plasma membrane. However, the situation is quite different in animal cells, where lysosomes are significantly smaller than vacuoles. The tubular structure of animal recycling endosomes facilitates efficient cargo sorting and segregation of receptors and transporters into specialized membrane domains55. Additionally, the elongated shape of tubular recycling endosomes possibly facilitates more efficient interactions with the cytoskeletal transport machinery, enhancing their capacity for long-distance transport47. This is particularly crucial in large or polarized cells, such as neurons, where cargo must traverse significant distances from the cell body to distant synapses56. Taken together, the unique structures of organelles are inherently adapted to their cellular environments, thereby optimizing their functions.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

All mutants and transgenic plants were in the Columbia-0 (Col-0) background. The following have been previously described: vps2920, snx151, snx2a51, snx2b51, vps9a57, mon1-245, ccz1a58, ccz1b58, vps35a-1b-2c-17, vps35b-1c-17, GFP-VSR159, VPS29-GFP20, SNX1-GFP/Ch8, mRFP-SYP32 (WAVE22)60, Ch-RAB5 (WAVE7)60, Ch-RAB7 (WAVE5)60, VHA-a1-mRFP61, mCIT-PHOSBP (P7Y)62. AtSNX1-Ch/mYongHong and MmSNX1-mYongHong were cloned into pCAMBIA1305 vector and generated by the floral dip method63. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Table 2. All seeds were surface sterilized with 75% (v/v) ethanol containing 0.05% Triton X-100 for 10 min, washed with ddH2O five times, and then sown on 1/2 MS plates. The plates were kept at 4 °C in the dark for 2 days before being moved to a growth chamber (22 °C, 16 h light/8 h dark).

High-pressure freezing and freeze substitution

The general procedures for high-pressure freezing and freeze substitution were essentially performed as previously described64. Briefly, 5-day-old Arabidopsis root tips were excised and rapidly frozen using a high-pressure freezing apparatus (EM ICE, Leica). For ultrastructural analysis, freeze substitution was performed using 2% OsO₄ and 0.5% uranyl acetate in anhydrous acetone at −80 °C for 96 h, followed by a gradual warming to −20 °C over a 24-h period. The samples were incubated at −20 °C for 12 h, then gradually warmed to 4 °C over a 4-h period. Following three acetone rinses at room temperature, the roots were removed from the planchets and gradually infiltrated with increasing concentrations of Spurr resin (Ted Pella) over a 96-h period. The sample polymerization was performed under vacuum at 70 °C for over 48 h. For immunogold labeling analysis, high-pressure frozen root tips were transferred to the AFS (Leica Microsystems) and freeze-substituted in dry acetone containing 0.1% uranyl acetate at −85 °C. Stepwise infiltration with HM20, embedding, and UV polymerization were performed at −45 °C. For immunolabelling, the working concentration of rabbit anti-VSR and anti-GFP antibodies were 40 μg/ml. Gold particle-coupled secondary antibodies were diluted at a ratio of 1:50, followed by post-staining with aqueous uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Sections were examined using a Hitachi H-7650 transmission electron microscope equipped with a charge-coupled device camera, operating at 80 kV. (Hitachi High- Technologies; www.hitachi-hightech.com/jp/).

Electron tomography observations

The general procedures for performing electron tomography have been described previously65. In brief, 200-nm-thick sections were imaged using a Tecnai F20 electron microscope (FEI Company) operating at 200 kV. For each grid, a tilt image stack comprising 81 images was collected from +60° to −60° in 1.5° increments, followed by the acquisition of a second tilt image stack after rotating the grid by 90°. Dual-axis tomograms were reconstructed from pairs of image stacks using the Etomo program within the IMOD software package (version 4.9.7). The 15-nm gold particles on the surface of the sections were used as fiducial markers to align individual images in the tilt series. After electron tomography analysis, the IMOD software allows the display of an optical slice cut through the 3D image data in any desired orientation. For model generation, contours were manually drawn and meshed using the 3dmod program within the IMOD software package. To account for the thinning of plastic sections during electron imaging, the z-scale factor was calculated to be 1.6 for the root cell tomograms.

Correlative light and electron microscopy

Choose seedlings displaying strong fluorescence, then subject them to high-pressure freezing followed by freeze substitution at −80 °C for 24 h in anhydrous acetone containing 0.25% glutaraldehyde and 0.1% uranyl acetate. The samples were then washed with precooled anhydrous acetone, embedded in graded HM20 resin (33%, 66%, and 100%), and polymerized under UV light for 18 h at −45 °C. The 200 nm semi-thin sections were cut, collected on slot grids, and air-dried. Fluorescent images were initially captured using a confocal microscope, and the sample-loaded grids were subsequently examined using 3D ET.

Confocal microscopy and image processing

Confocal images were acquired using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope. A 405 nm laser line was used to visualize PSV autofluorescence, and emission was collected between 440 and 490 nm. A 488 nm laser line was used to excite green fluorescence, and emissions for GFP were collected in the 500–550 nm range. A 552 nm laser line was used to excite red fluorescence, and emissions for mRFP or mCherry (Ch) were collected in the 600–650 nm range.

Cytological observation

Ovules were dissected from siliques and cleared with Hoyer’s solution, following the previously described protocol66. Images of cleared embryos were captured using a Zeiss LSM980 laser scanning microscope equipped with differential interference contrast (DIC) optics.

Y2H assay

The full-length coding regions of the genes SNX1, SNX2a, SNX2b, SNX3, SNX4, and SNX5 were cloned into the pGADT7 vector to create fusions with the activating domain. The full-length coding sequence of the AtSNX1 and MmSNX1 genes was inserted into the pGBKT7 vector to create fusions with the GAL4 DNA-binding domain. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Table 2. The indicated plasmid combinations were used for the protein-protein interaction test according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Clontech, http://www.clontech.com/).

Phenotype characterization

In vitro pollen germination was conducted in a Petri dish containing solid PGM [1% low melting point agarose (w/v), 18% sucrose (w/v), 0.01% boric acid (w/v), 1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM Ca(NO3)2, and 1 mM MgSO4, adjusted to pH 7.5 using 0.2 M KOH] at 28 °C, following a protocol modified from Boavida and McCormick (2007)67. Alexander staining (Solarbio, G3050) was performed to assess cytoplasmic viability, as described by Alexander68.

Chemical treatments

Oryzalin and PAO were prepared in DMSO and stocked at −20 °C. Seedlings were cultured in 1/2 MS liquid medium containing 50 μM oryzalin for 6 h or 50 μM PAO for 1.5-h before being observed by confocal microscopy. For PAO treatment of Arabidopsis protoplasts, 50 μM PAO was injected into the Arabidopsis protoplasts, which expressed the fluorescent signal, 1 h before being observed by confocal microscopy.

HeLa cells assay

AtSNX1 and MmSNX1 CDS were cloned into the plasmid pLV-IRES-EGFP to generate GFP-tagged forms and then infected HeLa cells with lentiviruses packaged using the pLV-IRES-AtSNX1-EGFP and pLV-IRES-MmSNX1-EGFP plasmids, respectively, to obtain HeLa-AtSNX1-GFP and HeLa-MmSNX1-GFP cell lines. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 15 min. After washing with PBS for three times, coverslips were mounted in ProLongTM Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Invitrogen). Cells were examined using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope with 63× objective and 488 nm laser line. A total of 10 cells were examined in each condition.

Antibodies

The primary antibodies, anti-Myc (XMU-CRC005), anti-GFP (Rockland, 600-401-215L), anti-His (Abbkin, ABT2050), anti-VPS29 (Agrisera, AS204401), and anti-VSR (Agrisera, AS204407) were purchased from commercial companies. For AtSNX1 primary antibody, AtSNXl cDNA was cloned into the pET28a plasmid, which incorporates an N-terminal histidine (His) tag into the expressed protein. Recombinant proteins were generated in Escherichia coli (Rosseta 2; Novagen) and purified using nickel agarose beads. Polyclonal antibodies were raised in rabbit.

Expression of recombinant protein, protein extraction

For the expression of MmSNX1, the full-length mouse SNX1 (NP_062701.2) was subcloned into the pGEX-6P-1 plasmid and expressed as glutathione S-transferase (GST)-tagged fusion proteins in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) cells. Cells were grown in Terrific Broth (TB) medium at 37 °C until reaching an optical density of 1.2 at 600 nm, then induced at 18 °C for 16 h with 0.24 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG). Cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer (140 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na₂HPO₄, 1.8 mM KH₂PO₄, pH 7.4, and 10% glycerol), and lysed by sonication. After centrifugation at 18,000 rpm for 30 min, the supernatant was collected and incubated with glutathione-Sepharose 4B at 4 °C, followed by washing with PBS buffer. Following cleavage with Precision Protease (Beyotime) to remove the GST tag, the eluted proteins underwent further purification by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare) using an elution buffer of 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.4) and 150 mM NaCl. The target protein was collected and stored at −80 °C.

For the expression of full-length AtSNX1 and AtSNX2a, the full-length coding sequences of Arabidopsis SNX1 (AT5G06140) and Arabidopsis SNX2a (AT5G58440) were subcloned into the pET28a-His6-SUMO vector. Plasmids encoding AtSNX1 or AtSNX2a were transformed into LOBSTR cells, and protein expression was carried out as described for MmSNX1 above. For the purification of AtSNX1 and AtSNX2a, cell pellets were lysed by sonication in His-lysis buffer containing 50 mM Na₂HPO₄, 50 mM NaH₂PO₄ (pH 8.0), 400 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, and 0.1% Tween 20. After clearing the bacterial lysates by centrifugation at 18,000 rpm for 30 min, the soluble fraction was incubated with Ni-NTA beads for 2 h. The beads were then washed with His-wash buffer A (50 mM Na₂HPO₄, 50 mM NaH₂PO₄ (pH 8.0), 400 mM NaCl, 60 mM imidazole, and 0.1% Tween 20). After cleavage with ULP1 to remove the His-SUMO tag, the eluted proteins were further purified by size-exclusion chromatography using a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column with an elution buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 8.0) and 400 mM NaCl. The target protein was concentrated and stored at −80 °C. For AtSNX1-AtSNX2a complex expression, full-length AtSNX1 was subcloned into the pMAL-C6t-MBP vector, and full-length AtSNX2a was subcloned into the pET28a-His6-SUMO vector. Overexpressed AtSNX1 and AtSNX2a cell pellets were mixed and lysed together in MBP buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4) and 200 mM NaCl. Then the AtSNX1-AtSNX2a complex was purified using Amylose resin. After cleavage with His-TEV to remove the His-MBP tag, the eluted fraction (SNX1-His-SUMO-SNX2a) was further loaded onto a Ni-NTA column and washed with buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 200 mM NaCl, and 40 mM imidazole to remove unbound samples. Following the removal of the His-SUMO tag by ULP1, the eluted protein was further purified by size-exclusion chromatography using a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column with an elution buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.4) and 200 mM NaCl. The purified target protein was concentrated and stored at −80 °C.

For the purification of His-tagged AtSNX1 and His-tagged AtSNX2a, the procedure was the same as that for full-length AtSNX1, except for slight variations in buffer compositions. For lysis and Ni-NTA affinity purification, His-lysis buffer (50 mM PBS buffer, pH 7.4, 20 mM imidazole, 0.1% Tween 20) was used. Unbound samples were removed with 60 mM imidazole, and the target protein was eluted with buffer containing 250 mM imidazole. The elution protein was collected and stored at −80 °C for lipid binding assay.

Protein-lipid overlay assay

The nitrocellulose membranes contained different lipids (PIP StripsTM membranes; P23751) used in protein-lipid overlay assay, were purchased from ThermoFisher. The nitrocellulose membranes were incubated for 2 h at room temperature in TBST buffer (Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20) containing 3% (w/v) fatty-acid-free BSA. After three washes with TBST buffer, the membranes were incubated at 4 °C for 4 h in TBST containing the purified His-tag protein at a final concentration of 3–5 μg/mL. After washing the membranes four times with TBST buffer, they were incubated at room temperature for 2 h with anti-His tag antibody (1:2000, Abbkine) in TBST buffer. This was followed by three washes with TBST and a 1-h incubation at room temperature with the secondary antibody (1:4000, Beyotime). Finally, the membranes were washed three times with TBST.

Liposome preparation

All lipids were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids. Stock solutions of lipids were prepared at 10 mg/mL in chloroform for 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DOPC), 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE), 1,2-dioleoyl-sn- glycero-3-phosphor-1-serine (DOPS), or dissolved in chloroform at 0.2 mg/mL for 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-(1′-myo-inositol-3′-phosphate) (PtdIns(3)P) and 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-(1′-myo-inositol-4′-phosphate) (PtdIns(4)P). Lipid molar ratios used for liposome preparation are 45% DOPC, 30% DOPE, 20% DOPS, and 5% PtdIns(3)P or PtdIns(4)P. Lipid mixtures were dried under nitrogen gas and kept under vacuum overnight. The dry lipid mixtures were resuspended in 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.4) and 150 mM NaCl for at least 30 min at 37 °C, then vortexed gently to obtain a homogeneous hydrated solution. The solution was extruded through 0.4 μm membrane filters to produce liposomes with a diameter of 400 nm.

Liposome-protein co-sedimentation assay

For liposome-protein co-sedimentation assay, the 400 nm liposomes (0.4 mg/mL) and protein (0.2 mg/mL) were incubated for 30 min at 20 °C before ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 30 min and then the supernatants and pellets were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) analysis.

Liposome tubulation assay and Negative-Stain EM

Protein (0.4 mg/mL) was incubated with liposomes (0.2 mg/mL) for 4 h at 4 °C. The mixture was then applied to a glow-discharged, carbon-coated EM grid and stained with uranyl acetate. The EM grids were examined using a Hitachi H-7650 electron microscope operating at 80 kV.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data acquisition

Four microliters of the sample (protein concentration: 3 mg/mL, liposome concentration: 1.5 mg/mL) were loaded onto a Quantifoil Au grid (R2/2, 300 mesh), followed by plunge-freezing in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher) at 4 °C and 100% humidity. Movies were recorded using a Titan Krios G4 300 kV cryo-TEM system equipped with a Falcon 4i direct electron detector and a Selectris X energy filter (Thermo Fisher). Cryo-EM data were acquired automatically with EPU v3.4.0.5704REL in EER mode, with a total dose of ~50 e−/Å2 and targeted defocus ranging from −1.0 to −2.5 μm, slit width of 10 eV, and a pixel size of 0.92 Å (×130,000 magnification). A total of 6,062 movies across 2 imaging sessions were collected.

Cryo-EM data processing

All processing was performed in CryoSPARC v.4.4.169. Micrographs were aligned with patch motion correction, and the contrast transfer function (CTF) was estimated using patch CTF estimation. A total of 1914 particles were manually picked using the manual picker tool from 58 randomly selected movies. Particle cleaning was performed using 2D classification, resulting in the selection of 1896 particles for Topaz training70. After Topaz picking, 487,808 particles were obtained and further aligned through iterative 2D classifications. During 2D classification, multiple 2D classes corresponding to tubes with different diameters were obtained from Topaz-picked particles (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Following asymmetric helical refinement and extensive symmetry searches in CryoSPARC, only two particle subsets reproducibly converged into stable consensus maps (highlighted by black and green boxes in Supplementary Fig. 9a). Other classes either failed to converge or yielded low-quality maps (highlighted by red and salmon boxes) and were therefore excluded from further analysis. Two populations with distinct diameters (Class Ⅰ: 49,380 particles; Class Ⅱ: 20,728 particles) were manually grouped, and initial 3D volumes were generated using asymmetric helical reconstruction. These volumes were subjected to symmetric search utility in CryoSPARC to determine twist and rise parameters. The symmetry parameters were applied in subsequent rounds of helical refinement. The final resolutions for the two maps were 7.2 Å for Class Ⅰ and 8.7 Å for Class Ⅱ, respectively. We attempted helical reconstruction in RELION 5 using BLUSH regularization and also tested symmetry expansion followed by subunit classification. However, these approaches did not improve the resolution relative to our final maps and were therefore not pursued further.

Model building

To assist with model building, Alphafold 371 was used to predict the dimer structure of plant SNX1. The dimer was rigidly fitted into the Class I and II density maps in ChimeraX72, followed by automatic molecular dynamics flexible fitting (MDFF) in Namdinator73. The final helical assemblies were obtained by docking multiple identical copies of the dimer model, followed by manual adjustment in COOT74 and real-space refinement in PHENIX75. Residues 1–23 in the PX domain of the final model were removed due to the absence of map density. After flexible model refinement, the subcomplex was docked into the two maps to reconstruct the intact scaffold structures of the two populations in ChimeraX. Figures were generated using ChimeraX.

All-atom molecular dynamics simulations

The GROMACS software was used for all-atom molecular dynamics simulations76. The CHARMM-GUI website (https://charmm-gui.org/) was utilized to select the CHARMM36 force field for generating system input files77,78. The cryo-EM structure of MmSNX1 (PDB: 7D6D) was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank, while the AtSNX1 cryo-EM structure was determined in this study37. The structure of AtSNX2a was predicted using the AlphaFold Server (powered by AlphaFold3; https://alphafoldserver.com)71. AtSNX2a-related information was retrieved from Uniport (Uniport ID: Q8L5Z7). Given that the N-terminus of AtSNX2a harbors a long intrinsically disordered region, only the amino acid sequence corresponding to residues 157–587 was used for structure prediction (parameters: protein entity, single copy, 10 replicate predictions). Each prediction run generated five models, and the top-ranked model (rank_0, highest confidence score) was selected as the representative structure. Predicted template modeling (pTM) scores were then compared, and the model with the highest pTM score (0.75) was selected for subsequent molecular simulations (Supplementary Fig. 23a). Additional predicted structures of AtSNX2a are shown in Supplementary Fig. 23b–e. While the overall folds of these models are highly conserved, the disordered region linking the PX and BAR domains is poorly resolved by AlphaFold3, leading to notable structural variability in this region (Supplementary Fig. 23f). The bilayer membrane system was composed of 45% DOPC, 30% DOPE, 20% DOPS, and 5% PI(3)P. The system was solvated using the TIP3P water model, and Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions were added to maintain charge neutrality. Using VMD, the protein was vertically positioned approximately 4.5 Å above the bilayer with its AH domain oriented toward the bilayer; overlapping solvent molecules were then removed to obtain the initial structure. The Particle-mesh Ewald (PME) method was utilized to handle electrostatic interactions. Electrostatic interactions were handled via the Particle-mesh Ewald (PME) method. Following the CHARMM-GUI workflow, energy minimization was first performed with the steepest descent (steep) algorithm for a maximum of 5000 steps. Subsequently, a 5-step pre-equilibration system was conducted for a total duration of 1875 ps, during which restraints were applied to the protein and lipid bilayer prior to the production run. The cutoff distance for Coulomb interactions and van der Waals radii was set to 1 nm, and the truncation value for non-bonded interactions was 10 Å. The Nose-Hoover thermostat was used to maintain the simulation temperature at 300 K, and the Parrinello-Rahman barostat was employed to control the pressure at 1 bar under semi-isotropic conditions. A 200 ns molecular dynamics simulation was carried out using GROMACS 2023 software. Statistical charts related to the molecular simulation data were generated using OriginPro. Protein and membrane visualizations were created using VMD and PyMOL.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data represent mean ± SEM. All experiments were performed with at least three independent replicates. Analyses used GraphPad Prism 10: two-tailed unpaired t-tests and paired t-tests (two groups). Significance was defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001, while “ns” indicates no significance (P > 0.05). Biological replicates are specified in figure legends. All schematic diagrams and cartoons presented in this study were originally created by our team using Adobe Illustrator (version 2021).
