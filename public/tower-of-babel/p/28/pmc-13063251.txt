Synaptic density imaging in behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia: A comparison of 1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 and 1 8F‐FDG PET
Authors: Arman Fesharaki‐Zadeh, Salih Cayir, Waleed Ibrahim, Yanghong Yang, Jean‐Dominique Gallezot, Mika Naganawa, Yanjun Wu, Takuya Toyonaga, Faranak Ebrahimian Sadabad, Ryan O'Dell, Brian Pittman, Nabeel Nabulsi, Stephen Strittmatter, Richard E Carson, Yiyun Huang, Christopher H van Dyck, Adam P Mecca, David Matuskey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONSynapse loss, a key feature of behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD), can now be visualized in vivo using positron emission tomography (PET) that targets synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A (SV2A). Comparing this new imaging method with the clinical standard of glucose metabolism using 1 8F‐fluorodeocyglucose (1 8F‐FDG) PET might improve diagnostic accuracy and disease monitoring in bvFTD.METHODSWe measured synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A (SV2A) binding using 1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 PET and glucose metabolism with 1 8F‐FDG PET in 10 patients with bvFTD and 10 age‐matched healthy controls.RESULTSSV2A binding was significantly lower in the frontal and temporal cortices, anterior cingulate, and insula in bvFTD patients, with more widespread and pronounced differences than those observed with 1 8F‐FDG PET. SV2A binding in the frontal cortex and insula significantly correlated with the Frontal Assessment Battery scores.DISCUSSIONOur findings suggest that, in bvFTD, the degree of lower synaptic density, as measured by SV2A PET, is larger than the degree of hypometabolism as measured by 1 8F‐FDG PET. SV2A imaging demonstrates considerable promise as a new in vivo biomarker for bvFTD.Keywords: 1 8F‐SynVesT‐1, 1 8F‐FDG, frontotemporal dementia, synaptic density, synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A

Background

Behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD) is a neurodegenerative condition characterized by early impairments in social cognition and behavioral symptoms such as disinhibition, compulsions, apathy, and changes in eating behavior. 1 , 2 It represents the most common subtype of FTD, accounting for approximately 5% to 15% of all dementia cases and has a prevalence comparable to Alzheimer's disease (AD) among individuals under 65 years of age. 3 , 4 Despite its significant burden and high prevalence, diagnosing bvFTD remains challenging due to symptom overlap with other psychiatric and neurological disorders, and the lack of a clearly defined diagnostic algorithm or disease‐specific biomarker. 5 , 6 , 7 Consequently, the diagnosis of nearly half of bvFTD cases are initially mistaken for other psychiatric conditions, resulting in an average delay of 5 to 6 years before the accurate diagnosis is made. 8

Structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI) and 18F‐fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (18F‐FDG PET) are neuroimaging tools currently used for bvFTD diagnosis. 2 sMRI may demonstrate frontal and anterior temporal atrophy; however, sMRI findings are subtle or normal in approximately half of early‐stage bvFTD cases. 7 , 9 , 10 Characteristic frontal and anterior temporal hypometabolism observed via 18F‐FDG PET can also aid in distinguishing bvFTD from AD. 11 , 12 Indeed, integrating 18F‐FDG PET into diagnostic protocols has improved diagnostic accuracy by detecting regional metabolic deficits preceding sMRI changes, 13 leading to widespread use for evaluating cognitive dysfunction of unclear etiology. 14 Nevertheless, 18F‐FDG PET is a nonspecific indicator of functional activity, reflecting overall glucose uptake rather than directly measuring neuronal or synaptic loss. 15

While metabolic reductions generally correlate with neurodegeneration, 18F‐FDG may be confounded by factors such as reactive glial activation during neuroinflammation, potentially masking early neuronal dysfunction. 15 Additionally, external influences including sensory stimulation, medication effects, blood glucose levels, and inadequate fasting can yield misleading or false‐positive results. 16 , 17 , 18 Hence, “the ideal biomarker for bvFTD is still missing”, highlighting the ongoing need for more robust and disease‐specific neuroimaging biomarkers. 19

The proteinopathic alterations, predominantly tau and transactive DNA‐binding protein (TDP‐43), and less commonly fused in sarcoma protein (FUS), 20 , 21 , 22 disrupt synaptic function early in disease progression, and synapses provide a possible new biomarker target. Indeed, synaptic loss is considered a hallmark pathology in bvFTD, potentially occurring decades prior to symptom onset. 20 Thus, assessing synaptic density in vivo could potentially enable an earlier and more precise diagnosis.

A novel and promising approach to measure synaptic density involves synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A (SV2A), a protein located on presynaptic terminals and expressed in nearly all brain synapses. 23 SV2A PET radiotracers, including 11C‐UCB‐J and 18F‐SynVesT‐1, facilitate quantification of synaptic density in the human brain. 24 , 25 , 26 This advancement has sparked significant interest with distinct lower synaptic patterns identified across various neurodegenerative disorders, including AD, 27 , 28 Parkinson's disease, 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 Lewy body dementia (LBD), 33 and recently bvFTD. 34 Malpetti et al. (2023) provided the first in vivo evidence of synaptic loss in bvFTD using SV2A PET. Their study of clinically probable bvFTD patients demonstrated significantly lower SV2A binding compared to controls. This predominantly affected frontal areas and to a lesser extent the temporal regions, insula, and anterior cingulate, aligning with known bvFTD atrophy and hypometabolism patterns. Notably, synaptic alterations detected via SV2A PET surpassed corresponding MRI‐derived structural changes, suggesting synaptic imaging might be more sensitive to underlying pathological processes. However, to date, no studies have directly compared SV2A PET with the established imaging standard, 18F‐FDG PET, within the same individuals.

Here, we present the first head‐to‐head comparison of SV2A PET imaging using 18F‐SynVesT‐1 and 18F‐FDG PET in bvFTD patients and healthy controls (HC). We hypothesized that: (1) 1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 binding would be significantly lower in primary bvFTD‐related brain regions of interest (ROIs) in participants with bvFTD compared to HCs, and (2) these reductions in 1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 binding would surpass those observed with 1 8F‐FDG PET. Our findings provide critical insights into optimal biomarkers for the early diagnosis and monitoring of bvFTD, carrying significant translational implications for future clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and participants

Potential participants, either diagnosed with bvFTD or HC, underwent screening and diagnostic evaluations to determine eligibility. All participants were excluded for major medical or psychiatric illnesses (not related to bvFTD). Participants with bvFTD were required to fulfill established diagnostic criteria for probable bvFTD 2 and to have a Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score of 26 or lower. 35 Research clinical measures, such as the Frontal Assessment Battery (FAB) were also performed. Probable bvFTD diagnosis required positive neuroimaging evidence from structural T1‐weighted MRI and 18F‐FDG PET, based on the consensus visual assessment of three experienced readers (A.F.Z., A.P.M., and D.M.). Additionally, as part of their clinical diagnostic workup all participants with bvFTD underwent lumbar punctures to measure cerebrospinal fluid Aβ42/Aβ40 ratios to exclude AD pathogenesis. Three participants were excluded from the bvFTD group: one with a MoCA score of 29 and negative neuroimaging evidence, another diagnosed with semantic variant primary progressive aphasia, and one who withdrew post‐screening. HC participants were primarily recruited from observational studies at the Yale Alzheimer's Disease Research Unit (ADRU). 36 Inclusion criteria required normal cognitive functioning demonstrated by a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score 37 of 0 and Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score of 27 or higher. 38 Additionally, nine of the 10 HC participants underwent amyloid imaging with Pittsburgh compound B (PiB), of which eight were negative. The study protocol was approved by the Yale University Human Investigation Committee and Radiation Safety Committee. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

PET imaging experiments

18F‐SynVesT‐1 was synthesized following previously established procedures. 24 , 39 All participants underwent a single dynamic PET scan using 18F‐SynVesT‐1 on a High‐Resolution Research Tomograph (Siemens), achieving a reconstructed image resolution of approximately 3 mm as detailed previously. 25 , 40 List‐mode data were reconstructed using the MOLAR algorithm, which incorporates event‐by‐event motion correction based on real‐time head tracking from an optical detector (Vicra, NDI Systems). 41 Additionally, dynamic 18F‐FDG PET scans were performed for up to 90 minutes. T1‐weighted MRI scans were obtained for all participants using a 3‐T whole‐body scanner (Prisma; Siemens) to facilitate PET image coregistration.

Quantitative analysis

Summed PET images were first coregistered with each participant's T1‐weighted MRI and subsequently aligned to a standardized MRI template. Primary (frontal cortex, temporal cortex, anterior cingulate, insula) and secondary (striatum, hippocampus, thalamus) ROIs were defined using the FreeSurfer 6.0 atlas based on the Desikan–Killiany cortical parcellation. 42 To minimize bias related to individual differences in gray matter volume, partial volume correction (PVC) was performed using the Iterative Yang algorithm. 43

For 18F‐SynVesT‐1, the primary outcome measure was non‐displaceable binding potential (BP ND), calculated using the simplified reference tissue model 2 (SRTM2) as previously described. 40 Parametric BP ND images were generated using the centrum semiovale (CS) as the reference region. Following the methodology described in, 40 a population‐derived k2’ value was applied. In the present study, the mean k2’ across a subset of 8 bvFTD participants was 0.036 and was used in all analyses. Additionally, the one‐tissue compartment (1TC) model (using 0‐ to 120‐minute data) with a metabolite‐corrected arterial plasma input function was applied to generate parametric images of distribution volume (V T) for the participants with available arterial input data (bvFTD: n = 8; HC: n = 3). The V T of the CS was highly similar across bvFTD (mean [standard deviation {SD}] = 2.99 (0.28) mL/cm3) and healthy control participants (mean (SD) = 3.03 (0.13) mL/cm3), with a percent difference of 1.5%, p‐value of 0.74, supporting the suitability of this region as a reference (Supplemental Figure 1). 1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 SUVR values were also calculated using the CS as the reference region and are presented in the Supplementary Material (Supplemental Table 1), showing results similar to BP ND and supporting the validity of this model and consistency with this region as a reference. Parametric images (60–90 minutes) for all participants, shown in their native space, are included in the Supplemental Figure 2.

For 18F‐FDG PET, standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) images were generated by summing frames from 60 to 90 minutes post‐injection and normalizing to the cerebellum. 44 , 45 The SUV (60–90 minutes) of cerebellum was also similar across bvFTD (mean (SD) = 5.29 (1.5) mL/cm3) and healthy control participants (mean (SD) = 5.45 (0.92) mL/cm3), with a percent difference of 3%, p‐value of 0.48. As a confirmation analysis, SUVR values were also calculated using the brainstem as the reference region, yielding highly similar results to those obtained with cerebellar normalization (Supplemental Table 2). Parametric images (60–90 minutes) for all participants, shown in their native space, are included in Supplemental Figure 3.

In addition to primary ROI analyses, voxel‐wise comparisons of BP ND and SUVR images between bvFTD and HC groups were conducted using Statistical Parametric Mapping version 12 (SPM12; Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, London, UK). Parametric BP ND and SUVR images were resliced to 2‐mm isotropic voxels and smoothed with a 6‐mm full width at half maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel. Confirmatory voxel‐wiseanalyses were performed with a cluster extent threshold of 50 voxels at a statistical threshold of p < 0.001 (uncorrected). Individual voxel‐wise z‐score maps for 1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 and 1 8F‐FDG in bvFTD participants (z = (bvFTD − meanHC) / SDHC) are provided in Supplemental Figure 4. MRI volumetric analyses of cortical and subcortical gray matter volumes were conducted using FreeSurfer version 6 (Massachusetts General Hospital).

Statistical analysis

Prior to analysis, data were summarized using descriptive statistics (mean, SD, range) and examined with graphical methods (e.g., histograms, normal probability plots) to assess distributional assumptions. 1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 and 1 8F‐FDG data from the primary ROIs were analyzed using linear mixed‐effects models. Each model included diagnostic group (bvFTD vs. HC) as a between‐subjects factor and brain region (frontal cortex, temporal cortex, anterior cingulate, insula) as a within‐subjects factor, along with a group‐by‐region interaction term. Within‐subject correlations due to repeated measures were modeled by evaluating multiple variance–covariance structures and selecting the best‐fitting structure based on information criteria, and model assumptions were verified by inspecting residual plots. Least‐squares (LS) means were derived from each model, and planned pairwise contrasts were used to compare LS means between groups within each region. The same mixed‐effects modeling approach was applied to secondary ROIs. In a separate analysis, tracer type (1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 vs. 1 8F‐FDG) was included as an additional within‐subject factor, with all interactions involving tracer (group × tracer, region × tracer, and group × region × tracer) included to assess tracer‐specific effects. Two‐sample Student's t‐tests were used to compare cognitive scores (MoCA/MMSE) and age between participants with bvFTD and HC, while Fisher's exact test was used to compare sex distribution between groups. The correlation analyses between FAB scores and regional values were calculated using Pearson's partial correlations, controlling for age and sex, and were conducted as exploratory analyses. All tests were two‐sided with a significance threshold of α = 0.05, and analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC). False discovery rate (FDR) correction (Benjamini–Hochberg) was applied to all post hoc comparisons to control for multiple testing; correlation analyses were not FDR‐corrected due to their exploratory nature.

1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 shows lower synaptic density in bvFTD in primary bvFTD‐related ROIs

Consistent with our hypothesis, 1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 binding was significantly lower in primary bvFTD‐related brain ROIs in participants with bvFTD compared to HCs (group main effect: F 1,18 = 15.6, p < 0.001). As depicted in Figure 1 and shown in Table 2, significantly lower SV2A‐specific binding in the bvFTD group was observed in the frontal cortex (–26%, p = 0.003), temporal cortex (–22%, p = 0.009), anterior cingulate (–32%, p = 0.004), and insula (–22%, p = 0.006). A significant group × region interaction was observed (F 3,18 = 3.7, p = 0.032), with the largest effects observed in the frontal cortex and anterior cingulate.

Analysis of secondary brain regions

In an analysis of secondary ROIs (Table 2) using a similar linear mixed‐effects model, a significant main effect of group (F 1,18 = 13.51, p = 0.001) was observed. Participants with bvFTD exhibited significantly lower SV2A‐specific binding in the striatum (–32%, p = 0.001), hippocampus (–29%, p = 0.002), and thalamus (–23%, p = 0.010). The group × region interaction was significant (F 3,18 = 3.7, p = 0.031), with the largest effects observed in the striatum.

1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 PET reveals larger group differences than 1 8F‐FDG PET in primary bvFTD‐related regions

To compare the group differences associated with standardized levels within each tracer, we incorporated tracer as an additional within‐subjects factor in the mixed‐effects model above. A significant group x tracer interaction (F 1,126 = 8.3, p < 0.01) was observed, reflecting larger group differences across all primary regions using 1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 compared to 1 8F‐FDG PET within the same participants. Likewise, Cohen's d revealed a larger effect size (d = 1.77) for 1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 compared to 1 8F‐FDG PET (d = 1.30) in combined primary regions. This was also observed in all individual primary and secondary ROIs (Table 2) and can be appreciated with examination of averaged parametric images and averaged z‐score maps of 1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 (BP ND) and 1 8F‐FDG PET (SUVR, 60–90 minutes) for each group (Figure 2). As an additional visualization, a scatterplot of effect sizes (Cohen's d) comparing the two tracers across both primary and secondary ROIs shows that all points lie below a line of identity, confirming larger effect sizes to detect group differences across all regions when using 1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 compared to 1 8F‐FDG PET (Figure 3). A confirmatory whole‐brain, voxel‐wise analysis of group differences in 1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 (Figure 4A) and 1 8F‐FDG PET (Figure 4B) was performed, which is consistent with significant group differences for both tracers, but with a larger and more extensive pattern of differences using 18F‐SynVesT‐1 PET. Volumetric MRI analysis (Supplemental Table 3) mostly showed lower volumes in bvFTD participants, with significant differences in the temporal cortex (17%, p = 0.014) and hippocampus (14%, p = 0.012).

Discussion

This study provides the first in vivo evidence of lower synaptic density in bvFTD as measured by 1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 PET. Furthermore, group differences in synaptic density were more pronounced than in metabolism, assessed by 1 8F‐FDG PET, in the same individuals. Specifically, we observed significantly lower SV2A binding (22%–32%) across key bvFTD‐affected regions—including the frontal cortex, anterior cingulate, insula, and temporal cortex—that exceeded the corresponding lower metabolism (10%–19%) detected with 1 8F‐FDG PET. These findings align closely with those of Malpetti et al., 34 who used 1 1C‐UCB‐J PET and similarly reported widespread synaptic loss in frontal, insular, cingulate, and medial temporal regions. The striking anatomical concordance of lower synaptic density across two independent cohorts, using different SV2A tracers (1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 and 1 1C‐UCB‐J), highlights synaptic density imaging as a robust and defining neuropathological feature of bvFTD. This has important implications for understanding disease mechanisms and developing imaging biomarkers.

As 1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 PET selectively binds to SV2A, a protein ubiquitously expressed at virtually all presynaptic terminals throughout the brain, 23 , 25 lower SV2A PET binding may directly reflect synaptic loss. In bvFTD this is likely caused by a sequelae of the accumulation of pathological misfolded proteins—such as tau, TDP‐43, or FUS—that are characteristic of the disease. 46 In contrast, 1 8F‐FDG PET measures glucose metabolism as a functional proxy for neuronal activity, but its signal is influenced by a range of biological confounders. 15 Notably, glucose uptake by activated microglia, oligodendrocytes, and astrocytes contributes substantially to regional 1 8F‐FDG signal, particularly in the cerebral cortex, where non‐neuronal cells comprise approximately 72% of the total cell population. 47 In the context of bvFTD, this glial contribution may represent a compensatory response that partially masks underlying neuronal and synaptic loss, potentially resulting in attenuated 1 8F‐FDG PET findings. This is supported by our regional and voxel‐based analyses (Table 2, Figure 4), which reveal more extensive synaptic deficits than metabolic abnormalities.

An alternative hypothesis posits that synaptic dysfunction and loss, as reflected by lower SV2A PET binding may precede the metabolic alterations detected by 1 8F‐FDG PET. The underlying mechanism could involve neuroinflammatory processes, which have been increasingly implicated in disease pathogenesis, 48 , 49 and the link between neuroinflammation and synaptic loss is well established in both preclinical and human studies. 50 Although the temporal sequence of pathological events in bvFTD remains less clearly defined, it is plausible that inflammation‐induced synaptic degeneration represents an early pathological event that subsequently contributes to broader metabolic dysregulation. Supporting this notion, early synaptic loss in presymptomatic C9orf72 mutation carriers, demonstrated by lower [1 1C]UCB‐J PET binding in the thalamus compared to HCs, provides further evidence for this proposed sequence. 51

Another factor contributing to the discordance between SV2A and 18 F‐FDG PET signals may involve regional differences in baseline glucose consumption. A recent dual‐tracer study using SV2A and 18 F‐FDG PET by Van Aalst et al. (2021) in healthy individuals demonstrated up to 19% higher relative 1 8F‐FDG uptake in cortical areas with high resting energy demands, particularly in the frontal cortex. 52 Given the central involvement of the frontal cortex in bvFTD, these findings are especially relevant‐ elevated metabolic activity driven by aerobic glycolysis may partially obscure early synaptic loss when assessed with 1 8F‐FDG. 53 , 54 , 55 Taken together, these mechanistic considerations—including non‐neuronal glucose uptake, and regional metabolic variability—underscore the complementary but distinct roles of SV2A and 1 8F‐FDG PET in evaluating neurodegeneration related to bvFTD.

SV2A and 1 8F‐FDG PET comparative studies have previously been conducted in AD and LBD. 33 , 56 In contrast to our findings in bvFTD, these earlier investigations generally report comparable or even greater magnitudes of hypometabolism relative to lower synaptic density across distinct brain regions in both disorders. The divergence between our results and prior studies in AD and LBD may be explained by two non‐mutually exclusive hypotheses: bvFTD may involve more severe or widespread early synaptic degeneration, or it may feature relative preservation of cortical metabolic activity during early disease stages as discussed above.

Supporting the first hypothesis, a recent systematic review 57 compiling SV2A PET studies across multiple neurodegenerative disorders reported that observed synaptic density differences were notably higher in bvFTD than in other neurodegenerative disorders (AD, LBD, or Parkinson's disease). Although the bvFTD data were drawn from a single cohort (Malpetti et al.), 34 these findings align with our results and suggest that synaptic loss in bvFTD may be more pronounced and extensive than in other conditions studied to date. One plausible explanation for this pattern lies in the distinct spatial and temporal dynamics of disease progression. Neurodegeneration in AD typically begins in the medial temporal lobe and particularly the entorhinal cortex, with atrophy and hypometabolism spreading gradually to association cortical regions. 58 , 59 In contrast, bvFTD is characterized by early and rapid degeneration of the frontal and anterior temporal regions, with cortical atrophy rates reported to be up to twice as fast as those in AD. 60 This accelerated progression may therefore capture a disease stage in which synaptic degeneration is already substantial, even in the early clinical phases.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the modest sample size (10 bvFTD patients and 10 HC participants) may not fully reflect the clinical, genetic, pathological heterogeneity of bvFTD. Importantly, diagnoses were based on clinical criteria without genetic or neuropathological confirmation. Given the diversity of underlying proteinopathies in bvFTD—including tau, TDP‐43, and FUS—the burden and distribution of synaptic loss may vary substantially across molecular subtypes. The present study did not include genetic profiling of individual participants; therefore, inherent genetic variability may have influenced the findings. This limitation underscores the need for larger‐scale studies incorporating comprehensive genetic characterization with disease duration and pathological validation to clarify the specificity of synaptic alterations in bvFTD. A second methodological consideration involves the use of the CS as the reference region for quantifying 18F‐SynVesT‐1 binding.

Although prior studies in AD 28 , 56 and other neurodegenerative disorders 26 , 29 , 61 support its use, the appropriateness of CS in bvFTD requires further validation. However, when we compared the V T values of CS in a subset of patients (n = 8) there was no significant difference between bvFTD and HC groups, supporting its suitability as a reference region. Different reference regions were used for the two tracers (CS for SV2A PET and cerebellum for 1 8F‐FDG PET). Although this is standard for SV2A PET, the cerebellum may be less ideal as a reference region for 1 8F‐FDG PET in bvFTD, as cerebellar gray matter can be affected in certain subtypes. However, in our sample, cerebellar SUV (60–90 minutes) did not differ between bvFTD and HC, with only a 3% difference (p = 0.48), supporting its suitability in this cohort. Moreover, SUVR values calculated using the brainstem as an alternative reference region yielded highly similar results (Supplementary Table 2), indicating that the primary findings are robust to the choice of reference region. Additionally, while 18F‐SynVesT‐1 has favorable imaging properties, such as high specific binding and longer radioactive half‐life, 62 its application in bvFTD remains novel, and further research is needed to establish its reproducibility. Furthermore, the study participants all had positive imaging findings on MRI and 1 8F‐FDG PET, which would exclude patients in the early “negative imaging” stage of the disease process and could in turn affect the generalizability of the study.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provides the first in vivo evidence that lower synaptic density in bvFTD, as measured by 1 8F‐SynVesT‐1 SV2A PET, exceeds glucose hypometabolism measured with 1 8F‐FDG PET in the same individuals. These findings align with prior SV2A PET studies and extend them through direct within‐subject comparison, suggesting that synaptic degeneration may precede or surpass metabolic dysfunction in bvFTD. While promising, these results warrant confirmation in larger cohorts. As precision diagnostics and targeted interventions evolve, SV2A PET may offer a robust, sensitive biomarker for disease staging, treatment monitoring, and clinical trial endpoints in bvFTD.
