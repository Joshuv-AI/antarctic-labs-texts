A Comparative Analysis of Pig-to-Rhesus Corneal Xenotransplantation with Various Immunosuppressive Regimens
Authors: Seung Hyeun Lee, Chang Ho Yoon, Hyuk Jin Choi, Hyun Ju Lee, Hee Jung Kang, Jong Min Kim, Chung-Gyu Park, Mee Kum Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePrevious studies had made efforts to find the optimal regimens to reduce xenogeneic rejection in corneal xenotransplantation. However, it had not been fully determined which immunosuppressive choice would be optimal for long-term graft survival. We aimed to compare clinical results in wild-type pig-to-rhesus corneal xenotransplantation with various immunosuppressive regimens.MethodsMedical records of 23 Chinese rhesus macaques who had received full-thickness corneal transplantation with wild-type pigs were retrospectively analyzed. They were administered various immunosuppressants including steroid, tacrolimus, anti-CD20 antibody, costimulation blockade (CoB) or their combinations. Graft survival, central corneal thickness, changes in plasma anti-αGal and donor-specific antibodies, aqueous complement levels, and blood effector and memory T/B cell subsets were compared between the groups with different regimens in the follow-up of 6 months.ResultsGraft survival time of anti-CD20 antibody (aCD20), anti-CD40 antibody (aCD40), or anti-CD154 antibody (aCD154)-based regimen group was significantly longer than that of steroid (S) or steroid and tacrolimus (S + T) group. Interferon gamma T cell responses, both anti-αGal immunoglobulin G (IgG) and donor-specific IgG levels, along with complement responses, were effectively managed under both the aCD20 and CoB regimens. Compared with aCD154 group, aCD40 group showed higher donor-specific IgG responses and aqueous C3a level. B and activated B cell concentrations were significantly lower in aCD20 group than in S, S + T, or CoB group.ConclusionsIt suggests that an aCD20-based regimen produces clinical outcomes comparable to those of an aCD154-based regimen in wild-type pig-to-rhesus corneal xenotransplantation, whereas an aCD40-based regimen does not.Keywords: Anti-CD154 antibody, Anti-CD40 antibody, Cornea, Rituximab, Xenotransplantation

Recent progress in gene-editing methods and immunosuppressive strategies has been increasing the feasibility of clinical pig-to-human xenotransplantation [1–9]. However, immune rejection of xenograft is still not overcome with multiple gene edition of the donor pig, and immunotolerance or immunosuppressive regimens are still on demand [10–12]. After xenotransplantation, three main types of rejection are as follows: hyperacute, acute humoral, and acute cellular rejection [1,13]. Hyperacute and acute humoral rejection can be reduced by using plasmapheresis and genetically modified pigs; however, complement inhibitor is still required to inhibit innate immunity [14]. Whereas, to attenuate acute and chronic cellular rejection, variable immunosuppressive therapies including those blocking costimulatory signals of CD40–CD154 and CD28/CTLA4–CD80/86 interactions have been introduced [10–12,14–18].

Since corneal disease is one of the significant causes of blindness, affecting millions of people worldwide, xenotransplantation can be one of the options for visual rehabilitation [19,20]. As to xenogeneic rejection of corneal xenotransplantation, hyperacute rejection is not seen [21,22]. However, systemic immunosuppression is still demanded in full-thickness corneal xenotransplantation to avoid the acute and chronic rejection [21,22]. Our group had previously reported preclinical efficacy of corneal xenotransplantation using wild-type pig in nonhuman primates with various immunosuppression regimens. In penetrating keratoplasty, our findings revealed a 100% graft survival rate for the anti-CD154 antibody (Ab)-based regimen over 6 months. Meanwhile, anti-CD20 Ab- or anti-CD40 Ab-based regimen resulted in a 72% to 83% graft survival rate [23,24]. With avascularity and immune-privileged characteristics of cornea [25], there have been reported relatively high survival rates of xenografts of cornea compared with those of other xeno-organs [20].

Although anti-CD154 Ab-based regimen is the most efficient immunomodulatory protocol in xenotransplantation, it has not been clinically available due to thromboembolism [14,26]. Therefore, we need to investigate clinically available 2nd best option for clinical trials of corneal xenotransplantation. Considering that cornea is an immune-privileged site, it is possible that milder immunomodulatory approaches, without the use of anti-CD154 Ab, may be effective even in the context of xenotransplantation. We aimed to compare the clinical and immunological outcomes of wild-type pig-to-rhesus corneal xenotransplantation, utilizing five distinct immunosuppressive regimens.

Materials and Methods

Ethics statement

All previous studies in our laboratory followed the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee (No. 11-0152, 15-0171).

Donors and recipients

Corneas from wild-type pigs were transplanted into right eyes of 23 Chinese rhesus macaques. Surgical procedures were conducted as described previously (PKP sizing of 7.5 to 8.0 mm-sized graft into 7.0 to 7.5 mm-sized recipient’s bed), and both preoperative and postoperative examinations were conducted with the same protocols, except for the types of immunosuppressive regimens [23,24]. All 23 subjects, regardless of rejection episodes, were included in this study, and the follow-up data of 6 months were fully examined. The experiments were not conducted simultaneously but sequentially, and the results were retrospectively analyzed from the entire records.

Immunosuppressive regimens

There were five groups of different immunosuppressive regimens as shown in Table 1: (1) steroid (S) only; (2) steroid plus tacrolimus (S + T); (3) anti-CD20 Ab (aCD20); (4) anti-CD40 Ab (aCD40); and (5) anti-CD154 Ab (aCD154). Cosimulation blocker (CoB) group included both aCD40 and aCD154 groups. The immunosuppressive regimens employed were as previously described [21,23,24]. For steroid group, topical, subconjunctival, and systemic steroid injection was used with the following protocol; 1% prednisolone acetate eyedrop (Pred Forte, Allergan) was administered topically once a day for 3 months, dexamethasone (JW Pharmaceutical) 1.5 mg/0.3 mL was injected subconjunctival once a week for 6 months, and methyl prednisolone (Solu-medrol, Pfizer) 2 mg/kg/day was injected intramuscularly over 5 weeks with tapering dose. The steroid group served as the control, and all medications administered to this group were uniformly applied across other groups. For S + T group, tacrolimus (Prograf, Astellas Pharma US) was added with the dose of 0.035 mg/kg twice daily for 4 weeks, and basiliximab (Simulect, Novartis Pharmaceuticals Corp.) was administered intravenously at a dose of 0.3 mg/kg on postoperative day 0 and 4. For aCD20 group, as an induction, 0.3 mg/kg basiliximab was administered on postoperative day 0 and 4. Anti-CD20 Ab (MabThera, Hoffmann-La Roche) 20 mg/kg was applied on postoperative day 0, 7, and every 2 months with tacrolimus 0.05 mg/kg twice daily for 4 weeks. Subsequently, the dosage of tacrolimus was adjusted to range between 0.035 mg/kg and 0.05 mg/kg to maintain target whole blood trough levels between 5 and 20 ng/mL for maintenance therapy. The aCD154 group received 15 to 19 intravenous injections of recombinant anti-CD154 Ab (V-regions from mouse 5C8 clone, C-regions human immunoglobulin [Ig] G1κ) at a dose of 20 mg/kg during the 6-month posttransplant period. In the aCD40 group, a mouse-rhesus chimeric monoclonal anti-CD40 Ab (2C10R4, NIH Nonhuman Primate Reagent Resource) was administered intravenously 15 times over 6 months, consisting of 50 mg/kg for the first eight doses followed by 30 mg/kg for the remaining seven doses. For the aCD20, aCD154, and aCD40 groups, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was uniformly administered as induction therapy at a dose of 1 g/kg on postoperative day 0 and at 2 weeks.

Comparative analysis of various clinical and laboratory parameters

For analyzing posttransplant status, both clinical ophthalmologic parameters and immune-related parameters of blood and aqueous humor were evaluated. For clinical ophthalmologic evaluation, slit-lamp biomicroscopy examination was done to evaluate graft status, particularly for clarity, edema, and neovascularization. Each item was graded on a scale from 0 to 4, allowing for a potential total score of up to 12 [27]. Grafts with scores greater than 6 were considered to be rejected [27,28]. To support a clinical trial, preclinical nonhuman primate data require a survival duration of more than 6 months for corneal xenografts in at least five out of eight consecutive nonhuman primates, representing a success rate of 62.5% for 6-month survival [27]. Also, long-term graft survival was compared between every two groups using Kaplan-Meier survival curves. Center corneal thickness was measured using ultrasound pachymeter (Quantel Medical), and intraocular pressure (IOP) was measured with tonopen (Medtronic Solan). Corneal mean refractive power was measured at sacrifice, using anterior-segment optical coherence tomography (Visante, Carl Zeiss Meditec). These ophthalmologic parameters were compared at postoperative 2 weeks, 1 month, and 6 months.

Peripheral blood and aqueous humor were collected and analyzed preoperatively and postoperatively. Data at baseline, postoperative 2 weeks, 1 month, and 6 months were compared in this study. Compared immunologic data are as follows: pig-specific IgM/IgG Abs, anti-αGal IgM/IgG Abs, aqueous humor C3a concentration, specific T and B cell results.

For determining plasma concentrations of donor pig-specific IgM/IgG Abs, flow cross-match technique was used, targeting donor peripheral blood mononuclear cells [23,29]. Concentrations of donor pig-specific Abs were semi-quantitatively expressed as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI). The level of Ab binding was determined as net mean fluorescence intensity (nMFI) [23]. nMFI values were calculated by subtracting MFI values of porcine plasma (negative control) from MFI value of the sample [23]. Plasma anti-αGal IgM/IgG Abs and anti-nonGal IgG Ab responses were measured using flow cytometry (FACSCanto, BD Biosciences) with α1, 3-galactosyltransferase gene knockout porcine endothelial cell lines (PEC69) [30]. The concentration of C3a in aqueous humor was assessed, using commercial ELISA kit (BD OptEIA Human C3a ELISA Kit, BD Biosciences).

T and B cell data were acquired with flow cytometry as previously described [23,24,30]. CD4+/CD8+ interferon gamma (IFNγ)+ T cell, CD4+/CD8+ CD28+CD95+ central memory T cell, CD4+/CD8+ CD28−CD95+ effector memory T cell, CD3−CD20+ B cell, CD3−CD20+CD28+ activated B cell were analyzed. Following antibodies were used; anti-CD3 Ab (1:200, Abcam), anti-CD4 Ab (1:50, Novusbio), anti-CD8 Ab (1:150, Abcam), anti-CD20 Ab (1:100, eBioscience), anti-CD28 Ab (1:200, eBioscience), anti-CD95 Ab (1:200, eBioscience). Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software v10 (Tree Star).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism ver. 10 (GraphPad Prism Inc.) was used. For graft survival, Kaplan-Meier survival curve and log-rank test was used to assess the statistical difference between every two groups. For comparative analysis of clinical and laboratory parameters, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn post hoc test or Mann-Whitney test was used for inter-group analysis, and Friedman test with Dunn post hoc test or Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test was used for intra-group analysis. We further calculated the proportional changes (in %) compared with the baseline, calculated as “(data - baseline data) / baseline data × 100” for aCD20, aCD40, and aCD154 groups, and compared the values. p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Immunosuppressive regimens and subject number of each groups undergoing pig-to-rhesus penetrating corneal keratoplasty are shown in Table 1. In steroid and S + T groups, all subjects showed rejection before 6 months. In aCD20 group, one graft showed rejection at day 134 and one subject died before 6 months without graft rejection, and other five grafts survived over 6 months. To calculate the graft survival rates accurately, instance of sudden death without rejection was excluded from the analysis. In aCD40 group, one graft showed rejection at day 41, while others survived more than 6 months, and in aCD154 group, all grafts survived more than 6 months. Graft survival was compared between every two groups, and Kaplan-Meier survival curves are shown in Fig. 1. Survival duration of aCD20 (p < 0.001), aCD40 (p = 0.003), and aCD154 (p = 0.004) groups was significantly longer than steroid group, while there was no difference between S + T and steroid group (p = 0.063). Survival duration of aCD20 (p = 0.02), aCD40 (p = 0.002), and aCD154 (p = 0.002) groups were also significantly longer than S + T group. There were no differences between aCD20 and aCD40 groups (p = 0.063), between aCD20 and aCD154 groups (p = 0.502), between aCD40 and aCD154 groups (p = 0.886). The 6-month survival rates for the corneal xenografts were 83.3% in aCD20 group, 83.3% in aCD40 group, and 100% in aCD154 group. Comparative analysis results of immunologic parameters are shown in Figs. 3 and 4. The immunologic profile data for the steroid group was recorded only up to 1 month, whereas the other groups exhibited their immunologic profiles up to 6 months. There were no significant differences of MFI of donor-specific IgM (Fig. 3A), while MFI of donor-specific IgG and anti-αGal IgG increased in steroid group, which were closely correlated with the progression of rejection (Fig. 3B, 3C). MFI of donor-specific IgG had the tendency to increase in all groups, but it was significantly lower in aCD20 and CoB groups at 1 month, compared with steroid group. MFI of anti-αGal IgG increased in CoB group compared with preoperative data, but it stayed significantly lower than the steroid group at 1 month. Regarding MFI of anti-nonGal Ab, it was lower in aCD20 group than in CoB group at preoperation, and postoperative 1 month; however there were no differences at 6 months (Fig. 3D). In comparison to the responses of anti-αGal IgG, anti-nonGal Ab responses were minimal across all groups. Therefore, subsequent analyses primarily focused on anti-αGal and donor-specific IgG responses as representative humoral immune responses. Aqueous C3a was significantly lower in aCD20 and CoB group than in steroid group at 1 month (Fig. 3E). For T and B cell analysis, there were no significant increases in the concentrations of IFNγ-secreting CD4+ and CD8+ T cells among the groups (Fig. 4A, 4B). Concentrations of B cells and activated B cells were sustained significantly lower in aCD20 groups compared with those in both S + T and CoB groups (Fig. 4C, 4D). There were no significant differences in memory T cell levels among the groups; however, a notable increase in the CD8+ effector memory T cell population was observed across all groups (Fig. 4E–4H). These findings suggest that the CD8+ effector memory T cell population is refractory to suppression by either tacrolimus or costimulatory blockade. However, the clinical significance of this increase remains unclear, as it was observed regardless of the occurrence of graft rejection. Data of proportional changes (in % values) compared with the baseline of three successful groups (aCD20, aCD40, aCD154) were further analyzed to assess the comparability of either anti-CD20 Ab or anti-CD40 Ab-based regimen to anti-CD154 Ab-based regimen (Figs. 5, 6). In Fig. 5A–5E, overall antibodies and complement were well controlled in aCD154 group. In aCD40 group, donor-specific IgG and aqueous C3a increased at early period and then were stabilized until 6 months. However, in comparison to the aCD154 group, the changes in the ratios of donor-specific IgG and aqueous complement were greater in the aCD40 group at specific time points. The proportional changes of antibodies and complement were not statistically different between aCD154 and aCD20 groups. For T and B cell analysis, there were no significant ratio changes in the concentrations of IFNγ-secreting CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6A, 6B) and no significant ratio changes in memory T cells among the groups (Fig. 6E–6H). However, ratio of B cells and activated B cells were significantly lower in aCD20 group than in aCD40 group at 2 weeks and 1 month (Fig. 6C, 6D).

Discussion

This study is noteworthy for its comparison of the efficacies of various immunosuppressant regimens in pig-to-rhesus corneal xenotransplantation, specifically utilizing wild-type pig corneas in the context of full-thickness corneal transplantation. Both anti-CD154 Ab and the combination of anti-CD20 Ab with tacrolimus demonstrated comparable survival outcomes, accompanied by effectively regulated T and B cell responses, as well as stable levels of donor-specific IgG, anti-αGal IgG, and aqueous complement C3a. In contrast, anti-CD40 antibodies exhibited reduced efficacy in regulating both donor-specific IgG responses and aqueous complement C3a levels.

Blocking CD40/CD154 costimulatory pathway, which is required for T cell and B cell activation has been recently suggested to be a promising immunosuppressive regimen in xenotransplantation [31,32], and it also showed promising results in our study. Although anti-CD40 Ab and anti-CD154 Ab are known to target the same CD40–CD40 ligand (CD154) costimulatory pathway, anti-CD40 and anti-CD154 Ab have been reported varying clinical results in the xenotransplantation field, with the priority of anti-CD154 as in efficacy [15,31,32]. Whereas, anti-CD40 Ab-based regimen has shown less efficiency in kidney xenotransplantation [33]. In this study, we found that the efficacy of anti-CD40 Ab treatment aligns with previous reports, indicating that this regimen may be less effective in corneal xenotransplantation. Meanwhile, anti-CD154 Ab regimen was also recently shown to be successful in pig-to-nonhuman primate transplantation of islet cells for more than 6 months [34]. Some studies have suggested that anti-CD154 antibodies may exert additional immunomodulatory effects beyond the CD40–CD154 signaling pathway, which could partially contribute to prolonged xenograft survival [35,36]. Although anti-CD154 Ab has been suggested as a promising immunosuppressant in corneal xenotransplantation [23], there had been a serious obstacle in clinical development of the use of anti-CD154 Ab since it showed thrombotic complications [23,26]. Recently, there have been efforts to modify the fragment crystallizable region of anti-CD154 Ab, reducing its interaction with platelets to lower thromboembolic risks [37–39]. Since there became emerging results of modified anti-CD154 Ab with reducing platelet activation while maintaining immunosuppressive efficacy, anti-CD154 Ab could be a promising candidate for the use in corneal xenotransplantation.

Interestingly, anti-CD20 Ab and tacrolimus combined regimen was also proven as effective as anti-CD154 Ab regimen. In aCD20 group, anti-CD20 Ab and tacrolimus were administered. Anti-CD20 antibodies, such as rituximab, target the CD20 proteins found on the surface of B cells [40], and tacrolimus is a calcineurin inhibitor that acts as an immunosuppressant by inhibiting T cell activation [41]. Since both anti-CD20 Ab and tacrolimus are already widely used in preventing allotransplantation rejection in clinical fields with promising efficacies [42,43], this regimen could be considered as the most practical regimen for corneal xenotransplantation with already commercialized agents.

For the clinical use of immunosuppressants, the possibilities of side effects are as crucial as the efficacy. In the previous study, we studied the long-term safety of systemic immunosuppressants in pig-to-rhesus corneal xenotransplantation [44]. Both costimulatory blockade-based and anti-CD20 Ab-based regimens showed tolerable biochemical, electrolyte, and rhesus cytomegalovirus monitoring results until 6 months, except for white blood cell decrease detected in anti-CD154 Ab regimen group and low incidence of infection in anti-CD20 Ab regimen group [44]. Indeed, similar to allotransplantation, infection monitoring is also crucial in corneal xenotransplantation.

There are several limitations of this study. We included 23 subjects in this study, which is a small number to show strong evidence. Due to the practical obstacles of performing rhesus experiments, we included a minimum number of subjects. In addition, the variability in survival duration among individual animals may limit the interpretation of differences between treatment regimens at the group level. Another limitation of the study is that the experiments of different groups were conducted not in a simultaneous manner, and thus the results were not directly compared between groups at the same time. However, each subject adhered to the same protocol for the corneal transplantation procedure and underwent identical preoperative and postoperative measurements, with the exception of the differing immunosuppressive agents used. Therefore, the results can be justified in the context.

A further limitation is that IVIG was administered as an induction therapy in all Ab-based regimens, and therefore its effects could not be separated from those of the other antibodies used. IVIG was intended to neutralize preexisting or newly induced antibodies against pig antigens and to inhibit complement-mediated cytotoxicity. Thus, IVIG may have influenced early Ab and complement responses. In addition, because IVIG preparations contain anti-Gal antibodies, their potential immunologic effects cannot be completely excluded.

In conclusion, this study suggests that the anti-CD20/ tacrolimus-based regimen achieved graft survival outcomes similar to those observed with the anti-CD154-based regimen. Given the clinical limitations associated with anti-CD154 antibodies, the anti-CD20/tacrolimus regimen may represent a clinically feasible alternative for pig-to-rhesus full-thickness corneal xenotransplantation. With the evolving gene-editing technologies to lessen immunity in xenotransplantation and with proven immunosuppressive agents, corneal xenotransplantation may be the reality of upcoming future.
