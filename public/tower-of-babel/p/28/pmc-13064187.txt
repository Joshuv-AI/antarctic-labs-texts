Maintaining longevity and fulfillment in academic surgery
Authors: Leah Aakjar, Mayur Narayan, Grace Rozycki, Adrian Park, Jamie Coleman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcademic surgery is a demanding and multifaceted career that places sustained pressure on clinical performance, scholarly productivity, administrative service, and personal resilience. While the risk of burnout is high, some surgeons achieve long-term fulfillment and professional longevity through intentional strategies. This was a panel discussion with trauma surgeon participants from all career stages that was held at the 2025 annual American College of Surgeons Point Counterpoint conference to discuss how to lead a long and fulfilling career in academic surgery. This manuscript reflects and expands on key themes from that discussion, and while there are no universal guidelines, it will highlight several strategies to foster longevity and fulfillment in an academic career.Keywords: workload, teaching, Research, Psychosocial Support Systems

Introduction

Academic surgery (AS) is among the most demanding and multifaceted careers in medicine. The combination of clinical duties, including maintaining and acquiring new skills, administrative responsibilities, research expectations, teaching roles, and promotion pressures may lead to an unsustainable pace.1 Despite these challenges, some surgeons maintain long-term engagement and fulfillment. These individuals tend to apply deliberate strategies that sustain meaning, focus, and resilience in high-performance environments.

A defining feature among these surgeons is clarity of purpose. Their professional pursuits align not only with institutional goals but with their own internal values. Rather than being driven solely by metrics or peer comparison, their careers are grounded in intrinsic motivation. While there is no universal roadmap, this manuscript includes strategies for fostering longevity and fulfillment in AS.

The panel discussion this manuscript expands on featured trauma surgeon participants across all career stages and took place at the 2025 annual American College of Surgeons Point Counterpoint conference. Participants included senior faculty members Dr. Grace Rozycki and Dr. Adrian Park, mid-career surgeon Dr. Jamie Coleman, and early career and fellow member Dr. Leah Aakjar. The panel was moderated by mid-career surgeon Dr. Mayur Narayan.

Clarifying purpose through a mission statement

The competing demands of AS frequently pull surgeons in multiple directions, risking disconnection from meaningful work. A personal mission statement serves as a reference point for decision-making, promoting alignment between professional activities and individual values, while offering space for reflection on the personal “why.”

Unlike institutional mission statements, personal missions are dynamic and evolve with professional growth and life circumstances. For instance, early-career surgeons may focus on technical skill development and team integration, whereas later stages of a career may focus on mentorship, leadership, or health equity. Regardless of stage, the mission should emerge from intrinsic values rather than the pursuit of external validation. A clear mission also shapes professional identity, or brand, and provides a foundation for career growth.

Effective mission statements are concise, intentional, and rooted in reflection on what sustains engagement and satisfaction. Once articulated, they can be used to evaluate new opportunities—not based on prestige, but on alignment with purpose. This strategic alignment fosters productivity while also promoting sustainability and long-term fulfillment.2

Managing expectations and the strategic use of “no”

Academic culture often equates visibility and service with career advancement. However, participation in committees, speaking engagements, leadership roles, and scholarly reviews can quickly accumulate, fragmenting focus and reducing the quality of work that is most meaningful.

The pressure to accept every opportunity is often driven by misconceptions about promotion criteria or adherence to institutional norms. In practice, sustained and high-impact contributions tend to be valued more than broad but superficial involvement.3 Selectivity signals clarity of purpose and enhances excellence in chosen areas.

Declining professional requests, when done thoughtfully, is not a rejection of responsibility but a strategic decision to preserve time and energy for work that aligns with one’s values. Framing a “no” with respect and, when appropriate, suggesting a colleague for the opportunity can reinforce collegiality while creating space for the growth of others. While the requesting party may initially be disappointed with a “no,” most, if not all, will welcome the honesty about the challenges to deliver high-quality work in the time frame given to finish it.

While reviewing invitations judiciously, there remain areas where surgeons have a duty to serve. Whether it is contributing to educational committees, supporting institutional quality initiatives, or participating in national societies, each surgeon must identify and accept a share of these essential responsibilities. There is, inevitably, some arduous tasks within AS, and part of longevity is recognizing when to take them on while managing expectations.

The ability to say no with confidence is grounded in a well-defined personal mission. With this foundation, external pressures become less persuasive, and decisions become more deliberate. Over time, this approach contributes to a reputation characterized by authenticity, integrity, and focus.

Work-life integration

Sustaining a fulfilling career in AS requires intentional integration of professional and personal responsibilities. The traditional pursuit of “work-life balance” can be misleading, as it implies an ideal state of equilibrium that may be unrealistic in high-acuity, unpredictable fields like AS. Rather than striving for symmetry, surgeons may benefit from a more flexible approach, coined “work-life integration.” This acknowledges the overlap between work and home domains.4

Integration emphasizes quality and intentionality over equal distribution of time. There are periods when professional responsibilities demand full focus like operating, consulting, or leading complex discussions. Equally, moments in personal life require undivided attention. Neither domain can substitute for the other; both require protected time and presence, but some work tasks may be completed at home and home tasks completed at work.

Strategies that support integration include setting boundaries, outsourcing non-essential tasks, and reserving free time for restoration, connection, or purpose-driven activities.5 These practices can mitigate cognitive and emotional fatigue while reinforcing meaning in both spheres.

Ultimately, work-life integration is not about achieving balance, but about investing time in alignment with personal and professional values. By accepting the ebb and flow of demands across roles and giving each the attention it requires at the right time, surgeons can sustain energy, presence, and a sense of fulfillment both inside and outside the hospital.

Mentorship and sponsorship

Mentorship and sponsorship are essential pillars of AS, supporting not only professional advancement but also identity formation, confidence, and long-term resilience. Effective relationships provide perspective, reinforce values, and enhance clarity of purpose.

Mentorship evolves over time. Early in a career, it may center on clinical skill development, research direction, and navigating institutional culture. As roles mature, the dynamic may shift toward collaboration, with mentors serving as peers who offer feedback, perspective, and accountability. Informal or organic mentorship is frequently perceived as particularly meaningful.6

Sponsorship, while distinct, is equally important. Unlike mentorship, sponsorship is characterized by advocacy. Sponsors, typically in senior positions, identify individuals with potential and actively promote them in professional spaces to which they may not yet have access on their own. Through sponsorship, both protégé and sponsor benefit, with the sponsor enhancing their influence as a leader and the protégé gaining critical opportunities for growth and visibility.7

No single individual can fulfill all mentorship or sponsorship needs. A diverse network spanning clinical, academic, administrative, and personal domains offers more comprehensive and nuanced support. At the same time, mentees must take active responsibility for developing and maintaining these relationships. Initiative, consistency, and intentional engagement are essential.

The transition from mentee to mentor, and eventually to sponsor, marks a significant milestone in professional development. Supporting others through teaching, advising, and advocacy reinforces one’s own mission and contributes to the growth of the profession. When rooted in mutual respect and shared values, mentorship and sponsorship serve as sustaining forces that cultivate leadership and a sense of community.

Developing a growth mindset

A growth mindset is the belief that abilities, intelligence, and performance can be developed over time through effort, reflection, and feedback.8 This plays a critical role in sustaining fulfillment in AS. This perspective reframes challenges and setbacks not as personal failures, but as opportunities for improvement and innovation. In a field where precision and excellence are expected, adopting a growth mindset fosters resilience, adaptability, and long-term professional development.

Sustaining fulfillment and preventing burnout

Longevity in academic AS requires more than endurance; it demands the intentional cultivation of fulfillment. Sustained engagement in high-intensity environments depends on clear boundaries, restorative habits, and a values-based framework that prioritizes meaning over output.

While institutional wellness initiatives may offer structural support, personal practices remain indispensable. Daily routines that foster reflection, connection, and renewal serve as insulation against emotional fatigue.9

Achieving holistic “well-being” requires deliberate attention to the interrelated domains of mental, physical, and spiritual health. Physically, this may include ensuring adequate sleep, regular exercise, and mindful nutrition, basic but often neglected practices in the demanding schedules of surgical practice. For example, setting protected time for physical activity, even 30 min a few times a week, can improve focus and resilience. Similarly, meal planning or delivery services can help maintain healthy nutrition without adding stress.

Spiritual nurturing, while individualized, is equally essential. This may involve structured practices such as meditation, yoga, self-reflection through journaling, or dedicated moments. For some, engaging in community service, spending time in nature, or attending cultural gatherings provides a grounding sense of perspective and connectedness. These practices reconnect the surgeon with values that extend beyond professional identity, offering a source of inner strength during high-stakes or emotionally taxing periods.

AS is not merely a job, but a calling that requires an enduring commitment to learning, discovery, and growth. Regardless of personal belief systems, maintaining a sense of mission and inquiry over the course of a career is vital. Complacency can easily set in after years of clinical repetition and academic routine, and the temptation to disengage can be subtle but erodes both personal satisfaction and professional contribution. To remain inspired and relevant, surgeons must cultivate a career-long attitude of inquisitiveness and continuous self-improvement. Whether through teaching, research, or refining operative technique, the surgeon must model a relentless pursuit of excellence in their chosen domain. This mindset ensures that one’s work and presence remain meaningful in a rapidly evolving field.

Ultimately, avoiding burnout does not require the elimination of stress. Rather, it involves ensuring that the energy invested yields meaningful contribution, connection, and growth to help better navigate that stress.10 With a framework rooted in intentionality, self-care, and the continuous pursuit of excellence, even the most demanding surgical careers can be sustained with vitality and purpose.

Conclusion

AS sits at the intersection of clinical urgency and scholarly ambition. Sustaining longevity and fulfillment in this environment requires more than just stamina; it requires intentional alignment of daily work with deeply held values.

Clarifying a personal mission, setting strategic boundaries, cultivating mentorship and sponsorship, fostering a growth mindset, and prioritizing holistic well-being are essential strategies for a sustainable and meaningful career. These efforts help shift the focus from traditional benchmarks to meaningful engagement, allowing surgeons to escape the relentless race for achievement.

This approach fosters resilience, deepens professional satisfaction, and amplifies long-term contributions to patients, colleagues, and the evolving field of AS.
