Negotiating Care and Control: Women's Experiences of Two Harm Reduction Services in Copenhagen
Authors: Sif Ingibergsdottir Mogensen, Cameron Duff
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn this paper, we build on literature examining care practices in harm reduction services to investigate some of the reasons why women who use drugs access, remain in or leave harm reduction services. Through the analysis of ethnographic fieldwork collected at two Danish harm reduction services, including 29 interviews with women who use drugs, this study examines how care is practised in these services, what conditions these practices and how women are affected by this care. Our findings indicate that care is enacted in material, social and affective practices and relations, including the provision of everyday necessities, bodily affection and staff expressions of concern and sympathy that promote belonging. However, these care practices are also conditioned by relations of control mediated by workplace factors, the impacts of national and organisational policies including ongoing resourcing challenges, staff understanding of care practices and intersecting dynamics of gender, ethnicity and class. Women's experiences of harm reduction services are mediated by their everyday struggles negotiating relations of care and control with significant implications for both the quality of care delivered in these sites and its impacts. We close by reflecting on the tensions between care and control in harm reduction programmes for women.Keywords: care, control, drug consumption room, emergency shelter, harm reduction, women who use drugs

Introduction

In this paper, we examine how policies and staff practices at two harm reduction services in Copenhagen function to exert control over women who use drugs by imposing (gendered) expectations and obligations to behave and/or comply with site policies, even as these services provide essential relations of care and support. Tensions between care and control are at the centre of harm reduction praxis in these sites, framing women's access to (and the quality of) programme and service delivery. Previous studies have indicated how women who use drugs, especially poor, pregnant and/or racialised women, are subjected to punitive and paternalistic practices, including child removal, incarceration or mandatory drug treatment, often justified as forms of care (Sybylla 2001; Knight 2015; Campbell 2000; Buxton et al. 2020; J. Boyd et al. 2022; S. C. Boyd 2004). These practices reveal the tension between care and coercion, reflecting how women are often required to comply with specific racial and gendered categorisations to access support, for example, in order to maintain contact with their children (Knight 2015). A common explanation for these practices is that they reflect broader social expectations about women's culturally assigned responsibilities for social reproduction, the family and the home (S. C. Boyd 2004; Campbell 2000). Accessing health and social care often requires women to embody these norms, with drug policies and interventions reinforcing and regulating gendered expectations placed on women who use drugs, mirroring expectations and obligations placed on women in general.

Much existing work on women's experiences of harm reduction services focuses on reducing barriers to access (Austin et al. 2023; Bardwell et al. 2021). Harm reduction is generally defined as policies and programmes that aim to minimise the health, social and legal consequences associated with drug use and related policies and laws (Harm Reduction International 2021). Although some note how these services impose new forms of control on service users, few examine how control is entangled with care in their very design and delivery. By emphasising this tension, we examine how ‘care’ is understood and operationalised within harm reduction (Houborg et al. 2025) and how its delivery entails rules and practices that regulate women's bodies, interactions, attachments and mobilities as conditions of care. Scholars note that behavioural expectations, rules and obligations common to mainstream healthcare also shape harm reduction services (Michaud et al. 2023). For example, surveillance practices, such as data collection and sharing, are often framed as ‘caring’ interventions to address health inequities, yet these practices can also be employed for coercive or repressive ends (Michaud et al. 2023; Sybylla 2001). Similar care–control dynamics may be observed in rules imposed by staff at harm reduction services to ensure the safety of staff members and people using the services (Duncan et al. 2019). These aspects indicate how care can produce both positive and negative outcomes, underscoring the value of analysing care and control simultaneously (Thelen 2021).

We foreground this care/control dynamic in what follows by examining the care practices involved in accessing and utilising harm reduction services in Copenhagen for women who use drugs, as well as the relationships between staff and women using these services. We emphasise how practices are framed by way of specific logics of care, shaped by political, material, social and affective conditions and how women who use drugs experience these practices. Drawing on feminist science and technology studies (Puig de la Bellacasa 2017; Martin et al. 2015; Murphy 2015), we highlight the material and affective conditions in which care is delivered and received, tracing entanglements of care and control to show how service delivery can both enable and constrain support. This analysis offers new insights into how care is practised in harm reduction services, what conditions this practice and its effects on women who use drugs.

The Research Setting and Policy Contexts

Despite Denmark's zero‐tolerance policy on illegal drugs (introduced in 2004), harm reduction remains a significant part of Danish drug policy. This creates a paradoxical situation where progressive harm reduction interventions coexist with the maintenance of more punitive policies grounded in prohibition. Harm reduction was first introduced in Denmark in 1984, initially as a social welfare measure to mitigate social exclusion, with public health considerations becoming a more prominent element in later years (Houborg and Moeller 2021; Jauffret‐Roustide et al. 2022). In 2012, Danish municipalities were permitted to open drug consumption rooms, leading to the opening of five facilities. Recently, two more have opened in Copenhagen and another is planned for the city, with others planned for regional centres. This study is set in Copenhagen, the capital of Denmark, where individuals can access a range of harm reduction initiatives including drug consumption rooms, emergency shelters, homeless shelters, health initiatives and other facilities available to people in structurally vulnerable positions.

This study was conducted in two of these sites: a gender‐neutral 1 drug consumption room and a women‐only emergency shelter, both applying harm reduction ideologies and practices. The drug consumption room (DCR) provides safe drug consumption tools and allows people to use preobtained drugs under the supervision of staff trained to respond in the event of an overdose (e.g., administer oxygen and naloxone) (Kerr et al. 2017). Besides providing space for inhaling and injecting drugs, the DCR also functions as a gateway for other social and health services, while also providing people with basic necessities such as food, drinks, clothing, access to hygiene products and toilets. In addition to emergency overnight accommodation, the women‐only emergency shelter has similar functions to the DCR, including service referrals and the provision of necessities. The shelter is for women who use drugs and/or participate in sex work and is meant to provide respite from the challenges they experience in the streets and at other services. The shelter provides safe drug consumption tools and condoms; however, alcohol and drugs were not allowed to be consumed on site at the time of the fieldwork. This rule often generated tensions and conflict between women and staff at the shelter. Reflecting these tensions and the service demands they point to, the emergency shelter had for some time been seeking to raise the necessary funds (and political support) to launch their own women‐only drug consumption room, which did eventually open for a 2‐year trial after fieldwork was completed. Gathering data in each site offered opportunities to explore the different political, sociomaterial and interpersonal conditions influencing care practices in women's experiences of service support.

Background and Conceptual Approach

Harm reduction services frequently struggle to identify and respond to the unique issues faced by women who use drugs, as access to these services is often constrained by racialised and gendered discrimination, stigma, structural and gender‐based violence and criminalisation within service settings (Austin et al. 2023; Fairbairn et al. 2008; J. Boyd et al. 2018, 2020; Bardwell et al. 2021; Shirley‐Beavan et al. 2020; Collins et al. 2019; Malin et al. 2020). Research specifically on DCRs demonstrates how they can disrupt structural and everyday violence, enabling women to assert agency and control over their drug use practices (McNeil and Small 2014; Fairbairn et al. 2008). However, scholars have also demonstrated that the gender‐neutral nature of DCRs often reinforces gendered and racialised power dynamics, limiting access for women. These spaces remain male‐dominated, where women frequently face harassment and the threat of violence (J. Boyd et al. 2018, 2020; Bardwell et al. 2021). Additionally, women are often expected to conform to specific gender roles and ‘behave better’ in these spaces, such as tidying up and consuming drugs faster (J. Boyd et al. 2018).

Findings like these have led to calls for women‐only harm reduction services to minimise the violence and stigma women often face in gender‐neutral services (J. Boyd et al. 2018, 2020; Bardwell et al. 2021; Shirley‐Beavan et al. 2020). However, few such services exist globally, limiting research on their effectiveness. Existing studies suggest these spaces offer temporary relief from violence, stigma and risks encountered in local street drug markets. Offering mutual care and support, sociability and nonjudgemental staff relationships, these services foster a sense of belonging, community and resilience among women (J. Boyd et al. 2020; Bardwell et al. 2021; Shirley‐Beavan et al. 2020). However, the barriers women experience attempting to access harm reduction services are often structural, and few services have managed to overcome them entirely (Shirley‐Beavan et al. 2020). It is further the case that many of the rules and policies operating in these sites present further barriers to access, although these findings typically generate heated debate (see Austin et al. 2023, Knight 2015 and/or Värmå Falk et al. 2020). Addressing these controversies, we explore how care practices and care relationships at harm reduction services are conditioned by political, sociomaterial and interpersonal aspects, excluding some women who use drugs while fostering a sense of belonging for others. We will approach these conditions in our analysis through the notion of a care/control continuum.

The Care/Control Continuum

Our approach to care builds on feminist perspectives that frame it as a distinctive social and affective practice, often devalued and rendered invisible (Puig de la Bellacasa 2017). Recent feminist STS scholarship further conceptualises care as an active, material and affective ‘doing’ (Puig de la Bellacasa 2017; Martin et al. 2015; Mol 2008), informing much of our approach below. In drawing from this work, we contribute to a small but growing feminist literature on harm reduction services that highlights the importance of going beyond the clinical aspects of service design and delivery to consider the social, material and affective practices that actively produce, express and sustain effective care in these settings (Duncan et al. 2019; Farrugia et al. 2019; Kolla and Strike 2020; Austin et al. 2023; Bardwell et al. 2021; Dertadian and Yates 2023; Piatkowski et al. 2024; Houborg et al. 2025; Dennis 2019; Dennis et al. 2020). This literature explores practices of care within harm reduction services to capture the nuanced ways care is enacted and experienced. Accordingly, we view care as a dynamic, situated and open‐ended practice that can foster positive experiences and build relationships, but also reproduce oppression and marginalisation (Thelen 2015, 2021; Murphy 2015). The latter, what might be called ‘costs of care’ for both staff and recipients, is a dominant feature of feminist discussion of care in social and health services contexts, challenging idealised notions of care by revealing its unintended harms and burdens (Austin et al. 2023; Duncan et al. 2019). Within these discussions, care emerges as a complex form of shared labour capable of yielding significant benefits for caregivers and recipients but often associated with significant costs for each (Puig de la Bellacasa 2012; Buse et al. 2018).

This way of conceptualising care draws our attention to how care and control are in constant tension. Care is inherently political and shaped by ideas about need, deservingness and care responsibilities. These produce specific forms of intervention and obligation, which may be perceived as caring by some individuals or unwanted and controlling by others (Singleton and Mee 2017; Thelen 2021). Caring for other people always involves negotiations of what constitutes a legitimate need, drawing boundaries between those deemed deserving and undeserving of this care. Consequently, care practices can perpetuate oppressive patterns of structural violence, because the practice of caring for others can reinforce inequalities by establishing contrasts between individuals (Thelen 2015, 2021). It is essential to acknowledge that perceptions of need and deservingness can also differ between groups, often tied to prevailing notions of a ‘normal’ identity that typically vary within a society based on factors such as gender, age, ethnicity and social status (Thelen 2021). We adopt the concept of the care/control continuum to illustrate how power shapes care and to clarify the complex ways care is experienced.

Alert to this tension between care and control, we will treat care as an affectively charged and selective way of directing attention, regard or concern towards specific individuals or situations while diverting it from others. This affective aspect of care is rooted in values, morals and compassion (or lack thereof) for those suffering (Lynch et al. 2021). Care is a way to connect with things and become affected by them, as well as the potential to impact others (Martin et al. 2015; Puig de la Bellacasa 2011). Receiving care can foster feelings of happiness, gratitude and belonging, while also being perceived as humiliating, limiting one's agency and creating a sense of indebtedness or obligation (Martin et al. 2015). This complexity is reflected in our empirical material, which revealed a range of emotional responses to the various care practices enacted within the two fieldwork sites, including happiness, gratitude, belonging, anger, shame, humiliation, fear and despondency. We address these emotional responses in our analysis, with a particular focus on belonging, as care practices can lead to inequalities, but also nurture a sense of ‘togetherness’ (Thelen 2015, 2021). To this end, we draw on Yuval‐Davis’ (Yuval‐Davis 2006) concept of belonging, which involves feelings of home, safety and the ability to understand one's place in the world. A person's sense of belonging can vary significantly over time and be influenced by their inclusion or exclusion from formal care relations (Thelen 2021). For women who use drugs, such relations can either enhance or diminish community belonging.

Methods and Participants

This paper draws from ethnographic research conducted at a drug consumption room and a women‐only emergency shelter in Copenhagen. Around 170 h of fieldwork were conducted from December 2022 to January 2024, typically in four‐hour sessions. During fieldwork, the first author conducted informal observations and conversations with staff and service users and assisted with some daily tasks. Observations and interactions were later documented in field notes. The first author was guided in these efforts by reference to ethnographic work on women's drug use (Maher 2002; Bourgois et al. 2004; Knight 2015; Collins et al. 2018, 2020; Collins, Boyd, Czechaczek, et al. 2020; Austin et al. 2023; J. Boyd et al. 2020; Bardwell, Austin, et al. 2021; Bardwell, Fleming, et al. 2021; J. Boyd et al. 2022) and a strong commitment to feminist ethnographic praxis, emphasising knowledge production as a reflexive and partial process embedded in power relations (Haraway 1988; Lather 1991). This involved close attention to matters of embodiment, emotion and identity during fieldwork, and the ambition to produce findings that responded to participant and community needs.

As part of the fieldwork, the first author also conducted 27 qualitative interviews with women who use drugs. Participants were approached and recruited by the first author with support from staff. Some participants approached the first author directly after hearing about the research through peer recommendations. Recruitment criteria included participants who identify as women and frequently use illegal drugs. Eleven women were recruited from the women‐only emergency shelter and a further 16 from the drug consumption room. The average age of participants was 45 years (range 24–66 years), with most identifying as Danish (21), followed by other Scandinavian or Eastern European countries, with a small number identifying as African or Middle Eastern. Most identified as heterosexual (21), with some identifying as bisexual or pansexual. Participants primarily consumed heroin, methadone, crack cocaine, cannabis and alcohol, with crack cocaine being the most common (17). Inhalation was the main method of consuming drugs (19), though many also injected (11). At the time of the interview, most reported having housing (15), usually individual apartments in social housing, whereas some slept rough or in shelters. However, approximately half reported periods of homelessness in the past 3 years. Most participants utilised harm reduction services daily, whereas a few used them weekly. Not all used both the drug consumption room and the women‐only shelter, and some of the reasons for this will be discussed in the findings section below.

All participants provided consent after being informed about the project's focus, confidentiality, their right to withdraw at any time and the voluntary nature of participation. Interviews took place in various settings according to participants' preferences for privacy, such as meeting rooms or offices at the study sites, in discreet public spaces such as benches and stairwells or while taking a walk. The interviews were semi‐structured, facilitated by a thematic interview guide and various improvised follow‐up questions in line with emerging themes. The topics were informed by relevant literature (as introduced in earlier sections) with a focus on women's experiences of drug use, drug markets, housing, violence, supervised consumption sites and other relevant harm reduction services provided in their neighbourhood. The interviews lasted 46 min on average (ranging from 17 to 119 min), and the participants were compensated with 200 Danish kroner (approx. 27 euros). Interviews were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim for all but four participants: one who did not wish to be recorded; two of the interviews were completed while walking with wind and other ambient noise preventing the completion of a usable transcript; on another occasion, the recorder malfunctioned. All identifying information (e.g., names, locations and other identifiable markers) was removed from the transcripts and field notes presented for this analysis, and participants were assigned pseudonyms to ensure confidentiality. This study followed relevant ethical and privacy guidelines and received institutional approval from Aarhus University by the Institutional Review Board (2022‐083).

Transcripts and field notes were uploaded to the qualitative data analysis software NVivo and analysed thematically. All data were processed through an open coding strategy, allowing for the identification of ‘surprising’ patterns (Timmermans and Tavory 2022), as specific themes emerged in the material. Thematic analysis followed the six phases of Braun and Clarke´s (Braun and Clarke 2022) reflexive TA. The first author began by getting familiarised with the material through listening to the audio recordings, reading transcripts and field notes and writing down reflections. Next, the material was inductively coded in NVivo by creating semantic codes that closely reflected the participants' own words (Braun and Clarke 2022). During the next phase, codes were sorted and compiled, resulting in the following themes: (1) structural conditions, (2) material conditions, (3) relations between staff and women, (4) relations between women. We next consulted relevant theoretical literature and developed a conceptual framework for the analysis. From this framework, the themes were refined by re‐reading the collated extracts. Reflexive writing, mind maps and drawings accompanied the coding process to deepen our understanding. The last phase involved writing up the findings and selecting the relevant extracts from the empirical material with a focus on the gendered aspects of care, which we present below organised in two sections. The first focuses on the structural and organisational conditions of care practices, whereas the second addresses the sociomaterial and interpersonal conditions for establishing care relationships between staff and women using the services.

Analysis

The Role of Structural and Organisational Policies in Shaping Care Practices

Several organisational policies were found to impact the design of harm reduction services at each site, with implications for women's access to services and the quality of care delivery therein. This is illustrated by two examples related to how women's access at the drug consumption room is moderated and how on‐site drug use is prohibited at the women‐only emergency shelter in ways that reveal relations of control over women's bodies and their drug use.

Staff Discretion Regulates Access to the Drug Consumption Room

Since Denmark introduced drug consumption rooms in 2012, the main goal of these services has been to support people who use drugs, primarily opioids and some types of cocaine. To access these services, people must register on site, allowing for tracking and monitoring of service use, how often individuals visit and for how long (Hyatt et al. 2023). The registration process and the tensions and problems it generates were recurring themes in field notes and interviews. Staff often faced challenges regulating access to the DCR, particularly during busy periods, as they were required to check registration status. Some people attempted to sneak in, whereas others, unaware of the registration rule, were turned away. Importantly, the character of people's drug use was a key factor in registration, in that staff were explicitly required to deny registration for individuals suspected of consuming drugs ‘recreationally’ in favour of ‘street‐involved’ consumers. Therefore, to prevent people from nearby nightlife venues who might be using drugs recreationally from accessing the service, new registrations were restricted to weekdays. This discretion frequently denied individuals access to this service, often leading to conflict.

Some DCR staff justified these policies due to concerns about the potential escalation of drug use among those they registered for the site, fearing that such access might prompt an increase in both the frequency and variety of their drug consumption. This seemed to entail a ‘slippery slope’ argument whereby people's drug use escalates from ‘recreational’ to more ‘entrenched’ or ‘problematic’ use, presumably as a result of their exposure to others in the DCR. In contrast, other staff expressed uncertainty about the rationale behind diverting people from the service, fearing that the rule was exclusionary. Either way, the policy serves as a powerful instance of control, inasmuch as the rule determines who has access to the DCR (and hence who may benefit from the harm reduction services offered therein), just as it reinforces the ‘identity work’ of membership and belonging manifested in the criteria for registering for the service.

This identity work is practised by staff in ways that inevitably involve judgements about belonging and community, for example, who belongs to local ‘street involved’ drug‐using communities and who is simply looking for a place to take drugs before heading to a local bar or nightclub. Although some justified this work as a form of harm reduction given their interests in ‘diverting’ the latter from any contact with the former, it also involved social and material judgements about ‘membership’ in the drug consumption room that often centred on women's appearance and comportment. In reflecting on her experiences of these aspects of care, Mette recounted how she tried to access the drug consumption room during a period when she was inhaling crack cocaine and consuming cocaine nasally, usually prior to sex work, only to be refused registration and access due to her appearance, which led her to unsafe situations:

Int: You don't use the drug consumption room?

Mette: No, no. They won't sign me in, so I can't get into the smoking rooms. […] I've tried once, where they were like, ‘Oh. You don't look like an addict [Danish word: “misbruger”]’. Or at least I wasn't allowed to. Then I said, that's just fine. I'll just go home and take the drugs myself [laughs]

(Mette, 41)

Later in her interview, Mette explained an instance where being refused access to the DCR led her to smoke crack outside the service, where she was scammed and her money and drugs were stolen. According to Mette, the staff's decision to divert her from the room was based on her appearance apparently not fitting the stereotypical image of an ‘addict’, in her words.

We should add that the harms Mette experienced on this occasion as a result of her inability to access this site should be regarded as an explicit outcome of the legislation that governs service delivery in Copenhagen's DCRs, which restricts service registration (and hence site access) to individuals with “a strong dependency as a result of prolonged and persistent abuse of euphoriant substances” (Copenhagen Municipality 2024). With limited guidance on how to enforce this condition, staff must make rapid judgements about eligibility based on appearance and demeanour. In this way, staff discretion, mandated by formal policy, regulates women's access to the delivery of care and support. This illustrates what we mean by the care/control continuum, as it operates through corporeal distinctions of belonging and exclusion. In Mette's case, both the relevant legislation and staff discretion combined to exclude her from the DCR, leaving her to smoke crack on the pavement. Control is exercised in a subjective judgement about Mette's appearance and her apparent deviation from how women experiencing ‘strong dependency’ typically look or are supposed to look. Judgements about appearance thus serve as a critical social and affective practice by which staff create boundaries between people who belong and do not belong to the service, fundamentally shaping who comes to experience care at this site.

Others have also noticed these forms of corporeal governance in harm reduction services in which women who use drugs are compelled to manage the physical manifestations of their drug use to maintain bodies that accord with normative gendered expectations of femininity (J. Boyd et al. 2022; Duncan et al. 2019). This may involve choices about which drugs to consume or ways of consuming drugs to counteract those effects of this consumption that might disturb normative expectations (Collins et al. 2020). This concern with bodily appearance, and the ways in which judgements about appearance create distinctions between groups of women, was also evident in our study. In view of Collins et al.’s (2020) findings, we can understand how Mette's exclusion from the DCR due to her appearance is so confounding for her. In order to register for this service, gain access to the drug consumption room and avoid consuming drugs in unsafe situations characterised by harassment and violence, Mette must walk a fine line between a bodily appearance that the staff associates with ‘strong dependency’ and how she chooses to present herself. Simultaneously, Mette needs to uphold certain standards or norms of appearance characterised as ‘womanly’, standards that are presumably further mediated by her involvement in local sex work, while also managing her appearance in ways that might permit her access to a safer place to consume drugs prior to this work. Control over Mette's body and appearance thus serves as the very condition of her access to care in these respects.

Prohibition of On‐Site Drug Use Generates Differential Access to the Women‐Only Shelter

Another example of how policy settings influence care practices in these harm reduction services is the prohibition of on‐site drug use at the women‐only shelter. The women‐only shelter does not require individuals to abstain from using alcohol or drugs to stay or receive services but prohibits substance use on site and often punishes women with bans and restrictions if they break this rule. The decision to prohibit all on‐site drug use at the women‐only emergency shelter is reportedly due to the lack of a proper ventilation system, which is a legal requirement of operating a drug consumption room under Danish policy, meaning it is not possible to inhale drugs at the service. To avoid discriminating between women on the basis of their drug use as a result of this shortcoming, the shelter decided to prohibit all substance use on site. Despite this rule, women at the shelter often attempted to conceal their drug use on site, for example, by using washrooms and other semi‐private spaces, something that others have also observed in similar contexts (Wallace et al. 2018; Pauly et al. 2018). Efforts to evade this rule at the shelter were clear in hidden alcohol bottles and discarded needles, although it also led to tensions among women using the shelter and between women and staff. For instance, if their drug use was spotted by staff, women would be temporarily banned from the service, losing their access to emergency shelter and risking homelessness for the following night(s). Perversely, this resulted in different experiences of care among women at the shelter depending on their mode/choice of drug consumption (something the policy of prohibiting all consumption in the space was explicitly aimed at avoiding) because women who injected drugs were able to hide their drug use more easily compared to women who smoked drugs or drank alcohol. This policy generated frustration and safety concerns among women using the services, as Lærke explained:

I went to [women‐only shelter] and smoked [drugs], got kicked out because [drug consumption room] was closed and I’m not allowed to smoke at [women‐only shelter]. […] If I try to shield myself in [women‐only shelter], [the local police] will be called, because I’ve smoked in the toilet, right? Which I only do because I can’t be anywhere else. So, no matter what I do, it’s not ok.

(Lærke, 40)

Lærke’s remarks here were clarified a little later in her interview as she explained how she attempted to smoke at the women's shelter to avoid the physical violence she had experienced at the drug consumption room. This resonates with other studies confirming how violence and/or the fear of violence are barriers to harm reduction services for women who use drugs (Bardwell et al. 2021; J. Boyd et al. 2018, 2020). Prohibiting drug use on site therefore operates as a form of control that although motivated by an intention to reduce barriers to care for women positioned as structurally vulnerable (in this case by seeking to avoid discriminating between women on the basis of their drug use behaviours), ends up installing new barriers to care. Control is here exercised in the name of caring for women positioned as structurally vulnerable even as the two goals (care and control) prove to be irreconcilable. Moreover, the practice results in frustration and risk for women attempting to access the shelter, friction in their relationships with staff and the exclusion of some women from the shelter, especially women who smoke drugs. We should anchor this tension in the policy stipulations requiring ‘proper ventilation systems’ in DCRs in Copenhagen, the financial constraints that prevent this shelter from installing such a system, and the subsequent establishment and enforcement of a rule at the shelter prohibiting all drug use in apparent interests of fairness and equity (i.e., if smoking drugs on site is not possible, then in fairness no one should be able to consume drugs on site). Once more, controlling women's drug use is justified as a means of taking care of them.

Negotiating the Conditions of Care

Despite these tensions, participants reportedly valued the DCR and women's shelter for access to essentials such as clothing, hygiene products, food and supportive relationships with staff. Yet fieldwork and interviews revealed unequal access to these care practices and resources. The following analysis examines how participants navigate the conditions that shape their access to these practices and resources, highlighting how service delivery operates along a care/control continuum that enables inclusion for some women while restricting it for others.

Giving up Independence to Receive Everyday Necessities

The material resources offered by the DCR and women‐only shelter, such as harm reduction supplies (e.g., needles, crack pipes, condoms), clothing, hygiene products, food and other essentials, are highly valued by women using these services (see Duncan et al. 2019). However, these resources are often stored in staff‐controlled areas, significantly mediating relationships between staff and the women using the services. Women thus often come to depend on staff for access to basic needs such as food, drinks (except occasionally self‐serve coffee and tea), showers, secure storage for their belongings and rest. These interactions help to foster connections and belonging at each site, as illustrated by the following extract from the field notes:

Emma arrives at the women‐only shelter and she’s very quiet and walks around a little hunched over. Emma comes to the kitchen and asks for toast before lying down on the mattress in the common room. While I make the toast, Amalie (staff) takes the time to leave the kitchen and find Emma. Amalie kneels in front of Emma who is lying on the mattress and asks if she’s ok and Emma shakes her head no and looks sad, but she doesn’t want to talk about it.

(Field note Jan. 2023)

Although Emma is reluctant to explain why she is upset, the need to request on‐site material resources from staff is likely why Amalie (staff) sought to check in on Emma's well‐being with concern and sympathy. The sociomaterial conditions of accessing resources in the shelter thus play a key role in mediating care relationships between the staff and women using the services. Indeed, the social, affective and material aspects of care are entangled here with the responsibilities staff have for managing access to resources and support. As we have noted, service rules govern access to most material resources, whether securing a bed or a safer space to use drugs, which is why we have framed care delivery along a care/control continuum. Yet it is through the relational and embodied negotiation of these rules that women and staff often come to develop novel relations of care and support. Negotiating access to material supports (such as a bed, food or clothing) creates opportunities for staff and women to engage in conversations about personal issues beyond drugs, which can strengthen their bonds and create the conditions for experiences of community, solidarity and belonging (see also Houborg et al. 2025).

Although some instances of managing access to material resources fostered a sense of belonging for participants, these practices (and the service rules that shape them) also resulted in long queues, impatience, frustration and anger, generating conflict and causing some women to leave or not utilise the services. Puig de la Bellacasa (2017) highlights how focusing on material conditions can reveal underlying tensions and power dynamics in the practice of caring for others. Reflecting this insight, multiple participants explained how accessing material resources and supports at these sites felt like giving up their independence or self‐sufficiency, impacting their well‐being and identity. As if needing help was tantamount to the concession that one could no longer independently look after oneself, something that has often been observed of service delivery in these kinds of contexts (see Austin et al. 2023; Duncan et al. 2019).

One participant, Ilana, mentioned how these aspects of service delivery stoked feelings of indifference or even apathy in relation to her life situation, especially given that she was not able to access or prepare food reflective of the culture and ethnicity she identifies with. Other participants expressed embarrassment, shame or humiliation at not being able to perform everyday self‐care practices themselves and hence for being dependent on emergency services for this support as a result. This is illustrated in the following field note of a conversation between a staff member at the women‐only shelter, and Mia and Helle who use the shelter:

Mia talks about how hard it is to live on the street and how embarrassing it is to need help for everything: “I can’t make my own cup of coffee or wipe up after myself”. Helle replies that the worst thing is that they always have to say, “may I, please, or excuse me”. They talk about how this is something they must do at the shelter as well.

(Field note Jan. 2023)

The emotional dynamics highlighted in this situation emphasise the control (or discretion) exercised by staff, who determine when and how care and support are made available, and the subsequent affirmation of the identity of ‘women in need’. Navigating the sociomaterial conditions of care in these services, including rules and policies determining access to material resources, and social and affective relationships with staff and other service users, all serve to mediate how care is delivered in these sites and, perhaps more importantly, what these care practices and relations may be said to achieve or produce (Puig de la Bellacasa 2017). Sometimes these conditions mediate care and belonging, and other times, dependence and humiliation.

The Tension Between Self‐Care and Reliance on Services

Ethnographic fieldwork and interviews indicated how small everyday interactions, bodily affection and expressions of recognition, concern and sympathy figure strongly in women’s reasons for accessing each service. One participant, Lea, expressed how the staff ‘really does make a difference. I mean, really big difference. It means a lot, because they become your family in here’ (Lea, 37). Another participant, Anita, explained how she appreciates that the staff know her well and that she feels welcome and ‘at home’. Although each service is designed to deliver support without barriers or restrictions, some are excluded from these care practices, as we have noted. Some women, Helle, Marianne, Nora and Lærke, attributed these exclusions to perceived favouritism or differential treatment, mistrust of staff and sometimes even how staff express concern and sympathy. Several participants raised concerns about staffing gaps and shortages, insufficient staff training and staff being overwhelmed by their workloads and how these aspects impacted care delivery. A common point of discussion was how staff shortages often led to staff being more attentive to some people using the services rather than others. Perceptions of staff favouritism led women to adopt different strategies to gain staff attention, primarily by asking calmly for help and waiting patiently or alternatively by expressing frustration and anger. With the first strategy, participants risked being overlooked, whereas the second strategy could result in women being sanctioned or ignored as ‘difficult’. Either way, staffing attitudes and practices in response to these strategies often led to further annoyance and frustration among women using the services.

For some participants, their frustration with people being treated differently led them to mistrust the staff and to find other ways to care for themselves. One participant, Marianne, shared an anecdote about a time when she was feeling sick and wanted ginger tea for relief, but she doubted that the staff would assist her: “Because I’m not one of those prioritised groups. You should scream and be more like [name of another woman]” (Marianne, 55). Therefore, she had someone steal the tea for her. Another participant, Melissa, indicated that she mistrusted the staff because she believed she was personally targeted and therefore faced sanctions, bans and less interpersonal care as a result. Some participants, Marianne, Lærke, Mia, Hanna and Thea, did not trust that the staff would help them when conflicts arose in the services, either because they believed staff themselves were too afraid to intervene or because staff did not believe their accounts of the conflicts. Another related aspect, equally important to establishing trust and rapport between staff and women using each service, involved individual staff members’ ways of expressing recognition, concern and sympathy. An example of this is when Helle was observed asking to borrow the phone at the shelter to call a family member. Amalie (a staff member) responded by expressing concern about Helle’s situation, given that the shelter was closed for the night, meaning Helle had to find another place to sleep in mid‐winter:

Amalie says, ‘I’ve been thinking about you these days, have you been cold, Helle?’. Amalie says it sweetly while looking at Helle caringly. Helle replies ‘Please don’t. I’ve told you that I don’t want that caring crap’. Amalie replies that she’ll leave it alone and Helle gets more and more frustrated and starts shouting that Amalie won’t leave her alone and that she just wants to talk to her family and now she can’t concentrate. Amalie looks down and replies ‘I won’t talk to you, but I am also allowed to be here’. Helle starts talking on the phone quietly.

(Field note Jan. 2023)

Amalie’s attempts to express sympathy and concern contrast with Helle’s frustration at being denied shelter, indicating once more how care relations may be experienced very differently by the parties to this relation. Sympathy can feel belittling, as it often positions the care receiver as inferior (Clark 1987). Similarly, Thelen (2021) notes that receiving care can be experienced as humiliating and limiting of one’s autonomy, which may explain Helle’s response. Performing care in expressions of recognition, concern or sympathy is influenced by power dynamics. Examples include staff’s discretionary power to deny service access, to offer or withhold basic necessities or in the manner in which they attend to or ignore requests from women using the services. Staff, indeed, have considerable power over women accessing services at these sites, including the control they exercise over valued resources and the ways they care for women.

Discussion

This study explored how women who use drugs negotiate practices of care and control at two harm reduction services in Copenhagen to further understand some of the reasons why women who use drugs access, remain in or leave these services. Applying the concept of a care/control continuum, we have emphasised how power relations shape the conditions and possibilities of care practices and relationships at each of these services. Following feminist and STS conceptualisations of care (Puig de la Bellacasa 2012; Martin et al. 2015; Buse et al. 2018; Puig de la Bellacasa 2017), our study highlights the material, social and affective dimensions of the services delivered at these sites and how these dimensions shape how care is enacted and experienced therein. Of course, what is caring for one woman is not necessarily caring for another. And what may be understood by staff as caring, conditioned by the political, material, social and affective possibilities shaping care delivery on site, is not always experienced as caring by women who use drugs.

Our analysis further indicates how gendered social norms and expectations influence how women who use drugs experience care at these two harm reduction services. These dynamics are in line with previous research illustrating how gendered barriers, expectations of feminine comportment, stigma and threats of violence restrict access to harm reduction services for women who use drugs (Bardwell et al. 2021; J. Boyd et al. 2018, 2020). Even though barriers such as violence and stigma were not the focus of this analysis, it is important to note how pervasive these experiences were for our participants. For example, Mette, who was denied access to the drug consumption room due to her appearance, or Lærke, who risked being banned from the women‐only shelter for smoking drugs on‐site, both explained how the pervasive threat of violence profoundly shapes women’s experiences of care delivery in harm reduction services. Understanding this broader context is crucial when assessing why women who use drugs access, remain in or leave harm reduction services, because this context inevitably shades every aspect of how care is understood, experienced or responded to in these settings.

In making this point, it is not our intention to disregard the important role staff serve in delivering care at harm reduction services. Previous studies emphasise the high quality of care provided by harm reduction workers and how they help to calm, support, uplift and empower individuals while mediating conflict (Duncan et al. 2019; Rance and Fraser 2011). Research further illustrates the emotional toll that people working in harm reduction services experience (Kolla et al. 2024). Although improving workplace conditions is essential for staff well‐being and therefore their ability to enact care, these policy and practice responses will not address the gendered inequities that women who use drugs experience. Identifying, designing and implementing effective harm reduction practices should be collaborative and predicated upon listening to and acting on the needs and concerns of people who use drugs and staff working in these services. Additionally, researchers have illustrated the ways peer staff and women with living experience of drug use can challenge societal stereotypes and discrimination against women who use illegal drugs and diminish power asymmetries in care relationships (J. Boyd et al. 2020).

Indeed, staff at each of the services that participated in this study were aware of and interested in responding more effectively to women's unique issues. For example, staff at the women‐only emergency shelter recognised that their on‐site drug use ban limited their engagement with local women who use drugs, leading them to fundraise for an on‐site women‐only consumption room to eliminate the need for this prohibition. Likewise, the gender‐neutral drug consumption room established a women‐focused staff group and then implemented a women‐only toilet at the request of the women using the service. In general, then, staff at each of these services try to solicit and implement suggestions from women using the services to improve care outcomes. This was corroborated by many participants, but also challenged by others who did not have the opportunity to attend meetings or felt that they were not being heard.

We should acknowledge our study limitations, particularly the under‐representation of trans and nonbinary gender identities in the sample. Although the women‐only site was transgender‐inclusive, all participants were ciswomen. Including a more diverse sample would provide deeper insights into how gender and other identity dynamics mediate the expression of care in harm reduction services. Likewise, further research is needed on how women who use drugs navigate care practices and relationships at harm reduction services to further understand why women access, remain in or leave these services. This work should continue to emphasise the care/control continuum to understand how some ways of practising care can inadvertently exclude women who use drugs, as well as investigate the conditions and potential of these practices.

Conclusion

Through ethnographic fieldwork at two harm reduction services and qualitative interviews, this study emphasises tensions between relations of care and control in the design and delivery of harm reduction services for women who use drugs in Copenhagen. Our findings reveal the complex and intersecting dynamics that influence whether and how women who use drugs engage with harm reduction services, presenting insights to help inform more relevant and effective services. In line with previous research, this study reveals sharp contrasts in the ways care is understood and operationalised in the delivery of harm reduction services, with key differences over the material, social and affective dimensions of care. By drawing on feminist work in science and technology studies and the concept of a care/control continuum, we examined how care is practised at harm reduction services, what conditions these practices and how women who use drugs are affected by these local enactments of care. Our findings indicate that the material, social and affective expressions of care evident in the provision of everyday necessities, bodily affection and staff expressions of concern and sympathy are essential parts of why our participants used these harm reduction services. Furthermore, these enactments express and sustain caring relationships between staff and women using the services, promoting greater belonging. However, these care practices are conditioned by national and organisational policies, such as the underfunding of harm reduction services, workplace conditions and staff understanding of care practices, as well as intersecting dynamics such as ethnicity, class and gender that often work at cross‐purposes to service goals and functions. These aspects condition how care is delivered and received by women using the services, establishing rules, practices and regimes of control over women´s bodies, interactions, forms of attachment and mobility. Care and control are thus held in tension in the delivery of harm reduction services in Copenhagen, requiring women to daily navigate these tensions to obtain the care they need.
