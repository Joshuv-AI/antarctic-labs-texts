Functional connectivity patterns differ as a function of co-occurring attentional problems in preschoolers with autism
Authors: Alex Boxberger, Bosi Chen, Lindsay Olson, Michaela Cordova, Judy Mahmalji, Adriana Rios, Annika C Linke, Inna Fishman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSymptoms of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) are common in children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and are associated with greater developmental challenges, poorer clinical outcomes, and alterations in functional connectivity (FC) of the brain. However, despite the consensus that ASD and other neurodevelopmental conditions emerge early in life, little is known about the trajectories of brain and behavioral development during the first years of life in children with ASD and co-occurring attention problems (AP).MethodsIn a sample of 122 young children (ages 1.5–5 years) with and without ASD, we examined whether toddlers and preschoolers with ASD and co-occurring AP already differ from peers with ASD without co-occurring AP on adaptive and developmental skills, ASD symptoms, and FC of the frontoparietal and salience networks, which have been previously linked to ADHD symptoms in older children with ASD and ADHD.ResultsResults of general linear model analyses revealed lower developmental and adaptive skills across multiple domains in children with ASD and elevated AP compared with their peers with lower AP, despite equivalent levels of ASD symptoms. Further, children with ASD and elevated AP showed reduced FC within the frontoparietal network (p = .027), between the frontoparietal and language networks (p = .004), and the frontoparietal and default mode networks (p = .046) in comparison to their peers with lower AP. No group differences in FC of the salience network were observed (all p > .05).ConclusionsThese findings provide evidence that neurodevelopmental and behavioral differences in children with ASD and co-occurring AP emerge very early in life, before a reliable diagnosis of ADHD is typically made. Specifically, these results demonstrate that early inattention symptoms are associated with unique connectivity patterns in executive circuitry as early as the first years of life in toddlers and preschoolers with ASD, likely contributing to the phenotypic and neural heterogeneity recognized in autism. Thus, our results underscore the importance of considering co-occurring conditions early in developmental research and clinical care, as further understanding these trajectories can inform early interventions during the critical time period when they have the greatest potential for positive impact.Keywords: Autism spectrum, Attention, Functional MRI, Network connectivity, Early development

Background

Co-occurring neurodevelopmental conditions, including attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), are present in a majority of children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). As many as 70%–80% of children with ASD are diagnosed with at least one other neurodevelopmental condition, and an estimated 50% are diagnosed with multiple [25, 27, 28, 58]. ADHD is one of the most common co-occurring conditions in ASD, with up to 40% of preschoolers and up to 76% of adolescents with ASD exhibiting symptoms of ADHD [12, 19, 23, 62, 68, 73], and symptoms of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity at 12 months in infants with familial history of ASD shown to be predictive of diagnoses of ADHD at school age (Miler et al., 2018). There is some evidence that attentional problems (AP) are more commonly present in ASD than symptoms of hyperactivity [32, 68]. More broadly, even when additional neurodevelopmental conditions are not formally diagnosed, subclinical co-occurrence is common, with symptoms of ADHD often observed in children with ASD [29] and vice versa [17, 18], suggesting that the two conditions might share a common genetic influence [55], Stergiakouli et al., 2015).

Critically, co-occurrence of other neurodevelopmental conditions in the context of ASD has significant practical implications for children with autism and their families, as it is associated with increased need for services and poorer adaptive, social, educational, and health-related outcomes than ASD alone [9, 21, 61, 62, 72]. Co-occurrence is also linked with significant delays in age of ASD diagnosis when compared to children with ASD who have no additional symptoms, leading to delays in initiation of services and early intervention, critical for improving developmental outcomes [13, 21, 31, 45]. Studies in predominantly school-aged children with co-occurring ASD and ADHD (ASD + ADHD) have revealed greater developmental delays and increased challenges with adaptive behaviors (i.e., practical skills required for daily life) when compared to their peers with ASD or ADHD alone [40, 52, 59, 62]. Curiously, ASD + ADHD co-occurrence is also associated with greater difficulties with reciprocal social communication and interaction relative to children with ASD alone [36], despite this behavioral domain being a core diagnostic feature of autism [1]. This phenomenon has been reported across childhood, in preschoolers, school-age children, and adolescents [12, 42, 52, 64]. Finally, children with ASD + ADHD are more likely to experience adverse mental health effects, including elevated symptoms of anxiety and depression, relative to children with ASD or ADHD alone [6, 10, 12, 20, 64, 73].

Importantly, behavioral conditions co-occurring with autism – rather than core symptoms of autism alone – are a significant source of distress and a major concern for parents of children with ASD, persisting into adolescence and adulthood [20, 47]. Such co-occurring conditions have also been shown to have substantial negative long-term effects on health and quality of life in people with ASD and their families, including poorer educational and occupational outcomes and overall well-being across the lifespan [26, 49]. However, despite this evidence underscoring the need for early support and targeted interventions for children with ASD + ADHD, many children with ASD + ADHD never receive interventions targeting their ADHD symptoms [23]. Children with co-occurring ASD + ADHD also tend to benefit less from interventions targeting communication, self-regulation, and social skills than their peers with ASD or ADHD alone, or those with ASD and co-occurring anxiety [3, 56].

Taken together, there is considerable evidence that co-occurrence of other neurodevelopmental conditions, most commonly ADHD, is associated with lifelong impacts on health and quality of life in people with ASD and their families. However, remarkably little is known about brain maturation, function, and connectivity in young children with ASD and co-occurring attentional problems at the age when these conditions first manifest (i.e., in early childhood). Despite substantial evidence of shared genetic influences and environmental risk factors [41, 46, 65], our understanding of brain development underlying combined ASD and ADHD is limited. The few available neuroimaging studies in older, school-age children with ASD + ADHD reveal a pattern of distributed alterations in brain connectivity [11, 33]. Such studies, however, are relatively scarce and their findings are inconsistent, partly due to differences in methodology, cohort ascertainment, and the known heterogeneity of autism. As a result, specific patterns characterizing neurodevelopment in ASD + ADHD remain poorly understood. Rather, most of the evidence of ‘shared’ neurodevelopmental alterations – implicating distributed brain networks, including the default mode network, cognitive control (frontoparietal), attention, and salience networks in both ASD and ADHD – comes from studies of independent, single-condition (either ASD or ADHD) cohorts of older, school-age children [14, 24, 54, 66, 74], which are not directly comparable and do not extend to early childhood, when these neurodevelopmental conditions emerge [44].

One of the few extant studies examining brain connectivity patterns as a function of ADHD symptoms in 6–17 years old children reported that, in youth with autism, weaker functional connectivity in the frontoparietal and salience/ventral attention networks was linked to greater ADHD symptoms [71]. In brief, the frontoparietal brain network spans portions of dorsolateral and medial prefrontal cortex, and intraparietal sulcus, and is linked to cognitive control processes such as response inhibition (one of the core behavioral manifestations of ADHD), while the salience/ventral attention network, comprising the anterior cingulate cortex, insula, precuneus/posterior cingulate cortex, and supramarginal gyrus, is thought to support multiple components of attention, including error monitoring and selective attention [15, 16, 50]. The present study sought to test whether atypical functional connectivity in these networks can be detected in a cohort of toddlers and preschoolers with ASD and elevated AP, at an age before ADHD can be reliably diagnosed. Specifically, we set out to examine functional connectivity within the frontoparietal and salience/ventral attention networks, as well as between these networks and other brain large-scale brain networks in young children with ASD with and without co-occurring AP, compared to their typically developing (TD) peers, using resting-state fMRI data acquired during natural sleep. We hypothesized that among young children with ASD, presence of elevated AP would be associated with distinct functional connectivity patterns in these circuits. Additionally, we examined whether co-occurring AP are related to adaptive and developmental skills and autism symptoms among children with ASD.

Methods

Participants

This study included data from toddlers and preschoolers enrolled in the San Diego State University (SDSU) Toddler MRI Project, a longitudinal study of early brain development in ASD. Cross-sectional data (from one of the longitudinal study visits completed between 2016 and 2020) from 80 children with ASD and 42 TD children between 18 months and 5 years of age were included. Children with known genetic conditions (e.g., tuberous sclerosis, Fragile X), neurological conditions other than autism (e.g., cerebral palsy), history of perinatal CNS infection or gross CNS injury, nonfebrile seizures, and contraindications for MRI were excluded from enrollment in the study during the screening process. Additionally, prospective TD participants were screened and excluded for prematurity (less than 36-weeks gestation age) and/or family history (first degree relative) of ASD, intellectual disability, or heritable neuropsychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia, bipolar disorder). Given the importance of considering the impacts of psychotropic medication [35], all participants were screened for medication use,only 12 children were reported to take over-the-counter medications (e.g., allergy medications, vitamins and supplements), and no children were reported to take psychotropic medications at the time of participation. High-quality resting-state fMRI data acquired during natural sleep were available in a subset of these children (44 children with ASD and 33 TD children). Participants with ASD and TD children, both in the larger cohort and in the subset with fMRI data, were matched at the group level on age and racial distribution (see Table 1).

Diagnostic and developmental assessments

Upon enrollment, all children with ASD, or suspected to have ASD, underwent full diagnostic evaluation using standardized measures (as described below) in combination with clinical judgment. Only children who met diagnostic criteria for ASD on the DSM–5 (American Psychiatric Association, 2013), or clinical best estimate in very young children [48], were included in the ASD group. Because diagnostic evaluation was repeated for children who returned for follow-up visits (conducted 1½−2 years following the first study visit) in the context of the larger longitudinal study, only data from children with confirmed diagnosis at all visits were included in the current dataset. ASD diagnoses were supported by the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition (ADOS-2 [38],), the Social Communication Questionnaire (SCQ [57],) or the Autism Diagnostic Interview–Revised (ADI–R [39],), and expert clinical judgment. The ADOS-2 Calibrated Severity Scores (also known as Comparison Scores) served as a measure of current autism symptoms in children with ASD.

Developmental skills were assessed in all (TD and ASD) participants with the Mullen Scales of Early Learning (MSEL; Mullen, 1995), a clinician-administered standardized assessment of cognitive, language, and motor development, which yields age-corrected standard scores. In addition to standard scores, Developmental Quotients (DQ), which approximate a child's standing relative to age expectations, were derived for each developmental domain (receptive and expressive language skills, fine motor skills, and visual reception or non-verbal skills) as the ratio of age-equivalent scores to chronological age, multiplied by 100 [43]. The DQ metric was used to circumvent the relatively common floor effect associated with norm-referenced standard scores in young children with ASD (cf. [37]), which was present in 18% of our ASD cohort. The Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales, Second Edition, Survey Interview [63], a semi-structured interview yielding age-normed standard scores, was administered to caregivers to assess the child’s adaptive (or practical behavior skills demonstrated at home and community settings. Caregivers also completed the Child Behavior Checklist for ages 1½–5 years (CBCL,Achenbach & Rescorla, 2000, a measure designed to assess broadband behavioral and emotional symptoms, and the Social Communication Questionnaire (SCQ; [57], a screener for ASD, used to rule out ASD-like behaviors in TD children. All TD participants’ scores on the CBCL Total Problems subscale fell within the normative range (all T scores < 63), and no TD children exceeded the SCQ clinical cut-off score of 15 (all TD scores ≤ 10). For inclusion and retention in the TD group, children were also required to demonstrate (upon testing) developmental skills falling no more than 1.5 SD below the normative mean for their age.

Attentional problems (AP) were determined using the CBCL Attention Problems subscale, with a cutoff score of T ≥ 65 indicating presence of clinically significant symptoms (i.e., elevated AP). Among children with ASD, 36 out of 80 (45%) had scores above the clinical cutoff for AP, making up the ASDAP group (as in [62]). Children with ASD and AP T-scores below 65 made up the ASDlowAP group (44 out of 80, or 55%). Only one child within the TD group scored above the clinical cutoff for AP (T = 67), however this was no longer true at follow-up 18 months later (AP T = 50 at 37 months), and this child was thus retained in the TD group. The ASDAP and ASDlowAP groups did not significantly differ on any demographic variables (see Table 1).

MRI data acquisition and preprocessing

MRI data were acquired during natural sleep on a GE 3 T MR750 scanner using a Nova Medical 32-channel head coil and following established sleep MRI procedures as in our previous studies [7, 34]. Two fMRI datasets (6-min duration each) were collected using a multiband EPI sequence with high spatial and temporal resolution (TR = 800 ms, TE = 35 ms, 2 mm isotropic voxel size, 400 volumes per run). A fast 3D spoiled gradient recalled (FSPGR) T1-weighted sequence was used to acquire high-resolution structural images (0.8 mm isotropic voxel size). In an attempt to standardize sleep stage during scans, scanning always commenced after approximately 30–40 min of sleep, with fMRI sequences acquired first.

MRI data were preprocessed, denoised and analyzed in Matlab 2017b using SPM12 and the CONN toolbox v17f [69]. The structural images were converted from dicom to nifti format and were coregistered to the mean functional images, segmented and normalized to MNI space using non-linear registration (see [7]) and the default tissue probability maps included with SPM12. The white matter (WM) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) probability maps obtained from segmentation of the structural image for each individual subject were thresholded at 0.95 and eroded by 1 voxel. WM and CSF time courses were extracted from the thresholded and eroded masks using aCompCor for subsequent nuisance regression of the functional data. Functional images were corrected for susceptibility-induced distortions using the two spin-echo EPI acquisitions with opposite phase encoding directions and FSL’s TOPUP tools, and motion-corrected using rigid-body realignment as implemented in SPM12. The Artifact Detection Toolbox (ART, as installed with CONN v17f) was used to identify outliers in the functional image time series from the resulting 6 motion parameters (3 translational and 3 rotational) that had framewise displacement (FD) > 0.5 mm and/or changes in signal intensity that were greater than three standard deviations. fMRI data were normalized to MNI, bandpass filtered (0.008–0.08 Hz), and underwent nuisance regression (using the 6 motion parameters, their derivatives and WM and CSF timeseries derived from aCompCor to remove physiological noise and motion confounds. In-scanner head motion (root mean squared displacement; RMSD, used as an estimate of data quality, was derived from the 6 motion parameters and used as a covariate in subsequent functional connectivity (FC analyses.

Functional connectivity analyses and statistical strategy

All statistical analyses were conducted using R [51]. We examined group differences (ASD vs. TD, ASDAP vs. ASDlowAP) in developmental and adaptive skills and autism symptoms, with group differences in AP examined only in the ASD vs. TD comparison to avoid circularity. All group-level behavioral analyses were performed using general Linear models controlling for age. For FC estimates, fMRI time series were first concatenated across the two runs and averaged across all voxels within 7 canonical brain functional networks derived from the Human Connectome Project and included with the CONN toolbox [69]. FC of the frontoparietal (FPN) and salience (SN) networks with other brain networks (default-mode, visual, sensorimotor, dorsal attention, and language) was estimated using bivariate Pearson correlations (standardized with a Fisher z-transform) between the time series averaged across the corresponding networks. Separate general linear models, controlling for age and RMSD, were used to test group effects (ASD vs. TD,ASDAP vs. ASDlowAP) on FC estimates. Results are reported at an uncorrected p < 0.05 as well as corrected for multiple comparisons using Benjamini–Hochberg False Discovery Rate [5],FDR) at q < 0.1, and effect sizes are indicated with partial eta squared, ηp2. For FC estimates with significant group effects, permutation testing was conducted with 1,000 iterations to further assess the significance of the observed effect. For each iteration, the group assignments were randomly shuffled and the results recalculated. Additionally, for significant group effects in FC, follow-up partial correlations were calculated between FC estimates and developmental skills, adaptive behaviors, and autism symptoms, while controlling for the child’s age.

Additionally, when compared to their peers with ASDlowAP, children with ASDAP had lower adaptive skills across all domains measured with Vineland, including communication skills (β = –10.68, t77 = –3.04, p = 0.003, pFDR = 0.014, ηp2 = 0.107), daily living skills (β = –6.15, t77 = –2.33, p = 0.022, pFDR = 0.007, ηp2 = 0.066), socialization (β = –7.79, t77 = –3.44, p = 0.001, pFDR = 0.014, ηp2 = 0.134), and motor skills (β = –4.65, t77 = –2.09, p = 0.040, pFDR = 0.093, ηp2 = 0.053), as well as the overall Adaptive Behavior Composite (ABC; β = –7.37, t77 = –3.23, p = 0.002, pFDR = 0.014, ηp2 = 0.119) (see Table 2 and Fig. 1B). Finally, the two ASD subgroups did not differ on autism symptoms measured with the ADOS-2 Comparison Score (β = 0.150, t75 = 0.282, p = 0.778, pFDR = 0.837, ηp2 = 0.007), indicating that the observed differences in developmental and adaptive skills cannot be attributed to variability in levels of autism symptoms.

Functional connectivity of the frontoparietal and salience networks in children with ASD with and without elevated AP

Within the subset of children with successfully completed fMRI scans, there were no significant differences on FC of the FPN and SN between children with ASD and TD peers, controlling for age and head motion during the scan (RMSD; all p > 0.05, see Table 3 and Fig. 2A). However, when taking presence of AP into account, children with ASDAP had reduced FC within the FPN (β = –0.246, t40 = –2.30, p = 0.027, pFDR = 0.094, ppermutation = 0.013, ηp2 = 0.117), between the FPN and language network (β = –0.174, t40 = –3.02, p = 0.004, pFDR = 0.028, ppermutation = 0.007, ηp2 = 0.186), and between the FPN and default mode network (β = –0.307, t40 = –2.06, p = 0.046, pFDR = 0.107, ppermutation < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.096), when compared to children with ASDlowAP (Fig. 2B). No other significant group differences in FC between the FPN, SN, and other networks were observed.

Finally, follow-up partial correlations revealed significant associations between within-FPN FC and Vineland communication (partial r = 0.306, p = 0.046) and daily living skills (partial r = 0.340, p = 0.025) domains after controlling for age (Fig. 2C); there were no other significant associations between FC and behavioral measures.

Discussion

Attentional problems, adaptive and developmental skills, autism symptoms, and FC of the frontoparietal and salience brain networks were investigated in a cohort of toddlers and preschoolers with and without ASD participating in a study of early neurodevelopment in autism using natural sleep MRI. Our analyses focusing on behavioral and functional connectivity patterns in children with ASD with and without co-occurring AP revealed two principal findings: First, regarding behavioral and developmental skills, we observed – as expected – elevated levels of attentional problems and lower adaptive and developmental skills in children with ASD relative to their TD peers. Notably, however, when children with ASD were divided into subgroups based on the presence of clinically significant attentional problems (AP), children with ASD and elevated AP had lower developmental and adaptive skills than their peers with ASD and lower AP. These findings contribute to the relatively limited literature on ASD + ADHD co-occurrence in early childhood by demonstrating that elevated AP can be identified in young children (toddlers and preschoolers) with ASD and is associated with greater developmental delays and lower adaptive behaviors. Critically, our findings showed that lower developmental and adaptive skills in children with ASD and elevated AP were not due to greater autism-related behaviors, as levels of autism symptoms did not differ between young children with ASD with and without elevated AP. Interestingly, this finding is in contrast with the reports of associations between attentional problems and increased autism symptoms in older, school-aged children and adolescents with ASD (e.g., [64], suggesting that AP and ASD symptoms may become increasingly confounded (in terms of their behavioral manifestation, ascertainment, or both) later in childhood. This possibility of the age-varying associations between autism and ADHD symptoms across childhood has been recently demonstrated in a relatively large cohort of children who were diagnosed with ASD at ages 2–5 years and had been followed prospectively across eight time points until age 12 [8].

Our second notable finding pertains to the observed links between elevated AP and patterns of functional connectivity in frontoparietal circuitry (which putatively supports inhibition and cognitive control) among young children with ASD. Namely, while FC of the FPN appeared quite similar between children with ASD and their TD peers, significantly reduced connectivity of the FPN was detected among children with ASD and elevated attentional problems, relative to their peers with ASD and relatively low AP. Specifically, the strongest effect of co-occurring AP was observed in FC between the FPN and the language network, with reduced FC also identified within the FPN, and between the FPN and default mode network. These findings are consistent with evidence of weaker functional connectivity of the FPN identified in older, school-age youth with ASD and co-occurring ADHD reported by Yerys et al. [71]. More broadly, our findings are also in line with evidence of differential brain function and structure in brain inhibitory circuits observed between children with ASD and ADHD, including unique patterns of brain activation during inhibition tasks [2] and distinct cortical morphology patterns, including cortical thickness and volume [4]. Within this context, the unique contribution of our study and its results is in illustrating that AP in the context of ASD may disrupt or affect brain functional connectivity earlier than previously recognized, in the first years of life. Furthermore, the observed effects of early-life AP appear to be limited to the brain inhibitory circuitry (frontoparietal brain network). While altered functional connectivity of the salience network had been linked to ADHD symptoms in school-aged youth with ASD [11, 71], connectivity of the salience network did not appear to vary as a function of elevated AP in our cohort of young children with ASD, suggesting that ADHD-related differences in this circuitry may emerge later in childhood and adolescence.

Overall, these results provide new evidence that the behavioral and neurodevelopmental differences observed in children and adolescents with ASD and co-occurring ADHD may already be present in early childhood, before a reliable diagnosis of ADHD can be made. Not only does this suggest that attentional problems can be identified in toddlers and preschoolers with ASD, but it also supports the transdiagnostic framework through which developmental conditions like ASD and ADHD are increasingly being conceptualized. Specifically, heterogeneity – or remarkable variability – at the level of clinical symptoms and outcomes, developmental trajectories, as well as underlying neurobiology is increasingly recognized as a notable characteristic of autism [22, 30, 70]. This heterogeneity may be, at least in part, explained or better understood by considering the co-occurring conditions – such as elevated AP – which are commonly present in autism and, as shown here, appear to meaningfully differentiate among children with ASD at an early age, along both behavioral and neurodevelopmental outcomes. Importantly, while elevated AP appeared instrumental in revealing distinct brain connectivity patterns among preschoolers with ASD, the autism diagnosis alone was not predictive of functional connectivity, as demonstrated by the comparisons between the ASD group as a whole and neurotypical peers. This emphasizes that the traditional case–control approach utilized for decades in autism research may conceal – rather than help uncover – the underlying neurobiology of autism. Rather, our findings suggest that co-occurrence of other neurodevelopmental conditions in the context of ASD is a meaningful neurobiological feature that should be considered in our efforts to parse the marked heterogeneity associated with autism.

While this study is one of the first to examine early neurobehavioral development in toddlers with ASD and co-occurring attentional problems, the findings must be interpreted in the context of several methodological limitations. First, we utilized a cross-sectional dataset spanning the first years of life, allowing us to study in-depth a relatively understudied early developmental window. However, cross-sectional data did not allow us to track developmental changes over time and to examine prospective associations between early attentional problems and neurodevelopment, and later clinical outcomes. Future work should aim to examine these developmental trajectories longitudinally, and should consider the possibility of employing machine learning-based models to predict the development of attentional problems in young children with autism. Future research with larger sample sizes should additionally consider examining whether sex differences interact with symptoms of ADHD in young children with autism. Second, due to the young age of our cohort, we were unable to include an ADHD comparison group to examine developmental and adaptive skills and functional connectivity in children with ADHD without co-occurring ASD. On the other hand, because of the young age, all the children enrolled in the study were medication-naive, which excluded the possibility of confounding effects of stimulant medications on functional connectivity estimates or neurocognitive effects associated with ADHD (unlike in most studies with older children; cf. [35]. Finally, it is important to mention that neuroimaging studies during natural sleep in young children with autism can be very challenging, and as such not all children enrolled in the current study had usable fMRI data. However, it is worth noting that natural sleep MRI allows for inclusion of children in neuroimaging research who have much higher support needs, allowing for a sample that is more representative of the full range of the autistic population (indeed, autism symptom severity did not predict scan success in our cohort).

Conclusions

To conclude, we observed for the first time lower adaptive and developmental skills and distinctly reduced functional connectivity of the frontoparietal network in toddlers and preschoolers with ASD and co-occurring attentional problems, when compared to their peers with ASD and relatively lower attentional problems, despite comparable levels of autism symptoms in these two subgroups. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of considering co-occurring conditions in both research and clinical practice with young children with ASD, as they may be associated with altered neurodevelopmental and behavioral trajectories as early as at 2 years of age. Given the scarcity of neuroimaging literature in toddlers with ASD, it is essential to continue examining these trajectories in larger, longitudinal cohorts, to further address such questions.
