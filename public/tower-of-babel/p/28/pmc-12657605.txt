The JPX/miR-495-3p/BRD4 axis mediates quercetin-induced toxicity via the X-inactivation center in NSCLC
Authors: Xiaofeng Lou, Jie Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Quercetin, JPX, miR-495-3p, BRD4, NSCLC

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the most lethal malignancy worldwide (Zhang, 2020), with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) representing 85 % of cases (Relli, 2019, Shi, 2020). Although molecular targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors have revolutionized treatment paradigms (Grant et al., 2021, Deng, 2022), their efficacy is often limited by drug resistance and significant adverse effects, underscoring the continuous need for novel therapeutic agents.

Flavonoids, a major class of dietary polyphenols, exhibit broad therapeutic potential against various diseases (Zeng et al., 2019). Quercetin, a ubiquitous dietary flavonoid (Yang, 2021), presents a complex profile in toxicological research, exhibiting a “double-edged sword” character with documented chemopreventive benefits alongside context-dependent adverse effects (Wong et al., 2020). Extensive studies highlight its role in multiple pathologies, such as cancer, metabolic disorders, neurodegeneration, and cardiovascular diseases (Wang, 2021, Shen, 2021). Its well-established antitumor properties, including the induction of apoptosis and suppression of proliferation, have been demonstrated across numerous in vitro studies (Koren Carmi, 2020, Tang, 2020). Additionally, quercetin could modulate key enzymes and signaling pathways involved in carcinogenesis (Lei, 2019). However, a key unanswered question for its therapeutic translation is how the mechanism confers selective toxicity to cancer cells while sparing normal ones. Elucidating this is essential for evaluating its therapeutic window and minimizing off-target effects.

Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), a class of functional noncoding RNAs without an open reading frame (Uchida and Dimmeler, 2015), play pivotal roles in regulating genome integrity, epigenetic modification, and gene expression (Bridges et al., 2021, Gil and Ulitsky, 2020). Increasing studies have revealed that aberrantly expressed lncRNAs contribute to tumorigenesis across various cancer types (Bhan et al., 2017). These transcripts exert their functions through diverse mechanisms, particularly by acting as miRNA sponges to modulate cancer-related gene networks. Such regulatory properties position lncRNAs as potential diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets. We hypothesized that quercetin's anticancer effects might be mediated through the specific modulation of oncogenic lncRNAs, thereby providing a layer of selectivity. Our investigation focused on JPX, a lncRNA known to function within the X-chromosome inactivation center (XIC) to orchestrate epigenetic regulation. While JPX has been implicated in oncogenesis, its role in NSCLC and, more importantly, its responsiveness to natural compounds like quercetin are unknown.

In this study, we therefore sought to investigate whether quercetin exerts its antitumor effects in NSCLC through the specific targeting of the JPX-mediated regulatory axis. The well-established NSCLC cell lines A549 and H1299 were chosen to mechanistically explore quercetin, acknowledging their limitations in modeling primary tumor physiology while valuing them as essential initial tools. We systematically demonstrate that quercetin effectively suppresses NSCLC cell viability, proliferation, and migration. We identify the upregulation of JPX as a key oncogenic driver in NSCLC and reveal that quercetin acts through the novel JPX/miR-495-3p/BRD4 axis to disrupt epigenetic regulation centered on the XIC. Overall, our findings shed the new light on the functions of quercetin in the treatment of NSCLC.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and transfection

The human NSCLC cell lines A549 and H1299, as well as the immortalized human bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS-2B, were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All cells were cultured according to the manufacturer's instructions. A549 and H1299 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin. BEAS-2B cells were cultured in Bronchial Epithelial Cell Growth Medium (BEGM, Lonza) supplemented with the recommended growth factors and hormones. All cells were kept at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5 % CO2, and the medium was refreshed every 48 h. JPX small interfering RNA (siRNA, GenePharma, China) and the corresponding control RNA (siRNA NC), or recombinant plasmid overexpressing JPX with the empty pcDNA3.1 vector (Tiandz, China) were transfected into cells in logarithmic growth phase. The transfection was performed using the Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The sequence of siRNAs used is shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Drug treatment

Quercetin (purity ≥ 98 %, CAS No. 849061–97-8, Sigma, USA) stock solution was prepared in DMSO according to the manufacturer's instructions. For quercetin treatment, NSCLC cells were incubated in culture medium containing various concentrations (0.1, 1, 10 and 100 μM) of quercetin at 37 °C for 48 h. As a control, NSCLC cell lines were treated with equivalent volumes of DMSO.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 8 % sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5 % skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation with specific primary antibodies at 4 °C. After washing three times with PBS containing 0.1 % Tween-20, membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Following three additional washes, membranes were imaged using Pierce ECL Western Blotting Substrate. The antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, 1 μg total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript-II-Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, China). RT-qPCR was performed on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). Relative mRNA levels were normalized to Actin using the comparative Ct method. Primer sequences for RT-qPCR are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) incorporation assay

Cells were seeded onto coverslips in 6-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h. BrdU (10 µM, Sigma Aldrich, B5002) was added to the culture medium for 2 h. Then, cells were then fixed in cold 70 % ethanol for 5 min at room temperature, treated with 1.5 N HCl for 30 min, and blocked with blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were incubated with BrdU primary antibody (BD, 555627, 1:250) and subsequently with Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated secondary antibody diluted in 1x PBS with 1 % BSA for 1.5 h at room temperature in the dark. Coverslips were mounted onto slides using a DAPI-containing mounting solution (SouthernBiotech, 0100–20). Images were captured using a Zeiss Axio Observer microscope.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min and permeabilized in 0.5 % Triton X-100 for 30 min. After three washes with PBS, cells were blocked with 3 % BSA for 1 h and incubated with specific primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following three washes with PBS, cells were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Scratch assay

For the scratch assay, cells were seeded in six-well plates and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium until they reached confluence. A uniform scratch was created with a sterile pipette tip. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated in medium containing 1 % FBS to inhibit proliferation. Wound closure was monitored at 0 and 24 h using phase-contrast microscopy. The relative wound area was quantified with ImageJ software, and all assays were conducted with three biological replicates.

Cell invasion assay

Cells (1 × 105) were seeded in the upper chambers of Transwell plates in serum-free RPMI-1640 medium. Complete medium containing 10 % FBS was added to the lower chamber. After 24 h of incubation, invasive cells were fixed with 4 % formaldehyde for 30 min and stained with crystal violet for 30 min. After extensive washing with PBS, invasive cells were observed and counted under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Cell viability assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured overnight at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2. CCK-8 solution (10 µL, Takara, USA) was added to each well, and cells were incubated for 4 h. Absorbance values were measured at 450 nm.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from three independent biological replicates. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.3.0. The normality and homogeneity of variances were verified using the Shapiro-Wilk and Brown-Forsythe tests, respectively. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired Student's t-test was used. For comparisons across multiple groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA was employed as appropriate to the experimental design. A significant ANOVA result was followed by appropriate post-hoc tests for pairwise comparisons. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Inhibitory effects of quercetin on the cell proliferation and migration of NSCLC.

To determine the role of quercetin in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), we initially explored the effects of quercetin on the cell proliferation and migration of NSCLC cells. The chemical structure formula of quercetin was shown as Fig. 1A. Next, human NSCLC cell lines (A549 and H1299) were treated with quercetin at different concentrations (0.1, 1, 10, 50, 100 μM) for 24 h. We performed CCK-8 assay to detect the effects of quercetin on the cell survival rate of normal human lung epithelial cells and NSCLC cells (Fig. 1B and S1A). We observed the inhibitory effects of quercetin at the concentration of 10, 50 and 100 μM in both two cell lines. We further identified whether quercetin could decrease NSCLC cell viability. The CCK-8 and BrdU incorporation assays were performed to examine the viability of A549 and H1299 cells treated with quercetin. The results showed that 10 μM quercetin significantly inhibits NSCLC cells proliferation in corresponding A549 and H1299 cells, respectively (1C and 1D and Fig. S1B). Besides, scratch and Transwell assays were conducted to assess the migration and invasion ability of cells. Similar with the proliferation assay, the results of cell scratch and Transwell assays indicated that 10 μM quercetin treatment could suppress the migration of A549 and H1299 cells (Fig. 1, Fig. 1 and S1E). Altogether, these results indicated the suppressive effects of quercetin on the cell proliferation and migration growth and proliferation in NSCLC cells.

JPX is upregulated and promotes NSCLC cells proliferation and migration

Given that some previous studies have widely reported that lncRNAs participate in the development of many cancer types (Li, 2018), we first examined the differential expression of lncRNAs in NSCLC cells (Fig. 2A). According to heatmap results, we selected lncRNA LUADT1, ZDHHC8P1, JPX, LINP1, AGAP2-AS1 as candidate genes in the subsequent experiments and we further found that JPX increased significantly in both two NSCLC cell lines (Fig. 2B). To determine whether lncRNAs were involved in the quercetin-mediated anti-cancer effects in NSCLC cells, we then performed RT-qPCR analysis to identify differentially expressed lncRNAs in quercetin treated human NSCLC cell lines. As shown in Fig. 2, Fig. 2, the expression of JPX was the lowest expressed lncRNA in 10 μM quercetin treated NSCLC cell lines. Therefore, we selected JPX as the potential target of quercetin. To deeply uncover the function of JPX RNA in NSCLC cells, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were employed to specifically knockdown JPX RNA, whereas the full-length recombinant plasmid with JPX was used to elevate JPX RNA (Fig. S2A). We found that si-JPX #1 could effectively downregulate JPX expression (Fig. 2E). Meantime, JPX RNA elevated when transfected with the JPX recombinant plasmid (Fig. 2F). CCK-8 (Fig. 2, Fig. 2) and scratch assays (Fig. 2, Fig. 2) revealed that JPX depletion could inhibit the growth and proliferation of A549 and H1299 cells, while the results of JPX overexpression were opposite (Fig. S2B and S2C). Overall, these results indicated that JPX is upregulated and promotes NSCLC cells proliferation and migration.

Quercetin suppresses NSCLC cells proliferation and migration by inhibiting JPX

To explore whether quercetin could exert its effects through the suppression of JPX, A549 and H1299 cells were treated with 10 μM quercetin or transfected with overexpression of JPX. Notably, we found that the cell viability of A549 and H1299 cells treated with quercetin was markedly attenuated while JPX overexpression reversed the cell viability of A549 and H1299 cells (Fig. 3A). The results of BrdU incorporation assay indicated that the suppressive functions of quercetin on the proliferation of A549 and H1299 cells could be reversed by JPX overexpression (Fig. 3B). Similar results were obtained using scratch assay (Fig. 3C and S3A). Transwell invasion assay showed that JPX overexpression could restore the invasion of A549 and H1299 cells inhibited by quercetin (Fig. 3D and S3B). Therefore, these observations confirmed that quercetin could suppress the proliferation and migration of NSCLC cells by inhibiting JPX.

JPX promotes proliferation and metastasis of NSCLC cells by inhibiting miR-495-3p

Here, we expected to obtain differentially miRNAs that bind with JPX. We used the miRNA-lncRNA interaction database (https://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/starbase2/index.php) to predict the potential miRNAs which could interact with JPX. The results showed that JPX shares binding sites with miR-495-3p, indicating its interaction with miR-495-3p (Fig. 4A). Compared with BEAS-2B cells, miR-495-3p in A549 and H1299 cells was downregulated (Fig. 4B). Next, we found that quercetin treatment could increase the level of miR-495-3p in A549 and H1299 cells (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, miR-495-3p was elevated when the A549 and H1299 cells were transfected with JPX siRNA (Fig. 4D). However, JPX reduced when the A549 and H1299 cells were transfected with miR-495-3p mimics (Fig. 4E). To investigate whether JPX regulates the cell phenotype via sponging miR-495-3p, the results of CCK-8 and BrdU incorporation assays have suggested that JPX overexpression could reverse the cell growth suppression induced by miR-495-3p overexpression in A549 and H1299 cells (Fig. 4, Fig. 4 and S4A). Similar results were obtained using scratch assay (Fig. 4H). Additionally, the Transwell assay found that miR-495-3p significantly suppressed the invasion of A549 and H1299 cells, while the effects were abrogated by JPX overexpression (Fig. 4I and S4B). Therefore, these collective results above demonstrated that JPX promoted proliferation and metastasis of NSCLC cells by inhibiting miR-495-3p.

BRD4 is the target gene of miR-495-3p, with abundant expression in NSCLC cells

Now, we have found that miR-495-3p could inhibit the malignant biological behavior of NSCLC cells. To further uncover the mechanism of miR-495-3p’s action, two databases (TargetScan and RNAhybrid) were employed to predict the downstream target genes of miR-495-3p. The results showed that BRD4, a key transcriptional regulator, could function as a downstream target gene of miR-495-3p (Fig. 5A). Compared with BEAS-2B cells, the expression of BRD4 in A549 and H1299 cells was markedly increased (Fig. 5, Fig. 5). Complementary sequences in the 3′-UTR of BRD4 mRNA and miR-495-3p was found, which may be the direct binding site (Fig. 5D). To further verify the effect of miR-495-3p on BRD4, we constructed miR-495-3p interference and overexpression system (Fig. 5E). Both BRD4 protein and mRNA levels decreased after overexpression of miR-495-3p and increased after inhibition of miR-495-3p (Fig. 5, Fig. 5). Furthermore, we have examined the levels of BRD4, miR-495-3p, and JPX RNA in NSCLC overexpressing JPX and treated with quercetin (Fig. S5A and S5B), as well as overexpressing miR-495-3p and treated with quercetin (Fig. S5C and S5D). Altogether, these results suggested BRD4 is the target gene of miR-495-3p and quercetin’s anticancer effects depends on the competitive effect of JPX on miR-495-3p.

Quercetin suppresses NSCLC proliferation and migration through inhibition of BRD4

To further investigate the role of BRD4 during quercetin’s anticancer effects on NSCLC cells, we detected and found that BRD4 downregulated markedly in A549 and H1299 cells after quercetin treatment (Fig. 6A). We next constructed separate siRNA and overexpression vectors of BRD4 (Fig. 6B). We performed BrdU incorporation and scratch assays, and then observed that BRD4 knockdown or overexpression obviously suppressed or promoted cell proliferation in A549 and H1299 cells, respectively (Fig. 6, Fig. 6). In addition, the results of Transwell assay suggested that BRD4 knockdown suppressed the invasion of A549 and H1299 cells and BRD4 overexpression promoted the invasion (Fig. 6E). The aforementioned data demonstrated that quercetin suppressed NSCLC proliferation and migration through inhibition of BRD4.

BRD4 inhibition contributes to the suppression of NSCLC proliferation and migration

The results of western blotting showed that quercetin, BRD4 inhibitors (JQ-1 and I-BET-762) significantly reduced the expression of BRD4 (Fig. 7A). Using BrdU incorporation (Fig. 7B and S6A) and scratch assays (Fig. 7C), we discovered quercetin, JQ-1 and I-BET-762 inhibited the proliferation of A549 and H1299 cells. Accordingly, Transwell assay showed that quercetin, JQ-1 and I-BET-762 suppressed the invasion of A549 and H1299 cells (Fig. 7D and S6B). The collective results suggest that BRD4 inhibition contributes to the suppression of NSCLC cells proliferation and migration, which further proves that quercetin's inhibition of NSCLC cells is dependent on BRD4.

Discussion

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related death globally (Collins, 2007, Wu, 2009), with NSCLC accounting for approximately 85 % of cases and exhibiting a poor prognosis (Socinski, 2016). During the past decade, important advancements in the treatment of NSCLC have been achieved. Although treatment strategies such as surgery and targeted therapies have advanced (Howington, 2013), the overall survival rate remains low, necessitating the exploration of novel therapeutic agents and targets (Balata, 2019). In this study, we propose a novel mechanism by which quercetin inhibits the proliferation and migration of NSCLC cells. We demonstrate that the JPX-miR-495-3p-BRD4 axis constitutes a critical downstream pathway mediating quercetin's antitumor effects (Fig. 7E). Our findings clarify a previously unrecognized molecular network in NSCLC and provide a new target for clinical investigation.

Accumulating evidence indicates that quercetin exerts inhibitory effects on various cancers through multiple mechanisms (Balata, 2019), including influencing cell signal transduction, promoting apoptosis, inhibiting proliferation (Chuang, 2016) and suppressing migration (Balakrishnan, 2016, Liu, 2017). Recent human and animal studies have confirmed its therapeutic potential against NSCLC cells (Alsharairi, 2023, Zhou, 2023, Chang, 2017). For instance, quercetin inhibits migration and invasion of A549 and HCC827 cells by suppressing ADAM9 and modulating the Akt pathway (Baby et al., 2018), and it induces apoptosis and cell cycle arrest via multiple pathways in NSCLC (Zhou et al., 2019, Alam, 2022, Hasan, 2023). However, the underlying mechanisms have not been fully elucidated. Our data reveal that quercetin suppresses NSCLC cells by inhibiting the lncRNA JPX-mediated pathway, thereby offering a new direction for targeted therapy.

Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), defined as RNA transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides (Choi et al., 2019, Hovhannisyan and Gabaldón, 2021), play crucial roles in regulating gene expression and are involved in diverse biological processes such as cell growth, apoptosis, migration, and invasion (Engreitz et al., 2016). They are closely associated with cancer development (Bhan et al., 2017) and have emerged as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets (Jin, 2021). Specifically, lncRNA JPX, known for its role in X-chromosome inactivation, has been reported to contribute to tumor progression (Pan, 2020, Jin, 2020). In our study, we found that quercetin downregulates JPX expression in NSCLC cells. Functional assays demonstrated that JPX promotes malignant phenotypes, and its overexpression attenuates the antitumor effects of quercetin. These results indicate that JPX is a key downstream effector of quercetin in NSCLC pathogenesis.

CeRNAs, novel post-transcriptional regulators, regulate the occurrence and development of tumor through miRNA-mediated mechanism (Karreth and Pandolfi, 2013). MicroRNAs (miRNAs), a class of small noncoding RNAs, regulate gene expression by targeting mRNAs for degradation or translational repression and are involved in various cellular activities and diseases, including cancer (Liu et al., 2014). In NSCLC, miRNAs are recognized as critical regulators, with studies showing that competitive inhibition of specific miRNAs like miR-361-3p and miR-615-5p can promote cancer progression (Chen, 2019), and tumor-derived exosomal miRNAs hold promise as diagnostic biomarkers (Jin, 2017). Our research adds to this field by demonstrating that JPX functions as a ceRNA for miR-495-3p, thereby contributing to NSCLC development.

Bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4), a member of the BET family, is characterized by two tandem bromodomains (Donati et al., 2018) and plays vital roles in transcription, replication, and DNA repair (Lam, 2020). It is implicated in various physiological processes and diseases, including cancers, cardiovascular diseases, and neurological disorders (Li, 2021). Studies have shown that BRD4 inhibition can suppress renal apoptosis by modulating FoxO4-dependent ROS generation via the PI3K/AKT pathway (Liu, 2019), and exosomal circLPAR1 inhibits BRD4 to suppress colorectal tumorigenesis (Zheng, 2022). While BRD4 exhibits context-dependent roles, being tumor-suppressive in breast cancer (Wu, 2020), it often acts as an oncogene; for example, lncRNA SNHG18 facilitates NSCLC growth and metastasis by regulating the miR-211-5p/BRD4 axis (Fan, 2021). Our results confirm that the suppression of NSCLC cell proliferation and migration by quercetin is dependent on BRD4 inhibition, underscoring the prominent role of BRD4 in NSCLC pathogenesis.

In conclusion, our study elucidates a novel pathway in which quercetin inhibits NSCLC progression by downregulating lncRNA JPX, which in turn releases miR-495-3p from its sponging activity, leading to the subsequent downregulation of BRD4. This JPX-miR-495-3p-BRD4 axis provides a mechanistic basis for the antitumor effects of quercetin and highlights potential new targets for the diagnosis and treatment of NSCLC.
