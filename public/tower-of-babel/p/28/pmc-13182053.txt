A single nucleotide mutation in FngMYB10 causes white fruit phenotype in Fragaria nilgerrensis
Authors: Qiang Cao, Xinyue Zhang, Jiamin Li, Yuanling Xia, Furong Wang, Sifan Wang, Ticao Zhang, Qin Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFruit color is a critical agronomic trait that determines consumer acceptance and market value in strawberry (Fragaria spp.). Fragaria nilgerrensis, a diploid strawberry endemic to Southwest China, exhibits a stable white fruit phenotype, but its genetic basis has not been experimentally validated.ResultsHere, we demonstrate that a single nucleotide mutation causing an R82S amino acid substitution in the core transcription factor FngMYB10 is the direct molecular cause of anthocyanin deficiency in F. nilgerrensis. Through site-directed mutagenesis, transient expression, and stable transformation experiments, we show that replacing serine with arginine at position 82 effectively activates the expression of key genes in the entire flavonoid biosynthesis pathway and recovers anthocyanin synthesis and fruit pigmentation. Yeast two-hybrid and bimolecular fluorescence complementation assays reveal that the R82S mutation specifically disrupts the critical protein-protein interaction between FngMYB10 and its transcriptional cofactor FngbHLH33, but does not alter its interaction with FngMYC1. These results indicate that FngbHLH33 is a key cofactor for anthocyanin synthesis in F. nilgerrensis, while FngMYC1 likely acts in other tissues or metabolic branches. Structural modeling revealed that this mutation eliminates essential hydrogen bonds within the conserved bHLH-interaction motif, preventing assembly of the functional MYB-bHLH-WD40 transcriptional complex required for anthocyanin gene activation.ConclusionsThe results confirm that the R82S missense mutation in FngMYB10 of F. nilgerrensis specifically disrupts its interaction with the key cofactor FngbHLH33, blocking the assembly of the functional MBW complex and ultimately leading to the complete loss of fruit anthocyanin biosynthesis. These findings advance our understanding of the transcriptional regulatory network of fruit color formation and offer direct targets for molecular breeding of strawberry fruit color.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08662-z.

Introduction

Fruit color is a key factor determining consumer acceptance and market value of strawberry (Fragaria spp.), directly influencing purchase decisions and fruit quality evaluation [1]. The vibrant red color of most commercial strawberry varieties originates from anthocyanin accumulation, a class of water-soluble flavonoid pigments with multiple biological functions, including ultraviolet protection, pathogen resistance, and pollinator attraction [2]. Additionally, anthocyanins serve as potent antioxidants with important health-promoting effects for humans, playing crucial roles in cardiovascular health and neuroprotection [3, 4].

Anthocyanin biosynthesis follows a well-characterized flavonoid metabolic pathway, initiated by phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), followed sequentially by chalcone synthase (CHS), chalcone isomerase (CHI), flavanone 3-hydroxylase (F3H), dihydroflavonol 4-reductase (DFR), anthocyanidin synthase (ANS), and finally completed by UDP-glucose: flavonoid 3-O-glucosyltransferase (UFGT) to form stable anthocyanin glucosides [5, 6]. This complex multi-step biochemical process requires precise transcriptional regulation. In plants, transcriptional regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis structural genes by R2R3-MYB transcription factors is the primary mechanism controlling pigment accumulation [7, 8]. In strawberry, MYB10 is the main regulatory factor for anthocyanin biosynthesis during fruit development [9, 10]. Functional analysis of diploid woodland strawberry (F. vesca) and octoploid cultivated strawberry (F. × ananassa) showed that MYB10 directly or indirectly activates key structural genes in the anthocyanin pathway, including early biosynthesis genes (CHS, CHI, F3H) and late biosynthesis genes (DFR, ANS, UFGT), as well as transport-related genes such as glutathione S-transferase (GST) [5, 6].

As the core transcription factor for anthocyanin biosynthesis, MYB10 does not act independently but cooperates with bHLH transcription factors and WD40 proteins to form a ternary MBW (MYB-bHLH-WD40) transcriptional regulatory complex, which is indispensable for effective activation of anthocyanin synthesis genes [7, 11]. MBW complex function depends on precise molecular interactions between component proteins. The R3 domain of MYB proteins contains a conserved bHLH binding motif [DE]Lx2[RK]x3Lx6Lx3R, where single-point mutations of any key amino acid in this motif may disrupt interactions with bHLH transcription factors [12, 13]. In strawberry, research has identified bHLH interaction partners, including FaEGL3, FabHLH3, FabHLH33, and FaMYC1, which form functional complexes with various MYB proteins to co-regulate flavonoid biosynthesis [14]. FaTTG1 (WD40) functions to maintain complex stability and enhance transcriptional efficiency [11]. The FaMYB10-FabHLH3/FabHLH33-FaTTG1 MBW complex in strawberry is considered the key mechanism regulating anthocyanin metabolism [10, 15].

In natural populations, the MYB10 gene is an important evolutionary hotspot for strawberry fruit color variation, with loss-of-function mutations typically directly causing white fruit phenotypes [16–18]. For example, in woodland strawberry ‘Yellow Wonder’, a G-to-C transversion causes a W12S amino acid substitution in the R2 DNA-binding domain, resulting in loss of function and changing fruit color from red to yellow [16]. Similarly, octoploid strawberry exhibits multiple MYB10 alleles including frameshift mutations, transposon insertions, and promoter deletions, which collectively explain natural color diversity in cultivated populations [5, 19]. In wild strawberries (such as F. vesca, F. pentaphylla, F. nubicola, and F. nilgerrensis), MYB10 polymorphism between white and red fruit individuals has been reported and is thought to be the key genetic basis for fruit color variation [18].

Fragaria nilgerrensis Schlecht is a diploid species endemic to Southwest China, with mature fruits consistently remaining white [20]. Although F. nilgerrensis has attracted attention in strawberry breeding projects due to its unique flavor characteristics [21, 22], the molecular basis of its stable white fruit phenotype remains insufficiently elucidated. Previously, Zhang et al. found that sequence variations in the upstream regulatory region of FnMYB10 in F. nilgerrensis led to reduced expression levels, potentially explaining the white fruit phenotype [20]. Beyond the promoter regulatory mechanism, our pan-genome analysis further revealed a specific missense mutation (c.246G > T, p.R82S) in the MYB10 gene of F. nilgerrensis compared to other red-fruited strawberry species. This mutation is located in the critical bHLH interaction motif region and is speculated to contribute to the white fruit phenotype [18]. Nevertheless, these bioinformatics-based speculations, as well as how the single-base mutation in MYB10 affects fruit color of F. nilgerrensis, remain to be further verified through experimental research. Our study provides the experimental evidence that naturally occurring missense mutations directly impact MYB10 protein interactions in strawberry, offering mechanistic insights into how single nucleotide variations drive alterations in key agronomic traits. These findings deepen our understanding of how single amino acid mutations modulate transcription factor interactions and transcriptional regulatory networks in fruit color formation, provide direct targets for developing molecular markers in precision strawberry breeding for fruit color traits, and offer actionable insights for formulating genome editing strategies to improve fruit color in Rosaceae crops.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

F. nilgerrensis plants were collected from Liang Wang Mountain, Chengjiang City, Yunnan Province, China (102°54.910′ E, 24°45.474′ N, elevation 2,100 m). Collected plants were transplanted to greenhouses at Yunnan Agricultural University and cultivated under conditions of 25 ± 2 °C temperature, 60–70% relative humidity, and 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod. Healthy ripe fruits (approximately 26 days after flowering) were collected for subsequent experimental analyses. Genome data [18] and transcriptome data [22] from different developmental stages of F. nilgerrensis fruits used in this study were sequenced in our laboratory previously.

Identification of MBW complex members in F. nilgerrensis

Identification of R2R3-MYB gene family members in F. nilgerrensis was performed according to the method of Xie et al. [23]. Based on Arabidopsis R2R3-MYB subfamily classification standards, we screened and identified R2R3-MYB genes related to anthocyanin synthesis in F. nilgerrensis. Expression data of R2R3-MYB gene family members at different fruit developmental stages were obtained from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra) under the accession number PRJNA1103866. Identification of bHLH and WD40 members in the MBW complex was performed according to the method of Yue et al. (2023). Phylogenetic trees were constructed using MEGA 11 software with the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method and bootstrap values set to 1000. Heatmaps were generated using TBtool software. AlphaFold3 server’s multimer mode was used to predict complex structures of MYB10 and bHLH transcription factors. PyMOL was used to analyze hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions.

Gene cloning and vector construction

Total RNA was extracted from mature F. nilgerrensis fruits using the E.Z.N.A.® Plant RNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, USA). cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Japan). Using cDNA as template, PCR amplification was performed using primer pairs FngMYB10-F/FngMYB10-R to obtain the wild-type FngMYB10 (WT) gene coding sequence (CDS). Mutant FngMYB10 (MU) was constructed using overlap extension PCR: the first fragment was amplified using primer pairs FngMYB10-F/FngMYB10-Rm, the second fragment was amplified using primer pairs FngMYB10-Fm/FngMYB10-R, and the two fragments were fused to obtain the site-directed FngMYB10 (MU) sequence. WT and MU sequences were respectively ligated into the pCAMBIA1301-35SN vector to construct recombinant expression vectors 35 S::FngMYB10 (WT) and 35 S::FngMYB10 (MU). Primer sequences used are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Functional verification of wild-type and mutant FngMYB10

Recombinant vectors were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 (Tsingke, CHN). For transient overexpression experiments, Agrobacterium was first cultured overnight at 28 °C in YEP medium containing corresponding antibiotics until OD600 = 0.6–0.8. Bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, 10 mM MgCl₂, 200 µM acetosyringone, pH 5.5) to OD600 approximately 0.8, then incubated at room temperature for 3 h before injecting bacterial suspension into F. nilgerrensis fruits using 1 mL sterile syringes. At least 10 fruits were selected for each treatment with 3 biological replicates. Fruit phenotype changes were observed 7 d after injection, and samples were collected and stored at -80 °C for subsequent gene expression analysis. Transgenic callus tissues were obtained using Agrobacterium-mediated leaf disc transformation, following methods established by Liu et al. [24].

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis methods were as described above. qRT-PCR reactions were performed on a QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) using TB Green™ Premix Ex Taq™ II (Tli RNaseH Plus) reagent kit (TaKaRa, Japan). FnActin [25] and GAPDH [5] were selected as reference genes, and the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method [26] was used to calculate the relative expression levels of genes. Each sample was set with 3 technical replicates and 3 biological replicates. Primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Anthocyanin content determination

The anthocyanin content in fruits and callus tissue subjected to different treatments was determined using an anthocyanin content detection kit (Solarbio, China, Cat# BC1385). The procedure was performed according to the instructions in the manual. Each treatment was replicated three times.

Subcellular localization analysis

Full-length coding sequences (CDS) of FngMYB10 (WT / MU) without stop codons were amplified and inserted into the pCAMBIA1302 plasmid to construct the CaMV 35 S::FngMYB10 (WT/MU)-EGFP recombinant plasmids. The empty pCAMBIA1302 plasmid and the aforementioned recombinant plasmids were respectively introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101, and Nicotiana benthamiana leaves were infiltrated using a 1 mL sterile syringe. Two days after transient transformation, the distribution of EGFP fluorescent signals was observed using an inverted laser scanning confocal fluorescence microscope (FV3000, Japan). Primers used are listed in Table S3.

Yeast Two-Hybrid (Y2H) analysis

Full-length CDS sequences of FngMYB10 (WT/MU) were respectively cloned into pGADT7 vector; full-length CDS of FngbHLH3, domain region (1–966 bp) of FngbHLH33 and domain region (1–600 bp) of FngMYC1 were respectively cloned into pGBKT7 vector. Using the PEG/LiAc method, equal amounts of recombinant plasmids pGADT7-FngMYB10 (WT/MU), pGBKT7-FngbHLH3/FngbHLH33/FngMYC1, and 100 µg carrier DNA were co-transformed into Y2HGold yeast competent cells (Coolaber, China). Transformants were first screened on SD/-Trp/-Leu medium, and positive clones were transferred to SD/-Trp/-Leu/-Ade/-His/X-α-gal/AbA medium to detect protein interactions. Primer sequences used are detailed in Supplementary Table S4.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) experiments

The CDS sequences of FngMYB10 (WT/MU) were cloned into the pSPYCE-35 S vector, and FngbHLH3, FngbHLH33, and FngMYC1 were respectively cloned into the pSPYNE-35 S vector, with stop codons removed for all constructs. Recombinant plasmids were transformed into Agrobacterium GV3101. After the recombinant pSPYNE and pSPYCE vectors were mixed at a 1:1 ratio, bacterial suspensions were injected into the lower epidermis of N. benthamiana leaves using needleless syringes. After injection, plants were dark-cultured for 12 h, then cultured under normal light conditions (16 h light/8 h dark) for 3 d. YFP fluorescence signals were observed using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica SP8, Germany). Primer sequences used are detailed in Supplementary Table S5.

Results

Identification of MBW components and anthocyanin gene expression in F. nilgerrensis

The R2R3-MYB transcription factors are widely recognized as master regulators governing the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway [7]. To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the formation of white fruits in F. nilgerrensis, we first identified candidate MYB genes potentially involved in anthocyanin metabolism regulation through comparative analysis with members of the R2R3-MYB family in Arabidopsis thaliana (Fig. S1A, B). These include eight R2R3-MYB genes that may be involved in anthocyanin synthesis (subgroups S5-S7) and transcription inhibition of anthocyanin synthesis (subgroup S4). However, the accumulation of flavonoids depends on the co-expression of structural genes, among which key genes such as CHS, CHI, and ANR are mainly regulated by the MBW complex. We further identified key MBW complex transcription regulatory factors through phylogenetic and metabolic pathway analysis. Phylogenetic analysis of the three transcription factor families revealed evolutionarily conserved regulatory components (Fig. 1A, B, C). Within the MYB cladogram, FngMYB10 (chr1.2669) clustered closely with established anthocyanin regulators (FaMYB10 and FvMYB10) from cultivated (F. × ananassa) and woodland strawberry (F. vesca), supported by maximal bootstrap value (100%), indicating functional conservation. At the same time, we also identified bHLH and WD40 orthologous genes in F. nilgerrensis.

D Nucleotide and amino acid sequence alignment highlighting the R82S mutation in the FngMYB10 gene Based on the published transcriptome data of F. nilgerrensis during fruit development, we analyzed the differentially expressed genes between developmental stages (Fig. 1A). We found that FngMYB10, a S6 subfamily gene encoding anthocyanin biosynthesis, was significantly highly expressed at the mature fruit stage (MF2) compared with the young fruit stages (YF1, YF2) (Fig. 1A; Fig. S1B). Intriguingly, while FngMYB10 exhibited significant upregulation during fruit ripening, genes encoding bHLH and WD40 proteins displayed either invariant expression or downregulation throughout fruit development (Fig. 1B). Further construction of the anthocyanin biosynthesis network in F. nilgerrensis and expression profiling analysis revealed that the expression of key flavonoid biosynthesis genes (including C4H, 4CL, CHS, CHI, F3H, DFR, ANS, LAR and ANR) showed a decreasing trend throughout the entire fruit development period (Fig. 1C). This contrasts sharply with the typical pattern of upregulation of these genes during the ripening period in cultivated strawberries with red fruits [27, 28]. It is worth noting that although the anthocyanin transport gene GST was significantly upregulated in synchrony with FngMYB10 during the MF2, the expression of key anthocyanin synthesis structural genes, especially DFR, which drives the conversion of dihydroflavonol to colorless anthocyanin, and ANS, which catalyzes the formation of colored anthocyanin from colorless anthocyanin, remained suppressed. The dysregulation of MBW complex members (especially FngMYB10 and bHLH/WD40) and the “decoupling” phenomenon, in which the expression of FngMYB10 activators is upregulated while downstream key structural genes (such as DFR and ANS) remain suppressed, collectively indicate that the functional assembly of the MBW complex in F. nilgerrensis may be defective. These results strongly suggest that the deficiency of anthocyanins is more likely caused by a functional defect in the FngMYB10 protein rather than the silencing of its transcriptional level. The aforementioned gene expression characteristics strongly support our previous hypothesis: the Arg82Ser (R82S) missense mutation identified in the FngMYB10 gene locus of F. nilgerrensis may disrupt its protein structure or function, ultimately leading to impaired anthocyanin synthesis (Fig. 1D). The serine at position 82 of FngMYB10 was designated as wild type (WT), whereas the arginine at position 82 was designated as mutant type (MU).

Transient expression validation of FngMYB10 R82S mutation-induced functional impairment

To directly validate the causal relationship between the R82S mutation and the white fruit phenotype, we constructed a mutant FngMYB10 vector (c. 246T→246G, MU) and performed transient overexpression experiments in F. nilgerrensis fruits via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation (Fig. 2A; Fig. S2). Phenotypic observations revealed that fruits in the empty vector control group remained white, while injection sites of fruits overexpressing the MU exhibited obvious reddening, indicating restoration of anthocyanin synthesis function (Fig. 2B). Anthocyanin content analysis showed that MU was significantly upregulated compared to the control group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2C), and qRT-PCR confirmed significant overexpression of the FngMYB10 (Fig. 2D). These results confirm that the c.246G > T variant (R82S substitution) is likely the direct cause of the white fruit phenotype in F. nilgerrensis. The mutant protein restored pigment synthesis capacity, indicating that other synthesis components function normally and that the R82S mutation solely results in FngMYB10 loss of function.

To analyze downstream effects of mutant FngMYB10, we examined expression changes in anthocyanin synthesis-related genes (Fig. 2D). Functionally restored FngMYB10 activated the complete regulatory network: MBW complex components (FngbHLH33, FngTTG1) were significantly upregulated, revealing positive feedback regulatory mechanisms. Simultaneously, anthocyanin synthesis pathway structural genes were comprehensively activated, including early genes (FngPAL, FngCHS, FngCHI, FngC4H, FngF3H), late genes (FngDFR, FngANS, FngUFGT), and competitive branch gene FngANR; the anthocyanin transport gene FngGST was significantly upregulated, suggesting FngMYB10 regulates both synthesis and transport processes. This comprehensive activation pattern exemplifies the central role of FngMYB10 as the master regulator of anthocyanin synthesis. FngMYB10 R82S mutation functional validation via stable transformation

To further validate the results of the transient expression assay, 35 S::FngMYB10 (WT) and 35 S::FngMYB10 (MU) plasmids were stably transformed into F. nilgerrensis via the leaf disc method. Following selection on resistant medium (250 mg/L Carb + 250 mg/L Tim + 5 mg/L Hyg) and PCR identification of the hygromycin resistance gene (Hyg), approximately 3% positive calli were successfully obtained (Fig. 3A, Fig. S3A). The only difference between these two FngMYB10 cDNAs was T in the WT cDNA and G in the MU cDNA. The phenotypic results of stable transformation were consistent with transient expression experiments: callus tissues overexpressing the MU exhibited obvious pigment deposition during cultivation, with color gradually changing from initial green to deep purplish-red, while callus tissues overexpressing WT showed no significant color changes (Fig. 3A). Anthocyanin content determination confirmed that anthocyanin accumulation in MU callus tissues was significantly higher than in WT and control groups (Fig. 3B).

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis revealed functional activation of the entire anthocyanin biosynthetic regulatory network exclusively in MU transgenic lines despite successful transgene expression in both constructs. This was specifically manifested by the coordinated upregulation of regulatory factors (FngbHLH, FngWD40), structural genes (FngPAL, FngC4H, FngCHS, FngCHI, FngF3H, FngDFR, FngANS, FngUFGT, FngANR), and transport genes (FngGST) (Fig. 3C). Notably, the abundance of FngMYB10 transcripts in MU lines was significantly higher than in WT lines (p < 0.001), suggesting that restoration of the R82 site not only repaired the protein’s transcriptional activation function but may also enhance its expression through improved protein stability or activation of positive feedback regulatory mechanisms. Collectively, these results clearly confirm the critical role of the R82 site in maintaining FngMYB10 transcriptional activation function, and that mutation at this site constitutes the key molecular basis underlying fruit color phenotypic differences in strawberry. To validate the functional conservation of the R82S mutation in the FngMYB10 gene, we further conducted heterologous expression validation in the white-fruited cultivated strawberry variety ‘Taokan’ (one of whose parents is F. nilgerrensis) (Fig. S3B). Transformation results showed that, consistent with performance in F. nilgerrensis, ‘Taokan’ callus tissues overexpressing the MUalso exhibited visible anthocyanin accumulation compared to WT, displaying a light red color (Fig. 3D). Consistent with the results observed in F. nilgerrensis, qRT-PCR analysis revealed that FngMYB10 expression was significantly higher in MU transformants than in WT (Fig. S3C). However, the degree of pigment accumulation in ‘Taokan’ strawberry callus tissues was significantly weaker than in F. nilgerrensis, and its complex genetic background and gene dosage effects may have attenuated the regulatory effects of the single transcription factor.

The R82S mutation does not affect the nuclear localization of FngMYB10

To rule out the possibility that the functional inactivation of FngMYB10 results from abnormal nuclear localization, we further verified the spatial distribution characteristics of WT and MU FngMYB10 through subcellular localization assays. We constructed fusion expression vectors (35 S::FngMYB10-WT-GFP and 35 S::FngMYB10-MU-GFP) by fusing FngMYB10 (WT/MU) with enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP), respectively (Fig. 4A). After Agrobacterium-mediated transformation into leaf epidermal cells of N. benthamiana, observations under a laser scanning confocal microscope showed that the EGFP fluorescent signals of both WT and MU overlapped with the mCherry signals of the nuclear control marker gene, and there was no significant difference in the intensity of their nuclear localization signals (Fig. 4B). This indicates that the R82S mutation did not alter the nuclear localization ability of FngMYB10, and its functional inactivation is not due to the inability to enter the nucleus and bind to the promoters of target genes, but rather caused by other molecular mechanisms.

R82S mutation disrupts FngMYB10-FngbHLH33 interaction

The R82S missense mutation identified in the FngMYB10 locus resides precisely within the conserved bHLH-interaction motif [DE]Lx2[RK]x3Lx6Lx3R (Fig. 5A), suggesting direct impairment of MYB10-bHLH molecular recognition. To clarify the molecular mechanism underlying its mediated anthocyanin biosynthesis deficiency, we investigated the protein-protein interactions between WT/MU FngMYB10 with candidate bHLH cofactors (FngbHLH3, FngbHLH33, FngMYC1) through Y2H and BiFC assays. Annotation via the Conserved Domain Database (CDD) reveals that the three bHLH proteins possess complete functional units (Fig. S4). Tissue expression profiling revealed that FngbHLH3 and FngMYC1 were highly expressed in fruit but weakly expressed in leaves and flowers, with the latter showing a progressive decline during fruit ripening. Conversely, FngbHLH33 displayed the opposite pattern, exhibiting high expression in leaves and flowers while maintaining consistently low expression levels throughout fruit development (Fig. S5). Y2H results showed that FngbHLH33 is a specific interaction partner of FngMYB10: MU could interact strongly with FngbHLH33, while WT completely lost this interaction ability. Neither WT nor MU exhibited interaction activity with FngbHLH3, but both could interact with FngMYC1 (Fig. 5B).

BiFC experiments further validated this result: in N. benthamiana mesophyll cells, co-expression of MU and FngbHLH33 reconstituted the complete fluorescent protein signal, with obvious fluorescence complementation observed in the nucleus, whereas no fluorescent signal was detected when WT and FngbHLH33 were co-expressed (Fig. 5C, D). Additionally, distinct fluorescence complementation was observed in the nucleus when WT and MU were co-expressed with FngMYC1, respectively, indicating the reconstitution of intact fluorescent protein signals. These results directly demonstrate that the R82S mutation disrupts the assembly of the functional MBW complex by impairing the molecular recognition between FngMYB10 and the key transcriptional cofactor FngbHLH33. Although WT can be normally expressed and localized to the nucleus, it ultimately completely loses the ability to activate the transcription of anthocyanin biosynthesis genes due to its failure to recruit the essential transcriptional cofactor FngbHLH33.

Structural basis for R82S-mediated disruption of the FngMYB10–bHLH33 interaction

To elucidate the structural basis of R82S-mediated functional disruption, we employed AlphaFold3 for multimeric structure prediction of WT/MU FngMYB10-bHLH33 complexes, followed by detailed interaction interface analysis using PyMOL (Fig. 6A). Structural analysis revealed that in the MU complex, the positively charged guanidinium group of Arg82 (R82) forms a stable hydrogen-bonding network with residues 92 and 106 on Chain B of bHLH33, constituting a critical intermolecular recognition interface. Quantitative distance measurements confirmed robust hydrogen bonding between R82 and these key residues (2.4 Å and 3.1 Å, respectively). Conversely, in the WT complex, the shorter hydroxy group of Ser82 (S82) failed to establish effective contacts with corresponding bHLH33 residues, exhibiting substantially increased intermolecular distances (7.2 Å and 5.6 Å) that abrogate essential hydrogen bonding. This atomic-resolution analysis elucidates how R82S destabilizes the protein interaction interface, ultimately compromising MBW complex functionality. Cross-validation against the A. thaliana WER crystal structure (PDB: 6KKS) confirmed the accuracy of our FngMYB10 model (Fig. S6). In summary, our results indicate that the R82S mutation in F. nilgerrensis prevents FngMYB10 from effectively recruiting the essential transcriptional cofactor FngbHLH33, completely impairing its ability to activate the transcription of anthocyanin synthesis genes. This elucidates the molecular mechanism underlying the formation of the white fruit phenotype in F. nilgerrensis (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

This study provided the first experimental confirmation that the substitution of arginine with serine at position 82 (R82S) in the FngMYB10 protein of F. nilgerrensis completely blocked anthocyanin biosynthesis, resulting in the white fruit phenotype. This critical arginine residue is located within the highly conserved bHLH interaction motif [DE]Lx2[RK]x3Lx6Lx3R [11, 29]. Crystal structure analysis of MYB-bHLH complexes such as Arabidopsis WER-EGL3 demonstrated that the arginine in this motif forms hydrogen bonds with bHLH partners, establishing the stable interaction interface essential for transcriptional activation [12]. Our AlphaFold3 structural modeling further revealed that in mutant FngMYB10, R82 forms strong hydrogen bonds with T92 and E106 of bHLH33 at distances of 2.4 Å and 3.1 Å, respectively, while in the wild type, these interactions are completely lost with distances increasing to 7.2 Å and 5.6 Å. This precise structural disruption directly elucidates how the R82S mutation impedes MBW (MYB-bHLH-WD40) complex assembly, consequently leading to the white fruit phenotype.

The molecular mechanisms underlying white fruit driven by MYB10 variations in the genus Fragaria exhibit remarkable diversity. Previous studies have identified: W12S substitution within the R2R3 DNA-binding domain disrupting protein function in woodland strawberry ‘Yellow Wonder’ [16, 17]; ACTTATAC insertion causing premature translation termination in octoploid strawberry [10]; and AG frameshift insertion resulting in truncated non-functional protein [19]. The uniqueness of the R82S mutation in F. nilgerrensis lies in its specific disruption of protein interaction function while maintaining an intact R2R3 domain (potential DNA-binding capacity). This emphasizes that MYB10 function requires not only DNA-binding ability but more critically depends on proper assembly of the MBW regulatory complex. Cross-species comparison further highlights the functional importance of this conserved motif and its site-specific effects: the R69L mutation in the R2 domain of grape VvMYB5b resulted in 5-fold reduced transcriptional activity and complete loss of interaction with VvMYC1 [30], with the loss-of-function phenotype consistent with the strawberry R82S mutation in this study. Notably, the S92F mutation within the bHLH interaction motif of Arabidopsis GL1 protein exhibited functional separation: while retaining interaction capacity with bHLH factors (GL3/EGL3), it completely lost transcriptional activation function [31]. Both R69L (grapevine) and S92F (Arabidopsis) are located within the R2R3-MYB core domain, but their phenotypic differences suggest that different sites within this region may have differential functions. Combined with crystal structure evidence from Wang et al. [32] showing spatial separation between the bHLH interaction motif and DNA-binding helix, we speculate that the R82 site in FngMYB10 may have dual regulatory roles: controlling interaction with bHLH partners (complex assembly) and potentially affecting transcriptional activation capacity (promoter binding).

In fact, wild strawberries with white fruits include F. vesca, F. pentaphylla, F. nubicola, F. daltoniana, and F. nilgerrensis. Our previous analysis revealed fixed single nucleotide mutations in the MYB10 conserved motif across white-fruited populations of different species [18], and this study experimentally validated that the predicted R82S mutation is the key factor for the white phenotype in F. nilgerrensis. The convergent mutations in the MYB10 gene across these species further confirm its important status as a hotspot for adaptive evolution of fruit color. Additionally, the white fruit phenotype in F. nilgerrensis may result from synergistic effects of transcriptional and post-translational regulation. Zhang et al. reported that FnMYB10 promoter variation reduces its expression level, while the R82S mutation discovered in this study creates post-translational defects by weakening protein function [20]. This dual-pathway regulatory mechanism may enhance the stability of white fruit traits in natural populations, obtaining selective advantages by ensuring sustained inhibition of anthocyanin synthesis.

Our results deepen understanding of MBW complex function in Rosaceae fruit coloration. The necessity of MYB10-bHLH33 interaction in F. nilgerrensis is consistent with findings in cultivated strawberry: FaMYB10 requires binding with FabHLH3 or FabHLH33 to activate anthocyanin synthesis genes [33, 34]. Research has shown that the complete strawberry MBW complex contains multiple WD40 proteins (such as FaLWD1, FaLWD1-like) that can stabilize MYB-bHLH interactions [35]. The hierarchical organization of the MBW complex explains why a single amino acid substitution produces such profound effects: structural studies demonstrate that core MYB-bHLH interaction must first be established, followed by WD40 recruitment to stabilize the complex, and finally synergistic DNA binding to activate target genes [36]. The R82S mutation disrupts this cascade at the initial step, preventing all downstream events. Furthermore, the MBW complex not only mediates direct protein interactions but may also be involved in constructing complex transcriptional regulatory networks. We observed that following FngMYB10 functional restoration, expression of its molecular chaperones (FngbHLH/FngWD40) was also significantly upregulated, suggesting a positive autoregulatory mechanism. This is similar to previous studies: MBW complexes typically form self-reinforcing loops by activating expression of their own components, ensuring sustained pigment synthesis [11, 15]. Indeed, bHLH promoter regions also contain MYB binding motifs [11]. This self-enhancement mechanism may explain the phenomenon where FngMYB10 (MU) expression in mutant lines is higher than wild-type FngMYB10 (WT).

Notably, different bHLH members exhibit distinct tissue-specific expression patterns in strawberry. Although we found that both wild-type (WT) and mutant (MU) FngMYB10 can interact with FngMYC1, FngMYC1 displays an opposite expression pattern to FngMYB10 in fruits, which may hinder the formation of functional MBW complexes in fruits. In F. vesca, despite the MYC1 gene being identified as a potential participant in flavonoid biosynthesis [14], its expression pattern during fruit ripening may differ from that of bHLH33. Furthermore, different bHLH cofactors may endow the MBW complex with distinct target gene selectivity. In Vitis vinifera, VvMYC1 interacts with VvMYB5 to induce only a specific subset of flavonoid genes [37], and in two-colored Vaccinium corymbosum, MYC1-type VmbHLH1 only induces a limited number of flavonoid genes, while TT8-type VmbHLH2 can comprehensively upregulate anthocyanin biosynthesis genes [38]. Based on this, it is speculated that the FngMYB10-FngMYC1 complex tends to regulate proanthocyanidin or fruit development-related genes, whereas the FngMYB10-FngbHLH33 complex is the key combination to activate the entire set of anthocyanin structural genes. This is the core mechanism by which the R82S mutation causes white fruit solely by disrupting the interaction with FngbHLH33.

Despite upregulated FngMYB10 expression during fruit ripening in R82S wild type, anthocyanin synthesis genes remained suppressed, indicating that MYB10 expression alone is insufficient to activate the pathway, highlighting the MBW complex as a critical transcriptional switch integrating MYB10 expression with downstream pathway activation. In contrast, functional FngMYB10 (MU) activation can synergistically upregulate both early (PAL, C4H, CHS, CHI, F3H) and late anthocyanin synthesis genes (DFR, ANS, UFGT). This result is consistent with conclusions by Medina-Puche et al. [9] and Wang et al. [10] regarding MYB10 as a master regulator in strawberry. However, in the wild type, FngMYB10 (WT) completely loses MBW complex formation capacity, blocking any compensatory mechanisms and ultimately resulting in complete anthocyanin absence. Anthocyanin synthesis blockade not only causes pigment loss but typically may also trigger upstream precursor accumulation, proanthocyanidin composition changes, and alterations in other phenylpropanoid derivatives [33, 39], thereby affecting fruit flavor, nutrition, and postharvest characteristics [40]. Understanding these metabolic cascade effects is crucial for breeding, as selecting for white fruits may inadvertently alter other key quality traits.

The R82S mutation in F. nilgerrensis provides new perspectives for analyzing evolutionary mechanisms of fruit color variation in the genus Fragaria. The multiple independent evolution of white fruits in different species suggests that anthocyanin deficiency may confer ecological adaptive advantages, such as escaping frugivorous animals or resource reallocation [5, 15, 20]. From a breeding perspective, this study establishes a foundation for precise improvement of strawberry fruit color: the R82S mutation provides an SNP molecular marker for early selection of white fruit traits, while base editing technology can precisely repair this mutation to restore red color in F. nilgerrensis, its unique flavor characteristics make it a valuable breeding resource [21, 22]. Future research should explore how environmental factors (light, temperature) regulate MBW complex activity [41, 42], and whether similar mechanisms control fruit color in other Rosaceae crops, thereby expanding the translational potential of this discovery.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study has clearly elucidated, through multi-level functional validation experiments and structural analysis, from a new perspective that the R82S missense mutation in FngMYB10 of F. nilgerrensis, located within the conserved bHLH interaction motif, specifically disrupts the interaction with the key cofactor FngbHLH33, thereby blocking the assembly of the functional MBW complex and ultimately leading to complete loss of fruit anthocyanin biosynthesis. Notably, although the R82S mutant still interacts with FngMYC1, the fruit remains white, strongly indicating that FngbHLH33 is an irreplaceable and essential cofactor for anthocyanin synthesis in F. nilgerrensis fruits, while FngMYC1 may play a major role in other tissues or metabolic branches. This finding reveals the genetic mechanism of strawberry fruit color variation, provides theoretical insights into plant anthocyanin regulation, and lays a foundation for molecular breeding/genome editing, aiding crop evolution research and breeding strategies.
