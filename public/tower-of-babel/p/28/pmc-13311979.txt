Lung cancer risk prediction models and asbestos exposure: a validation study on the Western Australia Asbestos Review Program
Authors: Chellan Kumarasamy, Kim Betts, Richard Norman, Kirsten Bennett, Peter Franklin, Martin Tammemägi, Fraser Brims
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAsbestos exposure raises the lung cancer risk and has supra-additive synergy alongside tobacco exposure. Lung cancer screening (LCS) is effective when high-risk populations are targeted. This study examined the utility of various LCS eligibility and risk prediction models in an asbestos-exposed population.MethodsThe Western Australia Asbestos Review Program (ARP) consists of individuals with known exposure to asbestos. All participants underwent annual review with low-dose CT screening. The performance of the Prostate, Lung, Colorectal and Ovarian (PLCO)m2012, PLCOm2014 and PLCOocc models, Liverpool Lung Project V.2 (LLPv2) and Bach models, together with the United States Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF)2021 and Australian LCS eligibility criteria were validated on the ARP.ResultsThe cohort consisted of 2126 participants of which 85.4% were male with a median (IQR) age of 70 (63–75) years old. Former smokers comprised 55.1% (n=1172) and never smokers 36.2% (n=769) of the cohort. Median smoking and cessation duration were 24 years (IQR: 13–72) and 32 years (IQR: 22–41), respectively. Lung cancer was diagnosed in 51 (2.4%) participants.When applying the risk models to the ARP cohort, the area under the curve for all models was modest, ranging from 0.602 to 0.675. All models underestimated risk in this cohort during calibration assessment, with the exception of the LLP V.2, which overestimated risk.ConclusionsIn an asbestos exposed population, current LCS eligibility criteria and risk models mostly underestimate the risk of lung cancer, reflecting the need for improved risk prediction models that adequately account for asbestos exposure.

Introduction

Lung cancer screening (LCS) of a targeted high-risk population using low-dose CT (LDCT) has demonstrated a mortality benefit of at least 20%.13 Effective population-based LCS is reliant on selecting a high-risk population likely to benefit the most from early lung cancer (LC) detection.4 Implementation of LCS is progressing with different jurisdictions currently proposing differing selection criteria for eligibility.57

Recommendations for participant selection in LCS include the two primary risk factors for LC, age and smoking exposure.8 Risk prediction models which predict the probability of LC incidence or mortality using risk factors beyond age and smoking have emerged as a means of improving participant selection beyond categorical (age and smoking) criteria.4911 The Prostate, Lung, Colorectal and Ovarian (PLCO)m2012 LC risk model has been validated in prospective and retrospective studies.1214 The model includes additional LC risk factors, such as body mass index (BMI), socioeconomic status, presence of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cancer history and ethnicity9 but does not include exposure to occupational lung carcinogens such as asbestos. Asbestos confers an increased risk of developing LC in a linear, dose-dependent manner.15 Additionally, asbestos has additive synergism with tobacco exposure, wherein the combination of the two have a more than additive, but less than multiplicative risk for LC, termed as relative excess risk by interaction (RERI).16

Asbestos was widely used in most developed countries in the last century and is still exported and utilised today by a minority of countries.17 18 Therefore, any LC risk model that does not take asbestos exposure into consideration may underestimate risk in exposed populations, thereby excluding potentially eligible participants from LCS.19 LC risk models that include asbestos exposure, such as the Liverpool Lung Project V.2 (LLPv2), Bach, Spitz and Etzel models, do so in the form of a binary variable, which may not adequately represent the risk contributed by asbestos exposure and do not account for RERI with concomitant tobacco exposure.10 11 20 21 Alongside PLCOm2012, the LLPv2 has also been proposed as a means of participant selection LCS programmes, and therefore was included for validation as part of this study.6

The Western Australian Asbestos Review Program (ARP) consists of participants with exposure to asbestos undergoing annual LDCT, regardless of tobacco smoke exposure.17 22 The aim of this study was to investigate the utility of existing LC risk models for selecting asbestos exposed individuals at a high risk of LC.

Methods

Cohort and asbestos exposure

The ARP cohort was established in 1990 and comprises individuals with (1) at least 3-month occupational exposure to asbestos (mostly from construction and manufacturing industries with mixed-asbestos fibre exposure,22 (2) the presence of pleural plaque on imaging and/or (3) former crocidolite (blue asbestos) mine workers and township residents from Wittenoom, Western Australia. A detailed history and establishment of the ARP is described elsewhere.2325

The ARP has been conducting LDCT chest screening since 2012 within an annual health assessment that also includes lung function, anthropogenic measurements and smoking status.22 For the Wittenoom workers and residents, estimated asbestos exposure was calculated from periodic measurements of airborne dust concentrations taken from the mine, mill and township (1948–1966), described in detail elsewhere.25 26 For the mixed occupational exposures, a validated Australian-specific job exposure matrix and detailed job history was used to estimate an individual’s asbestos exposure.27

Case definition

New LCs diagnosed between September 2012 and September 2023 were the outcome of interest for risk prediction. A diagnosis of LC was accepted with pathological confirmation (non-small cell or small cell LC), clinical consensus and/or through record linkage with the WA Cancer Registry.

Risk prediction models

Five LC risk prediction models were validated using the ARP cohort. Each model is described briefly below.

PLCOm2012, PLCOm2014 and PLCOocc

The PLCOm2012 is a logistic regression model that performs classification on the binary outcome of LC (no/yes), occurring within 6 years of assessment. The PLCOm2012 was derived on data from the control arm of the PLCO Cancer screening trial and has since been validated prospectively in the International Lung Screening Trial.9 14

As the PLCOm2012 was developed on data from current and former smoking individuals, the PLCOm2014 model was developed by Tammemägi et al, applicable for never-smokers.28 The PLCOm2012 and PLCOm2014 models do not include asbestos (or any other occupational) exposure as a risk variable. To account for this, a ‘PLCOocc’ model has been developed as a synthetic-hybrid version of the PLCOall2014 model. The PLCOocc model adds asbestos risk as an independent term, with an adjusted coefficient derived from the literature (although this does not account for the RERI with tobacco exposure). This PLCOocc model variant is yet to be validated and has currently not been published in the literature.

Liverpool Lung Project (V.2)

The LLPv2 model is a modification of the original LLP model that expands the lung disease variable beyond pneumonia, to also include COPD, bronchitis, emphysema and tuberculosis.10 29 The original LLP model was derived from a conditional logistic regression model that performs binary classification, by predicting risk of LC incidence within 5 years of assessment. It was built on a case–control study of LC in participants aged 20–80 in Liverpool (UK), by combining relative risk models with local rates of LC incidence. The model includes asbestos exposure as a self-reported binary (yes/no) variable, coded as a response to the question: ‘Can you recall any job or activity where you were exposed to Asbestos?’.

Bach model

The Bach model, developed by Bach et al using CARET study data, predicts risk of LC over a period of time (in years) by recursively applying multivariate Cox regression models that predict the 1-year risk of LC diagnosis and the 1-year risk of death in absence of an LC diagnosis.11 This model also includes asbestos exposure as a binary variable, with presence of exposure being defined as ≥5 years in an occupation with heavy asbestos exposure, or a first exposure over 15 years ago. The Bach model was selected as a contrast to the more expansive LLPv2 and PLCOm2012 models due to its sole focus on age, smoking and asbestos exposure criteria.

Other eligibility criteria

The United States Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) 2021 criteria for LC screening select individuals between the age of 50 and 80, with a minimum smoking exposure of 20 pack-years and a quit time below 15 years for former smokers.8 In 2023, the Australian Medical Services Advisory Committee (MSAC) established the selection criterion ahead of the nationwide LCS programme, set to start in 2025. Eligible participants are required to be between ages 50 and 70, with minimum smoking exposure of at least 30 pack-years and a maximum quit time of 10 years.5

Model performance

All analyses were conducted in R (V.4.4.0). The Bach, PLCOm2012 and PLCOocc models were only validated on ARP participants with current or prior smoking exposures. In contrast, PLCOm2014, which was developed to account for never smoking individuals, and LLPv2 (which does not place any restriction on risk evaluation of never smoking participants) were validated on the whole ARP population.

Model discrimination

Sensitivity, specificity and PPV were estimated for all LC risk models and eligibility criteria. Risk thresholds for each model were chosen to facilitate comparison. For the PLCOm2012 and its variants, a threshold risk of ≥1.51% was used to classify the predicted probabilities. This threshold was chosen based on prior literature as well as 1.51% being the eligibility criteria for the International Lung Screen Trial14 and the models’ use in the UK-based lung health check programme.6 All other PLCO model variants, LLPv2 and the Bach models were compared against the performance of PLCOm2012 via selection of model-specific risk thresholds that produced the same specificity as PLCOm2012 at a 1.51% risk threshold. This allowed for comparisons of sensitivities across all models.

However, as the LLPv2 risk model is incapable of predicting risk of individuals of age ≥80 years, the model predicts risk for a smaller subsection of the ARP cohort relative to the Bach and PLCO model variants. Therefore, further assessment was performed, assuming a worst-case scenario where the LLPv2’s inability to predict risk at advanced ages is interpreted as ineligibility for screening, allowing for direct comparison of LLPv2 to other models, as all models would then be assessing risk across the same age range.

Considering sample size limitations, an initial analysis was performed, with a maximum follow-up duration of 10 years in the ARP, to ensure inclusion of the maximum number of LC cases available. Consequently, the cases were not limited to the calibrated timeframe of the LLPv2 (5-year incidence) and PLCO model variants (6-year incidence). The Bach model predicts 1-year risk and was recursively applied across 10 years to predict LC incidence across a 10-year time period in the ARP cohort. This allowed for direct comparisons across all risk models with the caveat that the calibration of models might be underestimated. A secondary analysis was subsequently performed where the follow-up duration for the ARP was truncated at 5 years and 6 years to evaluate performance of the LLPv2 and PLCO model variants, respectively, at their calibrated timeframes.

Further evaluation of model performance employed the estimation of the F1 score, with its corresponding 95% CIs calculated using the Wilson score interval with continuity correction.30 The F1 score is a harmonic mean between precision (PPV) and sensitivity and ranges from 0 to 1, with a higher value indicating better performance. It is useful in the context of LC prediction, where the consequences of false positives and false negatives are both important. Additional evaluation metrics, such as the Brier Score, were considered, but found to be unsuitable due to low case numbers and weak predicted probabilities of the outcome.

Model calibration

Mean calibration compared the sum of predicted probabilities (E: Expected Outcomes) against the total number of LC cases (O: Observed Outcomes), as a ratio (E/O), with corresponding 95% CIs calculated assuming a Poisson distribution for observed events. A mean calibration of value <1 indicated underestimation of risk by model, while a value >1 indicated overestimation. However, as a mean calibration value of 1 does not necessarily imply perfect calibration across the range of predicted risk, mean-adjusted deaths compared against predictions (MADCAP) plots were constructed for further assessment. As the outcome of interest was LC incidence, it replaced deaths in the MADCAP plots.31

Clinical utility

Decision curve analysis was used to compare clinical utility of the models.32 The decision curve depicts net benefit of an intervention over a range of thresholds, where the net benefit as a metric, balances the benefit of the intervention (represented by true positives) against the harms of an intervention (represented by false positives), along with a weighting factor derived from the threshold at which intervention is considered.

Results

Population demographics

The ARP cohort consisted of 2195 participants, of which 2126 participants were selected for this study based on age threshold of 45–90 years, and availability of sufficient data for analysis, with a total follow-up duration of 10 770 person-years (mean follow-up: 5.1 years). The cohort was predominantly male (n=1815, 85.4%). The majority were former (n=1172, 55.1%) and never (n=769, 36.2%) smokers with an overall median age of 70 (IQR: 63–75) years. The median smoking duration and time since cessation were 24 years (IQR: 13–72) and 32 years (IQR: 22–41), respectively. The median smoking intensity was 17 cigarettes/day (IQR: 10–25). COPD was observed in 43.3% (n=930) of the cohort, and 12.7% (n=270) reported a personal history of cancer. Across the cohort, the median time since first exposure (latency) was 54 (48–59) years, and the median duration of exposure to asbestos was 15.5 (IQR: 6–24) years, with the median estimated cumulative exposure to asbestos being 0.41 (IQR: 0.001–1.55) fibres/mL-yrs.

A total of 51 LCs (2.4% of the cohort) were diagnosed, of which 36 were former smokers and nine were never smokers. Follow-up duration across the cohort ranged from a single baseline scan (T0) to 9.8 years, with a median follow-up duration of 5.7 years (IQR: 2.2–8.1). The median follow-up for individuals with and without LC were 4.1 years (IQR: 1.1–7.4) and 5.9 years (IQR: 2.2–8.1), respectively. Model discrimination

Model performance ranged from an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.602 (95% CI 0.505 to 0.698) for the PLCOm2012 model to 0.675 (95% CI 0.590 to 0.761) for the LLPv2 model. The PLCOocc model had the same discrimination as the standard PLCOm2012 model, and therefore shared an AUC of 0.602 (95% CI 0.505 to 0.698). All models indicated poor F1 scores at selected risk thresholds, ranging from 0.068 for the PLCOm2012 risk model to 0.0937 for the Bach model. When considering the optimal follow-up durations for LLPv2 (5 years) and PLCO model variants (6 years), improvements were noted with LLPv2 and PLCO model variants, all reaching an F1 score ≥0.1, but overall, the scores remained poor. All models performed better than the categorical USPSTF2021 and MSAC eligibility criteria, both of which had F1 scores of 0.0395 (see online supplemental S1).

Similarly, all risk models demonstrated higher sensitivity, over the USPSTF2021 and MSAC eligibility criteria. The MSAC criteria in particular had a very low sensitivity of 0.07, with it identifying only 3 out of 42 LC cases that occurred in ARP participants with tobacco smoking exposure. Model calibration

Mean calibration indicated that all risk models underestimated the LC risk, except LLPv2 which overestimated the overall LC risk (mean calibration value 2.54 (95% CI 1.9 to 3.65)). The PLCOm2014 model underestimated risk the most (0.58 (95% CI 0.46 to 0.79)), followed by PLCOm2012 (0.7 (95% CI 0.54 to 0.98)), the Bach (0.76 (95% CI 0.59 to 1.04)) and PLCOocc (0.83 (95% CI 0.63 to 1.18)) models. Only PLCOm2012 and LLPv2 had calibrations that were significantly different from the null. Similar results were noted when model-specific follow-up durations were investigated for the LLPv2 (5 years) and PLCO (6 years) model variants. The greatest improvement was observed in PLCOm2014 risk model, which increased by a value of 0.08, reaching a mean calibration of 0.66 (95% CI 0.49 to 1). Detailed information on the mean calibration of all investigated risk models is found in online supplemental S1.

Clinical utility

For the outcome of LC incidence in the ARP cohort, decision curve analysis provided a range of risk thresholds for each model where the net benefit of applying the models was considered positive (decision curves for all investigated risk models are available in online supplemental S1). Risk thresholds were consistent across all risk models ranging from 0.1% to 3%. Indicating that the models could be clinically useful for the ARP cohort, if a false negative (missing a person with LC) is considered by a proposed screening programme, to be over 34 times worse than screening a healthy individual who will never be positive for LC. This metric does not consider economic feasibility of a screening programme.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that current eligibility criteria for LCS perform poorly in an asbestos exposed cohort with modest tobacco smoking exposure. All five models tested on the ARP cohort showed moderate performance, with AUC-ROCs ranging from around 0.60–0.68, with poor calibration. All models tested showed improved sensitivity compared with the categorical USPSTF2021 and Australian MSAC criteria.

This study is the first to validate these models on a high-risk occupational cohort with substantial and well-described asbestos exposure. Prior studies have compared and validated the PLCOm2012, LLPv2 and Bach risk models in other (non-occupationally exposed) populations, demonstrating relatively good discrimination and satisfactory calibration.12 33 PLCOm2012 in particular has performed consistently well, across multiple prospective and retrospective studies.121433 However, the performance of the models differed greatly based on study populations, as noted in the validation study by Ten Haaf et al, where the AUC-ROCs for 6-year LC incidence ranged from around 0.69–0.79 for PLCOm2012, 0.66–0.74 for LLPv2 and 0.68–0.78 for the Bach model.12

The necessity for LC risk models to be tailored to specific populations and risk profiles is well documented and multiple risk models have been developed and calibrated for this purpose.213638 While no risk model has explicitly been developed to address the RERI conferred by asbestos, or other occupational exposures, the Bach and LLPv2 models tested in this study do integrate a binary variable accounting for asbestos risk.10 11

Regardless of the inclusion of asbestos exposure as a risk variable, the differences in performance between the LC risk models in the ARP cohort was not significant, with LLPv2, Bach and all PLCO model variants presenting generously overlapping 95% CIs for their AUC-ROC. The overall low discrimination and calibration of all risk models in this study indicate a need for improved LC risk models for asbestos exposed populations.

With regard to the risk models which do incorporate a measure of asbestos exposure, asbestos is not simply an independent risk factor for LC. Due to RERI, it exhibits a complex synergism with tobacco smoking, together accounting for a supra-additive risk of LC.16 Coexistence of both risk factors is not uncommon, especially for occupational cohorts. These synergistic relationships are not included in the risk models investigated and could offer a means of improving the performance of LC risk models in asbestos exposed cohorts. Developed countries such as the UK and Australia have a history of significant occupational asbestos utilisation, with complete bans on asbestos-related products only occurring as recently as 1999 and 2003, respectively.18 Therefore, in order to adequately represent a population’s LC risk in these exposed countries, eligibility criteria for LCS programmes in such countries may need to consider the additional risk conferred by asbestos exposure. It is noteworthy that the LLPv2 that will be utilised by the UK screening programme overestimates risk in our, and previous studies.33 However, it is also noted that, since the ARP is a LDCT screening cohort, the detected rates of LC are expectedly higher than general population, which is also likely to affect the calibration of the risk models. This increased LC rate in ARP introduces a surveillance bias, relative to general population, limiting the study’s ability to explicitly link the miscalibration of the models to asbestos exposure.

All the risk models used in this study have important differences. They were developed on different populations, include different risk factors and LC outcomes, with the absolute risk estimates of these models also differing from each other.12 All the risk models were developed on populations with limited or unknown asbestos exposure, which in turn limits their generalisability to asbestos exposed populations.

The highly detailed phenotype and comprehensive follow-up of the ARP cohort is one of this study’s strengths. The ARP consists of nearly one-third of never smokers, which is rare for an LCS cohort. The poor performance of these existing LC risk models in the ARP population highlights that such occupational cohort risk profile and prediction likely differ from other screening populations.

However, the uniqueness of the ARP cohort may also present some limitations with regards to the generalisability of the results presented in this study, as other asbestos exposed populations may not have the same levels or patterns of exposure to asbestos in the ARP. Although the male predominance is as expected for an occupational cohort, the relatively small size of the ARP cohort also limited the total number of LC cases available for assessment, leading to issues such as overlapping of 95% CI intervals for AUC-ROCs of the risk prediction models. Additionally, some predictor variables deviated between the risk models. Of note is the binary asbestos exposure variable in the Bach and LLPv2 models, both of which differed on the criteria they used for considering an individual as positive for asbestos exposure. Similarly, the COPD variable also differed in definition between the models and required adjustment, which could lead to minor differences in risk estimates of the models.

On the future directions for development of more bespoke LC risk models for asbestos exposed populations, it is noted that different fibre types of asbestos have variations in carcinogenicity, with blue (crocidolite) and brown (amosite) asbestos being around 10 to 50 times more potent compared with white (chrysotile) asbestos.39 40 However, the reality of historical commercial asbestos utilisation was that different fibre types were often mixed, depending on market factors. Therefore, outside of populations involved in the mining of a specific type of asbestos, it becomes very challenging to estimate or approximate relative exposures to different fibre types. As risk model development requires relatively large, detailed datasets, improvements in qualitative and quantitative understanding of asbestos exposures in occupational cohorts would be required.

In conclusion, our study highlights that proposed eligibility criteria for participant selection in LCS programmes underperform in a cohort with a history of asbestos exposure, as compared with their performance in other standard populations. This highlights the need for development and validation of bespoke LC risk prediction models for high-risk cohorts exposed to occupational carcinogens.
