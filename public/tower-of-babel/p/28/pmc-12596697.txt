Severe Menorrhagia as the Initial Presentation of Myxedema Coma With Pericardial Effusion
Authors: Carlos Andres Penaherrera, Aseel Abualnil, Jacqueline S Martins Torrontegui, Punerdeep Singh Sidhu, Ibrahim Hamad, Ami Bhalodia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyxedema coma is a rare but serious complication of severe hypothyroidism, often presenting without coma, complicating diagnosis. Cardiovascular and hematologic abnormalities can predominate, including pericardial effusion and bleeding diathesis. We describe a 38-year-old woman with no thyroid history who presented with significant menorrhagia and acute anemia. She had fatigue, cold intolerance, and intermittent bradycardia but no hypothermia or altered mental status. Evaluation revealed a prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time and a large pericardial effusion with early tamponade, requiring pericardiocentesis. Thyroid testing showed profound hypothyroidism. Her presentation suggested myxedema coma despite preserved consciousness. She was treated with intravenous levothyroxine, hydrocortisone, red blood cell transfusion, and progestin therapy, resulting in complete resolution of the effusion and stabilization of hemoglobin. She remained asymptomatic on oral levothyroxine at 2-month follow-up. This case highlights an unusual presentation of new-onset hypothyroidism manifesting as menorrhagia with coagulopathy and pericardial effusion. The reversible nature of both the hematologic and cardiovascular findings underscores the importance of early recognition and treatment. Clinicians should consider thyroid dysfunction in unexplained heavy menstrual bleeding or severe anemia, particularly when systemic features suggest endocrine involvement.Keywords: hypothyroidism, myxedema coma, pericardial effusion, coagulopathy, menorrhagia

Introduction

Myxedema coma (MC) is a rare, life-threatening decompensation of severe hypothyroidism characterized by multisystem dysfunction and high morbidity that can complicate timely recognition and treatment, and despite what the name may suggest, often does not present with altered mental status or coma [1]. To improve diagnostic consistency, a widely cited point-based scoring system was proposed by Popoveniuc et al that integrates thermoregulatory, neurologic, cardiovascular, and metabolic parameters to quantify severity and support the diagnosis in atypical presentations, such as those with unaffected mental status [2]. Contemporary analyses of hospitalized patients underscore the ongoing clinical burden and adverse outcomes associated with MC in the United States [3]. Cardiovascular involvement is particularly salient and may include low-voltage electrocardiography, bradyarrhythmias, and large pericardial effusions with tamponade physiology [4], with notable reversibility after thyroid hormone replacement and, when indicated, pericardial drainage [5, 6].

Severe hypothyroidism also perturbs reproductive and hemostatic physiology, predisposing to anovulatory abnormal uterine bleeding and, in select cases, catastrophic menorrhagia with profound anemia [7]. In both adolescents and adults, menorrhagia may be the sentinel manifestation of unrecognized thyroid failure, highlighting the need for endocrine screening in refractory heavy menstrual bleeding [8]. Hypothyroidism can affect both coagulation factors and platelet function [9]. Additional reports of MC with hypocoagulopathy and concurrent hematologic perturbations emphasize the multiplicity of pathways by which severe thyroid failure can present with life-threatening bleeding or clotting complications [10, 11].

Case Presentation

A 38-year-old woman with no significant medical history presented to the emergency department with 1 week of worsening menorrhagia. She reported that her menstrual bleeding had progressively become heavier over the preceding 3 months, but on this episode she required changing a pad almost every hour. She also described new-onset fatigue and cold intolerance during the same period.

Diagnostic Assessment

On arrival, hemoglobin was 4.9 g/dL (49 g/L) (reference: 12.0-16.0 g/dL [120-160 g/L]), white blood cell count was 3.1 × 10³/µL (3.1 × 10⁹/L) (reference: 4.8-10.8 × 10³/µL [4.8-10.8 × 10⁹/L]), and platelet count was 136 × 10³/µL (136 × 10⁹/L) (reference: 130-400 × 10³/µL [130-400 × 10⁹/L]). The activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) was prolonged at 37.5 seconds (reference: 25.2-28.1 seconds). No goiter, altered mental status, or hypothermia was noted, but she had intermittent bradycardia ranging from 57 to 65 beats/minute and brief episodes of hypotension as low as 85/54 mm Hg. The patient's weight was 149 pounds (67.9 kg), initial temperature was 37.2° C (99° F) and respiratory rate was 18 breaths/minute. Physical examination was notable for mild, nonpitting edema of both lower extremities and a neurological examination showed an alert and oriented patient without focal deficits. During evaluation for mild elevation in cardiac troponin, an electrocardiogram was obtained that showed low voltage but no acute ischemic changes. A transthoracic echocardiogram revealed a large circumferential pericardial effusion with early signs of tamponade (Fig. 1). Additional laboratory testing on arrival showed normal sodium of 138 mEq/L (138 mmol/L) (reference: 136-145 mEq/L [136-145 mmol/L]), potassium of 3.6 mEq/L (3.6 mmol/L) (reference: 3.5-5.1 mEq/L [3.5-5.1 mmol/L]), plasma glucose of 88 mg/dL (4.9 mmol/L) (reference: 72-108 mg/dL [4.0-6.0 mmol/L]), serum creatinine of 1.1 mg/dL (97 µmol/L) (reference: 0.7-1.5 mg/dL [62-93 µmol/L]), and a grossly unremarkable comprehensive panel besides a mildly elevated aspartate aminotransferase of 40 IU/L (40 U/L) (reference: 10-30 IU/L [10-30 U/L]).

Treatment

She was admitted for red blood cell transfusion and observation and started on oral medroxyprogesterone 20 mg twice daily for bleeding control. Pericardiocentesis was performed, removing 420 mL of serous fluid, with continued drainage over the next 48 hours. Further laboratory testing after admission revealed her TSH was >100 mIU/L (SI: > 100 mIU/L) (reference range: 0.4-4.5 mIU/L [0.4-4.5 mIU/L]) and free thyroxine was undetectable at <0.1 ng/dL (SI: < 1.3 pmol/L) (reference range: 0.93-1.7 ng/dL [12-22 pmol/L]). Unfortunately, morning cortisol levels and thyroid peroxidase antibodies were not tested during admission. She had no prior diagnosis of hypothyroidism. She received intravenous levothyroxine 100 mcg followed by a single dose of intravenous hydrocortisone 100 mg. A repeat echocardiography after 48 hours demonstrated complete resolution of the pericardial effusion, but a repeat aPTT level was not obtained.

Outcome and Follow-up

A precipitating event for MC was identified in the acute blood loss anemia from menorrhagia, and this along with the mild bradycardia and hypotension, pericardial effusion and electrocardiograph changes provided a total Popoveniuc score of 60, making MC very likely. Her hemoglobin improved to 10.3 g/dL (103 g/L) after transfusion of 3 units of packed red blood cells and remained stable. She was transitioned to oral levothyroxine 150 mcg daily and discharged in stable condition with endocrinology follow-up. Two months later, during office follow-up, her heart rate was 68 beats/minute, blood pressure was 96/80 mm Hg, and physical examination including neck examination was unremarkable. A repeat TSH was 0.1 mIU/L after adherence to daily oral levothyroxine replacement, and her dose had to be adjusted to 125 mcg to correct mild iatrogenic hyperthyroidism. She reported normalization of menstrual cycles and was fully asymptomatic.

Discussion

This case underscores how severe hypothyroidism may be discovered during the evaluation of life-threatening blood loss anemia from heavy menstrual bleeding, whereas a concurrent large pericardial effusion reveals the spectrum of fluid decompensation in myxedema states [1]. Interestingly, our patient did not have a goiter or symptoms of thyroid disease despite her severely abnormal thyroid hormones, delaying her diagnosis as her TSH was only checked after admission. Prior reports show that menorrhagia can be a sentinel manifestation of profound hypothyroidism and may culminate in dangerous anemia, mirroring our patient's presentation [7]. Adolescent data likewise document heavy menstrual bleeding as one of the initial signs of unrecognized thyroid failure, supporting a low threshold for thyroid testing in refractory bleeding [8]. Employing an objective framework such as the Popoveniuc diagnostic score helps justify the diagnosis of myxedema coma in noncomatose presentations like ours. This diagnostic score can be found within the original article by Poponeviuc et al and includes variables such as vital signs, gastrointestinal, neurological, metabolic, and cardiovascular findings along with consideration for a precipitating event [2]. Even with contemporary care, MC in hospitalized patients carries substantial morbidity and mortality, which reinforces the importance of early recognition when multisystem findings coexist [3].

The hemostatic disturbances of severe hypothyroidism provide a biologic explanation for our patient's hemorrhagic presentation and critical anemia. Thyroxine deficiency reduces thyroid hormone receptor–mediated transcription of von Willebrand factor and factor VIII in endothelial cells and hepatocytes, and blunts adrenergic-mediated von Willebrand factor release, leading to reduced synthesis and secretion of these factors and impaired platelet adhesion [9, 12]. Broader reviews delineate a spectrum of prolonged aPTT to combined factor defects, with bleeding risk predominating in overt hypothyroidism, explaining the pathophysiology of our patient's hematologic presentation [13]. These changes tend to correct rapidly on initiation of proper thyroxine supplementation [9]. Case literature in MC reinforces these mechanisms, including bleeding diathesis and pancytopenia as contributors to hemorrhagic risk in decompensated thyroid states [10]. Additional reports describe concomitant deficiencies of contact pathway factors XI and XII alongside septic precipitants, illustrating how superimposed triggers can amplify bleeding risk during myxedema [11]. In our patient, we can presume that untreated hypothyroidism lead to progressively worsening coagulation disruption, which in turn caused progressive menorrhagia ending with blood loss anemia. The latter condition triggered myxedema in the background of existing severe thyroid dysfunction. Prompt transfusion, progestin therapy, and thyroid hormone repletion paralleled stabilization of hemoglobin, aligning with the reversible nature of hypothyroidism-related coagulopathy [9]. Of note, in this case, beyond a prolonged aPTT, no other coagulation markers were obtained on arrival.

Pericardial and other serosal effusions in myxedema arise from increased capillary permeability, interstitial accumulation of hydrophilic glycosaminoglycans, and impaired lymphatic drainage, often compounded by low cardiac output and renal hypoperfusion, a pathophysiology consistent with our patient's large effusion [1]. Clinically, low-voltage electrocardiography, bradycardia, and echocardiographic evidence of early tamponade guide recognition and urgency of intervention [4]. Large effusions with hemodynamic compromise tend to regress after thyroid hormone repletion, though invasive procedures are warranted when tamponade physiology or diagnostic uncertainty mandates decompression, as in our case [5]. Multisystem reports further document coexisting manifestations, such as rhabdomyolysis and otologic symptoms, demonstrating the variety of serosal and tissue fluid shifts in profound hypothyroidism [6].

For clinical practice, this case supports routine thyroid screening in unexplained heavy menstrual bleeding or severe anemia, especially when systemic findings suggest endocrine involvement [7]. Contemporary reviews emphasize that MC may present without classic coma and that timely intravenous levothyroxine along with stress-dose glucocorticoids is central to reversing decompensation, which guided our management [14]. Morning cortisol level was not checked during her admission, and this is a recommended step to determine the presence of coexisting adrenal insufficiency, which would lead to prioritizing intravenous steroids over levothyroxine [14]. New-onset hypothyroidism culminating in MC has been reported in previously healthy adults, reinforcing a low diagnostic threshold when multisystem manifestations coexist [15]. A structured approach that combines an objective score with risk assessment and coordinated care across endocrinology, cardiology, gynecology, and critical care is most likely to optimize outcomes in presentations like this one [2].
