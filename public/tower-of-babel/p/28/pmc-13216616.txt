Association of neovascular glaucoma with increased mortality and cardiovascular risk: a multicenter propensity-matched study
Authors: Yossi Eshel, Shoham Kubovsky, Natan Lishinsky-Fischer, Tehila Shlomov, Itay Nitzan, Itay Chowers, Jaime Levy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeovascular glaucoma (NVG) is a severe, vision-threatening secondary glaucoma associated with ocular ischemia and systemic vascular disease. Whether NVG also serves as a marker of systemic vascular risk remains unclear. In this retrospective, multicenter cohort study, adults with NVG were identified from a global electronic health record network and 1:1 propensity score matched to controls, accounting for demographics, comorbidities, medications, and healthcare utilization. Follow-up was up to 10 years. Primary outcomes were all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, stroke, cardiac arrest, and three-point major adverse cardiovascular events (3P-MACE). Sensitivity analyses compared NVG with primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) and examined subgroups with proliferative diabetic retinopathy, retinal vein occlusion, or ocular ischemic syndrome. Among 17,020 matched pairs, NVG was associated with higher risks of all-cause mortality (HR 1.70, 95% CI 1.60–1.81), myocardial infarction (HR 1.53, 95% CI 1.40–1.67), cardiac arrest (HR 1.57, 95% CI 1.36–1.82), stroke (HR 1.62, 95% CI 1.48–1.79), and 3P-MACE (HR 1.59, 95% CI 1.51–1.68). Findings were consistent across sensitivity and ischemic retinal subgroups. Introduction

Neovascular glaucoma (NVG) is a vision-threatening form of secondary glaucoma, characterized by pathologic neovascularization of the iris and anterior chamber angle. In over 95% of cases, NVG arises secondary to severe retinal ischemia, which triggers upregulation of angiogenic mediators, most prominently vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)1,2. The resulting fibrovascular membrane obstructs aqueous outflow, leading to rapid intraocular pressure elevation and often severe glaucomatous optic neuropathy1.

The most common predisposing conditions for NVG are diabetic retinopathy (DR, 33% of cases), ischemic central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO, 33%), and ocular ischemic syndrome (OIS, 13%), collectively accounting for nearly 80% of cases2,3. Less frequent causes include other retinal vascular diseases with profound ischemia, such as central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO) and branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO), as well as intraocular neoplasms, chronic retinal detachment, severe intraocular inflammation, and sickle cell retinopathy2.

NVG has long been associated with poor visual outcomes. In one series, 68% of patients had best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) ≤ 20/200 at 6 months4, while other studies report that 75–90% of patients develop significant visual impairment or blindness5. Beyond ocular morbidity, emerging evidence suggests that NVG may serve as a clinical marker of systemic vascular disease. The conditions underlying NVG imply that ocular ischemia often reflects broader microvascular dysfunction affecting other end organs. NVG has been associated with a 2.24-fold increased risk of stroke6 and substantially higher mortality, with reported 5-year mortality of 38%7, median survival of 9.2 years following tube shunt surgery, and over 50% mortality at 10 years8. In the largest published cohort to date (186 NVG patients), all-cause mortality was 2.0–3.09 times higher than in patients with primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG)9, whereas POAG itself has not been linked to increased mortality10. These findings suggest that patients with NVG may represent a population at elevated risk of life-threatening cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events6–9.

Despite these observations, large-scale, population-level studies remain limited. In particular, it is unclear whether patients with proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) or retinal vein occlusion (RVO), the most frequent NVG etiologies, face higher systemic risks than individuals with the same underlying conditions who do not develop NVG. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a multi-institutional electronic health record–based observational study to evaluate associations between NVG and long-term mortality, myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiac arrest compared with matched controls without NVG.

Methods

Data source and study setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using TriNetX (https://live.trinetx.com), a large federated electronic health record (EHR) network covering more than 180 million patients from over 150 healthcare organizations (HCOs) across North America, Europe, and Asia. The database includes de-identified demographic, diagnostic, and encounter-level data. This analysis was conducted on September 9th, 2025.

Study population

Patients with NVG were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes. Because no single ICD-10 code uniquely defines NVG, a composite algorithm was applied, incorporating the most common etiologies of NVG2,3: retinal vein occlusions, diabetes mellitus with proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) or ophthalmic complications, and carotid artery occlusion. Patients were classified as having NVG if they subsequently received a diagnosis of glaucoma secondary to eye disorder or other specified glaucoma, consistent with ICD-10 Glaucoma Reference Guide11. To be eligible, patients were required to have any form of recorded follow-up at least 1 month after the diagnosis of glaucoma. This requirement was applied similarly to all study cohorts to ensure adequate capture of clinical outcomes within the database and to minimize the inclusion of individuals with incomplete records. The index date was defined as the first recorded NVG diagnosis. Because only de-identified data were used, the study was conducted in compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and was deemed exempt from institutional review board approval. Code lists are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Control cohort

The control group consisted of patients aged 50 years or older with a confirmed diagnosis of cataract, consistent with previous TriNetX studies on RVO12, and who had at least one follow-up visit occurring 1 month or more after the initial cataract diagnosis. Cataract patients were selected to serve as an initial ophthalmic comparator group commonly encountered in routine clinical practice. Patients were excluded if they had received any intravitreal injection, including anti-VEGF agents (aflibercept, ranibizumab, bevacizumab, brolucizumab, or faricimab), as identified by Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System (HCPCS), RxNorm, or Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes. Additional exclusions included prior treatment with retinal photocoagulation for extensive or progressive retinopathy, as well as diagnoses of proliferative diabetic retinopathy, other diabetic ophthalmic complications, retinal vascular occlusions, or carotid artery occlusions. Patients with secondary glaucoma or other specified glaucoma were also excluded. This design ensures the cohort represents cataract patients without neovascular glaucoma. Code lists are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Propensity score-matching

Extensive Propensity score matching (PSM) in a 1:1 ratio was performed using demographic, clinical, medication, and healthcare utilization variables assessed within the 3 years to 1 day before the index event. Demographics included age, sex, and race/ethnicity (White, Black or African American, Asian). Clinical covariates included hypertension, hyperlipidemia, obesity, smoking, chronic kidney disease, diabetes with complications, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, atrial fibrillation, mental and neurodevelopmental disorders, infectious diseases, malignancy, and musculoskeletal/connective tissue disorders. Medication variables encompassed long-term steroid use, cardiovascular therapies, anticoagulants and antithrombotics, insulin, metformin, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 inhibitors, and immunosuppressants. Healthcare utilization variables included ambulatory and emergency visits. Covariate imbalance was defined as a standardized mean difference > 0.1 with p < 0.05. Full Code lists are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were all-cause mortality, acute myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic), and three-point major adverse cardiovascular events (3P-MACE)13, defined as any of the codes listed above in addition to transient cerebral ischemic attacks and related syndromes, vascular syndromes of the brain in cerebrovascular diseases, and acute ischemic heart disease. Code lists are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

All outcomes were evaluated over a 10-year follow-up period, with a 30-day gap between the index event and the start of the follow-up window to minimize the risk of capturing incidents unrelated to the exposure of interest.

We assigned positive and negative control variables to test the validity of the cohort construction. The positive control included any of the following: aqueous shunts to extraocular equatorial plate reservoirs, either with graft or without graft, treatment of extensive or progressive retinopathy via photocoagulation, intravitreal injections of pharmacologic agents, anti-VEGF injections particularly ranibizumab 0.1 mg, bevacizumab 10 mg, brolucizumab-dbll 1 mg, faricimab-svoa 0.1 mg, and aflibercept 1 mg. Malignant neoplasm of colon was set as a negative control variable. Code lists are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Statistical analysis

Patients with a prior history of the outcome before the follow up window were excluded, and Follow-up was censored at the earliest occurrence of any of the following: (1) the end of the follow-up window, (2) the last recorded entry in a patient’s EHR, or (3) the occurrence of the outcome of interest in the patient’s EHR. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were then conducted for each outcome, and survival curves were compared using the log-rank test, which produces a chi-square (χ2) statistic with one degree of freedom. Log-rank test p-values were considered significant if they were smaller than 0.05. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were subsequently estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. The HR reflects the relative hazard of developing the outcome in the treatment group compared with the control group. The proportional hazards assumption, which requires HRs to remain constant over time, was evaluated in TriNetX using R’s survival package (v3.2–3), validated against SAS 9.4, with chi-square tests and corresponding p-values reported as indicators of proportionality.

Sensitivity analyses

Multiple subgroup sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings. First, we performed time-based comparisons of NVG versus cataract controls at 1, 3, and 5 years. Second, we compared NVG with primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG). Third, we carried out an etiology-based analysis, evaluating patients with conditions strongly associated with NVG and comparing those who developed NVG with those who did not. Etiology-specific analyses were preformed separately for both RVO and PDR cohorts, comparing patients who developed NVG with those who did not within each group. The purpose of the subgroup analysis was to evaluate the association in more comparable patient populations, thereby reducing the potential influence of confounding.

Additional stratified analyses within the NVG cohort included (1) patients with rubeosis iridis and (2) patients who underwent surgical interventions after NVG diagnosis, including aqueous shunt implantation, transscleral cyclophotocoagulation, and endoscopic cyclophotocoagulation without lens removal. These subgroups were compared against cataract controls. Code lists are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Results

NVG versus cataract

Before propensity score matching (PSM), the study population included 17,818 NVG patients and 2,078,651 cataract controls. NVG patients were younger (66.14 ± 9.55 vs 67.32 ± 7.86 years), predominantly male and Black, and exhibited higher rates of systemic comorbidities, greater use of relevant systemic medications (steroids, cardiovascular, and diabetes-related agents), and more frequent emergency healthcare utilization.

After 1:1 PSM, both groups included 17,020 patients. The cohorts were well balanced with respect to age (66.21 ± 9.54 vs 66.27 ± 8.56 years), sex, race, and most comorbidities. Diabetes and its complications, CKD, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, cardiovascular medication use, and emergent healthcare utilization remained slightly more frequent in NVG patients. Although some variables remained statistically significant due to the large sample size, the standardized differences indicated adequate balance between cohorts. Over 10 years of follow-up, NVG patients experienced consistently higher rates of adverse outcomes compared with matched controls. All-cause mortality reached 18.37% in the NVG group versus 9.76% in controls (HR 1.70; 95% CI [1.60, 1.81]; log-rank p < 0.0001). Acute myocardial infarction occurred in 8.10% of NVG patients compared with 4.83% of controls (HR 1.53; 95% CI [1.40, 1.67]; p < 0.0001), while cardiac arrest was observed in 3.02% versus 1.73% (HR 1.57; 95% CI [1.36, 1.82]; p < 0.0001). Stroke incidence was similarly elevated (7.51% vs 4.25%; HR 1.62; 95% CI [1.48, 1.79]; p < 0.0001), and intracranial hemorrhage occurred in 2.09% of NVG patients versus 1.42% of controls (HR 1.31; 95% CI [1.11, 1.54]; p = 0.0015). For the composite cardiovascular endpoint (3P-MACE), 26.59% of NVG patients were affected compared with 15.68% of controls (HR 1.59; 95% CI [1.51, 1.68]; p < 0.0001). Sensitivity analyses

NVG versus cataract outcomes at 1, 3, and 5 years

NVG patients demonstrated higher rates of mortality, acute myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, stroke, and the composite endpoint (3P-MACE) at 1, 3, and 5 years. The complete characteristic before and after PSM is provided in Supplementary Tables S4–S6. Intracranial hemorrhage showed no early difference at 1 year but became significant by 3 and 5 years. The complete results of the comparison between NVG and cataract patients are provided in Supplementary Table S7, Supplementary Fig. S1.

NVG versus POAG

The complete characteristics of both groups, before and after PSM, are presented in Supplementary Tables S8−S11. After 1:1 PSM, both groups included 15,833 patients. After matching, small residual differences persisted with adequate balance between cohorts.

At 1 year, mortality was higher in NVG (3.0% vs 2.0%; HR 1.44; 95% CI [1.25, 1.66]; p < 0.0001). This difference widened over time, with 10-year mortality reaching 17.1% in NVG vs 8.9% in POAG (HR 1.78; 95% CI [1.67, 1.90], p < 0.0001). Acute myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, and stroke were consistently more frequent in NVG, with hazard ratios between 1.3 and 1.7 depending on the outcome and follow-up period. For composite cardiovascular outcomes, 3P-MACE incidence was 5.9% at 1 year and 25.07% at 10 years in NVG, compared with 3.64% and 14.49% in POAG (HR 1.67; 95% CI [1.58, 1.77], p < 0.0001 at 10 years).

Positive control outcomes showed much higher event rates in NVG, while negative control outcomes showed no significant differences. The complete list of results of the comparison between patients with NVG and patients with POAG is summarized in Supplementary Table S12 and Supplementary Fig. S2 summarize and illustrate the outcomes of interest.

NVG versus all etiologies

In this analysis, we sought to determine whether patients with conditions most associated with the development of NVG (RVO, PDR, OIS) are at increased risk of mortality and cardiovascular complications compared with patients with the same underlying diagnoses who did not develop NVG. After 1:1 PSM (19,793 per group), demographic and clinical characteristics were well balanced (standardized differences < 0.05). Small residual imbalances persisted: NVG patients had slightly higher rates of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, CKD and type 2 diabetes with kidney complications. Cardiovascular drug use and anticoagulant use were also marginally higher in NVG. Emergency visits remained more frequent. Overall, differences were very small. The complete characteristics of both groups, before and after PSM, are presented in Supplementary Tables S13–S16.

NVG patients had higher mortality at 1 year (3.33% vs 2.81%; HR 1.21; 95% CI [1.08, 1.35]; p = 0.0012) and at 10 years (18.28% vs 16.00%; HR 1.31; 95% CI [1.25, 1.37]; p < 0.0001). At 10 years, NVG was also associated with increased risk of cardiac arrest (HR 1.18; 95% CI [1.05, 1.33]; p = 0.006) and composite cardiovascular outcomes (3P-MACE) (HR 1.16; 95% CI [1.11, 1.21]; p < 0.0001). Acute myocardial infarction, Stroke and intracranial hemorrhage showed no significant differences between groups at any follow-up period. Positive control outcomes showed substantially higher rates in NVG, while negative control outcomes showed no significant differences. The complete list of results of the comparison is summarized in Supplementary Table S17 and Supplementary Fig. S3.

We also isolated specific etiologies for NVG. Among patients with retinal vein occlusion (RVO), those who developed NVG had higher 10-year mortality (HR = 1.35; 95% CI [1.19, 1.53]) and an increased incidence of composite cardiovascular outcomes (3P-MACE) (HR = 1.17; 95% CI [1.05, 1.30]). A similar pattern was observed among patients with PDR where NVG was associated with higher 10-year mortality (HR = 1.30; 95% CI [1.22, 1.39]) and an increased incidence of composite cardiovascular outcomes (3P-MACE) (HR = 1.26; 95% CI [1.19, 1.34]). Additional analysis

We conducted two additional subgroup analyses. In the first, we compared patients who met previously described criteria for NVG in combination with other vascular disorders of the iris and ciliary body, the closest available surrogate for iris neovascularization, with a control group of patients with cataract. Over a 10-year follow-up, this cohort demonstrated higher all- cause mortality (HR 1.73; 95% CI [1.49, 2.01]) and an increased incidence of composite cardiovascular outcomes (3P-MACE) (HR 1.59; 95% CI [1.4, 1.8]).

In the second analysis, we compared patients with NVG who additionally underwent tube shunt surgery or cyclophotocoagulation with a control group of patients with cataract. Over a 10-year follow-up, this cohort also exhibited higher mortality (HR = 1.67; 95% CI [1.48, 1.88]) and an increased incidence of composite cardiovascular outcomes (3P-MACE) (HR = 1.61; 95% CI [1.45, 1.79]). Discussion

This study evaluated the association between NVG and the risk of mortality and major cardiovascular events using a large, multicenter electronic health record database encompassing more than 180 million patients. Outcomes were assessed in 17,818 individuals with NVG compared with a matched control population, applying propensity score matching to adjust for comorbidities and conducting multiple complementary analyses to test robustness. Patients with NVG had significantly higher risks of all-cause mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events compared with matched controls over 1-, 3-, 5-, and 10-year follow-up. Importantly, even among patients with ischemic retinal diseases strongly associated with NVG, including retinal vein occlusion (RVO) and proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), those who developed NVG experienced significantly higher mortality and cardiovascular event rates than matched counterparts who did not develop NVG. The use of cataract patients as a comparator group is consistent with prior TriNetX-based studies in retinal vascular disease, although the present study extends this approach through additional analyses across multiple comparator groups12.

These findings align with, yet extend, prior smaller studies reporting increased mortality among NVG patients6–9,14. Earlier investigations described five-year mortality rates of approximately 38% following tube shunt or cyclodestructive procedures⁷ and over 50% at ten years after tube shunt surgery8. The lower mortality in our cohort likely reflects limitations inherent to large databases, including incomplete data capture and loss to follow-up. In addition, visual acuity, previously linked to mortality among NVG patients with poor preoperative vision7,14, was unavailable and may partly account for differences in reported rates.

Clinically, these results suggest that the development of NVG may serve as a systemic warning sign indicating elevated cardiovascular risk, rather than being viewed solely as a complication of ocular ischemia. Integrating NVG-related risk assessment into ophthalmic and systemic care pathways may facilitate early cardiovascular evaluation and preventive management. Multidisciplinary collaboration between ophthalmologists, internists, and cardiologists is warranted to address this elevated systemic risk. Patient counseling should emphasize the importance of cardiovascular health and adherence to preventive measures, including lifestyle modification and strict control.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, although NVG was defined using diagnostic codes previously proposed in the ICD-10 Glaucoma Reference Guide11, coding variability and potential misclassification remain possible. The TriNetX platform provides de-identified aggregated data and does not allow access to individual patient records, ophthalmology notes, or imaging data. Therefore, chart-level validation and calculation of a positive predictive value for the NVG identification algorithm were not possible. Importantly, misclassification, either the inclusion of non-NVG patients in the NVG cohort or NVG patients in the control group, would likely bias results toward the null rather than inflate the observed associations. The persistence of significant associations despite this potential bias supports the robustness of our findings. To further strengthen the exposure definition, we performed additional high-specificity sensitivity analyses, including a subgroup of patients with vascular disorders of the iris and ciliary body (a surrogate for rubeosis iridis) and a subgroup undergoing procedures commonly associated with advanced NVG, such as aqueous shunt implantation and cyclodestructive procedures. These analyses yielded similar associations with mortality and cardiovascular outcomes, providing indirect support for the validity of the exposure definition. Second, although extensive PSM was performed, residual confounding related to systemic disease severity may persist, particularly in comparisons with cataract and POAG controls, as these populations are not typically associated with retinal ischemia and may therefore represent relatively healthier groups. To address this concern, we conducted additional analyses comparing patients with the same underlying ischemic retinal etiologies who developed NVG with those who did not. The persistence of elevated mortality and cardiovascular risk across these analyses supports the robustness of the observed associations. Third, the proportional hazards assumption was not fully satisfied for some outcomes across all three comparisons. When this assumption is violated, the reported hazard ratios should be interpreted as average effects over the follow-up period rather than constant effects over time. However, analyses across multiple predefined follow-up intervals for each comparison did not demonstrate consistent violations of this assumption. Finally, details on disease severity, duration, or imaging data such as fluorescein angiography (FA) and OCT angiography (OCTA) were not available within the dataset.

Despite these limitations, this cohort represents one of the largest reported to date, allowing the evaluation of mortality, myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiac arrest across extended follow-up intervals. Rigorous propensity matching and multiple sensitivity analyses enhance the validity of our findings and confirm their reproducibility across comparator groups.

Future research incorporating FA or OCTA could quantify the extent of retinal ischemia and further explore the mechanistic links between ocular neovascularization and systemic vascular risk. Large epidemiologic datasets including visual acuity measurements would also clarify whether ocular disease severity correlates with systemic outcomes.

In conclusion, this large, multicenter, propensity-matched analysis suggests that the occurrence of NVG is associated with increased long-term mortality and major cardiovascular events, even after controlling for known vascular risk factors and for ischemic diseases such as RVO and PDR. These findings suggest that NVG may serve as a clinical marker of systemic vascular disease and highlight the potential value of cardiovascular evaluation in affected patients.
