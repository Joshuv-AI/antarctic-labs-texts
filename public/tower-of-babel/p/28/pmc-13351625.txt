Sociodemographic and gender affirmation factors associated with gender euphoria: a cohort study
Authors: Charlotte Blacklock, Michelle Tollit, Carmen Pace, Bradley Elphinstone, Sav Zwickl, Ada Cheung, Ken C Pang, Simone Buzwell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundGender euphoria encompasses positive feelings associated with gender expression, identity, and affirmation, and is important for wellbeing. However, little is known about how gender euphoria is experienced by different subgroups within trans communities. Such knowledge could contribute to a more holistic understanding of trans health and promote more accurate and inclusive care. This study aimed to explore differences in gender euphoria by sociodemographic and gender affirmation factors.MethodsIn this cohort study in Australia, participants aged 16 years or older who were enrolled in a longitudinal trans-led project (TRANSform), with a gender other than that presumed for them at birth, were invited to complete surveys between 22nd November 2022 and 12th April 2023. Participants completed the Gender Euphoria Scale (GES) comprising three subscales: social-affirmation, self-affirmation, and community connection, and reported on frequency and strength of gender euphoria. Demographics were collected through the TRANSform study including rurality and socioeconomic deciles. Responses were subsequently categorised into those who had accessed hormones or surgery and those who were contemplating or desiring hormones or surgery. Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis H tests were used to compare differences between groups.FindingsParticipants (N = 732) were aged 16–79 years and were women (36·9%), men (36·9%), non-binary and gender diverse (25·8%) or had a culturally specific gender identity (0·4%). Non-binary participants reported higher community connection euphoria but lower self-affirmation euphoria than women and men. Gen X participants (born 1965–1980) reported higher social affirmation euphoria than millennial participants (born 1981–1996). Participants who had accessed gender affirming hormones had higher self-affirmation euphoria than those who had not, as did those who had undergone gender affirming surgery compared to those who had not. Gender euphoria did not significantly differ by birth continent, rurality, or socioeconomic indices.InterpretationResults highlight the diversity of gender euphoric experiences within trans communities and can be used to inform individualised supports across gender affirming care, mental health, and community settings.FundingThis study was funded by Australian Rotary Health, ASC is supported by a NHMRC Investigator Grant #2008956, SZ is supported by a Fellowship from the Faculty of Medicine, Dentistry, and Health Sciences, The University of Melbourne.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Gender euphoria describes positive gender-related feelings and is a significant facet of wellbeing for many trans people. Despite the importance of gender euphoria, it has historically been omitted from the academic literature. A systematic review and meta-synthesis published in February 2025 by Kulesza and colleagues searched Embase, ProQuest, PsycINFO, PubMed, and Scopus using the keywords and MeSH terms “gender euphori∗,” “euphori∗,” “transgender∗,” “nonbinary,” “gender nonconforming,” and “gender diverse,” with Boolean operators “AND” and “OR.” We replicated this search strategy and applied it to the timeframe spanning February 2025 to June 2025 to identify any subsequent studies. Collectively, these searches identified a growing body of qualitative research on gender euphoria but only two quantitative studies, reflecting a significant gap in the research. The limited number of quantitative studies likely stems from the fact that a tool to measure gender euphoria has only recently been developed. While most research on gender euphoria has focussed on its antecedents, little is known regarding the ways in which gender euphoria intersects with sociodemographic and gender affirmation factors.

Added value of this study

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to use a consistent and reliable tool to explore how dimensions of gender euphoria vary according to sociodemographic characteristics (such as gender, generational age, birth continent, rurality, and socioeconomic status) and different forms of medical affirmation. Our findings demonstrate that while gender euphoria is commonly experienced throughout trans communities, dimensions of gender euphoria differ by gender identity, age, gender affirming hormone use, and gender affirming surgery status.

Implications of all the available evidence

While previous work has predominantly focused on gender dysphoria, distress, and mental health disparities in trans communities, this study contributes to a growing body of research highlighting the importance of positive gender-related experiences. Knowledge of the ways in which gender euphoria may be shaped by societal norms, forms of recognition, and access to gender affirming care could be used to inform individualised supports for trans people. Integrating gender euphoria into clinical and public health frameworks may contribute to more holistic understandings of trans health and address the needs of trans communities by facilitating affirmative experiences that promote gender-related wellbeing.

Introduction

In the trans health field, considerable academic attention has focussed on the challenges faced by trans and gender diverse (hereafter trans) communities including experiences of gender dysphoria, stigma, discrimination, and associated mental health disparities.1,2 However, there has been a recent shift towards positive experiences reported by trans people. To this end, gender euphoria has emerged as an important area of study which seeks to counter the distress laden narratives historically ascribed to the trans experience.3, 4, 5 Gender euphoria is a term that arose within the trans community to encompass a multitude of positive gender-related experiences including feelings of joy, freedom, confidence, and contentment occurring in the context of internal, external, and social stimuli.4,6, 7, 8 Though not all trans people experience gender euphoria, it appears prevalent,9 and can play an important role in trans wellbeing.10

While the body of empirical research on gender euphoria is small, knowledge about the ways in which gender euphoria emerges is growing. Indeed, a recent systematic review identified 18 qualitative studies detailing gender euphoria and its origins.11 Among these studies, gender euphoria was found to arise in a range of contexts including when exploring and experimenting with gender expression,10,12 when being affirmed by others,4,10,13 while connecting with chosen family and members of LGBTQ+ communities,14, 15, 16, 17, 18 and in the process of receiving gender affirming care.15,19, 20, 21 Collectively, these studies have advanced understandings of the antecedents of gender euphoria. However, despite these gains, much remains unknown about how gender euphoria varies across trans subpopulations. Without this knowledge, meaningful differences in gender euphoric experiences may be overlooked, and in turn omitted from trans health initiatives.

To date, only two studies have quantitatively examined differences in gender euphoria based on sociodemographic and gender affirmation variables. One of these studies asked 2165 trans adults in the US to rate the extent to which they felt happy with, and accepted, their gender identity.22 The study found higher odds of gender euphoria occurring among Black participants, trans women and non-binary participants, those with private health insurance, and those who had accessed gender affirming hormones.22 A further study comprising 1359 trans adults living in Australia, asked participants if they had ever experienced gender euphoria defined as “the experience of feeling totally affirmed in one's gender”.15 Results indicated that the odds of having ever experienced gender euphoria were higher among trans men compared to non-binary participants, those aged 18–24 years, participants with an undergraduate university degree, those living in an inner-suburban area, and those with access to gender affirming care.15 However, associations in these studies were somewhat limited by the unidimensional approaches used to measure gender euphoria, which has recently been shown to comprise several different dimensions.9 These dimensions include social affirmation (i.e., gender euphoria arising from being socially affirmed), self-affirmation (i.e., gender euphoria stemming from one's physical appearance), and community connection (i.e., gender euphoria attributable to community representation and connectedness).9 It therefore remains unknown if and how sociodemographic and gender affirmation factors are associated with these different aspects of gender euphoria.

Examining this is important as gender euphoria is increasingly understood as a multidimensional construct comprising diverse experiences shaped by social context and may represent an important indicator of overall wellbeing. Sociodemographic factors are likely to shape access to, and experiences of gender euphoria. For instance, gender identity may influence how gender euphoria is experienced, with nonbinary people reporting different patterns of affirmation and wellbeing, potentially reflecting differences in social recognition and access to gender affirming care.15 Age-related differences may also arise due to variations in timing and availability of gender-affirming experiences across the life course. Similarly, socioeconomic positioning, rurality, and cultural context may influence both opportunities for affirmation and exposure to minority stressors, with downstream implications for mental health and quality of life. Finally, access to medical gender affirmation is associated with reduced distress and improvements to mental health19 suggesting that differential access to affirmative interventions could impact gender euphoria. In this way, gender euphoria may be conceptualised as a salutogenic process, reflecting experiences that actively contribute to health and wellbeing, rather than solely the absence of distress.

Considering the recent development of The Gender Euphoria Scale9 which assesses the extent to which gender euphoria is experienced across a range of gender-affirming scenarios, further examination of sociodemographic and gender affirmation factors potentially contributing to gender euphoria is warranted. Identifying the factors associated with gender euphoria could broaden understandings of this under-researched concept in addition to illuminating the diversity of gender euphoric experience across trans communities. This in turn could allow for the development of affirmative interventions that increase gender-related wellbeing by targeting specific trans subpopulations. Accordingly, the aim of this study was to examine whether dimensions of gender euphoria vary by sociodemographic and gender-affirming factors utilising the recently developed Gender Euphoria Scale.9

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited through TRANSform—a longitudinal trans-led study run by the Trans Health Research Group at the University of Melbourne. TRANSform participants are recruited through social media, community organisations, and gender clinics, and are eligible to participate if they live in Australia, are aged 16 years or older, and have a gender other than what was presumed for them at birth. Between 22nd November and 5th December 2022, all TRANSform participants (n = 1985) were invited to complete a survey on gender euphoria and provide additional data on rurality, socioeconomic status, hormone use, and surgery between 10th February and 12th April 2023.

Procedure

Those wanting to participate (n = 732) were emailed links with invitations to complete the surveys via REDCap,23 an electronic data capture tool hosted at the University of Melbourne. Respondents were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could opt out at any time prior to survey submission. Participants were also notified that survey submission implied consent to participate.

Measures

Sociodemographic questions

Demographic data, including gender, generational age,24 and country of birth were collected as part of enrolment in the TRANSform study. Rurality and socioeconomic deciles25 were extrapolated from postcode data provided in a follow up study conducted between February and March 2023 (i.e., 2–3 months after gender euphoria data were collected). Socioeconomic deciles were determined via the index of relative socioeconomic advantage and disadvantage (IRSAD). This metric ranks Australian suburbs from most disadvantaged to most advantaged based on factors including income, education, and employment. Lower IRSAD deciles represent relatively greater disadvantage and lack of advantage, while higher IRSAD deciles indicate a lack of disadvantage and greater advantage.25

Gender affirmation

Participants were asked to describe their use of and desire for gender affirming hormones and surgery (see Supplementary Material for response options). Responses were subsequently categorised into those who had accessed hormones/surgery and those who were contemplating or desiring hormones/surgery, with those who did not want hormones or surgery excluded from analyses. Participants who reported ‘no interest’ in hormones or surgery were excluded from analyses as it was assumed that these participants represented a group with a conceptually distinct affirmation trajectory from those who had accessed, or were contemplating, medical affirmation.

The Gender Euphoria Scale (GES)

Administration of the Gender Euphoria Scale (GES) has previously been described.9 For further details please see Supplementary Material.

Gender euphoria strength and frequency

Separate to the GES, participants were also surveyed on their past experiences of gender euphoria, including how many times they had experienced gender euphoria over the past two weeks, and how strong these experiences had been. Participants were categorised as experiencing gender euphoria less than or equal to once a week, or more than once a week, and as having non-existent to slight, or moderate to very strong gender euphoria. For further details please see Supplementary Material.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.26 Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participant demographic data. Shapiro-Wilk testing and examination of the relevant histograms indicated non-normality of the data. Consequently, Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis H tests were used to compare differences between groups. Significance testing was conducted on mean ranks rather than medians, due to uneven distributions of scores. Effect sizes were calculated using rank biserial correlations (r). Chi-square tests of independence were used to assess associations between group membership and frequency and strength of gender euphoria, with Cramer's V as a measure of effect size.

Ethics statement

Ethical approval for the study was granted by Thorne Harbour Health, ACON, and the Austin Health Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/57155/Austin-2019) and approved by the Swinburne University Human Research Ethics Committee (24/06/21). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary, participants were informed of the purpose of the study, and were able to withdraw any time prior to survey submission.`

Role of the funding source

TRANSform was funded by a National Health and Medical Research Council Grant to ASC (#2008956) and this study was funded by an Australian Rotary Health scholarship to CAB. Neither funding source played a role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, report writing, or in the decision to submit the paper for publication.

Results

Participant characteristics

All TRANSform participants (n = 1985) were invited to complete the survey. Of those, 732 people responded. Participants were self-selecting and no exclusion criteria were applied. Participants were women (36·9%), men (36·9%), non-binary and gender diverse (herein non-binary) (25·8%) or had a culturally specific gender identity (0·4%). Participants ranged in age from 16 to 79 years (median = 32 years, IQR = 25, 43) and most were born in Australia (83·1%). Gender

Due to the small number of participants with a culturally specific gender identity (n = 3), comparisons were restricted to men, women, and non-binary participants. There were no significant differences between these three groups for the total GES scores (Table 2) or social affirmation subscale scores (Table 3). Significant differences in self-affirmation (Table 4) and community connection (Table 5) were observed with higher self-affirmation scores reported in women (p = <0·001, r = 0·19). and men (p = <0·001, r = 0·29) compared to non-binary participants. Conversely, non-binary participants had significantly higher community connection scores than women (p = <0·001, r = 0·22) and men (p = 0·03, r = 0·12). We did observe a significant association between gender and gender euphoria frequency (χ2(2) = 7·09, p = 0·029), with a small effect size (Cramer's V = 0·106) (Table 6). These results indicated non-binary participants experienced gender euphoria less frequently than men and women. Generational age

There were no significant differences for the self-affirmation and community connection subscales based on generational age (Tables 4 and 5). Overall, there was a significant difference in total GES scores between groups (p = 0·032), however, pairwise comparisons revealed no significant differences between individual group pairs (Table 2). Pairwise comparisons did, however, indicate significantly higher social affirmation scores among Gen X participants, compared to Millennial participants (p = 0·006, r = 0·14) (Table 3). We found no significant associations between frequency and strength of gender euphoria based on generational age (Tables 6 and 7).

Continent of birth

There were no significant differences in total (Table 2), social affirmation (Table 3), self-affirmation (Table 4), or community connection (Table 5) scores based on continent of birth. We similarly found no significant associations between frequency or strength of gender euphoria and continent of birth (Tables 6 and 7).

Rurality

There were also no significant differences in total (Table 2), social affirmation (Table 3), self-affirmation (Table 4), or community connection (Table 5) scores based on proximity to major cities. Likewise, there were no significant associations between frequency or strength of gender euphoria based on rurality (Tables 6 and 7).

Socioeconomic advantage and disadvantage

Total (Table 2), social affirmation (Table 3), self-affirmation (Table 4), and community connection (Table 5) scores did not significantly differ by IRSAD decile. Similarly, frequency and strength of gender euphoria were not significantly associated with IRSAD decile (Tables 6 and 7).

Access to gender affirming hormones

As shown in Table 4, participants who had accessed gender affirming hormones had significantly higher self-affirmation scores than those who had not accessed hormones but wanted or were considering hormone use (p < 0·001, r = 0·20). Total GES, social affirmation, and community connection scores were similar in those who had accessed hormones and those who had not yet accessed hormones but were contemplating or desired these (Tables 2, 3 and 5). We found no significant associations between frequency or strength of gender euphoria and use of gender affirming hormones (Tables 6 and 7).

Access to gender affirming surgery

Those who had undergone gender affirming surgery had significantly higher self-affirmation scores than those who had not undergone surgery but wanted or were contemplating surgery (p = 0·001, r = 0·18) (Table 4). Conversely, participants contemplating or desiring gender affirming surgery reported significantly higher levels of social affirmation (p = 0·017, r = 13) and community connection (p = 0·004, r = 0·16) than those who had undergone a gender affirming surgery (Tables 3 and 5). There were no significant differences in total GES scores (Table 2) or associations between frequency or strength of gender euphoria and having undergone gender affirming surgery (Tables 6 and 7). Discussion

This study is one of the first to quantitatively explore relationships between gender euphoria and sociodemographic and gender affirmation factors among trans adults. Two approaches were used to assess this. The first asked participants to rate the frequency and strength of gender euphoria over the past two weeks using single item measures. With the exception of non-binary participants who reported gender euphoria less frequently than men and women, there were no significant associations between frequency or strength of gender euphoria and sociodemographic and gender affirmation factors. Instead, differences in gender euphoria tended to emerge via the second approach, which involved use of the more comprehensive Gender Euphoria Scale. These findings indicated significant differences in gender euphoria by gender identity, age, gender affirming hormone use, and gender affirming surgery. No significant differences in gender euphoria were observed based on continent of birth, rurality, or socioeconomic status suggesting that gender euphoria is experienced consistently across these different contexts.

Among the key findings, men and women reported significantly higher gender euphoria related to self-affirmation than non-binary participants. This may be due to the pervasiveness of masculine and feminine appearance standards, compared with the lack of widely recognised non-binary physical ideals. An absence of appearance norms for non-binary people may lead to more individualised expressions of gender, which in turn could result in physical appearance being a less central source of gender euphoria within this group. Consistent with this, non-binary participants reported significantly higher levels of gender euphoria related to community connection than men and women. This may be because non-binary people have varied experiences of validation in mainstream society (e.g., through legal recognition or social visibility), so connecting with other non-binary people or seeing non-binary representation in media may elicit especially strong feelings of gender euphoria.

Regarding age, the only significant comparison was that Gen X participants reported higher levels of social affirmation euphoria than Millennial participants. This is possibly because older trans adults, including those from Gen X, came of age during a time of less awareness and acceptance of trans identities. As a result, there may have been fewer opportunities for external identity affirmation. Hence, being socially affirmed in the present day (e.g., through correct pronoun use) may feel especially meaningful for this group. Additionally, earlier access to medical care may support people in having their gender more readily recognised by others, which can in turn strengthen experiences of social affirmation. Relatedly, older generations of trans people may be more likely to have binary gender identities which could also help to explain this finding.

The lack of significant differences in gender euphoria based on continent of birth is perhaps unsurprising given all participants were living in Australia at the time of the study. In Australia, trans adults have certain legal protections (e.g., the Sex Discrimination Act, 1984), and community organisations and supports that collectively may be more impactful in influencing gender euphoria than birth continent. Sample size variability may too have played a role as certain groups (e.g., participants born in the Americas and Asia) were underrepresented in the current study. Adding to this, as groups were categorised by birth continent, heterogeneity within each group may have impacted analyses. Thus, while the absence of differences by birth continent could indicate that gender euphoria is more closely linked to affirming conditions in one's present environment than to place of birth, sampling limitations should be considered.

There were also no significant differences in gender euphoria between those living in metropolitan, urban, or suburban areas and those living in regional, rural, and remote areas. This diverges from previous research which found those living in inner suburban areas to be more likely to have experienced gender euphoria than those living in a regional city or town.15 This discrepancy is likely due to the more granular breakdown of regions in this prior study. However, findings from the present study also add complexity to what is currently known regarding the mental health of trans individuals living in more isolated areas. Prior research has found that trans people living regionally, rurally, and remotely face unique barriers including reduced access to gender affirming care, and lower societal acceptance, ultimately contributing to poorer mental health outcomes.27 Thus, findings from the present study could indicate that experiences of gender euphoria can coexist alongside contextual barriers and mental health challenges. This notion is supported by others who have found that despite the challenges associated with living in more isolated areas, trans individuals tend to be intentional in their pursuit of community, establishing micro communities and chosen families, in addition to seeking connection and affirmation online.28 Indeed, online spaces are documented sites of community connection where experiences of gender euphoria are discussed and reinforced.29

Socioeconomic status similarly did not appear to be associated with levels of gender euphoria. This is potentially because experiences of gender euphoria are linked to feeling affirmed by oneself or others, and connecting with community, all of which can occur regardless of income, education, or occupation.

Participants who had accessed gender affirming hormones reported significantly higher self-affirmation euphoria than those contemplating or desiring hormones. This finding is consistent with prior work identifying associations between gender euphoria and access to gender affirming hormones.22 This likely reflects gender euphoria occurring in the context of physical changes that align with a person's gender identity. Such changes may produce feelings of comfort and confidence that in turn heighten gender euphoria when looking in the mirror. In the case of gender affirming surgery, those who had undergone at least one gender affirming surgery also reported significantly higher self-affirmation euphoria than those who were contemplating or desired gender affirming surgery. As with access to hormones, this underscores the importance of access to gender affirming medical care to help facilitate gender euphoria.

Notably, those who had not undergone gender affirming surgery but were considering or wanting surgery reported significantly higher social affirmation and community connection euphoria that those who had undergone gender affirming surgery. This may be because those who have had surgery have already experienced an extended period of social affirmation and have community supports firmly in place. Gender euphoria may therefore not feel as pronounced in these well-established domains. Additionally, people considering gender affirming surgery may be engaging community to help with navigating the healthcare system and this engagement could potentially result in stronger community connection euphoria. Thus, while access to gender affirming medical interventions may be important for evoking gender euphoria related to physical appearance, other dimensions of gender euphoria—such as social affirmation and community centred euphoria—appear to be achieved without such interventions.

Overall, findings from the present study contribute additional insights to the growing body of research on gender euphoria. Consistent with prior albeit limited research15,22, the current study found associations between gender euphoria and access to gender affirming medical care. These findings reinforce the importance of making medical interventions available to trans populations. However, findings also underscore the multidimensional nature of gender euphoria, with medical affirmation providing just one avenue through which to achieve this. In this way, the results challenge transnormative assumptions regarding affirmation pathways, by highlighting how experiences of gender euphoria occur across contexts. Clinically, such findings can be used to better understand how sites of gender euphoria—and barriers to achieving euphoria—may differ across trans subgroups. Understanding these distinctions can help to inform individualised support strategies, thus promoting more tailored care.

Although we found almost no significant associations between frequency or strength of gender euphoria and sociodemographic or gender affirmation factors, gender euphoria frequency and strength remained fairly consistent across groups, with the majority reporting moderate to very strong gender euphoria more than once per week. This suggests that gender euphoria is a pervasive and intense experience for many trans people. Differences in gender euphoria did, however, emerge in the subscales of the GES. To date, gender euphoria has been operationalised unidimensionally or through single item questions and proxies.15,22,30 Findings from the current study emphasise the need to explore different dimensions of gender euphoria and point to the utility of the GES in meeting this need. To this end, the GES could be used to measure specific facets of gender euphoria following peer-based workshops, mental health interventions, and gender affirming medical care. In this way, the GES could provide practitioners with a multidimensional metric of positive mental health, alongside more commonly used measures of gender dysphoria.

Despite the contributions of this study, there are some limitations to consider. Firstly, participants in this study belong to an ongoing, trans-led study which implies a certain level of trans community connectedness. Relatedly, the current study was conducted in Australia where aspects of gender affirming medical care (e.g., hormones) are available to adults through the public health system. Thus, findings from this study may not generalise to trans populations with fewer community supports and restricted access to gender affirming care. A further limitation is that study data were collected at two different time points, with rurality and IRASD deciles extrapolated from postcode data collected several months after gender euphoria data were collected. Hence, these variables may not perfectly align with the responses provided two to three months prior. Additionally, there were differences in group size with some groups (e.g., those who had not accessed gender affirming hormones) underrepresented in the current study. Taken together, these limitations indicate the need for future studies to explore how gender euphoria might vary among trans communities in less affirming environments. Furthermore, as noted by others,11 most of the research on gender euphoria has been conducted with Western samples, and this study is no exception. Further research is needed to explore how experiences of gender euphoria differ across cultural contexts to deepen and expand conceptual understandings of gender euphoria.

This is the first study to systematically examine associations between gender euphoria and sociodemographic and gender affirmation factors using a reliable, multidimensional measure. Findings indicate that gender euphoria is both widespread and varied across trans communities, shaped by intersecting influences such as gender identity, forms of recognition, and access to gender affirming care. These insights offer a foundation for more tailored approaches to supporting trans people, moving beyond deficit-based models by centring the positive and health-promoting dimensions of gender experience. Continued research is needed to further clarify how gender euphoria relates to other health outcomes including mental health and quality of life across diverse contexts and communities. Such work could inform community organisations, gender affirming care providers, and mental health support workers in developing environments and interventions that actively foster gender euphoria and in turn support overall wellbeing.
