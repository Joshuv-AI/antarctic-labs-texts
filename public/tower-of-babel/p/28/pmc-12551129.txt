Understanding Serratia bockelmannii transmission during a neonatal intensive care unit outbreak: a combined genotyping and case–control study
Authors: Kaisa Jaakkola, Aurélie Fischer, Louise Piquart, Pierre Cassier, Olivier Dauwalder, Jean-Charles Picaud, Mélanie Colomb-Cotinat, Stéphane Haÿs, Jean-Philippe Rasigade, Cédric Dananché
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSerratia marcescens complex (SMC) is a group of opportunistic pathogens frequent in neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) outbreaks. We report a seven-month NICU outbreak of multidrug-resistant (MDR) SMC strain and assess the acquisition risk factors.MethodsBacterial isolates were sequenced using Illumina technology and profiled for antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Cases (culture positive for SMC) were compared with birth date and ward-matched controls (SMC negative) to identify clinical and environmental factors associated with SMC acquisition.ResultsA single strain of Serratia bockelmannii caused 42 cases from June 2022 to January 2023. All sequenced isolates (n = 49) carrued the same AMR genes, while the antibiogram (n = 32) assigned 15 strains as MDR, and 17 as non-MDR. The outbreak strain was recovered from ward surfaces and an analytical study revealed that beds used by several cases increased the acquisition odds by 3.27 (95%CI 1.71–8.77, p-value = 0.003) after adjusting for sex and length of stay.ConclusionsThe genomic typing was pivotal in confirming clonality across S. bockelmannii isolates with varied antibiograms, and identifying the bacterial species. Serratia bockelmannii should be considered during SMC outbreaks. Incubator-related within-ward transmission highlights the need for tailored risk assessments and disinfection protocols to prevent pathogen transmission and to improve infection prevention protocols.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13756-025-01632-4.Keywords: Serratia marcescens complex, Neonatal intensive care unit, Hospital outbreaks, Antimicrobial resistance, Cluster identification, Multidrug-resistant bacteria

Background

Serratia marcescens is an opportunistic pathogen found in water and soil [1]. It is also an important nosocomial pathogen responsible for hospital outbreaks, especially in wards with vulnerable patients. Recent phylogenetic studies have assigned several Serratias (S. marcescens, Serratia bockelmannii, Serratia ureilytica, and Serratia nematodiphila) as members of the Serratia marcescens complex (SMC) [2, 3]. Our outbreak pathogen, Serratia bockelmannii, was first described in 2020 as a bacterium isolated from fresh produce [4]. Its clinical significance remains largely unclear, although it has previously been reported in intensive care unit settings [5].

Within neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) outbreaks, S. marcescens is the second most commonly reported pathogen [6, 7]. SMC give rise to a wide range of clinical manifestations in newborns: from asymptomatic colonisation to sepsis, bloodstream infection, meningitis, urinary tract infections, respiratory tract infections, and surgical wound infections [1]. The main risk factors for infection are: low birth weight, use of antibiotics, length of hospitalisation, invasive manoeuvres, and the use of indwelling medical devices [1, 6, 8]. Different modes of transmission have been suggested during SMC outbreaks: direct contact [1, 6], airborne [1, 9], foodborne [10, 11], and environmental cross-contamination via beds [12–14] or other equipment [11]. Despite the number of published outbreaks with suspected environmental transmission, there is a lack of analytical studies on environmental SMC acquisition [14].

We investigate a seven-month S. bockelmannii outbreak in a NICU. Outbreak investigation was initiated on 3rd August 2022, after five SMC-positive samples were identified from five different patients during ward surveillance.

Methods

Outbreak setting

The outbreak occurred in the NICU of the university-affiliated Hospices Civils de Lyon (HCL), a 45-bed level III unit, organised into three 15-bed open-space wards for critical care (REA), intensive care (ICU), and neonatal care (NEO). Nurses typically care for two to three neonates per shift, depending on care intensity. Computers are located between every two patients and at central ward stations. In REA and ICU, patients change beds every 15–21 days for closed incubators and every 21–30 days for open-closed incubators or radiant warmers.

Collection of samples

Patients were screened weekly using stool samples for Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus cereus, Gram-negative bacilli in the dominant culture, and third-generation cephalosporin-resistant Enterobacterales including SMC. Respiratory and blood samples were taken when infection was suspected. Routine environmental sampling, according to the plan focusing on high-risk objects and common touch points within the ward is conducted quarterly. Additional sampling was conducted to identify reservoirs targeting beds, cocoons, mattresses, cleaning wipes, and soap. Wipes (Dominique Dutscher, Bernolsheim, France), contact plates (Merck Millipore, Burlington, USA), and rectal swabs (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, USA) were used, and samples were cultured to identify Enterobacteriaceae. Each week four milk samples were collected from two randomly selected patients. Samples from healthcare workers were not obtained due to restrictions placed by local regulations.

Microbiological methods

Isolates were identified using Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time of flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry (Bruker, Billerica, USA). The antibiogram results were interpreted as “resistant”, susceptible”, or “susceptible at increased exposure” according to the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) [15] using Vitek® (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Etoile, France). Carbapenemase production was assessed with the Resist-5® immunochromatographic test (Coris BioConcept, Gembloux, Belgium). The Rapidec Carba NP® test (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Etoile, France) was performed in cases where the Resist-5® result was inconclusive or when phenotypic resistance to carbapenems was suspected without detection of common carbapenemase families by Resist-5®. According to standardised criteria for Enterobacteriaceae, a multidrug-resistant (MDR) isolate was defined as one that was non-susceptible to ≥ one agent in > three antimicrobial categories [16]. Isolates that were susceptible, or susceptible at increased exposure, in three or fewer antimicrobial categories were considered non-MDR.

Case definitions

A case was defined as a patient, admitted to NICU between June 2022 and January 2023, with at least one clinical or surveillance sample SMC-positive. Infection was defined as SMC isolation in the presence of compatible symptoms, whereas colonisation was defined as asymptomatic detection in surveillance or clinical samples. The attack rate was defined as the proportion of ward patients fitting the case definition during the study period. Controls were defined as patients hospitalised ≥ seven days, with at least one SMC-negative surveillance sample, and SMC not detected in any clinical or surveillance samples during the study period or within the following three months.

Sequencing, annotation, and genomic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted using Maxwell® RSC Blood DNA Kit (Promega Corporation, Madison, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed using an Illumina NextSeq system (Illumina Inc., San Diego, USA; coverage > 15 ×). FastQC version 0.11.9 [17] and Trimmomatic version 0.39 [18] were used for quality assessment and trimming, and reads assembled with SPAdes version 3.15.5(option: isolate) [19]. Contigs were circularised with Circlator version 1.5.5. Quality metrics on the final assembly were computed with Quast version 5.2.0 [20]. Genomes were taxonomically classified using Sourmash version 4.6.1 [21] and Genome Taxonomy database [22] as part of the standard WGS pipeline. For this study, taxonomic identification was further confirmed using the Type Strain Genome Server (TYGS) [23]. Antimicrobial resistance genes were detected with AMRfinder + version 3.11.2 [24]. Genomes were annotated with Prokka version 1.14.6 [25]. Plasmids were identified using Mob-Recon of the Mob-Suite version 3.1.4 [26].

Finally, the isolates were typed using a published core genome multilocus sequence typing (cgMLST) scheme and ChewBBACA version 3.3.10 [27, 28], and genomes compared using orthologs and paralogs identified with Panaroo version 1.5.0 [29]. Isolates were considered part of the same clonal cluster if they differed by < 15 alleles. The maximum likelihood tree of the core genome was created with IQ-TREE [30] including the reference genomes of SMC as well as other selected genomes (Table S1). The isolates sequenced here have been deposited at European Nucleotide Archive (ENA). For visualisation, iTOL and Grapetree version 1.5.0 were used [31, 32].

Analytical epidemiological investigation

A retrospective case–control study was conducted to investigate the acquisition risk factors in the REA ward (accounting for 73.8% of all cases). Cases were matched with controls by date of birth (± seven days) and ward (REA); unmatched cases were excluded from the analysis. The primary case was excluded to avoid bias due to potentially different exposure routes.

The collection date of first SMC-positive sample was considered as the acquisition event. Acquisition risk factors were collected from medical records: gestational age, birth weight, administration of corticosteroids before delivery, premature rupture of membranes at delivery, parity, delivery mode (vaginal/caesarean), length of stay by ward, type of respiratory support (invasive mechanical ventilation via endotracheal intubation, continuous positive airway pressure [C-PAP], or respiratory mask), type of nutrition (parenteral/enteral/breast milk), administration of antimicrobial therapy, and use of central venous catheter (CVC), umbilical venous catheter (UVC), umbilical arterial catheter (UAC), and peripheral venous catheter (PVC). Administration of antimicrobial therapy was defined as the use of any systemic antimicrobial agents (e.g., antibiotics or antifungals) administered after birth and throughout the NICU stay. Environmental risk variables were derived from hospitalisation records, including bed placement and bed ID throughout the NICU stay. Most acquisition events occurred during the first three weeks of hospitalisation, and this period and the bed used were considered for controls. For cases, the bed used at the time of the first positive SMC culture was defined as the 'acquisition bed'. Acquisition risk per bed was calculated based on the number of acquisition events, with beds having ≥ two events classified as high-risk and those with 0–1 event as low-risk. Other variables included bed type, prior use by cases (Previously contaminated: Yes/No), the uses by cases per bed, and the mean number of cases hospitalised per ward per week.

Statistical analysis

Logistic regression analysis was performed to calculate odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) for risk factors associated with SMC acquisition. Factors with p-value < 0.2 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis. Variables with p-value < 0.05 were considered significant. Final variable selection was based on clinical and statistical significance [33]. To address multicollinearity, we applied Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression (LASSO) and compared the goodness-of-fit using Akaike information criterion (AIC) to select the final multivariate model [33]. The statistical analyses were performed using the R Studio (version 2.4.0; R Core Team 2021).

Results

Descriptive epidemiology

Overall, 42 cases were identified. The outbreak strain was first isolated from a routine surveillance sample taken on 3rd June 2022, and the last new case was sampled on 12th January 2023, marking a seven-month duration for the outbreak. The outbreak had two peaks (Fig. 1): the first with eight cases in 12 days (30th July to 11th August 2022) followed by 14 more over the next two months. A second peak of nine cases emerged in November 2022, after 29 days without cases.

The mean acquisition age was 17 days (range 2–54), with 56% male cases. Most cases occurred in REA (n = 31, 73.8%), followed by ICU (n = 10,23.8%) and NEO (n = 1,2.4%). Attack rates were 15.3% in REA and 5.1% in ICU, with no significant difference between sexes. Most cases were asymptomatic colonisations (n = 38, 90.5%) and four had infections (respiratory tract n = 4; bloodstream n = 2). Two infected neonates died, though deaths could not be solely attributed to SMC, due to comorbidities and other pathogens.

Environmental investigation

Routine environmental sampling in July (n = 46) and October (n = 49) resulted in one (2.2%) and five (10.2%) SMC-positive samples, respectively (Table 1). Positive samples were recovered from a syringe driver (n = 1), a closed incubator (n = 1), medical carts (n = 2), and a shared work surface (n = 1), all collected during routine work activities. Outbreak investigation sampling produced one positive sample from a sink in December. Sampling of milk (n = 85) produced one SMC-positive sample in January 2023.

To verify the disinfection effectiveness, five samples from one closed incubator were collected post-disinfection in October; all were SMC-negative, but positive for Bacillus cereus. Whole genome sequencing and AMR results

We sequenced 49 isolates from 28 patients and all seven environmental samples (Table 1). All isolates clustered together (< 15 allelic differences) (Fig. 2). Sourmash identified the isolates as Serratia marcescens but further phylogenetic analysis and TYGS identified the outbreak strain as S. bockelmannii (Fig. 3). Two SMC isolates previously isolated at HCL were included in the analysis and identified as Serratia marcescens/nevei (Fig. 2).

Antibiogram identified 15 of 32 tested outbreak isolates as MDR and 17 as non-MDR. All isolates were intrinsically resistant to ampicillin and amoxicillin–clavulanic acid. Additionally, all isolates were resistant to cephamycin cefoxitin and aminoglycoside tobramycin. MDR strains were also resistant to extended-spectrum cephalosporins (cefotaxime, ceftriaxone), extended-spectrum penicillins (piperacillin–tazobactam), aminoglycoside gentamicin, and monobactam aztreonam (Fig. 3). All isolates were susceptible for meropenem, ertapenem, ciprofloxacin, trimethoprim-sulphamethoxazole and ceftazidime–avibactam. Within their genomes, all isolates carried similar antimicrobial resistance genes, including aac’6 aminoglycoside 6’ N-acetyltransferase, blaSRT-1 beta-lactamase, and tet(41) tetracycline efflux transporter (Fig. 3).

Beds within NICU

The acquisition bed was recorded for 36 cases: 22 acquisition events occurred in closed incubators; 13 in open-closed incubators, and one in a radiant warmer bed. Beds linked with ≥ two acquisition events (n = 10, 20.4%) were classified as high-risk; eight of these closed incubators (Fig. 4a). Beds with one (n = 10, 20.4%) or zero (n = 29, 59.0%) acquisition events were considered to have low-risk. Closed incubators involved in acquisition events were more likely to be involved in several events compared to other beds (chi-square, p-value < 0.05). During the outbreak, beds had been used by 1.8 cases on average (0–5) (Fig. 4b).

Case–control study

Male sex, low gestational age, low birth weight, use of UAC, administration of corticosteroids before delivery, and staying longer at the REA ward increased the odds of SMC acquisition (Table 2). Several bed-related variables also increased the odds of SMC acquisition. While beds previously contaminated by cases were not significantly associated with SMC acquisition, prior acquisition events and repeated uses by cases—defined as a previous stay in the same bed by another case—were linked to increased odds of acquisition (Table 2). After adjusting for sex (aOR 19.4; 95%CI 2.44–392, p-value = 0.017), each additional day in the REA ward increased the odds of SMC acquisition by 1.15 (95%CI 1.07–1.28; p-value = 0.002) and each additional prior use of the same bed by another case increased the odds of acquisition by 3.27 (95%CI 1.71–8.77, p-value = 0.003).

Outbreak control measures

The weekly NICU surveillance enabled early outbreak detection. Infection control for colonised or infected neonates followed French national guidelines, applying contact precautions (hand hygiene, single-use gowns, and gloves when in contact with biological fluids or mucous membranes). Patient cohort with designated areas and staff began on 3rd August 2022. New measures included changing medical carts and mobile computers after each patient, reducing stocks of single-use materials in the carts, and disposing remaining items post-discharge. Staff training reinforced hand hygiene, endotracheal suctioning practices, bed cleaning, and environmental disinfection. Enhanced cleaning started in August 2022. Following the second outbreak peak and frequent B. cereus detection, airborne surface disinfection with hydrogen peroxide-based sporicidal agent was implemented from November 2022. Additional measures in December 2022 and January 2023 included replacing a contaminated sink and monthly bleach treatments of all sink drains.

Discussion

We describe a seven-month single strain outbreak within a NICU unit affecting 42 patients. All sequenced environmental and clinical isolates were clonal, differing by < 15 alleles, and identified as Serratia bockelmannii. To our understanding, this is the first fully described S. bockelmannii outbreak. Not much is known of this member of SMC, but S. bockelmannii has been previously reported in clinical samples [5, 34], and one isolate from a neonatal patient from Mexico has been publicly deposited [35].

Using databases available in early 2023, both MALDI-TOF and Sourmash initially identified the outbreak isolates as S. marcescens. However, phylogenetic analysis of the sequenced outbreak isolates alongside SMC reference genomes identified them as S. bockelmannii. Notably, using the databases available in 2025 (20.07.2025), both Sourmash and TYGS identify outbreak isolates as S. bockelmannii. Identification at a species level within the SMC group is difficult, and the nomenclature still developing [3, 34]. The pathogenic mechanisms and virulence factors within the SMC group are not well understood and there is a lack of classical typing annotation for SMC organisms. For instance, SMC are not differentiated by multilocus sequence typing (MLST) using the PubMLST database. Additionally, the antibiogram produced variable susceptibility profiles, and sequencing was needed to confirm clonality. The limited reliability of antibiograms for cluster identification of SMC isolates has been reported previously [36] but elucidating the underlying reasons for this phenomenon will require further investigation. Our results indicate that standard microbiological methods may fail to identify species or clonality, and that outbreaks caused by other members of the SMC may be misattributed to S. marcescens. Accurate species identification and clonal assessment are critical for effective outbreak management and prevention. To support this, bioinformatics pipelines should be regularly reviewed and, where needed, updated to ensure reliable distinction between species within the SMC group.

The first outbreak cases were isolated colonisations and went unnoticed. A cluster of five cases triggered an alert two months after the primary case. Weekly surveillance and environmental sampling allowed relatively early outbreak detection but raised questions about appropriate cluster thresholds and characteristics. The lack of clear criteria complicates decision and control efforts which reduces the effectiveness of surveillance. Defining alert thresholds and determining when to escalate infection control measures remain critical for effective outbreak management. Variability in baseline colonisation rates and under-detection from intermittent screening hinder the establishment of cluster thresholds. The frequent asymptomatic presentation of cases adds further complexity. Balancing early detection, cost-effective surveillance, and timely control measures demands multidisciplinary expertise and real-time integration of microbiological and epidemiological data—key to effective outbreak management.

Cases had lower gestational age, lower birth weight, and longer length of stay at the ward providing the most intensive care. Male sex was associated with higher odds of SMC acquisition. These findings are consistent with previous observations that the risk of S. marcescens is elevated in more premature, male patients requiring prolonged intensive care [6, 8]. The beds used by several cases and beds with more than two previous acquisition events increased the odds for acquisition, even when adjusted for aforementioned risk factors. Notably, the beds involved in multiple acquisition events were primarily closed incubators, typically used for the most premature and vulnerable patients. To disentangle the effects of patient vulnerability and care intensity from the risk of SMC acquisition, we included invasive procedures and the use of indwelling medical devices as variables. Their lack of statistical significance suggests that certain beds themselves may have contributed, possibly as transient reservoirs facilitating the transmission, to increased odds of cross-contamination. Use of incubators, rather than cots, has been previously associated with SMC acquisition [14], and in a recent study, the hand wipes used for humification removal were identified as a potential contamination source [37]. In our study, the clustering of acquisition in specific incubators suggests a systematic failure in disinfection procedures independent of the incubator design. This may reflect acquired or external factors, such as surface wear, the bed placement within the ward, or environmental context, although we found no evidence to confirm these. A failure in disinfection procedures remains the most plausible explanation, underscoring the need for tailored disinfection protocols based on incubator characteristics in future settings. In NICUs, colonised patients are known reservoirs of SMC [1, 8, 12]. We presume that cross-transmission between patients, facilitated by certain incubators, drove within-ward spread. The risk likely stems not from the beds themselves, but from associated staff practices, cleaning protocols, or environmental factors (airflow or shared equipment). Tracking bed use and location proved valuable during the outbreak investigation and should be integrated into routine surveillance for early cluster detection. Sharing best practices in identifying environmental reservoirs and infection control gaps is essential for continuous improvement.

Our study has several limitations. Limited availability of controls prevented matching by sex, resulting in a sex distribution imbalance between cases and controls. If sex influenced the risk of acquisition, confounding of observed associations is likely. To mitigate this, we adjusted for sex, but residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Environmental sampling recovered the outbreak strain but did not confirm actual reservoirs. Given the suspected environmental source, we included environmental variables in the case–control study. However, variables derived from outbreak data risk circular reasoning, possibly reflecting pre-existing associations rather than independent predictors, even in the absence of clear correlations (Fig. S1). To address this, we performed sensitivity analyses using alternative environmental variables, which yielded consistent results. The sample size limited multivariate analysis to avoid overfitting. Finally, staff and visitor screening were not performed, limiting assessment of hand hygiene and the role of personnel or families in SMC transmission. Although the human gut is not a typical S. marcescens reservoir [1, 8, 12], healthcare workers’ hands are considered key transmission vehicles [1, 6].

Conclusions

The genomic typing was pivotal in confirming clonality across isolates with varied antibiograms, and identifying the outbreak strains as S. bockelmannii. Serratia bockelmannii should be considered during SMC outbreaks, and further research on its clinical relevance is warranted.

Surveillance systems and routine environmental sampling enable rapid outbreak detection and prompt infection control. Our results highlight the need for tailored risk assessments and disinfection protocols specific to each equipment type and version to prevent pathogen transmission within the ward.
