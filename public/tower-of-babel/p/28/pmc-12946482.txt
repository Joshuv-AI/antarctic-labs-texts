The Burden of Acute Respiratory Infections on Emergency Departments: A Retrospective Study From a University Hospital in Central Italy
Authors: Nunzio Zotti, Francesca Di Serafino, Luca Carmisciano, Laura Baglietto, Michele Cristofano, Francesca Foltran, Ombretta Paolilli, Andrea Davide Porretta, Guglielmo Arzilli, Mauro Pistello, Caterina Rizzo

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAcute respiratory infections (ARIs) have a relevant impact on public health in terms of prevalence and costs associated with the diseases. While the COVID‐19 pandemic highlighted the need to adopt accurate surveillance systems to face new emergencies, the aim of our work is to describe ARIs other then COVID‐19 and their impact on healthcare facilities.MethodsThis retrospective study used an ecological approach to connect emergency department records and laboratory test results from a University Hospital in Italy. A time‐series analysis has been performed to assess the association between weekly pathogen‐specific positivity counts and ARI‐related ED admissions.ResultsDuring the study period, 33,101 ED presentations for ARI were registered, resulting in 7426 hospital admissions. ARI cases and viral diagnoses showed a seasonal peak during the late weeks of the year and early weeks of the following one. A reduction in ED admissions has been found in 2020, while the average weekly rate was 30.8% in 2020, as compared with 21.7% in 2017–2019. Analysis by age group showed a peak of accesses in the last weeks of 2021 for the < 1 and 1–4 years old.ConclusionsData on ARI‐related admissions highlight seasonal trends and age‐related vulnerabilities, providing evidence to guide public health policies and support health system preparedness to respond to future epidemic waves.Keywords: acute respiratory infection, emergency department, influenza, RSV

Background

Acute respiratory infections (ARIs) represent a significant global health concern [1], contributing to substantial morbidity and placing a considerable burden on healthcare systems.

In particular during the COVID‐19 pandemic, the need for robust and timely surveillance systems remained critical to monitor the circulation of respiratory pathogens, detect changes in epidemiological patterns, and support public health decision‐making.

ARIs are caused by various infectious agents that affect different levels of the respiratory tract, resulting in clinical conditions. Some common conditions caused by ARIs are influenza‐like illness, bronchitis, and pneumonia [2, 3, 4]. These kinds of infections are typically transmitted through respiratory droplets, leading to the circulation of pathogens, especially in populations with high density and in enclosed spaces [5].

The influenza virus is the primary etiological agent of ARIs. It is one of the main causes of death from infectious diseases in industrialized countries and is responsible for common colds [6]. Second, parainfluenza viruses are ubiquitous and highly common, causing bronchiolitis, bronchitis, and pneumonia, mostly in children. The seasonality of infections varies depending on the serotype [7].

The severity and frequency of acute respiratory viruses admissions to emergency departments (EDs) can vary based on factors like circulating viruses, demographics, and vaccination rates. Public health measures, such as vaccination and hygiene, can alleviate this burden [8]. Additionally, the human population faces a growing risk of infectious diseases due to emerging viruses influenced by climate change, globalization, urbanization near diverse animal habitats, and a rising number of immunocompromised individuals. Continuous surveillance, research, and public health efforts are crucial to monitor, prevent, and control these emerging infectious diseases [9].

The seasonal ARI burden—mostly given by influenza and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) [10, 11]—translates into a tangible impact on healthcare delivery, since there is a surge in patient volume with common clinical features: (1) Severe symptoms that require immediate evaluation and treatment (high fever, severe cough, shortness of breath, chest pain, or worsening respiratory distress); (2) Complications, as pneumonia, bronchitis, or exacerbation of pre‐existing respiratory conditions like asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD); (3) The above‐mentioned conditions are more frequently reported in high‐risk populations, such as young children, older adults, pregnant women, and individuals with weakened immune systems [12]. Hospitalizations for ARIs frequently require prolonged lengths of stay (LOS), especially in older adults and individuals with comorbidities. Median LOS ranges from 3 to 7 days, depending on age and comorbidities [13], while RSV‐associated hospitalizations in older adults often exceed 5 days [14].

Annually, ARIs represent a major global health burden, accounting for ~4% of global disability‐adjusted life‐years and causing 2.5 million deaths due to ARIs in 2019 [15]. The burden of ARIs in high‐income countries is considerable. In the United States, 12% of all deaths in 2020 were attributed to ARIs, making them the fourth leading cause of mortality across all age groups [16, 17].

Productivity loss represents the dominant component of the economic burden associated with ARIs. Evidence from both low‐ and high‐income regions consistently shows that indirect costs—particularly those linked to premature mortality and work absenteeism—far exceed direct medical expenditures, highlighting ARIs as a major driver of socioeconomic losses [18, 19].

By leveraging technological advancements and real‐time data analysis, syndromic surveillance systems can enhance public health agencies' ability to monitor and manage ARIs effectively. These systems offer several advantages, including early detection of outbreaks, timely response, and efficient use of electronic health data complements traditional methods, serving as an early warning tool for insights into ARIs' trends [20].

This study aims to examine which ARIs contribute most to ED presentations, to characterize their patterns, and to compare ARI patients with non‐ARI patients, with the goal of informing the development of a comprehensive surveillance framework. This involves not only gathering data on ED access for ARIs but also integrating this information with laboratory‐characterized ARI cases to create an integrated surveillance system designed to monitor and respond to ARIs effectively.

Methods

Study Setting and Design

This retrospective observational study was conducted at Pisa University Hospital (Azienda Ospedaliero‐Universitaria Pisana, AOUP), a tertiary‐care teaching facility in Pisa, Tuscany, Italy. It provides hospital services, ranging from basic to highly specialized care. It has ~1200 beds, mainly given by surgical settings, and performs over 27,000 surgical procedures annually, including both inpatient and day hospital surgeries.

The study period covered five calendar years, from January 1, 2017 through December 31, 2021. The design aimed to describe the epidemiologic trends of ARIs other than COVID‐19 and to evaluate their impact on healthcare service utilization during both the prepandemic and early pandemic periods.

Study Population

The study population included all patients presenting to the ED of the AOUP between 1 January 2017 and 31 December 2021 with a clinical suspect of ARI. Patients whose encounters were solely related to COVID‐19 were excluded.

The study population was operationally defined by ED encounters with discharge diagnoses corresponding to the ICD‐9‐CM codes listed in Table 1, which represent clinical conditions compatible with ARIs.

Data Sources and Workflow

This study uses two distinct data sources, which were first analyzed separately and then jointly using an ecological approach.

EDs—Emergency Department Electronic Health Records Source

We retrospectively extracted aggregated data on individuals who presented to the AOUP ED (hereinafter referred to as “ED admissions”), collected from January 2017 to December 2021 within the electronic health records of the ED of AOUP hospital in Pisa, Tuscany, Italy. These data were stratified by age group, sex, ICD‐9‐CM diagnosis, admission status, year, and iso week.

LDs—Laboratory Data Source

Data on respiratory samples virology obtained from January 2017 to December 2021 were extracted from the Hospital Laboratory data set (OpenLIS) [21]. Both PCR and antigenic tests were used to detect adenovirus, bocavirus, coronaviruses (HKU1, NL63, OC43, MERS‐CoV, and SARS‐CoV‐1), enteroviruses, influenza (A, A/H1, A/H3, and B), metapneumovirus, parainfluenza (1–4), RSV (A and B), and rhinovirus. Virology test results were stratified by results, age group, sex, year, and iso week.

–Data collection workflow: ED medical records and laboratory records were extracted independently and underwent separate data cleaning procedures, including removal of inconsistencies and harmonization of stratification variables. After preprocessing, data sets were aligned by age group, sex, year and iso week to generate the final analytical data set used for descriptive and regression analyses.

Data Cleaning and Preprocessing

Data from the EDs had seven age classes, namely: below 1 year old (yo), from 1 to 4 yo, from 5 to 14 yo, from 15 to 24 yo, from 25 to 64 yo, from 65 to 84 yo, and above 85 yo or above.

The LDs included the aggregated number of tests that the AOUP laboratory performed. To harmonize quantitative and semi‐quantitative tests with qualitative tests, the results were coded according to positive or negative tests performed, such as rhinopharyngeal swab, pharyngeal swab, sputum, blood culture, serum/plasma culture, and all other kinds of swab or culture. The decision to request a test is at the discretion of the medical staff, based on the clinical presentation. The number of positive results for each test was then extracted by age group, sex, year, and iso week. Laboratory data could include multiple tests for a single sample, and multiple samples for a single subject with possibly multiple time‐points per subject. Confirmed cases of ARIs were defined as the number of positive results.

COVID‐19 cases were deliberately excluded, as the exceptional magnitude and unique epidemiological behavior of SARS‐CoV‐2 during the study period would have obscured the trends of other ARIs under investigation.

The frequencies and counts from the two sources (EDs and LDs) relative to the same year and iso week and according to the demographic strata (age class and sex) were merged to obtain a single data set for ecological analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Characteristics of ED admissions and laboratory tests were summarized as counts and percentages. All analyses reported in this study are exploratory and were not preplanned.

In both data sources, univariate logistic regression models were used to estimate associations between patient or admission characteristics (sex, age, and initial emergency code included as independent variables) and being classified as a suspected ARI case (dependent variable for EDs), or being classified as a laboratory‐confirmed ARI case (dependent variables for LDs). Estimates from these models are reported as univariate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Then, we run an ecological analysis to assess the relationship between the counts of laboratory‐confirmed positive results for each pathogen and the count of ARI cases in the ED, linking the two sources by iso week.

Observed positive laboratory test results for each virus were first visually compared with the corresponding number of ED visits for ARIs occurring over the same period. Then, we fit a multivariable linear regression model to assess the relationship between the weekly counts of laboratory‐confirmed positive results for each pathogen (independent variables) and the weekly count of ARI cases in the ED (dependent variable). Q–Q plot of the linear model residuals and the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) were used to assess the appropriateness and validity of the linear modeling approach. Despite all the virus counts being considered as explanatory variables, terms with negative coefficients were discarded from the model [22]. This strategy was a consequence of considering biologically implausible the hypothesis that any ARI‐related positive test could have a protective effect against ARI‐related ED admission, regardless of statistical significance. The model contribution of each virus counts was assessed in terms of the explained proportion of the ARIs‐related ED admission variability expressed as adjusted R 2. Adjusted R 2 as compared to raw R 2 mitigate the bias related to R 2 inflation when more than one independent variable is used. The attribution of the ARI's rate to each virus was generated by multiplying each regression coefficient by the virus count within the corresponding time window.

R statistical software version 4.3.0 was used for all analyses, with a two‐sided α = 0.05 significance level, in accordance with SAMPL guidelines.

Sensitivity Analysis

For stratified the sensitivity analyses described in the statistical analysis section, we decided to gather iso week counts by month to avoid having any lab count of zero by strata and period. For age‐related sensitivity analyses, we further grouped age‐classes into macro age‐classes to both limit the presence ofreduce sparse data sparcicity and maintain interpretable results. Indeed, macro age‐classes used subgroups generated with the 65‐ and 65‐years cutoffs. This allowed to separate groups of subjects with different expected ARI‐associated risk factors, namely infants and preschoolar children (aged 0–4), scholar children and adults (aged 5–64 years), and older adults and elder (above 65 years of age).

The confounding effects of age, sex, laboratory activity, and seasonal patterns were explored in sensitivity analyses. As an attempt to account for the age confounding effects, we fitted the multivariable linear regression model splitting each time period in age macro‐classes (0–4 years, 5–64, above 65 years), estimated the presence of a significant interaction between the age class and type of virus, and then compared the virus count contribution in terms of the explained proportion of ARIs variability in each age strata. A similar approach (assessment of heterogeneity and then conditional estimates) was used to explore the confounding effects of sex. As an effort to explain the possible confounding effect of a nonconstant laboratory activity, we run again the regression model keeping the number of positive tests as dependent variable but using the total number of performed tests as an offset. As an attempt to account for seasonal variations, we modified the regression model with a single intercept into a model with a different intercept for each month (representing a variable monthly number of unexplained ARI‐related ED admissions). When comparing nested models, we based on the likelihood ratio test the decision of whether include or not include an additional predictor to the model (such as age or sex). Collinearity among predictors was assessed using the VIF, analyses were conducted excluding any variable with a VIF above 5 to minimize redundancy and ensure stable coefficient estimates.

Ethical Approval

This study was conducted using routinely collected healthcare data. Any individual‐level record linkage and subsequent anonymization were performed exclusively for internal data management purposes by authorized personnel at the AOUP, within the institutional governance framework, followed by full anonymization and data aggregations.

The research team accessed only anonymized and aggregated data, with no access to direct identifiers and no possibility of reidentification. According to EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR, Art. 9.2.j) and the Italian Data Protection Code (Art. 110, Legislative Decree No. 196/2003), studies based on anonymized secondary data for scientific research purposes do not require formal approval by an ethics committee.

Results

The majority were adults aged 25–64 years (45.7%), followed by older adults 65–84 years (24.1%), while infants, young children, and the oldest adults (≥ 85 years) represented smaller proportions. Most patients were classified as having postponable (Green, 42.2%) or minor (Blue, 23.9%) emergencies, whereas true emergencies were infrequent (Red, 2.0%). Patient counts were stable from 2017 to 2019, declined markedly in 2020, and partially rebounded in 2021, reflecting the impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic on ED utilization.

Cases Attribution

Temporal trends in virus diagnoses from respiratory samples were cross‐referenced with the number of ARI‐related ED visits over the same time frame, revealing a consistent pattern of infections peaking in the final weeks of the year and the early weeks of the subsequent year, coinciding with the cold season (Figure 1).

A modeling approach was employed to assess the relationship between ARI cases and laboratory virus counts. Q–Q plot for the linear model shows the standardized residuals lying close to a straight 45° reference line, suggesting no major violation of the normality assumption underlying linear modeling assumptions. The weekly RSV and Influenza counts had a strong, positive, and significant correlation with ARI cases in the ED (Table 5). Adenovirus, Bocavirus, Coronavirus, and Parainfluenza weekly counts displayed a biologically implausible protective effect (with an estimated OR below 1). This may partly reflect collinearity among covariates; however, a sensitivity analysis excluding variables with a VIF above 5 yielded similar estimates. Meanwhile, enterovirus/rhinovirus and metapneumovirus showed a nonsignificant positive association with clinical suspect of ARIs at ED. These models explained ~35% of the variability in ARIs counts. To refine the analysis, independent variables that were negatively associated with ARIs or displayed a nonsignificant association (p > 0.05) were excluded.

This led to the development of a simplified reduced model that retained only two viral counts (influenza and RSV), explaining ~29% of the variability in ARI counts—a decline of just 6% compared to using all predictors.

However, when focusing on the two most prominent viruses, RSV and Influenza, distinct demographic patterns emerged. Specifically, a slight peak in influenza positivity among females aged 1–4 years old was observed in 2017 (16.7%), and a more significant peak in RSV positivity was noted among females aged < 1 year old in the same year (80.0%).

To address the potential confounding effects of ARIs varying prevalence across age groups, we first adjusted estimates for ages, then we added an interaction term in the model between age classes and the virus counts, and finally we stratified the analysis by age group. Age alone explained about 14%, of which about 9% was already explained by the two viral counts. A significant statistical interaction was observed between age classes and both virus counts, suggesting that the association between lab test positivity and suspected ARI ED admission varied across ages. Within each age group strata, ~50% of the variability in ARI cases could be elucidated by virus counts. Specifically, RSV accounted for roughly 35% of the ARIs variability in the 0–5 years old group, and < 3% in other groups. Influenza, on the other hand, explained about 45% of the ARIs variability in age groups above 6 years old, and ~20% in the 0–5 years old group.

Sex alone explained < 1% of ARIs ED variability and offer no contribution when included in the model with the Influenza and RSV (likelihood ratio test p = 0.178).

Hospital Admission

After ED visits and lab tests, 3066 patients were admitted to inpatient units as ARI cases and the allocation in the destination ward is usually related to the clinical status of patients and disease pattern. In our findings, patients were often admitted to General Medicine ward (1522, 49.6%), Geriatrics (540, 17.6%), Pediatrics (473, 15.4%), Infectious and Tropical Diseases (239, 7.8%) and Pneumology (99, 3.2%) wards.

Discussion

The main observation of this study is the trend of ARI‐related ED admissions, with a peak of infections during the cold season, as consistently reported in temperate climates [23]. The temporal correlation between ARI cases and RSV and influenza lab positivity underscores the role of these pathogens in determining seasonal fluctuations in respiratory illness, as underlined by the WHO too [24].

The study also highlights variations in ARI hospitalizations and virus positivity among different age groups, revealing potential disparities: higher rates of ARI hospitalization were found in 25–64 years old and 65–84 ones, with significant spikes in viral infections such as influenza, may reflect differences in access to health care or preventive measures, such as vaccination rates.

Modeling results also emphasize the prevalence of viruses such as RSV and influenza in younger age groups. Influenza accounted for 45% of the variability of ARI in children older than 5 years, suggesting that vaccines could be effective strategies to reduce ARI‐related ED admissions. This underscores the importance of vaccination campaigns and public health strategies on mitigating the spread of respiratory viruses and reducing ARI‐related ED admissions.

These findings should also be interpreted in light of current vaccination policies in Italy. Influenza vaccination is recommended and offered free of charge to older adults, young children, pregnant women, and individuals with chronic conditions, while RSV prevention has recently expanded through maternal vaccination and nirsevimab prophylaxis for infants, as well as vaccination for adults aged ≥ 60 years.

The association within vaccine‐preventable infections and ED admission may highlight a need for improved vaccination coverage and early detection protocol among children and elderly. Infection rates underscore a significant burden of ARI in infants aged < 1 year. This age group showed the highest risk of ED admissions for ARI, indicating their vulnerability to respiratory infections, mostly RSV‐related, in line with evidences on the burden of RSV in infants [25].

The prevalence of “green code” during triage in ED, indicating non‐life‐threatening conditions, may suggest overuse of emergency services and pressure on allocated resources. The distribution of ARI cases among different medical units after ED visits shows that patients were mainly assigned to General Medicine and Geriatrics departments, but a notable allocation of ARI cases to Pediatrics units underscores the burden of respiratory infections in children. In any case, children are more likely to be tested than adults, so it may lead to overrepresentation of pediatric cases in the laboratory‐confirmed data set.

Our results provide significant insights into the burden of ARIs within both primary pediatric care and elderly care in Italy, consistent with the international literature [26, 27, 28].

To contain the burden on ED, many of these nonsevere cases could be managed in primary care settings or outpatient services. Strengthening community care, improving patient education, and increasing the cooperation with General Practitioner or local healthcare facilities could help divert minor codes from the ED, optimizing resource allocation and improving patient flow.

Although this study contributes significantly to the understanding of the epidemiology of ARIs and their relationship with respiratory viruses, several limitations must be acknowledged. The study is retrospective in nature and based on existing cumulative data, and potential underreporting or incomplete reporting cannot be excluded. In addition, the year 2020 was characterized by substantial changes in healthcare organization, diagnostic practices, and ED utilization related to the COVID‐19 pandemic; although SARS‐CoV‐2 cases were excluded through specific coding, indirect effects such as diagnostic shifts and altered patient flows may have influenced ARI‐related ED presentations. Furthermore, there remains a critical need for future research to advance understanding and improve disease surveillance.

Conclusions

In summary, this study reveals important trends in ED admissions for ARI, revealing the role of RSV and influenza as key factors in seasonal respiratory infections. Age‐specific vulnerability underlines the importance of tailored interventions for different demographic groups. However, further research is needed to explore contributing factors, identifying seasonality and optimizing bed occupancy levels.

These findings have implications for public health strategies, emphasizing the importance of targeted interventions for better patient management. Strategies should prioritize vaccination campaigns, particularly for RSV and influenza, along with improved patient care protocols to relieve pressure on healthcare facilities and reduce the prevalence and severity of ARI.

In conclusion, while the current study has provided important insights into ARIs and the association with respiratory viruses, the advancement of syndromic surveillance systems represents a promising avenue for future research. The integration of real‐time data, advanced analytics and predictive modeling can improve our ability to monitor, predict and respond to ARI outbreaks, ultimately contributing to more effective public health strategies and better management of respiratory diseases.
