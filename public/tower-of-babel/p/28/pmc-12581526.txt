Effects of a multicomponent sleep enhancement protocol on delirium incidence in elderly critically ill surgical patients: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Nuanprae Kitisin, Suchanun Lao-amornphunkul, Nattachai Hemtanon, Napat Thikom, Nawarat Phochan, Chayanan Thanakiattiwibun, Onuma Chaiwat, Karuna Wongtangman, Paranee Trachuthamcharoen, Annop Piriyapatsom, Laurence Weinberg, Ary Serpa Neto, Nattaya Raykateeraroj
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep disturbances are common in critically ill patients and increase delirium risk, leading to worse outcomes. Non-pharmacologic sleep interventions show mixed results, with limited randomized controlled trial (RCT) data in surgical ICU populations.MethodsWe conducted a prospective, single-blind randomized controlled trial in a surgical ICU to evaluate a multicomponent sleep enhancement protocol vs. usual care in patients aged ≥ 65 years with expected ICU stays > 24 h. The intervention included environmental modifications (noise and light reduction, closed doors, alarm adjustments) and patient-specific measures (earplugs, eye masks). Delirium incidence over 7 days was assessed using the Thai version of CAM–ICU by blinded evaluators. Sleep quality was measured daily with the Richards–Campbell Sleep Questionnaire (RCSQ). Bayesian methods estimated treatment effects and posterior probabilities of benefit.ResultsOf 177 patients analyzed (89 intervention, 88 control), delirium occurred in 10.1% of the intervention group vs. 17.0% in controls (Bayesian OR 0.55; 95% credible interval [CrI] 0.22–1.31; posterior probability of benefit 90.6%). In a predefined high-risk subgroup, delirium incidence was 14.0% vs. 30.0% (OR 0.38; 95% CrI 0.12–1.83; posterior probability of benefit 95.7%). Sleep quality scores and clinical outcomes did not differ significantly. Adherence was high for environmental modifications but moderate for earplugs and eye masks.ConclusionsAmong elderly surgical ICU patients, a multicomponent, non-pharmacologic sleep enhancement protocol was associated with a high posterior probability of reducing delirium during the early ICU period, despite no measurable improvement in subjective sleep quality. Background

Critically ill patients frequently experience sleep disturbances, at rates of 68.0–73.9% [1–3]. These disturbances are characterized by altered sleep physiology, including decreased deep sleep, frequent awakenings, and fragmented sleep architecture [3, 4]. Poor sleep quality in patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) has been associated with numerous adverse outcomes, such as impaired immune function, prolonged recovery, and increased risk of delirium [5, 6], a condition that contributes to higher morbidity, prolonged ICU stays, and increased healthcare costs [5, 7]. Moreover, sleep disturbances can have long-term consequences on cognitive function and overall quality of life post-hospitalization [6].

These disturbances are multifactorial, and environmental factors (e.g., noise, light, and nighttime care activities) and internal factors (e.g., pain, anxiety, and physiological stress) are significant contributors [6, 8–10]. Given the strong association between sleep deprivation and delirium, improving sleep quality in ICU patients has become a critical focus of ICU care.

Pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic strategies have been explored. Melatonin is recommended by the Pain, Agitation/Sedation, Delirium, Immobility, and Sleep Disruption guidelines [11], whereas non-pharmacologic interventions, such as earplugs and eye masks, have shown improvements in sleep quality [12–15]. However, only one study has demonstrated a decrease in delirium severity [12], and most evaluations of bundled sleep protocols have used pre/post designs and yielded mixed results [6, 16–19]. Several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have evaluated ICU sleep interventions. For example, Guo et al. [20] demonstrated a significant decrease in delirium with a sleep protocol in ICU patients with oral cancer, while Zhang et al. [21] observed improved sleep quality without statistical significance in a non-randomized study. Recently, a network meta-analysis by Li et al. [22] reported that non-pharmacologic interventions such as earplugs, eye masks, and multicomponent protocols can improve perceived sleep quality and may reduce delirium incidence in postoperative ICU patients. Therefore, robust RCT evidence regarding sleep bundles in broader surgical ICU populations remains limited.

Evidence on ICU sleep protocols is limited, particularly in elderly surgical patients. This study evaluated whether a multicomponent sleep enhancement protocol could reduce delirium in critically ill elderly patients, aiming to guide ICU sleep practices and improve patient outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

This prospective, single-blind randomized controlled trial was conducted in a 14-bed surgical ICU at a university-affiliated national tertiary referral center from December 2022 to December 2024. Recruitment commenced after institutional review board approval (Si 445/2022) and trial registration (TCTR20221129003). The trial adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and CONSORT 2025 guidelines [23] (see Supplementary Appendix I). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or legal guardians.

The ICU comprises two units with seven windowless single-patient rooms each, separated by curtained doorways, with central nurse stations for continuous observation and a 1:1 nurse-to-patient ratio. Postoperative care (excluding cardiothoracic, neurosurgical, and trauma cases) is provided by critical care anesthesiologists and ICU nurses. A schematic ICU layout is shown in Supplementary Appendix II.

Participants

After ICU admission, patient eligibility was assessed, and informed consent was obtained. Enrollment was conducted on the first day of ICU admission. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 65 years; (2) ICU admission within 7 days after surgery; (3) anticipated ICU stay > 24 h; (4) ability to communicate in the local language; and (5) hemodynamic stability, defined as a norepinephrine-equivalent vasopressor dose ≤ 0.1 µg/kg/min, mean arterial pressure ≥ 65 mmHg, heart rate of 50–120 bpm, and peripheral oxygen saturation ≥ 96%.

The following exclusion criteria were considered: (1) pre-existing cognitive dysfunction; (2) severe visual or auditory impairment; (3) administration of continuous muscle relaxant infusion; (4) delirium at ICU admission before enrollment; (5) immobilization or paralysis requiring full mobilization assistance; and (6) a Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale score of − 3 to − 5.

Randomization and blinding

Consecutively eligible patients were enrolled according to a pre-specified screening protocol. After written informed consent and baseline assessments, patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the sleep enhancement or control group using a computer-generated randomization table with a fixed block size of two. The randomization sequence was generated and maintained by one of the two designated investigators (NK or NR) who were not involved in patient recruitment, consent, or outcome assessment. Consent and enrollment were performed by a separate team of four research staff (SL, NP, NT, PT) who were blinded to prior allocations and had no access to the randomization list. Outcome assessments were conducted by bedside ICU nurses or, at times, by a research nurse who was not aware of group allocation, ensuring separation of roles and minimizing the potential for allocation bias.

This was a single-blind trial: outcome assessors were masked to group allocation, whereas patients and treating clinicians were not. To preserve blinding, outcome assessments were scheduled outside the nighttime intervention window (22:00–05:00). Before each assessment, research staff removed earplugs and eye masks from intervention patients and standardized the environment across groups (lights on; routine monitor brightness/volume; doors open).

Intervention

The intervention began on the first ICU night for patients admitted before 08:00; otherwise, it started the following night. The protocol was applied nightly for up to 7 days or until ICU discharge. Earplugs and eye masks were discontinued if the patient developed delirium but were permitted to be kept for personal use after ICU discharge. The control group received standard ICU care without any structured sleep interventions.

Nocturnal vital sign monitoring was standardized across both groups. Non-invasive blood pressure was automatically recorded every hour, and heart rate, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation were continuously monitored on a central system. Temperature was measured every 4 h at 02:00, 06:00, 10:00, 14:00, 18:00, and 22:00, in accordance with ICU protocol. The nighttime period in the study was defined as 22:00 (lights out) to 05:00 (lights on).

In the intervention group, non-essential nighttime procedures, such as routine suctioning or scheduled repositioning, were avoided during this window to preserve sleep continuity, with clinically indicated interventions performed as needed. Nurses documented any unscheduled nighttime interventions (e.g., alarm response, catheter care, and unplanned repositioning). In contrast, the control group received routine ICU care, which included scheduled repositioning and endotracheal suctioning every 2 h overnight, as per standard practice.

Patients were withdrawn from the study if they developed any of the following conditions: (1) hemodynamic instability, defined as requiring vasopressor support exceeding a norepinephrine-equivalent dose of 0.1 µg/kg/min; (2) continuous infusion of a neuromuscular blocking agent; or (3) deep sedation, defined as a Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale score of − 3 to − 5.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was delirium incidence during the first 7 days of ICU admission, assessed twice daily at 08:00 and 18:00 using the validated Thai version of the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM–ICU), which has demonstrated high validity (sensitivity 92%, specificity 95%) and excellent inter-rater reliability (κ = 0.92) in critically ill patients [24]. Assessments were performed by specialist ICU nurses or a research nurse, masked to allocation (see Randomization and blinding), who had completed formal training in the Thai version of the CAM–ICU (didactic instruction, supervised bedside practice with educator sign-off, and periodic competency assessments), as well as standardized training in the Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale (RASS). Monitoring began on the first day after ICU admission and continued until day seven or ICU discharge, whichever occurred first.

The secondary outcomes encompassed multiple domains. Sleep quality was assessed daily at 08:00 using the Thai-validated Richards–Campbell Sleep Questionnaire (RCSQ) [25], following each intervention night. The Thai version of the RCSQ comprises five visual analog scale items (0–10 cm), averaged into a total sleep score, with higher values indicating better perceived sleep. RCSQ assessments were omitted on days with delirium. The original RCSQ [26] is provided in Supplementary Appendix III.

Other secondary outcomes included delirium characteristics, such as onset, duration, and subtype. Delirium duration was defined as the number of days with a positive CAM–ICU assessment, and ICU delirium-free days as the number of days without delirium during ICU stay. Subtypes were classified by RASS score: − 3 to 0 as hypoactive, 1 to 4 as hyperactive, and both patterns at different times during each evaluation as mixed delirium. Additional outcomes included antipsychotic use (median ICU dose), self-removal of catheters or endotracheal tubes, days on mechanical ventilation, ICU/hospital lengths of stay, and mortality.

Environmental and adherence metrics were recorded throughout the study. ICU nurses monitored protocol adherence daily using a standardized checklist. Adherence to each protocol component was recorded nightly by bedside nurses. For patient-worn devices (earplugs, eye masks), adherence was coded “yes” if the device was worn for > 50% of the night and “no” otherwise. This > 50% threshold was pre-specified a priori for pragmatic reasons (devices were often temporarily removed for comfort or clinical procedures) [27]. For environmental elements (e.g., lights off, patient-room door closed, reduced alarm volume, and avoidance of non-essential procedures), compliance was coded “yes” when delivered as per protocol and “no” when not. Nighttime noise (22:00–05:00) was measured using a smartphone-based sound meter application placed on the medical pendant near the patient’s head.

Data collection

Baseline characteristics included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists classification, comorbidities (e.g., psychiatric disorders and alcohol consumption), and prior medication use (sleep, analgesics, and psychiatric medications). Inadequate pain control was defined as any ICU assessment with a Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) score ≥ 4 or a Critical Care Pain Observation Tool (CPOT) score ≥ 3.

Cognitive function was assessed using the Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE), a validated screening tool for dementia [28]. Additional data collected included surgical type, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II score, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, presence of shock, use of mechanical ventilation, duration of ventilator support, laboratory values, and medications administered at ICU admission.

Statistical analyses

The full statistical analysis plan is provided in Supplementary Appendix IV. Sample size was calculated using a two-sample comparison of independent proportions, based on data from a previous observational study [17] that reported a reduction in delirium incidence from 33% in the control group to 14% in the intervention group. Assuming a two-sided α of 0.05 and 80% power (β = 0.20), the required sample size was estimated to be 77 patients per group. To account for an anticipated 20% attrition rate, the final target enrollment was increased to 92 patients per group, resulting in a total sample size of 184 patients.

Recruitment concluded at 181 participants at the protocol-specified calendar end date (December 2024). Attrition was 4/181 (2.2%), well below the 20% anticipated, and complete outcome data were available for all non-withdrawn participants, yielding a modified intention-to-treat (mITT) cohort of 177 (89 intervention, 88 control). No interim efficacy or futility analyses were planned or performed. The analyzed sample exceeded the pre-specified minimum of 77 per group.

The primary analysis was conducted on a mITT basis, including all randomized patients except those withdrawn prior to intervention delivery due to predefined clinical instability (e.g., deep sedation or hemodynamic compromise).

All statistical analyses were conducted using a Bayesian framework [29, 30] to estimate the probability of benefit associated with the sleep enhancement intervention. This approach allows for direct probabilistic interpretation of treatment effects and provides an intuitive alternative to traditional null hypothesis testing. All models were implemented using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods via the brms package [31] in R version 4.3.2. There were no losses to follow-up for the primary outcome, and a complete-case analysis was conducted for all outcomes.

Binary outcomes (e.g., presence or absence of delirium) were analyzed using Bayesian logistic regression with weakly informative priors, estimated considering a normal distribution centered at zero with a standard deviation of 2.5 on the log-odds scale. Continuous outcomes were analyzed using Gaussian, asymmetric Laplace (for quantile regression), or Gamma distributions with log links, depending on the distributional characteristics of the data.

Model parameters were summarized using posterior median estimates, 95% credible intervals (CrI), and the posterior probability of benefit. This was defined as P(OR < 1) for binary outcomes, i.e., the posterior probability that the odds ratio is less than one, indicating a beneficial effect of the intervention, and as P(MD > 0) or P(MD < 0) for continuous outcomes, depending on the clinical interpretation of benefit. Specifically, P(MD > 0) was used when higher median difference (MD) values indicated improvement (e.g., RCSQ scores), while P(MD < 0) was used when lower MD values were favorable (e.g., delirium duration, noise level, ICU and hospital length of stay). Posterior probabilities were interpreted in context, and given the exploratory nature, early phase, and limited sample size of this study, a posterior probability of ≥ 90% in favor of treatment benefit was interpreted as suggestive evidence. This threshold is consistent with conventions used in early phase Bayesian trials and is pre-specified to support transparent interpretation of findings [32].

Weakly informative priors were applied, typically Normal (0, 2.5), reflecting prior uncertainty and allowing the data to dominate the posterior. For quantile models, the prior was broadened to Normal (0, 5) to accommodate greater variability in location-specific estimates. Model diagnostics, including trace plots and R-hat statistics, were used to assess convergence.

A predefined subgroup analysis was performed in patients at high risk for postoperative delirium, identified by a validated delirium predictive score ≥ 125 developed by Chaiwat et al. [33]. This score includes age, SOFA score, perioperative benzodiazepine use, diabetes mellitus, mechanical ventilation, and cognitive decline (modified IQCODE > 3.42). The subgroup analysis aimed to explore potential differential treatment effects in this high-risk group.

As a post hoc sensitivity analysis, we performed a per-protocol analysis including intervention patients with ≥ 80% adherence and all controls to assess the robustness of the primary findings. We also conducted an exploratory logistic regression with an interaction term for treatment and baseline ventilation status. Both analyses were considered hypothesis-generating.

Results

Between December 2022 and December 2024, 1,696 patients were screened, and 181 were randomized to the sleep enhancement (n = 91) or control group (n = 90). Adherence to the sleep enhancement protocol

Patients in the intervention group received the sleep enhancement protocol for a median of 2 nights (IQR 1–3; range 1–7). Adherence was high for environmental modifications (~ 100%, e.g., clock provision, daytime wakefulness, and light/alarm control). Patient-specific components had lower adherence: earplugs use (64.4%) and eye masks use (67.4%), and limitation of non-essential nighttime procedures (57.1%). Overall adherence was 87.0% (Appendix V: Supplementary Fig. 1).

Delirium incidence and characteristics

Delirium occurred in 10.1% of the intervention group and 17.1% of the control group, with a Bayesian odds ratio of 0.55 (95% CrI 0.22–1.31), and a posterior probability of benefit of 90.6% (Table 2; Fig. 3). Delirium onset, duration, and delirium-free days were similar between groups, with posterior probabilities of benefit ranging from 56.4% to 71.4% (Table 2). Delirium subtype distribution (hyperactive, hypoactive, mixed), antipsychotic use (quetiapine, haloperidol), and self-removal of catheters or endotracheal tubes were also comparable. Sleep quality and noise exposure

Overall sleep quality (median total RCSQ score: 5.8 vs. 5.8 cm) and individual sleep dimensions were similar between groups (Table 2). Bayesian estimates indicated low to modest posterior probabilities of benefit for all sleep-related outcomes (26.1%‒48.3%). RCSQ score distributions are shown in Appendix V: Supplementary Fig. 2. Nighttime noise levels were comparable (median 45.5 dB vs. 46.4 dB), with a Bayesian median difference of -0.27 dB (95% CrI -2.03 to 1.45).

ICU and hospital outcomes

ICU and hospital lengths of stay were similar between groups. ICU mortality was 0% in both groups, and hospital mortality was 6.7% in the intervention group vs. 3.4% in the control group.

Exploratory analysis: interaction with baseline ventilation

The posterior probability of benefit was 47% in non-ventilated patients and 87% in mechanically ventilated patients. Subgroup estimates were OR 1.08 (95% CrI 0.09–12.87) for non-ventilated patients and OR 0.59 (95% CrI 0.22–1.46) for ventilated patients. The interaction term was OR 0.55 (95% CrI 0.04–6.64) (Appendix V: Supplementary Fig. 3).

Subgroup analysis: patients with high risk of postoperative delirium

Among 83 high-risk patients, according to the delirium prediction score, delirium incidence was lower in the intervention group (14.0%) than the control group (30.0%) with Bayesian odds ratio of 0.38 (95% CrI 0.12–1.83) and a 95.7% posterior probability of benefit. (Appendix V: Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 4). Delirium characteristics, subtypes, ICU delirium-free days, treatment variables, and ICU/hospital outcomes were similar between groups. RCSQ score distributions are shown in Appendix V: Supplementary Fig. 5.

Sensitivity analysis: per-protocol population

In the exploratory per-protocol analysis—restricted to 35 intervention patients with ≥ 80% adherence to each key protocol component and all 88 control patients—baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were similar to those in the modified intention-to-treat population (Appendix V: Supplementary Table 3). Delirium incidence was 5.7% in the intervention group vs. 17.1% in the control group (Bayesian OR 0.29; 95% CrI 0.06–1.12), corresponding to a 96.3% posterior probability of benefit. Secondary outcomes showed similar directional effects to the modified intention-to-treat analysis but with wider credible intervals (Appendix V: Supplementary Tables 4 and Supplementary Fig. 6).

Discussion

Key finding

This randomized controlled trial evaluated a multicomponent, non-pharmacologic sleep enhancement protocol for delirium prevention in elderly, critically ill surgical patients. Compared with usual care, the intervention was associated with a lower incidence of delirium, with a Bayesian posterior probability of benefit exceeding 90%. The effect appeared more pronounced in the predefined high-risk subgroup. Exploratory analyses suggested that the potential benefit may be greater among mechanically ventilated patients and in the per-protocol population with high adherence, although wide credible intervals limit firm conclusions. Differences in subjective sleep quality, nighttime noise levels, and other clinical outcomes were not significant.

Context within the existing literature

Previous ICU trials of sleep interventions have shown variable results. Several studies demonstrated delirium reduction, such as Guo et al. [20], who reported a decrease from 31% to 15% in oral cancer ICU patients, and Patel et al. [17], who observed substantial reductions in a mixed ICU cohort using a pre/post design. Kamdar et al. [16] combined bundled protocols with pharmacologic measures and achieved similar benefits. Van Rompaey et al. [34] found that nighttime earplug use reduced ICU-related confusion (HR 0.47, 95% CI 0.27–0.82). Akpinar et al. [12] demonstrated improved sleep quality and decreased delirium severity with earplugs and eye masks in coronary ICU patients. Conversely, Darby et al. [19], Gorecki et al. [18], and Obanor et al. [35] did not show delirium reduction despite improved perceived sleep in some cases. These differences likely reflect variability in patient populations, baseline delirium risk, intervention components, and adherence.

Advanced age is a principal risk factor for both delirium and sleep disturbance in ICU patients, largely due to diminished cognitive reserve, higher comorbidity burden, and increased vulnerability to circadian rhythm disruption [36, 37]. In this study, these factors likely amplified the risk of adverse neurocognitive outcomes, making the elderly cohort especially susceptible to the harmful effects of ICU-related sleep fragmentation [5, 37]. The observed benefit of the sleep enhancement protocol—particularly in high-risk patients—supports targeted prevention in this vulnerable group, where even modest delirium reduction can yield meaningful gains in function and quality of life [6, 36].

The intervention combined environmental modifications (noise and light reduction, clustering of care, alarm and monitor adjustments) with patient-specific measures (earplugs, eye masks) to support circadian alignment and minimize sensory overload [38]. Consistent day–night cues and reduced nocturnal disturbances help maintain orientation, modulate sympathetic activity, and promote restorative slow-wave sleep—factors known to protect against delirium [39, 40]. It is also possible that daytime engagement activities may have contributed to reduced delirium without affecting sleep quality. Similar to findings from Demoule et al. [41], earplugs and eye masks may reduce prolonged awakenings and increase N3 sleep as measured by polysomnography, even though such improvements may not be captured by subjective sleep assessments. This may explain why the lack of between-group differences in RCSQ scores does not necessarily preclude a physiologic benefit, particularly given that the RCSQ correlates only moderately with polysomnography (r = 0.58) [26].

Several factors likely attenuated the intervention effect. First, the overall delirium incidence in our cohort (13.6%) was lower than that reported in many previous ICU studies (typically 20–80%) [5, 42] and in our own historical data, 38% in elderly [43] and 24% across all ages [33]. This likely reflects the enrollment of a lower risk population, excluding hemodynamically unstable patients and those with pre-existing cognitive impairment, as well as the universal implementation of non-pharmacologic delirium prevention strategies in both study groups [43], which reduced baseline risk and limited the potential for further improvement. Second, adherence to earplugs/eye masks was moderate (~ 65–67%) with frequent discontinuation due to discomfort [27]; median nighttime noise was similar between groups (45.5 vs. 46.4 dB) and, although lower than reports from other ICUs [10, 44, 45], still exceeded the WHO nighttime recommendation of 40 dB [46]. The open layout of single-patient rooms adjacent to central monitoring stations, combined with this relatively low baseline, likely constrained the ability to detect between-group differences. Delaney et al. [10] also reported high variability in ICU noise, with staff conversations identified as the dominant source, underscoring the difficulty of eliminating environmental noise. Third, contamination is plausible, with staff awareness improving sleep-promoting practices in controls. Finally, the short median ICU length of stay (2 days) curtailed exposure to the intervention [21]. Together, these factors likely limited improvements in subjective sleep quality and are consistent with the modest delirium signal observed.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this trial include its randomized design, focus on an elderly surgical ICU cohort with a high prevalence of mechanical ventilation, rigorous blinded delirium assessments, high adherence to environmental modifications, and pre-specified Bayesian methods for direct probability estimation [29, 30]. This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single center, and our inclusion criteria excluded hemodynamically unstable patients (including those requiring relatively high vasopressor doses), cognitively impaired patients, and those with pre-existing delirium at baseline, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Second, exposure to the intervention was brief (median ICU stay, 2 days), likely attenuating its effect and restricting follow-up to the early ICU period. Third, sleep quality was assessed using a subjective tool (RCSQ), and evaluations were omitted on days with delirium. Fourth, environmental and implementation constraints may have limited mechanistic fidelity. Specifically, the centrally illuminated nursing station constrained light control; nocturnal interruption frequency was not systematically recorded—limiting our ability to link the intervention to reduced nighttime disturbances; and potential contamination cannot be excluded, which would likely bias results toward the null. Fifth, although enrolling staff were blinded to prior allocations and not informed of the block size, the use of a fixed block size of two could still theoretically permit allocation prediction under certain circumstances. Finally, because the intervention was delivered as a bundle, we could not isolate the effects of individual components (environmental modifications vs. earplugs/eye masks), and adherence was not randomized—so observed differences may reflect patient tolerance or clinical factors rather than true efficacy. In addition, adherence was defined pragmatically as use for > 50% of the night; as no validated per-night exposure threshold exists in ICU populations [12, 41, 47], this cutoff may have been insufficient to influence delirium, and future studies should capture continuous device wear time to enable dose–response analyses.

Future research directions

Future work should confirm these findings in larger, multicenter studies with longer follow-up, incorporate objective sleep measures, such as actigraphy or polysomnography [48], and develop strategies to improve comfort and adherence to earplugs and eye masks. Studies in higher risk or longer stay ICU populations may better elucidate the true impact of sleep enhancement protocols on delirium prevention. In addition, factorial or dismantling designs are needed to identify which components (e.g., environmental modifications vs. earplugs/eye masks) are most effective and feasible for clinical implementation.

Conclusions

Among elderly surgical ICU patients, a multicomponent, non-pharmacologic sleep enhancement protocol was associated with a high posterior probability of reducing delirium during the early ICU period, despite no measurable improvement in subjective sleep quality. Although the intervention did not improve other clinical outcomes, and the single-center design with short ICU stays limits generalizability, these findings are hypothesis-generating. Given its simplicity and low risk, the protocol may be a feasible adjunct to delirium prevention, warranting confirmation in larger multicenter trials with objective sleep measures and optimized adherence.
