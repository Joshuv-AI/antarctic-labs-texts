Autonomic regulation during cognitive reappraisal in major depressive disorder: a study of fMRI correlates
Authors: Ying Li, Micha Keller, Jana Zweerings, Chi Zhang, Fengyu Cong, Klaus Mathiak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmotion regulation mediated by cognitive reappraisal can induce activity changes in brain areas and fluctuations in peripheral physiological indicators regulated by the autonomic nervous system (ANS). However, little is known about the relationship between emotional and ANS regulation in major depressive disorder (MDD). In specific, the intermediate band (IM; 0.12–0.18 Hz) is thought to reflect relaxation and emotion processing.MethodsIn a real-time fMRI neurofeedback-guided cognitive reappraisal experiment in MDD patients, we utilized a general linear model to obtain brain activations of reappraisal, heart rate variability (HRV) IM band, and respiratory IM band. The emotion regulation-related brain regions involved in cognitive reappraisal were defined as regions of interest (ROIs). Statistical tests were performed on the average time course of voxels within these ROIs in the brain activations of HRV and respiratory IM band. This enabled the establishment of the coupling between brain regions involved in emotion regulation and peripheral physiological indicators.ResultsEmotion regulation and fluctuations in the IM band of HRV and respiration during cognitive reappraisal predicted brain activity, both for MDD patients and healthy controls. However, there were significant differences between patients and healthy controls in the coupling between ANS activity and neural activity during emotion regulation. In particular, the coupling between the IM band and prefrontal regions changed over training sessions only in MDD patients.ConclusionThis study established links between peripheral physiological indicators in the IM band and neural activity that deepen understanding of ANS regulation in MDD patients. The identified altered ANS coupling and its relation to cognitive reappraisal emphasize the close association between the IM band and emotion regulation, which may aid in assessing psychological states in patients with MDD.Keywords: Emotion regulation, Cognitive reappraisal, MDD, ANS, IM band, FMRI

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a common mental illness worldwide [1]. A review of nearly 20 years of research suggests that the lifetime prevalence of MDD ranges from 2 to 21%, with higher rates in some European countries [2]. A meta-analysis has shown that individuals with depression face a higher risk of all-cause mortality compared to the general population or those with other psychiatric disorders [3]. Moreover, the severity of depressive symptoms is positively correlated with the risk of all-cause mortality [4]. Altered emotional processing is a core feature of MDD, characterized by enhanced processing of negative emotions and weakened processing of positive emotions [5, 6]. Notably, depression leads to dysregulation of the autonomic nervous system (ANS), which is associate with increased mortality rates [7–9]. Therefore, investigating the neural correlates of ANS regulation during emotional processing in MDD is critical.

The ANS plays a central role in the regulation of physiological states. By balancing sympathetic and parasympathetic activity, the ANS helps individuals adapt to environmental stimuli and maintain homeostasis [10, 11]. In relaxed conditions, parasympathetic activity is enhanced, promoting physiological adjustments that facilitate bodily repair and recovery [10]. Conversely, in response to emotional challenges, the ANS dynamically modulates the interaction between sympathetic and parasympathetic activity to support effective emotional regulation [12, 13]. A review of ANS activity in emotion suggested that ANS responses to different emotions exhibit considerable specificity [14]. During emotion regulation, the ANS regulates physiological responses to reduce stress caused by negative emotions and facilitate emotional recovery [12, 15]. Notably, adopting appropriate emotion regulation strategies can enhance ANS modulation and promote psychological well-being in patients with MDD.

Cognitive reappraisal is an effective emotion regulation strategy, which can guide MDD patients to change their emotions according to new cognitions [16–18]. This approach primarily facilitates emotional change by encouraging individuals to re-evaluate their thoughts, thereby gaining new perspectives on emotionally charged situations [19, 20]. A meta-analysis of emotion regulation strategies has shown that cognitive reappraisal is particularly effective in reducing negative emotions [15]. Moreover, depending on the experimental context, reappraisal can be used to serve different emotional goals [21]. In high-stress or uncontrollable situations, cognitive reappraisal has been identified as a key moderator in the relationship between stress and depression among MDD patients [22, 23]. Research utilizing functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has been widely conducted to investigate emotion regulation through cognitive reappraisal [24–26]. Recent studies have revealed patterns of functional impairment in specific brain regions of patients with depression [24]. This also offers insights into ANS dysregulation at the neural level.

Cardiac and respiratory signals, as important biomarkers, are closely associated with emotion regulation [27–29]. A review has shown that the characteristics of these peripheral physiological signals are essential for studying ANS activity in emotions [14]. Regarding cardiac signals, heart rate variability (HRV) is one of the most prominent indicators in research on emotional and mental states [30, 31]. Previous studies have found that HRV is linked to improvements in stress and anxiety during biofeedback training [27, 32]. Specifically, participants in the cognitive reappraisal condition exhibited increased HRV, whereas those in the suppression and control conditions did not [33]. Although effective reappraisal strategies can enhance HRV in patients with MDD, existing treatments still fail to restore the characteristically low HRV in depressed patients to normal levels, even when depressive symptoms are alleviated [34]. In addition, unpleasant emotions and anxiety were found to alter breathing patterns in experiments involving mental stress and physical load [35, 36]. It was characterized by intense negative emotions leading to shallow and rapid breathing [37, 38]. Compared to healthy controls, patients with MDD exhibited higher pause duration and respiration frequency variability, and a lower expiration-to-inspiration ratio [39]. Moreover, they appeared to be more sensitive to changes in breathing patterns [40]. When negative emotions were significantly reduced through cognitive reappraisal, the respiratory rate was effectively regulated and significantly decreased [41]. Overall, cardiac and respiratory indicators demonstrate considerable sensitivity to cognitive reappraisal in MDD patients.

Autonomic regulation influences heart and respiratory rhythms in various frequency bands [42–44]. In studies on frequency band, 0.15 Hz was often considered as a boundary to separate bands and thus contributions of close frequency components overlooked [43, 45]. Recent research suggests that the cardiovascular rhythm around 0.15 Hz was related to the activity of reticular neurons in the lower brainstem network associated with relaxation [46]. At rest, HRV intermediate band (IM; 0.12–0.18 Hz) fluctuations were correlated with blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signals in the mid and posterior insula and the secondary somatosensory area—regions involved in interoceptive perception [47]. In a controlled study on the efficacy of autogenic training, cardiovascular rhythm at 0.15 Hz showed phase synchronization with respiration [46]. When respiratory oscillations were in the IM band, significant bottom-up and top-down information flows occurred between the brainstem and prefrontal cortex (PFC) [42]. Moreover, coherent BOLD oscillations have been found in the PFC, middle frontal gyrus (MFG), brainstem, and limbic system (amygdala and hippocampus) [42]. In particular, processing of fMRI-related anxiety and information flow between brain and body revealed a preponderance of oscillations in the IM band [48]. Due to its relation to ANS regulation related to mental health, we will subsequently focus on IM band contributions in the studied MDD cohort.

Emotion regulation guided by cognitive reappraisal not only stimulates ANS activity but also elicits responses from the central nervous system (CNS). Moreover, these two nervous systems are closely interrelated [49, 50]. ANS activity can be observed through brain imaging of the CNS [47, 51], while CNS activity is reflected in the body’s unconscious physiological regulation, such as heartbeat and breathing, which are controlled by the ANS [41]. Therefore, indicators for brain and peripheral physiological activity can be combined, and such studies have found that the interoceptive IM band was associated with relaxation and its neural correlates during resting-state [47]. In an assisted driving study, a profound coupling between the IM band and driving-related neural activity emerged that characterizes peripheral responses to cognitive events [52]. Mechanisms underlying learning of cognitive reappraisal and self-regulation in MDD may be reflected in the link between the IM band and emotion-related neural activity.

This paper aims to establish a link between emotional neural activity and IM band cardiorespiratory activity during cognitive reappraisal. This approach enables a clearer understanding of the differences in neural mechanisms underlying emotion regulation in patients with MDD and healthy individuals. First, we investigated whether the neural activity associated with cognitive reappraisal and the brain activity underlying peripheral physiological indicators in the IM band significantly differed between patients and healthy controls. Second, we hypothesized that (a) patients with MDD would show significant differences from healthy controls in the coupling between peripheral physiological indicators in the IM band and emotion regulation-related brain regions; (b) this coupling would undergo significant changes during cognitive reappraisal in MDD patients; and (c) these changes would primarily be observed in emotion regulation areas such as the prefrontal cortex.

Materials and methods

Participants

Twenty-four patients with major depressive disorder (MDD; 13 females, mean age ) and 24 age-matched healthy controls (HC; 11 females, mean age ) participated in the experiment. All participants had proficient knowledge of the German language, normal or corrected-to-normal vision, no contraindications for MRI, and were right-handed. Patients with depression had been on stable medication for at least one week prior to participation. This study protocol was approved by the local Ethics Committee of RWTH Aachen University Hospital (EK 050/17), and each subject provided written informed consent about the study procedure.

Experimental procedure

Before the formal experiment, participants were asked to complete a series of questionnaires. These included a verbal intelligence test (IQ, Wortschatztest - WST) [53], the Digit-span Task, the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ) [54], and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [55]. The questionnaires collected basic information on patients with MDD and healthy individuals (see Table 1).

Sample characteristics

The experiment was a real-time fMRI neurofeedback (NF)-guided cognitive reappraisal study conducted over two days, with at least one week between visits. This two-day experimental design allowed tracking of emotional changes induced by cognitive reappraisal in participants. Each visit consisted of a 480 s resting state session before training, four 460 s training sessions, and another 480 s resting state session after training (see Fig. 1). The resting state sessions were conducted with participants’ eyes open to prevent falling asleep. The training sessions were involved negative image stimulation from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS) [56]. A total of 72 images with comparable characteristics were selected based on valence (1–9, 1 = extremely negative) and arousal (1–9, 1 = no arousal), with a mean valence of and a mean arousal of . The images were evenly divided into 8 subsets for different sessions, with 9 images randomly presented in each subset. There were no significant differences in valence or arousal between the subsets (both ).

Experimental design of day 1 and day 2. In each day’s experiment, participants were required first to record 8 min of resting state, then complete four 460-second training sessions, and finally re-record their resting state. There were 9 view-reappraise cycles in each session of the experiment. This study used the emotions evoked by the View-image (‘’) to re-evaluate the Reappraise-image (‘+’). Each image was presented for 12 s, followed by a 6-second gap and a 4-second blank (‘%%’) or a feedback number

The experimental design of the training sessions included blocks of viewing and reappraising images. In the ‘view’ condition, participants observed an image for 12 s and were instructed to maintain the emotional state induced by the image. In the ‘reappraise’ condition, participants re-evaluated the content of a similar image for another 12 s by positively adjusting their interpretation of the previous stimulus. Additionally, a fixed 6-second interval followed each image presentation. Afterwards, participants received numerical feedback based on their brain activation in the target region selected for neurofeedback training. However, the current investigation focused on cognitive reappraisal efforts regardless of feedback success.

Real-time online feedback was accomplished using Turbo-BrainVoyager (TBV) Version 3.2 (Brain Innovation, Maastricht, NL) [57]. The feedback information originated from the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (vlPFC), which plays a pivotal role in emotion regulation [58, 59]. Specifically, it was obtained by calculating the difference in the BOLD signal between the view and reappraise blocks recorded in either the left or right vlPFC, expressed as . The selection of the left or right vlPFC for each session was conducted in a double-blind manner, ensuring that the number of left and right selections was balanced for each participant to avoid potential effects related to channel allocation.

Data acquisition

fMRI data were acquired using a 3 Tesla Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma whole-body MRI scanner (Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany) with a 20-channel head coil. The device’s MPRAGE sequence provided T1-weighted structural images (TR = 2 s, TE = 3.03 ms, TI = 900 ms, flip angle =, matrix = , 1 mm isotropic voxels, 0.5 mm gap, 176 slices). T2*-weighted functional images were obtained using echo-planar imaging (TR = 2 s, TE = 28 ms, flip angle = , voxel size = , matrix size = , 0.75 mm gap, 34 transverse slices). In addition, cardiac and respiratory signals were collected at a sampling frequency of 2000 Hz. Cardiac activity was recorded through photoplethysmography (PPG) placed on the participant’s left index finger, and respiratory data was recorded using a pneumatic respiratory belt strapped to the participant’s abdomen.

Data preprocessing and analysis

The analysis of peripheral physiological data involved cardiac and respiratory signals. The cardiac signal was corrected using Kubios HRV Premium software (version 3.1, Kuopio, Finland) with a 90% threshold, and the power of the HRV IM band was calculated [60]. The respiratory signal was preprocessed using low-pass and high-pass filters to estimate trends and remove noise, and the IM band power was calculated in MATLAB R2021a. The IM band ranged from 0.12 to 0.18 Hz. Power was calculated by performing a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) with a moving window of 30 s (15 volumes, TR = 2 s) and an overlap of 28 s. To account for hemodynamic delay, the power time series of HRV and respiration were shifted backward by 5 s relative to the fMRI signal time series. RETROICOR phase expansion was performed using the PhysIO Toolbox (version 5.1.0) to extract the second-order cardiac cycle, the second-order respiratory cycle, and the first-order cardiac-respiratory cycle interaction, producing 12 parameters to remove the impact of physiological noise on fMRI data [61, 62]. In addition, we summarized the IM band power during resting and task sessions and performed paired-sample t-tests to better understand basic physiological characteristics. To reduce skewness, the power values of the HRV and respiratory IM bands were logarithmically transformed.

fMRI image preprocessing was performed in MATLAB using the Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 toolbox (SPM12). To minimize T1-saturation effects, the first five volumes of each session were discarded. Then, motion correction realigned all functional volumes to the updated first volume, and the resulting six movement parameters were included in the first-level General Linear Model (GLM) to reduce head motion artifacts. Functional and anatomical images were co-registered, and spatial normalization to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space was performed. Spatial smoothing was applied using an 8 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel at the end of preprocessing. Mapping analysis was conducted based on the GLM in SPM12. At the individual level, the design matrix included the task conditions (view and reappraise), the power of the HRV IM band, the power of the respiratory IM band, six motion parameters, and 12 RETROICOR regressors. The view condition was treated as the baseline, and the reappraisal condition served as the experimental regressor, with each condition modeled using a 6-second time window based on the experiment. Accordingly, the reappraisal responses (reappraise > view) were used as the regulation responses. At the group level, the three contrasts of interest from the first-level GLM of the reappraise > view, HRV-IM, and respiration-IM were entered into a full factorial model. The second-level results were evaluated with at voxel level and with family-wise error (FWE) at cluster level.

ROIs analysis

The spatial determination of designated regions of interest (ROIs) was based on the atlas using the Brainnetome Atlas toolbox (version 1.0.2) in MATLAB, which functionally divides brain regions [63]. The ROIs were selected regarding areas of general interest in research on emotion regulation in patients with depression, mainly focusing on the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), hippocampus, insula, and prefrontal cortex [26, 64, 65]. The prefrontal cortex was further subdivided into the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC), ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), and vlPFC. Among these, the dmPFC involves the medial aspects of Brodmann’s areas (BA) 8 and 9, while the vmPFC covers BA10, BA11, and BA14 [66–69]. For the lateral frontal cortex, the dlPFC includes the lateral aspects of BA8, BA9, and BA46, and the vlPFC includes BA44, BA45, and BA47 [70–72]. Combined with the whole-brain activation map during cognitive reappraisal (reappraise >view) in this study, the dlPFC ROI analysis involved only the left hemisphere, while the other ROIs covered bilateral brain regions.

The SPM toolbox MarsBaR (version 0.45) was used to extract beta values within each activation cluster and obtain the mean ROI beta values from the contrast maps of HRV-IM and respiration-IM, respectively [73]. To evaluate differences among factors, the beta values were entered into a multiway analysis of variance (ANOVA) implemented in MATLAB, with considered statistically significant. Three-factor ANOVAs were performed for each ROI with false discovery rate (FDR) correction. The three factors included group (HC and MDD), day (1 and 2), and session (1, 2, 3, and 4). For ROIs located on the left and right sides of the same brain region, an additional four-factor ANOVA including side (left and right) as an extra factor was conducted, also with FDR correction. Furthermore, to explore differences within day or session factors, paired-sample t-tests were performed at an exploratory threshold according to .

Whole brain analysis

Cognitive reappraisal task

This study eliminated the interference of peripheral physiological signals to improve the detection of brain activation, differing from previous studies. The ‘reappraise > view’ contrast showed widespread activation of brain regions involved in emotion regulation in both healthy and depressed subjects, including the ACC, hippocampus, insula, and prefrontal cortex (see Fig. 2A; Table 3). Moreover, the supplementary motor area (SMA), pre- and postcentral gyrus, and temporal and occipital lobes were activated. The MDD patients showed stronger brain activation in these regions than healthy controls, except in the anterior part of the SMA (see Fig. 2B; Table 3). In summary, significant differences in neural activation during cognitive reappraisal emerged between patients with MDD and healthy controls.

Peripheral physiological indicators

The regulation of peripheral physiological indicators in the IM band is associated with neural activity in several brain regions [47]. During cognitive reappraisal, HRV IM band activity was positively correlated with activity in the pre- and postcentral gyrus, SMA, ACC, middle cingulate cortex (MCC), insula, and temporal lobe (see Fig. 3A; Table 4). Although the neural responses appear stronger in healthy subjects, no significant differences were observed between groups. In the respiratory domain, fluctuations in the IM band were associated with the Rolandic operculum, pre- and postcentral gyrus, SMA, cingulate cortex, and cerebellum (see Fig. 3B; Table 4). Moreover, activation in these brain regions was significantly greater in patients with MDD than in healthy controls. Interestingly, only respiratory activity yielded significant differences between MDD patients and healthy controls.

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between intermediate (IM) band cardiac and respiratory fluctuations and brain activity during emotion regulation in a real-time fMRI NF cognitive reappraisal training. The analysis found that cognitive reappraisal elicited neural activity in the ACC, hippocampus, insula, and four prefrontal regions. Patients with MDD showed lower reappraisal use according to the ERQ at baseline. However, both groups engaged similar brain regions during emotion regulation [22, 23]. Combined with the modulation of the ANS, peripheral physiological indicators in the IM frequency band were used to establish connections with the activity of the emotion-regulation brain regions [47]. Indeed, this IM band coupling analysis revealed significant differences between MDD patients and controls. The HRV IM band exhibited negative coupling with the right insula and right vlPFC, while the respiratory IM band showed greater positive increases in the MDD group. Importantly, in MDD patients only, IM band coupling with prefrontal areas increased during cognitive reappraisal. Accordingly, these results indicate differences between patients with MDD and healthy individuals by establishing correlations between neural activity and IM band peripheral physiological indicators, contributing to further exploration of the neurophysiological mechanisms of MDD.

Cognitive reappraisal effects

Depression is characterized by increased negative mood and difficulty in disengaging from negative material [16, 23]. Cognitive reappraisal is a collaborative approach that identifies, appraisals, and modifies thoughts, evaluations, and beliefs contributing to psychological disorders [74]. This strategy allows individuals with depression to reduce the valence of negative situations more positively [19, 75]. A systematic review suggested that fMRI-supported cognitive reappraisal can facilitate the learning of emotion regulation in patients with mental disorders [76]. Our study supports this finding, as brain regions typically involved in emotion regulation were activated in both MDD and HC groups during cognitive reappraisal (see Fig. 2). Furthermore, several meta-analyses have highlighted the key role of the prefrontal cortex in emotion regulation [24, 77]. In patients with depression, activity in the prefrontal cortex related to maladaptive emotion regulation and cognitive reappraisal differs from that of healthy individuals [24, 78, 79]. Consistent with our study, prefrontal mechanisms may improve emotion learning in MDD.

Regarding resting-state brain imaging insights into physiological processes, the IM band of HRV and respiration are closely associated with neural activity [47]. These connections are mediated by the coordination of the ANS, which enables the cranial nerves to actively regulate peripheral physiological indicators, thereby modulating cardiac and respiratory rhythms [27, 35, 41]. We also observed these correlations during cognitive reappraisal (see Fig. 3). Notably, fewer brain regions are involved in HRV regulation in patients with MDD. This finding is consistent with the characteristic widespread low HRV observed in MDD patients [41], corresponding to the IM band power results (see Table 2). Moreover, even if depression is successfully treated, existing treatments may not restore HRV in depressed patients to normal levels [34]. A previous study showed that although patients with depression can regulate HRV through effective reappraisal strategies, autonomic control still exhibits substantial variability [80]. In terms of the respiratory IM band, multiple brain regions in MDD patients were involved in its regulation, indicating relatively intact neural networks. Furthermore, these patients exhibited more widespread brain activation patterns than controls, which may reflect more effortful ANS regulation during cognitive reappraisal.

Brain regions associated with emotion regulation

The ACC, hippocampus, insula, and PFC are brain regions responsible for emotion regulation [24, 81, 82]. Therefore, these regions were selected for further analysis in this study. For the ‘reappraise >view’ contrast, patients were able to use feedback information to change cognition and regulate emotions, which stimulated activity in these ROIs, showing no significant difference compared to healthy individuals. Notably, for the dlPFC, only the left region was selected as an ROI because the right dlPFC in patients with MDD did not respond positively to cognitive reappraisal. This finding is associated with an imbalance in the functional status of the left and right dlPFC in MDD [83, 84]. Although patients with depression typically exhibit reduced cerebral blood flow and metabolism in the left dlPFC and hypermetabolism in the right dlPFC, only the left dlPFC activity has been shown to be inversely modulated by positive emotional valence [85]. In addition, the left dlPFC is the primary target for repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) in the treatment of major depression, which can effectively reduce symptom severity [86, 87]. These studies indicate that compared with the right dlPFC, the left dlPFC plays a more significant role for cognitive reappraisal in patients with depression.

In the regulation of HRV by the ANS, the insula and vlPFC play important roles [88, 89]. Furthermore, the right insula and right vlPFC are key structures involved in voluntary emotion regulation and are associated with reduced subjective negative emotions during reappraisal [58, 90]. Through the analysis of ROIs in the IM band of HRV, a connection between the HRV IM band and the neural mechanisms of emotion regulation was proposed. We found that the beta values of the right insula and right vlPFC were positive in the HC group but negative in the MDD group, with significant differences between the two groups (see Fig. 4). This may be related to the low HRV and deactivation of brain areas observed in patients with MDD [34, 41]. In addition, the relationship between the vmPFC and the HRV IM band changed significantly in patients over the training sessions (see Fig. 5 and Appendix A). In healthy individuals, vmPFC activity induced by cognitive reappraisal is inversely related to brain activation associated with the HRV IM band. However, in patients with MDD, vmPFC activity related to HRV regulation was increased on the first visit and returned to normal by the second visit. This aligns with previous research suggesting that the vmPFC plays a role in coordinating emotion-related neural activity and physiological responses [91, 92]. To better understand the relationship between the vmPFC and the HRV IM band, long-term cognitive reappraisal training and follow-up studies in patients with depression are warranted. The results regarding the HRV IM band support our hypotheses about differences between MDD patients and healthy individuals, as well as significant changes in prefrontal activation over training days in the patients.

Regulation of the respiratory system is closely linked to cortico-limbic circuits that overlap with regions known to mediate cognitive and emotional processing [93]. Moreover, the prefrontal lobes play an important role in the cerebral regulation of breathing rate [94, 95]. This may be reflected in the results of the ROI analysis in the respiratory IM-frequency band, which showed positive beta values in both the healthy and patient groups (see Fig. 4). Compared with healthy individuals, patients exhibited stronger connections between emotion regulation brain regions and brain activity associated with respiratory IM band, particularly in the ACC, hippocampus, insula, dmPFC, and vlPFC. This may be attributed to the stronger brain activity observed in the patient group during regulation of the respiratory IM frequency band (see Fig. 3). Previous studies have suggested that emotion regulation is closely linked to the rhythm of respiration in patients with MDD [37, 38]. In the current study’s dmPFC and vlPFC analyses, left and right ROIs showed similar trends, but significant between-group differences were mainly found on the right side. This may relate to the finding that right-hemispheric regions are more dominant in respiratory activity [94]. We also observed that the relationship between these two ROIs and the respiratory IM-frequency band gradually changed across sessions, primarily in patients with MDD (see Fig. 6 and Appendix A). Depression is characterized by impaired emotion regulation abilities, though appropriate strategies can help improve mood [6, 17, 22]. Previous studies have also shown that increased activity in the dmPFC and vlPFC is associated with cognitive reappraisal [78, 79]. Our findings further demonstrate staged changes in patients’ emotional states during cognitive reappraisal, supporting our hypotheses concerning ANS-CNS coupling in the respiratory IM band. These results provide a new perspective for future emotion-related research on patients with MDD and other mental health conditions.

Limitations

The current investigation included data from two visits. The results indicate differences between depressed patients and healthy controls, as well as changes over time in the patient group. In future studies, long-term data tracking and follow-ups could provide more robust evidence regarding the established link. In studying brain mechanisms alongside peripheral physiological indicators, we focused on exploring the IM frequency bands of HRV and respiration. However, a more comprehensive data acquisition, including multiple physiological signals and frequency bands, may further enhance our understanding. In addition, we selected core brain regions associated with emotion regulation for the ROI analysis that were in line with the primary aim of the study. However, emotion regulation brain areas are not limited to those we selected. Some brain structures, although not directly related to emotion regulation, may play crucial roles in the process and should be considered in future research.

Conclusions

This paper is based on a real-time fMRI-NF-guided cognitive reappraisal study and investigates the relationship between ANS activity and brain activity related to emotion regulation in MDD patients and healthy individuals. We focused on analyzing the IM band of respiratory indicators and HRV during reappraisal. The study demonstrated that patients with MDD exhibit stronger associations between ANS activity and emotional neural activity, especially in the respiratory domain. In patients with MDD, cognitive reappraisal induced phasic changes in the coupling between peripheral physiological indicators in the IM band and prefrontal brain regions. The data indicate that cardiac and respiratory indicators are closely related to emotion regulation in MDD patients, which may be helpful for detecting psychological conditions. In future work, peripheral physiological indicators may be used as markers to monitor the mental health of patients with affective disorders in clinical settings and to improve the detection of affective disorders in undiagnosed individuals.
