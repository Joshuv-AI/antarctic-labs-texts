High Prevalence of Steatotic Liver in Adolescents and Young Adults in a Northern California Community
Authors: Julia Vudoan Ernsting, Alexia Tran, Ashkawn Beheshtian, Vivian Nguyen, Bhargava Gannavarapu, Ruel Garcia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe prevalence of steatotic liver disease (SLD) in adolescents and young adults (AYA) is poorly understood. While the recognition of the importance of this condition as a precursor to serious liver disease is growing, the impact on young people is not well documented. The majority of SLD data is derived from older subjects undergoing a clinically indicated procedure. This study seeks to better describe the prevalence and associated factors for SLD in AYA.MethodsA convenience sample of 241 patients aged 14–30 in Northern California was recruited. Demographic, dietary, exercise and biometric data were collected. Logistic regression models were applied to assess the association between collected data and body mass index (BMI) categories with SLD presence. A diagnostic cut-off of controlled attenuation parameter ≥238 dB/m on transient elastography was used to define SLD.ResultsThe prevalence of SLD was 59/241 (24.8%). BMI was a predictor of SLD, with 0.0% (0/12) of underweight, 8.5% (11/129) of normal, 28.1% (18/64) of overweight and 83.3% (30/36) of obese participants having hepatic steatosis in this sample.ConclusionThe prevalence of SLD was unexpectedly high, particularly among subjects who were overweight or obese. The increasing diagnosis of SLD in young populations requires a better understanding of factors that may predict or protect from developing fatty liver. Similar to previous reports, BMI was the main predictor of SLD in our population. This study highlights a possible concerning finding of SLD starting at a young age and for consideration of standardized guidelines for screening in AYA.Keywords: Fatty liver, Adolescent, Young adult, Body mass index, Overweight, Obesity, Prevalence, Diagnostic imaging, Liver diseases

INTRODUCTION

Steatotic Liver Disease (SLD) is the most common cause of liver disease in adolescents and young adults (AYA) [1]. Steatotic liver disease is a broad term describing conditions marked by abnormal fat accumulation within the liver (also known as hepatic steatosis). SLD encompasses what was formerly referred to as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), now Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the preferred term under the 2023 international consensus, as well as alcohol-related liver disease [2]. The impact of SLD is growing, with MASLD becoming an increasingly common indication for liver transplantation for AYA in the United States [3]. While the understanding of the pathophysiology underlying SLD remains limited, it is thought to be the hepatic manifestation of metabolic dysfunction and is associated with insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, cardiovascular disease, and obesity [4].

MASLD is defined by excessive fat accumulation, that is hepatic steatosis, in the absence of a history of significant alcohol use or other known liver disease [5]. The change in nomenclature for this condition from NAFLD to MASLD demonstrates the increasing recognition of the metabolic factors that contribute to its development and progression. A proportion of patients with MASLD will progress to a more advanced form of the disease, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) which is characterized by hepatocellular injury and inflammation. MASH can lead to progressive liver disease through scarring and cirrhosis. The pathological spectrum of MASLD thus ranges from simple steatosis to MASH, fibrosis, cirrhosis, and possibly eventual development of hepatocellular carcinoma [6]. While awareness of the significance of this condition is growing for the adult population, the prevalence of SLD in AYA is uncertain because AYA are rarely included in SLD studies. The study of SLD in AYA has also been hindered by a lack of non-invasive diagnostic testing and an absence of standardized guidelines on screening indications.

Previous attempts to determine SLD prevalence in AYA have utilized a variety of populations and testing methods. Globally, a meta-analysis of pooled data using different methods, including liver chemistry, imaging, and/or histology, estimated the prevalence rate to be 7.6% in children and adolescents (age 1–19) [7]. The same study demonstrated higher SLD prevalence in clinically obese populations (mean prevalence 34.2%) than in the general population (7.6%) [7]. The Study of Child and Adolescent Liver Epidemiology (SCALE) was an autopsy-based study of children throughout the general population of San Diego, California, which found the prevalence rate of SLD was estimated to be 9.6% among children aged 2 to 19 years [1]. In the Teen LABS study of adolescents with obesity severe enough to warrant weight loss surgery, the prevalence rate of SLD was found to be 59% [8]. While these studies suggest that increased BMI or obesity is a factor in developing SLD, the subjects are not representative of the community population.

In the clinical setting, SLD in AYA continues to be under-diagnosed, under-studied, and under-treated. Understanding the burden of SLD among AYA has become increasingly relevant as prevalence of steatotic liver disease has risen over the past several decades which has important public health and policy implications [9]. As most patients with SLD are asymptomatic, it is a condition often diagnosed incidentally during another study such as laboratory testing, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or ultrasound. At risk population screening that would lead to a better understanding of the prevalence in the adolescent and young adult populations has not been attempted, partially due to a lack of widely accepted guidelines. When testing is indicated, there are multiple diagnostic options to evaluate for hepatic steatosis, but most either require expensive examinations (CT/MRI) or have significant risks associated (biopsy). Liver biopsy is a gold standard for the diagnosis and severity assessment of hepatic steatosis, providing direct evidence of the extent of tissue involvement [10]. However, liver biopsy is invasive and prone to complications such as bleeding, infection, and pain [11]. There is also high sampling error and variability in the pathological interpretation of liver biopsies [12,13,14].

The controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) derived from transient elastography (TE) is an established method to diagnose hepatic steatosis [15]. The advantages of TE with CAP are that it is a non-invasive test, easy to perform, provides immediate results, and has low cost in comparison with other measurement modalities [16]. Studies have demonstrated that the CAP has a high concordance with the results of liver biopsy and other diagnostic methods in detecting steatosis [17,18,19]. CAP quantifies degree of steatosis in the liver irrespective of etiology. The aim of this prospective observational study was to utilize CAP to understand the prevalence of SLD in the adolescent and young adult population in a Northern California community.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A convenience sample of 241 non-consecutive subjects aged 14–30 were recruited from community Gastroenterology clinics and high school and college campuses in Northern California, USA between November 2022 and May 2023. Clinic patients represented 78 (32.4%) of our population and community subjects were 163 (67.6%). Demographic, diet, exercise habits and alcohol consumption were self-reported. Height, weight, and waist circumference were measured. A CAP score of ≥238 was considered hepatic steatosis and liver stiffness of >7.0 kPa indicative of a hepatic fibrosis stage of F2 or higher [20,21]. CAP scores and fibrosis stage were obtained using a FibroScan® 230 (Echosens).

Subjects were classified as underweight, normal, overweight or obese. For adolescents up to age 18, utilizing the United States Center for Disease Control (CDC) growth charts for age and sex specific percentages, underweight was defined as less than the 5th percentile, overweight as 85th to less than 95th percentile and obese as 95th percentile or greater [22]. For subjects 18–30 years of age, the United States CDC definition of underweight as a body mass index (BMI) less than 18.5, normal weight as BMI greater than 18.5 and less than 25, overweight as a BMI of 25.0 to less than 30 and a BMI of 30 or greater as obese were used [23].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the sample characteristics. Continuous variables were expressed as means±standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables were presented as numbers (N) and percentages (%). The chi-square test was utilized to determine significant differences between the two groups based on hepatic steatosis status for each categorical variable. Fisher’s exact test was used instead when the expected count was less than 5. For numeric variables, Student’s t-test was applied.

Logistic regression models were applied to assess the association between BMI categories and hepatic steatosis status. Covariates that showed a statistically significant association (p<0.05) with hepatic steatosis in univariate analyses were subsequently included in the multivariate logistic regression model. The final adjusted model included BMI category, hours of moderate to strenuous exercise per week, sweets intake, and fast-food consumption. Age and sex were not included in the adjusted model because they were not significantly associated with hepatic steatosis in the univariate analyses. Multicollinearity among diet-related variables (e.g., sweets and fast-food frequency) was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) derived from the logistic regression model. The adjusted GVIF values for all predictors were below 1.1, suggesting negligible multicollinearity and confirming that all variables were retained in the model.

A forest plot was generated to illustrate the OR of hepatic steatosis for each BMI category, using normal weight as the reference group.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS (SAS 9.4, SAS Institute Inc.). Statistical significance was defined using a two-sided test with a p-value<0.05.

Ethics statement

This study was conducted with the approval of the Advarra Institutional Review Board (Advarra Inc., Columbia, MD, USA; IRB Registration No. 00000971; FWA No. 00023875; Protocol No. Pro00065878). Informed consent was obtained from the subject and from a parent or legal guardian for participants under 18 years of age.

The impact of BMI on risk for SLD was highly significant, with 0/12 (0.0%) of underweight, 11/129 (8.5%) of normal weight, 18/64 (28.1%) of overweight and 30/36 (83.3%) of obese participants having SLD in this population (Fig. 1). Calculating the normal weight as a baseline, the forest plot of ORs for BMI by category shows a trend towards a lower prevalence in subjects who are underweight (0.33, 0.02–6.12) and a statistically significant risk with being either overweight (3.62, 1.59-8.25) or obese (38.44, 12.24–120.71) (Fig. 2).

The large odds ratio (OR) for obesity (OR=38.4, 95% confidence interval: 12.2–120.7) reflects both a strong association and the limited number of obese participants in the sample (n=36). Because hepatic steatosis was present in more than half of the obese participants (30 out of 36), the model estimated a substantially elevated risk relative to normal-weight individuals. The wide confidence interval indicates imprecision due to the relatively small subgroup size, rather than model instability or overfitting. To confirm the robustness of this finding, we examined model diagnostics and multicollinearity (VIF, all <1.1), which did not indicate any estimation issues.

DISCUSSION

While SLD is increasingly recognized as a public health concern, awareness of its impacts and prevalence in AYA has lagged other populations [24]. Consistent with other studies we found that BMI was a predictor of SLD. A gradient of risk with underweight subjects having a lower prevalence to the increasing prevalence in overweight and obese groups, BMI is an important risk factor when considering which patients may have SLD. More studies are needed in AYA to clarify the prevalence and impact of SLD globally as there is significant regional variation in prevalence due to differing rates of obesity, genetic factors and socioeconomic conditions [25,26].

There is currently a lack of consensus on which patients should be considered for testing for SLD from leading Gastroenterology societies and government agencies. The Hepatology Committee for the European Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition does recommend screening for SLD in obese patients with liver function tests and ultrasound in children over 3 years of age [27]. For older patients European Association for the Study of the Liver recommends screening for steatosis in patients with insulin resistance or metabolic disorders [28]. In the United States consensus guidelines do not exist. The US Preventive Servies Task Force does not put forward for screening recommendations for SLD. Guidance from American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases recommends against general population screening, and only recommends testing for patients with a diagnosis of NAFLD for presence of advanced fibrosis in high-risk individuals, such as those with type 2 diabetes, family history of cirrhosis or more than mild alcohol consumption [29]. The North American Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition does recommend screening for obese and overweight children with an additional risk factor or persistent alanine aminotransferase or aspartate aminotransferase abnormalities [30]. This lack of consensus is a barrier to understanding the prevalence of SLD both in the AYA and general populations. Because strategies to combat the rising burden of noncommunicable diseases such as SLD should start early in life and focus particularly on adolescence when risk factors and lifestyles are established, understanding factors associated with SLD will be important in disease prevention and modification [31].

In addition to the incomplete knowledge of the population prevalence of SLD in AYA, risk factors for its development are also incompletely described. A better understanding of this will require similar studies that collect broad demographic, exercise, and dietary information. This study population demonstrates a trend toward exercise being protective against SLD was present but was not significant when multivariate regression modeling was performed. Similarly, frequent intake of sweets and fast-food frequency had a trend towards increased risk of SLD that was not maintained once BMI was included in the multivariate regression analysis.

It is important to note the limitations of this study. First it is modest in size and a convenience sample of a single geographic location. Self-reported answers on lifestyle habits such as diet and exercise may contribute to inaccuracies on data analysis. Additionally, no other laboratory or imaging studies were performed on the subjects. While most of our discussion is based on studies of NAFLD prior to the change in nomenclature, our study identifies steatotic liver disease and not specifically alcoholic liver disease or metabolic associated steatotic liver disease. While there was a good representation of subjects of White, Asian and Latin X descent, other ethnicities were underrepresented. More studies done with a larger, more diverse population may better characterize the prevalence of SLD in all ethnicities. Although TE is recommended to be performed in a fasting state, subjects in this study were not required to fast prior to participating. The impact of that is not clear, however there is evidence that recent food intake can cause an increase in liver stiffness measurements [32]. In one study of food intake effect on CAP there is evidence that a non-fasting state may lower CAP scores, indicating that this study may underestimate the prevalence of SLD due to the subject’s non-fasting state when the test was performed [33].

A larger sample including lifestyle factors in subsequent studies may reveal further associations with SLD that could be useful in targeting interventions. While our study reveals BMI as a major risk factor for SLD, additional studies may provide better description of the full range of associated factors that influence the development of SLD. Although the exact magnitude should be interpreted with caution, the direction and strength of the association are consistent with the well-established relationship between obesity and hepatic steatosis reported in previous epidemiologic studies.
