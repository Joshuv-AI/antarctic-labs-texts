Porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus type 1 and type 2 co-infection in a Japanese swine herd
Authors: Osamu TAIRA, Akira FUJIWARA, Ryosuke TAKAI, Nobuyuki TSUTSUMI, Katsuaki SUGIURA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe status of co-infection with porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus type 1 (PRRSV-1) and type 2 (PRRSV-2) in Japan is poorly understood. A case of such co-infection was identified on a PRRSV-1 non-vaccinated farm in Kagoshima prefecture. Both PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2 genomes were simultaneously detected in pig samples by RT-PCR, and molecular analysis confirmed PRRSV-1/PRRSV-2 co-infection in individual piglets. The PRRSV-1 strain (020-P4-EU) was classified as lineage 1 (sublineage: L1.2), showing high similarity to the Unistrain® PRRS vaccine and a Korean strain. The PRRSV-2 strain belonged to lineage 4 (Cluster III). These findings provide molecular evidence of PRRSV-1/PRRSV-2 co-circulation in Japan, suggesting the complex epidemiology of PRRSV in this region.Keywords: co-infection, Japan, porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus type 1, porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus type 2, vaccine-like strain

Porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome (PRRS) is a major viral disease causing substantial economic losses to the global swine industry [3]. The causative agent, PRRS virus (PRRSV), is a single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus within the genus Betaarterivirus, family Arteriviridae [12]. PRRSV comprises 2 distinct species: Betaarterivirus europensis (formerly European type, PRRSV-1) and Betaarterivirus americense (formerly North American type, PRRSV-2) [12, 21]. These 2 species share only about 60% nucleotide identity at the whole-genome level and are further classified into diverse genetic lineages [1]. PRRSV-1, represented by the Lelystad virus, first identified in the Netherlands in 1991, was initially confirmed mainly in European swine herds [24]. However, subsequent decades of research and surveillance have revealed that PRRSV-1 distribution is not limited to Europe but is reported extensively worldwide, including in North America and Asian countries [22]. Particularly in Asia, PRRSV-1 has been detected in China since 1994, showing continuous evolution and spread [18]. In South Korea, PRRSV-1 was first isolated after 2005 [10], with subsequent studies revealing high prevalence and genetic diversity [9]. To understand PRRSV-1 genetic diversity and evolution, a recent comprehensive study by Yim-im et al. proposed a revised classification system based on a large-scale global analysis of ORF5 gene sequences, which exhibit high nucleotide variation [28]. This system reclassifies PRRSV-1 from “subtype” to “lineage,” better reflecting its evolutionary phylogeny. According to this system, PRRSV-1 is divided into 4 lineages (L1, L2, L3, and L4). Lineage 1 (L1), the most diverse and globally distributed, is further subdivided into 18 sublineages (L1.1 to L1.18), providing a more detailed depiction of PRRSV-1 molecular epidemiology and transmission dynamics [28]. In addition to this global expansion and genetic evolution, co-infection and co-circulation of PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2 within the same regions or farms have been reported in various countries. In South Korea, the prevalence of PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2 co-infection has been investigated. A survey from 2013 to 2016 targeting 631 PRRS-affected farms found that 24.2% were simultaneously positive for both viral genotypes [9]. Previous research on PRRS-related abortion cases has shown that approximately half of PRRSV-positive aborted fetuses were positive for both types, suggesting that co-infection may enhance the severity of PRRS [26, 27]. In Russia, the first domestic case of mixed infection with PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2 was reported in 2019 and was associated with a respiratory disease outbreak [19]. An instance of PCR detection of both types was also reported on a farm in Thailand [16]. These findings underscore the significant presence and growing concern of such co-infections. In Japan, PRRSV-2 has long been considered the predominant etiological agent. However, PRRSV-1 (strain Jpn EU 4-37) was first confirmed in Japanese domestic pigs in 2008, exhibiting high ORF5 gene sequence identity with the subtype 1 reference strain SD-02-10 [4, 5]. Subsequently, disease diagnosis conducted by the National Agriculture and Food Research Organization (NARO)-National Institute of Animal Health detected one case of the European type, but it is considered limited in the area and not widespread [23]. After a period without new detections, a second PRRSV-1 field strain was reported in the Chugoku-Shikoku region in 2023; this strain showed high ORF5 gene sequence homology with the subtype 1 strain R14 from Spain [17]. Concurrently, there have been reports of vaccine-like PRRSV-1 strains being detected on non-vaccinated farms, where transmission from vaccinated neighboring herds was suspected [20]. This indicates that the epidemiological situation of PRRSV-1 in Japan has entered a new, more complex phase. Despite these sporadic PRRSV-1 outbreaks and its potential for expansion, the status of PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2 co-circulation in Japan, including its occurrence and epidemiological significance, remains largely undetermined. The objectives of this study were to confirm a suspected case of PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2 co-infection identified on a PRRS-affected farm in Japan and to characterize the ORF5 gene region of both PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2 obtained from clinical samples from this farm.

The investigation was conducted on a commercial swine farm in Kagoshima prefecture, Japan. The clinical investigation on this farm was prompted by respiratory disease in pigs after weaning. Concurrently, several sows exhibited noteworthy clinical signs, including anorexia and inability to stand. However, specific reproductive disorders such as an increase in abortions or stillbirths were not reported as a primary problem in the sows. Furthermore, a significant increase in mortality in the weaned pigs was not documented during this investigation. In April 2025, a total of 12 samples, derived from 20 animals, were submitted for diagnostic testing. These included 5 individual serum samples from sows, 3 individual serum samples from 30-day-old piglets, and 4 pooled serum samples, each pool representing 3 pigs from the 60-day-old, 90-day-old, 120-day-old, and 150-day-old age groups. RNA was extracted from all serum samples using the High Pure Viral RNA Kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Initial screening for PRRSV was performed on all 12 samples using an RT-PCR assay developed in this study for the simultaneous detection and differentiation of PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2. To differentiate between PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2, a novel genotyping RT-PCR assay was developed. The primers for this assay were designed based on an alignment of PRRSV-1 (reference strain: Lelystad, GenBank accession No. M96262) and PRRSV-2 (reference strain: VR-2332, GenBank accession No. AY150564) sequences, targeting a conserved region within ORF7 and the 3’ UTR.

The principle of differentiation for this newly designed assay was based on the presence of a natural 35-bp deletion within the amplified region of the PRRSV-1 genotype, a method utilized in the WOAH diagnostic protocol [25]. This deletion allows the two genotypes to be distinguished by the size of their PCR products. The expected amplicon sizes are 403 bp for PRRSV-1 and 438 bp for PRRSV-2. The primer sequences are Neo-1 (5ʹ-GCCAGTCAATCAACTGTGC-3ʹ) and Neo-2 (5ʹ-ACCCCCATGTGATCGCCCT-3ʹ).

RT-PCR was performed using the SuperScript III One-Step RT-PCR System with Platinum Taq DNA Polymerase (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Thermal cycling conditions included reverse transcription at 50°C for 30 min; initial denaturation at 94°C for 2 min; followed by 35 cycles of 94°C for 15 sec, 58°C for 30 sec, and 68°C for 1 min for amplification; and a final extension at 68°C for 5 min. The specificity and accuracy of the genotyping RT-PCR assay were validated using commercial PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2 modified live vaccines as positive controls. The assay successfully produced amplicons of the expected sizes. To confirm specificity, these amplicons were purified and sequenced, confirming 100% nucleotide identity with the respective vaccine strains.

Furthermore, the analytical sensitivity of the assay was compared to a commercial real-time RT-PCR kit (IDEXX RealPCR PRRSV RNA Mix; IDEXX Laboratories, Inc., Westbrook, ME, USA). Using 10-fold serial dilutions of quantified internal reference viruses, the detection limit of our genotyping RT-PCR was found to be comparable to the commercial real-time assay (approximately 10 TCID50/mL), demonstrating its high sensitivity.

To identify PRRSV-1 positive samples, analytical samples were subsequently tested by RT-PCR for full-length amplification of the PRRSV-1 ORF5 gene using previously described methods [15]. Based on this finding, the pooled sample from 60-day-old pigs was selected for further investigation to characterize potentially co-infecting viruses. The 3 individual pigs that originally constituted this pool (subsequently designated as Piglet #10, #11, and #12 to ensure clarity when reporting individual results) were analyzed separately. For these individual samples, full-length ORF5 amplification and sequencing were performed for both PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2 using previously described methods [15, 32]. The obtained ORF5 gene sequences (for both PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2) were analyzed using the ISU PRRSView program (Nextclade v3 or later) for lineage classification [14, 28, 30].

Results from the diagnostic investigation of serum samples from the swine farm in Kagoshima prefecture are summarized in Table 1. The initial genotyping RT-PCR, which targets the common region for the simultaneous detection and differentiation of PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2, revealed evidence of viremia with both virus types on the farm. Specifically, three of five sow serum samples (S1, S2, S3) and two of three 30-day-old piglet serum samples (P30-1, P30-3) tested positive for both PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2. The remaining sow samples (S4, S5) and one 30-day-old piglet sample (P30-2) tested negative. Among the pooled serum samples, the 60-day-old pool (Pool-60d) was positive for both PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2. The 90-day-old pool (Pool-90d) was positive only for PRRSV-2, while the 120-day-old pool (Pool-120d) was positive only for PRRSV-1. The 150-day-old pool (Pool-150d) tested negative in this assay. Of the eight samples that tested positive in the initial genotyping RT-PCR, amplification of the PRRSV-1 ORF5 gene was successful only for the Pool-60d sample; the other seven samples were negative (Table 1). Regarding the initial genotyping RT-PCR, there were clear differences in the band intensity on the electrophoresis gel among the positive samples, suggesting that the concentration of PRRSV-1 RNA varied between samples. Specifically, Pool-60d yielded a strong and clear amplification band in the genotyping RT-PCR, whereas other positive samples exhibited considerably weaker amplification signals, suggesting lower initial viral RNA concentrations. This discrepancy is attributed to the lower viral RNA concentrations in the other samples, which were likely below the detection threshold of the ORF5-specific PCR assay. This finding, which highlighted Pool-60d as clearly positive by both PCR strategies for PRRSV-1, led to the individual analysis of the 3 piglets (Piglet #10, #11, and #12) constituting this pool. Specific RT-PCR assays for full-length ORF5 amplification were performed on these individual samples for PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2, respectively, followed by sequencing. Analysis of PRRSV-1 ORF5 showed that Piglet #11 and Piglet #12 tested positive, while Piglet #10 tested negative. For PRRSV-2 ORF5, Piglet #10 and Piglet #11 tested positive, whereas Piglet #12 tested negative (Table 2). These results confirmed a co-infection with PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2 in Piglet #11. Piglet #10 was found to be infected only with PRRSV-2, and Piglet #12 only with PRRSV-1. ORF5 gene sequences obtained from these were subjected to further genetic characterization and phylogenetic analysis. The PRRSV-1 ORF5 sequences obtained from Piglet #11 and Piglet #12 were identical, showing 100% nucleotide identity. This sequence was therefore designated as representative of the PRRSV-1 strain identified in this study (020-P4-EU). Similarly, the PRRSV-2 ORF5 sequences from Piglet #10 and Piglet #11 were 100% identical and were designated as representative of the PRRSV-2 strain identified in this study (020-P4-NA). These representative ORF5 sequences of 020-P4-EU and 020-P4-NA have been deposited in GenBank under accession numbers LC877719 (020-P4-EU) and LC877720 (020-P4-NA), respectively.

For 020-P4-EU, the ORF5 sequence was classified as lineage 1.2 (L1.2) based on the classification system by Yim-im et al. and analysis using the ISU PRRSView program [14, 28]. Notably, 020-P4-EU showed 98.68% nucleotide identity with the VP-046bis strain (GenBank accession No. GU067771.1), the live attenuated PRRSV-1 vaccine virus in Unistrain® PRRS (HIPRA, Amer, Girona, Spain), which also belongs to L1.2. To better understand the epidemiological context of PRRSV-1 in Japan, genetic comparison with previously reported domestic isolates was considered important. However, the ORF5 sequences of the first Japanese PRRSV-1 isolate, Jpn EU 4-37 in 2008, and a field strain reported in the Chugoku-Shikoku region in 2023 are not publicly available. Therefore, surrogate strains were selected based on published data [4,5,6, 17]. For Jpn EU 4-37, strain SD-02-10 (AY395081.1), reported to share 97.7% ORF5 nucleotide identity with Jpn EU 4-37, was used as a surrogate. For the Chugoku-Shikoku 2023 field strain, strain R14 (OM893841; L1.17), which was reported to share the highest similarity (97.5% identity) with that isolate, was used as a surrogate. Table 3 shows the ORF5 nucleotide sequence identities of 020-P4-EU with these surrogate strains and the vaccine strain. The identity of 020-P4-EU with SD-02-10 was 90.26%, and with R14, it was 83.33%. To further explore the genetic relationships of strain 020-P4-EU (L1.2) with these reference strains (VP-046bis, SD-02-10, R14) and other globally representative strains, a phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the ORF5 gene (Fig. 1A), and a section focusing on the L1.2 cluster was enlarged (Fig. 1B). Phylogenetic analysis showed that strain 020-P4-EU (L1.2) clustered with the VP-046bis vaccine strain (L1.2), forming a group distinct from the clusters of SD-02-10 (L1.1A) and R14 (L1.17).

For 020-P4-NA, the ORF5 sequence was classified as lineage 4 (L4) using the ISU PRRSView program. This L4 strain corresponds to Cluster III within established Japanese PRRSV-2 classification systems [29]. Cluster III has been reported as one of the major genetic groups of PRRSV-2 circulating in Japan [7, 13]. Nucleotide sequence similarity analysis using BLAST revealed that the ORF5 sequence of strain 020-P4-NA showed its highest identity (93%) with PRRSV-2 strain KU-27-711S (GenBank accession No. MT796282). Strain KU-27-711S, a Cluster III virus, was detected in Kagoshima prefecture in 2016 [2].

This study describes a case of co-infection with PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2 on a commercial swine farm in Kagoshima prefecture, Japan, and provides molecular characterization of the involved viruses. Such co-infections can complicate disease pathogenesis, affect diagnostic approaches, and create challenges for PRRS control [31]. Co-infection with PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2 has been reported at varying frequencies in many swine-producing countries, indicating it is not a rare occurrence [9, 19, 31]. For instance, in South Korea, co-infection was detected in 24.2% of PRRS-affected farms surveyed from 2013 to 2016 [9], and co-circulation of both types associated with respiratory outbreaks has been reported in Russia [19].

A key finding of this study was the molecular characterization of the PRRSV-1 strain (020-P4-EU). This strain was classified as lineage L1.2 and showed 98.68% ORF5 nucleotide identity with the VP-046bis strain of the Unistrain® PRRS vaccine, which also belongs to L1.2. Based on the criteria of Yim-im et al. (≥98% identity), 020-P4-EU is considered a vaccine-like virus [28]. Additionally, 020-P4-EU shared 99.34% identity with a Korean PRRSV-1 strain (MW717279.1), likely belonging to a vaccine-like subgroup whose prevalence has reportedly increased in Korea [11]. Yim-im et al. also reported the global distribution of L1.2, including Asia, and the frequent detection of Unistrain-like viruses [28].

Significantly, the farm in this study reported no use of PRRSV-1 vaccines and employed only PRRSV-2 vaccines. The detection of a Unistrain® PRRS vaccine-like strain in this PRRSV-1 non-vaccinated herd suggests that the strain was introduced from an external source, implying potential farm-to-farm transmission of vaccine-like viruses. This finding is consistent with a previous report from Ibaraki prefecture, Japan, which described a similar situation: a vaccine-like PRRSV-1 strain was detected in a non-vaccinated farm, with the suspected route of introduction being from vaccinated herds in the vicinity [20]. These findings, including the case in this study, highlight the risk of circulation and establishment of vaccine-like PRRSV-1 strains in non-vaccinated populations, emphasizing the need for stringent biosecurity and careful consideration of regional vaccination strategies. The PRRSV-1 strain 020-P4-EU is genetically distinct from the first Japanese PRRSV-1 field isolate (Jpn EU 4-37; surrogate SD-02-10, L1.1A) and the second reported field strain from the Chugoku-Shikoku region (surrogate R14, L1.17), indicating a complex PRRSV-1 epidemiological landscape in Japan involving diverse genetic types.

The PRRSV-2 strain identified was classified as L4 (Cluster III), a major genetic group historically circulating in Japan. Its highest ORF5 identity (93%) was observed with strain KU-27-711S, a Cluster III virus detected in Kagoshima prefecture in 2016 [2]. The occurrence of this L4/Cluster III strain in the present case, also from Kagoshima prefecture, suggests potential local endemicity.

The co-infection on this farm with a vaccine-like PRRSV-1 strain and an endemic PRRSV-2 strain illustrates the intricate virological situation. Although clinical signs on this farm were reported to be transient, the impact of co-infection can be complex. Some studies suggest that co-infection may exacerbate clinical outcomes, while others report limited effects or viral interference [8]. A limitation of this study is the lack of individual clinical records for the sampled piglets, which prevented a direct comparison of clinical signs between pigs with different infection statuses (i.e., PRRSV-1 only, PRRSV-2 only, or co-infection). Future studies involving detailed clinical monitoring of individual animals would be valuable to elucidate the specific pathological differences between these infection patterns. The genotyping RT-PCR used in this study proved useful for simultaneously detecting and differentiating both PRRSV types, underscoring the importance of such diagnostic tools for understanding co-infection dynamics. Furthermore, while the co-existence of PRRSV-1 and PRRSV-2 theoretically poses a long-term risk of inter-species recombination [21], viable and transmissible natural recombinants between these 2 species are considered extremely rare or unproven.

In conclusion, this case report provides evidence of co-infection with a vaccine-like PRRSV-1 strain (L1.2) and a PRRSV-2 L4/Cluster III strain on a PRRSV-1 non-vaccinated farm in Japan. These findings underscore the evolving PRRSV epidemiology in the country, the potential for circulation of vaccine-like strains, and the ongoing need for molecular surveillance and robust biosecurity. Further studies are needed to understand the prevalence of such co-infections and vaccine-like strains, their clinical impact, and their routes of introduction into non-vaccinated herds.
