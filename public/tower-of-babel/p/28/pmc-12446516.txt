Development and Evaluation of a Culturally Targeted Apolipoprotein L1 Counseling Training Program for Transplant Clinicians: A Pilot Study
Authors: Elisa J Gordon, Jessica Gacki-Smith, Dahlya Manning, Catherine Wicklund, Debra Duquette, Clyde W Yancy, Lutfiyya N Muhammad, Siyuan Dong, John Friedewald, Alexander Gilbert, Matthew Cooper, Justin D Smith, Aneesha Shetty, Akansha Agrawal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationale & ObjectiveApolipoprotein L1 (APOL1) genetic testing is increasingly used to evaluate potential living kidney donors (pLDs) of African ancestry. Transplant clinicians may benefit from improving knowledge and skills in delivering APOL1 counseling. This multisite study developed and evaluated the effectiveness of a culturally targeted Counseling Training Program on APOL1 and Living Donation (“Training Program”) to train transplant clinicians on APOL1 biology, genetics, and genetic counseling.Study DesignA prospective, nonrandomized pretest/posttest clinical trial.Setting & ParticipantsTransplant nephrologists, surgeons, nurses, advanced practice providers, and general nephrologists at 6 kidney transplant centers.InterventionThe training program included 8 prerecorded videos about APOL1 and living kidney donation, counseling skills, cultural sensitivity, and shared decision making.OutcomesKnowledge and self-efficacy in genetic counseling, knowledge of genetic variation, use of race in clinical decision making, and beliefs about the relationship between genetics and race, as assessed via validated surveys.Analytic ApproachPaired tests of pre–post scores from validated surveys.ResultsIn total, 35 clinicians participated; 21 were female, and 34 had no formal genetics training. Training program exposure was significantly associated with an increase in knowledge of genetic variation: pre, 3.31 (1.41); post, 3.91 (1.27), P = 0.04. Participants reported increased confidence post-training in their ability to know when the APOL1 genetic test is indicated (P = 0.001), interpret APOL1 test results (P < 0.001), and communicate about APOL1 in a culturally sensitive manner (P < 0.001). There was a nonsignificant decrease in beliefs about relationships between genetics and race from pretest to posttest (biologic domain: pre, 9.63 (3.06); post, 9.06 (2.76), P = 0.32; clinical domain, pre, 11.03 (2.22); post, 10.57 (2.63), P = 0.36).LimitationsSmall sample size and nonrandomized design.ConclusionsOur findings suggest the training program can increase transplant clinicians’ competence in counseling pLDs about APOL1 genetic testing. Training program dissemination may facilitate integration of APOL1 testing and counseling into pLD evaluation nationally.Index Words: African ancestry, donor evaluation, education, genetic counseling, genetic testing, intervention, knowledge, living kidney donor, self-efficacy

Transplant program use of Apolipoprotein L1 (APOL1) genetic testing for potential living kidney donors (pLD) of African ancestry has increased over time1 despite caution over its clinical utility given the absence of population-level data.2,3 APOL1 testing is driven largely by transplant clinicians’ desire to stratify pLDs’ risks and foster pLDs’ informed decision making about donation.4,5 Additionally, advancements in understanding the association between APOL1 risk variants and transplantation,6 promising treatment that could warrant broader application of APOL1 testing,7 and growing momentum to perform genetic testing within the field of nephrology may also propel APOL1 testing.8,9

In this context, the demand for genetic counseling will grow, intensifying the need for a partnership between genetic counselors and nephrology clinicians.10 Genetic testing typically entails pretest counseling and posttest counseling, usually performed by genetic counselors. Counseling involves education about inheritance, benefits and limitations of testing, and psychosocial implications to promote value-based decision making via shared decision making.11 Genetic counseling is effective in reducing decisional conflict, which is important because many pLDs are uncertain about donating,12,13 as well as increasing knowledge and improving accuracy of risk perceptions,14 all of which improve informed consent. Yet, relatively few genetic counselors surveyed nationally identified nephrology as one of their areas of practice (n = 91, 4%) or as their primary area of practice (n = 5, <1%).15

Transplant nephrologists have reported a lack of practical knowledge and skills in APOL1 counseling and inconsistencies in shared decision making with pLDs.2,16,17 A national survey of US nephrologists found that nephrologists’ lack of knowledge about genetics and patients’ legal protections was associated with low referral rates to genetic services.18 These findings raise ethical concerns: by ordering APOL1 tests without providing appropriate counseling, clinicians may inadvertently harm pLDs. Moreover, the lack of referral to genetic services contributes to inequitable genetic care.8

To accommodate the increased practice of and demand for genetic testing and facilitate the integration of clinical genomics across clinical practices, the National Human Genome Research Institute of the US National Institutes of Health recommends that physicians gain genomics literacy.19,20 Transplant providers have also called for the training of nephrologists to provide counseling “to improve [pLDs’] comprehension of the complex associations between high-risk genes and outcomes.”21,22 Educational programs are effective in enhancing physicians’ knowledge, preparedness, and confidence in delivering genomic services in other clinical contexts.23,24 However, an evidence-based program to train transplant nephrologists on APOL1 genetics, counseling, and biology does not currently exist.

To address this training need, we developed the Counseling Training Program on APOL1 and Living Donation (henceforth “training program”) as a clinical trial nested within a larger clinical trial, called the APOL1 Genetic Testing and Counseling Program, which was implemented in 2 large volume transplant centers. The APOL1 Genetic Testing and Counseling Program was designed to serve as a model for integrating culturally appropriate genetic counseling and genetic testing into living donor clinical evaluation, as detailed elsewhere.25 This report evaluated the effectiveness of the training program in increasing knowledge and self-efficacy in counseling about APOL1 genetic testing through a pilot study.

Methods

Intervention

The training program was designed to train transplant nephrologists about genomics and how to counsel pLDs of African ancestry about APOL1 testing in a culturally appropriate manner, without the expectation that they would obtain all skills equivalent to a genetic counselor. The training program comprised 8 prerecorded video presentations. This suite of training tools addressed the relationship between APOL1 and kidney disease as well as living donation, counseling skills, cultural sensitivity about genetics, privacy and confidentiality, ethics, and shared decision making using case studies. Because most people with APOL1 risk variants are of African ancestry, providing culturally appropriate counseling that addresses shared cultural concerns can increase pLDs’ receptivity to and maximize the effectiveness of counseling messages.26,27

The training program was adapted from genetic counseling provider education programs to the APOL1 context.23,28 Content was derived from the literature on APOL1, nephrology, and genetic testing.29, 30, 31, 32, 33 Our multidisciplinary research team of genetic counselors, transplant nephrologists, transplant surgeons, clinician experts in Black/African American health, social scientists, and ethicists developed the training program content. The total training duration was 2.5 hours. Participants had the opportunity to obtain 3.5 American Medical Association Physician’s Recognition Award Category 1 credits Continuing Medical Education (CME) and/or 2.75 American Board of Internal Medicine Maintenance of Certification (MOC) points. Participants were initially compensated $50, which was later increased to $250, for their time (prior participants were provided the full amount). The training program development (Item S1) and content (Table S1) are described in the Supplementary Material.

Study Design

In this prospective, longitudinal, nonrandomized intervention study, we used a pretest-posttest design to evaluate the effectiveness of the training program on transplant nephrologists’ APOL1 counseling knowledge and skills using online surveys. This nonrandomized clinical trial was conducted as part of a larger clinical trial to integrate APOL1 genetic testing into clinical practice. Formal power calculations were conducted for the larger clinical trial only. We used the Template for Intervention Description and Replication for quality reporting of interventions.34 The trial was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04999436-part 2).

Setting and Participants

We initially administered the training program to transplant nephrologists at 2 transplant centers (Northwestern University [NU] in Chicago, IL, and Georgetown University [GU] in Washington, DC) from July to September 2022. Because the total number of transplant nephrologists across both sites was too small for analysis, we subsequently expanded recruitment to include an additional 4 kidney transplant programs from March 2024 to January 2025. Programs were selected based on reviewing the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network/United Network for Organ Sharing database of transplant programs with a large volume of Black/African American living kidney donors, program directors known to the multiple Principal Investigators as being receptive to APOL1 genetic testing, and programs’ geographic distribution. The number of centers included was limited by grant funding. We also expanded recruitment to include transplant nurses, transplant surgeons, and general nephrologists because other transplant providers (eg, surgeons) at some transplant programs may be involved in pLDs’ evaluation clinic visits and nurses or others may be involved in providing APOL1 test results to pLDs. The study was approved by NU’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) (STU00214038) and GU’s IRB (STUDY00003752), which relied on NU’s IRB. Participants provided written electronic informed consent.

Measures

A series of validated surveys were administered online before and after the training program, including the Genetic Variation Knowledge Assessment Index (GKAI) comprising 8 true/false items.35 Scores range from 0 to 6, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge of genetic variation. The Racial Attributes in Clinical Evaluation (RACE) includes 8 5-point Likert scale items anchored by “none of the time” and “all of the time.”35 Scores range from 0 to 28, with lower scores indicating less use of race in clinical decision making. The Health Professionals Beliefs about Race (HPBR) included 9 5-point Likert scale items anchored by “strongly disagree” and “strongly agree” divided across 2 domains: biologic domain (scores range from 4 to 20) and clinical domain (scores range from 3 to 15).35 Higher scores indicate stronger beliefs about the relationship between genetics and race. Internal consistency has been previously assessed for these measures (RACE alpha = 0.86; HPBR-biologic domain alpha = 0.69; HPBR-clinical domain alpha = 0.61).35 A Cronbach’s alpha was not generated for GKAI because it does not measure an underlying construct.36

Practical Knowledge and Self-Efficacy (PKSE) in Genetic Counseling was adapted to the APOL1 context and included twelve 5-point Likert scale items anchored by “not at all confident” and “very confident” drawn from 2 sources.23,28 Scores were assessed for each item, with higher scores indicating greater confidence in genetic counseling knowledge and abilities. In addition, we developed 18 MOC multiple choice questions (score range: 0-18), with 2-3 questions per training program module, based on the training program content, per MOC requirements (Item S2). Moreover, we collected meta-data on participants’ training program use (eg, mean and range of days spent to complete training modules) as fidelity outcomes.

Data Collection

We recruited clinicians by emailing a study invitation with a link to the electronic consent form in REDCap (NU) or DocuSign (GU). The consent form stated that participants would be expected to be in the research study for 2-3 hours over 1-3 weeks, and participants were provided multiple months to participate before the training program was closed. Those who provided consent were given access to the pretest survey in REDCap. The pretest survey included 13 demographic and clinical characteristic questions. Once clinicians completed the pretest survey, they were sent a link to the training program course hosted on the NU Feinberg School of Medicine CME website. Research staff monitored participants’ progress in completing the training program course through an online dashboard made accessible by the NU CME Office. On completion, clinicians were emailed a link to the posttest survey, followed by the optional MOC survey, via REDCap. After completing the surveys, clinicians were sent an email directing them back to the CME website to complete the course evaluation and claim CME credit. Reminders were sent out every 10 days up to 5 times to encourage completion of the pretest survey, the training program modules, and/or the posttest survey.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze survey data. Continuous variables were summarized as means (standard deviations) and medians (interquartile ranges). Frequencies and percentages are reported for categorical variables. Participants’ characteristics are reported by site and overall. Statistical tests were used to compare pretest and posttest survey scores. Paired t test and Wilcoxon signed rank test was used as appropriate to compare paired continuous pretest and posttest survey scores. A power calculation based on a paired t test was conducted to determine the Cohen’s d effect size at 80% power and an alpha level of 0.05. Paired categorical pretest and posttest survey scores were assessed using McNemar’s test. PKSE Likert scale categories were combined for the use of McNemar’s test because of zero frequencies occurring in multiple Likert scale categories for the pretest and posttest survey responses. We combined “not at all confident,” “a little confident,” and “somewhat confident” into one category. Responses “fairly confident” and “very confident” were also combined into one category. An intention-to-treat analysis approach was used to conduct our statistical analysis. Because 3 participants did not fully complete the training program, we conducted a per-protocol analysis as a sensitivity analysis among those who fully completed the training program.

Stakeholder Engagement Summary

Stakeholders, including transplant nephrologists, transplant surgeons, a cardiologist with APOL1 expertise, genetic counselors, implementation scientists, and ethicists and social scientists with expertise in social dimensions of genetics and health among underrepresented populations, were involved in the design of the study and development of the training program intervention to ensure feasibility and content validity. They have also been involved in the dissemination of the study’s findings.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

A total of 205 eligible transplant clinicians from 6 transplant centers were contacted, including: transplant nephrologists (n = 33), transplant nephrology fellows (n = 5), general nephrologists (n = 81), general nephrology fellows (n = 15), transplant surgeons (n = 21), transplant surgeon fellows (n = 6), APPs (n = 15), and RNs (n = 29). Of these, 67 clinicians from 5 centers provided informed consent to participate in the training program (Fig 1). Of these, 54 completed the pretest. Of these, 45 thereafter participated at minimum by launching module 1, but not all completed the posttest. Of these, 35 participated in the training program including pretest and posttest surveys. Of the 35 participants, 32 participants fully completed all modules in the training program. With 35 participants in the training program and 32 participants who fully completed all modules in the training program, there is 80% power to detect medium Cohen’s d effect sizes of 0.49 and 0.51, respectively, using a paired t test at an alpha level of 0.05.

Most participants reported having had no formal genetics training (97.14%, 34/35), ordered genetic tests (85.71%, 30/35), and consulted a genetic testing laboratory (48.57%, 17/35) 1 or more times per year. Relationship Between Training Program Exposure and Beliefs about Genetics and Race

Associations of the training program exposure with beliefs about the relationship between genetics and race were evaluated (Table 2). Specifically, average GKAI scores significantly increased between pretest and posttest (P = 0.04), indicating greater knowledge of genetic variation, as intended. HPBR-biologic domain and HPBR-clinical domain scores decreased (P = 0.3 and P = 0.4, respectively), indicating slightly weaker beliefs about the relationship between genetics and race, as intended. RACE scores slightly but not significantly increased between pretest and posttest (P = 0.8), indicating greater use of race in clinical decision making, not as intended. In our sensitivity analysis among participants who fully completed the training program, we observed consistent findings for the associations of program exposure with beliefs about the relationship between genetics and race (Table S2).

CME and MOC

Of the 35 participants, 30 (85.7%) claimed CME credit. Of the 32 physician participants, 24 responded to the MOC questions, and 22 requested MOC credit. The mean total MOC score was 15.85 (standard deviation [SD]: 2.51) (actual score range: 11-18) (Table 4). Several MOC questions were answered correctly by all participants including: “The presence of two APOL1 risk variants is associated with which health outcome?”, and “What treatment is available for people with two APOL1 risk variants?” (Table 5). The 2 MOC questions that most participants answered incorrectly included: “What are the likely outcomes for living donors who undergo APOL1 genetic testing?” and “Which of the following is untrue regarding race and genomic markers of disease?” We saw similar trends of correct responses in the sensitivity analysis (Table S4).

Discussion

Through a multisite evaluation, our novel Counseling Training Program on APOL1 and Living Donation intervention effectively increased knowledge and skills about APOL1 genetic counseling and testing among transplant clinicians. Transplant clinicians’ increased confidence in knowing when the APOL1 genetic test is indicated and in interpreting APOL1 test results holds promise for strengthening clinicians’ competence in counseling pLDs about APOL1 genetic testing on a broader scale. Scaling the training program nationally will be important because of the heightened attention to genetic testing in the transplant nephrology context.9

Barriers to the clinical integration of genomic medicine (counseling and testing) include providers’ lack of knowledge and preparedness to provide genetic services.37, 38, 39 Our training program demonstrated that it can surmount these barriers and thereby serve as an effective strategy for integrating genetic testing into clinical practice.40,41 With promising therapies for APOL1 risk variants on the horizon,7 transplant clinicians must become well-prepared to use genetic testing in the care and clinical evaluation of chronic kidney disease patients, transplant recipients, and living donor candidates. Further, the general principles learned about counseling for APOL1 can be applied to other conditions (eg, polycystic kidney disease).

A major finding was that training program exposure was significantly associated with increased knowledge of genetic variation. A nonsignificant trend also emerged indicating fewer beliefs about the relationship between genetics and race. Studies document that physicians’ beliefs about how race is related to genetics may perpetuate racialized medicine.35,42 Our study findings are important for highlighting how our training program can effectively dismantle “race”-based care or “racialized medicine.”43

Another key finding was that the training program increased transplant clinicians’ confidence in communicating about APOL1 in a culturally appropriate manner. This impact is important because nephrologists report difficulties building trust with ethnic minorities.44 Given that many individuals of African ancestry share cultural concerns about genetic testing (eg, concerns about misuse of testing, mistrust in the health care system),45,46 our intervention is promising for improving pLD clinical evaluation.

Our study has practical implications for transplant clinical practice, enhancing clinician–pLD communication and evaluation, and increasing pLDs’ access to genetic testing and counseling. Specifically, implementing the training program may overcome documented nephrologists’ barriers to genetic testing by providing education around interpreting and using genetic tests in counseling,16,17 and overcome documented nephrologists’ difficulties building trust with and counseling ethnic minorities44,47 by providing skills in counseling pLDs about APOL1 in a culturally sensitive manner. Accordingly, by promoting transplant clinicians’ training in genetic testing, our training program may help pLDs, and possibly their family members and transplant recipients, to gain equitable access to genetic testing and faster access to personalized care.8,48 Moreover, our study offers preliminary empirical support for implementing genomic testing in a multidisciplinary fashion.16,17

Ongoing research will evaluate our training program as a novel and ethical way to rapidly scale up APOL1 genetic testing nationally to stratify pLDs’ risks postdonation and enhance informed consent. Moreover, future research will evaluate implementation strategies for administering the training program to transplant clinicians with higher uptake. Future research should replicate the study in larger samples. We anticipate updating the training program content to incorporate advancements in scholarship relating to APOL1 and living donation.

Study strengths include multisite research involving participants nationally, supporting generalizability of study findings. Our inclusion of nurses, advanced practice providers, and surgeons in the sample supports the feasibility of involving a broader range of transplant clinicians in the APOL1 counseling process, and aligns with a recommended multidisciplinary approach to genetic counseling.8 The fact that nearly all participants claimed CME credit and most claimed MOC credit indicated that accreditation of this training program was a useful incentive for participation.

This study has some limitations. The small size of clinicians from each specialty precludes comparisons between provider types. It is unknown whether participants differed from non-participants demographically. Another limitation is the low participation rate despite providing ample time and reminders to complete the training program, providing CME and MOC credits, and ample compensation, most of which comprise documented implementation strategies.49 It is unknown why more clinicians did not participate. However, at the 2 study sites (NU and GU), having leadership buy-in was a strategy that likely contributed to greater participation. Although some clinicians provided informed consent (n = 67) indicating their interest in the training program, only half (n = 32) fully completed the training program, suggesting that clinicians’ competing time commitments may have been a limiting factor. If time constraints were a determining factor to training program participation, then we anticipate further challenges with clinician engagement given the workforce shortage in transplant nephrology. This possibility is disconcerting given the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education’s and the transplant field’s calls for training clinicians in genetics and raises a critical question about how transplant providers or other medical subspecialties can be effectively mobilized to undergo genetic training. Crosstraining nursing staff to fill multiple roles in the counseling process may decrease transplant nephrologists’ responsibility to perform all counseling. Crosstraining can be an effective strategy for supporting a flexible health workforce.50 Another strategy is to train more genetic counselors in nephrology to facilitate streamlined nephrologist–counselor communication.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest the Counseling Training Program on APOL1 and Living Donation holds promise for increasing transplant clinicians’ competence in counseling pLDs of African ancestry about APOL1 genetic testing. Our training program may serve as an ethical way to stratify pLDs’ risks and enhance their informed consent for APOL1 genetic testing.
