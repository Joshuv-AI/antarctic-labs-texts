A novel robotic-assisted lung lobectomy simulation model.

Central Message

Our lung lobectomy model, featuring vascular distention, heartbeat, and simulated bleeding with a blood substitute, was rated highly realistic and educationally valuable by both trainees and faculty.

Perspective

Robotic-assisted surgery is the most common approach for anatomic lung resections, yet more than one half of graduating residents feel undertrained. Although simulations are recommended, existing models are inaccessible or lack fidelity. We developed a model rated highly for fidelity by trainees and faculty, offering a cost-effective and reproducible training tool for individual program implementation.

Surgical simulation is a well-established educational tool, with validated evidence supporting its effectiveness of skill transfer to the clinical setting.1 This is critical for high-risk procedures like anatomic lung resections, in which operative opportunities may be limited and errors can have life-threatening consequences. In this context, the traditional Halstedian model of “see one, do one, teach one” is increasingly inadequate, particularly for newer approaches that demand a distinct skill set.2

The robotic-assisted approach has now surpassed video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery as the most common technique for anatomic lung resections in thoracic surgery.3 Reflecting this trend, 90% of thoracic surgery training programs responding to a 2024 survey reported wanting to hire a partner competent in robotic surgery.4 However, a 2016 survey found that more than one half of the graduating thoracic surgery residents expressed a need for additional training in robotic thoracic procedures.5 To address this gap, the Society of Thoracic Surgeons Task Force on Robotic Thoracic Surgery, in collaboration with the Workforce on E-learning and Education Innovation, assembled an expert group to develop recommendations for a standardized national robotic curriculum for thoracic surgery trainees.6 The resulting consensus comprised 12 key recommendations, including an annual wet laboratory simulation session, bedside assist experience, and emergency conversion training. Currently, most thoracic surgery training programs rely on industry-sponsored sessions and societal bootcamps or fellowships for robotic lung lobectomy simulation experience.7,8 Although they offer a high-fidelity model, they are not well described or commercially available, limiting access because of their high cost and one-time experience.4,9 In contrast, more cost-effective lung lobectomy simulation models have been described, but they often fall short in replicating the complexity of the real operative experience, thereby limiting skill transfer and educational value.9, 10, 11, 12, 13

Therefore, we developed a reproducible, cost-effective robotic-assisted lung lobectomy simulation model featuring high-fidelity elements including vascular distention, a dynamic operative field with a simulated heartbeat, and an artificial blood substitute capable of simulating bleeding for emergency conversion training. Our goal was to create an adoptable training tool for individual programs, enabling surgeons at all levels to learn and refine their techniques without having to rely on societal courses.

Methods

Porcine Block Preparation

Porcine heart-lung blocks with intact pulmonary vessels, aorta, and trachea were obtained from Animal Technologies (Animal Technologies). To prepare for a left upper lobectomy, right-sided vessels were dissected and ligated with 2-0 ties, the right-sided airways were transected, and a right pneumonectomy was completed (Figure 1). This was done to isolate the blood substitute, allowing drainage by gravity exclusively through the left-sided vessels. Although a right-sided lobectomy is technically feasible, the porcine left lung more closely resembles human pulmonary anatomy as the porcine right upper lobe bronchus originates directly from the trachea above the carina (known as pig bronchus), making anatomic dissection less realistic. The inferior vena cava was ligated with 2-0 silk ties, and both the superior vena cava and left atrium were cannulated with a Y-connector to serve as the inflow for the blood substitute, preserving the pulmonary artery and vein for dissection. To obtain an unobstructed surgical field, the Y-connector was positioned posterior to the pulmonary artery trunk. The proximal aorta was tied off to maintain the blood substitute within the pulmonary vasculature and prevent forward flow through the aorta. Importantly, the azygous vein should be dissected distally along the aorta and ligated; otherwise, leakage of the blood substitute will occur.

Blood Substitute Preparation

The blood substitute was prepared by mixing 500 cc of warm water with 3 to 4 tablespoons of carboxymethylcellulose (CMC), adding more CMC as needed until the blood-like consistency was achieved. CMC increases viscosity, thereby reducing parenchymal edema by slowing diffusion during perfusion. Once the mixture reached the desired consistency and the CMC had fully dissolved, one half a tablespoon of red dye powder was added. An empty 500-cc saline bag was then filled with the artificial blood substitute, connected to an intravenous (IV) drip tubing kit with the roller clamp initially closed, and attached to the Y-connector cannulated to the block.

Platform, Linear Actuator and Docking

The prepared block was placed on a plastic utility crate and covered by a 40-gallon trash bag, ensuring the apex of the heart was positioned over the point of the 12-V linear actuator (Figure 2). The linear actuator converts electrical motor output into linear motion, producing back-and-forth movement along a single axis—similar to a piston—which simulates the rhythmic pulsatile forces of a heartbeat. The trachea and apex of the heart were secured onto the platform with suture and the da Vinci Xi robotic system was docked over the model. The IV roller clamp was released to allow gravity drainage of the blood substitute (no specific flow rate), and the linear actuator was set to produce a heart rate of 70 to 80 beats per minute.

Material Costs

Nearly all components of the model are reusable, except for the heart and lung blocks ($50/block; Animal Technologies) and staple reloads (donated by Intuitive Surgical). The plastic utility crate ($5, Target), 12-V linear actuator ($40, Amazon) and tubing were reused through model development and formal simulation labs. Any Y-connector compatible with IV drip tubing and a roller clamp may be used. One-time purchases of 4 oz. CMC ($10, The Michaels Companies) and scarlet Rit dye powder ($6, Amazon) produced enough blood substitute for all 30 sessions, including model development. When amortized over these sessions, the estimated material cost per simulation is approximately $53. Reusable materials showed minimal evidence of wear by the end of the study, suggesting costs would decrease with additional sessions.

Study Participants and Workflow

Study participants included cardiothoracic integrated residents, general surgery residents, cardiac and thoracic fellows, and general thoracic faculty at our academic tertiary care hospital. All were recruited by e-mail. The institutional review board or equivalent ethics committee of Stanford University approved the study protocol (76506) on August 29, 2024. The patient(s) provided informed written consent for the publication of the study data.

Over a 4-month period, participants were scheduled in pairs (on the basis of availability) for a 90- to 120-minute session. At the start of each session, participants completed the presimulation survey, after which a member of the study team introduced the lung lobectomy model and outlined the simulation objectives. While 1 participant operated at the console, performing the 3 key steps of the simulation—dissection and stapling of the left superior pulmonary vein, left superior pulmonary artery, and left upper bronchus—the other participant served as the bedside assistant. If participants did not injure a vessel during the simulation, several were asked to intentionally enter the pulmonary artery or vein to simulate bleeding events. A postsimulation was then completed.

Survey Tool

The model was evaluated using pre- and postsimulation surveys in which participants rated their confidence and assessed the simulation across domains, including fidelity, key features, and overall value as a training tool, using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). Both trainees and faculty completed the same presimulation survey (Figure E1). The postsimulation survey consisted of 14 questions for trainees (Figure E2) and 12 questions for faculty (Figure E3). All participants were also asked to recommend the optimal frequency of laboratory participation and invited to provide qualitative feedback.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Continuous data are presented as mean (± standard deviation) or median (interquartile range, IQR), and categorical data are presented as frequency and proportions. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to evaluate the difference in pre- and postsimulation confidence scores. Data analysis was performed using R, version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, 2025).

Results

Participant Characteristics

Of the 20 participants, there were 15 trainees and 5 faculty (Table 1). Among trainees, 5 (33%) were female and 9 (60%) were postgraduate year (PGY) 4 or greater. There were 2 (13%) general surgery residents, 10 (67%) integrated cardiothoracic residents, 2 (13%) thoracic surgery fellows, and 1 (6.7%) cardiac surgery fellow. Trainees reported a wide range of robotic experience, with a median of 26 [IQR, 15-48] bedside assist and 5 [IQR, 2-23] console cases.

Model Fidelity and Confidence

Of the 9 features evaluated, trainees rated 8 (89%) features highly for fidelity with a mean score of 4 or greater. Bleeding received a score of 4.0 ± 0.8, whereas stapling experience (4.7 ± 0.4), lung tissue handling (4.7 ± 0.5), and lung tissue quality (4.5 ± 0.7) were among the greatest-rated features. Other highly rated features included airway dissection (4.4 ± 0.5), airway tissue quality (4.3 ± 0.6), vascular tissue quality (4.3 ± 0.6), and heartbeat (4.1 ± 0.9). Vascular distention received the lowest rating (3.9 ± 0.9). Overall, trainees found the simulation model highly valuable as an educational tool (4.5 ± 0.6) and sufficient for learning robotic lung lobectomy (4.7 ± 0.4) (Table 2). Trainee's confidence significantly improved after the simulation, from 2.5 presession to 3.9 postsession (P = .0014; Figure 3) and 80% preferred the laboratory to be held twice annually.

Subjective Responses

Regarding the most helpful aspects of the simulation, trainees highlighted the value of “getting time on the console with a moving target” and the “realistic vascular distention and dissection.” One trainee commented that the “bronchial vessels had some bleeding, which was very realistic” and that when bleeding was encountered “it flowed just like blood until we were able to find the injury.” Faculty emphasized practicing “dissection and encircling structures,” and noted that the most beneficial aspect was the “experience operating the robot and doing all aspects of the lobe,” adding that “most residents do not get to experience this in the real operating room.” One senior surgeon noted that the “this simulation is a great tool for non-robotic faculty surgeons to practice prior to their proctored sessions with real patients.”

Regarding areas for improvement, while the model was in its earliest phase of testing, trainees recommended to find “ways to get the vessels to plump up more” and for a “stronger heartbeat.” Other suggestions included the ability to “flip the lung to evaluate the posterior hilum” and to “incorporate taking down the inferior pulmonary ligament and developing a fissure.” Faculty noted “noticeable difference during dissection without insufflation” and recommended providing a “tutorial on expected porcine anatomy to maximize the benefit” of the simulation model.

Discussion

Our lung lobectomy model, featuring vascular distention, a dynamic operative field with a simulated heartbeat, and an artificial blood substitute capable of continuous bleeding, was rated as highly realistic and educationally valuable by both trainees and faculty. Importantly, the model is straightforward to construct and can be reliably reproduced using the described materials and steps, making it a practical training tool without having to rely on societal courses.

In developing this model, our initial task was to determine an effective method of cannulation that would provide reliable vascular distention to enable realistic dissection, while also creating a system capable of simulating active bleeding when vessels were injured. Although the model commonly used during societal courses have these features, it is not described in detail or commercially available making it inaccessible.9 Previously described models have achieved vascular distention; however, our approach—combining a novel cannulation site and blood substitute mixture—avoids premature dissection through structures like the pulmonary artery or vein, preservation of the heart and pericardium and enables continuous bleeding.12,13 Although a closed-circuit perfusion system with pulsatile flow would have offered the most realistic simulation, it proved unfeasible due to the compromised nature of previously frozen tissue. As a result, an inflow-only system was established and made feasible due to the blood mixture itself.

The next challenge was to develop a blood substitute that mimicked the flow characteristics of blood but resisted rapid diffusion into the parenchyma. An initial mixture of tap water and red dye led to significant parenchymal edema and fluid leakage within minutes of inflow by gravity drainage. To address this, CMC was added to warm water and after several up titrations, the mixture achieved an appropriately balanced viscosity. Participants took between 30 and 90 minutes to complete all three steps of the simulation, and even in the longest sessions, the lung parenchyma remained free of edema. Open-ended vessels, such as the draining azygous vein and vessels injured during organ procurement or block preparation, should be identified to prevent fluid leakage. Although not all participants encountered bleeding during the simulation, those who did described its’ continuous flow as highly realistic and noted that it effectively simulated the stress of a true intraoperative emergency. When bleeding occurred, participants achieved control using rolled gauze, followed by a discussion on management strategies and how to effectively convert emergently—aligning with the seventh recommendation from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons task force.6

Lastly, our novel heartbeat was generated using a single device. The device is placed beneath the utility crate with the apex of the heart overlying it, without the need to perform further dissection through the heart or requiring multiple components, as previously described.13 This device is small and has proved durable over the course of 30 uses. The heartbeat, initially perceived as too subtle by participants, was easily enhanced by raising the platform as shown in Figure 2, A, elevating the actuator to create a more pronounced effect and can easily simulate tachycardia with its adjustable speeds.

Although our model was rated highly, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, implementation of the robotic simulation relied on support from the Irwin Foundation and equipment donations from Intuitive Surgical. However, the high-fidelity model is adaptable and can be used to simulate other surgical approaches such as video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery or open. Second, this single-institution study also had a small sample size, although it included a diverse group of learners—from PGY1 to PGY9—and faculty with varying experience levels, from routine robotic surgeons to those less familiar with robotic lung lobectomy. Lastly, the model has not undergone formal validation or been directly compared to existing simulation platforms.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this model demonstrated high fidelity and value by both trainees and faculty and significantly improved trainee confidence. The next step is broader implementation, as the model's construction is simple and reproducible. In addition, nearly all components of the model are reusable, apart from the heart-lung blocks and staple reloads. Widespread adoption will allow for formal validation and evaluation of broader educational impact. Importantly, this model has potential for simulating disaster scenarios and can be further modified to create more challenging dissections.
