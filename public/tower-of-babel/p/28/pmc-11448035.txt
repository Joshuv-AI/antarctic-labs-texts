The impact of hepatitis B surface antigen seroconversion on the durability of functional cure induced by pegylated interferon alpha treatment
Authors: Wencong Zhang, Jia Chen, Wenjin Sun, Nana Xie, Fangbing Tian, Qiurong Ruan, Jianxin Song
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) loss is regarded as a pivotal criterion for assessing functional cure in patients diagnosed chronic hepatitis B (CHB). We conducted the research to investigate the real-world performance of HBsAg seroconversion in sustaining HBsAg loss.MethodsThis retrospective analysis confirmed 295 patients who attained HBsAg loss through combination therapy involving nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs) and pegylated interferon alpha (peg-IFNα). Employing Kaplan–Meier estimates method to conduct survival analysis. The forest plot was used to visualize the results of multivariate Cox regression, and selected variables were included in the nomogram.ResultsHBsAg seroreversion was observed in 45 patients during follow-up periods, with a lower recurrence risk in patients with HBsAg seroconversion at the end of peg-IFNα therapy (EOT) (10.3% vs 37.3% at 96-week, P < 0.0001). Moreover, the sustainability of hepatitis B surface antibody (anti-HBs) in participants continuing therapy after HBsAg seroconversion was superior to those discontinued prematurely (72.5% vs 54.5% at 96 weeks, P = 0.012). Additionally, the former group was also relatively less likely to experience HBsAg reversion during long-term observation (8.4% vs 14.3% at 96 weeks, P = 0.280). Hepatitis B envelope antigen (HBeAg) status, anti-HBs status and consolidation treatment screened by multivariable analysis were utilized to construct a predictive model for HBsAg reversion. The concordance index(C-index = 0.77) and calibration plots indicated satisfactory discrimination and consistency of nomogram.ConclusionsHBsAg seroconversion was beneficial for sustaining functional cure in patients treated with peg-IFNα. Continuing consolidation therapy after HBsAg seroconversion also contributed to maintain HBsAg seroconversion and improve the durability of HBsAg loss. The nomogram illustrated its efficacy as a valuable instrument in showcasing survival probability of functional cure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-024-02522-8.

Introduction

It is widely recognized that continuous replication of hepatitis B virus (HBV) can bring about host immunosuppression, which eventually exacerbates the progression of liver diseases. Fortunately, current clinical practice has witnessed the widespread utilization of safe and effective medications (eg., nucleos(t)ide analogues [NAs] and interferonα [IFNα]) in persistent virus infection.

Multitudinous researches have unequivocally demonstrated that the high potency of antiviral therapy in mitigating the risk of hepatocirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) associated with HBV infection [1, 2]. Importantly, individuals who achieved HBsAg loss exhibited even more pronounced reduction in HCC incidence [3–5]. No discernible disparity was also observed in the development of HCC between patients with spontaneous and NA-experienced HBsAg seroclearance, thereby underscoring its paramount significance for long-term clinical outcomes [6]. Consequently, recent studies have shifted their concern from mere suppression of viral load to proactive clearance of viral antigen. While spontaneous or NA-induced antigen clearance rarely occurred. It was not until the introduction of IFNα regimen that we could observe a higher proportion of antigen elimination in the short-term periods. Presently, IFNα is the significant alternative for combating chronic HBV infection [7, 8]. Previous research has shown that interferon-based therapy significantly improved the possibility of HBsAg loss compared to NAs monotherapy [9]. As expected, we observed early satisfactory clinical efficacy with peg-IFNα in patients with inactive HBsAg carriers (IHCs) and chronic hepatitis B (CHB); more than 30% achieved HBsAg loss [10]. Be worth being that IFNα also exhibited excellent performance in preventing the onset of liver cancer [2, 11], further substantiating its superiority in managing CHB patients.

In current treatment approaches, functional cure (characterized by the HBsAg loss with undetectable HBV DNA) is attainable, particularly in patients receiving IFNα therapy. However, the major obstacle to complete eradication of HBV has been the inability to eliminate covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) residing within the nuclei of infected hepatocytes [7, 12]. The persistent presence of cccDNA reservoir allows for reactivation of HBV infection in previously cured individuals, causing HBsAg or HBV DNA become detectable [13]. Until now, IFNα has been the only potential candidate with the capacity of eliminating cccDNA [14]. Given the limited evidence from current researches, more explorations are warranted to validate existing findings regarding the durability of HBsAg loss, especially after longer observation periods. Furthermore, it is essential to investigate whether HBsAg seroconversion can affect subsequent recurrence.

We conducted this real-world study on NA-experienced patients who have cleared HBsAg through peg-IFNα-2b therapy, our objectives were to assess the perdurability of HBsAg loss and to seek the association between HBsAg seroconversion and HBsAg seroreversion. We intended to establish a predictive model based on multivariate analysis and utilized a nomogram approach to evaluate the survival probability of HBsAg loss as well.

Materials and methods

Patients

In our study, we retrospectively gathered clinical data from patients who obtained HBsAg clearance based on peg-IFNα-2b therapy at Tongji Hospital, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, and Ezhou central hospital, from July 2019 to June 2024. All individuals received NAs therapy for a minimum of 6 months before adding peg-IFNα-2b regimen (180ug, subcutaneous injection, weekly, no less than 24 weeks). After achieving HBsAg seroclearance, most patients with good compliance were administered HBV vaccination (standard dose of 60ug, intramuscular injection, monthly) to induce production of anti-HBs. Adjusted the vaccination dosage according to the variation of anti-HBs titre and stopped vaccination at EOT. Participants were subjected to monitoring every 3 months in the initial year, followed by subsequent monitoring every 6 months. The inclusion criteria consisted of confirmed HBsAg loss (HBsAg < 0.05 IU/mL), a follow-up period of at least 12 weeks, and no restriction on HBeAg status before combination therapy. Exclusion criteria included decompensated liver cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, autoimmune liver diseases, coinfection with other viruses such as hepatitis C virus (HCV), hepatitis D virus (HDV), or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). The study was granted approval by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital (TJ-IRB20220929) of Huazhong University of Science and Technology.

Laboratory measurements

Serum HBsAg, anti-HBs and HBeAg was measured using Elecsys HBsAg II quant II, Elecsys Anti-HBs II, and Elecsys HBeAg (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany), respectively. HBsAg negativity was set at HBsAg levels < 0.05 IU/mL, and anti-HBs positivity was set at anti-HBs levels ≥ 10 IU/L. The Cobas TaqMan assay (Roche Diagnostics, Branchburg, NJ) was used to test serum HBV DNA level, with the lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ) of HBV DNA was 20 IU/mL.

Definitions and endpoints

The follow-up period commenced upon discontinuation of peg-IFNα therapy and extended until the HBsAg seroreversion. Patients who did not experience HBsAg reversion would continue to be observed for any potential changes. Sustained HBsAg loss referred to undetectable HBsAg at any time during follow-up observation. Confirmed HBsAg loss was characterized as at least two consecutive negative results during peg-IFNα treatment (excluding cases in which HBsAg reversed to positivity at EOT) or at least two consecutive negative results within 12 weeks after withdrawal (including one negative result when treatment stopped). HBsAg seroreversion was defined as the reappearance of HBsAg during observation period after EOT. HBsAg seroconversion was characterized by undetectable HBsAg (HBsAg < 0.05 IU/mL) and detectable anti-HBs (≥ 10 IU/L). The presence of anti-HBs indicated a positive anti-HBs result at EOT, while the absence of anti-HBs signified a negative anti-HBs result at EOT.

Statistical analysis

Median with interquartile range (IQR) was to present continuous variables, while counts and percentages were used to express categorical variables. To compare continuous variables in both groups, we applied with the Mann–Whitney U test; for categorical variables, we employed the Chi-squared test. The Kaplan–Meier method estimated the cumulative rates, followed by a Log-rank test to compare the differences between the different groups. Cox regression analysis was performed to evaluate the indicators associated with HBsAg reversion. The nomogram was used to calculate the survival probabilities of predictive model. The statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.2 and Graph Pad Prism version 9.5.0, with two-tailed tests at a significance level of 0.05.

Results

Clinical features of registered patients

We counted 403 patients receiving NAs and peg-IFNα-2b combination therapy, while 88 were excluded from the analysis on account of unavailable data, such as loss to follow-up, missing data, failure to complete or adhere to scheduled visits, and still under treatment. Besides, 6 were excluded because HBsAg reversed to positivity at EOT, and 14 were excluded as unconfirmed HBsAg loss. The flow chart illustrating case screening was presented in Fig. 1. Ultimately, a cohort of 295 patients were collected in this analysis. The median (IQR) age was 41(34–47) years old. Prior to adding peg-IFNα therapy, HBeAg negativity was observed in 239 patients. At EOT, the median (IQR) levels of ALT and AST were recorded as follows: ALT 28(20–40) U/L and AST 35(27–51) U/L, respectively. All included patients who lost HBsAg on treatment sustained functional cure before stopping peg-IFNα therapy. A majority of patients (66.4%, n = 196/295) attained HBsAg seroconversion though peg-IFNα add-on therapy. In patients with sustained HBsAg loss, 72.4% occurred HBsAg seroconversion, while this percentage was 33.3% in those with HBsAg reversion, P < 0.001. HBsAg level before combination therapy was lower in the group with sustain HBsAg loss than the group with HBsAg reversion(P = 0.015). The consolidation duration was also longer in the former than the latter, displaying a significant difference of consolidation time in both groups (P < 0.001). Serum HBsAg seroconversion

The proportion of HBsAg seroconversion performed well during peg-IFNα treatment. Over half of the patients fulfilled the positive anti-HBs before the finish of combination therapy. Actually, the formation of anti-HBs did not occur concurrently with HBsAg clearance in all cured patients. Among the entire population, 80 individuals achieved HBsAg seroconversion at the moment of HBsAg loss, while 116 acquired this outcome following the subsequent consolidation treatment. The final results manifested the sustained positive rates of anti-HBs were 80.1% and 66.5% after 48-week and 96-week observation periods (Fig. 2A). Further analysis showed no significant difference in terms of durability of anti-HBs with respect to the timing of appearance for HBsAg seroconversion (concurrence with HBsAg loss: 79.6% and 63.9% at 48 and 96 weeks; occurrence after consolidation treatment: 80.4% and 68.3% at the same time points, P = 0.577) (Fig. 2A).Fig. 2The proportion and durability of HBsAg seroconversion based on peg-IFNα therapy. The proportion of HBsAg seroconversion through consolidation therapy (with or without HBV vaccination) increased to 61.4% (116/189), which was evidently greater than the rate documented at the time of HBsAg loss (27.1%, 80/295), P < 0.0001(Fig. 2B). Vaccination against HBV at the same time as consolidation therapy tended to increase the proportion of HBsAg seroconversion before discontinuation. The positive rate of anti-HBs was 63.3% in patients receiving at least 12 weeks of consolidation therapy and no less than 3 doses of vaccination. With the extension of treatment time and the increase of vaccination frequency, this rate also evidently improved (consolidation time ≥ 24 weeks with ≥ 3 doses of vaccine: 75.3%; consolidation time ≥ 24 weeks with ≥ 6 doses of vaccine: 80.0%). Statistical differences were marked in the Fig. 2C.

Continuation therapy after HBsAg seroconversion

We conducted the research to investigate whether continuation treatment with peg-IFNα in patients who attained HBsAg seroconversion on therapy could contribute to enhancing the stability of anti-HBs levels. Among the group of patients who had already acquired anti-HBs, 129 individuals were administered consolidation therapy for a minimum duration of 12 weeks. Prior to treatment discontinuation, 62.8% of these individuals achieved a high level of anti-HBs (≥ 100 IU/L). In contrast, among those who achieved HBsAg seroconversion but did not continue with further treatment, only 34.3% reached an anti-HBs level ≥ 100 IU/L (P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 1). There was a notable difference in sustainability of anti-HBs levels between continuation treatment and premature discontinuation after HBsAg seroconversion. The survival probabilities of anti-HBs after EOT until available visits in the former group were 86.9% and 72.5% at 48 and 96 weeks of follow-up periods respectively, while were only 66.2% and 54.5% in the latter group (P = 0.012) (Fig. 3).Fig. 3The comparison of durability of HBsAg seroconversion during observation periods. The incidence of HBsAg seroreversion

In this study, 45 (15.3%) individuals who attained HBsAg clearance experienced reversion to HBsAg positivity. Of these patients, the median time of HBsAg reversion was 24(24–48) weeks (38 of 45 occurred events within 48-week follow-up), with a downward tendency in reversal probability after subsequent observation. And sure enough, our study found that patients who maintained negative HBsAg status for more than 2 years rarely experienced relapse events during subsequent follow-up. Only 2 cases were tested positive serum HBsAg after more than 24 months according to our results. The Kaplan–Meier estimated rates of HBsAg reversion were calculated in our analysis. During the first year of follow-up, only 15.1% of individuals occurred HBsAg reversion. Comparison of HBsAg seroreversion at different subgroup levels

We draw a comparative analysis to explore the discrepancy in HBsAg reversion between the two subgroups: one with HBsAg seroconversion (Anti-HBs presence at EOT) and another without seroconversion (Anti-HBs absence at EOT). Our findings revealed a significantly statistical disparity in reversion incidence between them. Only 5.9% and 10.3% of patients occurred recurrence events at 48-week and 96-week observation periods in anti-HBs presence group, respectively. While for anti-HBs absence group, the cumulative recurrence rates were 35.5% and 37.3% at the same time, respectively (Fig. 5A). To determine whether consolidation treatment conferred advantages in maintaining HBsAg loss, we categorized patients into the two groups: one with a consolidation time within 12 weeks (included patients without consolidation therapy), and another exceeding 12 weeks. The results showed the group with longer consolidation time exhibited better durability at each time point, with cumulative recurrence rates of 7.7% and 10.6% at 48-weeks and 96-week follow-up, respectively. Conversely, the group with shorter consolidation time displayed higher recurrence rates of 24.8% and 29.0% at the same time points. Significant difference was uncovered in regard to durability of HBsAg loss between the two groups (Fig. 5B).

As previously described, continuing treatment after HBsAg seroconversion was conductive to improve the durability of HBsAg seroconversion during follow-up periods. We further explored its impact on sustainability of HBsAg loss. The results indicated that patients who continued to receive peg-IFNα treatment for 12 weeks or more after HBsAg seroconversion were less prone to experience HBsAg reversion compared to those who discontinued treatment prematurely (5.1% vs 7.6% at 48 weeks, 8.4% vs 14.3% at 96 weeks, P = 0.280) (Fig. 5C).

Except for the above mentioned, the effects of HBeAg status and HBsAg level before combination therapy on sustainability of HBsAg loss were also discussed in this study. For the former, the rate of HBsAg reversion after 96-week follow-up were 16.4% and 31.8% in HBeAg negativity and HBeAg positivity patients (P = 0.145), respectively. For the latter, 13.7% experienced HBsAg reversion in patients with HBsAg level < 100 IU/mL, while 23.2% in those with HBsAg level ≥ 100 IU/mL, P = 0.076. These were visualized in Fig. 5D&E.

Cox analysis for predictors in HBsAg seroreversion

In a univariate regression result pertaining to indictors for predicting HBsAg reversion, anti-HBs status (HR = 0.19, 95%CI = 0.10–0.36, P < 0.001), and consolidation therapy > 12 weeks after HBsAg loss (HR = 0.29, 95%CI = 0.15–0.55, P < 0.001) were observably related to the durable HBsAg loss. It was important to highlight that the findings from the multivariable regression analysis indicated that HBeAg status (negativity vs positivity: HR = 0.42, 95%CI = 0.21–0.85, P = 0.016), anti-HBs status (presence vs absence: HR = 0.21, 95%CI = 0.11–0.40, P < 0.001), and Consolidation therapy (> 12 weeks vs ≤ 12 weeks: HR = 0.48, 95%CI = 0.24–0.93, P = 0.030) were as independent factors for predicting HBsAg reversion (Fig. 6A&B).Fig. 6The establishment of predictive model for HBsAg reversion. We employed a multivariate analysis approach to screen variables for the construction of prediction model, and utilized nomogram to visually display survival probability (Fig. 6C). HBeAg status (negativity or positivity), anti-HBs status (presence or absence) and consolidation therapy (> 12 weeks or ≤ 12 weeks) were selected for predicting survival probability. The concordance index (C-index) of the nomogram was 0.77 (95%CI = 0.70–0.84). Calibration curves showed the better calibration ability between the predicted and actually observed survival probability in the model (Fig. 6D-G).

Discussion

In the absence of additional intervention, the body mainly relies on its own immune defense function to eliminate virus. If the immune system fails to recognize and contain the infection, regardless of acquisition from exogenously or endogenously, there can undergo a substantial amplification in viral load, ultimately compromising the host's immune function and resulting in various hepatic complications. Therefore, our treatment focused on controlling the infection and striving for maximum possible elimination of the virus to improve the prognosis. Despite inadequacy in available treatment strategies, the relentless pursuit of optimal benefits for patients remains ongoing.

It is universally acknowledged that the formation of cccDNA pool and the integration of viral genetic material into host genome are responsible for virus persistence. Complete eradication of chronic HBV infection is nearly unattainable. Currently, achieving functional cure through antiviral treatment, particularly with IFNα, is feasible. What deserves attention is whether this serological “disease-free” status can maintain for a long time. Our research built upon previous works to explore the persistence of HBsAg loss in the real-world scenarios.

Among the cured patients we recruited, 15.3% (45/295) experienced HBsAg seroreversion during follow-up. Notably, more than half of these events (51.1%, 23/45) appeared within 24-week observation periods. Whereas, on the whole, the cumulative durability of HBsAg loss performed well according to the present research. Yip et al. uncovered both NA-induced HBsAg clearance and spontaneous HBsAg clearance were found to be durable outcomes [15]. Other researches also identified most patients who achieved HBsAg clearance on NAs therapy could maintain this ‘cured state’ after discontinuation [16, 17]. It was also found that discontinuation of oral medications had little effect on reversal risk [17]. While for IFNα, although it has been demonstrated the ability to induce durable HBsAg loss in certain studies as well [18–21], achieving complete cure remains challenging even with indirect targeting of cccDNA [7]. Recent research has also found that small amounts of cccDNA are still present in functionally cured patients [22], indicating that intranuclear cccDNA and integrated DNA may maintain transcriptional activity leading to continuous virion production despite apparent clearance of circulating viral antigens such as HBsAg or HBeAg from serum samples collected for clinical monitoring purposes over time post-treatment cessation. Actually, direct targeting strategies for cccDNA are currently being investigated including irreversible silencing, degradation or lethal mutations caused by gene editing techniques [12]. These cutting-edge technologies remain on the road to practical application due to major difficulties and challenges associated with their development and implementation. While indirect targeting strategies for cccDNA continue to rely on IFNα-based approaches, which have been shown previously to modulate histone epigenetic modifications [23], indirectly impact cccDNA though the overexpression induction of apolipoprotein B mRNA editing enzyme (APOBEC3) family proteins [24], and modulation innate as well as adaptive immune responses against HBV infection [25].

Patients who test negative for HBsAg and anti-HBs remain at risk for HBV reactivation on account of the existence of cccDNA. As mentioned in previous study, undetectable HBsAg measured by conventional assay might not imply a bona fide HBsAg clearance, since conventional reagents for detecting HBsAg were based on an immunoreaction using antibodies to HBsAg [26]. When an immune complex of HBsAg and HBsAb is present in the circulating blood, HBsAg levels may be underestimated. A substantial proportion of patients could detect HBsAg presence when used highly sensitive HBsAg (hs-HBsAg) assay, especially in those with anti-HBs negative individuals (P = 0.007, OR = 7.1) [27]. This further illustrated the importance of hs-HBsAg measure for anti-HBs negative patients. Among 99 patients who did not obtain anti-HBs positivity when peg-IFNα discontinuation, 30 in present analysis developed HBsAg reversion during follow-up, which might indicate that HBsAg clearance did not really occur in these patients when hs-HBsAg testing was used. Maybe, this highly sensitive assay has the potential ability to evaluate the risk of subsequent HBsAg reversion. Therefore, the widespread implementation of hs-HBsAg detection is essential for accurately determining the clearance of HBsAg from peripheral blood.

How to improve the capacity of HBsAg seroconversion in patients achieving HBsAg clearance was a problem worth considering. In this study, approximately 70% of patients achieved this outcome during peg-IFNα therapy, which was lower than the earlier reports [18, 19, 21]. Intriguingly, some patients who did not obtain HBsAg seroconversion on peg-IFNα therapy were able to observe the emergence of anti-HBs after treatment discontinuation. Not all cured patients could consistently sustain HBsAg seroconversion following discontinuation of peg-IFNα treatment; we found anti-HBs levels gradually declined or even disappeared in a subset of patients during follow-up. Our study revealed that 80.1% and 66.5% of individuals who obtained HBsAg seroconversion before EOT remained positive anti-HBs status at the first-year and second-year observation periods, respectively. Continuing treatment after anti-HBs production was favorable to maintain its stability. Additionally, we have learnt several studies exploring ways to improve the efficacy of interferon. Lee et al. reported that peg-IFNα add-on therapy followed by sequential HBV vaccination could increase the probability of HBsAg seroclearance and improve the anti-HBs levels [28]. A recent research also elucidated that combination of interferon consolidation therapy and HBV vaccine promoted HBsAg seroconversion [29]. Exactly the combined application of HBV vaccination has a certain adjunctive effect on expediting HBsAg seroconversion and elevating preexisting anti-HBs positive levels [30, 31]. We administered the HBV vaccine to the majority of patients following HBsAg loss in order to expedite the establishment of immune response. The current finding showed that 80.0% recipients achieved HBsAg seroconversion after consolidation treatment for more than 24 weeks with no less than 6 doses of vaccine, which increased significantly compared to those received shorter consolidation time and fewer doses of vaccine. In practical terms, the efficacy of the HBV vaccination varied among cured patients. It was certain that strong response (anti-HBs > 100mIU/mL) to vaccination was found to be beneficial in reducing the possibility of HBsAg reappearance in comparison to those weak response (anti-HBs ≤ 100mIU/mL) according to the previous research [32]. Well, more explorations required to determine the optimal combination strategy for accelerating HBsAg conversion via the application of vaccination.

HBsAg seroconversion facilitated to the maintain the durable HBsAg clearance. It is well-established that the dysfunction in HBV-specific T and B cells immunity is associated with persistent HBV infection [33]. Anti-HBs deriving from humoral immunity is associated with strong immune control over HBV infection [34]. Earlier research has validated the potency of anti-HBs in blocking the entry of virus into hepatocytes and eradicating infectious viral particles [35]. In addition, anti-HBs was capable of neutralizing HBsAg [36] and correlative with immune responses [37], which availed to sustained HBsAg loss. Patients who acquired sufficient levels of pre-withdrawal interferon-induced antibodies were less likely to experience subsequent HBsAg reversal. Wu et al. uncovered significantly lower recurrence rates among individuals with initial level of anti-HBs ≥ 100 IU/L than those with anti-HBs < 100 IU/L [38], which aligned with Jiang's result [32]. Guo et al. also reported the less recurrence risk in patients whose antibodies level ≥ 1.3log10IU/L at treatment discontinuation [21]. In present study, we observed a similar result as previous mentioned in patients with HBsAg seroconversion during follow-up periods. However, there has been always controversy surrounding the role of anti-HBs in sustaining HBsAg clearance, as previous findings have suggested that the appearance of anti-HBs may not be necessary for achieving long-lasting HBsAg loss [39]. Most patients for failure to HBsAg seroconversion occurred serological reappearance within one year, as indicated by our findings. Recurrent-free cases at follow-up were generally seen in patients with high anti-HBs levels; suggesting continuous inhibitory by the host immune system on cccDNA transcriptional activity or potential complete depletion/clearance of cccDNA within liver cell nuclei as the alternative possibility. More critically, sustained immune control provided support and assurance for safer withdrawal of antiviral medications.

Consolidation treatment time also influenced the long-term effectiveness of functional cure. Li et al. stressed the indispensability of a consolidation period lasting 12–24 weeks for the maintenance of functional cure [18]. While Guo et al. concluded that no significant difference was found in consolidation treatment time between patients who confirmed HBsAg loss and those who experienced HBsAg reversion [21], which was in line with aforementioned research [19]. In our study, we noted that the durability of HBsAg loss in patients receiving consolidation treatment exceeding 12 weeks was superior to those with consolidation treatment lasting ≤ 12 weeks. The primary reason might attribute to promote HBsAg seroconversion by prolonging consolidation therapy duration, which not only maintained stable levels of anti-HBs but also minimized the incidence of HBsAg reversion.

Research into predictors for HBsAg reversion was warranted, with particular emphasis on exploring the role of HBsAg seroconversion in patients with functional cure. Previously mentioned discovery elucidated that anti-HBs level served as an independent predictor for HBsAg reversion [18]. Consolidation treatment ≥ 12 weeks and HBeAg status were also found to be the related factors for predicting HBsAg reversion [40]. Furthermore, another study revealed that treatment strategies played a significant role on sustained HBsAg loss [41]; NAs therapy exhibited superior advantage in attaining more enduring functional cure compared to IFNα monotherapy. The emphasis of current study was to examine the predictive capacity of HBsAg seroconversion in the durability of HBsAg loss, aiming for a more comprehensive understanding. Our results illustrated that anti-HBs absence at EOT, baseline HBeAg positivity and shorter consolidation duration were significantly associated with HBsAg recurrence following treatment discontinuation. By incorporating these related factors, we successfully established a predictive model that could assist in clinical decision-making. For HBeAg-negative and anti-HBs positive individuals with consolidation time > 12 weeks, the cumulative probability of HBsAg reversal at 2 years was approximately 5%, while oppositely, it reached around 20% for anti-HBs negative patients. Therefore, we could conclude that anti-HBs was indispensable for achieving sustainable functional cure. We recommend that individuals who did not express positive anti-HBs at EOT should consider restarting IFNα therapy at an appropriate time to stimulate immunoreaction. In our samples, three patients achieved HBsAg seroconversion following retreatment with peg-IFNα and sustained HBsAg loss in recent observations. Nonetheless, further confirmation was required to validate the reliability of the deduction.

There were several limitations in this study. Firstly, most cured subjects still required to receive NAs therapy for a more secure termination of treatment; hence we have not comprehensively evaluated the relapse of HBV DNA during long-term follow-up. To data, we observed only one case with HBV DNA relapse after 6 months of NAs discontinuation. Secondly, HBV genotype data were not available in our retrospective study, and whether genotype has an impact on the persistence of HBsAg loss is unknown. Besides, we were unable to assess HBV RNA levels in recurrence patients after discontinuation due to limited detection techniques. Finally, we also did not evaluate the durability of HBsAg loss in patients who obtained functional cure by interferon monotherapy.

In general, this study demonstrated the favorable durability of functional cure induced by peg-IFNα-2b therapy, particularly in those who developed anti-HBs prior to treatment discontinuation. The role played by HBsAg seroconversion was crucial for sustaining long-term effectiveness of functional cure. Most noteworthy, continuing consolidation therapy following the production of anti-HBs proved the advantages in stabilizing its levels and reducing the likelihood of HBsAg reversion. This underscored the importance of avoiding premature cessation when utilizing interferon as a treatment option. The probability of HBsAg recurrence could be also calculated with comparative accuracy using a nomogram, thereby facilitating earlier decision-making. Undoubtedly, larger-scale and longer-duration observational studies should be conducted to better assess the real-world efficacy.
