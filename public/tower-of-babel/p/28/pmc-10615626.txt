Patient-focused outcomes after initiation of dialysis for ESRD: mortality, hospitalization and functional impairment
Authors: Benedikt Kolbrink, Katrin Schüssel, Friedrich A von Samson-Himmelstjerna, Grit Esser, Jürgen Floege, Ulrich Kunzendorf, Kevin Schulte
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOutcome data regarding clinically relevant endpoints after starting dialysis for end-stage renal disease (ESRD) are sparse, and early events after starting dialysis are particularly underestimated. The aim of this study was to describe patient-focused outcomes in ESRD patients starting from first dialysis.MethodsThe data basis for this retrospective observational study were anonymized healthcare data from Germany's largest statutory health insurer. We identified ESRD patients who initiated dialysis in 2017. Deaths, hospitalizations and occurrence of functional impairment within 4 years after starting dialysis were recorded starting from first treatment. Hazard ratios in dialysis patients compared with an age- and sex-matched reference population without dialysis were generated, stratified by age.ResultsThe dialysis cohort included 10 328 ESRD patients who started dialysis in 2017. First dialysis was performed in-hospital for 7324 patients (70.9%), and 865 of these died during the same hospitalization. One-year mortality for ESRD patients initiating dialysis was 33.8%. Functional impairment occurred in 27.1% of patients, while 82.8% of patients required hospitalization within 1 year. Hazard ratios of dialysis patients compared with the reference population for mortality, functional impairment and hospitalization at 1-year were 8.6, 4.3 and 6.2. Dialysis patients <50 years were disproportionately affected, with >40-fold increased risk of adverse events compared with their peers.ConclusionsThe emergence of morbidity and mortality after starting dialysis for ESRD is significant, especially in younger patients. Patients have a right to be informed about the prognosis associated with their condition.Keywords: dialysis, end-stage renal disease, functional impairment, hospitalization, mortality

INTRODUCTION

A diagnosis of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) typically resulted in premature death until the 1960s, when renal replacement therapy (RRT) by dialysis was introduced into routine clinical practice [1, 2]. While many parts of the world still experience significant problems in providing access to RRT for all ESRD patients, dialysis is generally universally available in most industrialized countries [3–5]. Today, there are at least 3 million people worldwide who are dependent on RRT. This number is expected to exceed 5 million by 2030 due to better availability of dialysis in developing countries and a general increase in ESRD because of ageing populations [3].

A particularly critical period in the lives of patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) is the time of transition to ESRD and the commencement of dialysis. This period is marked by many difficult decisions for patients and nephrologists, and is often accompanied by significant clinical burden and mortality [6]. Unfortunately, this critical period of transition has been incompletely studied. Most international registry data, from which the majority of evidence regarding the epidemiology and prognosis of ESRD patients initiating dialysis stems, is subject to selection and ascertainment bias. This is because patients are typically included in the registries after surviving a certain period after initiation of dialysis (typically 90 days) or after having received dialysis in an outpatient clinic. Therefore, early complications are excluded in particular, which may also introduce competing risk bias [7]. Consequently, the number of ESRD patients starting dialysis is likely to be systematically underestimated and their reported outcomes may not reflect real-world experiences. However, studies that focused on outcomes shortly after the initiation of dialysis have mainly examined mortality and either had small case numbers or pooled patients over long periods of time. This does not allow for a comprehensive and contemporary picture of the real-world situation [8, 9]. Therefore, prognostic assessment of life expectancy after transition from CKD to ESRD is difficult [6, 10]. Changes in quality of life after dialysis initiation are even harder to assess because impairment in functional status, which has a significant impact on quality of life, is not routinely recorded [11]. Furthermore, to put these outcome data into a meaningful context for patients, reporting absolute risks alone is not sufficient. Rather, as a reference, the corresponding risks for the general population of the same age must also be known so that the extent of the burden on patients becomes apparent.

With this study, we aimed to overcome the problems in assessing outcomes of dialysis patients by explicitly including the critical transition time after the start of dialysis. Our objective was to describe real-world phenomena of incident dialysis patients in Germany especially pertaining to patient- and quality of life–centered events, while simultaneously reporting the occurrence of these events in the population without dialysis for reference.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Dataset

The data source for this retrospective cohort study was anonymized nationwide administrative health claims data of the “German Local Health Care Funds” [Allgemeine Ortskrankenkassen (AOK)]. This is a statutory health insurance group covering 27 million people in the year 2020, which approximates one-third of the total German population.

Source population

The source population included all persons insured with the AOK at the beginning of the year 2017 and continuously enrolled in 2016. A total of 22 955 398 individuals were included in this cohort.

Identification and characterization of ESRD patients initiating dialysis in 2017

ESRD patients initiating dialysis were identified by the combination of the ICD-10 German Modification coded diagnosis for ESRD (N18.5) and the presence of documented dialysis treatments (Supplementary data, Table S1) in the dataset for the year 2017. Patients included in the analysis were those starting from the day of first dialysis documented in combination with the ESRD diagnosis (Supplementary data, Fig. S1), regardless of whether it was performed in the hospital or at an outpatient provider of dialysis. Using this definition, we identified a total of 10 328 ESRD patients starting dialysis in the source population from 2017. For further analyses, the cohort was divided into five age categories. ICD-10 and surgical procedure codes were used to define pre-existing comorbidities and CKD stages (Supplementary data, Table S2 and Fig. S2), the presence of an arteriovenous (AV) fistula or graft as vascular access (Supplementary data, Table S3), and potential underlying renal diseases (Supplementary data, Table S4) of incident dialysis patients.

Generation of a representative age- and sex-matched reference population

Each incident dialysis patient was assigned three reference individuals matched by sex and age. These were chosen from all individuals of the source population but without dialysis. The assessment of existing comorbidities was performed analogously to the procedure for dialysis patients. A total of 30 984 age- and sex-matched reference individuals without dialysis were identified from the source population in 2017.

Clinical endpoints

ESRD patients initiating dialysis in 2017 were followed from the first dialysis to the first occurrence of the respective endpoints. For individuals in the reference population, follow-up started at the date of dialysis initiation of the corresponding matching patient. Endpoints examined were:

“Death.”

“Death.”

“Functional impairment:” this endpoint was met when patients had an increase in “long-term care level” of at least one point. This parameter is specific to the German healthcare system and objectively reflects a person's dependence in the activities of daily living and the need for care. The care level can range from 0 (no restrictions) to 5 (highest need for care), and is assessed on the basis of six physical and psychosocial dimensions (additional information Supplementary Appendix, p. 6) [12, 13]. Patients who already had the highest possible care level (the equivalent of maximal functional impairment) at the beginning of the observation period (134 patients in the dialysis cohort and 379 patients in the reference population) were excluded from this analysis. Consequently, 10 194 patients in the dialysis cohort and 30 605 individuals in the reference population were included.

If dialysis was initiated during an inpatient visit, only the next subsequent admission to hospital was considered for this endpoint. Individuals from the dialysis or reference population whose follow-up started during an inpatient visit and who subsequently died during the same hospitalization were excluded from this specific analysis (865 patients in the dialysis cohort and 15 individuals in the reference population). Censoring of patients due to loss to follow-up

Observation of patients ended at the first occurrence of a respective endpoint, at the end of the study period (31 December 2020) or when insurance with the AOK was terminated, whichever occurred earlier. Observations were censored at the last observable date in the respective endpoint analyses. Notably, termination of AOK insurance rarely occurred. During the first year (365 days) of follow-up, 11 (0.1%) incident dialysis patients and 105 (0.3%) individuals in the reference population were lost to follow-up for this reason.

Statistics

The endpoint of “death” was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method. For analysis of the endpoints “functional impairment” and “hospitalization,” we used cumulative incidence functions because death was a competing risk [14, 15]. Hazard ratios (HRs) for dialysis vs reference cohort were calculated from Cox regression (endpoint death) or competing risk regression (endpoints functional impairment and hospitalization), respectively [15]. Data were analyzed and processed using R version 4.1.1 (packages survival 3.2–11, cmprsk 2.2–11, survminer 0.4.9, Rcpp 1.0.8.3, RODBC 1.3–19, dplyr 1.0.9 and grDevices 4.1.1; details see Supplementary Appendix, section R packages), RStudio version 1.4.1717, and Microsoft Excel, 2019. Data were processed at the Scientific Institute of the AOK (WIdO) and results were generated only in anonymized and aggregated form. Consequently, the study required neither ethics approval nor patients’ informed consent according to current German data protection regulations.

RESULTS

Epidemiology and characteristics of ESRD patients initiating dialysis in 2017

In 2017, the source population included 22 955 398 individuals, 10 328 of whom initiated dialysis for ESRD. Overall incidence of dialysis was 45.0 per 100 000 individuals. Only 438 (4.2%) patients started on peritoneal dialysis (PD) and 2182 (21.1%) had an AV fistula before their first dialysis. In total, 7324 (70.9%) patients started dialysis during an inpatient hospital visit for various reasons, with the most common main diagnoses being CKD and worsening of kidney function (Supplementary data, Table S5), and 865 of these patients died during their first hospitalization (Table 1). Age-specific incidence of dialysis for ESRD increased with age and reached a maximum in the 80–89 years age group, then sharply decreased in patients ≥90 years (Fig. 1A). However, the largest proportion of all incident dialysis patients was in the 65–79 years age group (Fig. 1B).

Of the incident dialysis patients, 4602 (44.6%) already had some form of functional impairment when starting dialysis (Table 1). Across all age groups, functional impairment was more frequently observed in dialysis patients compared with the reference population. There were fewer individuals with the highest functional impairment (care level) among incident dialysis patients ≥80 years than in the reference population (Supplementary data, Fig. S3 and Table S6).

The majority of comorbidities including diabetes mellitus and congestive heart failure were more frequent in dialysis patients than in the reference population (Fig. 1C–H). The only exceptions were dementia in patients ≥80 years and depression and cerebrovascular disease in patients ≥90 years, which were more common in the reference population than in dialysis patients (Fig. 1G and H).

Clinical outcomes after initiation of dialysis for ESRD

Endpoint of “death:” within the first year, 3492 of 10 328 dialysis patients (33.8%) died versus 1487 of 30 984 persons (4.8%) in the reference population {HR 8.6 [95% confidence interval (CI) 8.1–9.2]} (Supplementary data, Table S7). The hazard of death was highest early after dialysis initiation and decreased over time (Fig. 2A and B). Since this observation violates the model assumption of proportional hazards, the HR derived from the Cox regression may only be considered as an explorative analysis here. The absolute risk of death within the first year after initiation of dialysis increased with age up to 56.0% in those ≥90 years, whereas it was 23.7% in ≥90-year-olds in the reference population (Fig. 2B and C; Supplementary data, Table S7). However, the HR for death at 1 year in dialysis patients was greatest in the ≤49 years age group [48.1 (95% CI 17.5–132.3)] (Fig. 2D).

DISCUSSION

Herein, we present a cross-sector analysis of the healthcare data from 23 million people in Germany in order to elucidate the epidemiology and patient-centered outcomes of ESRD patients who started dialysis in 2017. Our study has three major findings: (i) rapid onset of mortality among incident dialysis patients is very high; (ii) after the start of dialysis, the occurrence of events that have a strong negative impact on the quality of life is to be expected; and (iii) preparation for the start of dialysis in ESRD patients could be significantly optimized in the German setting.

Mortality is by far the most frequently studied endpoint in dialysis patients and there are many registry data analyses, as well as a large number of prognostic score models on mortality [16–19]. Nevertheless, a significant problem with previous reports evaluating this relates to the fact that early death in ESRD patients cannot be measured accurately because of the structure of registry data or intrinsic selection bias in such studies [7, 20]. For example, the US Renal Data System (USRDS) has long-known problems in recording early deaths and only includes ESRD patients after being enrolled in the registry by an outpatient dialysis provider [7–9]. True mortality rates of ESRD patients being initiated on dialysis are therefore likely to be underestimated in most reports. Mindful of this, the selection algorithm we used ensured that all ESRD patients who had started dialysis during the relevant period, across both outpatient and inpatient sectors, were included from their first dialysis. Our reported 1-year mortality of incident dialysis patients was 34%. To put this finding into perspective: a study trying to develop a predictive prognostic mortality score using data from the European incident hemodialysis (HD) patient database (AROii) and the Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Survey (DOPPS) classified incident dialysis patients with a 1-year all-cause mortality of >19% as the highest risk group. However, it should be noted that patients who were lost to follow-up during the baseline period of 3 months were not included in the main analysis therein [19]. Nevertheless, it has also been reported for the AROii cohort that the greatest risk for cardiovascular complications and death is in the period shortly after initiation of dialysis [21]. This underlines that inclusion of the critical transition period is necessary for a realistic prognosis assessment. The USRDS also reports a significantly lower 1-year mortality than ours of 22% for incident HD patients in 2017 [22]. However, other studies from the USA that explicitly focused on the early period after dialysis initiation show that a significant proportion of patients died shortly after initiation of dialysis and therefore have a high probability of not being included in the USRDS. The 1-year mortality among elderly incident dialysis patients in the USA was also very consistent with that reported in our study [8, 9]. In particular, among those patients ≥80 years old and even more strikingly among ≥90-year-old incident dialysis patients in our study, there was a large number who died very shortly after starting dialysis. In addition, 1-year mortality was extraordinarily high in these age groups at 45% and 56%, respectively.

Apart from understanding mortality in dialysis patients, the central objective of this study was to describe patient-centered outcomes, with a focus on quality of life. For this, we looked at two endpoints: loss of functionality in activities during daily life and the occurrence of hospitalization. Deterioration in functional status correlates with reduced quality of life and shorter life expectancy in dialysis patients [23–26]. Although this is well-known, international data on the change in functional status after initiation of dialysis are scarce. Previous reports have focused only on elderly patients and nursing home residents who had very low functional status even before dialysis was initiated [11, 27]. With our study, we demonstrate that young dialysis patients have an absolute risk of approximately 13% in developing functional impairment in the first year on dialysis. Even more alarmingly, they were at a 40-fold higher risk of experiencing functional impairment compared with age- and sex-matched reference individuals of the general study population.

As a second endpoint that impacts daily life, hospitalizations largely contribute to poor quality of life in dialysis patients, while additionally placing a high economic burden on healthcare systems [28]. Strikingly, in our cohort hospitalizations in the first year after dialysis initiation affected approximately 80% of patients proportionately across all age groups. Consistent with this finding, hospitalization rates among both young and old dialysis patients in international studies were uniformly high regardless of age [29, 30]. However, the younger dialysis patients had a particularly high risk for hospitalization compared with the reference population.

Taken together, our data show for the first time that especially young patients are at significant risk of loss of quality of life, and of dying after starting dialysis, compared with their peers. Furthermore, older dialysis patients have very high absolute risks that must be considered in any potential treatment decisions. These findings have important clinical significance: first, realistic prognoses must be openly communicated with patients, and secondly, in terms of health policy, every effort should be made to improve this situation.

Lastly, a particular striking finding in our study was that 71% of patients started dialysis in the hospital. Furthermore, only 21% of patients had an AV fistula or graft for vascular access at the start of dialysis. Previously, comprehensive data on the proportion of long-term dialysis patients started in-hospital had only been available from the USA, where it was reported to be 46% in 2018 by the USRDS, and the proportion of incident HD patients with a catheter without a maturing fistula was only 63% [31]. Studies from the USA that focused on individuals >65 years of age, however, reported in-hospital start of dialysis in 73% of patients [8]. Moreover, the proportion of dialysis patients starting on PD (4%) appears extraordinarily low in our cohort by international standards. The USA and the rest of Europe simultaneously reported that about 11% of incident dialysis patients started on PD, while Canada reported 23% [31–33]. However, comparison of these registry data with ours is inappropriate, since registries are typically subject to selection bias and probably include a healthier population of patients with relatively better nephrologist care before initiation of dialysis. Therefore, we believe that our study more likely represents the real-life healthcare situation. We conclude that German CKD patients in our cohort are insufficiently prepared for the transition to ESRD and that previous reports from other industrialized countries might be underestimating the true burden of disease in this patient group. Nevertheless, there is an important question that remains, which is why the preparation of CKD patients for dialysis initiation cannot be better managed in Germany, where there is universal and unrestricted healthcare for all citizens. Future studies are urgently warranted in this area.

Limitations

The major limitation of our study lies in its observational nature, which is inevitably accompanied by selection bias and poses the problem that we cannot verify diagnoses in individual cases. The AOK had 6% higher expenditures per insured individual compared with the average from German health insurers in 2017 [34]. Hence, we cannot rule out that our cohort of AOK patients had a slightly higher burden of disease than the rest of the German population. However, the proportion of individuals who were lost to follow-up was less than 0.5%. Therefore, we assume that the specific endpoint observations from our dialysis patients are valid.

CONCLUSION

Although Germany is a high-income country with universal access to healthcare and dialysis, the outcomes of incident dialysis patients in our cohort are worse than expected when compared with previously published international data. Our study therefore shows that “simply” having access to dialysis for everyone by no means solves all the problems of ESRD. Far-reaching structural changes in the care and preparation of CKD patients transitioning to ESRD are needed. Ideally, the effectiveness of such undertakings should be monitored in large well-controlled studies in order to stimulate similar efforts worldwide.
