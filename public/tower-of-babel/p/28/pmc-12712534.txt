Arthroscopic Popliteus Reconstruction Using Anterolateral Portals
Authors: Philippe Beauchamp-Chalifour, Anna-Lee Policicchio, Gareth Ryan, David Longino, C Ryan Martin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPosterolateral corner injury is associated with cruciate ligament injury. Even when the lateral collateral fibular ligament is intact and provides adequate stability in the coronal plane, a posterolateral rotational instability may still be present due to popliteus insufficiency. In this clinical context, arthroscopic popliteus reconstruction may provide sufficient posterolateral stability, while being less invasive than open posterolateral reconstruction. We propose a modified arthroscopic popliteus reconstruction using anterolateral portals to increase the toolbox of the arthroscopic knee surgeon when faced with partial posterolateral corner injury.

Posterolateral corner injury is often associated with anterior cruciate ligament and posterior cruciate ligament rupture.1,2 Even when the lateral collateral fibular ligament (LCL) is intact and provides adequate stability in the coronal plane, as proven by a negative collateral stress radiograph, posterolateral rotational instability may still be present due to popliteus insufficiency.3 An increased dial test at 30° associated with increased lateral joint laxity under varus stress at more than 30° of flexion may be a sign of isolated popliteus insufficiency, classified as a Fanelli and Larson type A.4 In patients with isolated popliteus insufficiency, arthroscopic popliteus reconstruction may provide sufficient posterolateral stability while being less invasive than open posterolateral reconstruction.5

We propose a modified arthroscopic popliteus reconstruction technique to increase the toolbox of the arthroscopic knee surgeon when faced with partial posterolateral corner injury (summarized in Tables 1 and 2). This technique does not require special equipment or posterior portals and involves minimal disruption of meniscocapsular tissue as opposed to previous techniques described.5, 6, 7, 8

Surgical Technique

Physical Examination Under General Anesthesia

Under general anesthesia, routine physical examination is performed. Collaterals are both evaluated at 0° and 30° of flexion. Hyperextension is compared to the contralateral side. A supine dial test is performed at 30° and 90°. It is important to distinguish posterolateral instability from anteromedial rotatory instability when faced with increased external rotation on the dial test.9 This can be further evaluated by arthroscopic evaluation under anesthesia.

General Positioning and Arthroscopic Setup

The patient is positioned supine on the operating table. A thigh lateral support is installed, and a foot holder is placed so that the lower limb can hold neutral at 80° of flexion. The marking pen is used to identify key anatomic structures and planned accessory portals (Fig 1; Video 1, 00:16). Tibial tunnel anterior aperture is planned, a priori, between the patellar tendon and Gerdy’s tubercle at the flat spot (Fig 1; Video 1, 00:18).

General position and lateral structures identification. Left knee positioned at 80° of flexion on a thigh holder and standard sterile drapes. Key anatomic structures are identified: anterior joint, patellar tendon, Gerdy’s tubercle, fibular head, and lateral collateral ligament (LCL). Key portals and tunnel aperture are identified: tibial tunnel anterior aperture, accessory anterolateral portal, and high lateral portal.

Diagnostic Arthroscopy and Creation of Accessory Anterolateral Portals

Standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals are created with reference to the lower border of the patella. A bump is placed under the knee so that it is positioned at 30° of flexion. The arthroscope is positioned in the anterolateral portal, and diagnostic arthroscopy is performed to confirm posterolateral instability. Clues to posterolateral instability include popliteal laxity, lateral gutter drive-through sign, and minimal anterior tibial cartilage exposed in the shallow view of the lateral compartment, meaning that the tibia is subluxed posterolaterally. See Figure 2.

(D) Minimal anterior tibial cartilage visible showing with rotation of the tibia, meaning an increased posterolateral laxity.

The high lateral portal is created 3 cm above the proximal pole of the patella and off the border of the vastus lateralis muscle. A switching stick is inserted and brought into the anterolateral gutter, where the assistant retracts the anterolateral gutter to create a working space. The accessory anterolateral portal is created to access the lateral gutter, 1 cm proximal to the joint line and 1 cm anterior to the LCL. This becomes the working portal for the arthroscopic instrument. See Figure 3 and Video 1, 00:30.

Arthroscopic Posterolateral Dissection

Driving the arthroscope into the posterolateral gutter, the insufficient popliteus tendon remnant is identified. The posterior aspect of the anterior inferior meniscofascicle tissue is partially dissected with the Werewolf Coblation System (Smith & Nephew) to gain access to the posterolateral aspect of the tibia. The proximal tibiofibular joint is identified with the probe. See Figure 4 and Video 1, 01:08.

(B) Posterolateral aspect of the tibia with the probe identifying the proximal tibiofibular joint.

Tibial Tunnel Reaming

Once sufficient access to the exit point of the tibial tunnel is created, a meniscal repair root guide fixed at maximal angle (Meniscal Root Repair System; Smith & Nephew) is positioned via the accessory anterolateral portal. A guide curved to the left for left knees and curved to the right for right knees is used. A regular anterior cruciate ligament guide can also be used. The aiming portion of the root guide is positioned at the posterolateral aspect of the tibia at the myotendinous junction of the popliteus, 1 cm medial to the proximal tibiofibular joint.10 The exit point should also be below the visualized posterior cartilage border. If the exit point is well positioned, the starting point of the root guide should fall between Gerdy’s tubercle and the patellar tendon, representing the anterolateral flat spot on the tibia.10 A 2.4-mm short pin is drilled with the meniscal root guide under direct arthroscopic visualization of the posterolateral aspect of the tibia. Advancement of the 2.4-mm pin is stopped when fat marrow appears, indicating that the posterior cortex has been pierced. The meniscal root guide is removed. An 8-mm acorn reamer is advanced up to the far cortex. The 2.4-mm pin is removed from the 8-mm acorn reamer and flipped so that the blunt end is positioned at the posterolateral aspect of the knee for additional precaution. The 8-mm reamer is then advanced by hand and under direct visualization to complete the tunnel. The tunnel aperture is rasped. A 1.0 polydioxanone passing suture is passed via the cannulated portion of the reamer, and the loop is taken out of the accessory lateral portal. It is then replaced with a passing Ultratape suture (Smith & Nephew) to increase the pulling strength. See Figure 5 and Video 1, 01:51.

Femoral Tunnel and Popliteus Tenodesis Preparation

Visualizing from the anterolateral portal, the 8-mm acorn reamer is inserted through the accessory anterolateral portal in the anterior one-fifth of the popliteus sulcus, just hugging the cartilage. A 2.4-mm pin is then used to secure the position and drilled across the femur. The 8-mm acorn reamer is used to ream a femoral socket of 20 mm. A 4.5-mm reamer is used to pierce the far cortex for subsequent suspensory fixation. A 1.0 Ethibond suture passing suture (Johnson & Johnson) is placed in the femoral tunnel, with the loop end at the accessory anterolateral portal. Remove any suture bridge between passing sutures of the femoral and tibial tunnels. See Figure 6 and Video 1, 02:49.

Femoral tunnel. Left knee positioned at 30° on a jump and viewing from the standard anterolateral portal in the lateral gutter. (A) Identify the femoral tunnel insertion site, at the anterior one-fifth of the popliteus sulcus and just hugging the cartilage. (B) Insert the femoral socket reamer (8 mm) through the accessory anterolateral portal. (C) Ream the femoral socket to a depth of 20 mm.

Graft Preparation

An 8 × ≥150-mm allograft is prepared. We do not recommend any particular type of allograft, as long as the dimensions are respected. One end of the graft is secured with an Ultrabutton Adjustable Fixation Device (Smith & Nephew). The other end of the graft is whipstitched using a 1.0 Ultrabraid Suture (Smith & Nephew). See Figure 7 and Video 1, 03:37.

Graft Passage and Tensioning

The whipstitched end of the graft is passed first in the tibial tunnel from the accessory anterolateral portal. Make sure that there is sufficient length at the anterior tibia for subsequent screw fixation. The end of the graft with button fixation is then passed in the femoral tunnel, also from the anterolateral portal. The button is flipped, and the graft is provisionally docked in the femur. Arthroscopic visualization of the femoral socket is performed to ensure that enough graft has entered the femur. The graft is then secured at the tibia using an 8 × 25-mm Biosure Regenesorb Interference Screw (Smith & Nephew). The graft is finally tensioned with the button on the femoral side at 60° of flexion and neutral rotation. See Figure 8 and Video 1, 3:47. The postoperative protocol is described in Table 3.

Discussion

This modified arthroscopic popliteus reconstruction is a useful technique that allows addressing partial posterolateral corner injury (Finelli A). We propose a modification of previous arthroscopic popliteus reconstruction techniques that used posterior portals to access the posterolateral aspect of the tibia.6,8 We find that positioning the knee at 30° of flexion on a bump and using a retracting switching stick from the high lateral portal gives sufficient working space in the lateral gutter. Additionally, we modified a previously published technique that described dissecting completely the posterior popliteomeniscal fibers by only partially dissecting the posterior aspect of the anterior inferior meniscofascicle tissue.6 We think this gives sufficient access to the posterolateral aspect of the tibia in addition of preserving most of the meniscofascicle’s function.

There are few reports of clinical outcomes following arthroscopic popliteus knee reconstruction, as this is a rare injury and a rare treatment. A retrospective cohort study of 38 patients comparing open and arthroscopic reconstruction in Finelli A posterolateral corner injuries reported no difference in clinical outcomes at a 30-month follow-up.5 We believe the main advantage of this arthroscopic technique is that it allows addressing partial posterolateral corner injury (Finelli A), while being less invasive than open reconstruction. Additionally, it allows direct visualization of the posterolateral aspect of the tibia when compared to open techniques, where this can be limited. With direct visualization, precise tibial tunnel position can be achieved, and the risk of arterial injury could be limited. The main disadvantage of this technique is that it does not address complete posterolateral corner injury and requires a long allograft. Advantages and disadvantages are summarized in Table 4.

In conclusion, anatomic arthroscopic popliteus reconstruction is a useful technique that allows the arthroscopic knee surgeon to address partial posterolateral corner injury. Additional clinical data are needed to further support the use of this technique and refine its indication.
