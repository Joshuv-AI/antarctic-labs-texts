A Novel Perspective on a Genetically Altered Model of SARS-CoV-2 Myocarditis
Authors: Madeleine W. Cunningham, Leslie T. Cooper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Myocarditis after SARS-CoV-2 infection may be life threatening.1 The importance of understanding mechanisms of myocarditis in COVID (SARS-CoV-2 disease) has been highlighted by the recent SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, which led to myocarditis, heart failure, heart attack, or arrhythmias.2 A small but significant percentage of patients with acute SARS-CoV-2 infection will present with acute myocarditis; however, the major causes of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in older individuals with acute COVID are ischemic injury and sepsis. A much smaller percentage of subjects receiving mRNA vaccinations for SARS-CoV-2 will also develop myocarditis in the first 4 weeks after vaccination and, in men between ages 16 and 39 years, after the second vaccine dose.3 The risk of myocarditis following mRNA vaccination for SARS-CoV-2 is approximately 1 in 10,000. Myocarditis after mRNA vaccination usually resolves within weeks with supportive care. In contrast to both forms of acute myocarditis, “long COVID,” not to be confused with myocarditis, is a multisystem syndrome affecting up to 10% of acute SARS-CoV-2–infected patients and is characterized by persistent immune activation and proinflammatory responses including up-regulation of JAK-STAT, interleukin-6, and T-cell exhaustion pathways.4 The symptoms of long COVID can persist for months or years after initial infection, causing long-term disability.

Myocarditis from any cause accounts for nearly one-fifth of all cases of sudden death in young adults, and the combination of myocarditis and dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is the basis for approximately one-half of all heart transplants performed in the United States.5 Myocarditis can cause destruction of the heart muscle leading to a chronic heart condition known as DCM, an enlarged heart usually with cellular infiltrates. Most viral causes may lead to an autoimmune inflammatory disease of the heart often developing after viral infection.5 Coxsackieviruses, parvoviruses, and a host of other viruses are causes of acute myocarditis resulting in progression to DCM in approximately one-third of affected patients.5 Deterioration of heart function leads to heart failure. Recent murine studies of viral myocarditis have unequivocally demonstrated lymphocytic B and T-cell autoimmunity leading to loss of heart function in experimental autoimmune myocarditis as well as in human myocarditis.6 However, in SARS-CoV-2 hearts, autopsy and histochemical analysis demonstrate more of a macrophage/monocyte infiltrate and pathogenesis rather than the lymphocytic infiltrate seen in many cases of myocarditis related to other viral causes.7

In this issue of JACC: Basic to Translational Science, the novel report on COVID myocarditis presented by Capcha et al7 is a unique mouse model designed to overexpress cardiac-specific human low-density lipoprotein receptor (hLDLR) (Figure 1) using the genetic construct AAV9-cTnT-hLDLR. Most interesting was the myocarditis, which developed within 5 days in the hLDLR genetically modified mice infected with SARS-CoV-2. The authors discovered that the presence of the hLDLR was sufficient to drive myocarditis by its interaction with osteopontin (OPN), which led to an OPN-dependent recruitment of macrophages/monocytes to the heart, which is different from the more common lymphocytic human myocarditis pathology. The previously unrecognized high-affinity LDLR–OPN interaction was required for the macrophage/monocyte recruitment to the heart in the SARS-CoV-2 myocarditis. The authors further identified inflammasome activation with downstream Gasdermin-D–mediated pyroptosis as the primary cell death pathway in the damage of the heart during the SARS-CoV-2 infection and development of myocarditis in their novel mouse model.7 See Figure 1 illustration of the inflammasome pathway leading to pyroptosis in the SARS-CoV-2 myocarditis model. Thus, the mouse model promoted a myocarditic heart containing macrophage/monocyte infiltrates while B and T lymphocytes were not present in large numbers. This is in contrast to more common lymphocytic myocarditis seen during and after other viral infections of the heart. In further support of the LDLR-driven SARS-CoV-2 myocarditis mouse model, IDOL, or induced degrader of LDLR-mediated degradation, abrogated the myocardial inflammation and prevented myocarditis. Although COVID myocarditic hearts were not available from humans, myocarditis tissues taken from other causes demonstrated lymphocytic infiltrates and showed >10-fold increased expression of LDLR in autopsy myocarditis hearts where the LDLR and OPN were seen together in merged heart sections probed for both molecules.

The LDLR–OPN interaction is a novel, therapeutically important pathway in myocarditis after COVID infection or vaccination as well as in myocardial inflammation from any cause. OPN is a cellular matrix protein associated with inflammation and poor outcomes in cardiovascular diseases.7 Chronically elevated OPN levels in the blood signal LDLRs, drive fibrosis, and herald a poor prognosis of enhanced remodeling and inflammation in heart failure, including myocarditis and dilated cardiomyopathy.7 Monoclonal antibodies and siRNA drugs act as inhibitors of proprotein convertase subtilisin kexin type 9 (PCSK9), the enzyme leading to increased LDLRs. These PCSK9 inhibitors promote increased removal of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) by the liver9 and also reduce OPN with increased LDLRs, which can clear LDL in the liver. Thus, the PCSK9 inhibitors lead to LDL reduction in the blood, which may be seen at ≥50%, and reduction of OPN in chronic cardiovascular disease leads to reduced vascular inflammation.7

The potential therapeutic benefit of Gasdermin D inhibitors such as dimethyl fumarate, which can block the pore formation required for pyropoptosis,7 is dampened because Gasdermin D inhibitors are toxic, and more studies are required to identify more viable options for inhibition of the pore formation as well as inflammasome activation. Patient populations who would benefit from these drugs need to be identified in larger human studies of diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers and to identify patient populations who would benefit from LDLR–OPN pathway–directed therapy.

Although this highly novel model7 provides clarity to the complex disease of SARS-CoV-2 infection in mice genetically modified and infected with a chimeric virus altered with the pathogenic factors of SARS-CoV-2, there are limitations of the study to be considered. Although LDLR overexpression demonstrated the effects of LDLR in its apparent causal role in promoting SARS-CoV-2 myocarditis, the genetically altered mice may not recognize multiple factors important in human disease including unknowns in inflammation, dyslipidemia, and heart function. Furthermore, this study confined LDLR–OPN interactions predominantly to cardiomyocytes, whereas in native disease, LDLR is expressed across multiple cardiac cell types, including fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and vascular smooth muscle cells. Further, the small number of human myocarditis specimens examined were heterogeneous in etiology, and tissues included only lymphocytic and Chagas-associated cases rather than COVID-19/SARS-CoV-2 myocarditis cases. The small number of samples investigated limited statistical power analyses for translational correlations. On the other hand, there were important features of the human hearts studied that corresponded to the mouse model including the >10-fold elevation of LDLR in the myocarditis autopsy heart tissues, which was consistent with reports in heart failure, and Gasdermin-D–mediated pyroptosis has been reported in other types of myocarditis, including viral lymphocytic and immune checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis.7

The study utilized a chimeric spike viral protein (chSARS-CoV-2) used to develop the mouse chimeric model, which was safe for BSL-2 studies, but may not have represented all of the SARS-CoV-2 proteins involved in SARS-CoV-2 cardiac injury. Evidence has suggested prolonged elevation of spike protein free in circulation in post-mRNA vaccine myocarditis10; however, whether spike antigen can lead to LDLR-OPN–mediated monocyte/macrophage recruitment through a shared mechanism is not yet known. In the future, more studies of authentic SARS-CoV-2 infection and mRNA vaccination models of myocarditis will be important for additional answers.

In their studies of the purified proteins, LDLR and OPN convincingly demonstrated direct LDLR-OPN binding and sufficient OPN-dependent macrophage chemotaxis in the transwell cell migration assays. Their evidence did not exclude the well-established interactions of OPN with integrins and CD44, but rather identified LDLR as an additional receptor associated with heart and cardiac inflammation. Whether cell-surface heparan sulfate proteoglycans may modulate or be involved in the interactions in vivo is not known, and structural considerations of binding or involvement of the interactions in vivo or in vitro remain to be resolved by crystallography or cryo-EM. Finally, in future studies, LDLR-targeted therapies and more viable Gasdermin-D–mediated pyroptosis inhibitors should be sought in established myocarditis models where circulating OPN and LDLR blood levels may be important as biomarkers and promote identification of translatable therapeutic options in disease.
