Cognitive dysfunction and multiple sclerosis
Authors: David E. Freedman, Anthony Feinstein
Published: 2026

Cognitive dysfunction is common in patients with multiple sclerosis (MS)

Cognitive impairment occurs in 34% to 65% of people with MS,1 with an elevated prevalence in those with disease of many years’ duration, heightened physical disability, and progressive illness subtypes.1 Processing speed, memory, and executive function are frequently affected.1 Grey matter atrophy seen on imaging, particularly thalamic atrophy, is a key radiologic correlate of cognitive dysfunction in MS.1

Additional risk factors contribute to MS-related cognitive dysfunction

Ancillary contributors to cognitive dysfunction include depression, sleep disturbances, vascular comorbidities, cannabis tetrahydrocannabinol use, and anticholinergic medications.2,3 Personalized discussions can guide how to mitigate risk.

Patients with MS should undergo cognitive screening at diagnosis and annually thereafter

Cognitive dysfunction can present early in MS, even without other physical disability,1 and is often not detected by clinicians. Cognitive screening is recommended at annual visits with the Symbol Digit Modalities Test, as described in Appendix 1 (available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.251585/tab-related-content).2 Positive screens (> 1.5 standard deviations below normative data) or cognitive decline (e.g., 8-point decrease) indicate a need to refer for comprehensive neuropsychiatric or neuropsychological assessments.2,4

Management involves support, education, and discussion about compensatory approaches

Performance-based evidence of cognitive dysfunction can be both validating and distressing for patients, emphasizing the value of supportive feedback about cognitive test results. People with MS and cognitive impairment benefit from personalized compensatory strategies (e.g., to-do lists paired with sequential task completion) and accommodations (e.g., private workspaces),2 often specified by occupational therapists. Medications, including amantadine, acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, and memantine, do not enhance cognition in MS.5

Cognitive rehabilitation can improve cognitive performance

Cognitive rehabilitation employs varied methods of memory retraining, including computer programs and memory aids. Patients receiving cognitive rehabilitation showed improved processing speed, verbal memory, and quality of life (small standard mean differences of 0.25 to 0.30).6 It remains unknown whether these changes enhance real-world functioning. In Canada, access to cognitive rehabilitation is currently limited.
