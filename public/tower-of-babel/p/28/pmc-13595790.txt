Rehabilitation outcomes in patients with complex regional pain syndrome of the ankle-foot complex: a single-center observational study
Authors: Danelina Vacheva, Atanas Drumev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComplex regional pain syndrome is a debilitating condition that often involves an injury or surgery, leading to chronic pain, swelling, and functional impairment in affected limbs, particularly in the ankle-foot complex. The management is challenging, as patients may experience prolonged symptoms that impact mobility and quality of life. While rehabilitation interventions aim to improve functional outcomes, their effectiveness in restoring full ankle function and mitigating long-term effects varies widely among patients. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the changes in functional recovery during a structured rehabilitation program in patients with complex regional pain syndrome of the ankle-foot complex following trauma. This study was designed as an observational study. Seventy-two patients presented with ankle-foot trauma, 23 of whom were diagnosed with CRPS.MethodsPatients underwent a comprehensive rehabilitation program including kinesitherapy, cryotherapy, therapeutic massage, and electrotherapy. Functional recovery was assessed via goniometry, the symmetry index via the G-WALK inertial sensor, locomotor activity tests activities of daily life scoring and pain intensity assessment. Data were analyzed using SPSS.ResultsStatistical analysis of the 23 patients with complex regional pain syndrome revealed statistically significant changes over three rehabilitation courses within five months. Initial assessments revealed hypotrophy of the thigh (2.5 cm) and lower leg muscles (1.5 cm), alongside swelling in the ankle joint (3.5 cm) and foot (2 cm). By the end of the rehabilitation period, hypotrophy decreased to 1 cm in the thigh and 1.5 cm in the calf, with swelling reduced to 1.5 cm in the ankle and 1 cm in the foot. Range of motion improved significantly, with dorsiflexion increasing by 10.83º and plantarflexion by 33.33º. The symmetry index improved from 64.88% to 80.8%, indicating partial restoration of gait symmetry. Pain levels decreased from an average of 15 (severe) to 7.5 (moderate), indicating a reduction in symptom severity over time.ConclusionComplex regional pain syndrome following ankle-foot trauma requires a tailored rehabilitation program to restore mobility and functionality. While complete gait normalization may be prolonged, improvements in daily activity performance were observed during the rehabilitation period. However, due to the observational design and absence of a control group, causality cannot be established. Background

Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) is a chronic pain condition that most commonly develops after trauma or surgery and is characterized by persistent pain, edema, sensory disturbances, vasomotor changes, and functional impairment that are disproportionate to the initial injury [1–3]. The condition frequently affects the distal segments of the upper and lower limbs and may result in substantial limitations in mobility, gait performance, activities of daily living, and overall quality of life. CRPS type I accounts for approximately 90% of cases and occurs without identifiable nerve injury, whereas CRPS type II is associated with documented nerve damage [4, 5]. The clinical presentation is often heterogeneous, with symptoms varying in severity and duration among patients. Persistent pain, swelling, joint stiffness, and motor dysfunction may contribute to prolonged disability and delayed recovery, particularly when the ankle-foot complex is affected, where even minor functional deficits can significantly impair locomotion and weight-bearing activities [6, 7].

The pathophysiology of CRPS is complex and involves interactions between inflammatory, neurological, and vascular mechanisms that contribute to persistent pain and functional impairment [6]. These processes may result in edema, altered sensory perception, restricted joint mobility, muscle weakness, and impaired motor control, all of which negatively affect gait and locomotor performance [6, 7]. Several factors have been associated with the development of CRPS following trauma, including fractures near joints, prolonged immobilization, peripheral nerve injury, and persistent post-traumatic pain [7, 8]. The resulting functional limitations often require prolonged rehabilitation aimed at restoring mobility, reducing disability, and improving participation in daily activities.

CRPS most commonly affects adults between 40 and 60 years of age and has been reported more frequently in women than in men [9]. Despite advances in medical management, patients often experience persistent pain, reduced joint mobility, muscle weakness, and gait disturbances that may continue for months after the initial injury [10–13]. These impairments can substantially limit independence and participation in daily activities, making functional recovery a major challenge during rehabilitation.

Rehabilitation is considered a cornerstone of CRPS management and aims to reduce pain, improve joint mobility, restore muscle function, and facilitate recovery of daily activities and locomotion [14, 15]. Common rehabilitation approaches include kinesitherapy, physical modalities, manual therapy, and functional training, often combined with pharmacological management. Previous studies have reported beneficial effects of rehabilitation on pain reduction and functional improvement; however, outcomes remain heterogeneous, and objective assessment of gait recovery and functional performance is not routinely reported [16]. Furthermore, evidence specifically addressing rehabilitation outcomes in patients with CRPS affecting the ankle-foot complex remains limited.

Because CRPS affecting the ankle-foot complex is frequently associated with gait disturbances, reduced mobility, and prolonged functional limitations, further investigation of rehabilitation outcomes using both clinical and instrumental assessment methods is warranted. We hypothesized that participation in a structured rehabilitation program would be associated with improvements in pain, joint mobility, gait symmetry, locomotor activity, and activities of daily living.

The aim of the study was to assess functional outcomes following a structured rehabilitation program on functional recovery in patients with complex regional pain syndrome of the ankle-foot complex following trauma.

Methods

Study design

This was a single-center prospective observational cohort study evaluating rehabilitation outcomes in patients with CRPS of the ankle–foot complex following trauma. The study examined changes in clinical, functional, and gait-related outcomes using standardized assessment tools before and after participation in a structured rehabilitation program. The observational design allowed for longitudinal evaluation of patient progress under real-world clinical conditions. Given the observational nature of the study, no randomized controlled comparison was performed.

Study setting and duration

The study was conducted at the Clinic of Physical and Rehabilitation Medicine, University Multiprofile Hospital for Active Treatment “Dr. Georgi Stranski”, Pleven, Bulgaria. Patients were recruited from the Clinic of Orthopedics and Traumatology and subsequently referred for rehabilitation. Patient recruitment and follow-up were conducted over an approximately 8–10-month period in 2024, corresponding to the active rehabilitation phase. The study encompassed three consecutive rehabilitation courses, with assessments performed at the beginning of the repair phase and at the end of the remodeling phase.

Participants – eligibility, selection, and study population

During the study period, 72 patients with trauma involving the ankle–foot complex underwent post-traumatic rehabilitation. From this cohort, 23 patients (31.94%) who developed CRPS, diagnosed according to the Budapest diagnostic criteria [17], were included in the present analysis. Patients who did not meet the diagnostic criteria were excluded, as the aim of the study was to evaluate rehabilitation outcomes specifically in patients with CRPS of the ankle–foot complex. The final study group consisted of 14 women and 9 men with ankle sprains and fractures of the metatarsal bones, calcaneus, and malleoli.

Although the traumatic injuries were heterogeneous in nature, all patients shared a common complication – CRPS. Factors associated with the development of CRPS included fractures in close proximity to the joint, peripheral nerve injury, painful fracture reduction, persistent pain following fracture, and improperly applied or compressive plaster immobilization resulting in iatrogenic injury.

Treatment prior to inclusion in the study was individualized. Most patients received analgesic medication due to predominant pain symptoms. Immobilization was applied in the majority of cases, with a mean duration of approximately 45 days; however, immobilization duration varied considerably between patients, and in some cases no immobilization was used.

All patients were referred for rehabilitation by a specialist in orthopedics and traumatology and received appropriate pharmacological therapy primarily aimed at pain control (e.g., analgesics and anti-inflammatory agents).

Diagnosis of complex regional pain syndrome

The diagnosis of complex regional pain syndrome was established based on clinical criteria consistent with the Budapest diagnostic criteria [17], including the presence of persistent pain disproportionate to the inciting event, sensory disturbances, edema, and motor dysfunction affecting the ankle-foot complex.

Rehabilitation protocol

The rehabilitation program followed a structured rehabilitation framework outlining session structure, exercise types, and progression principles. Exercise intensity, frequency, progression, and specific exercises were individually adapted according to patient tolerance, pain levels, functional capacity, and recovery progress. While it is not feasible to detail individualized modifications for all patients, this approach allowed the protocol to be consistently applied while accommodating the substantial variability in range of motion, pain, and functional status observed among the cohort. The rehabilitation process consisted of three consecutive phases: the protection phase, repair phase, and remodeling phase. For the purposes of this study, these phases refer to clinically defined stages of rehabilitation progression rather than biological tissue healing phases. All rehabilitation procedures were administered according to the standard clinical protocols of the department and supervised by experienced rehabilitation professionals. The rehabilitation program was delivered through three consecutive rehabilitation courses, each typically lasting seven days in accordance with the rehabilitation pathway of the National Health Insurance Fund. Treatment sessions were conducted daily, excluding Sundays. Kinesitherapy sessions generally lasted approximately 45–60 min, depending on patient age, pain severity, and functional capacity. Therapeutic massage and cryotherapy sessions lasted approximately 10 min, while electrotherapeutic procedures were typically applied for approximately 15 min per modality. Depending on the individual rehabilitation plan, patients received two to four physical therapy modalities per treatment day.

Protection phase

During the protection phase, rehabilitation was initiated in the Clinic of Orthopedics and Traumatology and focused on patient education, selection of appropriate assistive devices, and training in ambulation with two underarm crutches. Isometric contractions of the major lower-limb muscle groups were performed using controlled contraction–relaxation cycles to maintain muscle activity while minimizing mechanical stress on the injured limb [17].

Repair phase

During the repair phase, patients underwent a comprehensive rehabilitation program including kinesitherapy, cryotherapy, therapeutic massage, and electrotherapeutic procedures [18].

Kinesitherapy was tailored to the neurological and pain-related characteristics of CRPS and followed a proximal-to-distal progression of joint involvement [19]. Due to hypersensitivity of the affected limb and the risk of symptom exacerbation, exercises were carefully dosed to avoid pain provocation while promoting gradual functional recovery. The program included isometric muscle contractions, active exercises within joint-specific planes of motion, post-isometric relaxation techniques for shortened muscle groups, and supervised ambulation with assistive devices to ensure safe load progression [20].

Cryotherapy was applied locally to the ankle joint using an ice stick and consisted of three cycles of one-minute application with gentle pressure and massage. Between cryotherapy applications, patients performed active dorsiflexion and plantarflexion exercises. This alternating sequence of cryotherapy and active movement was intended to reduce pain, inflammation, muscle spasm, and edema while facilitating subsequent kinesitherapeutic interventions through improved tissue tolerance and joint mobility [21].

Therapeutic massage of the calf muscles was performed for 10–12 min using standardized techniques, including effleurage, squeezing, friction, and vibration. Particular attention was paid to the triceps surae muscle and the Achilles tendon region, where metabolic by-products tend to accumulate following prolonged immobilization and altered gait patterns [22].

Electrotherapy included low-frequency pulsed magnetic field therapy (LFPMF), interferential current therapy (ICT), and ultrasound therapy (UST) combined with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), applied according to established therapeutic parameters [18]. The specific treatment parameters followed the standardized departmental protocols routinely used for the management of CRPS-related pain and edema. Low-frequency pulsed magnetic field therapy was used to promote vasodilatation, improve collateral circulation, reduce edema, and support tissue regeneration [23]. Interferential current therapy provided pronounced analgesic effects, enhanced local blood flow, improved lymphatic drainage, and supported osteogenesis, particularly in cases of delayed bone healing and severe CRPS manifestations [24]. Ultrasound therapy generated mechanical micromassage effects, enhanced tissue diffusion, and stimulated local metabolic processes, thereby contributing to pain reduction and improved soft-tissue elasticity [25].

Remodeling phase

The remodeling phase included the same rehabilitation components as the repair phase, with progression in kinesitherapy intensity. Physical load was gradually increased, resistance exercises were introduced to enhance muscle strength, post-isometric relaxation was applied to improve joint range of motion, and walking distances without aids were progressively extended [20]. Patients were encouraged to independently perform activities of daily living as tolerated. Patients were encouraged to independently engage in activities of daily living (ADL) as tolerated. All participants completed three rehabilitation courses until ambulation without aids was achieved.

Outcome measures

Recovery was assessed using clinical, functional, and instrumental evaluation methods. The selected outcome measures were chosen to capture complementary aspects of functional recovery, including joint mobility, limb morphology, gait symmetry, pain, and activities of daily living. Although not all instruments have been validated exclusively in populations with CRPS, they are widely used and validated in musculoskeletal and neurological rehabilitation settings, including studies involving post-traumatic lower limb conditions and gait disorders. This multidimensional assessment approach was adopted to provide a comprehensive evaluation of rehabilitation outcomes. Clinical assessment included goniometric measurement of ankle joint range of motion (ROM) using the SFTR methodology and tape measurements (in centimeters) to evaluate joint swelling and muscle hypotrophy. Gait symmetry was assessed using the G-WALK inertial sensor system, which calculates the symmetry index of pelvic oscillations during walking and expresses it as a percentage of normative values. Functional assessment comprised the Locomotor Activity test, scored according to the need for walking aids [26], Rusk’s Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scale [27], with scoring criteria presented in Table 1, and a modified Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for pain intensity assessment. A 0–20-point scale was used in accordance with the rehabilitation assessment approach adopted in our department, allowing a more detailed categorization of pain severity levels and their relationship to functional activity and rehabilitation progress. Blinding of evaluators was not feasible due to the nature of the rehabilitation setting. To reduce variability and improve consistency of measurements, all assessments were performed by the same clinical team using standardized protocols. All assessors were experienced clinicians trained in the applied evaluation methods, which further minimized inter-observer variability. This approach ensured reliable and reproducible measurements while reflecting real-world clinical conditions, where full blinding is often not possible in rehabilitation studies. The primary outcome was change in ankle joint range of motion, with secondary outcomes including gait symmetry, pain intensity, and functional performance.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0. Descriptive statistics were calculated, and changes between initial and final assessments were analyzed using Student’s t-test. Data were assumed to be approximately normally distributed based on the sample characteristics and are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and confidence intervals were calculated. No priori sample size calculation was performed, which is acknowledged as a limitation of the study. All patients meeting the inclusion criteria during the study period (n = 23) were included, reflecting the rarity of this condition.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Pleven. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. Patients were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequences for their medical care. Given the inclusion of patients experiencing severe pain, special attention was paid to ensuring patient comfort and safety throughout the study. Participants’ autonomy was respected at all times, and they were fully informed of their right to withdraw from the study without any consequences for their medical care. Study procedures were carefully explained, and any questions or concerns were addressed promptly to minimize distress and support ethical participation. The initial drafting of text was supported by a large language model (ChatGPT) for grammar refinement and organization of ideas. All content was reviewed and edited by the authors to ensure accuracy and adherence to research standards.

Clinical trial number: not applicable, as this study was an observational cohort study and did not involve interventional assignment requiring prospective trial registration.

Results

A total of 23 patients with trauma involving the ankle–foot complex who developed CRPS were included in the analysis, and their rehabilitation outcomes were evaluated using statistically processed clinical and functional measurements.

The demographic composition of the study group showed a predominance of female patients. The largest proportion consisted of women aged 31–50 years (43.48%, n = 10). Male patients accounted for 39.13% of the cohort (n = 7). A smaller number of patients were aged 18–30 years (n = 2, all women), while patients aged 51–75 years included both women and men (n = 4; 2 women and 2 men). No patients older than 75 years were included.

The study cohort was clinically heterogeneous with respect to the type of trauma and preceding management, including ankle sprains and fractures of the metatarsal bones, calcaneus, and malleoli, as well as variations in immobilization duration and pharmacological treatment.

Figure 1 illustrates the comparative measurements of both lower limbs taken at the beginning and end of the study period, highlighting the differences in muscle hypotrophy and joint swelling between the healthy and injured limbs across specific anatomical regions. Initial measurements revealed a substantial muscle hypotrophy in the thigh, with a 2.5 cm difference, and in the lower leg muscles, with a 1.5 cm difference. By the end of the rehabilitation period, these differences decreased to 1 cm in the thigh and 1.5 cm in the calf, indicating changes in muscle mass and tone over the observed period.

Swelling was also observed initially, with the ankle showing a 3.5 cm difference and the foot a 2 cm difference. Following the rehabilitation period, swelling measurements reduced to 1.5 cm in the ankle and 1 cm in the foot, indicating a reduction in swelling over the observed period.

Reductions in muscle hypotrophy and joint swelling were observed in the affected limb over the rehabilitation period.

At the beginning of the observed period, the symmetry index was recorded at 64.88%. By the end of the rehabilitation process, this value improved to 80.08%. The symmetry index increased from 64.88% at baseline to 80.08% at follow-up. The final value remained below the reference range of 90–100%.

Figure 4 presents the results of the tests for locomotor activity, Activities of Daily Living (ADL), and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) pain scores, showcasing the maximum values achieved in the respective tests as well as the initial and final examinations.

Functional outcomes and pain levels before and after rehabilitation. Data are presented as mean values (n = 23). Units are expressed as points

At the beginning of the observed period, all patients required two underarm crutches for locomotor activity, a necessity due to lower extremity fractures. This dependence is reflected in the locomotor test score of 4.48 points. Similarly, the ADL test results mirrored this limitation, with a score of 2.37, indicating that performing daily activities requiring ambulation with aids was challenging. By the end of the study, patients showed improvements in functional performance, achieving a locomotor test score of 13.33 points, corresponding to ambulation without walking aids. The ADL test score increased from 2.37 to 4.41 points.

Regarding pain levels, patients diagnosed with CRPS reported “severe pain, limiting their motor activity but responding to medication” at the beginning of the observed period, scoring approximately 15 points on the VAS. At the end of the observed period after achieving independent walking pain levels decreased to 7.5 points, which falls between the definitions of “moderate pain when walking, resolving at rest” (10 points) and “mild (impermanent) pain during normal motor activity” (5 points).

Recovery of locomotor function, defined as the ability to walk without aids, was a prerequisite for the completion of the study for each individual patient. The duration of the rehabilitation process in patients diagnosed with CRPS averaged 19.43 weeks.

Table 3 summarizes the changes observed between baseline and follow-up assessments. Ankle dorsiflexion improved from − 9.2° at baseline to 10.83° at follow-up, while plantarflexion increased from 19.17° to 33.33°. The symmetry index of pelvic oscillations improved from 64.88% to 80.08%.

Significant reductions in joint swelling were also recorded, with ankle circumference decreasing from 3.5 cm to 1.5 cm. Functional performance improved markedly, as reflected by an increase in the locomotor activity test score from 4.48 to 13.33 and in the ADL score from 2.37 to 4.41. No serious adverse events related to the rehabilitation interventions were observed during the study period. Mild transient discomfort, including localized pain or stiffness during therapy sessions, was occasionally reported, particularly during the initial protection phase when joint mobilization and weight-bearing exercises were introduced. No participant discontinued treatment because of these symptoms, and all patients completed the prescribed rehabilitation program.

Discussion

The observed clinical cases of patients with complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) following fractures in the ankle-foot complex underscore the necessity of a prolonged and intricate physical rehabilitation program during recovery. The demographic composition of the study, consisting of 14 women and 9 men, aligns with findings by De Mos, which indicate that women are more frequently affected by CRPS [28], and is further supported by recent epidemiological data reporting a female predominance in CRPS populations [29]. A key feature of the kinesitherapy program employed in this study is adherence to the principle of exerting force from proximal to distal joints, a method commonly utilized in neurological kinesitherapy [19].

Initially, patients presented significant swelling in the affected joint (3.5 cm) and foot (2 cm), along with pain, limited range of motion (notably, a dorsiflexion contracture of 9.2°), and hypotrophy of the thigh (2.5 cm) and lower leg muscles (1.5 cm). Patients with CRPS required a longer duration to achieve a normal gait without aids. The recovery of locomotor function is often prolonged, influenced by the severity of the condition. The transition from ambulating with two underarm crutches to one crutch typically occurs once pain subsides, swelling decreases, and the required range of motion in the ankle joint (5° to 10° of dorsiflexion) is achieved. In some cases, prolonged use of a regular cane may be necessary until muscle hypotrophy resolves and optimal range of motion is restored. An additional 3 to 4 weeks may be needed for gait stabilization to return to pre-injury functional levels.

The results from the locomotor activity test demonstrate a functional recovery rate of 88.87% by the end of the rehabilitation process, while the ADL test indicates that participants achieved a degree above 4, reflecting performance close to normal strength, speed, coordination, and endurance. The average duration for recovery of locomotor activity was 19.43 weeks, supporting findings by Bean D. that significant improvements typically occur within the first six months post-injury [30]. Similar recovery timelines and functional improvements following structured rehabilitation have been reported in previous studies of post-traumatic CRPS, although variability remains high depending on injury severity, pain persistence, and adherence to rehabilitation protocols. This study corroborates that many patients with CRPS recover within 6 to 12 months; however, a notable number of experience persistent symptoms and chronic pain beyond this period [2–4]. A study by Sucio O. indicated a recovery timeframe of 12 to 16 weeks for patients without complications [31]. The symmetry index of pelvic oscillations demonstrated functional recovery to 80.08%, although literature examining the symmetry index in patients with ankle-foot complex trauma complicated by CRPS is limited, highlighting a potential area for future research. Improvements in gait symmetry were associated with enhanced functional mobility and reduced reliance on assistive devices. Emerging neuromodulation approaches, such as transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), have shown promise in chronic pain management and may complement rehabilitation strategies in CRPS [32]. The rehabilitation framework was standardized; however, exercise intensity, progression, and loading were individually adapted according to pain tolerance, functional capacity, and patient progress, reflecting real-world clinical practice.

Although statistically significant improvements were observed across multiple outcome measures, including ankle range of motion, muscle hypotrophy, gait symmetry, functional mobility, and pain scores, the clinical significance of these changes should be interpreted with caution. Minimal clinically important difference (MCID) values, which indicate the smallest change that is meaningful to patients, have not been established for most of these instruments in CRPS populations, particularly in post-traumatic ankle-foot injuries. Consequently, it is difficult to determine whether the observed improvements correspond to perceptible benefits from the patient’s perspective. Therefore, although the improvement in the G-WALK symmetry index reached statistical significance, its clinical significance cannot be definitively established because validated MCID values for gait symmetry measures in patients with CRPS are currently unavailable. Nonetheless, the multidimensional assessment approach employed in this study, encompassing clinical, functional, and instrumental measures, provides insight into rehabilitation progress and functional recovery trajectories, offering practical guidance for clinicians managing similar cases.

The present study contributes to the limited literature examining rehabilitation outcomes in patients with CRPS affecting the ankle-foot complex. Unlike many previous reports that focus primarily on pain reduction or general functional recovery, the current study incorporated a multidimensional assessment strategy combining clinical measurements, functional outcome scales, and objective gait symmetry analysis using an inertial sensor system. A particular strength of the study is the longitudinal evaluation of patients throughout a structured rehabilitation program under real-world clinical conditions, providing a comprehensive perspective on recovery trajectories in this challenging patient population.

The present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The absence of a control group restricts causal inference, and therefore the observed improvements cannot be attributed exclusively to the rehabilitation program. The relatively small sample size and the absence of an a priori sample size calculation limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. The sample size reflects the low incidence of CRPS in post-traumatic ankle–foot injuries and the single-center nature of the study. In addition, although the outcome measures employed are commonly used and validated in musculoskeletal and neurological rehabilitation, not all have been validated specifically for CRPS. This reflects a broader limitation in CRPS research, where disease-specific validated instruments for objective functional and gait assessment remain limited. Data were collected during 2024, and future studies are needed to validate these findings in more recent cohorts. Additionally, variability in individual rehabilitation responses may have influenced the observed outcomes. An additional limitation is the clinical heterogeneity of the study population. The cohort included patients with ankle sprains and different fracture types involving the metatarsal bones, calcaneus, and malleoli. These injuries differ in severity, prognosis, and expected recovery trajectories. However, all patients shared a common clinical diagnosis of complex regional pain syndrome affecting the ankle-foot complex, which represented the primary condition under investigation. Consequently, the observed improvements may reflect a combination of rehabilitation-associated changes and the natural recovery process related to the underlying injuries. Due to the observational design and absence of a comparison group, these effects could not be separated.

Although these limitations affect the statistical power and generalizability of the findings, the cohort represents all eligible patients treated during the study period and provides a comprehensive longitudinal assessment using standardized clinical, functional, and gait-related outcome measures. Importantly, the observational design allowed evaluation of rehabilitation outcomes under real-world clinical conditions, offering clinically meaningful insights into recovery trajectories in a condition that is challenging to study using randomized controlled designs. In addition, blinding of evaluators was not feasible in this rehabilitation setting; however, all assessments were performed by the same clinical team using standardized protocols to minimize measurement variability. The follow-up period was limited to the completion of the rehabilitation program, and long-term sustainability of improvements could not be assessed. Future studies with extended follow-up are warranted to determine the durability of functional recovery in patients with CRPS of the ankle–foot complex.

In summary, this study provides insight into the rehabilitation outcomes of patients with CRPS of the ankle–foot complex following trauma. The structured, multidimensional rehabilitation program was associated with improvements in functional mobility, gait symmetry, and daily activity performance. While limitations such as the lack of a control group and short follow-up period exist, these findings offer valuable guidance for clinical practice and highlight areas for future research.

Conclusion

In this observational study, improvements in ankle joint mobility, gait symmetry, locomotor activity, activities of daily living, and pain intensity were observed during the rehabilitation period in patients with complex regional pain syndrome of the ankle-foot complex. Despite these improvements, not all patients achieved complete recovery, and persistent limitations including reduced range of motion, residual swelling, muscle hypotrophy, and pain during physical exertion remained in some individuals. Although gait symmetry improved throughout rehabilitation, full normalization was not achieved in all cases. Due to the observational design and absence of a control group, the observed changes cannot be attributed solely to the rehabilitation intervention. Further studies with controlled designs and longer follow-up periods are needed to confirm these findings and evaluate long-term recovery outcomes.
