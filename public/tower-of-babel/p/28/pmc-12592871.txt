Immunological Status of Saliva in Children with Asthma by Disease Severity
Authors: Svitlana Leshchuk, Zaki Hakami, Satish Vishwanathaiah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThe primary goal of this study was to evaluate the immunological status of saliva in those children suffering from asthma based on the severity and duration of the disease's prevalence.Materials and methodsA saliva test for the presence of immunoglobulins A (IgA) and G (IgG), and the secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA) and lysozyme activity was conducted in 7, 12, and 15-year-old children suffering from asthma (main group) totaling 98 children in all and another 68 healthy children (control group) to observe and determine the local immunity levels in the oral cavity. Making use of the radial immunodiffusion method in agar gel, the concentration of IgA and IgG in the oral fluid was conducted as a part of the study. The evaluation of immunoglobulins in asthmatics was carried out, depending on the severity and duration of asthma.ResultsChildren suffering from asthma experienced a decrease in the local immunoglobulin mechanisms of saliva protection. Compared to the control group, asthmatic children displayed a lower level of sIgA by 2.0 times, IgA by 1.6 times, lysozyme by 2.2 times, and an increase in the level of IgG by 1.2 times.ConclusionsWith the data obtained, it is clearly indicated that children suffering from asthma are subjected to decreased defense mechanism levels, which, in turn, could be the primary contributing factor for caries development in those children affected by asthma.How to cite this articleLeshchuk S, Hakami Z, Vishwanathaiah S. Introduction

Different environmental triggers and symptoms, including chest tightness, dyspnea, coughing, and wheezing, are the characteristic features of asthma, a chronic inflammatory disease that displays enhanced airway responsiveness to such symptoms. There are chances for spontaneous or medical treatment-related display of reversible airway obstruction based on clinical evaluation and functional demonstration, factors using which the diagnosis is based. Globally, more than 300 million people are suffering from asthma, an augmenting health concern.1

To avoid the onset of exacerbations, arrest the disease's progression, and limit symptoms, asthma treatment predominantly focuses on truncating airway inflammation and inducing bronchodilation. There are various treatment choices available based on the severity of the disease. Some of the main options include short-acting bronchodilators (could be used only in combination with anti-inflammatory drugs but never as a standalone choice), anti-inflammatory agents such as inhaled corticosteroids (CS) or oral CS that finds its use in a selective number of patients affected severely by the disease), leukotriene modifiers, and long-acting bronchodilators. Biologic drug treatments are usable only in patients with severe forms of asthma. Systemic effects2–4 are an outcome when various inhaled drugs are used to treat asthma.

The effects of the treatment procedures of asthma that could induce dental morbidity have been performed by numerous controversial studies.5 Several studies have focused on the impact of childhood asthma on dental caries development and enhancement of caries risk factors. A Slovenian study in asthmatic children confirmed that those children using antiasthmatic medicines regularly suffered from increased caries experience and caries progression over 3 years in both primary and permanent dentitions. There were many other factors linked with higher caries experience in children with asthma besides the use of antiasthmatic medications.6

In comparison to children without asthma, those adolescents and children suffering from the disease are at a higher risk of plaque accumulation and the development of dental caries. But, in the case of gingivitis, erosive tooth wear, or developmental defects of enamel, there was no difference in risk levels between asthmatic children and those not having the disease.7

The numerous biofluids present in the saliva contribute toward a strong biological defensive mechanism against dental caries development.8 Among the immunoglobulins in human saliva, the secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA) offers the most anchoring microbial adherence to the tooth structure, fighting against bacterial toxins and enzymes, and reducing bacterial accumulation. According to a research study conducted by scientists in Saudi Arabia, the lower the salivary flow rate and the sIgA levels, the higher the risk of dental caries in asthmatic children, especially in primary dentition. Reduced saliva flow rate and minimum sIgA levels were typically seen in those with severe asthma levels.9

The study shows inconclusive results concerning the association between inhalational asthmatic medication and tooth wear.10 We have numerous cofounding factors that play a vital role in triggering dental erosion in these patients. Hence, it is the dentist's responsibility to pay more attention to these factors when treating patients with asthma. To assist dentists and help them assess completely, the authors come up with an exhaustive checklist that improves overall evaluation, besides focusing on the medications.10

Selective immunoglobulin A (IgA) deficiency is a common primary immunodeficiency that is frequently diagnosed in Caucasians (with a prevalence ratio of 1:300 to 3,000).11 Moreover, the study results showed a notable decrease in sIgA mean level in those children with asthma, and there is a convincing negative correlation with the decayed, missing, filled teeth (DMFT) mean score. Saliva composition alteration showed a noteworthy decrease in sIgA mean levels, accompanied by substantial alterations to immune response and enhanced risk of dental caries progression.12

Equipping the body against bacterial adhesion and dental plaque formation is possible as the sIgA modifies the immunoglobulins secreted into the saliva. Genetic and environmental factors contribute to the contents of sIgA, which puts the individual at risk for dental caries and asthma, besides playing an active role in the immune system functioning. Many other researchers are vouching for reduced content of sIgA when individuals use CS as antiasthmatic inhalers.13,14

As per reports, infants having low IgA levels expressed a higher risk of asthma attacks and allergic symptoms despite such levels being normal for their age. Lower serum IgA levels were present in children who showed positive response to skin prick tests for common allergens, and children having lower salivary IgA levels were very common victims of allergic rhinitis or atopic eczema.15

Meager changes in IgA- and immunoglobulin G (IgG)2-mediated responses in asthma could be related to the disease. In healthy individuals not suffering from asthma, these levels are normal, and there are possibilities that in those with asthma, the level/quality of immunity protection provided by these isotypes could be modified to some extent.16 The primary goal of this study was to check the immunological status of saliva in those children suffering from asthma based on the severity and duration of the disease's prevalence.

Methodology

The study protocol was approved by the Commission for the Bioethical Expertise and Ethics of Scientific Research of Danylo Halytsky Lviv National Medical University (extract from protocol no. 4 dated April 23, 2012, and extract from protocol no. 8 of October 21, 2019). It was conducted according to Helsinki Declaration on Biomedical Research.

Information about children's somatic health was obtained from medical records of outpatients (form 025/0), obtained from treating doctors in clinical departments (pulmonary allergological). The degree of severity of bronchial asthma in children was determined according to the following classification: I degree—mild intermittent asthma; II degree—mild persistent asthma; III degree—moderate persistent asthma; IV degree—severe persistent asthma.

Study's Subject

In this study, children aged 7–15 years with normal growth and development patterns were included.

Children with dental abscess, congenital or systemic disease, normal growth and development patterns, absence of mucosal lesions, and no history of previous dental treatment undertaken by the time of the exam.17 No medication therapy (only for the control group). The entire process of dental examination was conducted without any use of stimulation for saliva collection but by only using a dental chair, a dental mirror, and an explorer. DMFT index was used to evaluate how much caries the subjects had experienced.

Saliva Sample Collection and Analysis

As per the description provided by Dawes and Weatherell, unstimulated salivary probe collection was conducted.18 For at least 1 hour before the salivary probe collection, children were not supposed to eat or drink anything. The last oral hygiene procedure was completed during the previous night. Between 7.30 am and 8.30 am for 10 minutes, salivary samples were collected in 15 mL test tubes. The samples (approx. 5 mL) were stored safely at a temperature of −70°C until further investigation. In order to assess the local immune response in the oral cavity, the oral fluid of 98 children with asthma (main group) and 68 healthy children (control group) aged 7, 12, and 15 years was collected to analyze the levels of IgA, IgG, lysozyme, and sIgA in both groups.

Utilizing the radial immunodiffusion in agar gel approach developed by Mancini et al.19 in 1965, the concentration of IgA and IgG in the oral fluid was measured using diagnostic tools from nongovernmental organization (NGO), Microgen, Nizhny Novgorod. The immunoglobulin concentration levels were determined in grams per liter (gm/L) using measurements from a calibration graph. A series of reagents (Vector-Best) was used to determine the sIgA.

Lysozyme concentration was carried out by the diffusion method in agar containing 0.05% biomass powder Micrococcus lysodeikticus (Paster EU. Immunology practicum/Paster EU, Ovod VK, Vychot NE. Kijiv-High School: 1989. p. 304). To determine sIgA, reagents [“Vector-Best”] were used. The results were calculated on the Star Fax 1904 immunoenzymatic analyzer. The concentration of sIgA was expressed in gm/L.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis and data illustration were carried out using the Statistica 8.0 computer program. According to the normality of the data, the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the groups. Coefficient and Kendall–Tau helped in determining and measuring any correlation dependencies. To figure out any sort of association between the tested parameters, the Spearman correlation coefficients (r) were calculated. The p-value was set at 0.05 for all tests.

Results

It was necessary to gauge the local immunity status of the oral cavity in those children affected by asthma by keeping in mind the pathogenesis, features of the clinical course, and treatment of asthma. Table 1 revealed the content of IgG, sIgA, IgA, and lysozyme in the saliva. Children suffering from bronchial asthma displayed an average content of sIgA value of 0.066 ± 0.014 gm/L, while the children in the control group showed almost two times higher content (0.131 ± 0.019 gm/L) with a significant difference between them (p < 0.001). The sIgA levels were also substantially lower in the oral fluids of 12- and 15-year-old children in the main group when compared to the children in the control group (0.050 ± 0.006 gm/L vs 0.120 ± 0.027 gm/L, p < 0.05) and (0.037 ± 0.010 gm/L vs 0.141 ± 0.037 gm/L, р < 0.01), respectively. The contents of sIgA decreased in children suffering from asthma, while the contents increased slightly in children belonging to the control group, as their age increased. The same pattern was observed concerning the content of IgA as well—the IgA content of the oral fluid in asthma-affected children was significantly lower compared to children in the control group (0.079 ± 0.010 gm/L vs 0.123 ± 0.025 gm/L, p < 0.05). When the same IgA contents in the oral fluids were determined in 12-year-old (0.125 ± 0.023 gm/L in the main group vs 0.132 ± 0.013 gm/L in the control group, p < 0.05) and 15-year-old children (0.052 ± 0.014 gm/L vs 0.179 ± 0.072 gm/L, respectively, p < 0.01), there was a significant difference in the IgA values between the two groups.

Discussion

The treatment of asthma, affecting millions globally, focuses on reducing airway inflammation and bronchodilation, with options ranging from short-acting bronchodilators to biologic drugs based on disease severity. Regular use of antiasthmatic medications in children with asthma may lead to increased caries progression, highlighting the importance of comprehensive oral health care. Lower salivary flow rates and sIgA levels are associated with higher dental caries risk in asthmatic children, especially in severe cases.20 However, the link between inhalation asthma medication and tooth wear remains unclear, prompting dentists to carefully consider confounding factors during treatment. Additionally, selective IgA deficiency correlates with reduced sIgA levels and increased caries risk in children with asthma, emphasizing the complex relationship between immunity, dental health, and asthma severity.21 Further investigation into the immunological status of saliva in asthmatic children is warranted, which formed the aim of the present study.

The study investigated the levels of sIgA, IgA, IgG, and lysozyme in the saliva of children with asthma compared to a control group. The study revealed lower sIgA, IgA, and lysozyme levels in asthmatic children compared to nonasthmatic peers, with no significant IgG difference. We found a moderate positive correlation between asthma and IgG, suggesting systemic immune involvement. Weak positive correlations with sIgA and negative correlations with IgA were noted. Asthma severity and duration were associated with decreased salivary immunoglobulins, while age affected IgG levels. Lower IgA levels correlated with higher dental caries severity.

Our study yields notable clinical implications, highlighting that reduced levels of sIgA, IgA, and lysozyme may elevate the risk of dental caries in asthmatic children. Regular monitoring of salivary immunoglobulin levels, particularly in those with severe or chronic asthma, can aid in assessing overall immune health and susceptibility to oral health problems. Additionally, recognizing the impact of age on IgG levels can inform tailored interventions and treatments for asthmatic children, aligning with their age-specific immune characteristics.9,20

The findings from our study shed light on the intricate immunological alterations associated with childhood asthma. In our study, we observed a notable decrease in the levels of sIgA, IgA, and lysozyme in children diagnosed with asthma compared to their nonasthmatic counterparts. However, no significant disparity was noted in the levels of IgG between the two groups. These results suggest a potential compromise in mucosal immunity in asthmatic children, as evidenced by reduced levels of key immunological components that play crucial roles in host defense against pathogens. Complementing our findings, the results from another study further delineate the immunological dysregulation characteristic of childhood asthma. Specifically, while IgG3 and IgG4 levels remained comparable between asthmatic and nonasthmatic children, significant discrepancies were observed in IgG1, IgG2, and IgA levels.22 This could be due to the immunosuppressive drugs administered during exacerbations are expected to cause progressive declination in immune function, thereby reducing the essential immunoglobulins.23,24

We observed a medium positive correlation between children with asthma and IgG levels, suggesting a potential association between asthma pathology and systemic immune responses mediated by IgG. Additionally, our results indicate a weak positive correlation between children with asthma and sIgA levels, hinting at alterations in mucosal immunity in asthmatic individuals. However, a negative correlation was observed between children with asthma and IgA levels, underscoring the complexity of immune dysregulation in this population. Comparing our findings with another study, significant differences in serum IgA levels were observed between asthmatic and control groups, with lower levels detected in asthmatic children.25 This discrepancy in IgA levels aligns with our observation of a negative correlation between asthma status and IgA levels. Interestingly, while our study did find a significant difference in IgG levels between asthmatic and control groups, the comparative study reported a nonsignificant positive correlation between asthma control test results and serum IgA levels.25,26 This suggests a potential link between asthma control and serum IgA levels, warranting further investigation into the mechanistic underpinnings of this association.

We observed a reduction in salivary immunoglobulin levels, including sIgA, IgA, and lysozyme, with increasing severity and duration of asthma. This finding underscores the potential impact of asthma pathology on mucosal immunity, highlighting the need for targeted interventions to address immune dysregulation in affected individuals. Interestingly, our study also revealed age-dependent alterations in IgG levels, with a reduction observed in 12-year-olds and an increase noted in 15-year-olds with worsening asthma severity. These findings suggest that the immune response to asthma may evolve over time, warranting further investigation into the underlying mechanisms driving these changes. Comparing our results with those of another study, which focused on serum IgA levels in a larger cohort of subjects, reported varying serum IgA levels across different patient groups, with higher levels associated with older age. This aligns with our observation of age-related changes in IgG levels in asthmatic children.27,28

Specifically, we observed a significant negative correlation between IgA levels and DMFT scores, indicating that lower IgA levels are associated with increased dental caries severity. This finding highlights the potential role of IgA in modulating oral microbial ecology and protecting against caries development. Interestingly, although IgG levels also exhibited a negative correlation with DMFT scores, this association did not reach statistical significance, suggesting a more nuanced relationship between IgG and dental caries in asthmatic children. Furthermore, while sIgA levels demonstrated a positive correlation with DMFT scores, this association did not achieve statistical significance, indicating that other factors may influence the relationship between sIgA and dental caries in this population. Consistent with our findings, prior research has demonstrated a significant increase in dental caries experience among children with asthma, particularly as the severity of the condition worsens.9,29,30 This heightened caries susceptibility may be attributed to various factors, including the use of antiasthmatic medications, such as β2-agonist bronchodilators, which have been implicated in reducing salivary flow rate and altering salivary composition, thereby promoting caries development.31 Additionally, nocturnal asthma symptoms, often managed with syrup or dry-powder inhaler forms of β2-agonists, may contribute to increased caries risk due to their sugar content or adverse effects on salivary flow.32

The chosen methodology for this study was based on established techniques described by Dawes and Weatherell, ensuring consistency and comparability with previous research. Unstimulated salivary probe collection was selected due to its noninvasive nature and ability to provide a representative sample of oral fluid. The strict guidelines regarding fasting and oral hygiene were implemented to minimize potential confounding factors and ensure the reliability of the collected samples. Additionally, the timing of sample collection in the morning hours aimed to capture salivary flow rates at a consistent time point, reducing variability associated with diurnal fluctuations.33

While the study unveils unique insights, it grapples with several limitations. Firstly, the exclusion of children with dental abscesses, congenital or systemic diseases, and those on medication (except for the control group) might have skewed the sample, possibly misrepresenting the broader population. Additionally, the study's geographic specificity may limit the generalizability of its findings to regions with differing demographics, lifestyles, or environmental factors. Moreover, the use of unstimulated saliva samples collected over a brief period may not fully capture the dynamic variations in saliva composition throughout the day. Furthermore, the study's focus solely on IgA, IgG, and sIgA overlooks other potentially relevant components of the immune system, such as cytokines or antimicrobial peptides.

Conclusion

Hence, from the data procured, it is clearly indicative that a decrease in the defense mechanism levels in the oral cavity in children suffering from asthma exists as one of the top contributing factors for caries development in those children suffering from asthma.
