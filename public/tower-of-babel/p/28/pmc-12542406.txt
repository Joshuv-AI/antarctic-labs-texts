Building and sustaining restorative community trust and engagement: the Co-Researcher Activation Network
Authors: Christy J W Ledford, Lillie D Williamson, Ebony B Whisenant, Traci T Greene, Samantha R Jones, Jennifer L Waller, Teena S Sidibe, Felton David Jackson Jr, Nikeshia LaShaundra Lawson, Justin X Moore, Leslie E Wolf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAfter identifying substantial trust gaps between our university and neighboring community, we developed the Co-Researcher Activation Network (CRANE) to cultivate restorative engagement through ongoing transformational relationships. Informed by the Culture-Centered Approach, CRANE is a network of community member groups who identify local health concerns, develop research questions, and generate community-driven solutions. Organized by place rather than interest, groups are systematically created within geographic areas. The model focuses on engaging community members as “co-researchers,” whereby community members play an active role and share in the decision-making process through a collaborative and interdependent relationship with the research team.MethodsUsing RE-AIM domains, we applied convergent mixed methods to test the effects of CRANE and to identify factors associated with fidelity and sustainability of the model.ResultsFrom 2022 to 2023, we convened 21 co-researchers in three groups that met bimonthly. Groups reflected age, racial/ethnic, economic, and educational diversity. Co-researchers’ perceptions of university researcher credibility (trustworthiness, expertise, and caring) significantly increased during the project. Five factors contributed to success: regular, audience-centered communication; small groups; gender segregation; scheduling flexibility; and community meeting spaces. Challenges included hiring issues, travel limits, low technology acceptance, transportation obstacles, and participant payment problems.ConclusionsCRANE is a blueprint for community engagement that honors community members and their expertise, strives for equitable partnership, and moves the needle on metrics of trust. The theoretically-grounded, co-researcher model can not only build but sustain restorative community trust and engagement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-025-00792-2.

Background

In the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic, scientists, clinicians, and policymakers recognized the crisis of trust facing the United States [1]. Trust and mistrust influence the utilization of health services [2, 3], the quality of overall healthcare [4, 5], and the perception of health information and recommendations [6, 7]. These relationships are of particular importance for persistently marginalized and historically underserved communities who are caught in a continuous cycle of negative experience and resulting mistrust that exacerbates health disparities [8]. Thus, building and sustaining trust is paramount to our ability to work collaboratively toward improving health.

The COVID-19 pandemic renewed efforts to examine issues around trust [1], with studies highlighting the importance of individual trust in communicating public health and medical recommendations throughout the pandemic [6, 9]. This generated work on the role of expertise and how experts can be seen as trustworthy [10, 11]. Studies like these, however, focus on individual assessments and perceptions of trust and expertise and neglect community-level factors. In a previous study on COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy, we uncovered ways in which local (e.g., medical college’s history of robbing graves in Black cemeteries) and regional (close proximity to Tuskegee, Alabama) histories influence community trust and relationships with medical institutions [12]. These histories, combined with ongoing community experiences (e.g., medical discrimination’s connections to Georgia’s maternal mortality crisis [13]), not only engender distrust, but underscore African Americans’ concerns about clinical researchers’ trustworthiness and concern [14]. Trustworthiness and caring, in particular, are best demonstrated through relationships. As a community member previously told us, “consistency, communication, and building a real foundation of relationship” [15] are necessary to engage with the community and build trust. Thus, there is a community need for building trusting relationships among community members, clinicians, and researchers.

To address this need, we developed the Co-Researcher Activation Network (CRANE) to build institutional and community capacity for engagement by bringing together community members, local clinical entities serving underserved communities (such as federally qualified health centers (FQHCs)), and university researchers in September 2021. The overarching aim of CRANE was to cultivate restorative engagement through ongoing transformational relationships. We propose CRANE as one model of a voice infrastructure that can activate community members as co-researchers to actively co-create health solutions for their community.

Methods

We conceptualize “community” as a socially-constructed, dynamic system [16] that is bound by place. By approaching community through a public health and community medicine lens, we prioritize the role of community members as neighbors who share the same physical environment. Within the National Institute on Minority Health and Health Disparities Research Framework, the role of place is further emphasized by the community level of influence that describes how community illness exposure, herd immunity, community functioning, community environment, community resources, community norms, local structural discrimination, and availability of health care and safety net services impact community health outcomes [17]. This conceptualization is disease agnostic; it does not focus on gathering people with a single, shared health condition, such as people who live with diabetes or people who have survived breast cancer. It is distinct from approaches to community as a group of people with shared interests or shared health concerns.

Program setting

For this project, we focused on three neighborhoods in the urban core of Augusta, Georgia, that include uninsured or underinsured and underserved, minority populations. Augusta-Richmond County (ARC) is ranked among the least healthy counties in Georgia (124 of 159 counties). The county is 57.5% Black/African American, 35.3% White, and 5.1% Hispanic/Latino [18]. According to the 2022 County Health Rankings [19], 7.6% of residents are unemployed, 39% of people under the age of 18 live in poverty, 16% lack adequate access to food, and 20% of households have overcrowding, high housing costs, lack of kitchen facilities, or lack of plumbing facilities. Residents in ARC report a higher amount of poor physical and mental health days compared to all of Georgia and the United States. Rates for smoking, obesity, physical inactivity, sexually transmitted infections, and teen births are significantly higher than state and national averages. An estimated 95% of students in Richmond County high schools qualify for free or reduced lunch [20].

Program model

The CRANE model is a network structure, which includes groups of community members who identify local health concerns, develop research questions, and generate community solutions. Recognizing the influence of culture in community health, CRANE is grounded in the Culture-Centered Approach [21]. As a culture-centered program, CRANE is a voice infrastructure in which community members generate and share knowledge and inform health decisions within locally situated knowledge [22]. This communication infrastructure has the potential to increase trust through community dialogues [23].

As a program, CRANE is a part of HamesNet, a practice-based research network (PBRN) headquartered in the Department of Family and Community Medicine in the Medical College of Georgia at Augusta University. Although CRANE is primarily resourced through the university, its physical “home” is at the HUB for Community Innovation, a community center located in a neighborhood alongside the university. In this way, the university facilitates collaboration among university researchers, academic clinicians, community clinicians, and community members. Thus, this approach is health system-agnostic; it does not focus on patients from only one health system but instead gathers people who receive care in a variety of local health systems and those who do not have a regular source of health care. Figure 1 illustrates the multi-sector collaboration. CRANE is an alternative model to traditional community advisory boards (CABs) [24] that typically follow the consulting model [25, 26] (e.g., community members choose from a menu of options to inform specific aspects of the research [27]). Content analysis and scoping reviews have demonstrated limited effects of CABs, such as inconsistent alignment with local needs [28].

The model engages community members as “co-researchers,” whereby they play an active role in the decision-making process while maintaining a collaborative and interdependent relationship with researchers. The co-researcher role empowers community members past token participation [29] to shared power throughout the research process. This approach can increase the quality and relevance of research [30], create acceptable and appropriate research designs [31], and result in more credible and relevant outcomes [29]. This approach to community engagement, which includes activities and actions in response to community needs that fall outside of planned research activities, can build meaningful trusting relationships [32].

The CRANE model incorporates the 10 core principles of community engagement: trust, ongoing, co-equal, equitably financed, culturally-centered, bi-directional, multi-knowledge, inclusive, co-created, and shared governance [33]. Our team is committed to addressing the power imbalances in traditional subject-researcher dynamics, recognizing and respecting the expertise of community members and “developing mutually beneficial and non-paternalistic clinical and advocacy partnerships with communities” [34]. We adopt the principle of cultural humility [35] within the context of community engagement by creating a process that seeks to understand and honor community perspectives and experiences in an ongoing manner as opposed to only seeking knowledge about community norms.

Program implementation

In building the Co-Researcher Activation Network (CRANE), we implemented a four-step process: (1) establishing a local linking pin, (2) conducting a needs assessment, (3) selecting and assigning community members to groups, and (4) convening community research groups. The neighborhood research groups were networked together through the linking pin and steering committee and composed of university researchers, community clinicians from each neighborhood, and the community coordinator—all of whom met quarterly.

Establishing a local linking pin

Our approach to community engagement required a local, in-person connection. Initially conceptualized by Likert in 1961, linking pin positions are team members who span two distinct groups within an organization [36]. CRANE is a university-resourced program that empowers voices outside of the university. To maximize the participative-group problem solving and decision-making systems within CRANE, it required a person who links CRANE to the university.

In CRANE, the community coordinator, our linking pin, was an integrated member of both the university research team and CRANE so that she could facilitate the flow of communication, influence, and resources between the groups to enhance mutual understanding and cooperation and reduce mutual suspicion [37]. In this role, the community coordinator was also a boundary spanner [38]. She functioned at the edge of the university, which enabled her to maintain regular contact with both the internal university team and the external CRANE program. To prioritize her role as community member over university employee, the community coordinator was a newly hired university employee who is enculturated in local norms. This position required expert interpersonal communication skills, rather than technical research skills.

Conducting needs assessment

A needs assessment was structured by geography to reflect the conceptualization of community. Semi-structured interviews with community leaders and community members elicited experiences and perceptions of health and well-being in the community as well as obstacles to health. Recruiting at community events, such as athletic events, community fairs, and religious organizations, rather than solely focusing on clinic populations collected a broader perspective. All recruitment occurred within the three neighborhoods of Augusta’s urban core as defined by the city’s Geographic Information System. At the end of interviews, community members indicated if they were interested in joining a community research group. University researchers conducted preliminary analysis of the needs assessment qualitative data to present at later group meetings.

Selecting and assigning community members to co-researcher groups

In line with small group theory, groups included 6–8 co-researchers, the community coordinator, community clinician, and university researcher for a total of 9–11 group members. Organized by place rather than interests, groups were systematically created within geographic areas (i.e., neighborhoods) to facilitate relationship building through shared community culture and in-person meetings with as few structural barriers (e.g., transportation, time) as possible. The community coordinator and project team discussed community members who expressed interest in joining co-researcher groups. These conversations included the interested community member’s demographics to maximize variation demographically and the interviewer’s perception of the interested community member’s communication skills, including their openness and approach to conflict.

Convening community research groups

Meetings were designed according to Tuckman’s stages of group development (forming, storming, norming, and performing) [39]. In line with the core principle of equitable financing and because the academic research team members’ time was compensated, we committed to recognizing community members’ valuable contributions through remuneration. Community members received $25 per hour to participate in group meetings.

Each convening included the community coordinator, a meeting facilitator (individuals who were trained and followed a facilitation guide), a meeting observer (also a university researcher), and co-researchers. Co-researchers were community members who represented the geographic area of interest and community clinicians who served within the geographic area of interest. In general, meetings included two clinician co-researchers and 6–8 community member co-researchers. Throughout meetings, Nominal Group Technique [40] and Q methodology [41] activities maximized inclusion of co-researcher perspectives.

In the first meeting, the community coordinator began the process of creating common purpose, mutual influence, and shared identity. The content of the initial four meetings was structured according to the 2030 Healthy People Social Determinants of Health priorities: economic stability, education access and quality, health care access and quality, neighborhood and built environment, and social and community context [42]. This broad approach allowed the co-researchers to identify the needs of the community (rather than predetermined priorities).

At the meetings, the meeting facilitator presented the preliminary analysis of data from the needs assessment within the context of each of the Social Determinants of Health priorities. The facilitator presented data using language such as “in interviews with you and other community members, we heard …” This language demonstrated that the ideas and concerns co-researchers had voiced during the needs assessment interviews were valued. With this starting point, the facilitator created space for co-researcher interpretation and extension of topics that were generated in the needs assessment. The facilitator recorded emerging topics on a whiteboard at the front of the room. At subsequent meetings, university researchers created written summaries of the previous meeting for co-researchers to review and provide additional input.

At the fifth meeting, the meetings shifted to co-creating solutions, research questions, and subsequent engagement. A professional graphic facilitator attended the group [43] and illustrated group conversation throughout the meeting. At the end of the meeting, she summarized the illustrations and invited feedback from the group. She then integrated the feedback into the illustrations to strengthen feedback loops. The process generated graphics for external-facing communication to describe community needs, research questions, and the community engagement processes.

Evaluation methods

Using the RE-AIM domains [44], we applied convergent mixed methods to test the effects of CRANE implementation and identify factors associated with fidelity and sustainability of the model. Across domains, the research team collected data through four methods.

Tracking metrics

University researchers maintained records of community outreach activities, co-researcher contacts, and attendance at meetings.

Direct observations

A university researcher acted as an observer at each co-researcher group, completing structured observation sheets at each meeting.

Co-researcher reported data

Since mistrust and distrust were noted barriers in this community, we measured change in credibility [45]. Credibility has long been implicated in issues of trust [46] and incorporates three dimensions relevant to drivers of (mis)trust—caring, competence (expertise), and character (trustworthiness). By applying this construct to the domain of research, we can parse apart how community members perceive university researchers as trustworthy versus how community members perceive university researchers to be experts or to care for community members. To test for change, we asked co-researchers to complete a semantic differential scale [47] via an anonymous, pencil-and-paper instrument as part of the initial co-researcher meeting (i.e., pre items) and then again at the final group meetings (i.e., post items). Periodic reflections

Throughout the program implementation, university researchers completed structured periodic reflections [48]. Periodic reflections included written personal reflections followed by guided discussions with the project team, which enabled a collaborative process of intersubjective recognition appropriate for participatory research [49]. The reflections themselves and the field notes from the discussions became part of the data trail.

All four data sources were included in data analysis. As a convergent mixed methods evaluation, we iteratively analyzed quantitative and qualitative data to describe the reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, and maintenance of the program. To continuously improve our processes between co-researcher group meetings, we applied Rapid Qualitative Analysis [50] to qualitative data so that learnings could be applied to next meetings. Each phase of analysis informed the next. Moreover, both university researchers and co-researchers were active in analysis. A critical part of that process was the inclusion of three co-researchers as authors on this paper. In writing, the co-researchers influenced the interpretation of meeting observational data and conclusions regarding sustainability and impact.

For the primary quantitative measure of effectiveness—university researcher credibility—two-sample t-tests, rather than paired t-tests, were performed to examine differences between measurements at the initiation of the program and one year later , due to not collecting subject identifiers to link data from pre to post.

Reach

Throughout the course of the project, we prioritized engaging with neighborhood residents within the community. We interacted with 686 community residents through volunteering and tabling at community events. At these events, we distributed information about CRANE’s goals, information on local medical and social services, and health information on nutrition and cancer screening.

From November 2021 to October 2022, we completed 35 formal interviews with community members. We also completed four interviews with community leaders (not for profit, church, and clinical leaders). In this needs assessment, community members explained how the availability of products and services, social structures, physical structures, and cultural and media messages influenced their trust in public health messages. [Qualitative findings were published elsewhere [15].

We convened 21 co-researchers in three co-researcher groups who met bimonthly beginning in May 2022. The groups included underrepresented racial/ethnic groups (85.7% describe themselves as Black or African American) and men and women (57.1% are women). Groups also represented age diversity (14.3% 25 or younger, 57.1% 26–64 year olds, 28.6% 65 or older); economic diversity (including community members who received Medicaid, federal disability, or housing/nutrition assistance programs); and education diversity (most had their high school diplomas). Community co-researchers had lived in the local area for an average 25.8 years (sd 21.9); they had deep and lasting knowledge of local histories and cultural norms.

We extended our reach through both community and academic dissemination of bi-directional learning and community-ready information. First, throughout the process, co-researchers identified need for a local community resource guide to help community members contact medical and social services. With co-researchers, we co-created a spiral-bound, printed resource guide. In one year, we distributed more than 350 guides through co-researchers and tabling at community events. The groups also co-created brochures for each of the priority areas they identified (See Table 2). The brochures included a description of the priority in the co-researcher language alongside population-level data that described the prevalence of the issue. See Fig. 2 for an example. We gave each community co-researcher a set of these brochures that they shared with their neighbors and community organizations. We also distributed more than 250 brochures through tabling at community events.

Second, we sponsored co-researchers to share the CRANE story at academic conferences. In September 2021, two community members, as well as two underrepresented in medicine medical students, attended their first academic conference to present a poster describing the needs assessment and engagement plan at the Society of Teachers of Family Medicine Quality Improvement Conference. Adoption

Two co-researcher groups were organized by neighborhood as planned. Neighborhood 1 had eight community co-researcher members and one clinical member. Neighborhood 2 had four community co-researcher members and two clinical members. However, these groups were unexpectedly all women. Therefore, we decided to create a third group, not bound by neighborhood but by gender. Group 3 was exclusively men, including nine community members; the clinical role in the men’s group was filled by two medical student members from the Black Men of MCG.

Throughout the project, observers documented behaviors that demonstrated co-researcher adoption. Notably, as a measure of shared identity, co-researchers regularly attended meetings wearing T-shirts and sweatshirts with the CRANE logo, which we had distributed at the beginning of the project.

From the onset of the project, the Steering Committee met quarterly. Although initially comprised of academic researchers, community clinicians, and the community coordinator, it evolved over the course of the project. After 13 months, we invited one co-researcher from each of the three CRANE groups to join this leadership team, which we renamed the CRANE Council. With this unplanned change, CRANE accomplished shared leadership with community members seated at the leadership table.

Implementation

The three co-researcher groups progressed through Tuckman’s stages of group development in the expected order. The groups created unique group norms and expectations. One group was advocacy-oriented and social services-focused, whereas the second was more policy-oriented, and the third was more focused on developing programs and social support.

The network identified three priority areas. Rather than the discipline-centric focus more traditionally held by clinicians and researchers, co-researchers communicated a clear understanding of the biopsychosocial model of care and the social determinants of health. The priority areas included mental health and healthcare, housing insecurity, and gun violence. See Table 2 for community description of each priority.

To facilitate bidirectional learning, where appropriate, as co-researchers identified specific needs, university researchers facilitated connections to local experts and programs to co-researcher groups. As an example, in response to co-researcher prioritization of housing insecurity as a priority, we coordinated a non-profit organization, which provides education and counseling on housing, credit, and financial management, to present to two of the groups.

Analysis of tracking, observational, and reflection data identified five characteristics that were connected to successful engagement: regular, audience-centered communication; small groups; gender segregation; scheduling flexibility; and community meeting space.

Regular, audience-centered communication

Co-researchers preferred texting and phone calls to email communication. The community coordinator adjusted to these preferences and maintained regular contact between meetings with the co-researchers.

Small groups

In planning, we anticipated attrition or absences so that we over-recruited for each group. However, among these groups, attendance was constant, and co-researchers were highly engaged. When all co-researchers were present and engaged, observers documented crosstalk and interruption that did not allow time for all members to share rich perspectives. By the end of the project, we divided larger groups into smaller meetings with a maximum of five co-researchers per meeting.

Gender segregation

Throughout needs assessment interviews, women outnumbered men as community member participants. This imbalance was reflected in co-researcher recruitment. Over the course of the project, the first two groups that formed were solely women. As those groups entered the norming phase, observers documented co-researcher openness in a gender-segregated environment.

As we continued recruitment for a third group, we intentionally recruited only men. A male university researcher reached out to a local community organization that focuses on mentoring Black men to recruit men for the third group. In these sessions, we limited women’s participation to research team members only. We recruited medical students from the Black Men of MCG, a student affinity group, to facilitate group meetings. In this gender-segregated grouping, the men were open and transparent about the unique challenges for Black men in the community.

Scheduling flexibility

Convening groups of community members with conflicting schedules and diverse personal preferences required constant flexibility in scheduling meetings. Each group fell into a routine of general time of day (i.e., afternoon or evening), but the preferred day of the week and specific time of the day shifted throughout the year to accommodate school schedules for parents, daylight savings time for seniors who did not want to drive in the dark, and community member vacations. Among this population with limited resources and job insecurity, some co-researchers changed jobs throughout the project that impacted their availability. Each time the community coordinator demonstrated flexibility in scheduling meetings, co-researchers communicated that it made them feel valued.

Community meeting spaces

Throughout the project, we empowered co-researchers to select meeting spaces. We met in locations as diverse as public library meeting rooms, history museums, community centers, and non-profit organization spaces. Locations as close to co-researcher residences as possible reduced co-researcher barriers to access. Meeting at sites of local partner organizations introduced co-researchers to community services they did not know about, and meetings at local museums created opportunities for general education and team building.

Effectiveness

Overall, effectiveness was demonstrated quantitatively. Table 4 gives the results for the differences between scores from the initiation of the program and one year later (May 2023) for each item, subscale, and the overall scale. The overall credibility scale score was higher at the end of the project than at first meetings. The expertise, caring, and trustworthy subscale scores showed significantly higher means scores.

Observations also demonstrated evidence of increasing trust and engagement with co-researchers. One specific action by a co-researcher demonstrated her perception of mutual value. She invited university researchers to attend her neighborhood association meeting to show her neighbors how our university team supported the co-researchers and their community. A key moment when the project reached bidirectional communication was the graphic facilitation meeting. Through the graphic facilitation process, co-researchers verbally communicated that the graphics illustrated their perspectives. Co-researchers said they felt heard and understood. In debriefs with co-researchers at the final project-period meeting, we collected qualitative data on their experiences throughout the project. Overall, co-researchers had positive feedback. They liked the group setting. They reported feeling heard when they started conversations about “things we see every day.” Some co-researchers were surprised by the experience. One member said, “I never expected it, but it was nice.” Co-researchers appreciated meeting new people and talking about new ideas. One co-researcher reported that “viewpoints that were expressed were eye opening.” By the end of the project, one member summarized, “We’ve become a small family.”

Maintenance

As a program, CRANE was catalyzed by funding through an extramural award. Yet, from the initiation, sustainability was a key feature of our goal to cultivate restorative engagement through ongoing transformational relationships. At the final co-researcher group meeting within the extramurally-funded project period, 100% of co-researcher attendees indicated that they would continue to attend meetings after the end of funding. Co-researchers viewed participation as a positive contribution to their communities. After hearing that we no longer had funding to pay co-researchers for meeting attendance, multiple co-researchers said that their participation was not dependent on payment. One participant said, “This has never been about the money; it’s always been about making this community better.” Co-researchers were concerned not only about the sustainability of the group meetings but also about the resources required to do the work. Co-researchers emphasized that we knew that “something is wrong – we need to do something about it” [emphasis indicated by italics].

At the end of the funded project period, in June 2023, the three co-researcher groups combined into one group, which met quarterly. Attendance has varied from 4 to 9 community co-researcher attendees. This group continues to inform development of community programs and designs of research studies. In collaboration with our community partner, the HUB Augusta Collaborative, we continue to apply for grant mechanisms to fund co-researcher activities.

Most notably, CRANE is part of a community-led project that was named a finalist in the 2024 National Institutes of Health (NIH) Build UP Trust Challenge, a program designed to identify new and promising scalable strategies that increase research participation and the adoption of existing and new tools and approaches to detect, treat, and monitor diseases, conditions, and disorders by improving engagement with minority health populations and populations with health disparities. The Build UP Trust Challenge monetary prize funded an additional year of co-researcher meetings.

Discussion

The CRANE experience suggests that community engagement can build and sustain community trust, while informing research that is salient to community needs and priorities. In developing CRANE’s infrastructure, this project seeded a community-clinical-university collaboration that identified three critical areas for programming and research in our local community: mental health and healthcare, housing insecurity, and gun violence. As an alternative model to traditional community advisory boards (CABs) [24], CRANE actively involved community members in decision-making throughout the course of research, recognizing their unique experiences and expertise. CRANE cultivated restorative engagement through the initiation of transformational relationships. Findings here demonstrated that this networked co-researcher model can impact community perceptions of researchers.

Three contextual factors likely contributed to the success of CRANE; without these three factors, our ability to be responsive to the community would have been limited. The network is hosted within a department of family and community medicine within the only public medical school in the state. First, as a public medical school, the mission of the school recognizes geographic borders of community and prioritizes the needs of the people of Georgia as a public service. Augusta University also has a stated priority of community engagement in the university strategic plan that facilitated university-level support of our efforts.

Second, family medicine, as a medical specialty, values and espouses comprehensive care. This foundational value enabled us to remain disease-agnostic. The network was not bound by a specific disease or disease system. This was important as we discovered that clinicians’ and researchers’ priorities may not align with those of the community. Retaining the commununity’s goodwill and continued engagement requires institutional commitments that go beyond a single research project.

During initial planning meetings, clinical partners indicated that hypertension was likely to be the dominant concern for community members since population level data demonstrated its high prevalence. However, blood pressure did not emerge as a community priority. Instead, co-researchers repeatedly prioritized stressors of daily living, particularly around housing, food insecurity, and gun violence. Similarly, we expected COVID-19 transmission and treatment to be a top concern because implementation started in May 2021. However, co-researchers only discussed COVID when the research team directly asked about it. Rather, co-researchers were focused on the daily struggle of providing shelter and food for their families. Although these emerging topics do not easily categorize into specialist system thinking (e.g., cardiology, infectious disease), they are hallmarks of quality primary care, and our team had resources to respond to biopsychosocial needs.

Third, family medicine cares for patients across the life course. This foundational value prompted us to create multigenerational groups that generated rich conversations about lived experiences in the community. As CRANE grows, this approach continues to direct our steps. In response to co-researchers’ concerns about minority youth, we are currently building a co-researcher group of 15–19 year olds to contribute their voices to this conversation.

The challenges we encountered may also be limited to our institutional and local environments. We expect that other researchers interested in implementing this model may have some of the same challenges that are connected to bureaucratic processes and approvals, such as hiring and travel limitations. Similarly, as researchers wrestle with how to most ethically compensate community partners, they will face institutional rules about payment systems. Outside of institutional walls, we encourage researchers to consider how their community uses technology and has access to transportation services, which are known limitations elsewhere, particularly in rural communities [51, 52]. We suggest our solutions are only a starting point for researchers to find the best way to address these challenges.

Although we had substantial university and departmental support, CRANE was catalyzed by an extramural award. All activities described here required budgetary support, including salary support for the community coordinator, payments for the co-researchers, and meeting costs. For community engagement to be ongoing, equitably financed, and culturally-centered, it will cost money, particularly to do it well. We assert that building and sustaining restorative relationships is money well spent.

The evaluation presented here has strengths and limitations. The application of RE-AIM as an evaluation framework and the mixing of quantitative and qualitative data enabled a richer understanding of the program’s effects. However, the design of this evaluation did not include quantitative measurement of a comparison group but instead relied on comparison to change from baseline measures. Future research should consider how to quantitatively test models of community engagement with randomized designs. Findings must also be considered within the context of our city and relationships between the community and the university. In each city and university, the context will be different.

CRANE is a blueprint for one approach to address the crisis of trust in a way that honors community members and their expertise, strives for equitable partnership, and moves the needle on metrics of trust. This co-researcher activation approach has the potential to improve the relevance, rigor, and validity of translational research.
