Reply: Comment on Nutritional Counseling for Patients With Gastric Cancer After Subtotal Gastrectomy: A Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Jae-Moon Bae
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We thank Dr. Peker for the thoughtful comments [1] on our randomized trial [2] evaluating intensive individualized nutritional counseling after subtotal gastrectomy for early gastric cancer.

We agree that postgastrectomy weight loss is multifactorial and that counseling alone may not fully address all underlying metabolic changes. While combinatorial strategies that integrate individualized counseling with metabolic and/or pharmacological approaches may offer additional benefits, pharmacological options specifically applicable to post-gastrectomy weight loss remain limited in routine clinical practice. Investigational agents targeting appetite and metabolic pathways, including ghrelin analogs (e.g., anamorelin) and receptor agonists, remain under evaluation. Our primary aim was thus to rigorously assess the efficacy of intensive individualized counseling in a strictly controlled study of patients who underwent subtotal gastrectomy only, to assess its value as a reliable, pragmatic, and immediately applicable intervention [2].

Exclusion of patients receiving adjuvant chemotherapy was an intentional methodological decision to reduce confounding factors and better isolate the effect of nutritional counseling, given that chemotherapy can substantially influence oral intake, gastrointestinal symptoms, and body composition through independent mechanisms. This limits the generalizability of our findings to high-risk populations. We agree that future well-designed studies should evaluate the efficacy of intensive counseling in patients undergoing adjuvant chemotherapy, where nutritional risk is greater.

We also appreciate Dr. Peker’s suggestion [1] to incorporate body composition and psychosocial components. In our intervention, dietitians tailored counseling to each patient’s daily life context, including guidance to improve dietary habits and encourage appropriate physical activity [2]. Explicit psychosocial support, family oriented strategies, and behavioral modification techniques may further strengthen adherence and outcomes. Future studies incorporating body composition analyses (e.g., skeletal muscle index and phase angle) and functional assessments will provide a more comprehensive evaluation of postoperative recovery beyond anthropometric and biochemical measures.

In conclusion, our study provides high-quality evidence supporting the adequacy of standard nutritional counseling for carefully selected low-risk postoperative patients with early gastric cancer. Our results align with uropean Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism and European Society for Medical Oncology risk stratification recommendations. We agree that multimodal strategies that incorporate dietary, pharmacological, psychological, and physical interventions warrant further investigation, particularly among high-risk populations.

We appreciate Dr. Peker’s valuable insights, which will help guide future research aimed at optimizing nutritional care for patients with gastric cancer.
