Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors and Interstitial Lung Disease: A Disproportionality Analysis Using the European Post-marketing EudraVigilance Database
Authors: Devron R. Shah, Simon P. Hart, Dominic L. Sykes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIncreasing global frequency of drug-induced interstitial lung disease (ILD) coincides with increasing market introduction of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs).ObjectivesThe aim was to detect disproportional reporting of TKI-induced ILD in the EudraVigilance post-marketing safety database and to scrutinise the prescribing information of these TKIs.MethodsData were gathered on the number of total and individual ILD case safety reports for each TKI marketed in the European Union (EU), together with indications and patient demographics. Information was also obtained on numbers of total and ILD reports for all drugs in the entire database, covering the period January 2002 to March 2024. Chi-squared analyses and two measures of disproportionality, the proportional reporting ratio (PRR) and reporting odds ratio (ROR), were used to ascertain the ILD-inducing potential of TKIs, both as a group and with each TKI individually. The latest prescribing information for each TKI was evaluated for ILD-related information.ResultsTKIs were collectively associated with a significantly stronger disproportionality signal for ILD reports compared to all non-TKI drugs (p < 0.001). There was marked variation in the disproportionality of ILD reporting across the 51 TKIs studied. Potential risk factors included male gender (p < 0.001), age 65–85 years (p < 0.001) and an oncological indication (p < 0.001), particularly non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Fatality rates among cases of TKI-induced ILD were 17.4% overall, 22.2% in patients with NSCLC and 11.5% in those with a non-oncological indication. The prescribing information of 11 TKIs lacked any reference to ILD despite a strong signal that indicated their potential association with ILD.ConclusionsDrug-induced ILD is emerging as an important safety issue, and physicians need to maintain a high index of suspicion of ILD in patients treated with a TKI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40801-025-00515-9.

Key Points

Available evidence suggests a close temporal relationship between increasing market introduction of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and increasing reports of drug-induced interstitial lung disease (ILD).This disproportionality study using the European EudraVigilance database suggests that TKIs as a class are likely associated with a potential to induce ILD, especially in elderly male patients with an oncological indication (particularly non-small cell lung cancer).Physicians need to maintain a high index of suspicion of this potentially fatal complication in patients being treated with a TKI.

Introduction

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is an umbrella term comprising a group of disorders characterised by inflammation and/or fibrosis of the lung interstitium. It is frequently associated with a range of clinical conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, systemic sclerosis and primary Sjogren’s syndrome. However, drugs are now emerging as a significant cause of ILD of varying severity. Reportedly, over 350 drugs may cause drug-induced ILD (DI-ILD), which is often recognised only either late in the development of the drug or during its post-marketing period [1]. In clinical practice, DI-ILD can be acute and in some cases rapidly progressive with a fatal outcome [2]. The American Thoracic Society and European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) now recognise DI-ILD as a sub-classification of ILD [3, 4]. Common to these subtypes are chronic respiratory symptoms such as cough, fatigue and progressive dyspnoea, which may lead to respiratory failure and death.

A recent study of the United States (US) Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) revealed that the top drug classes associated with DI-ILD were anti-neoplastic drugs (47.69%), followed by cardiovascular (11.18%) and anti-rheumatic agents (9.98%) [5]. Among its top 20 culpable drugs were osimertinib and erlotinib, both being tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) indicated for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Another study analysing the safety of 21 TKIs used predominantly for NSCLC identified 2999 cases of DI-ILD in the FAERS database [6].

Both the above studies also noted an increasing number of DI-ILD reports over their study periods (2004–2022). For example, Jiang et al. [5] reported that the number of DI-ILD reports had continued to increase, rising from about 700 reports per annum in 2006 to 3645 reports per annum in 2020. Interestingly, the periods covered by both the above studies [5, 6] closely correspond with the increasing introduction worldwide of targeted small molecule TKIs (also referred to as ‘-tinibs’) [7]. The first of this new class of drugs to be approved was imatinib in 2001 for the treatment of chronic myeloid leukaemia [8]. Since then, well over 50 ‘-tinibs’ have been approved for a range of clinical indications (mostly oncological) [8–10]. However, introduction of imatinib was soon followed by reports associating it with interstitial pneumonitis [11–13]. Subsequently, gefitinib (approved in 2002) and erlotinib (approved in 2004) also began to be associated with post-marketing reports of DI-ILD [14, 15]. These reports raise the possibility of a class effect.

Objectives

In view of the above reports of TKI-induced ILD and the two reports based on the FAERS database, it was decided to explore whether DI-ILD was disproportionately reported following the clinical use of TKIs approved in the European Union (EU) and, if so, to identify any patient- or drug-related risk factors (such as age, gender, indication or the pharmacological target of the TKI). EU-approved prescribing information, known as the Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC), for each TKI was scrutinised for its contents concerning pharmacological targets, indications and ILD-related warnings and precautions for use and undesirable effects.

Methods

This was a retrospective study aimed at quantifying disproportional reporting of ILD cases following the use of TKIs approved and marketed in the EU using the post-authorisation module of the EudraVigilance database of the EU. The study period included January 2002 (when the database was first incepted) to March 2024. As already stated, imatinib was the first TKI to be approved in the EU in November 2001.

EudraVigilance Database

The European Medicines Agency (EMA) of the EU is responsible for hosting and maintaining EudraVigilance, a database system designed for collecting individual case safety reports (ICSRs) of suspected side effects. It is used for evaluating the benefits and risks of medicines during their development (clinical trials module), as well as monitoring their post-marketing safety (post-authorisation module) in the European Economic Area (EEA). The data on suspected adverse drug reactions (ADRs) are initially submitted electronically to EudraVigilance by national drug regulatory authorities and by marketing authorisation holders in the EEA. Since the latter are required to report all suspected side effects regardless of their geographic origin, the EudraVigilance database also includes reports from outside the EEA. Although the Agency has limited control over the completeness or accuracy of the information available, it does carry out quality reviews (including the identification of duplicate reports) and provides feedback on the quality of information it receives. The database is updated weekly.

Identifying TKIs Approved in the EU

Our data lock date was the 10th of March 2024. We identified TKIs approved in the EU using their International Non-proprietary Names (INN) ending with the stem ‘-tinib’ [7] and downloaded the most recent SmPC for each drug.

Quantifying ADR Reports on TKIs and Associated Characteristics

The EudraVigilance post-marketing database was then explored for the number of ICSRs for each TKI held by the EMA. We used the MedDRA preferred term ‘interstitial lung disease’ for the adverse event of primary interest. We also used other MeDRA preferred terms such as ‘pneumonitis’, ‘pulmonary fibrosis’, ‘hypersensitivity pneumonitis’ and ‘idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis’ to exclude the possibility of drug-induced lung injury being more frequently reported by these alternative terms instead of ‘interstitial lung disease’.

The following data were recorded for each drug: numbers of total reports, respiratory reports, ILD reports and fatal ILD reports. For total, non-ILD and ILD reports, data were also collected in terms of their geographic origin (EEA vs non-EEA), reporter status (healthcare professional or non-healthcare professional), as well as age and gender distributions. Data on the numbers of total and ILD reports in the entire post-marketing safety database (all drugs including TKIs) were also obtained from the Pharmacovigilance Unit of the EMA (personal communication).

Significant disproportional reporting was considered to exist if there were at least three ILD reports for the drug concerned and either measure of disproportionality (PRR and/or ROR) was greater than 2. A potential safety signal was considered highly likely to exist if subsequently the lower boundary of the 95% CI of the disproportionality measure was ≥ 1.

Chi-squared analyses were also used to assess whether any readily available demographic characteristics (age and gender) were associated with significant disproportional reporting (and therefore may constitute potential risk factors for TKI-induced ILD). To further consider the potential association of TKI-induced ILD with indication, reports were analysed in terms of drugs indicated for oncological versus non-oncological conditions.

Finally, each SmPC was closely scrutinised for relevant pharmacological and clinical details such as indication, pharmacological target and information concerning the potential of the drug to induce ILD.

Results

As of the 10th of March 2024, there were 53 TKIs approved in the EU. Two of these (deucravacitinib and quizartinib) were not yet marketed and therefore could not be the subject of any post-marketing reports. Hence, the remaining 51 drugs are the subject of our analyses and discussion. Supplementary Data Table 1 provides an alphabetical list of these 51 TKIs (see the electronic supplementary material).

These 51 drugs targeted several different signalling pathways, with some drugs being active at multiple targets with varying potency. The principal pathways most frequently targeted (and the corresponding number of TKIs) were vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) (n = 9), Janus kinase (JAK) (n = 9), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) (n = 8), fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) (n = 8), platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR) (n = 6), anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) (n = 6) and tyrosine kinase from oncogenic transcript from fusion of Abelson1 gene and breakpoint cluster region gene (BCR-ABL) (n = 4). There were 44 TKIs with an oncological indication (of which 16 were indicated for NSCLC), while the remaining seven drugs had non-oncological indications.

Total and ILD Reports

There was a total of 15,715,286 reports in the entire post-marketing safety database for all drugs (including the TKIs), comprising 62,764 ILD and 15,652,522 non-ILD reports (personal communication from EMA). For the 51 TKIs under evaluation, there was a total of 333,194 reports, comprising 7147 ILD and 326,047 non-ILD reports. Of the 333,194 TKI reports, 48,315 (14.5%) concerned respiratory, thoracic and mediastinal system organ classes. The 7147 ILD reports represented 14.8% of respiratory reports and 2.1% of total reports (Supplementary Data Table 1; see the electronic supplementary material).

Demographic and Reporting Characteristics of All Reports Related to TKIs

Of the 333,194 TKI reports, 87,737 (26.3%) and 245,457 reports (73.7%) were of EEA and non-EEA origins, respectively. Reports were made by healthcare professionals (257,378 reports, 77.2%) and non-healthcare professionals (75,657 reports, 22.7%). Reporter status was not specified for the remaining 159 reports (0.1%). The patient age and gender demographics of the 333,194 total reports are presented in Tables 1 and 2. Of note, there were 115,198 reports (34.6%) in the age range 18–64 years and 106,391 reports (31.9%) in the age range 65–85 years. There were only 8197 reports (2.5%) in the 85+ years age group. Age was not specified in 99,851 reports (30.0%). Males represented 146,229 reports (43.9%) and females 165,355 reports (49.6%). Demographics and Reporting Characteristics of TKI-Induced ILD Reports

Of the 7147 ILD reports, 97.4% were made by healthcare professionals. As shown in Tables 1 and 2, the two main age groups were 18–64 years (1934 reports, 27.0%) and 65–85 years (3571 reports, 50.0%). These reports included 3677 males (51.4%) and 2928 females (41.0%); the gender was not specified for the remaining 542 reports (7.6%).

Of the 51 TKIs, 44 had oncological indications (of which 16 were for NSCLC) and seven were approved for non-oncological indications. These 7147 ILD cases were associated with 40 of these 51 drugs.

The percentage of ILD reports as a proportion of total reports was 2.5% for the 44 TKIs with oncology indications, compared to 0.6% for the remaining seven non-oncology TKIs. The corresponding percentages were 7.7% for the 16 TKIs indicated for NSCLC and 0.9% for the 28 TKIs indicated for a variety of other oncology conditions.

Disproportionality of TKI-Induced ILD Reports

Chi-squared analysis revealed that TKIs as a group were associated with a significantly higher frequency of ILD reports compared to all non-TKI drugs combined (χ2 = 26,076.57, df = 1, p < 0.001), suggestive of a class effect. A subsequent Chi-squared test of association (within the entire class of 51 TKIs) demonstrated that individual TKIs differed significantly in their potential to induce ILD (χ2 = 28,117.41, df = 50, p < 0.001).

The PRR and ROR measures across all the 51 TKIs ranged from 0.00 to 60.15 and from 0.00 to 78.11, respectively, with 22 drugs meeting our pre-defined criteria for clinically significant disproportionality (PRR and/or ROR measure greater than 2, and with the lower boundary of the 95% CI ≥ 1; see Table 5). Potential Risk Factors for TKI-Induced ILD

Chi-squared analyses (of data that excluded ‘not specified’ cohorts) revealed that ILD frequency was statistically more frequent in males (χ2 = 206.92, df = 1, p < 0.001) and in patients aged 65–85 years (χ2 = 736.05, df = 4, p < 0.001). An analysis comparing the frequency of TKI-induced ILD with indication revealed that ILD reports were statistically more frequent in association with the TKIs indicated for oncological indications (χ2 = 982.51, df = 1, p < 0.001). NSCLC by far dominated the range of oncology indications.

Seven drugs with exclusively non-oncology indications were identified (abrocitinib, baricitinib, filgotinib, fostamatinib, ritlecitinib, tofacitinib and upadacitinib). The SmPCs of the remaining 21 TKIs revealed a mixed pattern; 11 drugs had no cases of ILD reported following their use at data lock-point, six had too few reports (collectively 24 reports between them) to warrant any SmPC comment and four included the risk of ILD in section 4.8 on ‘Undesirable effects’. This section of the SmPC typically presents ADRs by five categories of frequency: very common (≥ 1/10), common (≥ 1/100 to < 1/10), uncommon (≥ 1/1000 to < 1/100), rare (≥ 1/10,000 to < 1/1000) and very rare (< 1/10,000).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge this is the first study to analyse the ILD-inducing potential of a whole class of TKIs, including agents with oncological and non-oncological indications, using data from the EudraVigilance database.

Of the 51 TKIs in our study, eight targeted EGFR and six targeted ALK (accounting for 61 and 7%, respectively, of our 7147 ILD reports). This compares well with the 64 and 6%, respectively, of the 21 TKIs studied by Zhang et al. [6]. The pathogenetic mechanisms of DI-ILD induced by anticancer drugs, although not well understood, are believed to involve two possible mechanisms, namely, direct cytotoxic effects and immune-mediated injury, leading to inflammation [17]. Our analysis revealed that ILD reports were associated with TKIs active at a variety of receptors (VEGFR, JAK, EGFR, FGFR, PDGFR, ALK, BCR-ABL), albeit with varying potency. Therefore, although TKIs as a class are associated with ILD, the potential and potency to induce ILD likely varies widely between different TKIs, an observation supported by a previously reported analysis [18]. Our data confirms this variability as illustrated by Fig. 1, which shows that the 15 TKIs with the highest PRR and ROR in our study are active at different targets without any consistent pattern. Interestingly, there are numerous case reports of successful substitution of a culprit drug that induces ILD with another agent active at the same target, thus suggesting that there is an intrinsic variability in the ILD-inducing potential of TKIs regardless of their pharmacological target. Examples of this include switches between agents targeting EGFR [15, 19–22], BCR-ABL [23, 24], ALK [25–27] and MET [28]. To complicate matters, there are also reports of successful re-administration of the same culprit drug, suggesting some patient-specific factors. Examples of this include successful re-challenge with erlotinib [29], crizotinib [30] and other agents targeting EGFR [31, 32].

Our data on the age and gender distributions of ILD cases are broadly consistent with those of Jiang et al. [5]. The age group 18–80 years accounted for 70% of their 32,821 cases, compared with the age group 18–85 years, which accounted for 77% of our 7147 cases. Similarly, males and females accounted for about 49 and 40% of their cases, respectively, compared with 51 and 41% of ours. In their series of 2999 reports of ILD in patients receiving 21 TKIs for the treatment of NSCLC, Zhang et al. [6] reported males and females being equally distributed (45% each, gender not specified in 10% of cases) and a fatality rate of 36.75%. In our analysis of 4883 reports of ILD associated with 16 TKIs indicated for NSCLC, 51% were males and 41% were females (gender not specified in 8%), and the fatality rate was 22.2%.

The two measures of disproportionality (PRR and ROR) in our study correlated well with each other, and the ranking of the signal strength from highest to lowest was identical for both measures (Table 5). Both revealed disproportional reporting of ILD in terms of TKIs as a group relative to all other drugs in the EudraVigilance database. Our study also highlighted a wide variation in the strength of disproportional reporting of ILD cases across the 51 TKIs. Of the 51 TKIs, 22 yielded a measure of disproportionality > 2, with the lower boundary of the 95% CI being ≥ 1. Therefore, these drugs may be considered to generate a potential safety signal. Of these 22 drugs, the PRR and ROR values ranged from 60.15 and 78.11, respectively, for gefitinib, to 2.10 and 2.10, respectively, for axitinib.

The fatality rates among the drugs we studied also varied substantially given their wide-ranging targets and potency. Our analysis of 7147 ILD cases identified an overall fatality rate of 17.4% (1243 fatalities). Among our 44 TKIs with oncological indications, the fatality rate was 17.7% (1201 fatalities out of 6781 cases) compared to a rate of 11.5% (42 fatalities out of 366 cases) for the seven drugs with non-oncological indications. Of 4883 reports associated with the 16 TKIs indicated for NSCLC, the fatality rate was 22.2% (see Table 3), with the individual rates ranging from 0% for four TKIs (capmatinib, entrectinib, lorlatinib and selpercatinib) to 33.9% for gefitinib. In terms of the pharmacological targets of these 16 TKIs, the fatality rates were 24.5% for the five EGFR and 6.0% for the six ALK inhibitors. This pattern is broadly consistent with findings by Zhang et al. [6], who reported an overall fatality rate of 36.75% among 2999 cases of ILD in patients with NSCLC, with a higher rate of 44.53% following EGFR inhibitors and a much lower rate of 14.97% in those receiving ALK inhibitors.

Only 26.3% of the 333,194 total reports from the EudraVigilance database in our analysis originated from within the EEA. We have no information on the origin of the remaining 73.7% of the reports in our analysis. However, Pinheiro et al. have previously reported that as of 31 December 2014, there were 26,551 case reports of ILD in the EudraVigilance database, of which 17,526 (66%) had originated from Japan [33]. Therefore, a significant proportion of our ILD reports are also likely to have originated from Japan, given the reporting requirements on the marketing authorisation holders [34]. This assumption is also supported by findings from the two studies using FAERS data that the top three countries reporting DI-ILD cases were Japan (34.4 and 44.1%), the USA (13.5 and 22.8%) and France (3.4 and 11.6%) [5, 6]. A systematic review of DI-ILD by Skeoch et al. [1] also noted that more than one-third of studies on DI-ILD originated from Japan, mainly large post-marketing studies.

Ethnicity is now recognised as an important risk factor in DI-ILD [35]. The incidence of TKI-associated ILD is known to be higher in the Japanese population (around 4–5%) than in Western populations (≤ 1%) [36, 37]. Saijo [38] has reported that the prevalence of DI-ILD following treatment with TKIs targeting EGFR is high in the Japanese population and that the frequency of EGFR mutations in tumours is also significantly higher among East Asian populations. Amongst TKIs that target EGFR, Ishibashi et al. [39] have reported a significantly higher incidence of DI-ILD associated with osimertinib (a third-generation TKI) compared with first-generation TKIs (such as erlotinib and gefitinib). Osimertinib has been shown to bind irreversibly to certain mutant forms of EGFR at an approximately ninefold lower concentration than to the wild-type [40]. These findings emphasise the challenges faced by regulatory authorities regarding the origin of spontaneous reports when evaluating post-marketing safety signals and contemplating domestic safety alerts.

The results of our Chi-squared analyses suggested that the use of a TKI in the treatment of malignancy was associated with a higher risk of developing ILD, particularly in patients with NSCLC. Conversely, malignancy reportedly has a higher frequency in older non-oncology patients with a primary diagnosis of ILD. In one study of 5350 patients with a primary diagnosis of ILD, 248 patients (4.6%) were subsequently confirmed to have developed malignancies. Lung cancer was the most common (incidence of 1.9%) followed by gastrointestinal malignancies (incidence of 0.9%) [41]. These authors concluded that male ILD patients aged between 60 and 69 years were at the highest risk of developing a malignancy. Consistent with this observation, another study reported 324 patients with lung cancer among 3178 patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) [42]. By the end of a 10-year follow-up period, 26.6% of IPF survivors had been diagnosed with lung cancer. Interestingly, the frequency of severe ILD has been reported to be higher when associated with thoracic cancers [43].

Of the seven TKIs with non-oncological indications in our study, six had low disproportionality measures for ILD and failed to meet our pre-defined criteria (see Table 6), the exception being filgotinib. Six of these seven TKIs are JAK inhibitors, whereas fostamatinib is a spleen TKI. This finding is consistent with the report that the JAK2/STAT3 axis is a major pathway activated in ILD patients and animal models, leading to the conclusion that the JAK/STAT pathway plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of ILD [44]. This signalling pathway is activated in ILD by a variety of cytokines and growth factors [45] and JAK inhibition is associated with a reduction in inflammation [46]. This would suggest that JAK inhibition mitigates the risk of ILD. In this context we note that three other JAK inhibitors (fedratinib, momelotinib and ruxolitinib) among our 51 TKIs, despite having an oncological indication (but with low inflammatory burden), accounted for only 51 cases of ILD (0.24%) among a total of 21,286 reports. In contrast, although filgotinib is also a JAK inhibitor, it is indicated for previously unresponsive moderate to severe active rheumatoid arthritis or ulcerative colitis. The heavy inflammatory burden associated with these indications may explain its potential to induce ILD.

Our analysis of the SmPCs of the 51 drugs regarding ILD-related information revealed that there were 11 SmPCs with no reference at all to ILD despite post-marketing reports of this event (Table 7). Importantly, the fatality rates for these drugs ranged from 5.5 to 16.7%. These included five of the 22 TKIs that met our criteria for significantly disproportional reporting of ILD (lenvatinib, filgotinib, cabozantinib, gilteritinib and axitinib). Interestingly, Jiang et al. had also reported that the US drug labels of half of their top 20 culprit drugs lacked any comment on their potential association with ILD [5]. In Japan, ILD-related label changes to the ‘clinically significant adverse reactions’ sections of product labelling were made in the post-marketing period for 93 drugs between April 2002 and March 2017. For 87 of these drugs (92%), the changes involved the inclusion of ILD in the label, whereas for the remaining six drugs (8%), the changes involved further characterisation of the pre-existing ILD risk. Anti-neoplastic drugs and immunomodulators represented 21 of these 93 drugs (22.6%) [35].

Although designated by the World Health Organization (WHO) as an angiogenesis inhibitor and not as a ‘-tinib’, we briefly comment on nintedanib since it illustrates well the regulatory challenges involved when evaluating and interpreting spontaneous reports of ADRs. It is a TKI approved in the EU for use in patients with NSCLC (brand name VARGATEF®) as well as in patients with IPF, chronic fibrosing ILDs with a progressive phenotype and systemic sclerosis-associated ILD (brand name OFEV®). It is principally a triple angiokinase inhibitor, blocking VEGFR, PDGFR and FGFR kinase activities. A comprehensive review of data has reported that nintedanib is effective in reducing the decline in forced vital capacity and improving overall survival and progression-free survival [47]. In a phase 3 clinical trial in patients with fibrosing ILD (INBUILD trial), progression of ILD was reported in 4.8% of nintedanib-treated patients compared to 11.8% on placebo [48]. In another large dataset of six clinical trials (including the two INPULSIS trials), the rates of progression of IPF were substantially lower in the nintedanib group compared to placebo [49]. The results of a retrospective real-world study involving 187 patients with IPF across three UK tertiary IPF centres also showed that nintedanib is effective and has an acceptable adverse event profile [50]. Surprisingly, the EudraVigilance database included 10,144 total reports of ADRs associated with nintedanib as of May 2024, which included 141 reports of ILD (1.4%) with 36 fatalities and 504 reports of IPF (5.0%) with 221 fatalities. Of these reports, 91% were associated with patients treated with nintedanib for the above IPF indications. These data suggest that due to failure of efficacy, the deterioration of a condition under treatment could unwittingly be reported as an ADR.

The term ‘interstitial lung disease’ is the term most frequently used to report drug-induced lung injury, not only by reporters but also by regulatory authorities in drug labels. However, we recognise that other terms such as ‘pneumonitis’, ‘pulmonary fibrosis’, ‘hypersensitivity pneumonitis’ and ‘idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis’ may also be used (especially if reports are made by those more familiar with international ILD classification guidelines or whom have access to a diagnosis by a multi-disciplinary team). To ensure that we have not materially underestimated the extent of drug-induced lung injury, we checked for the number of reports with these additional terms. Our dataset of 7147 reports of DI-ILD, extracted using exclusively the search term ‘interstitial lung disease’, captured 75% of all the cases reported by these five terms collectively. This is reasonably close to the 83% rate reported previously from Japan, compared to 29% from the rest of the world [51].

Notwithstanding, our study has several limitations that are typically associated with systems of spontaneous reporting of ADRs. Firstly, there is no information on the number of patients exposed and often on the extent and pattern of drug usage (in terms of its indication, dose and duration, presence of co-morbidities and use of interacting medications). Secondly, there is significant under-reporting of ADRs which varies between drugs and over time [52–56]. The seriousness or otherwise of an ADR is also a highly relevant factor that determines whether an event is reported [55, 57]. Often, the reporting pattern is influenced by publicity resulting from social media, publication of a case report or an alert from a regulatory authority [58–60]. Despite these limitations, spontaneous reporting systems are crucial in surveillance of drug safety at large, and they have proved highly valuable in generating ‘real world’ safety data. Together with published case reports, spontaneous reports of ADRs have been pivotal in regulatory drug safety alerts to promote safe use of drugs and most drug withdrawals from the market [59, 61–63].

Conclusion

In conclusion, DI-ILD is a rapidly evolving safety issue that deserves as much importance as that attached to other specific safety issues such as drug-induced QT-interval prolongation, hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity [64]. The importance of DI-ILD in terms of increasing incidence over time and its future clinical impact cannot be underestimated given the rapid progress in the development of novel drugs, especially in the field of oncology.
