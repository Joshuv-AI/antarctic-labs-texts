Intraoperative 3D Navigation‐Guided Resection of Enlarged Transverse Process for Treating Bertolotti Syndrome Unresponsive to Noninvasive Treatments: Case Report
Authors: Jakub Jankowski, Marcin Glin, Natalia Maślanek, Wiktoria Chyra, Agnieszka Pawełczyk, Maciej Radek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBertolotti syndrome is a lumbosacral anomaly that induces persistent lumbar pain. This case describes 3D navigation‐guided resection of an enlarged transverse process. Sufficient decompression provided pain relief refractory to noninvasive treatments. ODI and SF‐36 scales validated clinical efficacy, reflecting profound enhancement in the patient's functional and health‐related quality of life. Introduction

Bertolotti syndrome is a congenital defect associated with the presence of lumbosacral transitional vertebra (LSTV), resulting in chronic lower back pain. This defect involves either the sacralization of the lowest section of the lumbar spine or the lumbarization of the highest sacral section [1, 2].

Castellvi developed a fourfold classification of LSTV based on morphological characteristics. Type I includes unilateral (Ia) or bilateral (Ib) dysplastic transverse processes, which are triangular and measure at least 19 mm. Type II features unilateral (IIa) or bilateral (IIb) pseudoarticulation of the transverse process and sacrum, with incomplete lumbarization or sacralization. Type III describes unilateral (IIIa) or bilateral (IIIb) complete lumbarization or sacralization, where the connection between the transverse process and the sacrum is fully ossified. Type IV, also referred to as mixed type, exhibits features of Type II on one side and Type III on the other [1].

While the prevalence of Bertolotti's syndrome was initially estimated to range from 4% to 8% of the general population [3, 4, 5], more recent data have found this value to be as high as 30%. However, the rates of diagnosis vary considerably between studies, and the true prevalence of undiagnosed Bertolotti's syndrome remains unclear. Indeed, the condition is frequently overlooked in clinical practice, with a Malaysian case series indicating that only one of 16 patients with confirmed Bertolotti's syndrome had been previously diagnosed [6]. Furthermore, a considerable gap exists between the onset of symptoms and their diagnosis, with a mean duration of 41.4 months [7] There is hence a great need to raise awareness of the condition among clinicians.

Epidemiological studies indicate that Bertolotti's syndrome is approximately two times more prevalent in male patients compared to female patients. Interestingly, in such cases, the men are more likely to demonstrate L5 sacralization, and the women lumbarization of the sacrum and the presence of an accessory articulation between L5 and S1 [4, 8, 9]. A study of 841 patients with Bertolotti syndrome by Lorenzo Nardo et al. found no significant association between human race and incidence of LSTV [10]. According to Castellvi, 40% of LSTV cases are classified as type I or II. Although type Ia is the most common, a higher level of pain is observed in types II, III, and IV. Among patients with type II, more than 70% report lumbosacral pain [8].

A key factor triggering the emergence of pain in Bertolotti syndrome is biomechanical disruption characterized by hypomobility of the lumbosacral joint: any limitation in mobility of L5‐S1 is compensated by hypermobility of the intervertebral lumbar joints above the LSTV, which increases the risk of disc herniation [8, 9, 11]. Another factor is the presence of degenerative changes during pseudarthrosis, attributed to the anomalous transverse process and ala of the sacrum [8, 9, 12, 13]. Abnormalities in the structure of LSTV can lead to nerve impingement arising from intervertebral disc compression, potentially manifesting as neuropathic pain [13]. The LSTV may also influence the spinal canal and foraminal stenosis, supporting the pathogenesis of Bertolotti syndrome [11, 13, 14].

Case History

A 29‐year‐old man presented with a 6‐year history of chronic lower back pain. During this period, the patient underwent numerous consultations with physicians from various specialties, yet a definitive diagnosis was not established. His medical history failed to reveal any comorbidities or other risk factors.

The pain was localized near the midline to the spine, around the upper part of the left sacroiliac joint area. The pain was so excruciating that it prevented him from undertaking normal activities. The patient had even been laid off from his office job due to excessive sick leave. Despite prior treatment comprising conventional analgesic and anti‐inflammatory pharmacotherapy, as well as antiepileptic drugs used for neuropathic pain and physiotherapy, the patient reported no significant relief.

Methods

Physical assessment revealed focal tenderness upon palpation of the sacroiliac joint. The pain was exacerbated with trunk rotation. The patient reported no radiation to the lower limbs. Provocative standard sacroiliac joint maneuvers were inconclusive. Neurological examination findings were unremarkable, with no discernible motor deficits or sensory aberrations noted.

CT imaging revealed left‐sided pseudoarticulation of the transverse process and sacrum with incomplete fusion (Figure 1), indicating a diagnosis of Bertolotti syndrome type 2a [1].

Preoperative imaging. 3D CT scans of the enlarged left‐sided transverse process of the L5 vertebra (arrow) which articulate with the sacral ala (asterisk): Postero‐lateral view (A) and anterior view (B).

Based on the results of the clinical examination, a diagnostic block was performed under x‐ray. A combination of methylprednisolone acetate (40 mg), 0.5% bupivacaine (6 mL), and 1% lignocaine (1 mL) solution was injected. The procedure was uneventful, and the patient reported complete relief of pain. However, 2 days after the administered block, the symptoms recurred, confirming the diagnosis of Bertolotti syndrome. Consequently, a determination was made to proceed with processectomy using 3D navigation.

The patient was placed in a prone position. A midline incision of the skin was performed at the level of the L4 spinous process. The spinous process of the L4 vertebra was exposed and a navigational clamp was placed. O‐arm images were obtained and the L5 left transverse process was identified. Using 3D navigation, a paramedian skin incision was performed, oriented perpendicular to the medial iliac crest. The abnormal transverse process was gradually reached by detaching ligaments and cutting through muscle layers under the operating microscope. A Caspar retractor was used. The distal fragment of the abnormal transverse process was reduced using a high‐speed drill, and then the further part of the transverse process was cut with a Kerrison and withdrawn precisely. Following this, the edges of the bones were waxed. Postoperative O‐arm images were taken, and the optimal range of decompression was achieved (Figure 2).

Preoperative and postoperative comparison. Preoperative CT scans of the left‐sided enlarged transverse process of the L5 vertebra: (A–C). Postoperative CT scans of the reduced left‐sided transverse process of the L5 vertebra: (D–F).

The criterion for successful decompression of the lumbosacral transitional vertebra (LSTV) was the creation of a gap between the proximal portion of the transverse process and a narrow residual bony fragment representing its distal segment; this should be visible using intraoperative O‐arm imaging. In this case, the gap extended across the full section of the transverse process and exceeded the diameter of the high‐speed drill bit used for resection, measuring approximately 0.6 cm.

Throughout the hospitalization period, starting from the day of surgery, the patient received intravenous metamizole (2.5 g) and dexketoprofen (50 mg) twice daily. This analgesic regimen provided effective postoperative pain control.

Active mobilization, including assisted verticalization and ambulation with a walker, was initiated on postoperative day 1. On the following day, the patient achieved independent verticalization and ambulation without the need for a walker or assistance. A gradual improvement in mobility was observed over the next few days, accompanied by a significant reduction in pain intensity.

The patient was discharged on postoperative day 4 in good general condition, with no evidence of neurological decline. At the time of discharge, the patient was prescribed diclofenac (75 mg) on an as‐needed basis for pain management and was referred to a rehabilitation facility to support further postoperative functional improvement.

Currently, no additional imaging has been planned. The patient was instructed to seek immediate medical attention in the event of recurrent spinal pain, at which point appropriate imaging studies (such as MRI or CT) would be performed based on clinical necessity.

Conclusions

The patient was evaluated both before surgery and 6 months afterwards using a 10‐point Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for pain assessment, the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), and the 36‐item Short Form Health Questionnaire (SF‐36) [15, 16]. The pain intensity was described as the maximum value of 10 preoperatively; however, this value fell to two points 6 months after the procedure.

The Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) is used to assess functional disability. It has demonstrated substantial validity, as it encompasses activities of daily living that pose challenges for patients experiencing back pain [15]. The ODI scale includes pain intensity, walking, standing, sitting, sleeping, personal hygiene, lifting, sexual life, social life, and traveling [17]. Before the operation, the patient was found to have an ODI of 54%, indicating severe disability, that pain remained a major problem, and that activities of daily living were significantly impaired. In the postoperative period, the score fell to 4%, indicating minimal disability; the patient could manage most activities of daily living and take up gainful employment.

The SF‐36 questionnaire allows the patient to make a self‐assessment of their own health. It consists of 36 statements that define physical functioning, limitations due to physical health, pain perception, general sense of health, vitality, social functioning, emotional functioning, and mental health [18]. The results indicated an improvement in all sections. The greatest progress was observed in emotional well‐being, with the score increasing by 80% from the first to the second time point. In addition, overall health rose from 10% in the preoperative period to 70% postoperatively.

The processectomy performed using 3D navigation yielded a conspicuous improvement in the patient's condition. The level of pain intensity significantly decreased, allowing the patient to return to performing basic activities, such as lifting objects or climbing stairs, which were previously impossible to perform. Most importantly, the procedure allowed the patient to return to work, following his previous dismissal due to excessive sick leave.

After a 6‐month postoperative follow‐up period, our findings indicate that intraoperative 3D navigation‐guided resection of the enlarged transverse process represents a rapid, safe, and effective treatment for relieving pain associated with Bertolotti syndrome.

Discussion

The treatment of Bertolotti syndrome remains a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge with specific obstacles. One such limitation is that, despite the widespread availability of advanced imaging techniques, the condition is still often overlooked—primarily due to its low prevalence and relatively low profile among diagnosticians [3, 8, 9]. Diagnostic confusion may also arise due to the overlap of symptoms, which may derive from commonly observed etiologies of low back pain, such as sacroiliac joint dysfunction or lumbar disc pathology. Furthermore, even standard imaging techniques have significant limitations [9]. For instance, standard anteroposterior radiographs demonstrate only 76%–84% accuracy for detecting lumbosacral transitional vertebrae (LSTV), and 53%–58% accuracy in correctly classifying them [9, 19]. Misidentification on MRI is also frequent: Tokgoz et al. report that 35.1% of patients with an LSTV were incorrectly diagnosed as having normal segmentation, while 60% of correctly diagnosed LSTVs were associated with an incorrect number of vertebral levels. These findings underscore the need for high‐resolution imaging and meticulous anatomical evaluation, especially when surgical planning is considered [9, 20]. One significant therapeutic challenge associated with Bertolotti syndrome is the limited detection rates in the literature; indeed, most current studies comprise case reports and research involving small patient cohorts. The existing literature also lacks consistency with regard to the choice of follow‐up periods; this prevents any definitive and validated conclusions being drawn regarding the efficacy of specific conservative treatment modalities. Existing publications suggest that patients who implement lifestyle modifications, engage in targeted physical therapy, and receive pharmacological interventions experience temporary alleviation of pain and enhanced range of motion [21, 22].

One conservative treatment method is the administration of a blockade. In most cases, this can result in a reduction or cessation of pain, particularly in Bertolotti syndrome type 2a [23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. However, this approach typically does not provide lasting effects, and the duration of its action can vary significantly. This variability is illustrated in a cohort study by Marks et al., which reports that the length of pain reduction following nerve block ranged from 1 day to 27 months [26]. Additionally, a study by Jonsson et al. suggests that a correlation may exist between the response to the blockade and subsequent outcomes of surgical treatment [25, 28]. Although Santavirta et al. did not explicitly observe this association, they still recommended the use of anesthetic injections as part of the diagnostic process [27].

The most common forms of surgical treatment involve either partial removal or complete resection of the LSTV [13]. A significant challenge faced by the surgeon is the anatomical proximity of the LSTV to the L5 nerve, which, combined with the limited operative field, increases the risk of iatrogenic nerve injury. In addition, the procedure is relatively expensive and uncommon in less developed countries. Nevertheless, a retrospective study found patients with Castellvi type I or II Bertolotti's Syndrome who underwent transverse processectomy reported a 50% decrease in pain from their pre‐surgery levels, as assessed by VAS [28, 29]. The effectiveness of processectomy was also demonstrated in a study by Johnson et al., in which nine out of 11 patients reported significant relief from pain following the procedure; of the remaining two, one had severe degenerative changes in the lumbar spine, for which the available literature recommends spinal fusion surgery, and the other case remains unclear. Consequently, it is advisable to consider LSTV resection in younger individuals who do not exhibit degenerative changes in the spine [13, 25, 27].

Current publications underscore the need for further research into the biomechanics and the impact of LSTV on the degeneration of adjacent spinal segments, which could ultimately refine treatment selection and improve outcomes [30, 31, 32].

Biometric analysis conducted by Zhu et al. demonstrates an increasing interest in Bertolotti's syndrome, with primary research focusing on minimally invasive techniques that hold promise for further therapeutic advancements [33]. In the realm of minimally invasive treatment, there is growing interest in the use of radiofrequency ablation (RFA) as a method of pain management, with novel techniques such as pulsed radiofrequency ablation yielding favorable outcomes while significantly reducing procedural discomfort [34, 35]. Although ablation may alleviate both nociceptive and mechanical pain associated with Bertolotti's syndrome, current studies are insufficient to establish its superiority as a treatment modality [13].

Crane et al. suggest that given the variability in anatomical presentation in Bertolotti's syndrome and the extent of the associated degenerative changes, the overall efficacy of surgical intervention may appear modest when assessed across heterogeneous patient populations. However, they underscore that optimal surgical outcomes are contingent upon rigorous patient selection, particularly in cases of Castellvi Type IIa LSTV with persistent symptoms unresponsive to conservative measures. Indeed, studies have found patient stratification to play an important role in achieving meaningful clinical improvement: Li et al. report favorable results following minimally invasive resection in carefully selected groups of patients, as did Santavirta et al. in a long‐term follow‐up of surgical cases [13, 36, 37]. The classification proposed by Jenkin's et al. indicates that patients with types I and II can achieve favorable outcomes from surgical intervention, recommending resection for type I and fusion for type II [32]. Mikula et al. also noted that, in the long term, fusion tends to provide better overall results; however, in patients with less pronounced adjacent segment degeneration, resection may still represent an effective treatment strategy [38]. Parallel research by McGrath et al. confirmed that pseudoarthrectomy in patients with refractory pain leads to significant improvement [39]. Although both methods yield comparable short‐term results, the insufficiency of studies and minor cohort populations currently prevent the literature from recommending one specific therapy over another [13, 32, 38].

Clinicians should consider Bertolotti syndrome as a differential diagnosis in patients presenting with persistent, severe back pain [10]. An additional factor that may contribute to an accurate diagnosis of Bertolotti syndrome is the manifestation of symptoms at an early age. However, in young patients, particularly males, ankylosing spondylitis should be excluded as a primary consideration [40, 41]. Quinlan et al. conducted an analysis of MRI scans from 769 consecutive patients presenting with low back pain and found that 11.4% of patients under the age of 30 exhibited a lumbosacral transitional vertebra (LSTV). Additionally, the data revealed that the mean age of patients diagnosed with LSTV was 32.5 years. These findings suggest that LSTV is relatively prevalent among younger individuals within this population and may be a contributing factor to their reported low back pain [4].
