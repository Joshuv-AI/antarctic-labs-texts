Uncovering the transcriptional landscape of estrogen-induced gonadal feminization in the Chinese giant salamander
Authors: Shijun Yang, Qin Wang, Youyou Li, Yadong Luo, Chao Yang, Yuting Pan, Junyang Zhu, Lishan Xu, Xinlin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExogenous estrogen (17β-estradiol, E2) induces partial sex reversal in the Chinese giant salamander (CGS) at low concentrations (25 µg/L), and complete sex reversal at high concentrations (1000 µg/L), suggesting potential risks of skewed sex ratios in wild populations of this critically endangered species. Simultaneously, controlled sex reversal offers a strategy to enhance the aquaculture efficiency of captive CGS. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying this process remain largely unexplored. Here, we generated histological evidence of male-to-female sex reversal in CGS under E2 treatment and characterized dynamic, multi-timepoint transcriptomic changes during this process. Integrating histological and transcriptomic data, we identified 210 days post-hatching (120 days after hormone treatment) as a critical window for male gonadal sex reversal. During this period, E2 upregulates meiosis-related genes, promoting and maintaining the transition from male to female gonads. Concurrently, E2 suppresses DMRT1 expression, inhibiting male-specific differentiation pathways and facilitating testicular feminization. Notably, early E2 exposure increases CYP19A expression, but as E2 accumulates, it gradually supplants role of CYP19A in hormone-mediated regulation, resulting in a progressive decline of CYP19A function in gonads. These findings provide a comprehensive molecular and histological framework for understanding E2-induced sex reversal in CGS, offering crucial insights for the conservation of wild populations and for improving aquaculture practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12709-8.Keywords: Chinese giant salamander, 17β-estradiol, Sex reversal, Transcriptome

Introduction

The Chinese giant salamander (CGS) Andrias davidianus, the largest living amphibian and a species endemic to China, is classified within the order Caudata. Its wild populations have experienced severe declines due to human-induced factors such as habitat degradation and overexploitation. Consequently, the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) has listed this species as Critically Endangered [1]. On a positive note, the expansion of CGS aquaculture has led to an increase in the number of farms, enabling the release of captive-bred individuals into natural habitats, thereby supporting conservation efforts [2]. Captive breeding not only aids in population restoration but also leverages the species’ unique nutritional and medicinal properties, creating significant economic value for the farming sector [3]. Despite these advances, studies focusing on optimizing breeding techniques for CGS remain limited [3]. To date, studies on sex determination in CGS have largely focused on single sex-related genes [4–6]. Building upon this foundation, our previous time-course transcriptomic analyses identified candidate genes involved in sex determination [7] and sex-based microRNAs associated with sex differentiation in this species [8]. More recently, we employed Adeno-associated virus 9-mediated RNA interference to preliminarily verify the relationship between TRPM1 and calcium ions, providing the first molecular evidence supporting a calcium-mediated model of sex regulation in CGS [9]. Collectively, elucidating the mechanisms underlying sex determination in CGS is of great significance for both species conservation and the enhancement of aquaculture productivity.

From an aquaculture perspective, advances in understanding the genetic and molecular mechanisms of sex determination, along with the identification of sex-linked genetic markers, have facilitated the development of various sex-control technologies based on sex reversal and selective breeding. For instance, in several economically important fish species, the application of exogenous hormones and backcrossing strategies has led to the successful production of specialized genotypes such as YY “super-males,” WW “super-females,” ZW sex-reversed males, and ZZ sex-reversed females [10, 11]. In common carp (Cyprinus carpio) and rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss), all-female monosex populations were generated by crossing sex-reversed XX males with normal XX females [12]. Similarly, in the ZW sex determination system of giant freshwater prawn (Macrobrachium rosenbergii), mating sex-reversed ZZ females with normal ZZ males yielded all-male progeny, which not only enhanced rearing efficiency but also significantly improved economic returns [13, 14]. In CGS, adult males are approximately 30% larger than females [15], providing a distinct growth advantage for aquaculture. Previous studies have demonstrated that estrogen treatment can induce feminization in CGS [15]. By producing ZZ females (neofemale) through estrogen-induced sex reversal and crossing them with ZZ males, all-male offspring can be obtained. This approach has the potential to improve production efficiency, eliminate the need for complex molecular sex identification, and facilitate research on all-male lineages.

From a conservation standpoint, amphibians are highly sensitive to environmental contaminants, particularly during reproductive and larval stages that depend on aquatic habitats. Previous studies have shown that exogenous hormone exposure can induce premature gill regression in CGS larvae [16]. Estrogen, a common contaminant derived from contraceptive compounds in wastewater effluents, has been reported to induce feminization in various fish species [17]. Species that also live in aquatic environments for extended periods, such as amphibians, can undergo male-to-female sex reversal upon exposure to estrogens, as observed in Xenopus laevis, Hyla arborea, and Bufo viridis, although the degree of sensitivity to hormonal disruption differs among these species [18]. Conversely, high temperatures can cause female-to-male sex reversal in Rana dalmatina, and estrogen treatment fails to reverse this thermally induced masculinization [19]. Other endocrine-disrupting chemicals such as bisphenol A and glyphosate have also been implicated in inducing sex reversal in amphibians [17, 20]. Evidence of sex reversal has already been documented in wild amphibian populations [21, 22], suggesting that environmental pollutants may profoundly influence natural sex ratios and reproductive viability.

From both utilization and conservation perspectives, CGS thus provides an excellent model for investigating environmentally and hormonally induced sex reversal. Previous research demonstrated that treatment with 17β-estradiol (E2) at 25 µg/L significantly increased the proportion of phenotypic females [15], and environmental concentrations exceeding this level have been detected in natural freshwater systems [23, 24]. This suggests that wild CGS populations may also experience sex ratio biases driven by environmental estrogens. Complete male-to-female sex reversal was reported at 1000 µg/L estrogen exposure [15], although histological evidence supporting this observation was lacking.

In this study, we exposed CGS individuals at 90 days post-hatching (dph) to exogenous estrogen and conducted histological examinations at 150, 210, 270, and 360 dph to determine whether genetic males underwent feminization and to characterize gonadal development in genetic females. Additionally, multi-stage transcriptomic profiling was performed to elucidate the molecular pathways underlying estrogen-induced sex reversal. Our findings provide both phenotypic and molecular evidence of estrogen-driven gonadal feminization in CGS, offering a theoretical foundation for the development of sex-control strategies that can enhance aquaculture productivity and support the conservation of this endangered species.

Methods

Genetic sex identification

All Chinese Giant Salamander (CGS) used in this study were captive-bred individuals obtained from the Yunxing Professional Breeding Cooperative of Chinese Giant Salamander (Ya’an, Sichuan, China). All experimental individuals were offspring of the same parental group. Prior to sampling, CGS were anesthetized using MS-222 mixture (Yubao brand, Fujian, China), a widely used aquaculture anesthetic, at a concentration of 1 mL per 20 L of water. Once fully anesthetized, tail-tip tissues were collected for genomic DNA extraction using a commercial DNA extraction kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). Sex identification was performed by PCR amplification using the female-specific primer adf340 as described previously [25] (Table 1). Based on the genetic sex, individuals were randomly assigned to four groups, with 20 individuals in each group: control females (CF), control males (CM), 17β-estradiol (E2)-treated females (TF), and E2-treated males (TM), Each individual was kept in a separate tank and subsequently used for further experiments.

Hormone treatment and sample collection

For the hormone-treated groups, immersion treatments with E2 (Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) began at 90 dph. Initially, 1 g of E2 was dissolved in 20 mL of absolute ethanol to prepare a stock solution. The concentration of E2 was gradually increased over five days from a low initial dose (200 µg/L) to a target concentration of 1000 µg/L (by adding 20 µL of the stock solution to 1 L of water), while the CGS were closely monitored to prevent stress-induced mortality. In the control group, 20 µL of absolute ethanol was added to 1 L of water. Each immersion lasted 6–8 h daily. The rearing water was changed every three days, and the E2 solution was renewed in a separate immersion tank at the same interval. All individuals were maintained under the same photoperiod without special light treatment. The rearing temperature was kept constant at 20 °C. As in the procedures described above, MS-222 was used for anesthesia. Once the CGS were fully anesthetized, cervical vertebral dislocation was performed prior to tissue sampling. Gonadal samples were collected at 150, 210, 270, and 360 dph, corresponding to 60, 120, 180, and 270 days of hormone treatment, respectively, from the TF and TM groups. All gonadal tissues were collected under a dissecting microscope, and three female and three male gonadal samples at each of the four time points were collected and preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde to prepare histological section. In terms of molecular study, due to the limited size of gonadal tissues in CGS, we combined the gonadal tissues of 2 individuals into one sample. Subsequently, six male and six female gonadal samples were collected at each stage (three for RNA-seq and three for qPCR), Samples for molecular analyses were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C, while those intended for histological observation were fixed and stored at 4 °C.

Histological observation

Fixed gonadal tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70–100%), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Serial transverse Sect. (5 μm thick) were prepared using a rotary microtome. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to examine gonadal morphology. Histological observations and microphotographs were obtained using a Nikon Eclipse Ci-L microscope (Tokyo, Japan). Histological assessment was conducted with knowledge of group identity.

RNA extraction, library construction, and transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from gonadal tissues using the RNAiso™ Plus RNA Kit (Takara, Ohtsu, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 5400 Fragment Analyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). RNA-seq libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA), with unique index tags assigned to each sample. Qualified libraries were pooled according to effective concentration and expected data yield, and high-throughput sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (PE150 strategy) at Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Transcriptome assembly, annotation, and expression quantification

The sequencing data generated in this study were combined with our previously published transcriptome dataset (accession number: PRJNA1029821) for joint analysis [7]. Raw reads were filtered using seqtk v1.3-r106 to remove low-quality sequences, and the clean reads were assembled de novo using Trinity v2.4.0 with the parameters “-group_pairs_distance 230 -min_contig_length 600 -min_glue 4.” Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted using TransDecoder v3.0.1 (http://transdecoder.sourceforge.net/), and coding DNA sequences (CDS) were extracted. Assembly completeness was evaluated using BUSCO (Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs). Annotation of the longest predicted proteins was performed using BLASTP searches (-line 2 -word_size 4 -evalue 1e-5 -top 3) against the SWISS-Prot, NCBI NR, and KEGG databases. Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation was conducted using an in-house Perl script (see Supplementary Information) based on SWISS-Prot results, and the GO terms were classified using WEGO 2.02. Gene and isoform expression levels were quantified using RSEM v1.3.3, and normalized expression was expressed as TPM (Transcripts Per Million). Sample correlations were visualized using the R packages corrplot (https://github.com/Taiyun/Corrplot) and hclust (https://github.com/mljs/hclust).

Differential expression and Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA)

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed in R to evaluate sample relationships and clustering patterns based on gene expression profiles. This study employed a stage-wise comparative design, with each developmental stage analyzed independently and only a single factor (sex or treatment) included in each comparison. Differential expression analyses were conducted using DESeq2 with its default normalization and statistical settings in R [26]. Because all samples were sequenced in the same sequencing batch, no batch factor was included in the DESeq2 design. Transcripts with |log₂ (fold change)| ≥ 1 and adjusted p < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Venn diagrams were generated using Evenn (http://www.ehbio.com/test/venn/#/) to visualize DEGs associated with hormone-induced sex regulation.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was conducted using the WGCNA R package [27] based on TPM values to construct gene co-expression modules. TPM values was used to construct co-expression networks because it reflects relative transcript abundance across samples and is commonly applied in WGCNA analyses. Genes with low expression (TPM below 0.5 in the majority of samples) were removed prior to network construction. Sample clustering was performed to detect potential outliers, and no samples were excluded. A scale-free topology criterion was applied to select the appropriate soft threshold. Modules were defined using hierarchical clustering and the dynamic tree-cut algorithm (MergeCutHeight = 0.25), with a minimum module size of 30 genes. Module eigengenes were calculated, and modules most strongly correlated with the hormone treatment phenotype were identified. Genes from the top two hormone-associated modules were subsequently extracted for downstream analysis.

Functional enrichment and Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) analysis

To elucidate the biological functions and signaling pathways of target genes, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted using g: Profiler (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/gost) and the OEbiotech Cloud Platform (https://cloud.oebiotech.cn/task/), respectively. Enrichment results were visualized using Chiplot (https://www.chiplot.online/). Based on enrichment results, sex-related candidate genes were further screened. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/), with an interaction confidence score threshold of > 0.4, and the resulting networks were visualized using Cytoscape software (Cytoscape Consortium, San Diego, CA, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) validation

Total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Gene-specific primers are listed in Table 1, and β-actin was used as the internal reference gene. All reactions were performed in triplicate, and relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. The results were visualized using GraphPad Prism 8.0. qRT-PCR was performed to independently validate the expression patterns observed in the RNA-seq analysis.

Results

Histological observations

Histological sections of gonadal tissues from the hormone-treated CGS groups are shown in Fig. 1. The control group results (indicated by red circles) were cited from our previous study on gonadal development in natural populations of CGS (Fig. 1 in Ref. 5), and are presented here for comparative purposes. At 150 dph, primary follicles were observed in TF, whereas they were absent in CF, at this stage, TM showed no notable differences from the natural control. By 210 dph, TF contained substantially more well-developed oocytes, while CF still exhibited numerous primary follicles. In contrast, TM displayed a mixed gonadal phenotype, characterized by the coexistence of both male and female germ cell types, including spermatogonia, primary follicles, and even a small number of developing oocytes. At 270 dph, the gonads of TF were smaller than those of CF, although the oocytes in both groups were comparable in size; meanwhile, TM showed clearly identifiable oocytes. By 360 dph, oocytes in TF became highly prominent and nearly filled the entire cross-sectional area of the gonad, and similarly, TM contained abundant and well-defined oocytes as well as primary follicles (Fig. 1).

Transcriptomic data characteristics

The 24 newly generated RNA-seq libraries correspond exclusively to TF and TM at four developmental stages (150, 210, 270, and 360 dph), with three biological replicates per group per stage (2 treatments × 4 stages × 3 replicates), generating approximately 919 million raw reads. After quality filtering, 862 million clean reads were retained for downstream analyses (Table 2). All samples exhibited Q30 values > 89.63% and GC content between 45.18 and 48.93%, indicating high-quality sequencing suitable for transcriptomic analysis.

DEGs between hormone-treated and control groups

In this study, DEGs were identified for the comparisons TF vs. CF, TM vs. CM, and CF vs. CM at each of the four developmental stages (Fig. 3A). Venn diagrams were subsequently generated for the DEGs from the three comparison groups at each stage (Fig. 3B). We considered genes that were differentially expressed between CF and CM to be sex-related; based on this criterion, we then intersected these sex-related genes with DEGs responsive to estrogen in both male and female gonads. Screening across the four developmental stages revealed 15 hormone-regulated sex-related genes at 150, 502 at 210, 2,039 at 270, and 1,046 at 360 dph. Finally, taking the union of hormone-regulated sex-related genes from all four stages yielded a total of 3,198 genes for subsequent analyses.

Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) of hormone-regulated sex-related DEGs

WGCNA was performed on the 3,198 genes identified above to explore co-expression patterns associated with natural and hormone-treated groups. Based on pairwise correlations between gene modules and phenotypic traits, a scale-free network topology was achieved when the correlation coefficient threshold was set to 0.9. To ensure robust estimation of gene interaction strength, the optimal soft-thresholding power was determined to be 6 (Fig. 4A).

Hierarchical clustering analysis identified 13 distinct co-expression modules, each represented by a unique color, with each branch corresponding to an individual gene. Module eigengenes (MEs) were used to summarize the expression profiles within each module and to assess their correlations with sex-related traits. Modules with highly correlated MEs were subsequently merged (Fig. 4B). In addition, the module–module correlation analysis indicated that the co-expression modules were largely independent of one another (Fig. 4C).

Among all modules, the salmon and lightcyan modules exhibited the strongest correlations with hormone-treated traits (r = 0.80, p = 3 × 10⁻⁷; r = 0.62, p = 4 × 10⁻⁴, respectively). Together, these two modules contained 390 hormone-regulated, sex-related genes that were considered key candidates for downstream analyses (Fig. 4D).

Functional enrichment and PPI network analysis

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on the 390 hormone-regulated, sex-related genes identified above. The GO enrichment results showed that these genes were significantly enriched in sex-related terms such as spermatogenesis (GO:0007283) and oogenesis (GO:0048477), as well as multiple meiosis-associated terms, including homologous chromosome pairing during meiosis (GO:0007129), male meiotic nuclear division (GO:0007140), meiotic cell cycle (GO:0051321), reciprocal meiotic recombination (GO:0007131), and male meiosis I (GO:0007141) (Fig. 5A). KEGG pathway analysis further revealed significant enrichment in pathways involved in steroidogenesis and meiosis, including Steroid hormone biosynthesis (hsa00140), Ovarian steroidogenesis (hsa04913), and Oocyte meiosis (hsa04114) (Fig. 5B).

GO enrichment analysis (A) and KEGG enrichment analysis (B) of key sex-related genes regulated by hormones. BP: Biological Process; CC: Cellular Component; MF: Molecular Function

Subsequently, the genes associated with the enriched GO and KEGG terms described above were further examined. We analyzed whether these genes were upregulated or downregulated in response to estrogen in male and female gonads at 150, 210, 270, and 360 dph (Table 3). The results showed that, in males, genes associated with male-related terms were uniformly downregulated at 150 dph under estrogen treatment, whereas nearly all of them were upregulated at 210 and 360 dph. In females, however, these genes exhibited more variable expression patterns; interestingly, nearly all of them were upregulated in female gonads at 360 dph in response to estrogen. For genes associated with female-related terms, no significant differences were detected in females at 150 dph. At 210 and 360 dph, however, the oogenesis-related genes HORMAD1 and NANOS1 showed consistent upregulation in both sexes. The CYP19A gene, which was a focal gene in our previous work, was upregulated in males at 150 and 210 dph but downregulated at 360 dph. Starting at 210 dph, genes associated with meiotic processes were consistently upregulated in males. In contrast, these genes tended to be downregulated in females at 150 and 210 dph but shifted to predominantly upregulated patterns at 270 and 360 dph. Genes associated with hormone-related pathways displayed no uniform trend across developmental stages or sexes.

Discussion

Sexual dimorphism is a key trait of interest in aquaculture, as establishing monosex populations plays a crucial role in improving economic efficiency. For instance, in species such as Acipenser ruthenus [28] and Takifugu obscurus [29], the gonads themselves have direct commercial value. The roe of female sturgeons is processed into the world-renowned caviar, making all-female populations particularly valuable in aquaculture. Conversely, T. obscurus are prized for their testes, which are regarded as a delicacy; thus, the development of all-male populations is desirable for maximizing production yield. Sexual size dimorphism is the most common manifestation of sexual dimorphism, typically resulting in differences in body size and growth rates between males and females [10, 30]. In tilapia, all-male populations have been successfully produced through androgen-induced masculinization since the last century [31]. However, due to concerns about hormone residues and environmental risks, direct endocrine manipulation has been banned. Nonetheless, alternative approaches such as exogenous hormone treatments combined with backcross breeding have proven effective in mitigating hormone residues and environmental damage [32]. In CGS, sexual size dimorphism has been previously documented [15], with males being approximately 30% larger than females. However, direct hormone induction not only poses ecological and physiological risks but also inhibits growth and development in CGS [15], ultimately counteracting the goal of improving aquaculture productivity. Therefore, identifying an appropriate strategy for producing all-male progeny would provide substantial benefits for CGS aquaculture. One potential approach involves inducing feminization of ZZ males via exogenous E2 treatment to generate sex-reversed ZZ females, which can then be crossed with normal ZZ males to produce all-male offspring.

Although earlier studies have reported E2-induced sex reversal in CGS, those studies lacked histological evidence [15]. In this study, we, for the first time, provide histological section data demonstrating sex reversal in CGS (Fig. 1) and further corroborate phenotypic evidence that exogenous E2 induces male-to-female sex reversal. At 150 dph, primary oocytes were observed in the testis-to-ovary reversed individuals (TF) but not in CF, suggesting that exogenous E2 accelerates early germ cell development. At 210 dph, more developed oocytes were observed in TF than in CF, reinforcing this finding. At 270 dph, the gonads of TF were smaller than those of CF, but oocyte size was comparable, indicating that estrogen exposure may retard overall somatic and gonadal growth while maintaining oogenic progression. This pattern persisted at 360 dph. Notably, after 150 days of estrogen immersion (1000 µg/L at 210 dph), both male- and female-type germ cells coexisted in the testis of TM, indicating that 210 dph may represent a critical transitional window when male gonads are most sensitive to endocrine disruption. Continued estrogen exposure led to increasingly evident feminization of male gonads by 360 dph. Because CGS reaches sexual maturity at approximately five years, further research is needed to determine whether continuous estrogen exposure is necessary to maintain sex reversal throughout maturation, and whether such reversals remain stable after treatment cessation. Addressing these questions will be vital for improving aquaculture efficiency and could also inform conservation strategies. If environmental estrogen pollution in natural habitats continues to rise, skewed sex ratios or even monosex populations could pose extinction risks to wild CGS populations. Therefore, elucidating the effects of exogenous estrogens on sex reversal is essential for both the utilization and conservation of this endangered species. We acknowledge that histological assessment in the present study was not performed in a blinded manner, which may introduce observer bias and should be considered when interpreting the qualitative histological findings. In addition, histological observations in this study are qualitative in nature and are intended to support transcriptomic inferences rather than to provide quantitative morphological comparisons. Future studies incorporating blinded evaluation and quantitative histological metrics, such as the proportion of sections containing oocytes in TM, oocyte stage distribution, and the frequency of ovotestis-like phenotypes, would further strengthen the robustness of histological assessments and allow a more comprehensive evaluation of the extent of sex reversal in CGS.

Sex reversal provides a valuable framework for understanding mechanisms of sex determination and gonadal differentiation and can also be applied directly or indirectly to sex control in aquaculture. Our results revealed a sharp increase in DEGs between TM and CM starting at 210 dph, likely reflecting the activation of numerous genes involved in sex reversal. Combined with histological and phenotypic data, these findings further confirm that 210 dph is a critical window during which male gonads are highly responsive to endocrine induction. Given that 210 dph corresponds to the natural onset of ovarian differentiation, we hypothesize that estrogen at this stage activates the expression of female differentiation-related genes in genetic males, thereby initiating male-to-female sex reversal. Key sex-related genes regulated by estrogen were significantly enriched in meiotic pathways, consistent with meiosis marking a pivotal transition in germ cell development [33]. SYCP3 [34] and DMC1 [35] are well-established molecular markers for meiosis initiation. In carp [36], high expression of these genes indicates the onset of meiosis and gonadal differentiation. In our study, both SYCP3 and DMC1 were upregulated in TM compared to CM at 210 dph, suggesting that estrogen accelerates meiosis in male gonads, thereby driving feminization. This also supports that 210 dph is a critical period for male gonadal plasticity, during which sex reversal occurs primarily via meiotic activation. Furthermore, other meiosis-related genes remained upregulated in TM at 270 and 360 dph, indicating sustained meiotic activity that promotes and maintains male-to-female gonadal transition. Conversely, the key male regulatory gene DMRT1 was consistently downregulated in TM compared to CM at 150, 270, and 360 dph, possibly due to direct suppression by estrogen or estrogen-responsive pathways, leading to the inhibition of testicular differentiation. The estrogen-synthesis gene CYP19A was upregulated in TM at 150 and 210 dph, indicating that estrogen exposure enhances CYP19A expression in early male gonads to promote sex reversal. However, CYP19A expression declined at 360 dph, likely due to feedback inhibition caused by the accumulation of exogenous estrogen, reducing the need for endogenous estrogen biosynthesis. This observation is in line with previous reports showing that elevated circulating estrogen levels inhibit the release of pituitary gonadotropins, thereby diminishing the stimulation of ovarian granulosa cells and consequently downregulating the expression of aromatase, which is encoded by the CYP19A gene [37].

Our previous study demonstrated that high expression of TRPM1 in CGS facilitates Ca²⁺ influx into germ cells, subsequently upregulating GDF9, FOXL2, and CYP19A expression to promote ovarian development. In contrast, when Ca²⁺ entry into cells is inhibited, DMRT1 expression increases, activating SOX9, CYP17A, WT1, and AMH, thereby promoting testicular differentiation [9]. Furthermore, earlier research has shown that estrogen can elevate intracellular Ca²⁺ concentrations [38–40]. This study did not detect a direct effect of estrogen on the expression of TRPM1, a key regulator of TRP channel gating. However, E2 (1000 µg/L) has been reported to rapidly induce Ca²⁺ influx by activating membrane-bound estrogen receptors and L-type calcium channel, resulting in a rapid increase in intracellular Ca²⁺ concentration from approximately 75 nM to 150 nM within minutes [40]. This suggests that distinct Ca²⁺ entry channels may operate between normal individuals and those undergoing hormone-induced sex reversal. Moreover, elevated intracellular Ca²⁺ levels have been shown to downregulate DMRT1 expression [9], which provides further support for the decreased DMRT1 expression observed in this study.

Conclusion

In this study, we constructed histological sections of male-to-female sex reversal in CGS, providing further evidence that exogenous estrogen can induce gonadal feminization in this species. The stage at 210 dph (150 days after hormone treatment) was identified as a critical and sensitive period for male gonadal sex reversal. During this phase, estrogen promotes and maintains the transformation from testes to ovaries by upregulating meiosis-related genes. Meanwhile, estrogen suppresses the expression of DMRT1, thereby inhibiting male gonadal differentiation pathways and leading to the gradual feminization of the testes. Early exposure to estrogen also upregulated CYP19A expression; however, with prolonged treatment, the continuous accumulation of exogenous estrogen appeared to replace endogenous hormone synthesis, resulting in a progressive decline in CYP19A activity within the gonads. Overall, this study provides essential evidence of estrogen-induced sex reversal in both male and female gonads of CGS. These findings hold great significance for the development of all-male breeding strategies to enhance aquaculture productivity, as well as for the conservation of wild populations that may be threatened by estrogenic contamination in natural aquatic environments.
