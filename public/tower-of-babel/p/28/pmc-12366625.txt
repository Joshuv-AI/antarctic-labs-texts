Pharmacological and structural characterization of vibostolimab, a novel anti-TIGIT blocking antibody for cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Thierry O Fischmann, Daniel Malashock, Eunseon Ahn, Hongmei Wang, Komal Pradhan, Jeffery Grein, Srishti Chakravorty, Soheila Bahmanjah, David Ban, Ellen Chien, Mark Hsieh, Todd Mayhood, Jing Yuan, Janica C Wong, Maribel Beaumont, Jeanne Baker, Mark A McCoy, Mingmei Cai, Douglas Wilson, Wendy Blumenschein, Sybil M G Williams, Laurence Fayadat-Dilman, Wolfgang Seghezzi, Tanya Keenan, Jin-Hwan Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVibostolimab is a humanized anti-T cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin (Ig) and ITIM domains (TIGIT) antibody was recently investigated in late-stage clinical trials. Vibostolimab was developed based on its specific binding property to the human and cynomolgus monkey TIGIT protein and its functional blocking activity of the TIGIT:CD155 interaction.MethodsThe biochemical properties of vibostolimab, in a comparison with tiragolumab, were assessed with Biacore, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), and X-ray crystallography, while the T-cell activation was examined using engineered Jurkat cells in an in vitro co-culture system by measuring interleukin (IL)-2 production as a readout of T-cell activation. The mouse surrogate anti-TIGIT antibodies were identified and used to extensively investigate the mechanism of action of anti-TIGIT antibodies as monotherapy or in combination with anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) antibody in vivo by gene expression profiling and flow cytometry analyses with a mouse syngeneic tumor model.ResultsStructural analyses with solution NMR and X-ray crystallography revealed that vibostolimab binds the CC’C’’FG loop interface of TIGIT, completely competing for the binding site of CD155 with a larger coverage area than another anti-TIGIT antibody tiragolumab, consistent with surface plasmon resonance analysis, though mouse models with similar antibodies revealed no functional difference in antitumor efficacy. Biacore-based binding data indicate that both vibostolimab and tiragolumab bind on TIGIT with comparable binding affinity (KD), but different binding kinetics characterized by faster on and off rates, which may contribute to enhanced T-cell activation with vibostolimab, as evidenced by greater IL-2 production in an in vitro cell-based assay. Functional experiments in mouse models demonstrated additional detailed biological mechanisms of the mouse equivalent of vibostolimab, including the differentiating role of myeloid cell activation in addition to improved T cell cytolytic activity, both of which are further enhanced by anti-PD-1 combination therapy.ConclusionsVibostolimab is a novel anti-TIGIT antibody that completely blocks CD155 binding and induces T-cell activation. From experiments using a mouse tumor model and mouse surrogate anti-TIGIT antibody, we demonstrate direct evidence where it not only activates cytotoxic T cells but also induces the activation of antigen-presenting cells. The clinical relevance of vibostolimab, based on these mechanisms, in combination with pembrolizumab was recently tested in registrational trials.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, T cell, Monoclonal antibody

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study discovered structural, binding, and biochemical differences between two anti-TIGIT antibodies, vibostolimab and tiragolumab, including differences in interleukin-2 production, that did not translate to greater tumor shrinkage in vivo in mouse tumor models. For both antibodies, in vivo mechanistic studies showed upregulation of surface markers for antigen-presenting cells, including major histocompatibility complex class II, CD86, CD40, and CSF-1R, consistent with enhanced myeloid cell activation mediated by the anti-TIGIT antibody engagement of Fcγ receptors on myeloid cells within the tumor microenvironments (TME).

Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of morbidity worldwide, and cancer-related mortality reached an estimated 19.3 million patients in 2021.1 Despite the accelerating progress of cancer research, there remains an unmet medical need for improved treatments. Immune checkpoint blockade is a more recent approach and has a proven track record of success.2 When antigen-specific CD8 T cells encounter cognate peptide/major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I complex to mount immune responses to a pathogen, the activated T cells simultaneously express co-inhibitory receptors, also known as immune checkpoints, including programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) and T cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin (Ig) and ITIM domains (TIGIT), as a rheostatic mechanism. Under chronic viral infection or cancer development, persistent viral or tumor antigens drive continuous T-cell activation, concurrent with continuous upregulation of coinhibitory receptors and amplification of downstream intracellular inhibitory signaling cascades. This leads to T-cell exhaustion, which is a state where the antigen-specific T cells lose their effector functions over time.3 Exhaustion is driven by the interaction between the co-inhibitory receptors, such as PD-1 and TIGIT, on T cells and their endogenous ligands (PD-1/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) or CD155) on the target cells, such as virally infected cells or tumor cells. Therapeutic interventions disrupting the receptor:ligand interactions, such as pembrolizumab and nivolumab, result in the reinvigoration of antigen-specific CD8 T cells against endogenous malignant cells in patients with cancer.4,6 The discovery and development of immune checkpoint inhibitors have revolutionized the treatment of patients with cancer.7

TIGIT is an immune receptor present on a fraction of T cells and natural killer (NK) cells.8 It binds to CD155 with higher affinity and CD112 with lower affinity. Binding of TIGIT to its ligand inhibits CD8+ T-cell activity, thus acting as an immune checkpoint.9,11 Preclinical proof-of-concept studies have demonstrated that disrupting the TIGIT:CD155 interaction by a blocking antibody induces highly effective antitumor responses.12,14 15 Therefore, the development of antibodies directed against TIGIT was thought to be a valuable immunotherapy approach against cancer in the clinic, and with initial clinical studies yielding mixed results, a deeper understanding of the biological mechanism of anti-TIGIT antibodies may elucidate innovative future therapeutic approaches. One anti-TIGIT antibody is vibostolimab, a humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody (mAb) capable of engaging Fcγ receptors (FcγRs) on myeloid cells.

In this work, we demonstrate the blocking property of vibostolimab that prevents the CD155:TIGIT interaction, as well as the binding affinity and kinetics of vibostolimab to TIGIT at the atomic and molecular level by structural and biochemical analyses, respectively, in addition to the induction of T-cell activation by vibostolimab. Using a murine equivalent of vibostolimab, we further show here that this FcγR-engaging anti-TIGIT antibody induces activation of not only CD8 T cells but also antigen-presenting cells, which is differentiating from PD-1 mAbs, in the tumor microenvironment (TME) of mouse syngeneic tumor in vivo.

Methods

Generation of vibostolimab and tool antibodies

Wild-type BALB/cNTac (Taconic) mice were immunized with recombinant human TIGIT-human Fc protein along with RIBI adjuvant and hybridomas were generated. Hybridomas were generated by electrofusion between isolated lymphocytes from draining lymph nodes and the myeloma fusion partner P3X63-AG8.653 using the Cytopulse Hybrimune electrofusion system (BTX). Antibodies were selected based on binding to human and cyno monkey TIGIT and based on competitive blocking activity of the TIGIT:CD155 interaction. “Humanization” of anti-TIGIT antibodies was carried out by complementarity-determining region (CDR), comprised with variable heavy chain and variable light chain grafting technology onto a human IgG1 antibody-germline sequence using structure guided design. Analogs of anti-TIGIT human IgG1 antibody tiragolumab (referred to as tiragolumab) and anti-IgE human IgG1 antibody omalizumab (referred to as hIgG1) were used as positive control and negative control tool antibodies, respectively, for in vitro binding and blocking assays.

TIGIT binding by cell ELISA

CHO-K1 cells were transfected with a vector encoding human TIGIT using Lipofectamine 2000 following the manufacturer’s instructions. For cell ELISA, 40,000 TIGIT-expressing CHO-K1 cells in 100 µl/well of CHO medium (Fisher Scientific) were seeded on a flat-bottom 96-well plate, which was placed at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The next day, the culture medium was removed and anti-human TIGIT antibodies or human IgG1 isotype control was added. After 1 hour incubation at 37°C in a 5% CO2, the unbound antibodies were washed out, and 5 µg/mL of the ligand hCD155-hIgG-Biotin (made internally) was added and incubated for 1 hour at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Unbound, excessive ligand protein was washed out and streptavidin-horseradish peroxidase (HRP) detection reagent in CHO medium was added and incubated for 1 hour at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After the unbound streptavidin-HRP was washed out, the 3,3',5,5'-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate was added to develop the signals at room temperature until the reaction was stopped by adding 0.5 M H2SO4. The intensity of the signal was measured with the absorbance at 450 nm by Perkin Elmer plate reader using the end-point ELISA protocol.

TIGIT binding affinity and epitope binning

To measure binding affinity, anti-human capture antibody (Cytiva) was immobilized on a Series S CM5 Sensor Chip in a Biacore T200. 6 nM vibostolimab or tiragolumab was captured for 50–57 s at 10 µl/min. 0.51 nM to 20 nM recombinant human TIGIT-His protein in a 2.5-fold dilution series was injected for 3 min at 50 µl/min. Dissociation was monitored for 10 min, and the chip was regenerated with a 30 s injection of MgCl2 between binding cycles. Data were double referenced and fit to a 1:1 binding model using Biacore T200 Evaluation Software V.3.0. To assess vibostolimab and tiragolumab blocking one another binding to TIGIT, human TIGIT was immobilized at a level of 189 response units on a Series S CM5 Sensor Chip in a Biacore T200, 80 nM vibostolimab or tiragolumab was injected for 3 min followed immediately by the other antibody or buffer control. Between cycles, the chip was regenerated with a 30 s injection of 10 mM Glycine, pH 1.5. Running buffer for all experiments was 10 mM HEPES, 0.15 M NaCl, 3 mM EDTA and 0.05% v/v Surfactant P20, pH 7.4.

IL-2 immunoassay with engineered cell lines

interleukin (IL)-2 production was measured in a co-culture reaction with Jurkat cells expressing human TIGIT and JY cells expressing human CD155 in a plate where mouse anti-human CD3 antibody (1 µg/mL: clone HIT3a; BD Pharmingen) was precoated at 4°C the night before. After the co-culture at 37°C in 5% CO2 incubator for 16–24 hours, the concentration of IL-2 in supernatants was determined using MESO Human IL-2 Tissue Culture Kit (Meso Scale Discovery) according to the manufacturer’s instruction.

Recombinant TIGIT protein expression and purification

Uniformly 13C and 15N labeled human TIGIT IgV domain (22–137, all residues numbered according to the Uniprot Q495A1 entry) was produced in E. coli using conventional labeling protocols and was purified via refolding according to published protocols.16 The same isotope-labeled reagent that was used for nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) studies was also crystallized in complex with the vibostolimab fragment antigen-binding (Fab) protein.

Structural analyses of the TIGIT:vibostolimab Fab complex

Details of TIGIT:vibostolimab and TIGIT:tiragolumab structural analyses, including NMR spectroscopy, crystallization, data collection and structure determination can be found in online supplemental information.

Binding activity of anti-mouse TIGIT antibodies to recombinant mouse TIGIT protein

ELISA plates (MaxiSorp; Thermo Nunc) were coated with 1 µg/mL recombinant mouse TIGIT protein (Acro Biosystems). After washing with Wash Buffer (1× Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) with 0.05% Tween 20), the plates were blocked with Protein-Free Blocking Buffer (1× DPBS with 0.05% Tween 20; Thermo Pierce) by incubating them at room temperature for at least 1 hour. After washing out the blocking buffer, serially diluted anti-mouse TIGIT antibodies (clone 18G10 or 11A11) with mouse IgG2a isotype were loaded to the plates and incubated at room temperature for 2 hours. Unbound anti-mouse TIGIT antibodies were washed out, and secondary antibody, a goat polyclonal anti-mouse IgG antibody (cross-adsorbed with mouse IgM, mouse IgA, human immunoglobulins and pooled human sera; Southern Biotech) conjugated with HRP, in the Protein-Free Blocking Buffer, was added and incubated at room temperature for 1 hour. After wash-out, bound antibodies were developed by adding substrate, and measuring the optical density at 450 nm wavelength (SpectraMax M3).

Blocking of murine TIGIT:CD155 interaction by anti-mouse TIGIT antibodies

Anti-mouse TIGIT antibodies were bound to the recombinant mouse TIGIT protein as described above. After washing off unbound anti-mouse TIGIT antibodies, 8 µg/mL of recombinant mouse CD155:human IgG1-Fc fusion protein (mCD155:hIgG1-Fc, produced in-house) in Protein-Free Blocking Buffer and incubate for 30 mins at room temperature. Unbound mCD155:hIgG1-Fc was washed out, and secondary antibody, goat polyclonal anti-human Fcγ (Jackson ImmunoResearch) conjugated with HRP was added, and then developed with TMB as described in the binding activity methods section above.

Preparation of mouse tumor-infiltrating leukocytes

A mouse syngeneic tumor cell line, CT26, was implanted subcutaneously on the right flank of 8-week-old, female naïve BALB/cNTac (Taconic) mice. When tumor volume reached an average of 100 mm3, chimeric anti-murine PD-1 (clone DX400, isotype mIgG1-(D265A)),17 chimeric anti-murine TIGIT (clone 18G10 or 11A11, isotype mIgG2a), or their corresponding isotype controls were administered. Preoptimized dose of anti-PD-1 (10 mg per kg) or anti-TIGIT (18 mg per kg) was dosed intraperitoneally every 4 days. The mice were sacrificed 4 days after the second dose on day 4 (ie, on day 8), tumors were dissected, and tumor single-cell suspension was prepared as described previously.12

Immunophenotyping of mouse tumor leukocytes by flow cytometry

Four groups of treatment were included for immunophenotyping: isotype control group (co-dose of a 10 mg per kg isotype control antibody with mouse IgG1-(D265A), Iso:mIgG1-(D265A), and 18 mg per kg mouse IgG2a isotype, Iso:mIgG2a); anti-PD-1 group (co-dose of 10 mg per kg anti-murine PD-1 (clone DX400):mIgG1-(D265A) and 18 mg per kg Iso:mIgG2a); anti-TIGIT group (co-dose of 10 mg per kg Iso:mIgG1-(D265A) and 18 mg per kg anti-TIGIT (clone 18G10 or 11A11):mIgG2a), and anti-PD-1 + anti-TIGIT group (co-dose of 10 mg per kg anti-murine PD-1 (clone DX400):mIgG1-(D265A) and 18 mg per kg anti-TIGIT (clone 18G10 or 11A11):mIgG2a). A single-cell suspension of CT26 tumors was prepared as described above 4 days after the second dose on day 4 (ie, on day 8). Prepared single-cell suspension was washed and stained with the following reagents: CD45 (clone 30-F11), CD11b (clone M1/70), MHC Class II or I-A/I-E (clone M5/114.15.2), Ly-6C (clone HK1.4), Ly-6G (clone 1A8), FcγRIV or CD16.2 (clone 9E9), CSF-1R or CD115 (clone AFS98), CD80 or B7.1 (clone 16–10 A1), CD86 or B7.2 (clone GL1), CD40 (clone 3/23), CD4 (clone GK1.5), PD-L2 (clone TY25), CD155 (clone TX56), CD25 (clone PC61), CD226 (clone 10E5), PD-L1 (clone 10F9G2), CD8 (clone 53–6.7), Foxp3 (clone FJK-16s), granzyme B (clone GB11), CD49b (clone DX5), Helios (clone 22F6), and NKp46 (clone 29A1.4) from either BioLegend or BD Biosciences, and Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 506 (eBioscience). The stained cells were acquired with the Aurora instrument (Cytek Biosciences) and analyzed with FlowJo software (BD Biosciences).

FACS staining and sorting of mouse tumor leukocytes

Isolated tumor single-cell suspension was stained for CD11b (clone M1/70), TCRβ (clone H57-597), CD3 (clone 145–2 C11), CD45 (clone 30-F11), CD4 (clone RM4-5), CD8α (clone 53–6.7), NKp46 (clone 29A1.4), CD49b (clone DX5), and CD25 (clone PC61). CD8 T cells (CD45+CD3+ TCRβ+ CD11b− NKp46− CD49b− CD8α+CD4−), regulatory T cell (Treg) (CD45+CD3+ TCRβ+ CD11b− CD8α− CD4+CD25+), myeloid cells (CD45+CD11b+), or NK cells (CD45+CD3− TCRβ− NKp46+CD49b+). The antibodies were purchased from either BioLegend or BD Biosciences. Each stained population was sorted by FACSAria (BD Biosciences).

Real-time quantitative PCR for gene expression

For real-time PCR analysis, total RNA was isolated from cells using Arcturus PicoPure RNA Isolation method, according to manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific). DNase-treated total RNA was reverse-transcribed using QuantiTect Reverse Transcription (Qiagen) according to manufacturer’s instructions. 20× primer assays were obtained commercially from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Gene-specific preamplification was done on 10 ng complementary DNA per Standard BioTools Biomark manufacturer’s instructions (Standard BioTools). Real-time quantitative PCR was then done on the Standard BioTools Biomark using 20X Taqman primer assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with Taqman Universal PCR Master Mix with UNG. Samples and primers were run on a 96.96 Dynamic Array per manufacturer’s instructions (Standard BioTools). Ubiquitin B levels were measured in a separate reaction and used to normalize the data by the ΔCt method. Using the mean cycle threshold value for ubiquitin and the gene of interest for each sample, the equation 1.8ˆ(Ct ubiquitin minus Ct gene of interest) × 104 was used to obtain the normalized values.

Results

Binding of vibostolimab to human TIGIT protein and its TIGIT:CD155 blocking activity

Specific binding of vibostolimab to human TIGIT was evaluated by a cell-based ELISA using human TIGIT-transfected CHO-K1 cells. First, dose-titrated vibostolimab was incubated with human TIGIT-expressing CHO-K1 cells. We confirmed that vibostolimab was bound to TIGIT-expressing cells in a dose-dependent manner (figure 1A). When 5 µg/mL of recombinant human CD155 protein was added after vibostolimab binding to the human TIGIT-expressing CHO-K1 cells, the interaction of the CD155 recombinant protein with cell surface TIGIT was reduced (figure 1B), indicating that vibostolimab functionally blocks the human TIGIT:CD155 interaction. In this experiment, a tiragolumab analog was compared with vibostolimab in the same assay and demonstrated that the TIGIT binding property and the functional blocking activity of the TIGIT:CD155 interaction by vibostolimab and tiragolumab were comparable (figure 1).

Antigen binding and functional ligand blocking property of vibostolimab. (A) Binding of vibostolimab and a tiragolumab analog to TIGIT was assessed using CHO-K1 cells expressing human TIGIT. (B) Recombinant CD155 protein conjugated with HRP was loaded in vibostolimab-bound or tiragolumab-bound TIGIT-expressing CHO-K1 cells to evaluate blocking of TIGIT:CD155 interaction by the anti-TIGIT antibodies. Representatives from two experiments with technical triplicates are shown. HRP, horseradish peroxidase; TIGIT, T cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin (Ig) and ITIM domains.

Using a surface plasmon resonance approach, the binding kinetics and competition between vibostolimab and tiragolumab were analyzed. Biacore data indicated that vibostolimab and tiragolumab both bind human TIGIT with affinities of 130 pM, though vibostolimab had significantly faster ka (p=5.8×10-5) and kd (p=0.00021) rate constants (figure 2A). In a competitive binding assay, the binding signal of vibostolimab and tiragolumab sequentially injected over immobilized TIGIT was not substantially larger than the binding signal of vibostolimab alone (figure 2B). Sequential injection experiments demonstrated that when vibostolimab binds to TIGIT first, the binding of tiragolumab was not detected (figure 2B, left), whereas vibostolimab was able to provide additional binding when tiragolumab was injected first (figure 2B, right).

Binding of vibostolimab at the CC’C’’FG loop interface of TIGIT

Previous structural analyses revealed that the TIGIT/CD155 interaction is formed in a heterotetramer with two TIGIT/CD155 dimers that has a core TIGIT/TIGIT cis-homodimer, and the interactions between TIGIT and CD155 take place on the front ß-sheets (CC’C’’FG) on TIGIT.18 In this study, the atomic resolution and crystal structure of the TIGIT:vibostolimab complex were determined by solution NMR spectrometry and X-ray crystallography to support the TIGIT-specific functional blocking by vibostolimab in vitro from a structural point of view.

Mapping residues in the crystal structure of the TIGIT:vibostolimab interaction by solution NMR revealed that vibostolimab binds at the CC’C’’FG loop interface of TIGIT (figure 3A and online supplemental figure 1). Other residues in the short helix between strands B and C on TIGIT are relatively distal to the primary binding site of vibostolimab, thus less likely to contribute to the TIGIT:vibostolimab interaction.

The formation of the TIGIT:vibostolimab (Fab) complex buries approximately 900 Å2 of TIGIT surface area (figure 3C). A total of 15 hydrogen bonds, but no salt bridges, were found in the interface (figure 3D and online supplemental Table 1) with the light chain of vibostolimab Fab contributing to a vast majority of these polar interactions with the antigen (12 of 15 hydrogen bonds). In addition, out of six CDRs, five are involved in the recognition of TIGIT. The only CDR that did not contribute to the recognition is the light chain CDR2. The epitope encompasses residues from three segments: T55 to N58, Y65 to D83, and H111 to T117. A total of 11 TIGIT residues (Q56, N58, N70, D72, L73, H76, P79, L82, H111, Y113, D115 and G116) are involved in its high degree of surface complementarity interactions with the Fab fragment of vibostolimab either through a hydrophobic network, at least one hydrogen bond, or both interactions (figure 3D–G).

We next compared the X-ray crystal structure of TIGIT in the apo state (PDB 3Q0H) to the TIGIT:vibostolimab Fab complex to further assess any structural rearrangements that occurred in the antigen on vibostolimab binding (figure 4). The structures superpose well with a root mean square deviation of only 0.32–0.56 Å (depending on the monomer compared in the PDB 3Q0H asymmetric unit). However, significant local rearrangements are observed for two loops, each connecting two β-strands of TIGIT (N70 to G74, T112 to G116) (figure 4A and B). This conformational change allows for hydrogen-bond formation between D72 or Y113 and the Fab portion of vibostolimab. Importantly, Y113 was known to be the prominent aromatic residue of the “key” in the signature lock-and-key motif (CC’C’’FG loop) that latches TIGIT and CD155 trans-dimer together.18 19

We further compared the crystal structures of TIGIT:CD155 to TIGIT:vibostolimab Fab. The surface on which the TIGIT antigen is bound by the antibody and by CD155 has significant overlap (figure 4A and B). However, the change in TIGIT structure on vibostolimab binding has a minimal impact on the binding surface with CD155, indicating that vibostolimab does not block the TIGIT:CD155 trans dimerization, potentially limiting functional blockade effects by the antibody (figure 4A and B).

Contacts between one single TIGIT molecule in the crystal asymmetric unit and its symmetry-related molecule only occur with one unique copy of the antigen. The two TIGIT monomers are held together by main-chain interactions; however, we did not observe a 2-fold increase in chemical shifts deviations that could reconstitute a cis-homodimer. Additionally, the buried surface of vibostolimab-bound TIGIT is only 40 Å2, which is significantly smaller than a cis-homodimer interface and at the lower range of the non-specific surface area in crystal formation.20 21

The availability of the vibostolimab and tiragolumab Fab-TIGIT structures (figure 4C and D), as well as of the MG1131 (humanized anti-human TIGIT antibody from Mogam Institute for Biomedical Research) Fab-TIGIT costructure (PDB 7vyt) (online supplemental figure 2),22 allows for a comparison of the binding modes and epitopes of these Fabs. All three Fabs bind to the same “face” of the antigen, but the orientation of the variable regions relative to the TIGIT molecule is strikingly different where vibostolimab Fab and tiragolumab Fab bind to TIGIT with a nearly 180 degree rotated angle (figure 4C and D). Additionally, the surface recognized by tiragolumab is more contiguous and less spread out than the area bound by vibostolimab where vibostolimab interacts with the entire FG and BC loops of TIGIT whereas tiragolumab does not. The buried surface by tiragolumab (800 Å2) is smaller than that by vibostolimab (900 Å2) as well. As a result, the antigenic surface for vibostolimab extends further (figure 4C and D). Thus, while the TIGIT-Fab structures infer that the antibodies share the same primary mode of action, that is competition with CD155, differences could be expected in cellular potency assays (with full monoclonal antibodies) based on the different binding orientations and surfaces. We next investigated whether the observed structural differences in binding translated to differences in functional activity in vitro.

Vibostolimab-mediated in vitro cytokine production in an engineered co-culture system

To evaluate immune activation properties induced by blocking the TIGIT:CD155 interaction by anti-TIGIT antibodies, human TIGIT was exogenously expressed on Jurkat cells to represent T cells with TIGIT-mediated immune suppressive signals, while human CD155 was expressed on a human B cell line, JY, to serve as engineered antigen-presenting cells in the co-culture system. Blocking the TIGIT:CD155 interaction by vibostolimab enhanced the production of IL-2 in a dose-dependent manner. A tiragolumab analog was used in this set of experiments to compare its IL-2-inducing activity with vibostolimab. Interestingly, both anti-TIGIT antibodies showed a comparable EC50 range (3–6 nM) with a significantly (p=0.019) improved Emax of IL-2 production by vibostolimab (877 pg/mL) over tiragolumab (668 pg/mL) (figure 5).

Identification of anti-mouse TIGIT antibody clones with different surface area coverage

In order to recapitulate the different binding dynamics of vibostolimab versus tiragolumab, we screened for anti-mouse TIGIT antibody clones that have the functional blocking property of CD155:TIGIT interaction with different surface binding area on mouse TIGIT protein. We identified two clones that have high affinity binding with about twofold difference of EC50 (clone 18G10 vs 11A11 with 0.07 vs 0.13 nM, respectively; figure 6A). Both antibodies blocked the interaction of mouse TIGIT and mouse CD155 in vitro (figure 6A). Biacore competitive binding studies indicated that clone 18G10 was still able to bind on mouse TIGIT protein even when 11A11 was first bound. The reverse order binding of the two antibodies (18G10 first and then 11A11) confirmed that once 18G10 binds on the mouse TIGIT protein, 11A11 lost the binding area, most likely due to the preoccupation of its epitope by 18G10 (figure 6B). These data imply that the anti-mouse TIGIT antibody clones functionally block TIGIT:CD155 interaction with different surface binding areas, similar to vibostolimab versus tiragolumab (figure 2B).

We evaluated whether such a difference in binding surface area with little binding affinity difference had a detectable difference in antitumor efficacy in vivo. Overall, both clone 18G10 and 11A11 showed comparable antitumor efficacy in vivo, with clear additive effects of anti-TIGIT antibodies to an anti-PD-1 antibody in CT26 tumor-bearing mice (online supplemental figure 3), suggesting that such differences in vibostolimab versus tiragolumab would not induce significant differences in antitumor effects as single agents.

The in vivo mechanism of action of the anti-TIGIT antibody-mediated TIGIT:CD155 interaction blockade

To understand molecular and cellular mechanism of action at early events in the TME when blocking the TIGIT/CD155 interaction with an anti-TIGIT antibody in vivo, we analyzed the gene expression profile of sorted immune compartments (figure 7) and changes of cellular phenotypes by flow cytometry (figure 8) and from tumors after anti-TIGIT antibody treatment in a mouse syngeneic CT26 tumor model. Antitumor regression following treatment with either anti-PD-1 or anti-TIGIT monotherapy has been reported.12 A mouse functional surrogate antibody, clone 18G10, was used,12 since vibostolimab does not have cross-reactivity with mouse TIGIT and there was little difference in antitumor efficacy between clone 18G10 and 11A11 (online supplemental figure 3). It is important to point out that the isotype of anti-mouse TIGIT antibody we used in these experiments was mouse IgG2a, which can interact with various FcγRs in the TME, similar to human IgG1 (isotype of vibostolimab), to drive parallel biology between the mouse and human immune systems.

We focused on several groups of genes, such as those related to chemokines, Treg identity/function, and effector molecules, in addition to the genes involved in the PD-1 and TIGIT pathways, based on our previous findings from gene expression profiles of unsorted, whole tumors after anti-PD-1 or anti-TIGIT antibody treatment.12 Findings in our earlier work, using unsorted, whole tumors, included upregulation of chemokines (Cxcl10 and Cxcl11), cytolytic effector molecules (interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), Perforin, and Granzyme B) and activation markers for antigen-presenting cells (MHC class II, CD86 and CD40) after anti-TIGIT antibody treatment in vivo.12 While the differences in gene expression profiles between anti-PD-1 and anti-TIGIT antibody treatment were significant, it remained unclear as to what cell types within the TME were responsible for the differential expression of the genes of interest. Here, we isolated and sorted tumor-infiltrating leukocytes, CD8 T cells, Tregs, myeloid cells, and NK cells based on their surface markers after two doses of either anti-TIGIT or anti-PD-1 antibody treatment to CT26 tumor-bearing mice (experimental design shown in online supplemental figure 4). The tumors were harvested 8 days after the first dose of either isotype control, anti-PD-1, or anti-TIGIT antibody (after two doses every 4 days: online supplemental figure 4) when the tumor volume between the three groups was not significantly different, in order to capture molecular changes within the tumor environments at the early phase of treatment to understand the “cause” of later phenotypic differences (eg, tumor volume changes). We were able to sort clean, matched immune compartments efficiently based on the unique surface markers expressed on each cell lineage (online supplemental figure 5). Targeted gene expression profiling was then performed with TaqMan real-time PCR approach to characterize relative expression levels of genes of interest from each sorted cell population after isotype control, anti-PD-1, or anti-TIGIT antibody treatment in vivo.

We discovered that the expression of chemokines, Cxcl10 and Cxcl11, was significantly increased only in myeloid cells in the TME at the highest level after anti-TIGIT antibody treatment (figure 7A and B). Simultaneously, expression of the master regulators for Tregs, Foxp3 and Helios, was downregulated with anti-TIGIT antibody treatment. This confirms the notion that the TIGIT:CD155 interaction for Tregs is critical for the maintenance and function of intratumoral Tregs and blocking the TIGIT:CD155 interaction on Tregs with anti-TIGIT antibody significantly attenuates the immunosuppressive function of intratumoral Treg (figure 7C and D).23,25 We have also elucidated that granzyme B was expressed highly in NK cells regardless of therapeutic treatments in the TME suggesting that NK cells are poised to lyse transformed cells by having a high baseline granzyme B expression. Besides NK cells, CD8 T cells in the TME were able to express granzyme B, more after anti-PD-1 than after anti-TIGIT antibody treatment (figure 7E). On the other hand, granzyme K was remarkably induced in CD8 T cells on anti-PD-1 or anti-TIGIT antibody treatment but not much by NK cells (figure 6F). Anti-TIGIT treatment induced IFN-γ expression significantly higher than anti-PD-1 treatment only in CD8 T cells in the CT26 TME (figure 6G). While anti-PD-1 stimulated higher perforin expression in CD8 T cells than NK cells, anti-TIGIT treatment strongly upregulated perforin expression in NK cells, suggesting that the main cellular source of perforin can differ by targeting different coinhibitory receptors in the same TME (figure 7H). As a sign of T-cell activation, PD-1 expression was upregulated in CD8 T cells by both anti-PD-1 and anti-TIGIT antibody injection. In contrast, PD-1 expression was drastically downregulated with anti-TIGIT antibody treatment in Tregs compared with anti-PD-1 treatment (figure 7I). Furthermore, anti-TIGIT injection induced a higher PD-L1 expression in Tregs and a higher PD-L2 expression in myeloid cells when compared with anti-PD-1 injection, reinforcing the combination therapeutic rationale with anti-PD-1 antibody (figure 7K and L). Anti-TIGIT antibody treatment appeared to influence the TIGIT pathway biology more in Tregs than in CD8 T cells by the downregulation of Tigit expression in Tregs (figure 7M) in contrast to the PD-1 upregulation by anti-PD-1 antibody treatment in CD8 T cells (figure 7I). The costimulation molecule, CD226, that competes with TIGIT for the binding with CD155, was induced by anti-TIGIT antibody treatment in both CD8 T cells and NK cells, suggesting that blocking TIGIT:CD155 interaction may reprogram them to be poised for activation mediated by CD226:CD155 engagement (figure 7N). The baseline expression of the endogenous ligand of TIGIT, CD155, was higher in myeloid cells and this was further elevated on anti-TIGIT antibody treatment (figure 7O). It was also noteworthy that costimulatory molecules, such as CD80 (B7-1) or CD86 (B7-2), were also upregulated on anti-TIGIT antibody treatment (figure 7P and Q). CD80 expression was significantly upregulated on anti-TIGIT antibody in sorted CD8 T cells and myeloid cells compared with that from isotype control-treated group (figure 7P). On the other hand, it was obvious that CD86 upregulation was seen only in myeloid cells on anti-TIGIT antibody treatment, but not anti-PD-1 antibody treatment, suggesting that anti-TIGIT antibody with functional Fc may induce the “FcγR-mediated reverse activation signals”, resulting in upregulation of the costimulatory molecules.

To verify whether the changes of immune markers seen in gene expression (figure 7) are also found at the protein level, we performed flow cytometry analyses for the markers corresponding to those characterized by gene expression (figure 8). In this experiment, we included both mouse surrogate anti-TIGIT antibody clones, 18G10 and 11A11, both with mouse IgG2a isotype, and also their combinations with anti-PD-1 antibody. Consistent with gene expression (figure 7C and D), anti-TIGIT antibodies reduced the frequency of Foxp3+Helios+ Tregs with treatment of both anti-TIGIT antibodies with or without anti-PD-1 antibody (figure 8A). PD-L1 expression on the existing Foxp3+Helios+Treg trends to be upregulated by anti-TIGIT antibodies as monotherapy, consistent with gene expression data (figure 7K), while their combinations with anti-PD-1 did not significantly upregulate PD-L1 expression (figure 8B), suggesting that the phenotype of Treg was dominantly regulated by anti-TIGIT antibodies in both monotherapy and combination with anti-PD-1 treatment. We were able to see functional improvement of CD8 T cells in anti-PD-1 or anti-TIGIT monotherapy or combination of them by flow cytometry analysis (figure 8C–F). Compared with isotype control group, all treatment groups showed increased frequency of granzyme B+ CD8 T cells (figure 8C) and increased amount of granzyme B expression (shown as higher mean fluorescence intensity) within CD8 T cells (figure 8D), consistent with the gene expression profile (figure 7E). It is noteworthy, however, that the combination of anti-PD-1 and anti-TIGIT antibody treatment did not show additive effects of granzyme B expression, compared with monotherapy of anti-PD-1 or anti-TIGIT (both clones) monotherapy, highlighting the limitation of anti-PD-1 and anti-TIGIT combination in incremental improvement of CD8 T cell function (figure 8C and D). We hypothesized that anti-TIGIT antibody mediates CD8 T cell activation via functional enhancement by the upregulation of CD226, which is a counterpart costimulatory receptor of TIGIT. Similarly to figure 7N, we were able to observe both quantitative (frequency of CD226+ CD8 T cells; figure 8E) and qualitative (upregulation of CD226 on overall CD8 T cells; figure 8F) increase of CD226 expression at the protein level. Interestingly, the combination of anti-PD-1 and anti-TIGIT antibody treatment showed a trend of additional upregulation of CD226 expression within the CD8 T cells (figure 8F), while the frequency of CD226+CD8 T cells was not increased (figure 8E). We observed upregulation of PD-L1, PD-L2, and CD155 expression by gene expression on myeloid cells with anti-TIGIT antibody treatment (figure 7K, L and O). We investigated whether those markers were also upregulated on myeloid cells at the protein level. We observed the consistent trend of increased CD155 expression both by frequency and density on both clones of anti-TIGIT antibody monotherapy treatments (figure 8G–L). However, the combination treatment of anti-PD-1 and anti-TIGIT antibody showed only equivalent or even lower expression of them, suggesting that the expression of PD-1 and TIGIT ligands on myeloid cells was compromised in the anti-PD-1 and anti-TIGIT combination therapy.

As an additional step, we investigated whether the alterations of gene expression induced by anti-TIGIT antibody were seen in protein levels on the surface of myeloid cells as indicative of FcγR-mediated “reverse activation” signals. CT26 tumor-bearing mice were treated with anti-PD-1, anti-TIGIT, or the combination of both twice at day 0 and day 4. Four days after the second dose of treatment (day 8), tumors were harvested for flow cytometry analyses (gating strategy is shown in online supplemental figure 6). We focused our analyses on monocytes and macrophages that express FcγRIV, which is a cellular receptor on myeloid cells for the isotype of anti-mouse TIGIT antibody, mouse IgG2a,26 based on the “FcγR-mediated reverse activation” hypothesis. For myeloid cell activation markers, we characterized the expression of MHC Class II, CD86, CD40, and CSF-1R. Both frequency of FcγRIV+ macrophages or monocytes that express the surface markers (figure 9A) and the surface expression density, were quantitated by mean fluorescent intensity, of FcγRIV+ macrophages or monocytes (figure 9B) to demonstrate their expression levels at the single cell level and to correlate the results with the gene expression profile of sorted cells (figure 7). Overall, anti-TIGIT antibody therapy increased the number of myeloid cells with activated phenotypes (figure 9A) and upregulated the protein expression of activation markers on the cell surface (figure 9B). The increase in activated myeloid cells (figure 9A) and upregulation of the activation markers (figure 9B) were more pronounced in macrophages than in monocytes, most likely because higher FcγRIV expression on macrophages (than monocytes) can induce stronger downstream activation signals. Combination of anti-PD-1 and anti-TIGIT antibody further elevated the myeloid activation phenotypes in the TME. The upregulation of MHC class II, CD86, and CD40 expression was primarily driven by the anti-TIGIT antibody without significant additional effect by the anti-PD-1 antibody, while the upregulation of CSF-1R was further enhanced in the anti-PD-1 and anti-TIGIT combination. These data are consistent with the results observed in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells after treatment with anti-TIGIT and anti-PD-L1 combination therapy in a clinical trial,27 suggesting that the “FcγR-mediated reverse activation” signals induced by anti-TIGIT antibody therapy may be a common mechanism across species.

Discussion

The vibostolimab antibody was selected as a clinical candidate based on its favorable profile, which includes high affinity and selective binding to TIGIT that completely blocks the TIGIT:CD155 interaction. We first investigated the ability of vibostolimab to block the interaction of TIGIT:CD155 from the protein structural point of view.

The structural analysis of the vibostolimab:TIGIT interaction from solution NMR and X-ray crystallography data provides a structural basis for several of these properties. The intricate network of both polar and hydrophobic interactions, including water-mediated contacts, provides the basis for the high affinity of the antibody to TIGIT. The crystal structure also shows unambiguously that the antibody blocks CD155 binding to TIGIT by steric hindrance induced by the significant overlap between the vibostolimab and CD155 epitopes.

The technique of solution NMR spectroscopy is ideal when compared with other techniques to elucidate the dimerization state of a protein such as TIGIT where atomic resolution can be achieved. The system is studied in a near-native solution. No tagging nor chemical modification of the sample is required, nor is the sample injected into a column-matrix environment. Importantly, NMR is highly sensitive to interactions, such as receptor self-interactions, which have been hypothesized to be of weak affinity (dissociation constants in the μM–mM range). In this work, we used NMR to establish unambiguously the dimerization states of apo TIGIT, furthering our understanding of the implications of the TIGIT oligomerization states on its biological activity.

TIGIT was known to be a dimer both in solution and in several crystal forms both in apo and ligand-bound states.18 19 This is also consistent with the in-solution NMR results presented here (figure 3). Yet, vibostolimab-Fab bound TIGIT is in the monomeric state in the crystal and by NMR in solution, indicating that the antibody disrupts TIGIT dimerization. The structural basis for the absence of a cis dimer is unequivocal: the dimerization surface of apo cis TIGIT is blocked by the antibody. Nonetheless, one would expect that TIGIT can still dimerize in trans as observed in some TIGIT structures including the TIGIT:CD155 complex where the ABED strands from the TIGIT back sheet are involved. It has been suggested that back-sheet interactions are relevant to ligand–receptor assembly necessary for signaling. The structural basis for how binding of the antibody prevents TIGIT from associating itself in trans is not clear since the trans dimerization surface is not blocked by the antibody. Subtle structural changes on antibody binding propagated through the antigen structure to the area buried in the trans dimer may play a role. Nonetheless as TIGIT trans dimerization is key to TIGIT-mediated cell signaling,18 the structure of TIGIT:vibostolimab suggests a dual antibody-mediated inhibition mechanism of endogenous receptor:ligand signaling by (1) blocking CD155:TIGIT interaction and (2) disrupting the formation of TIGIT trans dimerization.

Vibostolimab and tiragolumab have similar overall binding affinities (figures1 2), although vibostolimab demonstrates faster on and off rates, while tiragolumab shows slightly slower on and off rates (figure 2). X-ray crystallography data show that vibostolimab has a larger surface contact area on TIGIT than tiragolumab (figure 4). With the similarities in overall binding affinities, vibostolimab showed greater IL-2 production than tiragolumab in an engineered immune cell co-culture experiment (figure 5). How and whether (1) the different binding kinetics and the surface contact area between vibostolimab and tiragolumab (figures2 4) contribute to the potential difference in immune responses; and (2) the observed difference in IL-2 production from an in vitro co-culture setting (figure 5) translates into immune responses in the TME of patients are unclear. Based on the in vivo antitumor efficacy data (online supplemental figure 3) with our mouse surrogate anti-TIGIT antibodies with different surface contact area on TIGIT (figure 6), it is reasonable to postulate that the biophysical differences seen in vitro between vibostolimab and trigolumab (figures14) may not be sufficient to create significant differences in clinical antitumor efficacy as a single agent.

Further mechanistic analyses using a mouse surrogate anti-TIGIT antibody with mouse IgG2a isotype, which is regarded as the functional equivalent of human IgG1, demonstrated that the disruption of TIGIT:CD155 triggers downstream signaling not only in CD8 T cells and NK cells where effector molecules are significantly induced, but also in myeloid cells where chemokines and cellular ligands of the PD-1 and TIGIT pathways are also strongly upregulated and antigen presentation is enhanced.12 It is also feasible to speculate that myeloid-cell activation by “FcγR-mediated reverse activation signals” may represent a potential liability with immune-related adverse effects in clinical studies, as such a mechanism can lead to local and systemic inflammation.

In this study, we share the structural analyses of vibostolimab that provided a fundamental basis to delineate how the TIGIT:CD155 interaction can be blocked to induce immune activation, evidenced by increased cytokine production in multiple in vitro assays. Furthermore, the injection of anti-TIGIT antibody with a functional Fc in a syngeneic mouse tumor model in vivo resulted in tumor growth inhibition, as shown previously,12 well correlated with the activation of downstream biological pathways in various cell types, besides T cells, such as NK and antigen-presenting cells (figures7 8). Overall, these data demonstrate that (1) anti-TIGIT antibody treatment is associated with chemokine expression in myeloid cells significantly higher than anti-PD-1 antibody monotherapy; (2) the “FcγR-mediated reverse activation signals” on myeloid cells following the engagement of anti-TIGIT antibody with TIGIT on T cells resulted in enhanced maturation of antigen-presenting cells, demonstrated by upregulation of MHC Class II, CD86, and CD40 at both gene expression and protein level; (3) the interruption of the TIGIT:CD155 interaction by anti-TIGIT antibody resulted in the downregulation of Foxp3 and Helios within Tregs, implying reduced immunosuppressive functions of intratumoral Tregs; (4) anti-TIGIT antibody monotherapy can massively upregulate effector molecules such as granzyme B, granzyme K, IFN-γ, and perforin to similar levels as anti-PD-1 monotherapy; (5) the biological outcomes within different cell types driven by anti-PD-1 or anti-TIGIT antibody treatment are different; and (6) anti-TIGIT antibody treatment may well potentiate combination therapy with anti-PD-1 antibody by up-regulating the receptors and ligands of the PD-1 pathway. Indeed, initial clinical studies of anti-TIGIT antibodies indicate enhanced efficacy with anti-TIGIT antibodies in combination with anti-PD-(L)1 inhibitors than as monotherapy.15

The immune activation profile in T cells by anti-TIGIT and its combination was evident in our studies, shown in gene expression profiling (figure 7) and flow cytometry analysis (figure 8): suppression of Tregs (figures7C, D, 8A B) and upregulation of granzyme B (figures7E, 8C D) and CD226 (figures7N, 8E F) in CD8 T cells. Upregulation of PD-L1, PD-L2, and CD155 on myeloid cells was also found with anti-TIGIT antibody treatment (figures7K, L, O and 8G8L). It is important, however, to indicate our observation where the combination treatment of anti-PD-1 and anti-TIGIT antibody (for both clones) not just did not further increase the expression of PD-L1, PD-L2, and CD155 but also downregulated their expressions at times, suggesting that the biological effect of anti-PD-1 may not be to further enhance the immune activation properties of anti-TIGIT antibody (figure 8G–L). It might explain why anti-PD-(L)1 and anti-TIGIT combination in clinic did not show as much antitumor efficacy as anticipated.

In this work, we demonstrated how anti-human TIGIT blocking antibodies, vibostolimab and tiragolumab, interact with TIGIT to block its interaction with its major endogenous ligand, CD155 by extensive structural analyses. Although we were able to find a significant difference in the induction of IL-2 production using an in vitro engineered cell assay, it is unclear whether such difference in such an artificial setting translated in clinic.

Altogether, our novel findings provide detailed biological mechanisms of how anti-TIGIT antibodies block the receptor:ligand interactions to induce a robust antitumor activity and how anti-PD-1, anti-TIGIT, or their combinations differentiate immune activation pathways using a well-established mouse model.
