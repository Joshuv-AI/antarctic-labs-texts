Impact of 18F-FDG radioactivity outside the field of view on image quality in a SiPM-based time-of-flight dedicated breast PET scanner: a phantom study
Authors: Takuro Shiiba, Hana Katakami, Aiko Naito, Maki Takamura, Masanobu Ishiguro, Masanori Watanabe, Masaki Uno
Published: 2025

Background

Breast cancer is the second most common cancer worldwide, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases and 685,000 deaths in 2022 [1]. Imaging plays a crucial role in the management of breast cancer, influencing its diagnosis, staging, prognosis, treatment response evaluation, radiotherapy planning, and recurrence detection [2, 3]. Among imaging modalities, 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) dominates due to its comprehensive capabilities [4–6].

Whole-body PET/CT scanners facilitate extensive imaging assessments of the entire body but feature limited spatial resolution due to the large gantry aperture required [7]. Conversely, ring-type dedicated breast (db) PET scanners, with their smaller ring diameter and small crystals, offer higher sensitivity and resolution [7–9]. Such db scanners have shown high detection sensitivity for invasive cancers comparable to that of combined digital mammography with digital tomosynthesis and breast ultrasound, highlighting their potential usefulness in breast cancer screenings [10].

On the other hand, Sato et al. reported that, in db PET, the increase in noise at the ring-type detector edge [11] and 18F-FDG uptake in the myocardium (outside the field of view [FOV]) reportedly affects image quality [12]. In clinical PET imaging, myocardial 18F-FDG uptake is typically minimized by fasting for at least 6 h prior to the scan. However, residual myocardial uptake can still be observed in some cases, particularly in patients with metabolic conditions or poor adherence to fasting protocols. Additionally, certain imaging protocols may not strictly enforce fasting, resulting in non-negligible myocardial activity. Previous evaluations of the effect of radioactivity outside the FOV focused on photomultiplier tube (PMT)–based db PET scanners [12], while studies are lacking that examine the impact of 18F-FDG myocardial uptake on image quality in silicon PMT (SiPM)–based db PET scanners. Several characteristics of PMT- and SiPM-based db PET scanners differ in their photodetector technologies and time-of-flight (TOF) capabilities. Given these differences, the impact should be evaluated for each scanner type independently. Additionally, prior evaluations were confined to uniform phantoms, and none used hot spheres to simulate tumours [12].

This study aimed to evaluate the impact of 18F-FDG uptake outside the FOV on image quality in a SiPM-based TOF db PET scanner using a cylindrical breast phantom and simulated myocardium.

Methods

SiPM-based TOF db PET system

The SiPM-based TOF db PET scanner (BresTome™, Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan) [10–12] used herein featured a 162 mm axial FOV (AFOV). The scanner is equipped with three detector rings, each measuring 300 mm in diameter (bore diameter: 283 mm), with 16 detector modules each. These modules work in tandem with their opposing counterparts to achieve an effective trans-axial FOV exceeding 264 mm. Each module includes a single layer of LGSO (Lu1.8Gd0.2SiO5:Ce) crystals that are optically coupled to a SiPM. The crystal elements are small, measuring 2.1 mm × 2.1 mm × 15 mm, and arranged in a 24 × 24 array. A previous study reported typical physical performance, including a full-width at the half-maximum value at the centre of the FOV of 2.5 mm (radial) and a hot contrast ⊿QH (10 mm) in the image quality evaluation of 49.9%, according to the National Electrical Manufacturers Association NU2-2012 measurement standard [13]. The system had a coincidence time window of 3 ns, and an energy window of 435–650 keV.

Phantom design

Figure 1 shows the geometric arrangement of the phantom and detector used in the experiment. A cylindrical breast phantom [14] containing four plastic spheres of varying diameters was used. The inner diameter and height of the cylinder were 100 and 140 mm, respectively. The spheres, arranged in a planar circle within the phantom, had diameters of 3, 5, 7.5, and 10 mm. The cylinder and the four spheres were filled with an 18F-FDG solution to maintain a sphere-to-background (BG) radioactivity concentration ratio of 8:1, as described previously [12]. The BG radioactivity concentration at the start of the data acquisition was set at 2.09 kBq/mL. This value was chosen to simulate the average radioactivity concentration approximately 90 min after an administration of 3.7 MBq/kg to a 60 kg patient. This concentration level is also consistent with that used in a previous study on this topic [12]. The cylindrical breast phantom was positioned so that the centre (81 mm) position (height) of the detector was aligned with the centre of the hot sphere. To simulate 18F-FDG accumulation in the myocardium, a cylindrical plastic container (diameter, 80 mm; height, 5.5 mm; volume, 250 mL) filled with 18F-FDG was placed on the cylindrical breast phantom. Scans were performed without and with varying levels of myocardial radioactivity concentration, which was set at ratios of 1, 5, 10, and 15 relative to the BG portion of the cylindrical breast phantom.

Geometric arrangement of the detector ring, breast phantom, and simulated myocardium. The dashed line indicates the position where the coefficient of variation was calculated

Data acquisition and PET image reconstruction

The four spheres of the breast phantom were positioned in the same transverse axial plane. The breast phantom was placed at the centre plane of the detector ring (centre of AFOV) and scanned in three-dimensional (3D) list mode for 10 min. The scans were conducted without myocardial radioactivity concentration and then with myocardial radioactivity concentration set at ratios of 1, 5, 10, and 15 relative to the BG portion of the cylindrical breast phantom, resulting in a total of five scans.

All images were reconstructed using a 3D list-mode dynamic row-action maximum likelihood algorithm (LM-DRAMA) [15] with one iteration and 128 subsets with image-space point spread function modelling. In this study, both 10 min and 8 min images were reconstructed from the data acquired in list mode for 10 min. The 8 min acquisition duration was selected based on the standard clinical imaging protocol used at our institution, ensuring consistency with routine imaging conditions. In LM-DRAMA, relaxation parameter λ depends on the subset number, and the noise propagation from the subset to the reconstructed image is suppressed as the subset number increases, resulting in fast convergence at a reasonable signal-to-noise ratio [15–17]. Relaxation parameter λ is defined by factor β, which determines the amount of reduction within a single iteration. We reconstructed the images using β-values of 50 and non-local mean post-filters with a smoothing intensity of 0.5. Attenuation correction was performed using the boundary method [18], which extracts the contours of the emission images and assigns soft-tissue attenuation coefficients to the interior. Single scatter simulation-based scatter correction, random coincidence correction using the delayed coincidence method, and decay correction were applied. The reconstructed images had a matrix size of 240 × 240 with a voxel size of 1.1 × 1.1 × 1.1 mm3 and were represented in Bq/mL (radioactivity concentration).

Statistical analysis

To assess the relative variation in CRC values across different sphere diameters, COV was calculated for each sphere diameter (3 mm, 5 mm, 7.5 mm, and 10 mm) under different myocardium-to-BG radioactivity concentration ratios. The COV was computed as the standard deviation divided by the mean CRC value across all conditions for each sphere diameter. This metric was used to evaluate whether smaller spheres exhibited greater fluctuations in CRC due to partial volume effects and noise. Additionally, to evaluate the statistical significance of changes in CRC values across different myocardium-to-BG radioactivity concentration ratios, the Kruskal–Wallis test was performed for each sphere diameter separately for both the 10-min and 8-min acquisition durations. A significance level of 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Results

Figure 3 illustrates the COVBG for different myocardial radioactivity concentration ratios measured at three positions for 10 min (Fig. 3a) and 8 min (Fig. 3b) acquisitions. For both acquisition durations, the COVBG was consistently higher at the 2-cm position across all myocardial radioactivity concentration ratios. For the 10-min acquisition duration (Fig. 3a) without myocardial radioactivity, the COVBG ranged from approximately 3.9% at the centre to 5.7% at 2 cm. As the myocardial radioactivity concentration ratio increased, the COVBG generally increased for all positions, peaking at 4.8%, 6.1%, and 7.1% for the centre, 4 cm, and 2 cm positions, respectively, at a ratio of 15. For the 8 min acquisition (Fig. 3b), the COVBG without myocardial radioactivity ranged from approximately 4.7% at the centre to 6.6% at 2 cm. While the myocardial radioactivity concentration ratio increased, the COVBG did not show a consistent increase across all positions. For example, at the centre, the COVBG peaked at 5.7% at a ratio of 10, before slightly decreasing to 5.0% at a ratio of 15. Similarly, at 4 cm, the COVBG showed its lowest value of 4.5% at a ratio of 1, then increased to 6.4% at a ratio of 15. At 2 cm, the COVBG generally increased, peaking at 7.6% at a ratio of 15.

Figure 4 presents CRC as a function of sphere diameter for different myocardial radioactivity concentration ratios at 10 min (Fig. 4a) and 8 min (Fig. 4b) acquisitions. The CRC increased with increasing sphere diameter up to a 3 mm to 7.5 mm for all conditions. At a 3 mm sphere diameter, the recovery coefficients for all myocardial radioactivity conditions were clustered at around 0.2. As the sphere diameter increased to 7.5 mm, a significant rise in the recovery coefficient was observed, reaching approximately 0.45. Beyond this point, the recovery coefficients stabilized, with minor variations around 0.5 for all myocardial radioactivity concentration ratios. Notably, the presence and concentration of myocardial radioactivity appeared to have a more pronounced effect on CRC for smaller (3 and 5 mm) versus larger (7.5 and 10 mm) diameters.

Tables 1 and 2 present the CRC, their COV, and the results of the Kruskal–Wallis test for different hot sphere diameters under varying myocardium-to-BG radioactivity concentration ratios. For the 10 min acquisition (Table 1), the COV values indicated that smaller spheres exhibited greater relative variability in CRC. The COV for the 3 mm sphere was 15.39% higher compared to 4.70% for the 5 mm sphere, 3.12% for the 7.5 mm sphere, and 2.93% for the 10 mm sphere. This trend was consistent for the 8 min acquisition (Table 2), where the COV values were 13.46%, 5.51%, 3.07%, and 2.09% for the 3 mm, 5 mm, 7.5 mm, and 10 mm spheres, respectively. The Kruskal–Wallis test showed no statistically significant differences in CRC values across different myocardium-to-BG radioactivity concentration ratios for any sphere diameter under either acquisition duration.

Discussion

Our study results demonstrated that COVBG, CRC, and DI are influenced by the radioactivity concentration in the simulated myocardium. As the radioactivity concentration increased, both CRC and DI tended to decrease, and COVBG tended to increase, particularly at positions closer to the edge of the detector ring. These findings suggest that higher radioactivity concentrations outside the AFOV affect the image quality and quantitative accuracy of the SiPM-based TOF db PET scanner.

The COVBG results revealed varying trends depending on the myocardial radioactivity concentration ratio and detector position. For the 10 min acquisition, the COVBG generally increased with higher myocardial radioactivity concentration ratios, with the most pronounced increases observed at the 2 cm position. This finding suggests that the edge regions of the detector ring are more sensitive to BG noise caused by outside FOV radioactivity. These observations align with previous studies reporting increased BG variability at positions less than 2 cm from the edge of the detector for both PMT- and SiPM-based db PET systems [11, 19]. For the 8 min acquisition, however, the relationship between myocardial radioactivity concentration and COVBG was less consistent. While the COVBG at 2 cm generally increased with higher myocardial activity, the COVBG at the centre and 4 cm positions exhibited more variable behaviour. For instance, at the centre, the COVBG peaked at a myocardial radioactivity concentration ratio of 10 before decreasing slightly at a ratio of 15. At 4 cm, the COVBG showed its lowest value at a ratio of 1 and then increased at higher ratios. These variations may be attributed, in part, to statistical noise, which is more pronounced in shorter acquisition durations. The reduced number of detected events in the 8 min acquisition likely amplified the impact of random fluctuations, leading to less consistent trends compared to the 10 min acquisition. Scattered and random coincidences likely contribute to the observed noise amplification at the periphery of the detector ring, particularly near the edge and in proximity to the simulated myocardium. Random coincidences are known to increase proportionally to the square of the radioactivity value [20], and their influence becomes more pronounced as myocardial radioactivity concentration increases. In the SiPM-based dbPET system used in this study, the necessity of optimizing the relaxation parameter (β) of LM-DRAMA, considering the presence of radioactivity outside the AFOV, as well as the noise suppression filter, was suggested in addition to the impact of noise depending on the position within the AFOV.

Although our study did not include direct measurements of random or scattered coincidences, prior research has demonstrated a strong correlation between outside AFOV radioactivity concentration and increased noise due to these effects [12]. For example, Matheoud et al. reported that high radioactivity concentration outside the scanner’s FOV significantly increases random and scattered events, leading to a ~ 30% reduction in the peak noise equivalent count rate and a noticeable degradation in contrast-to-noise ratio [20]. These findings underscore the importance of addressing outside AFOV radioactivity to improve image quality and quantitative accuracy in PET imaging. Since our study observed variable noise amplification and reduced detectability with higher myocardial activity levels, these results are likely driven by similar mechanisms and emphasize the need for further investigation into noise correction techniques tailored to specific detector geometries and imaging conditions.

One possible approach to mitigate these effects is adjusting scan duration to compensate for the increased noise levels caused by out-of-FOV activity, as suggested in previous studies. Additionally, modifying acquisition parameters, such as narrowing the energy window and adjusting the coincidence time window, could potentially help reduce the impact of scattered and random events, particularly in dedicated PET scanners with limited shielding from external activity. For PMT-based db PET, it was reported that, at a BG-to-myocardial radioactivity concentration ratio of 1:16, the COVBG at the centre was approximately 6%, while that at 2 cm from the detector ring edge was 12% [12]. In contrast, our study found that, with a BG-to-myocardial radioactivity concentration ratio of 1:15, the COVBG was 4.7% at the centre and about 6.6% at 2 cm from the detector ring edge. Although these results are not directly comparable, the effect of radioactivity outside the AFOV might be less pronounced in SiPM-based db PET due to the benefits of TOF [21, 22].

These CRC results demonstrate the well-known partial volume effect [23] in PET imaging, with smaller spheres showing lower recovery due to limited spatial resolution. The larger COV observed for smaller spheres further highlights the increased variability in CRC for small structures, which can be attributed to their greater sensitivity to noise and partial volume effects [23]. This variability underscores the challenges in accurately quantifying small lesions or structures, particularly in the presence of high simulated myocardial uptake. For larger spheres, the CRC values remained relatively stable across different myocardium-to-BG radioactivity concentration ratios, suggesting that outside of FOV radioactivity has a less significant impact on the quantification of larger structures. The lack of statistically significant differences in CRC values indicates that changes in myocardial uptake have a limited effect on CRC under the tested conditions. The similarity in trends observed for both the 10 min and 8 min acquisition durations supports consistency of these quantitative findings, although the slight increase in variability for the 8 min acquisition highlights the expected influence of higher noise. Our observation of robust CRC values, particularly for larger structures, aligns with previous studies that have demonstrated stable quantitative performance using advanced reconstruction algorithms incorporating PSF and TOF [24, 25]. The absence of a notable difference in CRC values among the myocardium-to-BG ratios can also be partially attributed to the configuration of this study, where the hot spheres were positioned at the centre of the AFOV. This geometric setup is optimal for the PET system’s detection efficiency and spatial resolution [26], which may reduce the sensitivity to changes in BG noise caused by higher myocardial activity concentrations. If the hot spheres were positioned closer to the edge of the AFOV, the impact of increased scatter and random coincidences from high myocardial activity concentrations might be more pronounced, potentially leading to greater differentiation between these ratios[20]. Future studies could investigate the effect of sphere positioning within the AFOV, particularly near the edges, to better understand the spatial variability in CRC performance under conditions of high myocardial uptake.

The DI results indicate that smaller spheres experienced greater relative reductions as the myocardium-to-BG ratio increased. This suggests that the decrease in DI for smaller spheres can be attributed to contrast reduction caused by partial volume effects and the presence of activity outside of the FOV, which leads to increased scattered and random coincidences. In contrast, larger spheres exhibited more stable DI values, with lower proportional reductions compared to smaller spheres. This stability is likely due to their higher contrast recovery and reduced sensitivity to BG noise. The observed trend was consistent across both acquisition durations, further reinforcing the impact of myocardial activity on lesion detectability. These findings emphasize the need for optimized imaging protocols or post-processing techniques to mitigate the effects of outside AFOV radioactivity, particularly when evaluating small lesions. Single-scatter simulation-based scatter correction was used in this study. However, this method has limitations, particularly in compensating for scattered photons originating from outside the FOV. Given that outside AFOV radioactivity, such as myocardial uptake, contributes to increased scatter events that may not be fully accounted for by this approach, other scatter correction techniques, such as the estimation true method [27] or hybrid dual energy window method [28] should be considered.

This study has several methodological limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the evaluation was performed with the hot spheres positioned at the centre of the AFOV, where the PET system’s detection efficiency and spatial resolution are optimal. This may have reduced the sensitivity to the effects of outside-of-FOV activity on CRC. Future studies should investigate the impact of sphere positioning near the edges of the AFOV, where the influence of scatter and random coincidences may be more pronounced. Second, this study did not include direct measurements of scatter and random coincidences. Although prior research has demonstrated their correlation with outside-of-FOV activity, a direct quantitative assessment would provide a more comprehensive understanding of their impact on image quality. Future studies should incorporate these factors to further elucidate their contribution. Third, this study used a fixed set of image reconstruction parameters. While the selected parameters were optimized for the imaging system, different reconstruction settings, including variations in iteration numbers, noise regularization techniques, and point spread function modelling, could influence the observed results. Further studies are needed to explore the impact of reconstruction parameter selection on CRC stability under varying BG activity levels. Given these limitations, the present study should be considered a preliminary investigation. Future iterations should incorporate more comprehensive assessments, including a broader range of acquisition conditions, alternative reconstruction techniques, and additional metrics for image quality evaluation, to provide more robust conclusions. This limitation is particularly relevant for clinical cases where lesions are located near the detector edge, as off-centre performance characteristics may require different optimization of reconstruction parameters than those optimal for the centre of the AFOV. Fourth, this study was conducted with a single realization per condition, which may limit the statistical robustness of the findings. Conducting multiple realizations would allow for a more accurate estimation of variability independent of the reconstruction algorithm and improve the reliability of the results. Future studies should include repeated acquisitions to enhance statistical reliability.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study evaluated the impact of outside-of-FOV myocardial uptake on image quality in a SiPM-based TOF db PET scanner. While variations in CRC were observed across different myocardium-to-BG radioactivity concentration ratios, these differences were not statistically significant. The COV analysis of CRC showed that smaller spheres exhibited greater variability, likely due to noise amplification and partial volume effects.

The presence of radioactivity outside the AFOV may contribute to increased noise levels, particularly for small structures, which could influence lesion detectability. However, this study was conducted with hot spheres positioned at the centre of the AFOV, and direct measurements of scatter and random coincidences were not included. Additionally, only a fixed set of image reconstruction parameters was used. These methodological limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Addressing the challenges posed by high myocardial radioactivity concentrations remains important for improving the diagnostic accuracy and reliability of dedicated breast PET imaging. Further research incorporating clinical data and investigating the effects of sphere positioning, direct scatter and random coincidence assessments, and alternative reconstruction strategies will be necessary to refine these conclusions and explore potential solutions to mitigate the impact of myocardial uptake on PET image quality.
