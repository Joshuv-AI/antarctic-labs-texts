Unveiling the impact of bisphenol a exposure on gene expression and immune response in diabetic nephropathy through integrative toxicogenomics and molecular dynamics approaches
Authors: Yan Zhang, Shan Lu, Yijun Dong, Jin Shang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBisphenol A (BPA) exposure has been implicated in various health issues, including diabetic nephropathy (DN). Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying BPA-induced DN is crucial for developing effective therapeutic strategies.MethodsWe analyzed the dataset GSE96804 to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with DN. We intersected these with BPA-related toxicity targets from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database. Enrichment analyses were performed to elucidate the functional implications of these genes, and machine learning models (LASSO, RF, SVM-RFE) were employed to identify key feature genes. Immune cell infiltration and immune-related pathways were analyzed using the ssGSEA algorithm, and molecular docking simulations were conducted to explore interactions between BPA and key proteins.ResultsOur findings revealed significant enrichment of BPA-related DEGs in critical pathways such as the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway, diabetic cardiomyopathy, and the renin-angiotensin system. Machine learning identified four key genes (ALB, CTSD, GAS6, VDAC1) consistently associated with BPA exposure and DN. Immune cell infiltration analysis indicated altered immune microenvironments in DN, with significant correlations between feature gene expression and immune cell types. Molecular docking and dynamics simulations demonstrated stable interactions between BPA and ALB, highlighting specific amino acid residues critical for binding.ConclusionThis study underscores the significant impact of BPA on gene expression and immune responses in DN. The identified key genes and their interactions with BPA provide valuable insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms of BPA-induced DN, paving the way for future research and potential therapeutic interventions.Keywords: Diabetic nephropathy, Bisphenol a, Immune cell infiltration, Bioinformatics, Machine learning, Molecular docking

Introduction

Diabetic nephropathy (DN) is one of the most serious complications of diabetes mellitus, affecting millions worldwide and significantly increasing morbidity and mortality rates among diabetic patients [1]. DN is a leading cause of end-stage renal disease, which presents a substantial burden to global healthcare systems [2]. Environmental factors, particularly chemical pollutants like Bisphenol A (BPA), have emerged as critical contributors to the pathogenesis of diabetes and other chronic diseases [3, 4]. BPA, an industrial compound widely used in plastics and resins, has been detected in various consumer products and environmental sources, resulting in widespread human exposure [5]. BPA is a recognized endocrine disruptor, and its exposure is linked to numerous health risks, including metabolic disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and kidney dysfunction [6, 7]. In diabetic patients, BPA exposure may exacerbate renal damage, accelerating the progression of DN and complicating disease management [8].

Recent studies have explored BPA’s adverse effects on human health, particularly its role in kidney disease [9]. Mechanistic studies indicate that BPA may contribute to kidney injury by disrupting mitochondrial homeostasis, inducing oxidative stress, and triggering inflammatory responses [10–12]. Moreover, serum bisphenol A levels could serve as a prognostic biomarker for chronic kidney disease in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus [13]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying BPA-induced DN remain inadequately understood, warranting further research into specific gene expression changes and immune responses. Toxicogenomic approaches have been employed to examine how BPA alters gene expression in reproduction-related diseases, offering insights into its biological impact at the genomic level [14, 15]. These findings underscore the importance of investigating BPA’s role in DN pathogenesis, particularly in high-risk populations such as diabetics, whose renal systems are already compromised.

Network toxicology, a multidisciplinary field combining bioinformatics, toxicology, and systems biology, offers a novel approach to understanding complex interactions between environmental toxins and biological systems [16, 17]. In the context of DN, network toxicology allows for the mapping of BPA’s interactions with gene expression, immune response pathways, and cellular networks. Integrative toxicogenomics, which combines data from various molecular biology fields, is instrumental in identifying key pathways and molecular targets impacted by BPA exposure in DN. Additionally, molecular dynamics simulations provide valuable insights into BPA’s interactions with specific proteins involved in DN. These simulations can elucidate structural and binding affinities, advancing our understanding of how BPA directly influences the molecular players in DN.

The main objectives of this study are to employ integrative toxicogenomics and molecular dynamics approaches to examine the impact of BPA on gene expression and immune responses in DN. Specifically, we aim to: (1) identify DN-associated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and their intersection with BPA toxicity targets; (2) characterize BPA-modulated functional pathways and immune responses via enrichment and immune cell infiltration analyses; and (3) elucidate BPA-protein binding interactions using molecular docking simulations. This integrated strategy provides a comprehensive framework to uncover BPA’s mechanistic role in DN pathogenesis. Our findings could advance risk assessment and therapeutic strategies for BPA-aggravated DN, with potential implications for environmental health policies.

Methods

Data acquisition

DN-related datasets were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Among them, the GSE96804 dataset including 20 control (Con) samples and 41 DN samples was used as the analysis dataset. GSE30528 and GSE30529 combined dataset was used as the validation dataset including 25 Con samples and 19 DN samples. The raw CEL files of datasets were downloaded and preprocessed using the affy R package. We performed background correction and normalization. This step involved using the robust multi-array average (RMA) method, which includes background adjustment, quantile normalization, and summarization of probe-level data into expression values for each gene. Genes with a mean expression value lower than the 20th percentile across all samples were excluded using the genefilter R package. To eliminate batch effects and ensure that gene expression data from different samples were comparable, we utilized the limma R package for normalization. A ComBat algorithm from the sva R package was used to correct any potential batch effects arising from the microarray platform.

Identification of BPA-related toxicity targets

To identify DEGs between DN and Con samples, we used the limma package for differential expression analysis. Differential expression was defined by an adjusted p-value of < 0.05. We retrieved a list of BPA-induced toxicity targets from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) (https://ctdbase.org/). The list of BPA-related targets intersected with the DEGs identified in the DN dataset to identify genes that are both differentially expressed in DN and associated with BPA exposure. To visualize the expression profiles of the identified BPA-related DEGs, a heatmap was generated using the pheatmap R package. Z-scores were computed for the expression levels of the genes in the DN and Con samples. A hierarchical clustering algorithm with Euclidean distance and average linkage was applied to group the samples and genes. Next, a correlation network of the BPA-related DEGs was constructed using the igraph R package. The network was based on the Pearson correlation coefficients between gene expression values, with edges representing significant correlations (|r| >0.6, p < 0.05).

Enrichment analysis of BPA-related DEGs

Enrichment of Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways was performed using the clusterProfiler R package. We applied the Benjamini-Hochberg method to adjust for multiple comparisons, and pathways with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted to examine the molecular functions (MF), cellular components (CC), and biological processes (BP) associated with the BPA-related DEGs. The clusterProfiler package was used for GO term enrichment, and terms with an FDR-adjusted p-value < 0.05 were retained.

Machine learning models for feature gene identification

To identify key feature genes associated with BPA-induced DN, we applied three machine learning models: Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), Random Forest (RF), and Support Vector Machine Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE). The models were trained on the 31 BPA-related DEGs. We performed LASSO regression using the glmnet R package, selecting features based on the minimal binomial deviance using 10-fold cross-validation. An RF model was trained using the randomForest R package. The importance of each gene was determined by the mean decrease in the Gini index, which measures how much each feature contributes to the prediction of DN. SVM-RFE was implemented using the e1071 and ggplot2 packages. The optimal number of features was selected based on cross-validation accuracy and error rate. A Venn diagram was created using the VennDiagram R package to identify the overlap of key features across all three models.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

Immune cell infiltration in DN samples was assessed using the single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) method, which was implemented using the Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) package. The ssGSEA algorithm computes enrichment scores for each immune cell type based on gene expression signatures for various immune populations. To examine the relationship between immune cell infiltration and the expression of the four feature genes, we calculated Pearson correlation coefficients between ssGSEA scores for immune cell types and gene expression values.

Analysis of immune-related pathways

To explore the potential immune-related pathways involved in DN, we conducted ssGSEA for immune-related pathways such as the TNF receptor family, TGF-β family, interleukins, cytokines, and chemokines. This was done using the GSVA R package.

Molecular docking simulations

To investigate the interaction between BPA and the key proteins encoded by the feature genes (ALB, CTSD, GAS6, and VDAC1), molecular docking simulations were performed using AutoDock Vina. The structures of the proteins were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/), and BPA was modeled using ChemDraw software. Docking simulations were run to estimate the binding affinity of BPA to the target proteins, and the results were visualized using PyMOL.

Molecular Dynamics Simulations

Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted to evaluate the stability of the ALB-BPA complex. Simulations were performed using the Desmond/Maestro software package. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) and root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) were calculated to assess the stability and flexibility of the protein-ligand complex. The interactions between ALB and BPA were further analyzed to identify key amino acid residues involved in binding.

Cell culture and experimental procedures

The human proximal tubular epithelial cell line (HK-2) was obtained from FuHeng Biology and maintained in DMEM/F12 medium, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified environment. To ensure experimental reproducibility, HK-2 cells between passages 8 and 10 were utilized for all assays. For treatment, HK-2 cells were incubated with either 50 µM BPA or an equivalent volume of DMSO (vehicle control) for 48 h. The BPA concentration was selected based on prior dose-response studies [8]. Post-treatment, cells were rinsed twice with PBS and harvested for downstream analyses. Total RNA was extracted using Thermo Fisher Scientific’s RNA isolation reagent, followed by cDNA synthesis with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit. Gene expression profiling was performed via quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) on an ABI 7900HT system (Applied Biosystems). The 2–ΔΔCt method was employed to calculate relative gene expression, with β-actin as the endogenous reference for data normalization.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Intergroup comparisons were conducted using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, with a threshold of p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification and analysis of BPA-associated DEGs in DN

In this study, we identified a total of 10,143 DEGs associated with DN from the dataset GSE96804 (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, we retrieved 44 toxicity targets related to BPA exposure from the CTD. By intersecting the DEGs from GSE96804 with the BPA-induced toxicity targets, we identified 31 differentially expressed BPA-related targets that are implicated in DN (Fig. 1B). To further elucidate the expression patterns of these 31 targets, we employed heatmap visualization, which clearly delineates the expression levels of these genes across the samples (Fig. 1C). Additionally, we constructed a correlation network to illustrate the interrelationships among these BPA-related targets, providing insights into their potential interactions and collective roles in the pathogenesis of DN (Fig. 1D). These findings underscore the significant impact of BPA exposure on gene expression alterations associated with DN, highlighting the need for further investigation into the underlying mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets.

Enrichment analysis of BPA-related DEGs in DN

To gain further insights into the functional implications of the 31 differentially expressed BPA-related toxicity targets identified in DN, we conducted an enrichment analysis. Figure 2A presents a Sankey diagram coupled with a dot plot, illustrating the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the 31 DEGs. The analysis revealed significant enrichment in key pathways including the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications, the renin-angiotensin system, diabetic cardiomyopathy, the relaxin signaling pathway, and the VEGF signaling pathway, among others. The GO terms are categorized into three main ontologies: MF, CC, and BP (Fig. 2B). Notable terms enriched under MF include peptidase activities (metalloexopeptidase and carboxypeptidase) and receptor-ligand activity. For CC, terms such as platelet alpha granule lumen, membrane raft, and collagen-containing extracellular matrix were significantly enriched. In the BP category, the regulation of blood pressure, blood vessel remodeling, and regulation of systemic arterial blood pressure mediated by a chemical signal were prominently featured. These enrichment analyses underscore critical BP, CC, and MF involved in BPA exposure that may drive the pathogenesis of DN, providing a foundational understanding for future mechanistic studies.

Identification of key feature genes using machine learning models

To identify key feature genes among the 31 differentially expressed BPA-related toxicity targets in DN, we employed three machine learning models: LASSO, RF, and SVM-RFE. Figure 3A shows the LASSO regression model, where features are selected based on minimal binomial deviance using cross-validation. The model identified 10 genes as potential predictors. Figure 3B illustrates the RF model, highlighting the importance of genes based on the mean decrease in the Gini index. This measure indicates the relative importance of each gene in differentiating between control and DN samples, with higher values representing more significant genes. Figure 3C and D display the SVM-RFE model, which determines the optimal number of features based on cross-validation accuracy and error rate. The model achieved maximum accuracy and minimum error rate with the selection of 14 key genes. Figure 3E presents a Venn diagram that combines the results from the three machine learning models. Four genes (ALB, CTSD, GAS6, and VDAC1) were consistently identified as significant across all models, indicating their robust association with BPA exposure and DN. Figure 3F further validates the expression levels of these four feature genes (ALB, CTSD, GAS6, and VDAC1) in the GSE96804 dataset. The Violin plots show significant differential expression between Con and DN groups, with all four genes presenting distinct expression patterns (***p < 0.001).

Validation of expression levels of the four feature genes

To further validate the expression levels of the four feature genes (ALB, CTSD, GAS6, and VDAC1) identified as being significantly associated with BPA-induced DN, we utilized the combined dataset from GSE30528 and GSE30529. As shown in Fig. 4, the expression levels of ALB, CTSD, GAS6, and VDAC1 were significantly downregulated in DN samples compared to Con samples (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01). These results, derived from the combined dataset validation, corroborate the findings from the initial analysis and reinforce the potential role of these four genes in the pathogenesis of BPA-induced DN.

These results underline the intricate relationship between key gene expressions and the immune landscape in BPA-induced DN, providing valuable insights into the immunopathological mechanisms at play.

Molecular docking analysis of BPA with key proteins

To elucidate the potential interactions between BPA and the proteins encoded by the four feature genes (ALB, CTSD, GAS6, and VDAC1), we performed molecular docking simulations. The docking of BPA with ALB yielded a Vina score of -8.0. The binding site involves interactions with residues M123, L182, G189, and R117, suggesting a strong binding affinity between BPA and the ALB protein (Fig. 7A). The interaction of BPA with CTSD resulted in a Vina score of -6.6. Key residues involved in binding include D231, T234, and Y15, indicating a moderate binding affinity and potential impact on CTSD function (Fig. 7B). BPA docking with the GAS6 protein produced a Vina score of -6.9. Significant contact points were observed with residues L270, L268, C262, and H261, suggesting a notable binding interaction (Fig. 7C). For the VDAC1 protein, the docking simulation showed a Vina score of -7.2. The interaction is characterized by contacts with residues T175, F157, and M155 demonstrating a strong binding potential between BPA and VDAC1 (Fig. 7D). These results highlight the probable binding sites and affinities of BPA to the aforementioned proteins. The identified binding interactions provide critical insights into the molecular mechanisms through which BPA may exert its toxic effects in DN, underscoring the importance of these feature genes in mediating BPA-induced pathophysiology.

Molecular dynamics simulation analysis of the ALB-BPA complex

The molecular dynamics simulation analysis of the ALB-BPA complex, as depicted in Fig. 8, reveals significant insights into the stability and interaction dynamics of the protein-ligand system. In Fig. 8A, the RMSD of the protein remained relatively stable throughout the simulation, ultimately stabilizing at approximately 2.8 Å. Concurrently, the RMSD of the ligand exhibited fluctuations, stabilizing between 1.8 and 2.4 Å. These results indicate that both the protein and ligand maintained a high degree of conformational stability during the simulation, suggesting an interaction between ALB and BPA. Figure 8B illustrates the RMSF values of the amino acid residues involved in the interaction, with the residues forming interactions with the BPA (highlighted by green lines) showing acceptable fluctuation levels. This stability further supports the reliability of the binding interactions observed. Figure 8C and D detail the specific interactions formed during the simulation. Notably, the amino acid TYR138 was identified as a critical residue, forming hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions with frequencies of 44% and 42%, respectively. This underscores the essential role of TYR138 in the binding process. Additionally, other hydrogen bonds and water bridges were formed, facilitating the stability of the ALB-BPA complex. Overall, these findings provide compelling evidence of the stable interaction between ALB and BPA, highlighting the importance of specific amino acid residues in mediating this binding, which may have implications for understanding the toxicological effects of BPA exposure in the context of DN.

Discussion

Our study revealed significant gene expression changes and immune modulation in DN linked to BPA exposure. The enrichment of BPA-related DEGs in pathways such as the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway, diabetic cardiomyopathy, and the renin-angiotensin system indicates BPA’s potential role in exacerbating DN. The AGE-RAGE pathway, known for its involvement in inflammation and oxidative stress, plays a crucial role in DN progression [18–20]. The renin-angiotensin system, a key regulator of blood pressure and renal function, is also critically implicated in DN [21, 22]. Our findings suggest that BPA’s influence on these pathways may accelerate renal damage by promoting pro-inflammatory and fibrotic processes, further complicating DN management.

The identification of key genes (ALB, CTSD, GAS6, VDAC1) associated with BPA exposure aligns with previous studies highlighting these genes’ roles in kidney diseases. For instance, ALB has been recognized as a marker of kidney damage [23]. In type 2 diabetes, baseline CTSD levels were closely linked to albuminuria, while elevated levels were the best predictor of albuminuria improvement after four years [24]. GAS6 as a non-invasive biomarker for the early identification of DN [25]. Specifically, GAS6 encodes a protein involved in modulating inflammation, cell survival, and tissue repair in the kidney [26, 27]. In DN, GAS6 downregulation may impair its anti-inflammatory and reparative functions, contributing to glomerular injury and tubulointerstitial fibrosis by exacerbating inflammatory responses and inhibiting resolution mechanisms. Regarding VDAC1, this mitochondrial outer membrane protein regulates mitochondrial permeability, apoptosis, and oxidative stress [28, 29]. In DN, VDAC1 dysfunction may promote mitochondrial instability, leading to increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, podocyte apoptosis, and subsequent renal damage [30, 31]. We propose that BPA exposure may disrupt GAS6 and VDAC1 functions through downregulation of their expression, potentially via epigenetic alterations or direct interference with transcriptional regulators. This disruption compromises their protective roles, accelerating inflammation and mitochondrial stress in DN. Our data establish a novel link between BPA exposure and the dysregulation of these specific genes within the DN context.

The ssGSEA analysis of immune cell infiltration in DN, revealed significant alterations in various immune cell populations, including TReg, Th2 cells, and macrophages. The elevated levels of these immune cells in DN samples reflect an altered and pro-inflammatory immune microenvironment. TRegs and Th2 cells, in particular, play crucial roles in immune modulation. TRegs are essential for maintaining immune tolerance and preventing autoimmunity; however, their dysregulation can contribute to chronic inflammation and tissue damage in DN [32]. Increased Th2 cell levels may indicate a shift towards a Th2-dominated response, which is often associated with chronic inflammatory conditions and fibrosis [33, 34]. Furthermore, the presence of elevated macrophages in DN suggests an enhanced inflammatory response, as macrophages are key players in promoting inflammation and fibrosis in kidney diseases [35, 36]. These findings suggest that BPA exposure might exacerbate the dysregulation of these immune cell populations, thereby promoting a pro-inflammatory microenvironment that accelerates DN progression.

Molecular docking and dynamics simulations revealed stable binding between BPA and ALB. ALB plays a crucial role in preserving the integrity of both vascular and glomerular structures. The binding of BPA to albumin may hinder its functionality, which is significant given that lower serum albumin levels are recognized as independent prognostic factors for mortality in DN [37]. Furthermore, our findings on BPA’s impact on immune cell infiltration directly link environmental exposure to the immunomodulatory mechanisms known to be significant in DN pathogenesis and progression [38, 39]. These mechanistic insights (BPA-protein binding and immune dysregulation) collectively underscore BPA’s biological significance in DN pathogenesis and highlight potential therapeutic targets to mitigate toxin-related renal damage.

The public health implications center on DN risk management. Understanding the gene expression and immune responses induced by BPA can inform preventive health policies aimed at reducing exposure to environmental toxins. Such policies could be especially critical in high-risk populations, like diabetic patients, who are more susceptible to renal damage from environmental pollutants. Public health strategies focusing on BPA reduction could play a significant role in reducing the incidence and progression of DN.

Despite its strengths, this study has limitations. The molecular docking simulations provided insights into BPA-protein interactions, but they may not fully capture the in vivo dynamics of these interactions. Future research should involve larger, more diverse datasets and experimental validation of the identified interactions to enhance the accuracy and applicability of the results. Therapeutic strategies targeting the identified pathways and genes, as well as interventions to mitigate BPA exposure, should also be explored in clinical settings.

Conclusions

This study elucidates the molecular mechanisms by which BPA exacerbates DN. We identified significant enrichment of BPA-related differentially expressed genes in critical pathways (AGE-RAGE signaling, diabetic cardiomyopathy, renin-angiotensin system) and pinpointed four key feature genes (ALB, CTSD, GAS6, VDAC1) using machine learning models. Immune cell infiltration analysis revealed an altered microenvironment in DN correlated with these genes. Critically, molecular docking and dynamics simulations demonstrated stable binding between BPA and ALB at specific residues, suggesting impaired albumin functionality. These findings establish that BPA exposure disrupts renal pathways, dysregulates genes governing inflammation/mitochondrial function, and promotes immune dysregulation in DN. This integrative approach provides a framework for investigating environmental toxins; future work should prioritize clinical validation of these targets and strategies to mitigate BPA exposure in high-risk populations.
