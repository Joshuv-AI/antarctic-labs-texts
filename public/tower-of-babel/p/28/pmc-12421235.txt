Single-cell transcriptomic profiling reveals distinct tumor microenvironments in HPV-associated penile squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Lianbang Zhu, Deyun Shen, Jiahao Zhou, Chen Cheng, Zhiyao Xu, Yong Liang, Wen Pan, Tao Tao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPenile squamous cell carcinoma (PSCC) is a rare yet potentially lethal malignancy, often resulting in devastating disfigurement, with a 5-year survival rate of only ∼50%. Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection is implicated in approximately half of PSCC cases and is associated with improved clinical outcomes; however, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood.MethodsTo elucidate HPV-associated differences in the tumor microenvironment, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing on tumors from 11 treatment-naïve PSCC patients, analyzing a total of 52 980 single cells. Unsupervised clustering identified 49 distinct cellular clusters across immune and stromal compartments.ResultsHPV-positive tumors exhibited an increased abundance of mast cells and a reduction in the proliferative macrophages subpopulation compared to HPV-negative tumors. Notably, CD8+ T cells in HPV-positive PSCC expressed lower levels of immune checkpoint molecules, suggesting a less exhausted immune state. Conversely, TIGIT and its ligands were significantly enriched in HPV-negative tumors, potentially fostering an immunosuppressive niche.ConclusionCollectively, our study delineates the single-cell landscape of PSCC and highlights distinct tumor microenvironment remodeling associated with HPV status, suggesting that the reduced immunosuppression in HPV-positive tumors may underlie their more favorable prognosis.

Introduction

Penile cancer represents ∼0.5% of all cancers among men, with a prevalence of 0.1–1 per 100 000 men in western countries [1]. Although rare, it is associated with significant disfigurement, and only half of the patients survive beyond 5 years [1]. Penile squamous cell carcinomas (PSCC), arising from environmental- or human papillomavirus (HPV)-driven malignancies, account for >95% of penile cancer [2]. Persistent and/or recurrent exposure of the penile tissue to HPV leads to the integration of HPV DNA into the host genome and eventual transformation of the host cell into a malignant phenotype. The resulting overexpression of viral oncoproteins E6 and E7 elicits cell cycle dysregulation and host genomic instability through interaction with p53 and pRb, respectively, initiating and maintaining malignant propensity [3]. According to World Health Orgnization guidelines, PSCC can be divided into HPV-driven and non-HPV-related tumors [4], and a recent meta-analysis reported a pooled HPV prevalence of 50.8% in PSCC [5].

Despite being HPV-infection driven malignancies, HPV-positive (HPV+) PSCC are associated with improved survival outcome [6–8] and display better clinical responses to radiotherapy [9], radio-chemotherapy [10], and radio-immunotherapy [11] compared to HPV-negative (HPV−) cases. Owing to the rarity of PSCC and the limited availability of clinical samples, previous studies aimed to reveal genomic profiling and somatic mutation alteration through next-generation sequencing (NEG) using formalin-fixation and paraffin embedded (FFPE) PSCC tissues [12–15]. Recent single cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) in PSCC investigated the altered tumor micro-environment (TME) heterogeneity of penile cancer as it evolves from non-lymphatic to lymphatic metastasis [16]. There is an urgent need to dissect the TME heterogeneity and cellular composition differences between HPV+ and HPV− PSCC in order to explain the dual-roles of HPV-mediated carcinogenesis and its clinical advantages.

In this study, we collected fresh tumor tissues from 11 treatment-naïve primary PSCC patients and performed scRNA-seq on live single cells to analyze the cellular composition of the TME, focusing on differences between HPV+ and HPV− PSCC. We further characterized the heterogeneity within key stromal and immune populations, including endothelial cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), myeloid cells, and T cells. In addition, ligand–receptor (LR) interaction analysis uncovered extensive cell–cell communication networks in PSCC, revealing distinct LR pairs enriched in HPV-positive versus HPV-negative tumors. Overall, we created a comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic atlas of HPV-associated PSCC, enabling detailed insights into tumor heterogeneity, immune infiltration, and gene expression patterns. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the clinical advantages observed in HPV-positive PSCC and establish a valuable resource for future studies on viral-induced carcinogenesis.

Methods

PSCC samples

We collected fresh tumor tissues from 11 patients with penile cancer in the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China, under the authorization of the hospital ethics committee (No. 2016KT67). All PSCC patients had not received any form of tumor-directed treatment prior to tumor collection, including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or surgical intervention. The pathological characteristics of all tumor samples were squamous cell carcinoma, including 6 HPV-related PSCC. According to the AJCC (2017) TNM staging rules, the 11 PSCC patients included 7 T1 stage, 1 T2 stage, 2 T3 stage, 1 T4 stage, and 2 with lymph node (LN) metastasis. Although 2 of the 11 patients had LN metastasis, the sequencing samples did not include tissues from LN or visceral metastatic sites. The selection criteria were strictly limited to primary penile tumor tissues to focus on the intrinsic TME. Meanwhile, we obtained FFPE tissue samples for subsequent staining verification. The HPV status of PSCC tumor samples was determined using p16INK4a (p16) immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining [17, 18] and HPV E6/E7 protein immunofluorescence (IF) staining. p16 positivity was defined as strong, diffuse nuclear, and cytoplasmic staining in at least 70% of the tumor cells, based on validated criteria for multiple HPV-related cancers, including PSCC [19] and HNSCC (Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma) [20, 21]. Tumor samples were considered HPV-positive only when both p16 and HPV E6/E7 protein staining were positive. Conversely, samples were classified as HPV-negative only when both p16 and HPV E6/E7 staining were negative. All procedures were conducted following the patients' informed consent.

IHC and IF

IHC staining of p16 protein (mouse anti-human p16, MXB-bio, MAB-0673) was performed on the patient's tumor tissue slices. The tumor tissue slices were dewaxed, antigen repaired, blocked, incubated with primary and secondary antibodies, and positive reaction developed for immunohistochemical staining. Finally, the image analysis software Image J was used to analyze the p16 staining images. The IHC staining results were delineated by two trained pathologists. For IF staining, FFPE tissue slices were treated the same as for IHC staining, then incubated with mixed primary antibodies overnight (mouse anti-human CD4 antibody ZsBio ZM-0418; mouse anti-human CD8 antibody, Servicebio GB12068; HPV E7 antibody, Abcam ab308296; HPV E6 antibody Santa cruz sc460), incubated with fluorescein-conjugated secondary antibodies, and finally counterstained with DNA dye DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole), mounted with anti-fading mounting medium, and stored at 4 °C in the dark until imaging. Images were processed using Image J software, HPV E6/E7 protein positive signal was analyzed and the number of CD4+ and CD8+ positive cells was counted.

Tumor tissues process

PSCC tissues were washed three to five times with PBS (Phosphate-buffered saline) containing 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco), then minced into small fragments ∼1–5 mm² in size. The tissue pieces were digested in 10 ml of 5 mg/ml collagenase type IV (Solarbio, C8160-100mg) in Advanced DMEM/F12 (Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12, Gibco) supplemented with 2.5% FBS (Fetal bovine serum) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, at 37 °C for 30 min with gentle shaking. The resulting single-cell suspension was centrifuged at 800 × g for 5 min and resuspended in PBS with 0.04% BSA (Bovine serum albumin). Cells were then counted using a Countess® II Automated Cell Counter (Thermo Fisher) with a final viability of >85%.

Library construction and sequencing of single cells

Single-cell RNA-seq libraries were prepared using Chromium Single Cell 3ʹ Reagent Kits v3 (10x Genomics). Cell suspensions were loaded onto a Chromium Chip along with the reverse transcription master mix and single-cell gel beads, targeting the capture of 2 000–10 000 single cells per reaction. Following the generation of single-cell gel bead-in-emulsions (GEMs), reverse transcription, purification, and amplification were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. Each single-cell RNA-seq library was generated with a unique sample index and sequenced on a NovaSeq platform (Illumia) to produce 150 bp paired-end reads.

Single-cell gene expression quantification

Clean reads were aligned to the human GRCh38 reference genome using the CellRanger pipeline (10x Genomics, version 6.0.1) with default parameters to generate gene/barcode matrices. Following alignment, a unified gene/barcode matrix was obtained for each sample and imported into Seurat [22] (v.4.3.0) for downstream analysis. Quality control at the cell level involved filtering out cells that expressed <200 genes, >5 000 genes, and had >10% mitochondrial gene expression. Additionally, genes expressed in <10 cells, as well as mitochondrial and ribosomal genes, were excluded. After filtering, a total of 52 980 cells from 11 libraries were retained for subsequent analyses.

Identification of the clusters and major cell types

Gene expression matrices were analysed using the R package Seurat. For data integration and batch correction, the functions ‘FindIntegrationAnchors’ and ‘IntegrateData’ were applied to data. The top 2 000 highly variable genes were identified and selected by the function ‘FindVariableGenes’ based on high dispersion, and were used in subsequent data scaling and principal component analysis (PCA) with the functions ‘ScaleData’ and ‘RunPCA’. Louvain clustering was performed with the ‘FindClusters’ function using suitable PCs and resolution parameters. Marker genes of each cluster were obtained using the function ‘FindAllMarkers’ or ‘FindMarkers’. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined with false-discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.05 and a log2-fold change in average expression ≥ 1 across clusters. Notably, 17 clusters, as shown in Fig. 1, were grouped into seven major cell types based on the expression of known cell-type-specific marker genes, including tumor-infiltrating T-cells (TIL-Ts), tumor-infiltrating B-cells (TIL-Bs), natural killer (NK) cells, myeloid cells, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, and CAFs. Further heterogeneity re-analysis was performed on the previously identified TIL-Ts, myeloid cells, endothelial cells, and CAFs. For comparisons involving iCAFs and antigen-presenting cells (APCs) by HPV status, the cut-off of DEGs were: FDR ≤ 0.05 and a log2-fold change in average expression ≥ 0.5. Major cell types were annotated based on canonical marker genes.

Signature enrichment analysis

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed by using R package fgsea (v.1.20.0) with Hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) [23]. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted using R package clusterProfiler [24] with the function ‘enrichGO’ (v.4.0.5), in conjunction with the org.Hs.eg.db (v.3.13.0) annotation package.

Cell–cell communication analysis

We employed CellPhoneDB [25, 26], a publicly available repository of curated LR interactions, to investigate potential intercellular communication across different cell types in the HPV+ and HPV− PSCC TME. Single-cell transcriptomic profiles from annotated epithelial cells, endothelial cells, CAFs, TIL-Bs, TIL-Ts, NKs, and myeloid cells were input into CellPhoneDB for cell–cell interaction analysis. The analysis identified statistically enriched LR interactions between cell types, based on the co-expression of receptors in one cell type and their corresponding ligands in another.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.4.0) and R (v.4.1.2). A two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test between two groups was used to determine significance. For scRNA-seq data, DEG analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Data availability

The raw sequence data reported in this paper have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (Genomics, Proteomics & Bioinformatics 2021) in the National Genomics Data Center (Nucleic Acids Res 2022), China National Center for Bioinformation/Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences and are publicly accessible at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa-human/ via accession number PRJCA024930.

Code availability

R and other custom scripts for analyzing data are available upon request.

Results

Single-cell expression atlas of the TME in PSCC

To investigate the heterogeneity of the TME in PSCC, we collected fresh surgical tumor specimens from 11 treatment-naïve PSCC patients and performed scRNA-seq on single-cell suspensions derived from enzymatically digested whole tumor tissues (Fig. 1A). Among 11 PSCC biopsies, 6 were HPV+ and 5 were HPV− cases, as determined by a combination of IF staining for the HPV E6 and E7 oncoproteins [27, 28] and p16 IHC [18] (supplementary Fig. 1A–D, see online supplementary material). After applying quality control filters, we preserved a total of 52 980 high-quality single cells from both the HPV+ and HPV− tumors for downstream transcriptomic analyses.

We next employed the Seurat [22] (v.4.3.0) R package for unsupervised clustering and used Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) for dimensionality reduction and cell visualization. A total of 52 980 single cells were grouped into 17 clusters using unsupervised Louvain clustering (Fig. 1B). These clusters were consistently detected across all PSCC patients (Fig. 1C) and were independent of HPV status (Fig. 1D), indicating minimal batch effects. Based on classical cell marker genes, we classified 17 clusters into seven major cell types: epithelial cells (KRT5, n = 8 572), endothelial cells (VWF, n = 3 359), CAFs (COL6A2, n = 7 551), and immune cells (PTPRC, n = 33 498), which included TIL-Bs (CD79A, n = 6 667), TIL-Ts (CD3G and PTCRA, n = 14 693), NK cells (KLRD1, n = 967), and myeloid cells (LYZ and MS4A2, n = 11 171) (Fig. 1E, G). The identity of each cell type was further validated by the top 10 differentially expressed marker genes, which clearly delineated each population (Fig. 1F). All seven cell types were broadly widespread in tumor samples, highlighting the complex and heterogeneous cellular architecture of the PSCC TME, consistent with findings from a recent single-cell transcriptomic study of PSCC in a Chinese cohort [16].

To explore TME heterogeneity between HPV− and HPV+ PSCC, we calculated the proportions of the seven major cell types in each tumor and stratified the 11 samples according to HPV status (Fig. 1H). All seven cell types were consistently identified across PSCC tumors, though their relative abundances varied, likely due to grade, stage, and inherent sampling variability, etc. Overall, we observed no statistically significant differences in the proportions of major immune and non-immune cell types between HPV+ and HPV− PSCC (Fig. 1H). Notably, TIL-Bs showed a trend toward higher abundance in HPV+ tumors (P = 0.06), while TIL-Ts and myeloid cells trended higher in HPV− tumors (P = 0.09 and P = 0.06, respectively) (Fig. 1I). The remaining cell types exhibited comparable distributions between the two groups (Fig. 1I). Taken together, our scRNA-seq analysis highlights the complex and heterogeneous TME in PSCC, with subtle HPV-associated trends in immune-cell composition.

Stromal cell heterogeneity in PSCC

To better understand the stromal cell landscape in PSCC, we bioinformatically isolated 7 551 CAFs and 3 359 endothelial cells from 11 tumor samples for separate re-clustering analyses. Based on differential gene expression profiling, we identified five CAFs clusters, including two myofibroblastic CAFs (myoCAFs) (ACTA2+, C1 and C2) and two inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs) (LUM+, C3 and C4) and one proliferative CAF (MKI67+, C5) (Fig. 2A, C). Each CAF cluster expressed the canonical CAF marker COL6A2 and was shared across different PSCC patients from HPV+ and HPV− PSCC, albeit in different proportions (Fig. 2B–D). Among five CAF clusters, there were no statistically significant differences in HPV+ and HPV− groups (Fig. 2E).

CAFs exhibiting either a matrix-producing contractile phenotype or an immunomodulating secretome are often termed ‘myoCAFs’ and ‘iCAFs’, with the prefixes alluding to a myofibroblast phenotype and regulation of inflammation, respectively [29]. We further focused on the potential functional roles of iCAFs between HPV+ and HPV− PSCC using differential gene analysis (Fig. 2F). We found that iCAFs in HPV+ PSCC produced high levels of chemokines, such as CXCL13, CXCL3, CXCL12, and CXCL5, suggesting a more active chemotactic and immunoregulatory phenotype. (Fig. 2F, G). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of the differentially expressed genes revealed distinct functional signatures between groups. The 179 genes upregulated in HPV− PSCC iCAFs were enriched in pathways such as ‘response to hypoxia’ and ‘wnt signaling and planar cell polarity pathway’. In contrast, the 87 upregulated genes in HPV+ PSCC iCAFs were associated with ‘response to transforming growth factor beta’ and ‘cell chemotaxis’ biological process (Fig. 2H, I). These findings suggest that iCAFs in HPV+ tumors may adopt a more immune-stimulatory profile.

Among the 3 359 endothelial cells identified by expression of VWF, PECAM1, and CDH5, we classified four vascular endothelial cell (VEC) (PLVAP, ACKR1 and TYROBP, C1-C4) clusters [30, 31] and one lymphatic endothelial cell (LEC) (LYVE1 and CCL21, C5) [32] cluster (Supplementary Fig. 2A–C). Further analysis of the VECs revealed four distinct subclusters based on selected and differentially expressed gene signatures: POSTN+SELEhi (C1), NOTCH4+SELEmid (C2), ID1+SELElo (C3), and TYROBP+SELE− (C4) (supplementary Fig. 2C, D, see online supplementary material). These endothelial subpopulations were consistently observed across both HPV+ and HPV− tumors, and no significant differences in their relative proportions were detected between the two groups (supplementary Fig. 2E, F). Collectively, these data provide a comprehensive view of stromal cell heterogeneity in PSCC, underscoring the diverse functional states of both CAFs and endothelial cell populations within the TME.

Characteristics of myeloid cell composition in PSCC

To investigate the composition and functional diversity of tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells in PSCC, we isolated 11 171 myeloid cells from 11 patients, including 6928 cells from HPV− tumors and 4 243 cells from HPV+ tumors. These cells were grouped into 11 distinct clusters using unsupervised clustering (Fig. 3A, B). Among them, we identified seven monocyte–macrophage clusters (C1–C7), all expressing CD14, CD68, and CD86, and further defined them as: CD14+FCN1+ monocytes (C1), TREM2+ macrophages (C2), CCL13+ macrophages (C3), GCLM+ macrophages (C4), IL1A+ macrophages (C5), LYVE1+ macrophages (C6) and TOP2A+ proliferative macrophages (C7). We also identified two dendritic cell (DC) populations: LAMP3+ DC (C8) and CLEC9A+ DC (C9). The remaining clusters were annotated as mast cells (MS4A2, C10) and neutrophils (HCAR3, C11) (Fig. 3A, C). Each cluster exhibited distinct gene expression signatures, reflecting the functional heterogeneity of myeloid subpopulations in PSCC (supplementary Fig. 3A, see online supplementary material). For example, TREM2+ macrophages, previously shown to possess immunosuppressive functions in cancer [33, 34], were distinctly present, whereas FOLR2+ tissue-resident macrophages, associated with favorable prognosis in some cancers [35], were noted within LYVE1⁺ clusters. These findings highlight the heterogeneity of tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells in PSCC, as well as conserved features shared across different cancer types.

We next examined the relative proportions of each myeloid cluster across PSCC samples. All 11 clusters were consistently detected across patients, although their abundance varied, reflecting inter-patient heterogeneity rather than technical bias (Fig. 3B, D). Notably, two clusters showed significant differences between HPV+ and HPV− tumors (Fig. 3E). Proliferative macrophages (C7) were significantly enriched in HPV− PSCC (P = 0.03), while mast cells (C10) were significantly more abundant in HPV+ PSCC (P = 0.02) (Fig. 3E). To explore the potential functional roles of these cell populations, we performed GO analysis on marker genes of clusters 7 and 10. Cluster 7 (proliferative macrophages) was enriched in cell cycle-related processes, including ‘nuclear divison’, ‘chromosome separation’, and ‘DNA replication’ (Fig. 3F), In contrast, cluster 10 (mast cells) was enriched in processes such as ‘mast cell activation’, and ‘degranulation and mediate immunity’ (supplementary Fig. 3B). To further validate the proliferative macrophages findings, we performed GSEA [23]. Cluster 7 showed significant enrichment in ‘Hallmark G2M checkpoint’ (P = 0.001) and high expression of proliferation and cell cycle-related genes, such as TOP2A, MKI67, and BIRC5 (Fig. 3G, H and supplementary Fig. 3C).

Distinctive functional phenotypes of APC cells in HPV+ and HPV− PSCC

Considering the high abundance of APCs—including macrophages and DCs, which comprised 89.3%±6.3% of myeloid cells in PSCC—we next examined their functional differences in HPV+ and HPV− tumors, despite their being no significant difference in their overall proportions (supplementary Fig. 3D). We performed differential gene expression analysis of APC stratified by HPV status and identified 27 DEGs enriched in HPV+ tumors and 40 DEG enriched in HPV− tumors (Fig. 3I, supplementary Fig. 3E). APCs in HPV+ PSCC highly expressed the S100 gene family of S100A4, S100A7, and S100A8, and these DEG were enriched in ‘chronic inflammatory response’ and ‘cytokine production’ biological processes, including interleukin 1 (IL-1), IL-6, IL-12, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) (Fig. 3I, K). Conversely, APC in HPV− PSCC highly produced chemokines CXCL5, CXCL1, and CCL13, and these DEGs corresponded to cell chemotaxis and migration processes, such as ‘leukocyte chemotaxis’ and ‘myeloid leukocyte migration’ (Fig. 3I, J). These findings underscore the distinct immunoregulatory programs of APCs in HPV-associated PSCC, suggesting that HPV status shapes the inflammatory and migratory dynamics of the tumor immune microenvironment.

T cells composition and transcriptional profiling in HPV+ and HPV− PSCC

To further characterize TIL-T cells in PSCC, we bioinformatically isolated 14 693 TIL-Ts from 11 tumor samples, including 8 775 cells from HPV− tumors and 5918 from HPV+ tumors (Fig. 4A–C). Unsupervised clustering revealed 11 distinct clusters, annotated as follow: CCR7+ naïve CD4+ T cells (C1), CCR7−CD40LG+ effector CD4+ T cells (C2), FOXP3+ regulatory T cells (C3 and C4), PTCRA+ precursor T cells (C5) [36, 37], CD8+ T cells (C6-C9), MKI67+ proliferative T cells (C11), and individual T cells (C10). C10 expressing multiple markers including CD4, CD8A, CCR7, and FOXP3, likely represented a mixed or ambiguous population predominantly from one HPV− sample (PT6) (Fig. 4D, supplementary Fig. 4A, see online supplementary material). Given its mixed phenotype and sample-specific origin, cluster C10 (PT6) was excluded from downstream analyses (Fig. 4C, D). CD8+ T cells were further grouped into four clusters based on differential expression of CRTAM, GZMK, KLRG1, and PDCD1 (supplementary Fig. 4A). The remaining clusters were consistently observed across different PSCC patients, indicating minimize-batch or sampling bias (Fig. 4C, E). We observed no significant differences in the proportions of each TIL-Ts cluster or broader CD4+/CD8+ subpopulation between HPV⁺ and HPV− PSCC tumors (supplementary Fig. 4B, C). These findings were further supported by multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF), which confirmed comparable numbers of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells across both groups (Fig. 4F, G).

Given the critical role of intra-tumoral CD8⁺ T cells in orchestrating anti-tumor immune responses [38–40], we next focused on their transcriptional landscape in HPV+ and HPV− PSCC. Using Hallmark gene sets from the MSigDB [23], we performed GSEA on CD8 + T cells stratified by HPV status. We found that 'oxidative phosphorylation' and 'MYC targets V1' were significantly enriched in the HPV− group (Fig. 4H, I), while HPV+ PSCC was primarily associated with ‘TNFα signaling via NF-κB’ and ‘inflammatory response’, reflecting a more inflammatory immune context (Fig. 4J, K). To further characterize functional status, we analyzed the expression of cytotoxic effector molecules. CD8+ T cells from HPV− PSCC expressed higher levels of granzymes (GZMA, GZMB), whereas interferon-perforin (PRF1), interferon gamma (IFNG), and transforming growth factor-beta (TGFB1) expression remained similar between the two groups (Fig. 4L). Strikingly, the expression of key immune checkpoint molecules—including PDCD1 (PD-1), CTLA4, HAVCR2 (TIM-3), LAG3, and TIGIT ( T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains)—was significantly reduced in CD8+ T cells from HPV+ PSCC compared to HPV− tumors (Fig. 4M). This suggests that CD8+ T cells in HPV+ tumors may exist in a less exhausted, potentially more responsive state. Together, these findings reveal distinct transcriptional and functional programs in CD8+ T cells based on HPV status, which may contribute to the favorable clinical outcomes observed in HPV+ PSCC.

Complex intercellular communication networks in the PSCC TME

To further characterize the molecular interactions distinguishing HPV+ from HPV− PSCC, we applied CellPhoneDB [25, 26] to construct a cell–cell communication network via known LR pairs across the 7 major cell types, including TIL-Ts, TIL-Bs, NKs, myeloid cells, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, and CAFs. Broadcast ligands for which cognate receptors were detected demonstrated extensive communication in various cell types in PSCC (Fig. 5A). Notably, each cell type exhibited a distinct number of LR interactions towards other cell subpopulations, varying between the HPV+ and HPV− groups (Fig. 5B, C, supplementary Fig. 5A–E, see online supplementary material). Given the high abundance of TIL-Ts and myeloid cells in the TME, we specifically focused on analyzing their interaction patterns with other cell types, to elucidate immune-mediated signaling differences associated with HPV status.

For TIL-Ts, we observed 17 coinhibitory LR pairs within the PSCC microenvironment (Fig. 5D, E). Notably, the TIGIT–PVR (TIL-Ts versus epithelial/myeloid cells) and TIGIT–NECTIN3 (TILTs versus endothelial cell) interactions were significantly enriched in HPV− PSCC (Fig. 5D). Human TIGIT, an inhibitory receptor expressed by NK and T cells and mainly known to interact with PVR, Nectin-2, Nectin-3, and Nectin-4, critically limits anti-tumor and CD8+ T cell-dependent chronic immune responses [41–43]. Consequently, targeting TIGIT has emerged as a promising strategy in cancer immunotherapy [44]. In our dataset, TIGIT expression was highly elevated in both T and NK cells in HPV− PSCC (Fig. 5H). In parallel, we identified 102 co-stimulatory LR interactions among TIL-Ts in PSCC, of which 8 pairs were unique to HPV+ PSCC and 13 pairs were specific to HPV− PSCC (supplementary Fig. 6A, B, see online supplementary material), indicating differential activation states of TIL-Ts based on HPV status. For myeloid cells, the co-inhibitory interactions TIGIT–PVR and ICOSLG–ICOS were uniquely enriched in HPV− PSCC, while 15 additional inhibitory pairs were shared across both HPV groups (Fig. 5F, G). Importantly, expression of TIGIT ligands—including PVR, NECTIN2, NECTIN3, and NECTIN4—was elevated in various cell types within HPV− PSCC (Fig. 5H), reinforcing the immunosuppressive landscape of this subgroup (Fig. 5H). Collectively, these findings suggest that co-inhibitory signaling pathways, particularly those involving TIGIT and its ligands, may play a critical role in shaping immune evasion and could potentially influence clinical outcomes in PSCC.

Discussion

PSCC is strongly associated with HPV infection, which acts as an oncogenic driver in a significant number of cases [45, 46]. Despite its oncogenic phenotype, HPV+ PSCC is associated with better outcomes compared to HPV− cases [6–8]. Recent studies reported the heterogeneity of penile cancer from non-lymphatic to lymphatic metastasis in a Chinese cohort [16]; however, these investigations were not centered on HPV infection. The global incidence of PSCC varies significantly due to multiple risk factors, including HPV infection, chronic inflammation, smoking, and poor genital hygiene, along with the absence of protective measures such as routine infant circumcision [5, 47]. In view of the rarity of penile cancer and the limited availability of clinical samples, it is both essential and urgent to adopt high-throughput approaches to comprehensively characterize the ecosystem of the TME in HPV+ and HPV− PSCC.

In this study, we employed single-cell transcriptomic analysis to generate a detailed cellular and molecular atlas of the PSCC TME in a Chinese cohort. We analyzed a total of 52 980 cells from 11 patients—25 905 cells from 5 HPV− tumors and 27 075 cells from 6 HPV+ tumors. Our hierarchical clustering identified 49 distinct cellular clusters, encompassing epithelial cells, endothelial cells, CAFs, TIL-Bs, TIL-Ts, NK cells, and myeloid cells. Our results revealed a heterogeneous cellular composition, diverse gene expression profiles, and distinct intercellular communication networks across HPV statuses.

Notably, proliferative macrophages were significantly enriched in HPV− tumors, whereas mast cells were more abundant in HPV+ PSCC, suggesting divergent roles of innate immunity in these subtypes. Although the overall proportions of other major cell types—such as CAFs, endothelial cells, myeloid cells, and T cells—did not differ significantly, we observed distinct transcriptional signatures within these compartments, indicating functional heterogeneity between HPV+ and HPV− groups. HPV− myeloid cells exhibited increased expression of various chemokines, which recruit immune cells and have distinct effects on tumor progression and therapeutic outcomes [48–50]. For instance, CXCL5 released from macrophages plays a pro-tumor role in hepatocellular carcinoma [51]. CXCL8 is predominantly secreted by macrophages and contributes to the immunosuppressive microenvironment in gastric cancer [52]. CXCL1 derived from tumor-associated macrophages is associated with poor survival in breast cancer [53] and hepatocellular carcinoma [54]. In contrast, CD8+ T cells in HPV+ PSCC exhibited significantly lower expression of multiple immune checkpoint molecules [55, 56], including PDCD1, CTLA4, HAVCR2, LAG3, and TIGIT. These findings suggest that the upregulation of chemokines and immune checkpoints from HPV− tumors may contribute to the observed differences in immune suppression and potentially explain the less favorable clinical outcomes associated with HPV− PSCC compared to HPV+ PSCC.

Furthermore, we characterized the complex intercellular communication network in PSCC through LR interaction analysis. Each major cell type exhibited a distinct number of LR interactions with other subpopulations, reflecting the cellular crosstalk heterogeneity in the PSCC TME. Most notably, inhibitory receptor TIGIT and its ligands PVR and NECTIN2 [41, 43, 57]—known to suppress anti-tumor immunity—were significantly enriched in HPV− PSCC, suggesting a potential immunosuppressive axis that may contribute to poorer patient survival in the HPV− group. However, inherent to the scRNA-seq analysis, low sample size is a limitation and validation of signatures in large PSCC cohorts is needed. In summary, our single-cell data describes a comprehensive ecosystem of microenvironment between HPV+ and HPV− PSCC in a Chinese cohort, and establish a valuable resource for future studies on viral-induced carcinogenesis. We also provide new insights into the clinical advantages in HPV+ PSCC, and these insights may inform future therapeutic strategies and biomarker in PSCC.
