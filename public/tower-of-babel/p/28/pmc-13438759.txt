Quercetin-loaded cellulose nanofibers improve memory, learning, and attenuate endoplasmic reticulum stress in a rat model of Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Mahdi Jalili, Parvin Babaei, Mostafa Golshekan, Mahmood Abedinzade, Sina Andalib
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) lacks effective disease-modifying therapy. This study evaluated the neuroprotective efficacy of quercetin-loaded cellulose nanofibers (QNP) in a streptozotocin (STZ)-induced AD rat model, focusing on memory impairment and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. Forty-eight male Wistar rats were randomly allocated to six groups (n = 8): Control, AD model (ICV STZ 3 mg/kg on days 1 and 3), STZ + empty cellulose nanoparticles, STZ + free quercetin (10 mg/kg IP), STZ + QNP (10 mg/kg IP, equivalent quercetin dose), and STZ + donepezil (1 mg/kg IP) for 14 days. Morris water maze testing revealed that QNP significantly reduced escape latency during acquisition (31%, P < 0.001 vs. AD model) and increased time spent in the target quadrant during the probe trial (P < 0.01). QNP treatment resulted in significantly greater improvement compared to an equivalent dose of free quercetin (P < 0.05). RT-PCR demonstrated XBP-1 mRNA splicing exclusively in the AD model group, indicating activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR). Both free quercetin and QNP completely prevented this splicing, demonstrating potent suppression of ER stress. Cellulose nanofiber-mediated delivery significantly enhances quercetin’s bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy, ameliorating cognitive deficits in AD likely via mitigation of ER stress. Introduction

Currently, about 50 million people worldwide suffer from dementia, with an estimated 60–70% of cases related to AD, the most common cause. AD is a progressive neurodegenerative disease characterized by cerebral atrophy, synaptic dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and the accumulation of amyloid-β plaques and neurofibrillary tangles, leading to cognitive decline1. The intracerebroventricular (ICV) injection of streptozotocin (STZ) at a non-diabetogenic dose is a well-established model that induces memory impairment, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation (characterized by increased interleukin-1 and -8), closely resembling sporadic AD2.

A key pathological process in AD is endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, triggered by the accumulation of misfolded proteins like Aβ, which disrupts proteostasis3. ER stress activates the unfolded protein response (UPR), a signaling network aimed at restoring homeostasis. However, chronic UPR activation can switch from an adaptive to a pro-apoptotic response, contributing to neuronal death. Therefore, molecules that modulate ER stress represent promising therapeutic targets4.

Quercetin, a natural flavonoid found in fruits and vegetables, exhibits potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties. It has been shown to inhibit Aβ aggregation and tau phosphorylation. However, its clinical application is limited by poor bioavailability, low blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration, and rapid metabolism. Nanotechnology-based delivery systems offer a strategy to overcome these limitations5.

Cellulose nanofibers, derived from abundant natural sources, are excellent drug carriers due to their biocompatibility, high surface area, and ease of functionalization6. Electrospun cellulose nanofibers, in particular, can provide sustained drug release and have shown potential for enhanced brain delivery7. While various nano-carriers have been explored for quercetin, the application of electrospun cellulose nanofibers for delivering quercetin in an AD model, with a concurrent assessment of cognitive behavior and ER stress markers, remains unexplored.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the neuroprotective effects of quercetin-loaded cellulose nanofibers (QNP) in an STZ-induced rat model of AD, with a specific focus on cognitive performance and markers of endoplasmic reticulum stress.

Materials and methods

Materials

Cellulose acetate (MW ≈ 30,000, 39.8% acetyl content, Cat# 180955, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) was used to prepare nanofibers. Quercetin (≥ 95% purity, Cat# Q4951), streptozotocin (Cat# S0130), and other chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), unless otherwise stated.

Synthesis and characterization of quercetin-loaded cellulose nanofibers (QNP)

Electrospinning of cellulose acetate nanofibers

A 12% (w/v) solution of cellulose acetate was prepared in a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of acetone and acetic acid under vigorous stirring for 6 h at room temperature. The solution was loaded into a 5 mL syringe fitted with a 21-gauge blunt needle. Electrospinning was performed using a NANON-01 A unit (MECC Co., Ltd., Japan) under the following optimized parameters: applied voltage: 15 kV, flow rate: 0.8 mL/h, tip-to-collector distance: 15 cm, ambient temperature: 25 ± 2 °C, and relative humidity: 45 ± 5%. Fibers were collected on a rotating drum covered with aluminum foil. The collected nanofiber mat was then vacuum-dried at 40 °C for 24 h to remove residual solvents8–10.

Quercetin loading

The dried cellulose acetate nanofiber mat was immersed in an acidic solution (pH = 5 adjusted with 0.1 M HCl) for 1 h to introduce surface charge. Subsequently, the mat was transferred to 50 mL of an ethanolic solution containing quercetin (2.5 mg/mL) and stirred gently at room temperature for 24 h in the dark. The QNP mat was then washed with distilled water to remove surface-adsorbed drug and freeze-dried (Christ Alpha 1–2 LDplus, Germany) at -80°C under a pressure of 0.01 mbar for 48 h to obtain a dry powder. The loading efficiency (LE%) and drug loading (DL%) were determined indirectly by measuring the concentration of unloaded quercetin in the supernatant using UV-Vis spectroscopy (Shimadzu UV-1800, Japan) at λmax = 374 nm. “LE% was calculated as [(initial quercetin mass - unloaded quercetin mass)/initial quercetin mass] × 100. DL% was calculated as (mass of loaded quercetin/mass of QNP) × 10011.

Characterization

Morphology was examined by Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM; Quanta FEG 200, FEI, Netherlands) and Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM; Philips CM30, Netherlands). Fiber diameter distribution was analyzed from FE-SEM images using ImageJ software (n = 150). Crystallinity was analyzed by X-ray Diffraction (XRD; Panalytical X’Pert Pro, Netherlands) using Cu Kα radiation. Chemical interactions were studied by Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR; Shimadzu 8400 S, Japan) in the range of “4000 − 400 cm–1” using KBr pellets12–15.

In vivo experimental design

Animals and groups

Forty-eight male Wistar rats (200–250 g) were purchased from the Pasteur Institute (Tehran, Iran) and housed under standard conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 2 °C, free access to food and water). After one week of acclimatization, they were randomly divided into six groups (n = 8): (1) Control (sham surgery + vehicle), (2) AD Model (ICV-STZ + vehicle), (3) STZ + Empty NP (empty nanofibers, 10 mg/kg, IP), (4) STZ + Free Quercetin (10 mg/kg, IP), (5) STZ + QNP (10 mg/kg IP, equivalent to 2.2 mg/kg quercetin), (6) STZ + Donepezil (1 mg/kg, IP, positive control). Treatments began one day after the second STZ injection and continued for 14 days.

Intracerebroventricular STZ injection

Rats were anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine (100/10 mg/kg, IP) and placed in a stereotaxic frame (Stoelting, USA). A schematic of the injection site in the coronal plane is shown in Fig. 1a. STZ (3 mg/kg) dissolved in normal saline was injected bilaterally into the lateral ventricles (coordinates from Bregma: AP = -0.8 mm, ML = ± 1.5 mm, DV = -3.8 mm) on days 1 and 3 (5 µL/ventricle at a rate of 1 µL/min). The control group received saline. Injection site accuracy was verified in pilot animals by injecting methylene blue (Fig. 1a)16.

(a) Schematic representation of the coronal section showing the intracerebroventricular (ICV) injection site in the rat lateral ventricle (LV). (b) FE-SEM image of QNP. Scale bar: 200 nm. (c) Histogram showing the diameter distribution of QNP (mean diameter: 68 ± 12 nm, n = 150).

Morris Water Maze (MWM) test

Spatial learning and memory were assessed from days 19–21 post-first STZ injection using a black circular pool (diameter 148 cm). The protocol consisted of a 4-trial/day acquisition phase for 2 days (platform hidden 2 cm below the water surface in the NW quadrant) and a 90-s probe test on day 3 (platform removed). Escape latency, time spent in the target quadrant, and distance moved were recorded using EthoVision software (Noldus, Netherlands).

Molecular analysis

After behavioral tests, rats were euthanized (CO2 asphyxiation), and hippocampi were dissected. For RNA analysis, tissue was homogenized, and total RNA was extracted using RNX-Plus (Sinaclon, Iran). cDNA was synthesized, and XBP1 splicing was analyzed by RT-PCR using specific primers (Table 1). PCR products were run on a 3% agarose gel.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Normality was checked with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Behavioral data over training days were analyzed by two-way repeated measures ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Other data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test. Significance was set at P < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS 16.0 (IBM, USA).

Results

The precision of the injection site is revealed by the blue dye in the coronal section (Fig. 1a). FE-SEM analysis revealed smooth, bead-free, and randomly oriented nanofibers with an interconnected porous network (Fig. 1b). The diameter distribution was narrow, ranging from 48 to 92 nm, with an average of 68 ± 12 nm (Fig. 1c), confirming successful fabrication of nanoscale fibers.

Characterization of quercetin-loaded cellulose nanofibers (QNP)

TEM images (Fig. 2) confirmed the fibrous structure and suggested a core-shell morphology, indicating incorporation of quercetin within or on the fiber matrix.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) image of QNP showing the nanofiber structure. The core-shell morphology (arrows) suggests successful incorporation of quercetin within the fiber matrix. Scale bar: 100 nm.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis

The XRD patterns of pure cellulose acetate (CA), pure quercetin (Q), and QNP are shown in Fig. 3. Pure cellulose acetate showed characteristic peaks at 2θ ≈ 8.5°, 11.5°, and 17.5°, indicating semi-crystallinity. Pure quercetin showed sharp crystalline peaks. The QNP pattern showed a significant reduction in the intensity of crystalline peaks from both components and a prominent amorphous halo, indicating a decrease in crystallinity index from approximately 82% (pure CA) to 64% (QNP). This amorphization confirms the successful incorporation and molecular-level dispersion of quercetin within the cellulose matrix .

Comparative XRD patterns of Cellulose Acetate (CA), Quercetin (Q), and Quercetin-loaded Nanofibers (QNP). The reduction in sharp peaks and increased amorphous background in QNP indicates successful drug loading and amorphization.

FT-IR spectroscopy

FT-IR spectra (Fig. 4; Table 3) confirmed the presence of both components in QNP. The broad band at ~ 3415 cm (O-H stretching) was more pronounced in QNP compared to pure cellulose, suggesting hydrogen bonding between quercetin’s phenolic OH and cellulose’s hydroxyl groups. Characteristic quercetin peaks at 1660 cm (C = O), 1610 & 1520 cm (aromatic C = C), and 1380 cm (phenolic O-H) were present in the QNP spectrum, confirming successful loading. The C-O-C stretching vibrations of cellulose (1165, 1110, 1058 cm) were also observed.

The FT-IR spectra of the final nanocomposite confirm the structure of the desired composition.

QNP improves cognitive deficits in STZ-induced AD rats

In the MWM acquisition phase (Fig. 5), the AD model group showed a significantly longer escape latency compared to the control group (P < 0.001). Treatment with QNP significantly reduced this latency compared to the AD model group (P < 0.001), and this improvement was significantly greater than that achieved with an equivalent dose of free quercetin (P < 0.05). The donepezil group also showed significant improvement.

Comparison of the latency time to reach the hidden platform in the acquisition stage of the MWM. *P < 0.05 in comparison to normal group, **P < 0.001 in comparison to normal group. #P < 0.05 in comparison to the STZ group with the treatment groups.

In the probe trial (Fig. 6), the AD model group spent less time and traveled a shorter distance in the target quadrant compared to controls (P < 0.01). QNP treatment significantly increased the time spent (Fig. 6b) and the distance traveled (Fig. 6c) in the target quadrant compared to the AD model (P < 0.01), performing comparably to the donepezil group. Free quercetin showed a moderate but less potent effect. No significant differences in swimming speed were observed between groups, indicating that motor performance did not confound the memory results.

QNP attenuates ER stress in the hippocampus

RT-PCR analysis of XBP1 splicing (Fig. 7) showed a clear spliced XBP1 (sXBP1) band (~ 454 bp) only in the hippocampal tissue of the AD model group, indicating activation of the IRE1α arm of the UPR. This splicing was absent in the control group and all treatment groups, including free quercetin and QNP.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that quercetin, when delivered via cellulose nanofibers (QNP), exerts superior neuroprotective effects compared to free quercetin in an STZ-induced rat model of AD. The enhanced efficacy is manifested as a more pronounced improvement in spatial learning and memory and a stronger attenuation of hippocampal ER stress.

The ICV-STZ model successfully replicated AD-like cognitive deficits, as evidenced by impaired performance in the MWM. Streptozotocin increased the time to reach the hidden platform in both acquisition and retrieval phases. It also reduced the time spent in the target area and increased the distance traveled to reach the target area. Our findings align with previous reports linking STZ to insulin signaling disruption, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and ultimately, neuronal dysfunction3,16.

Lannert and Hoyer showed in their research that STZ reduces the metabolic activity of neurons and increases lipid peroxidation in the hippocampus17.

In another study, it was shown that streptozotocin, by entering pancreatic beta cells and reducing the amount of GLUT-2 receptors, causes a decrease in insulin and leads to the formation of GSK3β. This ability increases the level of amyloid beta and the deposition of amyloid plaques and increases phosphorylated tau, resulting in the loss of neurons and synapses and Alzheimer’s18. Increased Aβ causes the release of calcium from intracellular sources from AMPA, NMDA, and ryanodine receptors, and this high volume of calcium leads to neuronal toxicity and apoptosis19,20.

In this study, treatment of Alzheimer’s rats with cellulose nanofibers containing quercetin led to significant improvements in spatial memory and learning. The results of the Morris water maze test showed that rats treated with cellulose nanofibers containing quercetin, in comparison to the STZ group, were able to spend less time reaching the hidden platform and also spent more time in the target area22.

Mechanistically, STZ has been known to increase two pathologic hallmarks of AD: amyloid Beta, and phosphorylated tau, also inflammatory cytokines(21, 23). Meanwhile, STZ is capable of disrupting glucose metabolism24, and increase activity of acetylcholinesterase, and reducing the brain acetylcholine.

The superior cognitive benefits of QNP over free quercetin are likely attributable to the advantages offered by the nanofiber delivery system. We achieved a high drug loading efficiency (~ 87%) and an amorphous composite, as confirmed by XRD, which typically enhances dissolution and bioavailability25. While we did not directly measure pharmacokinetics in this study, the sustained release profile inherent to nanofiber matrices12, coupled with the potential for enhanced BBB permeability due to the nano-scale size and surface properties18, provides a plausible explanation for the improved in vivo efficacy. The cellulose nanofibers likely protect quercetin from rapid degradation and clearance, prolonging its systemic circulation and facilitating its delivery to the brain.

Quercetin, known as a natural flavonoid compound with strong antioxidant properties, can help protect neurons by inhibiting inflammatory pathways and reducing the production of free radicals. Kampkötter et al. reported that quercetin could reduce damage caused by oxidative stress by increasing the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase and catalase. This mechanism plays a key role in protecting neurons, especially in neurological diseases such as Alzheimer’s26.

In addition, Nguyen et al. showed that quercetin could reduce microglial activity by inhibiting inflammatory pathways such as NF-kB, thereby preventing neuroinflammation, which is a key factor in neuronal degeneration in Alzheimer’s disease. In this study, it was also observed that quercetin reduced the detrimental effects of STZ on memory and cognitive function in rats27.

In addition, the inhibitory role of quercetin on beta-secretase in the amyloid beta formation pathway and the prevention of amyloid plaque deposition has been demonstrated in the study of Elizabeta Zaplatic et al. Also, the protective effects of quercetin through the inhibition of NFK-β and JNK, the blockade of the AKT/GSK3β pathway, and the inhibition of CREB, which leads to the reduction of TNF-α, interleukin 1 and 6, COX2, and VEGF, have been proposed28.

In general, according to numerous articles, the effects of quercetin include reducing ROS and improving mitochondrial morphology and reducing its dysfunction29. reducing tau phosphorylation and reducing cognitive deficits30, and reducing neuroinflammation and amyloid beta levels31.

The increase and accumulation of Aβ can cause dysfunction of various cellular compartments, such as mitochondria (ROS) and the endoplasmic reticulum. Endoplasmic reticulum stress leads to the activation of a protective system called the UPR32. Activation of the UPR in the early stages of AD through autophagy can be protective. However, sustained activation of the UPR can worsen the condition of neurons. It has been shown that expression of XBP1 in Drosophila (expression of the AD-associated Aβ peptide) reduces neurotoxicity. Also, the interaction of IRE1 with presenilin through the JNK pathway activates proapoptotic signaling.

In a study, quercetin has been shown to prevent tau hyperphosphorylation by affecting the IRE1 pathway and XBP1 expression33. In another study, it has been shown that translation of the XBP1 transcript leads to the production of XBP1s protein. This protein can induce autophagy by activating the BECN1 and ATG pathways. It can also inhibit endoplasmic reticulum stress through ERAD34.

In another study, the dual role of XBP1 in the cell has been well investigated. In this study, it was shown that the BiP protein can both lead to cell survival and drive the cell towards autophagy by activating the UPR system and producing XBP1 transcript and its splicing35.

Martínez et al. also reported that XBP1 helps protect neurons by inhibiting inflammatory pathways and reducing oxidative stress. These results suggest that quercetin may have positive effects on memory and learning function by activating protective cellular pathways36.

Studies have shown that the use of nanoparticles as drug carriers can lead to increased stability of natural drugs such as quercetin. In a similar study, Hu et al. showed that cellulose nanoparticles increased bioavailability and reduced the rapid degradation of the drug in the environment. This is especially important for compounds such as quercetin, which have low water solubility37.

Based on the mentioned findings, XBP1 mRNA could not normally be spliced in the hippocampus tissue; however, ICV injection of STZ leads to the presence of XBP1s in cells of the mentioned tissue. Previous studies showed that XBP1 splicing was the first direct sign of UPR activity resulting from ER stress. In fact, the presence of Aβ elevated by STZ probably stimulated the proinflammatory cascades, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and disruption of calcium homeostasis, which all cause ER stress, UPR induction, and, eventually, apoptosis induction and neuron death38.

The lack of XBP-1 splicing in control groups indicates that the IRE-1 pathway activation did not happen in the animals of the study groups. Therefore, based on animal yielded results authors hypothesized that inhibition of IRE-1 to prevent XBP-1 mRNA splicing might be a new therapeutic strategy against AD. Further study could unlock more secrets of AD pathogenesis due to neuro-inflammatory and ER-stress mechanisms of disease.

In addition, no XBP1-mRNA splicing was found in either of treatments groups. It seems that treatment with quercetin inhibits ER stress and initiation of UPR activity. However, further research is needed to study the downstream signaling pathways of ER, such as protein kinase RNA-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK) and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6).

Mechanistically, we focused on ER stress, a converging pathway in AD pathogenesis. The presence of spliced XBP1 exclusively in the AD model group confirms UPR activation via the IRE1α pathway. The complete prevention of XBP1 splicing by both free quercetin and QNP indicates their potent ability to alleviate ER stress at the transcriptional level.

Limitations and future perspectives

While our findings are promising, this study has limitations. First, the precise pharmacokinetics and brain distribution profile of QNP were not characterized. Future studies should quantify quercetin levels in plasma and brain tissue. Second, our investigation of ER stress, though expanded beyond XBP1 splicing, did not cover all UPR branches (e.g., PERK-eIF2α-ATF4, ATF6). Assessing phosphorylation of PERK and processing of ATF6 would provide a more complete picture. Third, histopathological correlates such as neuronal survival (Nissl staining), amyloid burden, and glial activation were not evaluated. Finally, long-term safety studies of QNP are necessary. Future work will address these points, including dose-response studies, evaluation of other AD hallmarks (Aβ, p-tau), and exploring synergistic effects with other therapeutic agents.

Conclusion

In conclusion, electrospun cellulose nanofibers serve as an efficient carrier for quercetin, significantly enhancing its therapeutic potential against STZ-induced Alzheimer’s-like pathology in rats. The QNP formulation improved cognitive performance more effectively than free quercetin and provided robust attenuation of hippocampal ER stress, as evidenced by the inhibition of XBP1 splicing. These results highlight the promise of cellulose-based nanofibers as a biocompatible platform for delivering neuroprotective compounds and underscore the modulation of ER stress as a valuable therapeutic strategy for AD. Further research is warranted to elucidate the detailed pharmacokinetics and long-term efficacy of this nano-formulation.
