Preclinical efficacy of iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells against esophageal squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Yuting Peng, Tian Guan, Yi Xiao, Shugeng Lin, Jijun Wu, Yan Lin, Juping Wang, Haoyu Zeng, Changchun Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMUC1 is frequently overexpressed in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), but MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells remain unexplored in this malignancy.MethodsMUC1 expression was assessed by IHC in 90 ESCC specimens, 47 paired adjacent normal tissues, and normal human organs. Introduction

Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, particularly in China, where it constitutes the predominant histological subtype [1, 2]. Despite multimodal treatment strategies—including surgery, chemoradiotherapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitors—the prognosis for patients with advanced or metastatic ESCC remains dismal, with five-year survival rates below 20% [3, 4]. Acquired and intrinsic resistance to existing therapies underscores an urgent unmet need for novel treatment paradigms.

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy has revolutionized hematologic malignancies [5]. However, its application to solid tumors such as ESCC is hindered by on-target off-tumor toxicities, cytokine release syndrome, neurotoxicity, an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, and high manufacturing costs [6, 7]. In contrast, natural killer (NK) cells offer a compelling alternative. Unlike T cells, NK cells exert major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-independent cytotoxicity, substantially lowering the risk of graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) and severe cytokine-related adverse events. This safety profile enables the development of allogeneic, “off-the-shelf” CAR-engineered NK cell products [8, 9], as exemplified by the clinical success of CD19-targeted CAR-NK cells in lymphoid malignancies [10].

Primary NK cells, however, exhibit donor-dependent variability and limited expansion capacity. Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) provide a scalable, standardized platform for generating homogeneous NK cell products with consistent quality and potency [11, 12]. iPSC-derived NK (iPSC-NK) cells can be readily engineered and expanded to clinical scale, making them ideally suited for broad application.

Effective CAR therapy further requires a tumor-associated antigen with high expression in malignancy and minimal presence in normal tissues. Mucin 1 (MUC1), a transmembrane glycoprotein, is frequently overexpressed and aberrantly glycosylated in multiple epithelial cancers, including ESCC [13, 14]. The tumor-associated MUC1 promotes oncogenic signaling, metastasis, and immune evasion, while its expression in normal adult tissues is largely restricted to the apical surface of glandular epithelia [15]. Although MUC1-targeted CAR-T cells have shown preclinical activity against various solid tumors [16], and our recent work demonstrated efficacy of MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells against tongue squamous cell carcinoma [17], the therapeutic potential of iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells specifically for ESCC remains unexplored.

Here, we first comprehensively characterized MUC1 protein expression in a large ESCC cohort, paired adjacent normal esophageal epithelium, and normal human organs. We then generated MUC1-specific CAR-NK cells from iPSCs via lentiviral transduction and directed differentiation. Finally, we evaluated their antitumor activity against MUC1-expressing ESCC using patient specific organoids (PSOs) combined with matched primary ESCC cells, as well as against established MUC1-positive ESCC cell lines (KYSE140 and KYSE150) and KYSE140 xenograft in vivo. Collectively, this study establishes a preclinical rationale for developing iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells as a novel, off-the-shelf immunotherapy for ESCC.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks from 90 ESCC patients and 47 paired adjacent normal esophageal epithelial tissues (collected > 2 cm from the tumor margin) were obtained from the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Cancer Hospital of Shantou University Medical College (SUMC), between February 2015 and March 2017. FFPE sections from normal human organs (lung, stomach, heart, liver, thymus, colon, testis, cerebellum, and prostate) and a known MUC1-positive breast cancer specimen served as controls. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of SUMC (Approval Nos. 2022103, SUMC2022-342 and SUMC2026-002), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

IHC was performed on 4-μm-thick tissue sections. Following deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was conducted under high temperature and high pressure in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2, and non-specific binding was prevented by incubation with normal horse serum. Sections were then incubated with a primary anti-MUC1 antibody overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, immunoreactivity was visualized using a two-step detection kit with AEC as the chromogen. Sections were counterstained with Mayer's hematoxylin. MUC1 expression was independently evaluated by two pathologists blinded to clinical data. Positive staining was defined as distinct red signals localized to the cell membrane and/or cytoplasm, and the percentage of positively stained tumor cells was quantified for each case.

Cell lines and culture

The human ESCC cell line KYSE150 was kindly provided by Professor Xu Liyan (SUMC). Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% L-glutamine, 1% sodium pyruvate, and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. HEK293T cells (used for lentivirus production) were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS. All cell lines were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ and were routinely confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

Construction of MUC1-CAR lentiviral vector and transduction

The MUC1-CAR construct comprised an anti-MUC1 single-chain variable fragment (scFv) extracellular domain, a transmembrane region, and the intracellular signaling domains of 4-1BB and CD3ζ. The CAR gene was cloned into the pWXLD lentiviral transfer plasmid. For lentivirus production, HEK293T cells were transfected with the packaging plasmid psPAX2 (2 μg/mL), the envelope plasmid pMD.2G (1 μg/mL), and the CAR transfer plasmid using polyethylenimine (PEI, 10 μg/mL) in Opti-MEM medium. After 6 h, the medium was replaced with high-glucose DMEM. Viral supernatants were collected every 24 h, and lentivirus was concentrated using a virus concentration reagent (Accurate Biology Inc., Hunan, China, Cat. #AG51001).

iPSCs were reprogrammed from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) using the CoMiP system. For transduction, iPSCs were seeded into 12-well plates at 1–3 × 106 cells per well and incubated with concentrated lentivirus in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene for 24 h, after which the medium was replaced with fresh culture medium. Successfully transduced iPSCs were expanded for subsequent differentiation.

Differentiation of iPSCs into MUC1-CAR-NK cells

Differentiation of CAR-iPSCs and control iPSCs into iPSC-derived CAR-NK or NK cells was performed according to the STEMdiff™ NK Cell Kit (STEMCELL, Cat. #100–0170) protocol. Briefly, CD34+ hematopoietic progenitor cells were first generated from iPSCs. iPSCs were seeded in AggreWell™ 400 6-well plates (STEMCELL, Cat. #34,421) and cultured with embryoid body (EB) formation medium. The medium was replaced with EB medium A on day 2 and EB medium B on day 3. On day 5, EBs were eluted and collected. CD34+ hematopoietic progenitor cells were isolated on day 12 using the EasySep™ Human CD34 Positive Selection Kit II (STEMCELL, Cat. #17879A). The isolated CD34⁺ cells were then seeded in coated AggreWell™ 400 24-well plates (STEMCELL, Cat. #34,411) and cultured in StemSpan™ Lymphoid Progenitor Expansion Medium for approximately two weeks. On day 14, the medium was switched to StemSpan™ NK Cell Differentiation Medium (STEMCELL) and cultured for an additional two weeks. On day 40, mature iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells were harvested for downstream experiments.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cell phenotypes were characterized using a BD FACS Aria II or BD Accuri C6 flow cytometer. The following markers were examined at successive stages: undifferentiated iPSCs (MUC1-CAR+, Oct4+, SSEA3+, SSEA4+, TRA-1–60+, TRA-1–81+, Nanog+); CAR-NK progenitors (MUC1-CAR+, CD34+, CD45+); and mature CAR-NK cells (MUC1-CAR+, CD45+, CD3−, CD56+, CD16+). CAR expression frequencies across iPSC, progenitor, and mature stages are reported in Results section.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from KYSE150 and HEK293 cells using a commercial kit. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized with a ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Kit. qPCR was performed using a Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System. GAPDH served as the endogenous control, and relative MUC1 expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. At least four technical replicates (n ≥ 4) were analyzed per sample.

Cytotoxicity assays

CCK-8 Assay: The viability of ESCC cells (patient-derived primary ESCC or KYSE150 cells) co-cultured with the iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells, iPSC-derived NK cells, and the blank NK-culturing medium was analyzed by Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, ESCC cells were resuspended at a density of 2 × 105 cells/ml and seeded into 96-well plate, with 100 μL (2 × 104 cells) in each well for 24 h at 37 °C, followed by treated with iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells and iPSC-derived NK cells at the same effector cells to target cells’ ratio of 1:1, respectively. After incubated for 12 h, the NK-culturing medium and NK cells suspension were removed, washed, and replaced by new medium. 10 µL CCK8 reagent was added to each well; the plates were shielded from light and incubated for 2 ~ 4 h at 37 °C. Optical density (OD) values were measured by enzyme labeled instrument at a wavelength of 450 nm using ESCC cells without co-culturing with NK cells as blank controls. The specific cytotoxicity was calculated as follows: % Cytotoxicity = [1 – (ODexperiment / ODtarget alone)] × 100%.

xCELLigence Real-Time Cell Analysis system: The cytotoxicity of the iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells against human ESCC lines (KYSE150 and KYSE140) was dynamically measured by the xCELLigence Real-Time Cell Analysis system, respectively. RPMI with 10% FBS was added to E-Plate 16 (Agilent) at 50 μL per well for measuring background impedance. Add 100 μL of KYSE150 cell suspension to each well of E-Plate 16 at a density of 1 × 105 cells/ml. Place the 16-well plate at 37 °C and 5% CO2; measure impedance every 15 min for approximately 22 h. After that, replace half of the RPMI in the 16-well of E-Plate with NK-culturing medium, which was used as a blank control group. The experimental group used 50 μL iPSC-derived NK or iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cell suspension diluted to a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL. Impedance is measured every 15 min for approximately 24 h. The cell index of each group measured by xCELLigence RTCA was normalized across the timeline.

Generation of ESCC PSOs and the corresponding primary cultured cancer cells

Fresh tumor tissues were surgically obtained from ESCC patients and maintained in storage buffer (Guangdong Procapzoom Biosciences, Inc., Guangdong, China) to preserve viability. Tissues were transferred to a cell culture chamber, washed 3–6 times with washing buffer, and minced into fragments < 200 μm in diameter in storage buffer. The suspension was filtered through a 100-μm pore-size membrane to generate pre-organoid tissue blocks. These blocks were seeded into 96-well plates in GelMA gel (Engineering for Life, Suzhou, China). Meanwhile, single cells released during the process were collected and cultured in flasks coated with gelatin (Sigma-Aldrich, Shanghai, China, Cat. #48,722) for the generation of patient-derived primary ESCC cells. Both PSOs and primary cells were cultured in primary cell culture medium at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After 24 h, activated PSOs adhering to the GelMA gel were used for NK cell killing assays, while non-adherent, non-viable organoids were removed by washing.

Live/dead staining of PSOs

After 12 h of co-culture with NK cells, organoids were collected and stained using a Live/Dead Cell Staining Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, PSOs were incubated with 2 μM Calcein-AM (green fluorescence, labeling live cells) and 4 μM propidium iodide (PI, red fluorescence, labeling dead cells) for 30 ~ 45 min at room temperature. Stained organoids were imaged using a fluorescence microscope, and fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ software.

In vivo xenograft experiment

NOD-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1/Bcgen (BNDG) mice [17] were subcutaneously inoculated with 2 × 106 KYSE140 cells in the right hind flank. Treatment was initiated one week after inoculation (defined as day 0). Mice received weekly tail vein injections on days 0, 7, 14, 21, and 28, according to the following groups: (1) saline control (200 μL per mouse), (2) non-transduced iPSC-derived NK cell control ( 2 × 106 cells in 200 μL saline per mouse), and (3) MUC1-CAR-NK cell treatment ( 2 × 106 cells in 200 μL saline per mouse).

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 and GraphPad Prism 10.0. The Chi-square test was used to assess associations between MUC1 expression and clinicopathological parameters. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method with the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to identify independent prognostic factors. For in vitro experiments, one-way ANOVA followed by Newman–Keuls post hoc test was used for multiple pairwise comparisons. Tukey's test was applied for xCELLigence RTCA data. Two-way.

ANOVA and Sidak t test were performed to analyze the differences in tumor size and body weight of mice in different groups. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

MUC1 expression in human ESCC and its association with clinical parameters

MUC1 protein was predominantly localized to the cytoplasm and/or plasma membrane in 70% (63/90) of human esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) specimens, a frequency significantly higher than that observed in adjacent normal esophageal epithelial tissues (14.9%, 7/47; P < 0.001). Among MUC1-positive ESCC cases, 88.9% (56/63) and 42.8% (27/63) exhibited MUC1-positive tumor cell proportions exceeding 50 and 80%, respectively (Fig. 1A–D and Table 1). In autopsy-derived normal tissues, MUC1 expression was absent in the heart, liver, thymus, colon, testis, cerebellum, and prostate, with only weak staining detected in normal lung and gastric tissues (Fig. 1E).

Efficacy of iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells against MUC1-expressing ESCC patient-derived organoids and patient-specific primary cells

Given the established predictive value of patient-derived organoids (PDOs) for therapeutic responses, including responses to investigational drugs and chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell immunotherapy, we established a novel model combining ESCC patient-derived organoids (PSOs) with patient-specific primary ESCC cells to evaluate the antitumor efficacy of iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells [18]. A total of six MUC1-expressing ESCC specimens were used to generate PSOs, from which dissociated single cells were subsequently cultured in flasks to establish patient-specific primary cancer cell lines.

Following co-culture with iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells at a consistent dose, increasing effector cell accumulation around PSOs was observed from 0 to 12 h, accompanied by visible structural disruption and peripheral disintegration of the organoids. In contrast, these features were markedly less pronounced in PSOs treated with control iPSC-derived NK cells or NK culture medium alone (Fig. 2A). To quantify cytotoxicity, EthD-I staining (red fluorescence, indicative of dead cells) was performed. PSOs exposed to MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells for 12 h exhibited significantly higher red fluorescence intensity compared with both control groups (both P < 0.01; Fig. 2B). Consistently, the killing efficiency of MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells was significantly superior to that of the two controls throughout the assessment period (Fig. 2C).

We further evaluated the activity of iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells against patient-specific primary ESCC cells derived from six individual patients using CCK-8 assays. MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells consistently and significantly reduced the viability of all six patient-specific primary ESCC cell lines compared with iPSC-derived NK cells or NK culture medium alone (all P < 0.001; Fig. 2D). Immunohistochemistry confirmed MUC1 expression in the parental tumors (Fig. 2E). The originally constructed iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells had MUC1-CAR expression rates of 98%, 95.4%, and 98.8% in iPSC, CAR-NK progenitor, and mature CAR-NK stages, respectively (Fig. 2F). Collectively, these findings demonstrate the specific therapeutic potential of iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells against MUC1-expressing ESCC.

Potent cytotoxicity of iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells against MUC1-expressing ESCC cell lines

The MUC1 expression status of the ESCC cell lines KYSE150 (Fig. 3A, E) and KYSE140 (Fig. 3F) was characterized by qPCR (MUC1 mRNA) and flow cytometry (MUC1 protein), with HEK293 cells serving as negative controls. Flow cytometric analysis revealed robust MUC1 protein expression on the surface of 80.2% of KYSE150 cells and 61.9% of KYSE140 cells, whereas HEK293 cells were uniformly negative (Fig. 3D–F). These results validated KYSE150 and KYSE140 as MUC1-positive ESCC models for subsequent in vitro cytotoxicity assays.

The CCK8 assay demonstrated that co-culture with iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells significantly reduced the viability of KYSE150 cells (P < 0.0001, Fig. 3B) and KYSE140 cells (P < 0.0001, Fig. 3C), respectively, compared with either the iPSC-derived NK cell group (effector-to-target ratio = 1:1) or the NK culture medium control group. Using xCELLigence real-time cell analysis (RTCA), we observed that the viability of KYSE150 (P < 0.0001, Fig. 3G) and KYSE140 (P < 0.0001, Fig. 3H) co-cultured with iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells declined progressively and more steeply than those co-cultured with iPSC-derived NK cells (both P < 0.0001) or the NK culture medium control. The cell indices of KYSE150 and KYSE140 decreased to approximately 20 and 60% at 3 h, respectively, suggesting that the magnitude of cytotoxic activity correlated with the relative MUC1 expression levels on the target cells.

iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells inhibit KYSE140 xenograft growth in BNDG mice without causing death and body weight reduction

In vivo, weekly administration of MUC1 CAR-NK cells (five total doses) in a KYSE140 xenograft model produced sustained tumor growth inhibition over approximately three weeks (Fig. 4). Significant divergence from control groups became evident around day 15 and persisted through day 33. This prolonged in vivo efficacy suggests that the infused CAR-NK cells remained functionally active over an extended period, indirectly supporting their persistence and sustained killing capacity within the tumor microenvironment. Compared with control groups, the iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK treatment group showed no significant body weight loss or treatment-related death, indicating a favorable systemic safety profile.

Discussion

MUC1 is aberrantly overexpressed in the majority of ESCC cases (70% of specimens in this cohort) and is predominantly localized to the cytoplasm and/or plasma membrane. In contrast, its expression is minimal or absent in most normal adult tissues examined, including heart, liver, thymus, colon, testis, cerebellum, and prostate, with only weak staining detected in normal lung and gastric tissues. These expression patterns position MUC1 as a plausible immunotherapeutic target in ESCC. Notably, among MUC1-positive patients, nearly 90% exhibited MUC1 expression in > 50% of tumor cells, and over 40% showed expression in > 80% of tumor cells, suggesting that a substantial subset of patients with MUC1-positive ESCC may benefit from MUC1-targeted therapies. However, MUC1 expression was not an independent prognostic factor for overall survival in our cohort after adjustment for TNM stage, which remained the dominant predictor. This finding indicates that while MUC1 serves as a tumor-associated antigen, its expression alone does not dictate clinical outcome—an important consideration for patient selection in future trials.

In this study, we generated iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells and evaluated their cytotoxic activity against MUC1-expressing ESCC using three complementary preclinical models: patient-specific organoids (PSOs) combined with matched primary ESCC cells [18], established ESCC cell lines (KYSE150 and KYSE140) in vitro, and a KYSE140 xenograft model in BNDG mice in vivo.

Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) are increasingly recognized for their ability to recapitulate the cellular diversity, histological architecture, and drug response profiles of original tumors, with emerging evidence supporting their predictive utility for CAR-T cell immunotherapy [19–22]. By incorporating both PSOs and matched primary cell cultures from six individual patients, our approach enabled cross-validated assessment of CAR-NK efficacy. To further characterize the kinetics of CAR-NK-mediated killing, we employed real-time impedance-based xCELLigence assays. MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells initiated target cell elimination within hours of co-culture and sustained progressive cytotoxicity over the entire observation period (up to 24 h), with no evidence of early functional exhaustion. These time-course data are consistent with preserved serial killing capacity. In vivo, weekly administration of MUC1 CAR-NK cells (five total doses) in a KYSE140 xenograft model produced sustained tumor growth inhibition over approximately three weeks. Significant divergence from control groups became evident around day 15 and persisted through day 33. This prolonged in vivo efficacy suggests that the infused CAR-NK cells remained functionally active over an extended period, indirectly supporting their persistence and sustained killing capacity within the tumor microenvironment.

Collectively, the consistent and significant cytotoxicity observed across complementary models—patient-derived organoids, primary patient cells, conventional cell lines, and a xenograft system—supports the conclusion that iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells exert specific and robust antitumor activity against MUC1-expressing ESCC.

The choice of CAR-NK over CAR-T cells is supported by several considerations. Although CAR-T cells have achieved remarkable success in hematologic malignancies, their application in solid tumors such as ESCC remains constrained by risks of cytokine release syndrome (CRS), immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), lengthy autologous manufacturing, and limited persistence in immunosuppressive microenvironments [23, 24]. CAR-NK cells offer potential advantages: a favorable safety profile with lower CRS risk, intrinsic ability to kill via both CAR-dependent and innate receptor-mediated mechanisms, and suitability for "off-the-shelf" allogeneic use [25, 26]. Generating CAR-NK cells from iPSCs further circumvents donor variability and limited expansion capacity associated with primary NK cells [27, 28]. Our differentiation protocol consistently yielded a highly pure CD3−CD56+ NK cell population with stable, high-level MUC1-CAR expression across all developmental stages, supporting the feasibility of a standardized, scalable product.

Despite the promising preclinical efficacy data presented here, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the in vivo efficacy was evaluated in a subcutaneous xenograft model lacking a fully reconstituted human immune system, which may not fully recapitulate the tumor microenvironment or potential immune-mediated regulatory interactions. Second, although we characterized MUC1 expression in normal tissues, long-term safety and the potential for on-target/off-tumor toxicity against low MUC1 expressing normal epithelia (e.g., pancreatic or pulmonary) remain to be systematically addressed. Third, our serial killing data were generated under optimized in vitro conditions in the absence of stromal or suppressive immune cells; thus, the actual persistence and killing capacity within the tumor microenvironment may differ. Fourth, CAR-NK cells derived from iPSCs are subject to inherent batch-to-batch variability in differentiation efficiency, which could affect reproducibility across manufacturing runs. Future work should incorporate orthotopic models, prolonged observation periods, and combinatorial strategies (e.g., checkpoint blockade or IL‑15 support) to enhance sustained antitumor activity.

Looking forward, rigorous preclinical evaluation should include orthotopic ESCC models, extended observation windows for persistence and late toxicity, and combinatorial approaches (e.g., IL-15 support to enhance survival, or checkpoint inhibition to counteract exhaustion). Ultimately, whether the PSO/primary cell model system described here more accurately predicts clinical responses than traditional cell line or xenograft models remains to be validated in prospective clinical trials. Nevertheless, the consistent and specific cytotoxicity of iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells against MUC1-expressing ESCC across three complementary human-derived preclinical models provides a rationale for further development and supports early-phase clinical investigation in this difficult-to-treat malignancy.

Conclusion

Collectively, iPSC-derived MUC1-targeted CAR-NK cells exhibit specific and robust cytotoxicity against MUC1-expressing ESCC in multiple human-derived preclinical models, warranting further optimization and clinical translation in this difficult-to-treat malignancy.
