De novo transcriptome assembly and gene expression analysis of Cnidium officinale under high-temperature conditions
Authors: Sohee Shin, Eonyong Han, Hyeju Seong, Yong Il Kim, Inuk Jung, Woosuk Jung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe medicinal plant Cnidium officinale (CO) is widespread in Northeast Asia and vulnerable to heat stress. The naturally occurring composition of pharmacological ingredients of CO results in overall physiological consequences; therefore, it is crucial to have a comprehensive understanding of metabolic response to ambient heat in terms of acclimation to estimate how much CO is exposed to threatening environmental conditions.ResultsTranscriptome analysis is critical for understanding the consequences of long-term physiological adaptation of CO to abiotic stress. However, transcriptome analysis on this species, particularly under prolonged stress conditions, has remained limited. We employed a temperature gradient tunnel (TGT) to subject CO to high-temperature exposure for four months, enabling us to observe the cumulative effects of heat and assess its acclimation mechanisms.In the absence of genome sequencing data, we performed de novo transcriptome assembly and compared DEGs from temperature treatment plots of a TGT and a growth chamber (GC).Since interpreting transcriptomic data can be complex, we employed a sequential analytical approach, including DEG clustering, GO enrichment, KEGG pathway mapping, miRNA-target gene analysis, and multiple rounds of RNA sequencing validation. DEGs were classified into two categories: genes exhibiting significant fold changes and genes showing significant count changes rather than fold changes. Then, we analyzed the functional roles of DEGs to determine which pathways respond to ambient and stressful high temperatures and validated the findings through cross-comparison with GC. Additionally, we conducted miRNA analysis to investigate post-transcriptional regulation under high temperatures. CO grown under higher ambient temperatures exhibited slight upregulation of pathways related to protein stability and turnover, ABA biosynthesis, and energy production, such as photosynthesis and oxidative phosphorylation. However, under extreme heat stress, most metabolic pathways were downregulated except for those involved in transcription, translation, oxidative phosphorylation and the biosynthesis of cutin, suberin, and wax.ConclusionThis study demonstrated that proper clustering of genes based on expression levels and fold changes in two different experimental conditions, along with pathway mapping, may provide a comprehensive understanding of CO’s response to heat stress. These insights could contribute to future research on heat tolerance and crop improvement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12051-5.

Background

Cnidium officinale (CO) is an herbal plant found widely throughout China, Korea, and Japan. The root and stem of CO possess mild, aromatic properties and high medicinal value [1]. Traditionally, CO has been widely used in food processing as a nutraceutical. Its primary chemical constituents include essential oil, alkaloids, phenolic acids, and phthalide lactones, which exhibit vasodilatory, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antiproliferative activities both in vivo and in vitro [1]. In addition, active compounds such as polysaccharides, amino acids, alkaloids, and various secondary metabolites of CO have also been reported [2]. Several compounds, including senkyunolide A, (Z)-ligustilide, and the polyacetylene falcarindiol, have demonstrated significant biological activities without cytotoxicity at low concentrations (e.g., 10µM). The medicinal effects of CO are attributed to the coordinated natural composition of its pharmacological ingredients.

CO usually thrives in cool temperatures; however, it is susceptible to environmental stressors such as high temperatures [3]. Physiological changes resulting from heat stress occur when temperatures exceed 28℃ for more than three days [4]. If the temperature rises above 30℃, CO experiences severe growth inhibition [5, 6], summer depression, and eventual wilting [7]. Additionally, exposure to temperatures above 35℃ for more than an hour causes the leaf tips to dry and turn brown, starting from the edges. In severe cases, prolonged heat stress can result in the entire plant wilting and potentially dying [8].

In addition, previous studies have shown that the levels of major bioactive compounds in CO, such as ligustilide and butylidenephthalide, increase at temperatures 2–4 °C above outdoor temperatures. However, their concentrations tend to decline when temperatures increase by 4–6 °C above ambient [9].

Therefore, it is crucial to observe and understand the long-term physiological responses of CO to evaluate how it encounters and responds to changing environmental conditions.

To achieve this, we employed a temperature gradient tunnel (TGT) for a long-term experiment allowing us to observe the cumulative effects of heat stress and assess the plant’s acclimation status. Previous studies have primarily investigated heat stress using extreme temperature treatments for short durations. However, our study subjected CO to prolonged heat stress over four months to simulate the mild heat stress conditions that are more likely to occur due to global warming. Additionally, unlike previous studies that utilized constant temperature treatments, we applied variable temperature conditions with day-night fluctuations to more closely resemble natural field conditions. We also conducted a short-term growth chamber (GC) experiment to evaluate CO’s response to heat shock and stress under mild ambient temperatures and elevated heat conditions. These additional experiments provided supporting data to better understand the impact of temperature stress on CO. Furthermore, we aimed to identify sets of genes that could serve as diagnostic markers for assessing the environmental stress response of plants.

As CO genome sequencing has not yet been completed, gene expression and transcriptome analysis remain challenging and time-consuming. De novo assembly has attracted increasing attention as a rapid, cost-effective method for short-read sequencing in non-model organisms [10]. Specifically, transcriptome assembly offers a cost-effective approach to investigate the expression of protein-coding transcripts and microRNAs (miRNAs).

In this study, we aimed to understand CO’s response to ambient temperature changes at the transcriptome level. mRNA and small RNA samples were collected and sequenced at temperature gradients of 1℃, 2–4℃, and 4–6℃ above the normal temperature using the TGT, designed to simulate annual temperature variation. Although these changes may seem modest, their accumulation over the six-month growing period of CO corresponds to an increase in growing degree days (GDD) of approximately 180 to 1080 °C. These varying temperatures reflect a time-course-like phenological series, inducing diverse metabolic changes that lead to accelerated growth and aging processes, earlier flowering, and ultimately a reduction in yield [11, 12]. Additionally, we conducted indoor model studies using CO grown in a temperature-controlled GC to validate the mRNA sequencing results obtained from the TGT experiments. We analyzed the transcriptomes of three replicate CO samples from the indoor model studies and performed quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR). Both mRNA and small RNA-seq samples were used to assemble transcripts and identify protein-coding genes and miRNAs. Finally, we investigated the target genes of miRNAs and analyzed the modulation of gene expression across the three ambient temperature conditions. We hope that the results of this study may provide valuable insights into the changes in bioactive natural compounds and contribute to future research in investigating the acclimatization and adaptive genetic mechanisms of temperature-sensitive herbal plants. In addition, the identified DEGs and miRNAs may serve as candidate markers for the early detection of temperature stress in field-grown CO and contribute to the development of heat-tolerant cultivars through molecular breeding approaches. Moreover, insights into changes in bioactive natural compounds may inform cultivation strategies aimed at maintaining the quality of CO under elevated temperature conditions.

Methods

Plant materials for de novo transcriptome assembly and quantitative RT-PCR

We cultivated CO plants in a TGT (3 m width, 3 m height, and 27 m length) at the National Institute of Horticultural and Herbal Science, Pyeongchang, Republic of Korea, to investigate its physiological responses under varying ambient temperatures [13]. The TGT was regulated by a greenhouse environment control system (TGC-Soldan, Soldan Co., Seoul, Korea). The entrance of the tunnel was open to the outside, with a ventilator installed at the far end and heaters placed on the floor to emit warm air. Temperature measurements were collected using one sensor at the entrance, and three additional sensors (1400 − 101, LI-COR Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA) placed at regular intervals inside the tunnel; all sensors recorded hourly temperature measurements. The heaters and ventilator maintained temperatures at the entrance similar to the ambient temperature, while progressively higher temperatures were maintained toward the far end, thereby creating a stable temperature gradient. The C1 section was 0–1℃ higher than the outside air, C2 was 2–4℃ higher, and C3 was 4–6℃ higher. We maintained the temperature gradient from April to October, allowing CO to grow under consistently elevated temperatures. The monthly average temperatures for the C1, C2, and C3 sections within the TGT are summarized in Fig. 1C. Samples were harvested on August 10, when the average temperature for the month up to that point was 25.4℃, and the average maximum temperature was 31.85℃. mRNA and small RNA samples were collected from CO leaves at each temperature section. Immediately after harvesting, the leaves were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 70℃ until RNA extraction. Leaves from approximately 10–15 plants were collected from each experimental plot and pooled for subsequent analysis. Total RNA was extracted from homogenized pooled samples using the Qiagen RNA Extraction Kit (Qiagen, USA). As shown in Fig. 1, plants near the entrance grew well compared to those at the far end of the TGT, implying that their growth was influenced by high-temperature conditions.

To verify the CO RNA-seq results obtained from the outdoor TGT experiment, we conducted additional indoor model experiments, including RNA-seq and quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) for 15 representative genes (Table 1). For the indoor study, CO samples were obtained from a GC designed to simulate field-grown conditions and temperature stress. The rhizome of CO obtained from the National Institute of Horticultural Science was used as the experimental material. We initiated high-temperature treatment after approximately one month of growth under controlled conditions (25℃, 16/8-hour light/dark photoperiod). The temperature was initially set at 25℃ and increased gradually by 1℃ every two days until it reached 30℃. In the growth chamber, the temperature was automatically controlled and monitored using a programmable environmental control system (DS-310FL, Dasol Scientific, Korea). Leaves were harvested at each temperature interval (25–30℃), immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 70℃ until RNA extraction. Three replicate samples were collected for each treatment. Leaves from individual plants were collected and pooled for mRNA extraction. RT-qPCR was then performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Bioneer, Korea).

De novo transcriptome assembly

CO samples grown in the TGT and GC environments were used for de novo transcriptome assembly. Initially, raw FASTQ files were subjected to quality control and trimming to remove adapter sequences. Paired-end reads for each plant were merged into a single FASTQ file, and assembly was conducted using Trinity (version 2.9.1) [14]. For the assembly, we specified the input read type as FASTQ while disabling in silico normalization and setting a minimum contig length of 200 bp. Default parameters were maintained for all other Trinity settings. Following the assembly, the completeness of the transcriptome was assessed using Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO v5.8.2) [15]. The analysis was conducted by searching against the Viridiplantae database to evaluate the presence of conserved single-copy orthologs. The BUSCO assessment provided quantitative measures of transcriptome completeness, identifying complete, fragmented, and missing genes from the expected plant gene set.

Subsequently, scaffolding of the Trinity-assembled contigs was performed using CAP3 [16]. CAP3 was executed with parameters optimized for overlap detection and contig merging: a clipping cutoff (-c) of 12, a clipping range (-distance) of 100 bases, a quality cutoff for differences (-b) of 20, and a maximum difference score (-d) of 200. The alignment scoring was configured with a match score factor (-m) of 2, a mismatch score factor (-n) of −5, and a gap penalty factor (-g) of 6. Default parameters were maintained for all other settings. Potential protein-coding transcripts were identified using Coding Potential Calculator 2 (CPC2) [17], which assessed the protein-coding potential of the CAP3-processed transcripts.

For annotation, the putative coding contigs were subjected to a BLAST search (Version 2.10.0) [18] against the Non-Redundant (NR) database [19] using default blastn options and an E-value threshold of 10−5 to define significance. Contigs with at least one significant BLAST hit were designated as unigenes and used for downstream analysis. To validate the annotations, additional databases such as Swiss-Prot [20] and Ensembl Plants [21] were also consulted.

Gene expression analysis

High-quality short reads from each sample were aligned to the assembled unigenes using the STAR aligner [22] and quantified using HTSeq [9] to examine the expression profile of unigenes in CO. The resulting raw read counts were log 2-transformed to correct for skewness and then subjected to quantile normalization to reduce sample variability. The TGT experiment provided samples representing the three temperature conditions (C1, C2, and C3). Each sample was generated by pooling RNA from 10 to 15 plants at each designated temperature position. This pooling strategy effectively averages the biological variability and yields a robust, representative transcriptomic profile for each condition. Consequently, even without multiple replicates, determining differentially expressed genes (DEGs) based on fold changes relative to library size remains a valid and informative approach. In fact, RNA sample pooling has been shown to reduce technical variability and generate a reliable average expression estimate, which is particularly advantageous when resources or sample availability are limited [23, 24].

To validate the mRNA sequencing results obtained from the TGT experiments, we conducted an additional analysis using an indoor model study in a temperature-controlled growth chamber (GC). In this experiment, three biological replicates were collected for each temperature condition (25 °C, 27 °C, and 29 °C). The GC samples were also aligned to the assembled unigenes and quantified using the same STAR aligner and HTSeq pipeline.

Differential expression analysis of the GC dataset was performed using the edgeR package [25] from Bioconductor. In this analysis, temperature conditions were treated as factors, low- expressed genes were filtered out using the filterByExpr function, and library sizes were normalized with calcNormFactors. Counts per million (CPM) were calculated to assess expression levels across samples, further validating the normalization process. Dispersion estimates were then computed using estimateDisp, and differential expression was tested with exactTest. Significant DEGs were identified using an FDR-adjusted p-value threshold of 0.05.

After the DEG analysis, time-series analysis was performed to capture dynamic gene expression patterns, as the C1, C2, and C3 conditions also represent sequential time points reflecting changes in ambient temperature and growth location. The Short Time-series Expression Miner (STEM) [26, 27] was used to cluster genes with similar temporal profiles after concatenating the expression data across the three conditions into a single time-course dataset. STEM was run with default parameters. For biological interpretation, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [28] pathway mapping was performed on the TGT data. KEGG Orthologies (KOs) exhibiting significant upregulation or downregulation (based on a log fold change (absolute logFC ≥ 1 threshold) were identified. KOs that were present in both upregulated and downregulated sets were excluded, ensuring that only uniquely expressed KOs were mapped to their respective metabolic pathways. Finally, quantitative RT-PCR was conducted on a subset of 15 representative genes from the GC experiment to validate the RNA-seq results, and a correlation analysis was performed to assess the consistency between RT-PCR measurements and RNA-seq data.

Homology analysis and primer design for quantitative RT-PCR

Homology analysis was performed using the BLASTP tool from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. Genes were identified based on the most significant E-value in the BLAST analysis results, with a cutoff E-value of 10−5. In cases where multiple genes shared the most significant E-value, the gene with the highest Bit score was selected (Table 1). Gene-specific primers were designed using the NCBI Primer-BLAST program and the NCBI BLASTN tool. The targeted PCR amplification products ranged from 100 to 200 bp, with melting temperatures (Tm) between 59 °C and 62 °C and GC contents between 40% and 60%. Primer nucleotide sequences were aligned with the CO transcriptome database obtained through RNA sequencing of CO leaves (knu.ac.kr: cobi.knu.ac.kr/projects/herbalplant/) using NCBI BLASTN. Specific primers were selected based on ≥ 6 sequence differences from non-target genes or ≥ 2 mismatches within five base pairs near the 3’ end (Supplementary Table S1). Actin was used as the reference gene for gene expression normalization. Previous studies identified actin as the most stable gene in Daucus carota leaves, a species that shares high genetic similarity with CO [29]. Similarly, actin isoform exhibiting the most stable expression in CO was selected as the reference gene.

Transcription factor analysis

To elucidate the regulatory mechanisms underlying gene expression in CO under varying temperatures, the PlantTFcat tool [30] was used for transcription factor (TF) analysis. PlantTFcat systematically identifies and categorizes plant TFs, transcriptional regulators, and chromatin regulators based on InterProScan-derived domain signatures and prediction logic, assigning open reading frames (ORFs) to specific TF families. High-quality short reads from samples C1, C2, and C3 were used to predict ORFs, and the resulting peptide sequences were submitted to PlantTFcat using default parameters. The tool generated a detailed categorization of TF families, which was then used to assess the contribution of various regulatory factors to the temperature-induced transcriptional response in CO.

miRNA analysis

A systematic approach was applied using both contig and small RNA-seq data to identify gene-regulatory miRNAs. First, small RNA-seq samples were preprocessed using TrimGalore [31] to remove adapter sequences. miRDeep2 [32] was then used to identify mature miRNAs, their hairpin precursors, and star sequences. The expression levels of miRNAs were quantified by collapsing all miRNA sequences into a single unique set and measuring the expression of each miRNA across different samples. These expression counts were then log2-transformed, yielding normalized miRNA expression values for each CO condition [33].

The mature miRNA sequences were compared with known plant miRNA sequences in miRBase [34]. miRNAs were then annotated as either conserved orthologs or novel: those with significant matches to miRBase sequences were labeled as conserved, while those with no significant matches were considered novel. Additionally, miRNAs that matched sequences from only a few plant species were categorized as orthologs. miRNA-target genes were identified using psRNA Target [35], focusing on those targeting protein-coding unigenes. Potential gene-regulatory effects were assessed by examining significant negative correlations between miRNAs and their target genes. miRNAs and target genes exhibiting such correlations were selected for further functional analysis.

Results

Transcriptome assembly results

De novo assembly is essential for identifying genes and conducting downstream gene expression analyses in species lacking fully annotated reference genomes [36]. We used high-throughput sequencing data of CO to assemble high-quality overlapping contigs and then quantified the gene expression per sample. This approach enabled the detection of gene expression variations influenced by different temperature conditions and the identification of biological mechanisms significantly impacted by these variations. The workflow for obtaining the final set of potential protein-coding unigenes used in our analysis is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Transcriptome assembly was performed using Trinity, with a total of 234,243 contigs assembled for CO. The completeness of the assembly was evaluated using BUSCO v5.8.2, comparing the results to the Viridiplantae database. The assessment revealed a high-quality assembly with 97.4% of the benchmarking universal single-copy orthologs (BUSCOs) identified as complete (414/425 total BUSCO groups). Of these, 19.8% (84) were classified as single-copy and 77.6% (330) as duplicated BUSCOs. Only 1.6% (7) of BUSCOs were fragmented, and 0.9% (4) were missing, indicating a nearly complete representation of expected gene content in the Cnidium transcriptome. After scaffolding with CAP3 [16], the number of contigs was reduced to 24,684, of which 9,130 had high protein-coding potential. Ultimately, 7,292 contigs had at least one significant hit to a gene in the plant-based NR database and were used as the final contigs or unigenes used in the analysis. The N50 of the unigenes was 1,630 bp, and the GC content was 38.69%.

Functional annotation of unigenes

For functional annotation of the assembled contigs, we queried the plant-based NR protein database from NCBI to identify overlapping groups with significant similarity. A total of 7,292 unigenes were successfully annotated. The BLAST analysis revealed that 6,522 (91.0%) CO genes showed the highest similarity to proteins from Daucus carota subsp. sativus, followed by Apium graveolens 202 genes (2.8%) and Camellia sinensis 35 genes (0.5%). To further validate this, we performed a second, more targeted search against a curated database of reference proteins, Swiss-Prot database containing key plant species, which again showed D. carota as the top hit for 93.76% of the unigenes. As a final confirmation, a separate BLAST search against the Ensembl Plants Daucus carota reference proteome showed that 94.75% of our unigenes found a significant homolog (E-value < 1e-5), confirming the high degree of sequence conservation. Supplementary Data S2 provides the DNA and protein sequences of the unigenes. Additionally, Supplementary Table S3 presents the BLAST results.

To further validate the genetic similarity between CO and Daucus carota, we performed additional BLAST searches using other databases, including NCBI and Phytozome. We selected 17 genes involved in basic metabolism and the biosynthesis of bioactive compounds for this analysis. The results demonstrated that most of these genes showed the highest sequence homology with members of the Apiaceae family, particularly Daucus carota. Specifically, 15 genes displayed the closest matches with Daucus carota in the Phytozome database, and 13 genes showed the highest similarity with members of the Apiaceae family (including Daucus carota and Heracleum sosnowskyi) in the NCBI database. These results are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Gene expression analysis

We utilized edgeR [25, 37] to identify DEGs across different temperature environments of the TGT. DEGs were identified through all pairwise comparisons between temperature conditions. The expression levels of genes from the C1, C2, and C3 samples were log2 quantile-normalized and sorted by log fold change (logFC). Genes with logFC > 2 were classified as upregulated, while those with logFC < −2 were considered downregulated. The number of upregulated genes in CO at each temperature pair was as follows: 329 from C1 to C2, 745 from C2 to C3, and 1,039 from C1 to C3. Conversely, the number of downregulated genes was 400, 964, and 1,288 for the C1 to C2, C2 to C3, and C1 to C3 comparisons, respectively. The counts of upregulated and downregulated genes for each temperature comparison in CO are shown in Table 2.

Physiological interpretation based on gene expression analyses

We performed four analytical approaches for physiological interpretation: DEG clustering, GO enrichment analysis, mapping representative DEGs onto the KEGG pathway, and analyzing miRNAs and their target gene expression. These analyses provided a comprehensive understanding of plant responses to heat stress, from mild ambient heat to more stressful high-temperature conditions, with temperature increases of + 4–6℃.

The C1, C2, and C3 samples represent CO grown under elevated air temperatures of approximately + 1℃, + 3℃, and + 5℃, respectively, with a variance of ± 1℃. To identify transcriptional changes associated with increasing heat stress, DEG analysis was conducted through pairwise comparisons between temperature conditions. The comparisons, denoted as C1 to C2, C1 to C3, and C2 to C3, represent defined increments in ambient temperature used to evaluate gene expression changes in response to heat stress.

DEGs were classified into two groups. The first group of DEGs showed significant fold changes, indicating their active involvement in metabolic pathways. Many of these genes were either newly expressed or disappeared genes under high temperature conditions. These genes, despite weak expression, may reflect the initial response or the activation of new metabolic pathways under heat stress. Conversely, disappearing genes might imply the re-orientation of metabolic pathways and the induction of alternative pathways and metabolite production. However, if temperature exceeds a critical threshold, plant responses may shift toward urgent responses to protect against overwhelming stress. The second group of DEGs demonstrated significant counts but mild fold changes. These genes exhibit mild or weak changes during elevated temperatures but remain dominant and probably physiologically conservative. This suggests that these genes may be involved in homeostatic or active acclimation processes.

In addition to newly expressed genes, several representative genes with fold change > 2 and significant counts were identified and shown in Table 4. These genes are associated with key cellular processes, including detoxification, protein protection, energy metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, cell death, and signal transduction, suggesting their crucial roles in high-temperature stress adaptation. Genes involved in detoxification, such as cytochrome P450 (CYP72A219-like and CYP71A1-like), peptide methionine sulfoxide reductase A3 (PMSR), glutathione S-transferase U17 (GST), and peroxidase 16-like protein, were significantly upregulated. Peroxidase plays a crucial role in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis by contributing to lignin synthesis and strengthening the cell wall, thus acting as a defense mechanism against pathogens and environmental stress. GST detoxifies ROS and other harmful compounds, thereby strengthening the antioxidant defense system within the cell. Under environmental stress conditions, GST activation is expected to protect cells from oxidative stress and maintain cellular homeostasis. Additionally, it is involved in reactive oxygen species (ROS) detoxification and stress response regulation. These genes likely play a crucial role in mitigating oxidative stress by neutralizing reactive oxygen species and repairing oxidatively damaged proteins, thereby enhancing cellular stability under thermal stress. For protein homeostasis, the upregulation of heat shock protein 70 kDa protein 8 (Hsp70-8), autophagy-related protein 9 (ATG9), and sHSP suggests their critical roles in protein turnover. Genes related to energy metabolism, including NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase and purine permease 1-like (PUP), were also highly expressed, indicating a potential increase in ATP production and metabolic activity to sustain cellular function under high-temperature conditions. Validation of TGT RNA-seq data via comparative analysis with GC RNA-seq

To compare the results from the TGT and GC experiments, we conducted a clustering analysis of RNA sequencing data obtained from the GC experiment. Genes were categorized by logFC values, with logFC > 2 representing upregulated genes, logFC < −2 representing downregulated genes, and the cluster between − 2 and 2 representing no significant changes in expression. We then compared the DEGs of 25℃ to 27℃, 27℃ to 29℃, and 25℃ to 29℃ conditions from the GC experiment with the DEGs from the C1 to C2, C2 to C3, and C1 to C3 comparisons of the TGT experiment. The TGT experiment was conducted outdoors, whereas the GC experiment was carried out under controlled conditions. As a result, differences in gene expression could be attributed to factors such as the diurnal cycle, fluctuations in light intensity, variations in temperature during the day and night, and mechanical and biological stresses from microorganisms, insects, and wind. Despite these potential sources of variance, we speculated that both transcriptome datasets would contain sufficient information for comparison. Therefore, we focused on identifying genes that exhibited similar expression patterns rather than on fold changes or expression intensities. In the GC experiment, we observed an apparent color change on CO leaves at 28℃, indicating heat stress. We also observed this phenomenon at C3 in the TGT experiment. The average day temperature during sample collection from the TGT experiment was 25℃, with a daily temperature range of 5℃. While the highest recorded temperature reached 32℃, no significant signs of heat stress were observed in the C1 and C2 plots.

A comparison of transcriptome data from the TGT and GC experiments revealed several upregulated and downregulated genes in both experiments (Supplementary Table S7). For example, genes related to plastid movements, such as PMI1 and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, were upregulated in the C1 to C2 of the TGT experiment and 25℃ to 27℃ treatment plots of the GC experiment. When we expanded the temperature range to C2 to C3 and compared it with 25℃ to 27℃ treatment plot, many genes were upregulated, such as COX2, cyclin-D6 (CYCD6), ATP synthase F1 subunit alpha (ATP5F1A), RNA-dependent RNA polymerase gene, and pre-mRNA-splicing factor cwc23 (CWC23).

Additionally, GO and pathway analysis of the RNA sequencing data from the GC experiment indicated that the observed gene expression changes may reflect alterations in the transcription machinery due to heat stress. A number of genes showed decreased expression, including PIF3, genes encoding ATPase 1, chromatin structure-remodeling complex proteins, glutathione reductase (GR), CDPK-related kinase 7 (CRK7), ABC transporter G family member 22 (ABCG22), and linoleate 13 S-lipoxygenase 2 − 1 (LOX2).

Gene ontology

In this study, we conducted GO enrichment analysis to systematically identify genes and pathways affected by higher temperatures, revealing critical biological processes involved in the heat stress response (Supplementary Table S8). When the temperature increased from C1 to C2, genes related to RNA binding were significantly upregulated. On the other hand, genes that were downregulated were linked to ATP binding, nucleotide-related terms (adenyl nucleotide binding, purine nucleotide binding), cell movement and shape maintenance related terms (cytoskeletal protein binding, tubulin binding, and microtubule binding) under heat stress. As the temperature increased from C2 to C3, upregulated genes were associated with nucleoside phosphate biosynthesis, organophosphate biosynthesis, and intracellular organelle maintenance. Conversely, downregulated genes were involved in a wide range of biological processes, including various binding activities (nucleotide, ATP, protein, ion), regulation of gene expression (gene silencing, negative regulation of gene expression, RNA processing, RNA exonuclease activity, helicase activity), ATP hydrolysis, cellular processes (regulation of cell shape, localization within the cell), and transport functions (P-type calcium transport, ATPase-coupled transmembrane transporter activity).

A. Increased Initiation from C1 to C2

As the system transitioned from C1 to C2, pathways involved in glycerophospholipid metabolism, spliceosome activity, chlorophyll metabolism, and protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum were activated. These changes are associated with modifications in membrane composition, metabolism of chlorophyllide A, phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidyl ethanolamine turnover, regulation of gene expression, and protein degradation in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). These metabolic adjustments suggest that the system was regulated in a way that enhances structural stability and improves adaptation to stress.

B. Increased Acclimation from C1 to C2.

In the context of C1 to C2 acclimation, we identified numerous genes involved in protein processing in the ER, ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation, glutathione degradation, and components of the Skp, Cullin, F-box containing (SCF) complex related to circadian rhythm, all contributing to protein degradation. Additionally, genes associated with lipid synthesis, particularly unsaturated fatty acid synthesis and caffeoyl-coenzyme A (CoA) synthesis (a key step in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway), pathways related to phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, cysteine and methionine metabolism, glutathione metabolism, autophagy, and the methionine salvage pathway were activated.

C. Decreased Initiation from C1 to C2.

Genes involved in tetrahydrofolic acid-mediated amino acid synthesis, such as enzymes converting glycine to serine and PIF3, were either absent or nearly absent in C1 compared to C2. We also observed the downregulation of genes involved in plant hormone signal transduction (auxin, cytokinin (CK), ABA, and brassinosteroid (BR)), C1 metabolism, intracellular transport, and circadian rhythm control. Furthermore, genes related to the synthesis of cofactors (SAM), amino acids (cysteine, methionine, tryptophan, tyrosine, and phenylalanine), and long-chain fatty acids (chain length > 16) were also significantly downregulated.

D. Decreased Acclimation from C1 to C2.

During the transition from C1 to C2, genes involved in glucose, trehalose, and phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) synthesis, methionine and cysteine metabolism, fatty acid elongation, cytoskeletal regulation, DNA sensing, MAPK signaling, and plant hormone signal transduction pathways were downregulated. Among the secondary metabolic pathways, the coumarin synthesis pathway was identified as a significantly responsive pathway under high-temperature stress.

E. Increased Initiation from C2 to C3.

The metabolic pathways associated with pre-mRNA 3’-end processing, heme and coenzyme synthesis, porphyrin metabolism, and mRNA surveillance pathways were newly upregulated from C2 to C3.

F. Increased Acclimation from C2 to C3.

During the transition from C2 to C3, defensive pathways, such as oxidative phosphorylation, ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, glycerophospholipid metabolism, steroid biosynthesis, and secondary metabolic pathways, were likely activated as part of the heat stress response. The upregulation of spliceosome formation and pre-mRNA processing may be linked to alternative splicing events. Additionally, the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway, as well as the GA and salicylic acid (SA) hormone signal transduction pathways, might be involved in thermomorphogenesis or the activation of defense mechanisms. The synthesis of cofactors, including biotin, coenzyme A, and riboflavin, may indicate elevated metabolic activity.

G. Decreased Acclimation from C2 to C3.

Under high-temperature stress conditions, genes associated with energy metabolism, such as glycolysis, the TCA cycle, glyoxylate cycle, photorespiration, and dicarboxylate metabolism, and gluconeogenesis, were downregulated. Additionally, genes involved in fatty acid chain elongation, amino acid biosynthesis, starch and sucrose metabolism, the glycine cleavage system, hormone signal transduction, and AMPK signaling were also suppressed. Notably, many breakdown processes were also downregulated, such as the lipid, starch, and RNA degradation pathways.

Quantitative RT-PCR analysis

CO experiences high-temperature damage when the daytime temperature exceeds 28℃ for three or more consecutive days. In line with this observation, our study also noted the onset of chlorosis when temperatures surpassed 28℃ (Supplementary Figure S10). Since visible signs of high-temperature damage become evident at 28℃, it is plausible that molecular responses to high-temperature stress may be triggered even before the temperature reaches this threshold. Under heat stress, various pathways such as hormone signaling, membrane modification, ROS regulation, Ca2+ signaling, MAPK cascade, heat shock factor (HSF) – HSP pathway, cell cycle regulation, and epigenetic mechanisms collectively contribute to the stress response. We analyzed the expression patterns of key genes involved in the heat stress response via qRT-PCR. However, challenges in primer design arose due to the absence of Cnidium officinale in the Primer-BLAST database. Consequently, we prioritized genes with fewer copies in the genome but that play significant roles in heat stress response pathways for qPCR analysis (Table 1).

The RT-qPCR results were categorized into four groups based on their expression patterns (Fig. 4). Group A genes exhibited an initial increase in expression from 25℃ to 27℃, followed by a decrease around 27℃, and a subsequent increase around 29℃. These genes, including WRKY, GI, and AGO, displayed a distinct expression profile, with an initial upregulation between 26℃ and 27℃, followed by a marked decrease at temperatures above 27℃. In contrast, Group B genes, such as small heat shock protein (sHSP), cyclin-dependent kinase A (CDKA), C2-domain ABA-related (CAR), GST, and elongated hypocotyl 5 (HY5), demonstrated sustained upregulation above the 26℃ threshold, indicating a continuous response to elevated temperatures. Group C genes, represented by 1-acyl-sn-glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase (AGPAT), phospholipid hydroperoxide glutathione peroxidase (PHGPx), mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase (MAPKKK), and SAUR, showed a more pronounced stress response at higher temperatures, suggesting heightened sensitivity to heat stress compared to milder fluctuations. Finally, Group D genes, including HSP70, DELLA, and calmodulin-like (CML), exhibited sensitivity to minor temperature variations,, with altered expression levels in response to subtle changes.

To validate the RNA sequencing results from the TGT experiment, we compared the expression patterns of 15 genes analyzed by qPCR between the TGT and GC RNA-seq data. The selected 15 genes showed similar patterns in expression in both the TGT and GC experiments. In both C1 to C2 in the TGT and 25℃ to 27℃ plots in the GC experiment, all 15 genes displayed identical tendencies, while minor differences existed between C2 to C3 and 25℃ to 27℃ plots. Transcription factor analysis

TFs regulate the biological characteristics in response to spatial, temporal, and environmental stimuli. These processes are controlled at the transcriptional level [38]. In this study, we aimed to identify TFs and the most abundant TF families. Figure 6 shows the number of unigenes related to these factors in CO. Among the identified genes, we observed significant differential expression levels across 80 transcription factor families. The most critical DEGs, represented by specific TF families, act as transcriptional regulators of gene expression during cell differentiation and development. A detailed list of the identified TFs is provided in Supplementary Table S12.

We distinguished TFs based on their increased or decreased expression under high-temperature conditions. The top 10 TFs, categorized by contig count, were compared between TGT and GC. From C1 to C2, eight out of 13 TFs, including C2H2, WD40-like, CCHC (Zn), C3H, and MYB-HB-like, showed increased expression, while eight out of 10 TFs, such as bHLH, AP2-EREBP, WD40-like, CCHC (Zn), and plant homeodomain (PHD), exhibited decreased expression. These patterns were also observed in GC from 25℃ to 27℃ (Supplementary Table S12). Similarly, from C2 to C3, eight out of 11 TFs, including C2H2, WD40-like, CCHC (Zn), bHLH, and AP2-EREBP, showed increased expression, while seven out of 12 TFs, such as WD40-like, C2H2, CCHC (Zn), PHD, and MYB-HB-like, exhibited decreased expression. These trends were consistently observed in GC from 27℃ to 29℃ (Fig. 6).

C2H2, CCHC (Zn), and C3H are zinc finger proteins, that constitute one of the most prominent TF families. They are characterized by their finger-like structure and ability to bind Zn2+ ions [39]. C2H2-type zinc finger proteins regulate abiotic stress responses in plants [40]. WD40 proteins, primarily localized in the cytoplasm, are crucial regulators of numerous eukaryotic cellular processes [41]. They play critical roles in histone modification, phytohormone regulation, DNA damage repair, and signal transduction.

AP2-EREBP TFs, which are unique to plants, regulate vital developmental processes, such as floral and leaf identity determination, while also aiding in plant responses to biotic and environmental stress throughout their life cycle [42]. The PHD family is crucial in plants, playing significant roles in various biological processes. Numerous studies have demonstrated that PHD TF family is pivotal in regulating plant growth and development, defense against pathogens, and responses to diverse stresses [43]. The bHLH transcription factors, initially identified for its promotion of thermoresponsive growth, serves as a central regulatory node in thermomorphogenesis [44].

WRKY TFs represent a plant-specific group of transcriptional regulators that play crucial roles in plant responses to high-temperature stress [45]. They either facilitate or suppress the expression of heat stress response genes by binding to specific DNA sequences activated under stress conditions. Furthermore, WRKY TFs function as integral components of the heat stress signaling network, modulating other transcriptional regulators and heat stress response pathways. These processes are pivotal for aiding plant survival under higher temperatures. In summary, TFs modulate physiological responses in plants by regulating gene expression under high-temperature stress, thereby minimizing cellular damage and enhancing survival chances [46]. This study focuses on analyzing temperature-dependent changes in gene expression in TGT and GC, with particular emphasis on identifying TFs that exhibit increased or decreased expression under high-temperature conditions among the 80 identified TFs.

miRNA analysis

A total of 40 miRNAs were identified in CO, of which 21 had an average read count of > 50. These 40 miRNAs were classified into three categories based on the E-value resulting from a BLAST search using the miRNA sequences as queries. If no matches were found, the miRNAs were classified as novel; those with E-values < 0.001 were classified as conserved; the remaining were considered orthologs. Among the 21 miRNAs, seven were conserved in other plant species, eight were orthologs with significant E-values, and six were novel miRNAs identified in CO. miRNA sequences, including mature, start, and hairpin sequences, are provided in Supplementary Table S13, while Supplementary Table S14 lists the miRNA-target gene pairs.

A total of 357 miRNA–target gene pairs showed a significant negative correlation (i.e., Pearson’s r < − 0.8), and most miRNAs in CO exhibited decreased expression. KEGG pathway analysis of these target genes that these genes are involved in oxidative phosphorylation, antioxidant defense systems, hormone signal transduction (e.g. auxin and ABA signaling), starch and sucrose metabolism (particularly glucose degradation), and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis (Table 12). These pathways are known to play crucial roles in responses to high-temperature stress.

Oxidative phosphorylation is critical for managing oxidative stress induced by elevated temperatures through the regulation of ROS in mitochondria [47]. Similarly, the antioxidant defense system mitigates oxidative damage by activating antioxidant enzymes [48]. Hormone signaling pathways, including those of ABA and auxin, are also crucial; ABA regulates stomatal closure, whereas auxin modulates growth and stress signaling [49]. The involvement of genes related to starch and sucrose metabolism suggests a shift in energy distribution to cope with stress conditions [50]. Moreover, ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis contributes to the removing damaged proteins and modulating stress-responsive proteins [51]. Collectively, these findings suggest that miRNA-regulated target genes in CO play a significant role in adaptive responses to high-temperature stress.

Several novel miRNAs identified in CO were found to target genes involved in important physiological processes. For example, co-miR-4 exhibited prominent expression, and the eight target genes of co-miR-4 showed a negative correlation with its expression. These target genes, including kinase, dehydrogenase, and amino acid transporter genes, are involved in lignin biosynthesis, regulation of stomatal development, and amino acid absorption [52–54] (Supplementary Table S14). The expression of co-miR-4 decreased with increasing temperature. Additionally, the expression of novel co-miR-9 was downregulated from C1 to C2 but upregulated from C2 to C3. Four target genes showed a significant negative correlation with co-miR-9, and these genes are involved in lysine biosynthesis and chloroplast development [55, 56]. Furthermore, co-miR-12 and co-miR-17 showed continuous downregulation under elevated temperatures. Co-miR-12 and co-miR-17 had 10 and 14 target genes, respectively.

The target genes of co-miR-12 include genes encoding sugar transporter SWEET6a (SWEET6a), a DNA repair protein, and an E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase, all of which may contribute to stress tolerance [57–59]. The target genes of co-miR-17 encode a nuclear pore complex protein, an elongator complex protein, ribonucleoproteins, cytochrome c oxidase, and a mitochondrial S-adenosylmethionine transporter (Supplementary Table S14). The structures of the mature and star sequences of these four miRNAs are shown in Fig. 7. Discussion

The TGT is designed to simulate a gradient of temperatures; however, interpreting the effects and results of experiments using the TGT is complex. The TGT may represent different temperature conditions at various locations, simulating varying ambient temperature conditions. Concurrently, it may reflect the immediate status of plant growth in terms of phenological stages, such as time-lapse effects. Since all CO plants in the TGT were planted on the same day, and allowed the same growth periods, the observed differences reflect the influence of elevated temperatures.

High-throughput transcriptome sequencing has become a common tool for sequencing non-model organisms, such as Gingko kernels [60], Rehmannia glutinosa [61], Gossypium hirsutum [62], Liriodendron chinense [63], and Drosophila [64]. Prior to this study, nucleotide sequences for CO were unavailable in public databases, limiting research into molecular mechanisms underlying secondary metabolite biosynthesis, physiological adaptations, and growth. Many studies have shown that high-throughput sequencing and de novo assembly are effective techniques for identifying novel genes in non-model plants [65, 66]. We utilized the Illumina HiSeq sequencing platform to sequence RNA from CO grown at different temperatures. Our data provide a valuable resource for studying gene regulation at different temperatures in CO, which applied to research in novel gene discovery, comparative genomics, and phylogenetics.

We identified 7,292 unigenes from CO, indicating that our assembly results are of high quality. The genes were mapped to known unique proteins from databases to predict their function. Unannotated single genes, which may possess poorly conserved regions, will require further investigation. We identified DEGs under three different temperature conditions. Quantitative analysis of DEGs in each TGT comparison revealed that many genes affected by high temperatures in CO were downregulated rather than upregulated. When gene expression is downregulated at the transcriptional level, a decrease in transcript abundance typically results in a reduced pool of gene products, limiting the capacity of downstream regulatory mechanisms to restore functional protein levels. It is unlikely that the corresponding protein function will be sustained, regardless of downstream regulation.

We performed four different analyses for the physiological interpretation of heat stress: DEG clustering, GO enrichment analysis, mapping representative DEGs to KEGG pathways, and analyzing miRNAs and their target gene expression These analyses provided a comprehensive understanding of heat stress responses, ranging from mild ambient temperatures to stressful high temperatures.

DEGs were classified into two categories: those exhibiting significant fold changes, including newly emerged or disappeared genes, and those showing significant count changes but mild fold changes. To gain deeper insights into their functional roles, we analyzed and summarized the metabolic pathways associated with each group (Tables 3 and 10).

Membrane fluidity-related lipid metabolism was one of the earliest responses to elevated temperature. For example, sterol biosynthesis, including cholesterol, campesterol, and stigmasterol, increased significantly during the C1 to C2 initiation stage. The fatty acid degradation pathway was another early response pathway, although overall fatty acid degradation slowed. Fatty acid elongation was not sensitive to temperature, maintaining homeostasis. However, the overall fatty acid biosynthesis pathway exhibited a strong shift from anabolic to catabolic modes, particularly during the C2 to C3 acclimation phase to stressful conditions. Lipids with large polar head groups, such as phosphatidylcholine (PC) and phosphatidyl ethanolamine (PE), showed accelerated breakdown during C1 to C2 but slowed in C2 to C3. These metabolic changes likely contribute to the regulation of membrane fluidity. Additionally, the biosynthesis of cutin, suberin, and wax was upregulated from C2 to C3.

In addition, stress-response genes were activated during the early phase. From C1 to C2, genes involved in protein stability, transcription and translation, hormone biosynthesis, ion homeostasis, detoxification, and energy metabolism were upregulated, while those related to cell wall formation were downregulated. These changes suggest an active cellular response to help plants adapt to heat stress. As the transition progressed from C2 to C3, gene expression patterns shifted. Genes related to transcription and translation continued to increase, while many involved in DNA repair, protein synthesis, folding, sorting, turnover, and stress tolerance decreased. These findings indicate that plants were undergoing increased physiological stress in response to prolonged high temperatures.

Many known metabolic changes caused by elevated temperatures showed a consistent response, including alterations in glutathione metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation. For example, cytochrome c oxidase and F-type ATPase were upregulated, leading to increased ATP synthesis. Photosynthesis was upregulated, and carbon metabolism shifted from catabolic to anabolic. Amino acid metabolism, however, was more complicated. Cysteine and methionine synthesis were downregulated, while valine and isoleucine synthesis were upregulated. However, based solely on transcriptome analysis, we could not reach a definitive conclusion about the metabolic changes of many other amino acids.

Additionally, we traced pathways that were differentially activated or repressed by elevated temperatures to gain a comprehensive understanding of the physiological response of CO from initiation through acclimation to heat stress. We then mapped the DEGs with an absolute logFC ≥ 1 for further analysis. Specific changes in selected metabolic responses are summarized in Table 13. In amino acid metabolism, genes related to phenylalanine and tryptophan pathways showed increased expression, while those associated with cysteine and methionine metabolism were downregulated. Glutathione metabolism exhibited varied patterns, with expression levels either increasing and then decreasing, or continuously increasing. The coordinated increase in phenylalanine and tryptophan metabolism may contribute to cellular adaptation to heat stress, as both are aromatic amino acids involved in protein synthesis and stress response mechanisms [67]. However, the contrasting patterns observed in cysteine and methionine metabolism could indicate a shift in the cellular redox balance. The downregulation of these pathways could reflect a reduction in sulfur-containing amino acids, which are essential for antioxidant defense [68]. The divergent pattern of glutathione metabolism, either increasing or returning to baseline, may reflect the cell’s efforts to maintain homeostasis under heat stress. Glutathione (GSH) is a key antioxidant that protects cells from oxidative stress by neutralizing ROS and free radicals [69]. Gene expression associated with several carbohydrate and lipid metabolism pathways, including the citrate cycle, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, starch and sucrose metabolism, fatty acid degradation, fatty acid biosynthesis, and glycerolipid metabolism, was downregulated under heat stress. This suggests a shift in energy metabolism, carbohydrate storage, utilization, and lipid turnover.

However, gene expression associated with oxidative phosphorylation and photosynthesis as upregulated under heat stress. This upregulation suggests a cellular strategy to enhance energy production and maintain cellular function under stressful conditions. The increased expression of genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation and photosynthesis, key processes in ATP production, indicates that the cell may be attempting to compensate for energy deficits caused by the downregulation of other metabolic pathways, such as the citrate cycle. By increasing ATP synthesis through oxidative phosphorylation and photosynthesis, the cell ensures a continuous energy supply to support essential processes like stress response, protein repair, and maintenance of cellular integrity.

These varying patterns in gene expression across metabolic pathways highlight that both changes induced by heat stress and adaptive mechanisms are occurring simultaneously. This suggests that the plant actively responds to heat stress by ensuring energy homeostasis and promoting cellular resilience under the stress conditions.

DEG clustering revealed the presence of many hormone-related genes. Based on this result, we analyzed the expression patterns of genes associated with hormone biosynthesis and signaling (Fig. 9). Abiotic stress significantly impacts plant growth and survival, prompting plants to employ various hormonal regulatory mechanisms. Each phytohormone functions through biosynthesis and signaling pathways, playing a critical role in balancing growth and stress adaptation. This intricate hormonal crosstalk enables plants to optimize physiological responses and enhance survival under adverse environmental conditions. Given their significance in thermal stress responses, we focused on analyzing the expression patterns of key genes involved in the biosynthesis and signaling of these hormones.

In auxin biosynthesis pathways, PIF and YUC expression increased during the C2-C3 transition, whereas ALDH, an alternative auxin biosynthesis gene, was downregulated. This result aligns with previous findings that high-temperature conditions induce PIF-mediated YUC activation, leading to enhanced auxin biosynthesis [70]. The primary auxin biosynthesis pathway occurs through the TAA1/YUC-dependent indole-3-pyruvic acid (IPyA) pathway, whereas ALDH participates in an alternative indole-3-acetaldehyde (IAAld)-dependent pathway [71]. Our findings suggest that the alternative pathway is suppressed under heat stress, while the primary pathway is preferentially activated. In the auxin-signaling pathway, the receptor proteins transport inhibitor response 1 (TIR1) and auxin-signaling F-box (AFB) function as auxin receptors, initiating the degradation of the auxin-signaling repressor auxin/indole-acetic acid protein (auxin/indole-3-acetic acid (IAA)). This degradation activates the auxin response factors (ARFs), thereby regulating the expression of auxin-responsive genes. In CO, ARF and TIR1 were each mapped to a single contig and exhibited continuous downregulation as temperature increased (C1→C2→C3), with a sharp decline between C2 and C3. In contrast, IAA (a transcriptional repressor) and SAUR (an auxin-responsive gene) were downregulated from C1 to C2 but upregulated from C2 to C3. The IAA and SAUR genes were assigned to five and three contigs, respectively.

In cytokinin (CK) biosynthesis, IPT expression decreased, while two of the three CKX contigs exhibited increased expression, leading to a net reduction in CK levels. IPT is a rate-limiting enzyme in CK biosynthesis, playing a pivotal role in CK production [72, 73]. The upregulation of CKX, which catalyzes CK degradation, suggests that heat stress induces cytokinin breakdown, thereby promoting leaf senescence [74]. This response likely reflects a physiological adaptation aimed at minimizing energy expenditure and prioritizing survival. In the cytokinin signaling pathway, cytokinin receptors Arabidopsis histidine kinase (AHK) and type-B arabidopsis response regulators (ARR-B) exhibited continuous downregulation with increasing temperature. AHK initiates cytokinin signaling by binding to cytokinin. The histidine-containing phosphotransfer protein (AHP) then translocates into the nucleus after receiving a phosphate group from phosphorylated AHK, where it phosphorylates ARR. ARR functions in transcriptional regulation, playing a crucial role in modulating the signaling pathway through a negative feedback loop.

In the gibberellin biosynthesis pathway, GA20ox and GA3ox were downregulated, indicating a decline in GA levels. This result aligns with previous reports showing that GA3 levels decrease under heat stress in wheat [75]. Concurrently, downregulation of IPT and upregulation of CKX resulted in reduced CK levels. The simultaneous decline in CK and GA under prolonged heat stress suggests a shift from growth promotion to stress adaptation, indicating that plants prioritize survival by modulating hormonal homeostasis. In the gibberellin signaling pathway, the Gibberellin-Insensitive Dwarf 2 (GID2) gene expression increased with rising temperatures. GA signaling is regulated by the GID1 receptor, which, upon binding to GA, facilitates the ubiquitination and degradation of DELLA proteins through the SCFSLY1/GID2 complex. This process activates transcription factors such as PIF, thereby promoting the expression of GA-responsive genes.

NCEDs (9-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase) are rate-limiting enzymes in ABA biosynthesis, acting as primary regulators of ABA-mediated stress responses [76, 77]. Our results show that NCED expression exhibited an overall increasing trend, suggesting activation of ABA-mediated long-term stress adaptation mechanisms. Specifically, NCED expression peaked at C2, indicating that plants perceived heat stress and upregulated ABA biosynthesis to enhance stomatal regulation, stress signaling, and antioxidant defenses [78]. At C3, NCED expression slightly declined, along with that of the downstream ABA biosynthesis gene aldehyde oxidase 3 (AAO3). This reduction may reflect a feedback regulatory mechanism preventing excessive ABA accumulation and maintaining optimal ABA levels. Furthermore, the increase in ABA and the concurrent decline in GA and CKs suggest a well-established hormonal interaction, in which ABA upregulation suppresses GA and CK biosynthesis, reinforcing a typical stress-induced response. This indicates that plants prioritize stress resistance over growth under heat stress conditions. Previous studies have shown that GA and CKs primarily regulate plant growth, whereas ABA functions as a key modulator of stress responses [79, 80], supporting our findings. In the abscisic acid signaling pathway, the ABA signaling repressor protein phosphatase 2 C (PP2C) and the activator sucrose nonfermenting-1-related protein kinase 2 (SnRK2) were mapped to two and three contigs, respectively, and exhibited a decreasing expression from C2 to C3. When ABA binds to its receptor (pyrabactin resistance/pyrabactin resistance-like/regulatory component of ABA receptors; PYR/PYL/RCAR), PP2C is inhibited, leading to the activation of SnRK2, which regulates stomatal closure and other stress responses under abiotic stress conditions.

Among brassinosteroid (BR) biosynthesis genes, CYP724, a C-22 hydroxylase of the cytochrome P450 family, was transiently upregulated at C2, followed by downregulation at C3 [81]. This suggests that BR biosynthesis is activated within a specific temperature range but is downregulated under prolonged heat stress, potentially serving as an adaptive mechanism for heat stress tolerance. In the brassinosteroid signaling pathway, brassinazole-resistant 1 (BZR1), a key transcriptional regulator of BR-responsive genes, and the receptor brassinosteroid-signaling kinase (BSK) showed a downregulation pattern from C1 to C2. BR signaling is regulated by the BR-insensitive 1 (BRI1)-BRI1 associated receptor kinase 1 (BAK1) receptor complex, which perceives BR and inhibits BR-insensitive 2 (BIN2), thereby activating the transcription factors BZR1 and BRI1 EMS Suppressor 1 (BES1).

Salicylic acid (SA) biosynthesis involves enhanced disease susceptibility 5 (EDS5), a transporter in the isochorismate (ICS) pathway that facilitates isochorismate export from plastids to the cytosol [82]. Our findings indicate differential expression patterns of four EDS5 contigs: three were upregulated at C2 but returned to C1 levels at C3, while one exhibited a sustained increase with rising temperatures. These results suggest that SA biosynthesis is regulated by temperature, potentially modulating stress perception and defense signaling. In the salicylic acid signaling pathway, nonexpressor of pathogenesis-related genes 1 (NPR1), a key signal receptor and immune regulator, exhibited decreased expression from C2 to C3, whereas TGA (a transcriptional regulator that promotes the expression of pathogenesis-related (PR) genes) showed an increasing trend in expression. SA signaling operates through the translocation of NPR1 into the nucleus, where it interacts with TGA, to regulate the expression of pathogen defense-related PR genes.

Conversely, jasmonic acid (JA) biosynthesis was suppressed under high-temperature conditions. The early JA biosynthesis gene LOX2S was downregulated, indicating suppression of JA biosynthesis from the initial stage. Meanwhile, 12-oxo-phytodienoate reductase (OPR) exhibited transient upregulation, suggesting a possible accumulation of intermediate metabolites. In the final β-oxidation step of JA biosynthesis which occurs in peroxisomes, OPCL1, MFP2, and ACX expression decreased, indicating an overall inhibition of JA production. Consequently, suppression of JA biosynthesis may lead to reduced JA-dependent defense responses under heat stress.

Additionally, we performed GO enrichment analysis to comprehensively identify pathways affected by elevated temperatures (Supplementary Table S8). In the transition from C1 to C2, upregulated genes were significantly enriched in RNA binding-related terms. This suggests that post-transcriptional regulation, RNA stability, and translation-associated processes play crucial roles in the early response to heat stress. In contrast, downregulated genes were associated with ATP binding, adenyl nucleotide binding, purine nucleotide binding, cytoskeletal protein binding, tubulin binding, and microtubule binding. The downregulation of nucleotide binding-related pathways suggests a reduction in nucleotide metabolism, potentially affecting DNA and RNA synthesis. Furthermore, the suppression of cytoskeleton-related pathways suggests an impact on cell movement and maintenance of cellular structure under heat stress.

During the transition from C2 to C3, upregulated genes were enriched in nucleoside phosphate biosynthetic processes, organophosphate biosynthetic processes, and components of intracellular organelles. These findings indicate an increased demand for nucleotide metabolism and intracellular organelle maintenance, which may be critical for sustaining cellular function under higher temperatures. In contrast, downregulated genes were associated with a broad range of biological processes such as binding activities (nucleotide, ATP, protein, ion), regulation of gene expression (regulation of gene silencing, negative regulation of gene expression, RNA processing, RNA exonuclease activity, helicase activity), ATP hydrolysis, cellular processes (regulation of cell shape, establishment of intracellular localization), and transport functions (P-type calcium transport, ATPase-coupled transmembrane transporter activity). The downregulation of these pathways suggests a reduction in various cellular activities, including energy production, gene expression, and intracellular transport.

Furthermore, we analyzed of miRNA expression and their target genes. A total of 40 miRNAs were identified in CO, most of which exhibited decreased expression. The significant negative correlation observed in 357 miRNA-target gene pairs (Pearson’s r < − 0.8) suggests a crucial role for miRNAs in post-transcriptional gene regulation under heat stress conditions. KEGG pathway analysis of miRNA target genes further revealed their involvement in key biological processes, including oxidative phosphorylation, antioxidant defense mechanisms, hormone signal transduction (such as auxin and ABA signaling), starch and sucrose metabolism (particularly glucose degradation), and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis. These pathways are well-documented for their essential roles in plant adaptation to high temperatures, highlighting the complex regulatory networks through which miRNAs modulate responses to heat stress.

Taken together, metabolic responses were less intense during the initial transition from C1 to C2. However, overall metabolic changes became more pronounced as the progression continued, revealing a complex network of physiological adaptations. To better interpret these changes at the transcriptomic and pathway levels, we integrated the results from DEG clustering, GO enrichment, KEGG pathway mapping, and miRNA–target gene analysis. Through this approach, we found that CO grown under ambient high temperatures exhibited a mild upregulation of pathways related to protein stability and turnover, ABA biosynthesis, and energy production, including photosynthesis and oxidative phosphorylation. In contrast, under extreme heat stress, a widespread downregulation of metabolic processes was observed, particularly in pathways associated with fatty acid degradation, cysteine and methionine biosynthesis, and nucleotide metabolism.

Despite the observed gene expression changes, the physiological implications of these changes are complex and require cautious interpretation. For instance, we observed that several genes within the same pathway exhibited both upregulation and downregulation in response to elevated temperatures from C2 to C3, indicating the limitations of drawing broad conclusions from DEGs. While a comprehensive view at the transcriptome level is possible, overall biological consequences should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

This study analyzes the mRNA transcriptome of CO under varying and fluctuating ambient temperatures. An mRNA integration analysis was conducted to investigate how CO responds to temperature changes associated with climate warming. Specifically, multiple publicly available datasets were utilized to assemble high-quality contigs for the accurate quantification of gene expression levels. To our knowledge, this is the first study to perform an mRNA integration analysis of medicinal crops under varying ambient temperatures. Additionally, our study presents a detailed transcriptomic profile of CO and explores the comprehensive molecular mechanisms underlying its responses to varying environmental temperatures.

This work also demonstrates that RNA-seq and de novo assembly are both feasible and cost-effective approaches for large-scale transcriptomic analysis. The study’s sequential analytical processes (based on experimental logic, plot designs, and specific research objectives) include normalization, DEG clustering, GO enrichment, KEGG pathway mapping, miRNA-target gene expression analysis and multiple rounds of data validation using RNA sequencing under controlled conditions. These analyses offer valuable guidelines for physiological interpretation.

Although this study provides valuable transcriptomic insights into CO’s response to heat stress, further integration with proteomic and metabolomic analyses will be essential to validate and expand our understanding of the underlying regulatory networks.

In conclusion, our research exemplifies an effective method for analyzing complex transcriptome data. These data will contribute to future research on heat tolerance and may facilitate of candidate genes for the molecular breeding of heat-resistant crops.
