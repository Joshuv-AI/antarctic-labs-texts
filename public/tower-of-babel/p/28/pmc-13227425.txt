Challenges of Electronic Medical Record Implementation From the Perspective of Hospital Leaders in Riyadh, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia's Public Hospitals: A Mixed-Methods Study
Authors: Atheer A. Alghamdi, Samiha H. Sayed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:To assesses the challenges of implementing electronic medical records (EMR) from the leaders' perspective in public hospitals in Riyadh, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), and explores their suggestions for improvement.Methods:A concurrent mixed research approach using a purposive sample of all hospital leaders (61) involved in EMR in 5 randomly selected public hospitals utilizing EMR in Riyadh. The researchers deployed a semi-structured interview schedule to collect the data. They used descriptive statistics and ordinal regression analysis for quantitative data, and thematic analysis for qualitative data.Results:Indicate that 80.3% of the hospital leaders had a good perception of EMR implementation. They also reported a high level of staff perception (88.5%). The ordinal regression analysis shows that sufficient infrastructure (p = 0.003) and the total staff perception score (p = 0.002) are significant predictors of higher perceptions of EMR implementation. Thematic analysis identified several implementation challenges: training and adoption (29.5%), system issues (23%), time (21.3%), change management (16.4%), and infrastructure/data management (4.9%). Suggested improvements emphasized user training and education (34.4%), system enhancements (24.6%), user involvement and feedback (18%), infrastructure and resources (13%), project management (6.6%), and implementation strategy (3.3%).Conclusion:Hospital leaders play a critical role in EMR implementation and adoption, a topic underrepresented in the existing literature. It highlights the need for collaboration among IT professionals, healthcare staff, and policymakers to address ongoing challenges.

Introduction

Electronic medical records (EMRs) are digital versions of patient charts that contain a patient's medical history, diagnoses, treatments, and test results [1]. Over time, they have advanced from simple storage tools to integrated platforms that support data exchange and improve patient outcomes [2]. Global adoption of EMRs is regarded as essential for developing healthcare quality, efficiency, and safety, which leads to greater digital transformation in many institutions [345]. In the Gulf region, especially Saudi Arabia, EMR implementation has been prioritized to address inefficiencies and promote interoperability [367].

Implementing EMRs offers significant benefits for healthcare systems. Medication errors from illegible handwriting in intensive care units account for 43% of cases, and EMRs help reduce such errors, minimize procedure duplication, and improve efficiency [8]. EMRs also support integrated care by offering a holistic patient view, enhancing continuity of care, and reducing waiting times [9]. They address key problems of paper records, such as inconsistent data, limited searchability, and risk of data loss [1011]. However, several barriers may limit its successful adoption, including resistance to change, data security concerns, resource and training constraints, limited technical support, low technological proficiency, and poor communication with vendors and developers after implementation [101213].

In Saudi Arabia, using EMRs has grown significantly, though implementation levels still vary across regions and services [7]. Recent studies highlight that although EMR usage has improved in areas such as prescribing medications, accessing laboratory results, managing hospitalizations, and follow-ups, significant skill gaps persist due to the lack of structured training programs [14]. The EMR implementation continues to face challenges in Saudi Arabia, including interoperability issues, security concerns, resistance to change, financial pressures, and insufficient training and technical support [7]. To increase healthcare providers' proficiency and confidence in using EMR systems, the literature emphasizes the need for training programs and optimization techniques [1415].

Hospital leaders—such as executives, administrators, and department heads—play a critical role in EMR implementation, as their decisions strongly influence adoption, integration, and optimization [3]. Gaining insight into their perspectives, challenges, and recommendations is therefore essential; however, research from the leadership viewpoint remains limited in Saudi Arabia. While global studies exist, more investigation is needed to understand the unique experiences of Saudi hospital leaders [16]. A recent case study at one of Saudi Arabia's largest tertiary hospitals highlighted medication errors, particularly in the transmission of drug allergy information, as a key leadership concern during EMR deployment. It also emphasized that EMR implementation is not merely an IT project but a complex, adaptive process that requires strong leadership competencies [3]. Evidence shows that a centralized project management model has facilitated large-scale EMR adoption and addressed barriers in primary healthcare settings in the country [16]. Thus, the perspectives and experiences of healthcare leaders are critical, as they offer valuable insights to policymakers, administrators, and IT specialists involved in the design, implementation, and optimization of EMR systems.

This study seeks to develop evidence-based strategies to enhance EMR utilization in public hospitals by examining leaders' challenges and suggestions. Healthcare organizations can develop targeted measures to overcome barriers, boost user acceptance, and improve patient outcomes. Using a mixed-methods design, this study offers fresh insights into the little-known context of Saudi Arabia, focusing on organizational challenges, leadership roles, and tactics that influence EMR implementation in Riyadh.

Method

A concurrent mixed methods research approach that was conducted in 5 public hospitals (randomly selected) that utilized the EMR system in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. The target population comprised all hospital leaders working at the five selected public hospitals in Riyadh. Those who did not participate in the EMR implementation or hold leadership positions were excluded, as were hospitals located outside Riyadh. The researchers randomly selected five public hospitals from the pre-prepared sampling frame of all hospitals in Riyadh that used EMR. They also purposively selected, from each hospital, the leaders involved in the EMR implementation process, the health informatics department head, the assigned team leader, and EMR committee members (61 leaders in total).

The researchers developed a semi-structured interview questionnaire following a review of the recent literature: Part I: Personal data: age, gender, profession, and serving years in the institution. Part II: EMR-related data: The researchers developed it based on credible evidence [1317]. It contains eight questions. One question about the objectives of implementing EMR. Two open-ended questions about the name of the employed EMR system and the reasons for not achieving its purpose, if applicable. Additionally, 5 “yes” or “no” questions pertained to achieving EMR objectives and to the availability of an EMR Committee, a unit-based team, sufficiently trained staff, and adequate infrastructure. Part III: Leaders' perspective on staff's perception of EMR System Scale: The researchers adapted it from credible evidence [13]. It has seven questions with ratings on a 5-point Likert scale, from “rarely” (1) to “always” (5). The weighted mean scores were analyzed for each item using the 5-point Likert scale cutoffs: rarely (1.00-1.80), sometimes (1.81-2.60), usually (2.61-3.40), often (3.41-4.20), and always (4.21-5.00). The researchers classified total scores into poor (≤3.39), fair (3.40-3.79), and good (≥3.80). Part IV: Leader's perception of the EMR implementation system Scale: The researchers adapted it from the latest evidence and adjusted it to meet the study context [318]. The scale encompasses 18 items rated on a five-point Likert scale from very low (1) to very high (5). The weighted mean scores were used to analyze each item using the 5-point Likert scale cutoff values: very low (1.00-1.80), low (1.81-2.60), moderate (2.61-3.40), high (3.41-4.20), and very high (4.21-5.00). The researchers further categorize the total scores into poor (≤3.39), fair (3.40-3.79), and good (≥3.80). Part V: Leaders' perceived challenges and suggestions for improvement were developed based on previous literature: [1319] It contains four questions: a “yes” or “no” question regarding user complaints from the EMR system, and a related open-ended question about the reasons for complaints (if applicable). The remaining two were open-ended about the challenges of implementing EMR and suggestions for improvement. They were subjected to thematic analysis to explore the themes and sub-themes.

Instrument's validity and reliability, and pilot study

A panel of 5 experts evaluated the instrument and reached consensus on its relevance, wording, organization, and item scoring. The investigators made the adjustments in line with their feedback. The questionnaire achieved a Content Validity Index above the acceptable threshold (>70%), indicating a satisfactory level (CVI = 0.81). The quantitative parts of the questionnaire were assessed for internal consistency reliability using Cronbach's alpha, which revealed satisfactory scores (>0.70): Part II (α = 0.742), Part III (α = 0.724), and Part IV (α = 0.917). The overall reliability score was α = 0.890. The questionnaire was piloted with 10 healthcare leaders from two hospitals (omitted from the study) to assess clarity and understanding. Regarding qualitative data, the researchers used rigorous thematic analysis to ensure the objectivity, trustworthiness, and transferability of the data.

The researchers gathered data over seven months, from April to October 2023, following ethical approval. They conducted semi-structured on-site interviews with hospital leaders at public hospitals, who gave oral consent after being fully informed, during private interviews that lasted about 20 to 30 minutes and were audio-recorded with their consent. The researchers supported their verbatim transcriptions of the recordings with reflexive field notes to capture nonverbal clues and context.

Data analysis

The researchers employed IBM SPSS Statistical software, Version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The researchers used descriptive statistics for continuous variables (mean and standard deviation) and categorical variables (counts and percentages). They also used ordinal regression to examine predictors of hospital leaders' total perception score. Model fitness was confirmed by the Likelihood Ratio Chi-Square test using the (-2 log-likelihood) function, and goodness of fit was tested using the Pearson Chi-square test. The cutoff value for significance was <0.05.

Qualitative data were analyzed using rigorous thematic analysis. Researchers transcribed and reviewed all data multiple times, identified themes through initial coding, and employed dual coding with discussion until they reached consensus. All coding and analysis steps were reviewed to ensure data dependability, credibility, and confirmability.

Discussion

Insights from this study highlight the crucial role of leaders in EMR implementation and adoption and explore the multifaceted challenges they face. With the highest ratings for management support and clarity of organizational vision, most hospital leaders had a favorable opinion of EMR implementation, reflecting clearly defined system goals and objectives. However, system design and maintenance, staff acceptance, system rollout, and staff monitoring received lower ratings. These results raise questions about the practical aspects of EMR implementation, consistent with difficulties encountered across various healthcare system settings [202122].

Hospital leaders also highlighted the need for organizational and infrastructural support for the successful implementation of EMRs. The institutionalization of governance is reflected in the establishment of unit-level monitoring teams and EMR committees in every hospital. Ordinal regression analysis demonstrated that leaders' perception of EMR implementation is significantly predicted by sufficient infrastructure and positive staff perceptions. Similarly, evidence reveals the importance of supportive technological environments and satisfactory human factors in sustaining EMR use [20212223]. Conversely, evidence from Nigeria suggests that infrastructure is less significant, with barriers instead being tied to an unreliable power supply and a lack of hospital websites [24].

The studied hospital leaders believe that most staff hold positive perceptions of EMRs, view their data as more complete than paper records, and prefer them over traditional charting. These findings align with studies reporting favorable provider perceptions of EMRs [25262728]. A Malaysian study by Ramoo et al. [26] noted that staff views vary depending on training, work unit, computer access, and duration of EMR use. In contrast, a qualitative study by Provenzano et al. [29]. highlighted both benefits—such as improved data access and patient safety—and drawbacks, including technostress, a heavier workload, and disruptions to teamwork [29]. Overall, the results highlight the importance of administrative preparedness and supportive strategies in promoting staff well-being.

Thematic analysis revealed persistent challenges to EMR implementation, including training and adoption, system-related issues, infrastructure limitations, time management, change management, and data management—similar to challenges reported in various healthcare settings [12242730]. Training and adoption emerged as the most prominent issues, underscoring the need for ongoing, tailored education for both clinical and administrative staff [2731]. Additionally, Janssen et al. [32] identified post-implementation gaps in technical support, increased workloads, and steep learning curves as barriers to successful use [32]. These difficulties are further intensified by the reluctance to technological change and varying digital literacy levels [310].

System-related challenges—such as high complexity, poor usability, software bugs, and weak integration with legacy systems—undermine workflow efficiency and user satisfaction, consistent with international findings on design flaws and interoperability barriers [122027]. Evidence suggests that involving end-users in co-design and participatory approaches can improve usability and better align EMR functions with clinical workflows [220]. Furthermore, workflow interruptions and added time burdens during adoption mirror global concerns, pointing to phased implementation and workflow analytics as strategies to reduce disruption [33].

This study identified key themes in leaders' suggestions for improving EMR implementation, including system enhancements, training and education, implementation strategies, user involvement and feedback, project management, and infrastructure—themes that align with best practices in the literature [1018]. According to a study conducted in Ethiopia by Yehualashet et al. [30], the main facilitators were training, user-friendliness, workflow alignment, user knowledge, change management, management support, and sufficient resources [30]. Regular refresher training, role-specific interactive workshops, and organized feedback systems can significantly improve user acceptance and competency. Overcoming challenges during EMR rollout also requires strong governance, with well-defined milestones and efficient risk mitigation [1229].

This study found that financial and infrastructural readiness are both facilitators and areas requiring sustained investment, particularly in strengthening hardware capacity and network connectivity to prevent delays and data loss [234]. In the U.S., Zheng and Jiang [35] identified device availability, internet access, and perceived usefulness as key enablers of EMR adoption, while highlighting the importance of continuous training and effective communication [35]. Social dimensions are also crucial because collaboration between clinical staff and the IT team has a substantial impact on the success of EMR implementation [23]. Likewise, a study in Riyadh by Arabi et al. [3] stressed that EMR adoption depends not only on technical enhancements but also on strategic change management, organizational commitment, and leadership capacity [3].

Strengths and limitations

This study offers valuable insights by focusing on hospital leaders' perspectives on EMR implementation, an area that has received little attention in Saudi Arabia. Using a mixed-methods approach with validated tools and careful thematic analysis enhances the credibility and depth of the findings.

However, tracking how perceptions change over time is limited when data is collected all at once. The emphasis on public hospitals in Riyadh might restrict the applicability to private institutions or other areas. Although leaders offer crucial perspectives, the study does not account for patients' or frontline employees' day-to-day experiences. To strengthen the evidence for successful EMR adoption, future studies should use longitudinal designs and involve a diverse range of stakeholders across different healthcare settings.

In conclusion, the study highlights that hospital leaders in Riyadh's public hospitals perceive a good EMR implementation process, with success strongly associated with organizational management support, clear and shared vision, interdisciplinary collaboration, and communication between IT and healthcare staff. The leaders reported Noteworthy challenges concerning organizational change, time management, system usability, and staff training and adoption. To overcome these challenges, leaders underscore the need for comprehensive, ongoing, and role-specific training programs, alongside system advances that prioritize user integration and usability. Moreover, end-user involvement in system design and feedback processes, strong project management, dependable infrastructure, and easily accessible technical support are necessary.

Finally, the results highlight the need for a comprehensive approach that combines organizational and human factors with technology development to ensure an EMR is successfully adopted. Leveraging EMRs to improve healthcare delivery will require ongoing stakeholder collaboration, investments in infrastructure and workforce capacity, and adaptable project management techniques.
