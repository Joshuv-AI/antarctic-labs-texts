Leveraging social media footprints for predicting college student anxiety: a machine learning approach
Authors: Chenyan Dong, Aneta Neumann, Yingping Sun, Kexuan Xin, Yichang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnxiety is one of the most prevalent mental health concerns among college students worldwide, yet traditional assessment methods relying on self-report questionnaires are time-consuming, susceptible to response bias, and difficult to scale. Social media platforms, which students use extensively, generate rich behavioral and linguistic data that may reflect underlying psychological states. This study investigates whether passively collected social media footprints are associated with anxiety scores among college students and may support the prediction of anxiety-related patterns.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, we surveyed 3,211 students across 11 universities in four provinces of China using the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), yielding 2,368 valid responses (α = 0.918, KMO = 0.838). With informed consent, we collected 83,968 Weibo posts from participants’ public social media accounts. After rigorous preprocessing, 56,060 posts from 1,316 users were retained. We extracted multi-dimensional features spanning linguistic, emotional, cognitive, behavioral, and temporal dimensions. Four machine learning models—Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, and SVR—were trained and compared. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were used for model interpretation.ResultsThe Random Forest model achieved the best predictive performance on the test set (R² = 0.77, RMSE = 13.41, MAPE = 25.94%, MSLE = 0.087). SHAP-based interpretation identified grade level, professionalism, emotional expression, risk-related language, curiosity index, emotional tone, and word count as major features contributing to the model. These features align with established psychological dimensions of anxiety symptomatology, including emotional, cognitive, and behavioral indicators.ConclusionsPublic social-media signals were associated with SAS anxiety scores in this consenting, active Weibo-user sample and supported cross-sectional score prediction within the study context. The proposed pipeline offers a transparent, reproducible, and privacy-conscious approach to estimate cross-sectional anxiety-related patterns from publicly available social media data. These findings demonstrate the potential for integrating computational approaches with psychological assessment for research on anxiety-related behaviors and digital phenotyping among college students.Keywords: Anxiety, College Students, Social Media, Machine Learning, Explainable AI

Introduction

College students, as a special group, are in a stage of development in all aspects of their values and worldview. At the same time, in this diversified and rapidly developing society, they face a variety of pressures from academics, interpersonal relationships, future planning, and financial aspects [1]. Under the dual influence of an immature value system and multiple pressures, college students are prone to psychological problems such as anxiety. Anxiety disorder is the most common mental health disorder in the world, characterized by feelings of anxiety and divided into two forms: chronic anxiety disorder (also refers to Generalized Anxiety Disorder) and acute anxiety disorder [2, 3]. Anxiety in college students can have adverse effects. In mild cases, it can affect the normal functioning of the body, learning, and daily life, such as causing nervousness, decreased memory, loss of appetite, or reduced social motivation [4]. In severe cases, it can lead to negative behaviors that affect not only the individual but also society, such as suicide or retaliatory actions [5].

Currently, the proportion of college students experiencing psychological anxiety is high. A study conducted in Jhansi, Uttar Pradesh in 2017 showed an anxiety disorder prevalence rate of 71% [6, 7]. A survey of 400 students from different schools in 2020 indicated that 48.30% of college students had anxiety symptoms [8]. In the same year, a survey of 645 college students from Shandong, China, showed an anxiety symptom detection rate of 25.7% [9]. Anxiety has become a significant public health issue. Understanding factors associated with anxiety symptoms may help inform future prevention and support efforts.

The mental health issue of anxiety among college students has become a focal point of attention for universities. How to assess anxiety-related symptoms or anxiety scores among college students has attracted the attention of scholars. Existing research on anxiety identification mainly focuses on traditional anxiety scale measurement methods and machine learning prediction methods based on basic feature sets. On the one hand, in questionnaire measurements, widely used scales include the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7), the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), and the Adult Social Anxiety Questionnaire (CASO-A30) [10, 11]. However, this questionnaire-based measurement method is not only time-consuming but also labor-intensive, resulting in low measurement efficiency [12, 13]. Furthermore, in machine learning modeling studies based on basic information, the selected information dimensions are often not directly obtainable and also need to be acquired through questionnaires. For example, personality traits [14], self-rated health [15], and perceived life stress [16] are commonly used. There is still an urgent need to explore more accurate and efficient application method for predicting anxiety in college students.

Meanwhile, in the internet era, college students, who frequently use the network to search for, receive, and disseminate information, leave a large amount of behavioral and textual data on social media, providing a rich source for studying their psychological conditions [17, 18]. Research has shown that historical behaviors in digital footprints can be used to assess and infer personality traits and other psychological characteristics [19]. Currently, some scholars have begun to explore the use of artificial intelligence and machine learning techniques based on social media data to investigate individual anxiety. For example, D Gruda and S Hasan [20] used a non-intrusive machine learning method to measure anxiety ratings of individuals on Weibo. However, their study considered semantic embedding vectors and emoji features in user posts, but did not take into account user characteristics such as cognition and emotion. In fact, the identification of anxiety disorder symptoms mainly comes from four aspects: emotional symptoms [21], physical symptoms [4], cognitive symptoms [22], and behavioral symptoms [23]. These four dimensions can all be inferred from digital footprints. Emotional and cognitive symptoms can be obtained from the textual data on users’ personal profiles through natural language processing (NLP) techniques. When college students express physical and behavioral symptoms on social media, it is possible to identify these aspects by matching professional dictionaries with user textual data. In addition, studies have shown that social media use is related to anxiety [17]. Therefore, it is feasible to extract basic semantic features, emotions, cognition, physical symptoms, and behavior from digital footprints to predict anxiety among college students.

Based on this, this paper draws inspiration from the social media footprints of college students to extract relevant features and establish a machine learning model to predict their anxiety. First, we innovatively identify implicit features such as emotions and cognition using big data analysis methods like Natural Language Processing (NLP), and integrate these with basic personal information features such as gender and age to construct a high-dimensional feature set. Then, we train four regression models in machine learning, including Support Vector Regression (SVR), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), and Random Forest (RF), and compare their performance. Finally, based on the model with the best fit, we use SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) to interpret and visualize the regression results, providing insights into factors associated with variation in anxiety scores among college students.

Research objectives

This study explores how students’ digital behaviors may reveal social-media indicators associated with anxiety scores and outlines three objectives to examine such signals in a responsible and scientific manner.

(1) Build a responsible bridge between psychology and digital life

We construct a large-scale, ethically sourced dataset that integrates standardized Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) assessments with real-world Weibo activity from 1,316 consenting students across 11 universities. All data are carefully de-identified and collected through opt-in participation to ensure privacy and trustworthiness.

(2) Translate digital behavior into interpretable psychological signals.

We design a multi-dimensional feature system that captures how students express themselves online. This includes linguistic tone, emotional polarity, curiosity toward new topics, cognitive stance, posting frequency, and daily rhythm. These features draw on psychological theories and validated lexicons to ensure interpretability.

(3) Predict anxiety scores using interpretable machine learning models.

We evaluate the predictive power of widely used machine learning models including RF, XGBoost, LightGBM, and SVR. The models are trained to predict continuous SAS anxiety scores and are evaluated using multiple metrics such as RMSE, R², MAPE, and MSLE. SHAP analysis is used to interpret which features contribute most to model-estimated anxiety scores, making the results transparent and easier to interpret.

This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews existing research on anxiety measurement and social media–based prediction. Section 3 outlines the study design, including data collection, feature engineering, labeling strategies, and model specifications. Section 4 presents the experimental study, descriptive statistics, comparative model evaluation, and interpretability analyses. Section 5 summarizes the main findings, and discusses theoretical and practical implications, along with directions for future research. Section 6 concludes the paper.

Related works

Anxiety prediction

Existing research on identifying anxiety primarily focuses on traditional anxiety scale measurement methods, such as the Likert scale [11], and machine learning prediction methods based on basic feature sets.

The measurement of anxiety scales is the most direct method, as it can directly measure the level of anxiety through respondents filling out the scales. For example, H Gao, J Chen, X Zhang, L Hou, L Wang, Y Meng, W Gao and Y Huang [11] used the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7) to investigate anxiety levels through a stratified random cluster sampling method. PEM Valera, MR Bouroncle, A Gallegos, VDA Bravo and J Valencia [10] identified anxiety among college students based on the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) and the Adult Social Anxiety Questionnaire (CASO-A30). T Zhang, ER Lu, QM Liao and DL Sun [2] measured students’ academic anxiety levels using the academic anxiety subscale from the “College Student General Academic Emotion Questionnaire.” However, this traditional method of measurement has many drawbacks. First, filling out questionnaires is not only time-consuming but also labor-intensive, resulting in low measurement efficiency [12]. Second, since schools require real names when distributing questionnaires, some students are reluctant to reveal their privacy and thus fill out the questionnaires dishonestly, which greatly undermines the effectiveness of the measurement. Finally, existing research has shown that the incidence of depression among college students is high, and the situation for campus mental-health research and support planning still faces workload and scalability challenges [24]. However, given the reality of a large student population and a low student-to-faculty ratio, anxiety mental health monitoring still faces issues such as heavy workload, fatigue, and low work efficiency. There is an urgent need to explore new approaches to address these challenges [25].

Machine learning predictions based on basic information features usually have a relatively high degree of accuracy. For example, the precision of training information related to college students using the SVM model can reach up to 69.7% [15], the AUC value of training data related to adolescents using the artificial neural network model reaches 0.8255 [26], and the AUC score contributed by training the Canadian census population using the LightGBM model is 87.45% [16]. However, the information dimensions required for this prediction method are usually not directly obtainable and need to be acquired through questionnaires or interviews. For instance, A Almadhor, S Abbas, GA Sampedro, S Alsubai, S Ojo, AA Hejaili and L Strazovska [14] measured students’ personality traits as features to predict anxiety. M Mahalingam, M Jammal, R Hoteit, D Ayna, M Romani, S Hijazi, I Bou-Hamad and C El Morr [15] first required students to self-report on aspects such as self-rated health, income, and adherence to COVID-19 guidelines in order to collect feature data. CC Li, BT Xu, ZW Chen, X Huang, J He and X Xie [27] needed to investigate students’ disclosures about internet addiction and the level of parental concern to obtain data. Undoubtedly, against the backdrop of the high incidence of depression among college students, the acquisition of these data is also often time-consuming and labor-intensive.

Currently, there is still a lack of a method that can obtain data information from college students at a relatively low cost while accurately and efficiently predict their anxiety scores.

Anxiety and social media

In the digital age, college students, as one of the most active and representative user groups on social media platforms, express their opinions and attitudes on various social media, leaving a vast amount of digital footprints [17]. These footprints range from daily updates to interactions, comments with others, and participation in community discussions, often reflecting their exploration and identification with their own identities. This provides a rich source for investigating the psychological conditions of college students [28, 29]. For example, individuals who are more inclined to share their personal emotions and daily lives may be going through psychological issues related to intimacy and loneliness.

The information contained in digital footprints can be used to infer a person’s mental health status. Currently, scholars have already used digital footprints to infer the personality traits of social media users. For example, Y Jiang, S Deng, H Li and Y Liu [30] found in their study that the personality trait prediction model built using machine learning techniques is reliable, indicating that social interaction data on social media platforms can predict user personality. MA Kosan, H Karacan and BA Urgen [31] retrieved data from Twitter and created an LSTM-based prediction model through structural analysis, achieving good results. In addition, it has been proven to be feasible to predict a person’s mental health status from digital traces [13].

Anxiety, as one of the mental health symptoms, can also be inferred from the digital traces left by users. For example, D Gruda and S Hasan [20] constructed a machine learning model to predict anxiety levels using semantic embedding vectors and emoji features from digital traces. However, relevant studies have shown that in addition to textual semantics, there are many other factors in digital traces that affect users’ anxiety. For instance, the frequency of social media use is correlated with anxiety, that is, positive use of social media is negatively correlated with social anxiety [32, 33]. In addition, anxiety are related to users’ curiosity to a certain extent, and anxiety induced by early-life stress can affect curiosity behavior during adolescence [34]. At the same time, emotional levels and judgment abilities are also closely related to anxiety. For example, M Weeks, LL Ooi and RJ Coplan [35] found that shyness and judgment bias significantly predict social anxiety. These factors can all be analyzed from users’ digital traces based on data mining methods. Therefore, this study extracts basic semantic features, emotions, cognition, physical symptoms, and behavior from digital traces to build a machine learning model to accurately predict anxiety among college students.

Methods

We intentionally do not adopt instruction-tuned, generative large language models (LLMs) in this study. Research applications that explore anxiety-related patterns at scale prioritize data minimization, interpretability, reproducibility, and cost. LLMs typically require access to raw textual content and are often served by third-party infrastructure, raising governance and data-transfer concerns in social-media research with human participants. In health-adjacent settings, transparent models are preferred to support actionable, low-burden interventions; recent reviews highlight that clinical-grade effectiveness and safety boundaries of LLMs for mental-health use remain uncertain. Given these constraints, we benchmark lightweight, explainable models under identical splits and report feature-level explanations to inform practice [36].

Data collection and processing

We conducted surveys in 11 universities across four provinces in China: Shandong, Zhejiang, Jilin, and Sichuan. The questionnaire included SAS, grade level, gender, age, and Weibo account ID of students. We sent 3,211 questionnaires to college students. In the informed consent form, we explicitly inform participants that the Weibo ID they provide will be used solely for academic research purposes. We will collect information visible on your public profile page (including publicly posted content, reposts, and interaction counts) via automated tools to analyze correlations between language patterns and psychological states. All data will be de-identified and analyzed in aggregated form; participants’ personal information won’t be displayed individually, and they also have the right to withdraw their consent at any time and request the deletion of their data. We collected A total of 2,984 questionnaires and Weibo IDs. Eligible participants are currently enrolled undergraduate students who have signed an informed consent form and provided a verifiable Weibo ID; all records verified as invalid were excluded. After verification, 2,368 valid questionnaires and IDs were obtained. The measurement results showed that the Cronbach’s coefficient of the scale was 0.918, the KMO value was 0.838, P < 0.001, indicating that the questionnaire had good validity and reliability.

Weibo is one of the most visited websites in China and is highly popular among young people. Chinese young people mainly use Weibo to satisfy their needs for information and social connections [37]. Therefore, this study selected Weibo platform data to predict the anxiety of college students. The study analyzes individual student users as the basic unit, ensuring that each user’s social media data is strictly matched with their questionnaire data. Data isolation is implemented throughout the data processing to prevent cross-contamination. Therefore, we created web crawlers to collect the posts posted and forwarded by the users and the corresponding number of likes, comments, and reposts they received. We strictly adhere to the principle of data minimization, crawling only publicly available fields directly relevant to our research objectives (text content, timestamps, interaction counts). We did not attempt to obtain, decrypt, or store any non-public information, private messages, or friend lists. Upon data download, all Weibo IDs were immediately converted to anonymous identifiers via hash encryption. The mapping between original IDs and encrypted files is stored on an offline, locally encrypted server managed by the project lead. A total of 83,968 posts corresponding to 2,368 users were crawled.

We pre-processed the collected data, which often contained meaningless special symbols, hyperlinks, and other information. We had to clean these to obtain a standardized text dataset. Data cleaning was divided into three steps: (1) Reducing data noise through data filtering, deleting duplicates, removing blank and invalid texts, and eliminating hyperlinks. (2) Eliminating meaningless ultra-short posts (such as repost fields) and posts with fewer than 20 reposts. A low number of reposts is considered insufficient to effectively infer mental health levels [38]. (3) To ensure timeliness, deleting Weibo IDs that have not left any digital traces in the past month. Ultimately, 1,316 users and their corresponding 56,060 posts were retained. Therefore, the final sample for this study consists of college students who both completed valid questionnaires and maintained active Weibo use within the past month, constituting a convenience sample defined by specific inclusion criteria.

We refer to this resource as the “Weibo Corpus.” It comprises Chinese-language posts authored by 1,316 consenting students, with content-level fields (post text and engagement counts) and user-level fields (followers, followings, posts, registration time). The corpus intentionally reflects recent activity through a 30-day recency filter and supports two analysis units: user-level aggregation and post-level text features. Feature families extracted from the Weibo Corpus include basic attributes and interaction statistics, LIWC-style lexical categories, sentiment polarity, curiosity indices, and stance-based judgment.

We constructed a set of indicators, which includes basic features and special features. Basic features refer to the information directly contained in the user’s personal profile on social media. Special features are the information that can be mined from personal digital traces through methods such as natural language processing and dictionary matching.

Special feature extraction

Language style reflects cognitive processes. According to the language style matching theory, the frequency of using first-person singular pronouns (such as “I”) is associated with negative emotions and depressive tendencies [40]. Linguistic features are significantly correlated with mental health levels [31]. Based on this, we extracted the full text data of the posts published by users and used the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) dictionary to measure general linguistic features in the statistical data. LIWC is a technique in natural language processing that can perform quantitative analysis on text content and calculate the percentage of different categories of words (especially psychological words) in the imported text files, such as causal words, emotional words, cognitive words, and other psychological word categories in the entire text [41, 42]. In this study, we extracted the LIWC values of the user’s text data, including total word count, use of question marks, use of exclamation marks, first-person pronouns, family words, and risk words.

Based on affect theory, prolonged expression of negative emotions serves as a core indicator for predicting mental health issues [43]. For example, extreme emotional exposure may indicate that a user’s psychology is in a highly unstable state. To quantify emotional expression, we performed sentiment analysis of participants’ Weibo posts using the publicly available pre-trained Chinese sentiment classification model “IDEA-CCNL/Erlangshen-Roberta-330 M-Sentiment” from the Hugging Face Transformers library. The model is based on the RoBERTa architecture and was developed by the International Digital Economy Academy Chinese NLP Laboratory (IDEA-CCNL). It was pre-trained on large-scale Chinese corpora, including news articles, social media texts, and encyclopedic content, enabling robust sentiment representation across diverse linguistic contexts [44]. The model was accessed through the Hugging Face repository (https://huggingface.co/IDEA-CCNL/Erlangshen-Roberta-330M-Sentiment) by using Transformers version 5.3.0 and PyTorch version 2.11.0.

Anxiety measurement

To predict the anxiety of college students using a supervised training model, it is necessary to extract the anxiety of each student from the labeled dataset. Based on this, the study employed the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) developed by Zung [50]. The scale consists of 20 items, covering four dimensions: anxiety and panic, vestibular sensations, somatic control, and gastrointestinal sensations. A four-point rating scale is used, where 1 = none or a little of the time, 2 = some of the time, 3 = a good part of the time, and 4 = most or all of the time. Higher scores indicate higher levels of anxiety. Among the items, items 5, 9, 13, 17, and 19 are reverse-scored, while the rest are positively scored. The total score is calculated by summing the scores from 20 items, with a range of 20 to 80 points. The total score is then multiplied by 1.25 to obtain a standard score, which ranges from 25 to 100 points. The cutoff score is 50; scores above this threshold indicate the presence of anxiety.

Model construction

This paper selects four state-of-the-art machine learning methods to construct four models and evaluate the most effective training model.

(1) random forest (RF)

RF is based on the Bagging idea, generating multiple sub-datasets through bootstrap sampling and training a decision tree model on each sub-dataset. The final prediction results from multiple models are combined through voting or averaging [51]. This method reduces the variance of individual decision trees, thereby improving the overall robustness and accuracy of the model. RF is particularly suitable for handling high-dimensional datasets and complex feature interactions, as it randomly selects a subset of features for splitting during the training process, enhancing the model’s diversity and ability to prevent overfitting. This model demonstrates superior performance in predicting student suicide risk levels and psychological flexibility levels, among other mental health indicators [52, 53].

(2) extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)

XGBoost is an algorithm based on the Boosting idea, which enhances the overall model performance by iteratively correcting the prediction errors of the previous model [54]. In its implementation, XGBoost introduces regularization and parallel processing, enabling it to remain efficient when dealing with large-scale data and complex models. It can automatically handle missing values, support a variety of loss functions, and has strong anti-overfitting characteristics, making it a powerful tool for solving nonlinear regression and classification problems. In the training process, it uses level-wise decision trees, splitting all nodes at each iteration. This model demonstrates superior performance in predicting the mental health and stress levels of college students and healthcare workers [55, 56].

(3) light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM)

LightGBM is an algorithm based on the Boosting idea. Its core concept is the leaf-wise decision tree algorithm based on histograms. This means that at each step, it selects the leaf node with the maximum gain for splitting. This strategy significantly improves training speed and memory efficiency [57]. LightGBM model demonstrates remarkable accuracy in predicting psychological issues such as anxiety levels during pregnancy and dementia in the elderly [58, 59].

(4) support vector regression (SVR)

SVR is an extension of Support Vector Machines (SVMs) for regression problems, specifically designed to predict continuous variables. By incorporating an ε-insensitive loss function and kernel functions, it seeks the optimal regression hyperplane within a permissible error range, thereby balancing model fitting and generalization. Its core advantages lie in its robustness against noise and outliers and its suitability for high-dimensional, small-sample data. This model was applied to predict the international roughness index for second-tier cities, achieving an explanatory variance score of 0.91 [60].

Its value ranges from 0 to 1, where a higher value indicates that the model can better explain the data variance. MSLE calculates the average of the squared differences between the logarithmic transformations of the predicted and actual values, emphasizing relative changes rather than absolute differences. It pays more attention to predictions of small values and imposes less penalty on prediction errors of large values.

Results

Descriptive statistics

To assess potential biases from sample attrition, we compared demographic variables (gender, grade level) and anxiety scores across valid questionnaire respondents with IDs (N = 2368) and the final active user sample (N = 1316). Despite statistically significant differences in anxiety scores between the final sample and the dropout sample (p < 0.05), the actual effect size was extremely small (Cohen’s d = 0.12). Moreover, the distribution trends of all key variables remained consistent. Specifically, the final sample exhibited slightly higher anxiety tendencies, while retention rates were relatively higher among third- and fourth-year students. This indicates that although the sample exhibits a slight statistically significant attrition bias, its overall structure remains largely consistent with the original sample. Therefore, sample attrition has a limited substantive impact on the inferences drawn from the primary research findings.

We conducted statistical analyses of the collected demographic and basic information features. Interpretive analysis

Based on the analysis in Sect. 4.2, the RF model was identified as the best-performing model. We conducted a SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) interpretability analysis on the predictions made by the RF model. SHAP is a method used to explain the predictions of machine learning models. SHAP values indicate the contribution of each feature to the model’s predictions, similar to the Shapley values in game theory, which fairly allocate the contribution of each feature to the model’s output [61].

We first analyzed the contribution of multi-class features, as shown in Fig. 3. Each bar represents the average impact of a feature on the output of the RF model. The x-axis indicates the average impact of the feature values on the model output, with larger values indicating a greater influence of the feature on the model output. The y-axis lists the various features, including Grade, Emotion, Curiosity, and 15 other features. We found that Grade has the greatest impact on the model output.Professionalism and Emotion also have significant effects. Other features, such as Total words, Risk, and Question Value, have varying degrees of influence on the model output.

SHAP feature contribution

To conduct a more detailed analysis of the contribution of each feature value to anxiety, we calculated the SHAP explanation values corresponding to the anxiety, as shown in Fig. 4. Each row represents a feature, and the x-axis indicates the SHAP value. The features are ranked according to the average absolute value of SHAP, which indicates the most important features for the model. Wide bars indicate a large number of samples clustered together. Each dot represents a single sample. More intense red colors indicate higher feature values, while more intense blue colors indicate lower feature values.

SHAP Interpreted Values for Anxiety

In Fig. 4, we found that different features exert significantly different degrees of influence on model outcomes. Features such as Grade and Emotion play pivotal roles in model decision-making, exerting substantial impact with multidimensional effects—capable of both positively driving results and potentially inducing negative suppression. Features like Professionalism and Exclamation predominantly exhibit positive influence trends; higher values typically prompt favorable shifts in model outputs. Conversely, features like Risk and Question predominantly exhibit negative influence characteristics. When these features are at higher levels, they often constrain model outcomes. The Curiosity feature shows instability in its direction of influence, exhibiting uncertainty. Features such as Gender contribute relatively little to the model’s decision-making process, exerting a limited impact on results.

Discussion and implications

Key findings

This study demonstrates that cross-sectional anxiety levels among college students, as measured by the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), are associated with patterns observed in social media footprints and can be statistically estimated within this study sample. By linking 2,368 validated survey responses with 56,060 Weibo posts from 1,316 consenting students across 11 universities, we constructed a multidimensional feature set integrating linguistic, behavioral, and psychological signals. Among four benchmarked models, Random Forest achieved the highest predictive performance (R² = 0.77, RMSE = 13.41, MAPE = 25.94%, MSLE = 0.087). SHAP-based interpretation identified grade level, emotion, professionalism-related terms, risk terms, and question-mark use as prominent model-contributing features for predicted SAS scores.

This work extends prior research in several ways. Traditional studies rely heavily on self-administered questionnaires, which are time-consuming and prone to bias [62], while digital phenotyping efforts such as the Student Life dataset primarily capture behavioral rhythms through sensors but rarely connect these data to standardized psychological measures [63, 64]. We address these limitations by grounding digital signals in validated psychometrics, and expanding feature design to include stance cues, curiosity indices, and clinically relevant symptom lexicons beyond sentiment alone [65]. While deep learning approaches often function as opaque “black boxes” [66], our method may be useful as a research framework for studying anxiety-related digital signals in consenting university samples. Furthermore, given privacy and governance concerns associated with generative LLM-based tools [36], our pipeline is lightweight, transparent, and privacy-conscious, making it a useful research framework for studying anxiety-related digital signals in university populations.

Theoretical and practical implications

Theoretically, our findings extend digital phenotyping in information science by demonstrating that online behavioral rhythms (e.g., posting frequency, late-night activity) and linguistic markers (e.g., negative-emotion words, self-referential language) jointly capture individual variation in anxiety when anchored to a validated psychometric instrument. The integration of transparent models with SHAP-based explanations further translates predictive patterns into testable propositions concerning emotional expression, curiosity toward novel topics, stance cues, and stress-related symptom lexicons, thereby reinforcing the theoretical bridge between computational signals and established psychological constructs.

Practically, the proposed pipeline provides an exploratory framework for examining associations between students’ self-reported anxiety levels and patterns in their social media activity. Compared with prior studies, this work makes several distinctive contributions. First, it links Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) scores with individual social media traces collected across multiple institutions, providing a richer and more diverse empirical basis for analysis. Second, it extends feature engineering beyond conventional sentiment measures by incorporating curiosity indices, stance detection, and medically informed symptom dictionaries. Finally, the study emphasizes model interpretability by identifying features that are associated with elevated anxiety scores. Rather than supporting clinical diagnosis or operational screening, the framework is intended as a research tool to understand potential behavioral correlates of anxiety-related self-reports and to inform future studies on explainable mental health assessment using social media data.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations that point to directions for future research. First, the final analysis sample is limited to those college students who are willing to provide their social media IDs and remain publicly active. These students may have a higher tendency toward self-disclosure or more regular social media use, and cannot represent those who do not frequently use Weibo or are inactive ‘lurkers’. Therefore, the study’s conclusions should be generalized with extreme caution to the entire college student population and to less active or private users. Future research should attempt to obtain a more comprehensive sampling frame or use statistical methods such as weighting to try to correct the bias. Second, the sample consists exclusively of undergraduate students in mainland China, which constrains population diversity and limits generalizability beyond this cohort and context. The present corpus relies on a single platform, Weibo; subsequent studies will expand the platform ecology to include Douyin and Bilibili within China and, in each international setting, locally prevalent services such as X, Reddit, Instagram, YouTube, and TikTok. The analysis is monolingual in Chinese; upcoming research will pursue cross-lingual and cross-cultural designs by standardizing feature definitions, curating parallel lexicons and annotation guidelines, and testing measurement invariance so that observed differences reflect substantive phenomena rather than artifacts of language or platform choice. Furthermore, due to data privacy restrictions and the sensitivity of the datasets, opportunities to obtain external datasets with matching psychometric labels are extremely limited. Consequently, external validation cannot be conducted at this time. In future research, we will strive to expand the sources and scale of our datasets to enable comprehensive validation of the model. Finally, while this study used the SAS scale to measure anxiety levels only once, college students’ anxiety levels may fluctuate across different stages of the semester, such as the beginning, middle, and end. Therefore, the model predicts cross-sectional anxiety scores rather than long-term or causal anxiety states.

Conclusion

This study makes several important contributions. For the first time, this study proposed to mine special feature data of emotions, cognition, physical symptoms, and behaviors from online data, constructing a dataset that includes 15 features. Secondly, we designed 4 machine learning algorithms and evaluated their performance using 4 evaluation metrics, achieving a coefficient of determination of 0.77, which enhances the accuracy of predicting cross-sectional anxiety scores among college students. Furthermore, SHAP analysis identified model-contributing features associated with predicted SAS scores. These findings may inform future research on anxiety-related digital patterns among consenting students with public social-media activity.
