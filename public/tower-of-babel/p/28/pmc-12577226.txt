Post-COVID-19 resurgence of respiratory viruses in Taiwan: an interrupted time series analysis from three regional medical centers (2015–2023)
Authors: Tu-Hsuan Chang, Ya-Li Hu, Wan-Chen Chen, Chi-Tai Fang, Shu-Yuan Ho, Tai-Fen Lee, Yu-Tsung Huang, Hung-Jen Tang, Luan-Yin Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic and associated nonpharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) profoundly disrupted the transmission dynamics of respiratory viruses worldwide. However, the long-term impact on multiple respiratory pathogens, particularly in Taiwan, remains unclear.MethodsWe conducted an interrupted time series analysis using negative binomial regression to assess monthly trends of five respiratory viruses—adenovirus, enterovirus, influenza, parainfluenza virus (PIV) and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)—on the basis of data collected from three tertiary medical centers in Taiwan (2015–2023). The study period was divided into three phases: pre-COVID-19 (2015–2019), during COVID-19 (2020–2022), and post-COVID-19 (2023). Negative binomial regression with seasonal adjustment and lag variables was used to assess virus-specific trends. Percentage changes in case numbers were calculated.ResultsA total of 60,368 virus cases were identified. During the COVID-19 period, significant decreases in the number of adenovirus (-31.6%), enterovirus (-67.1%), and influenza (-85.0%) cases were observed. Postpandemic, the number of enterovirus and influenza cases rebounded sharply, with increases of 499.3% and 619.5%, respectively. PIV and adenovirus cases also increased by 223.8% and 151.2%, respectively. In contrast, the number of RSV cases did not significantly change during the study period. However, RSV exhibited altered seasonal patterns and atypical peaks during and after the pandemic.ConclusionThe COVID-19 pandemic led to substantial disruptions in respiratory virus circulation in Taiwan, followed by divergent rebound patterns postpandemic. Our findings highlight the complex interplay between public health interventions and viral epidemiology. Continued surveillance is vital for understanding future transmission patterns and informing preparedness strategies.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11930-2.

Introduction

The widespread implementation of nonpharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) during the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic led to a marked decline in respiratory virus activity [1–3]. Epidemiological data revealed a significant reduction in influenza circulation and the disruption of the seasonal patterns of various respiratory viruses [4]. NPIs also contributed to a global decline in respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) cases [5]. Similarly, other respiratory viruses, including metapneumovirus, parainfluenza virus (PIV), and human coronavirus, have exhibited substantial decreases in transmission [6].

As NPIs were gradually lifted, respiratory virus activity rebounded, often with altered seasonal patterns and increased intensity. In Taiwan, there has been a resurgence of influenza and RSV infections, in some cases surpassing prepandemic levels [7, 8]. Similarly, enteroviruses re-emerged with atypical seasonal peaks, suggesting shifts in viral epidemiology [7]. This rebound highlights the complex interplay between population immunity, viral transmission dynamics, and public health interventions in shaping postpandemic respiratory virus trends.

While previous studies have examined the resurgence of individual respiratory viruses following the relaxation of NPIs, few have provided a comprehensive analysis of multiple respiratory pathogens over an extended period. To address this gap, the aim of our study was to investigate the ongoing trends of adenovirus, enterovirus, influenza virus, PIV, and RSV transmission in Taiwan, covering a broader range of respiratory pathogens and an extended timeline through the end of 2023. By analyzing the long-term epidemiological patterns of these viruses, we sought to provide insights into their postpandemic circulation dynamics.

Method

Study design

In the present study, we explored the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on respiratory tract infections by analyzing data across three distinct periods. In Taiwan, the first imported COVID-19 case was reported in January 2020, coinciding with the implementation of strict border controls. By 2023, with increased vaccine coverage and the relaxation of mask-wearing policies, the landscape shifted significantly [9, 10]. On the basis of this timeline, we divided our study into the following three periods:

Statistical analysis

The laboratory results were classified by pathogen type, and changes in respiratory pathogen numbers were evaluated across three periods: before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic. An interrupted time series analysis was performed to assess variations in the identified virus numbers across these intervals.

The dataset was structured as time series data, with monthly case counts serving as the dependent variable, following established practice and relevant studies [13, 14]. To account for seasonal variations, sinusoidal terms (sine and cosine transformations) were incorporated to model recurring seasonal patterns. We used this approach because it is a standard, parsimonious Serfling-type specification for annual respiratory seasonality and avoids the over-parameterization that month-indicator models can introduce [14]. A first-order lag variable was created to adjust for autocorrelation and to account for the influence of the number of cases during the prior month on current observations. To evaluate the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, two types of intervention variables were included in the model. First, binary step indicators were used to define the pandemic period (beginning in January 2020) and the postpandemic period (beginning in January 2023). Second, time-since-intervention variables were generated to capture changes in trends during and after the pandemic, allowing the model to account for evolving effects across time.

Because the monthly virus case counts exhibited overdispersion (variance substantially exceeding the mean), we applied a negative binomial regression model, which appropriately accounts for this distributional property. The model included terms for time, the two step-change and trend-change intervention variables, seasonal components, and the lagged dependent variable. The model can be expressed as:

This transformation allowed the estimated effects to be expressed as relative increases or decreases in case counts. The predicted number of cases were generated for all the time points. To visualize the modeled trends, the predicted values were stratified by period and plotted alongside the observed case counts. Model verification consisted of overlay plots of observed and model-predicted monthly counts and correlograms of residuals (lags 1–12). The absence of statistically significant residual autocorrelation indicated adequate time-series specification. For series with extreme outbreak peaks (e.g., influenza, adenovirus, parainfluenza), we applied virus-specific upper winsorization thresholds (influenza 1,000, adenovirus 250, and parainfluenza 120) to stabilize the fit. As a sensitivity analysis, we varied these thresholds by ± 20% and refit the same models; the sign and inference of intervention effects were unchanged, so we retained the reported value as the preferred thresholds.

Adenovirus

Figure 1 illustrates the fluctuations in the number of adenovirus cases across the three studied periods. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, adenovirus cases gradually increased, peaking in 2018. During the pandemic, a significant reduction in the number of adenovirus cases was observed, decreasing by 31.6% overall (95% CI: 2.6% to 52.0%; p = 0.035), with a monthly decline of 4.6% (95% CI: 3.1% to 6.2%; p < 0.001). Postpandemic, adenovirus cases rebounded significantly, increasing by 151.2% overall (95% CI: 44.6% to 336.3%; p = 0.009), with a monthly growth of 10.9% (95% CI: 2.6% to 19.9%; p = 0.009).

Enterovirus

Figure 2 shows the changes in the number of enterovirus cases across the three periods studied. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, the number of enterovirus cases exhibited a consistent seasonal trend, peaking during the warmer months (April through October). During the pandemic, the number of enterovirus cases decreased significantly, by 67.1% overall (95% CI: 38.4% to 82.4%; p = 0.001). Additionally, a monthly decline of 3.0% (95% CI: 0.16% to 5.66%; p = 0.038) was observed. Postpandemic, a marked recovery in the number of enterovirus cases was recorded, with a total increase of 499.3% (95% CI: 130.0% to 1,461.5%; p < 0.001). The monthly growth during this period was 2.8% (95% CI: -7.9% to 14.8%; p = 0.623), although this increase was not statistically significant.

Influenza virus

Figure 3 illustrates the trends in the number of influenza virus cases across the three periods analyzed. Before the COVID-19 pandemic, the number of influenza cases followed regular seasonal patterns, with substantial peaks observed during the colder months (December to February). During the pandemic, the number of influenza cases experienced a significant decline, decreasing by 85.0% overall (95% CI: 74.3% to 91.2%; p < 0.001). Given the near-zero number of cases during this period, the monthly decline of 0.94% (95% CI: -3.0% to 1.1%; p = 0.368) was not statistically significant. Postpandemic, the number of influenza cases demonstrated a substantial recovery, increasing by 619.5% overall (95% CI: 186.3% to 1,708.4%; p = 0.008). However, the monthly growth rate of 0.11% (95% CI: -13.0% to 15.2%; p = 0.988) remained statistically nonsignificant.

PIV

Figure 4 illustrates the trends in the number of PIV cases across the three analyzed periods. Before the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, the number of PIV cases exhibited consistent seasonal fluctuations, with modest annual peaks. During the pandemic, the number of PIV cases decreased by 4.8% (95% CI: -47.2% to 71.9%; p = 0.871), although this change was not statistically significant. RSV

Figure 5 illustrates the trends in the number of RSV cases across pandemic periods. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, the number of RSV cases exhibited a consistent seasonal peak in autumn, which shifted to winter during and after the pandemic. During the pandemic, the number of RSV cases decreased, declining by 36.8% overall (95% CI: -74.1% to 54.3%; p = 0.314) without statistical significance. Additionally, a monthly increase of 1.3% (95% CI: -2.2% to 4.9%; p = 0.471) was observed during this period, although this increase was also nonsignificant. In the postpandemic period, the number of RSV cases exhibited signs of recovery, with an observed increase in the number of cases. However, no statistically significant trends were detected for either the overall change in the number of cases or the monthly growth rate during this period.

Discussion

Our study provides a comprehensive analysis of the epidemiological trends of commonly circulating respiratory viruses in Taiwan, highlighting the various impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on their transmission dynamics. We observed significant disruptions in virus circulation during the pandemic, with sharp declines in adenovirus, enterovirus, and influenza cases, followed by a substantial postpandemic resurgence. In contrast, PIV and RSV exhibited distinct patterns, with the number of parainfluenza cases increasing after the pandemic and the number of RSV cases showing no statistically significant changes.

The number of adenovirus cases exhibited an increasing trend before the pandemic, largely driven by a widespread outbreak from 2018 to 2019, a pattern also observed in other countries [15, 16]. During the COVID-19 period, the number of adenovirus cases significantly decreased, but there were still a relatively high number of adenovirus cases compared with the number of cases of other respiratory viruses in our analysis, with fewer fluctuations. This stability may be attributed to the nonenveloped structure of adenovirus, which allows it to be more resistant to environmental factors and disinfection measures [17]. Consistent with global observations from the United States and Germany, adenovirus circulation persisted despite stringent interventions [18, 19]. Regionally, South Korea reported a rapid rebound to prepandemic levels after restrictions eased [20].

Enteroviruses and influenza viruses exhibited similar epidemiological patterns in Taiwan, with well-defined seasonal circulation before the COVID-19 pandemic [21]. However, following the implementation of strict NPIs, both viruses experienced a sharp and sustained decline in activity. Unlike RSV and adenovirus, which showed more variable trends, influenza and enterovirus remained at historically low levels throughout the pandemic. This trend in Taiwan contrasts with global observations, where influenza activity nearly disappeared in many regions due to NPIs, whereas enteroviruses and rhinoviruses remained active in some countries (e.g., Finland and Australia) [22–24] Their persistence elsewhere may be attributed to differences in restriction strength, compliance, and transmission routes. Some researchers have proposed using the number of respiratory enterovirus cases as a sentinel indicator of NPI effectiveness and stringency [25]. Taiwan’s strict and prolonged public health measures likely contributed to the simultaneous suppression of both influenza and enterovirus, a pattern rarely observed in other countries.

The PIV exhibited a unique circulation pattern during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. While the overall number of cases remained relatively stable after 2020, there was a notable shift in seasonal prevalence from spring to winter, with a substantial increase in cases observed in 2023. Although PIV detection methods shifted predominantly to PCR testing after 2020, this alone does not fully explain the surge in cases. Similar trends have been reported in Asia, where intermittent COVID-19 outbreaks significantly altered the seasonal distribution of PIV [26]. In southern China and Korea, PIV detection rates increased during the COVID-19 pandemic, whereas in Scotland, PIV prevalence declined during the peak of the pandemic, followed by at least a partial resurgence [27–29]. These regional discrepancies likely reflect the complex interplay between viral epidemiology, population immunity, and various public health interventions.

RSV exhibited a distinct pattern in Taiwan, differing from the trends observed for other respiratory viruses. According to our model, the overall trend was not significantly affected by the COVID-19 pandemic. However, its circulation pattern deviated from that in previous years, with a significant loss of seasonality and out-of-season activity [8, 30]. In Europe, surveillance data showed a near-complete suppression of RSV in winter 2020–2021, followed by a gradual increase and an early, intense peak in 2021–2022, with seasonality not yet normalized by 2022-2023 [31]. In China, the usual spring RSV peak was absent in 2020, and the epidemic season persisted for an unprecedented 87 weeks into 2022 [32]. The pandemic likely created an immunity gap, where reduced RSV exposure led to a larger pool of susceptible individuals, particularly young children [33, 34]. Consequently, postpandemic RSV outbreaks have been more prolonged and have occurred outside the usual peak seasons. This irregular circulation pattern raises concerns about potential long-term shifts in epidemiology. Continuous surveillance is necessary to determine whether the RSV will gradually return to its prepandemic seasonal trend or establish a new transmission pattern.

This study has several limitations. First, the study relies on multiple diagnostic techniques, including PCR, antigen-based assays, and viral isolation, which varied across hospitals and over time. We lacked test denominators (numbers tested), precluding positivity-rate adjustment or full control for testing intensity. Accordingly, part of the post-2022 increases, especially for PIV and RSV, may reflect greater detection sensitivity with PCR rather than true incidence. Nevertheless, similar pandemic-related declines and 2023 rebounds were observed even where the primary method changed little, suggesting the temporal patterns are not solely method-driven. Our inferences therefore emphasize within-series changes over time rather than cross-center level comparisons, and unmeasured shifts in clinical testing practices may remain. Second, the lack of individual-level demographic and clinical data (e.g., age, comorbidities, vaccination status) limits further subgroup analysis. Third, as the data were derived from tertiary medical centers, the findings may not fully reflect community-level trends. Fourth, changes in public health policies (e.g., masking, school closures) may have affected viral circulation, but quantifying their impact was beyond the scope of this study. Finally, the viral subtypes were not distinguished, which may have led to shifts in strain-specific dynamics being overlooked.

Conclusions

In the present study, we highlighted the significant disruptions in the prevalence of certain viruses caused by NPIs and the various patterns of resurgence across different viruses. While adenovirus, enterovirus, and influenza exhibited sharp declines followed by strong rebounds, RSV and PIV showed more variable or delayed trends. These results indicate the complex interplay between public health measures and viral transmission dynamics. Continued surveillance is essential to monitor evolving virus transmission patterns and guide future preparedness strategies.
