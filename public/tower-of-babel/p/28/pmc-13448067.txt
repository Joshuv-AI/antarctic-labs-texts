White-box modeling of asphaltene precipitation during natural depletion of oil reservoirs
Authors: Sara Sahebalzamani, Mohsen Mohammadi, Ghazal Piroozi, Fahimeh Hadavimoghaddam, Abdolhossein Hemmati-Sarapardeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Asphaltene precipitation and deposition pose significant challenges in the oil industry, leading to well blockage, formation damage, impairment of process equipment, and reduced reservoir permeability, which ultimately affect oil production and economic efficiency. Traditional laboratory-based methods for detecting asphaltene precipitation are often costly and time-consuming, emphasizing the need for rapid and reliable predictive techniques. This study develops predictive models for asphaltene precipitation using three white-box machine learning algorithms: Group Method of Data Handling (GMDH), Gene Expression Programming (GEP), and Genetic Programming (GP). The models were trained and validated based on a dataset of 308 laboratory measurements from 25 types of crude oils, considering relevant parameters influencing model performance. Their predictive capability was benchmarked against a thermodynamically consistent cubic-plus-association (CPA) equation of state developed in this work. Statistical evaluations confirmed the reliability of the developed models. Among the correlations, GMDH demonstrated the best performance, achieving the correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.858 and a root-mean-square error (RMSE) of 0.171. GMDH delivers quick forecasts for new cases without requiring further tuning, while still accurately predicting asphaltene precipitation. Sensitivity analysis revealed that pressure (P), oil °API gravity (°API), and bubble point pressure (Pb) exhibit the strongest negative correlations with asphaltene precipitation, indicating that the highest risk occurs during P drops near the Pb and in heavier crude oils with lower °API values. Finally, Leverage-based outlier analysis confirmed the reliability of the dataset, with 97.080% of data points within the valid region, reinforcing the credibility of both experimental measurements and predictive models.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55015-9.

Introduction

Crude oil consists of hydrocarbons with various structures containing saturates, aromatics, resins, and asphaltenes (SARA)1. Asphaltenes are the heaviest and most polar fraction of crude oil. They are being soluble in aromatic solvents such as toluene and benzene, but insoluble in low molecular weight alkanes, namely normal pentane and normal heptane2–4. Although asphaltene particles suspended in crude oil are not inherently problematic, their precipitation and deposition during the production can pose significant challenges5. Asphaltene becomes problematic when it precipitates and deposits on solid surfaces, such as reservoir rock, flowlines, production tubing, and surface facilities. This deposition can cause serious operational challenges affecting production, storage, transport, and refining processes6. Therefore, understanding the conditions under which asphaltene particles precipitate from the solution, as well as quantifying the amount of precipitated /deposited asphaltene, is essential for optimizing production7. Determining asphaltene phase behavior experimentally is often costly, complex, and requires considerable time. As a result, numerous researchers have focused on developing predictive models of asphaltene phase behavior8–14. These models are generally divided into two main categories: colloidal and solubility models15. The concept that asphaltenes exist in oil as colloids was initially introduced by Nellensteyn16 and later developed by Pfeiffer and Saal17,18. In this model, asphaltenes are treated as solid particles that combine with resins to form micelles. The polar parts of asphaltenes stay in the center while resins attach through their polar side, dissolving in oil via their non-polar side. Precipitation occurs when the resin layer falls below a critical level18. This colloidal approach has been employed in all the referenced studies14,19–22. According to the solubility model, asphaltenes are dissolved uniformly in crude oil, forming a homogeneous solution when their interactions with other oil components are considered. Among thermodynamic approaches, the solubility parameter method is the most widely used for analyzing the solubility behavior of asphaltenes. A larger difference between the solubility parameters of asphaltene and the solvent increases the probability of precipitation. Models based on regular solution theory, Flory–Huggins, and Scott-Magat theories fall into this category. While, the first two assume asphaltenes are homogeneous, the Scott-Magat assumes a heterogeneous structure18,23–27. Li and Firoozabadi6 pointed out that these models divide crude oil into only two pseudo-components, asphaltene and solvent, which is an oversimplification. Moreover, they noted that such models cannot operate independently at pressures below the bubble point (Pb). To address this, vapor–liquid equilibrium (VLE) calculations must first be performed using an equation of state (EoS), followed by application of the solubility model to evaluate the potential for asphaltene precipitation from the liquid phase.

The assumption that the asphaltenes precipitation does not influence the VLE behavior is not entirely accurate. In order to resolve these deficiencies, EoS were applied by researchers to forecast asphaltene phase behavior. Cubic equations of state (CEoS) were employed by Sabbagh28, Díaz29, and Agrawal30 to model the precipitation of asphaltenes. Although CEoS is simple and widely used, Abutaqiya et al.31 proposed an innovative characterization approach for the Peng-Robinson (PR) EoS. As per their perspective, one of the main reasons for the low predictive accuracy of CEoS is the use of inadequate characterization methods. To overcome the limitations of CEoS in modeling asphaltene phase behavior, the perturbed-chain statistical associating fluid theory (PC-SAFT) EoS can be applied. Tavakkoli et al.32 developed a model for asphaltene precipitation in three live oil samples using the PC-SAFT EoS. They employed a polydisperse approach when characterizing the asphaltene in one of the samples, thereby incorporating several asphaltene pseudo-components. The individual asphaltene subfractions differ in their molecular weights and aromaticities, yet their binary interaction coefficients with other oil constituents remain the same. The model accurately predicted Pb and asphaltene onset pressure (AOP), but struggled to precisely determine the amount of precipitated asphaltene. Zhang et al.33 utilized two EoS, cubic-plus-association (CPA) and PC-SAFT, to model asphaltene precipitation across a wide range of temperatures and pressures. They investigated the effect of gas injection on six live oil samples. In their study, they demonstrated that the CPA EoS, as proposed by Firoozabadi et al.6,27, provides excellent predictions for asphaltene precipitation and also for the impact of gas injection on asphaltene stability. A comparison between the two models revealed that the results obtained from the CPA EoS are significantly more accurate than those from PC-SAFT34, making it suitable for industrial applications. Shirani et al.35 predicted asphaltene precipitation in three live oil samples using the CPA EoS. Both the EoS parameters and the binary interaction coefficients between asphaltene and other components were tuned using experimental data on the weight percent of precipitated asphaltene. For calculation simplicity, they performed a VLE instead of a three-phase vapor–liquid-liquid equilibrium (VLLE) for pressures below the Pb, assuming that asphaltene did not affect the vapor phase. Arya et al.36 developed a new approach using the CPA EoS to ascertain the asphaltene precipitation onset in diverse reservoir fluid samples, differentiating their method from prior techniques. Their approach involved a straightforward oil characterization method, consistent with SARA analysis. This method helped them classify the oil plus fraction into asphaltene and a heavy component. Additionally, they considered both asphaltene self-association and the cross-association of asphaltene with heavy components. One of the major drawbacks of EoS-based models is the necessity for comprehensive fluid characterization, often encompassing a range up to heavier components like C30+. Another challenge lies in the convergence of these models, particularly given the polydisperse nature of asphaltene. Besides, common thermodynamic models can not accurately predict asphaltene precipitation unless they are37,38. On the other hand, researchers have shown that Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) and mathematical correlations have demonstrated acceptable accuracy in prediction of the asphaltene phase behavior39. For instance, Hemmati-Sarapardeh et al.40 predicted asphaltene precipitation using 157 sets of experimental data from 32 different crude oils. They employed intelligent models such as Least-Squares Support Vector Machine (LSSVM), ANN, and ANN optimized by Particle Swarm Optimization (ANN-PSO). These models not only accurately simulated the actual physical relationship between pressure changes and asphaltene precipitation but also achieved an average absolute percent relative error (AAPRE) of 18.690%.

In another study by Hemmati-Sarapardeh et al.41, Radial Basis Function (RBF) and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) neural networks were used to predict asphaltene precipitation based on 123 data points from 24 different crude oil samples. These models were trained with various optimization algorithms, including Levenberg–Marquardt (LM), Bayesian Regularization (BR), Imperialist Competitive Algorithm (ICA), and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO). The results indicated that the RBF method optimized with PSO and the MLP method optimized with BR provided the best performance, with AAPRE of 18.750% and 26.770%, respectively. Recently, Amiri Ramsheh et al.42 applied intelligent models, including Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Cascade Forward Neural Network (CFNN), and Generalized Regression Neural Network (GRNN), to estimate asphaltene precipitation. Among these, the developed LightGBM technique demonstrated superior performance, yielding the lowest AAPRE of 8.570% and the highest correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.990. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no universal and accurate model based on a large database has yet been developed to predict asphaltene precipitation in oil reservoirs. Moreover, most existing models are black-box in nature, making it virtually impossible to establish a clear relationship between inputs and outputs. In addition, these models often suffer from limited data, insufficient accuracy, and a lack of universality.

To address these challenges, the present study introduces three novel mathematical correlations for predicting asphaltene precipitation, developed using advanced correlations: the Group Method of Data Handling (GMDH), Gene Expression Programming (GEP), and Genetic Programming (GP). These models are trained and validated using a comprehensive dataset of 308 laboratory measurements of asphaltene precipitation, incorporating key input parameters including pressure (P), temperature (T), Pb, oil °API gravity (°API), and SARA fraction all carefully selected to optimize predictive performance.

The primary novelty of this research stems from its systematic benchmarking of proposed white-box correlations against a thermodynamically consistent CPA equation of state, developed within this study. This comparative framework not only highlights the significant improvements in predictive performance and computational efficiency offered over existing methodologies but also leverages the inherent interpretability and ease of application of these white-box models, distinguishing them from conventional black-box approaches. By integrating physics-based principles with data-driven insights, this work provides robust predictions and advances the understanding of asphaltene precipitation modeling. Methodology

Data collection

Each model incorporated its own intelligent feature-selection approach, improving predictive accuracy while keeping model complexity low. Consequently, the models were not trained on identical input sets; instead, each used the variables deemed optimal for its specific strategy. The selection of the GMDH, GEP, and GP algorithms was motivated by their strong capability in symbolic regression and nonlinear system identification. Asphaltene precipitation is governed by complex thermodynamic interactions and multivariable dependencies involving P, T, Pb, °API, and compositional fractions, which exhibit highly nonlinear behavior. Conventional interpretable models, such as linear regression or shallow decision-tree approaches, are generally insufficient to capture such nonlinear and interaction-driven relationships. In contrast, evolutionary-based white-box algorithms are capable of automatically discovering nonlinear functional structures without requiring predefinition of the mathematical form, while still generating explicit analytical correlations. This characteristic enables direct implementation of the derived equations in engineering calculations and reservoir simulations, thereby combining predictive accuracy with interpretability. Furthermore, these methods are particularly suitable for moderate-sized datasets, where deep learning techniques may introduce unnecessary complexity or risk of overfitting.

The reliability of white-box models is strongly dependent on the quality of the training data. In this work, 308 experimental datasets related to asphaltene precipitation were compiled from 25 different crude oil samples, with data obtained from several scientific publications34,40,43–48. Distinct groups of input variables were used to construct each model. The GMDH model was trained using a unique set of parameters, including P, Pb, °API, aromatic, resin, and asphaltene. The GP model relied on a different combination consisting of P, T, Pb, °API, saturate, aromatic, and asphaltene. In contrast, the GEP model adopted a more streamlined input set, using only P, T, saturate, resin, and asphaltene. Description of the white-box model algorithms

Group Method of Data Handling (GMDH)

The GMDH, known as the Polynomial Neural Network (PNN), is considered a powerful tool for solving complex nonlinear problems. The proposed data-driven algorithm provides explicit and accurate correlations between inputs and outputs, effectively addressing the complexity and nonlinearity inherent in such networks52. The GMDH features a layered architecture where quadratic polynomials in each layer combine to form a new set of neurons in the subsequent layer. This integration occurs through functions that connect distinct neurons, leading to the creation of new ones. A quadratic polynomial function serves to link two distinct neurons, with the result being the formation of new nodes for the next layer53. Genetic programming (GP)

GP, introduced by John Koza in 199456, is recognized as a powerful and significant branch within evolutionary algorithms. Inspired by natural evolution, this method generates computer programs randomly and then optimizes them to find the best solutions for various problems57. The primary goal of GP, built upon the genetic algorithm, is to achieve accurate networks and correlations. By identifying and combining effective subexpressions within a program, GP constructs an extensive network whose primary goal is to optimize the correspondence between inputs and target values58. This approach is considered a "white-box" technique, meaning its internal processes are interpretable and understandable by engineers and scientists, a key differentiator from many "black-box" methods59. In GP, an initial population of random functions is established to serve as chromosomes that will operate on a dataset60. During computations, both the network’s architecture is built and its parameters are optimized. These chromosomes form the next population, and these iterations (generations) continue until a stopping criterion is met61. Gene expression programming (GEP)

GEP, first introduced by Ferreira62, is a powerful machine learning (ML) for symbolic regression63. It is an advanced version of GP that overcomes some of its limitations. One of its prominent advantages is that it does not require the pre-definition of a specific functional form to obtain the best estimation of real measurements64. GEP is a complete solution for analyzing genotype and phenotype, combining the ease of use found in genetic algorithms and the strong performance of genetic programming65. GEP operates on two main components: chromosomes and expression trees (ETs). The chromosomes identify initial candidate solutions, which are then translated into ETs through a process inspired by DNA-to-protein translation, providing more accurate solutions66. Figure 6 shows the simple flowchart for this technique. The first step in this algorithm is to establish a starting population of solutions, which you can create through random assignment or by including relevant input information for the problem67. In fact, GEP leverages fixed-length linear chromosomes as well as expressive parse trees, which are diverse in shape and size68. Since chromosomes consist of numerous genes, the performance of each chromosome in the initial population is first evaluated, and its fitness is then determined using a fitness function. In fact, those chromosomes that show higher efficiency will have a greater chance of being passed on to the next generation. After being selected, they get reproduced, and a genetic operator introduces modifications62.Fig. 6Flowchart for the GEP algorithm.

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method

Lundberg and Lee69 introduced the SHAP method to provide explanations for the outputs of ML models. SHAP is an additive explanation model rooted in cooperative game theory. As the name "Shapley Additive Explanation" implies, it operates under the principle that all features act as contributors to the prediction70. The SHAP value is a measure of the impact of an individual feature on the model’s prediction for a specific instance71. For instance, in a GMDH model defined for a group N with n features, which predicts an output for N, the SHAP method determines each feature’s contribution to the model’s output, v(N), by calculating its marginal contribution. Thermodynamic models

CPA EoS

The CPA-EoS integrates the ease-of-use of a CEoS with an association (chemical) term based on Wertheim theory, mirroring the approach of the SAFT model72. This model separates molecular interactions into two distinct areas: the physical part covers physical interactions, and the association part is dedicated to chemical interactions. The molecules of asphaltene have a strong tendency to self-associate and interact with other components, ultimately forming large aggregates. The self-association of asphaltene molecules is the main cause of asphaltene deposition. This tendency for the molecules to bind to one another causes them to precipitate, forming the new precipitation phase. Several studies have viewed asphaltene as a solid phase12,79,80. Based on research by Firoozabadi81, the asphaltenes that precipitate at reservoir temperatures have characteristics closer to liquids than to solids. Consistent with many previous studies4,15,32, this research models the precipitated asphaltene phase as a second liquid phase. Thus, the thermodynamic modeling of asphaltene precipitation in live crude oils may require two kinds of phase equilibrium calculations according to the pressure conditions. Fluids characterization

To predict the phase behavior of crude oil using EoS, the oil must be characterized as a set of pure and pseudo-components. For this purpose, the method presented by Li and Firoozabadi27 is employed. In this characterization, the reservoir fluid is divided into three categories: pure components, hydrocarbon pseudo-components, and hydrocarbon residue. The hydrocarbon residue is then further subdivided into heavy components and asphaltenes. All alkanes, aromatics, and resins that are not classified as pure components are grouped under the heavy components category27. In this study, to simplify the model, the interaction of all components, including resins and aromatics, with asphaltenes are assumed to be identical and of the same nature. These interactions are represented by considering by four association sites on both the heavy component and the asphaltene molecule. This classification reduces the number of model assumptions and parameters, which is essential for practical engineering and industrial applications.

The physical parameters for the pure components, pseudo-hydrocarbon components, and asphaltene, including molecular weight (Mw​), PC, TC​, and ω, have been reported by Li and Firoozabadi27 and are provided in Appendix A. To calculate the critical properties of the heavy component, such as Tc and Pc, empirical correlations that depend on its Mw and specific gravity (SG) can be used.

As previously mentioned, the CPA EoS consists of two distinct contributions: the physical part (derived from PR) and the association part. Accordingly, the parameter estimation was performed separately for these two contributions, followed by a final simultaneous fine-tuning. For the physical part, due to the lack of experimental data for the heavy component and considering that the uncertainty in Pc is generally higher than that of Tc for heavy hydrocarbons81, the Tc of the heavy component was calculated using the Riazi and Daubert85 empirical correlation, while the Pc was treated as an adjustable parameter and tuned by fitting to the oil Pb data. Subsequently, the ω of the heavy component was calibrated by fitting to the AOP data. This sequential approach ensures that each physical parameter is constrained by the most sensitive experimental observable. Regarding the association contribution, the CPA model includes four adjustable parameters: the cross-association energy parameters between asphaltene-heavy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon^{AH}$$\end{document}), asphaltene-asphaltene (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon^{AA}$$\end{document}) components, which were determined by fitting to precipitation data, and the association volume parameter for these same interactions (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta^{AH}$$\end{document},\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta^{AA}$$\end{document}). Following established practice in the literature27, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta^{AH}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta^{AA}$$\end{document} were fixed at a constant value of 0.01. Fixing β reduces the degrees of freedom, enhances numerical stability, and mitigates the risk of overfitting. After obtaining initial estimates from the sequential steps described above, all four adjustable parameters (Pc, ω, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon^{AH}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon^{AA}$$\end{document}) were tuned simultaneously by minimizing the objective function presented in Eq. To manage the computational intensity of the optimization, all calculations were executed on a high-performance computing (HPC) cluster equipped with AMD processors (AMD128 partition). Each optimization job was allocated 4 GiB of memory and utilized 8 CPU cores for parallel processing, significantly reducing the overall computation time. This parallelization enabled extensive robustness checks, including testing multiple initial guesses and performing cross-validation efficiently. The combination of the multi-start strategy and parallel HPC resources ensured that the global optimum was reliably identified, and the final solution was verified by observing consistent convergence from different initial points, confirming the uniqueness and stability of the optimized parameter set. Results and discussion

Performance comparison of white-box models

In this research, three white-box models were utilized to predict the amount of asphaltene precipitation using a dataset comprising 308 datapoints from 25 different types of crude oils. In the initial phase, the dataset was randomly split into two subsets: a training set and a test set. During training, both the model architecture and coefficients are optimized to form a suitable input–output correlation. This optimization process continues until the discrepancy between experimental measurements and model predictions is minimized. To evaluate the predictive capability of the developed model, the testing dataset was employed. High accuracy during training is no guarantee of the model trustworthiness. Consequently, testing the model on unseen datasets allowed for the simultaneous evaluation of both its accuracy and reliability. 80% of the data was allocated to the training set and 20% to the test set. The selection of the 80–20 train–test split was based on commonly adopted practices in ML studies involving moderately sized datasets. Given that the compiled database consists of 308 experimental datapoints, allocating 80% of the data to the training subset ensures sufficient representation of the underlying nonlinear relationships among variables, while preserving 20% of the data for independent evaluation of generalization capability. This ratio provides an appropriate balance between model learning capacity and unbiased performance assessment. Although k-fold cross-validation is recognized as a robust validation technique for maximizing data utilization, its implementation in the present study was not adopted due to the computationally intensive nature of evolutionary algorithms such as GMDH, GEP, and GP, which require repeated population-based optimization across multiple generations. To determine the optimal hyperparameter configuration for each white-box model (GMDH, GEP, and GP), a systematic Grid Search optimization procedure was employed. For each model, a predefined search space was constructed by specifying discrete candidate values for all key hyperparameters, including population size, number of generations, mutation rate, crossover rate, maximum tree depth, polynomial degree, and number of neurons per layer. The selection ranges were determined based on preliminary sensitivity analysis and recommendations reported in the literature to ensure both computational feasibility and sufficient exploration of the parameter space.

All possible combinations within the defined hyperparameter grid were exhaustively evaluated. For each configuration, model training was performed using the training dataset (80% of total data), and predictive performance was assessed using statistical criteria, primarily the RMSE and R2. The optimal hyperparameter set was selected based on minimizing RMSE while simultaneously maximizing R2.

To prevent overfitting and ensure model generalization capability, the selected configuration was subsequently validated using the independent testing dataset (20% of total data). The final hyperparameter values reported in Table 5 correspond to the configuration that achieved the best balance between predictive accuracy and robustness across both training and testing datasets.

Model trend analysis

A different and insightful approach to assessing the model’s results is to analyze how variations in the input parameters influence the output. The validity of the developed models was assessed by plotting the measured asphaltene precipitation for two crude oil samples across a range of pressures, as shown in Fig. 15. In this figure, the predictions of the thermodynamic CPA model are shown alongside those of two top-performing correlations, namely GMDH and GEP, enabling a comprehensive comparison of their predictive performance. As the pressure decreases from reservoir conditions to the Pb, the oil density drops, thereby reducing its capacity to keep asphaltenes in solution. Below the Pb, a decrease in pressure leads to the liberation of light hydrocarbons. This loss of light components increases the density and solubility parameter of the residual oil, thereby enhancing its solvating power for asphaltenes. During depressurization, the system initially exists in a single-phase region when the pressure is above the upper onset. Asphaltene precipitation begins when pressure falls between the upper onset and the Pb, establishing an oil-asphaltene two-phase equilibrium. Further pressure reduction into the range between the Pb and the lower onset shifts the system toward a gas-oil-asphaltene three-phase equilibrium. Finally, when the pressure drops below the lower onset, asphaltene precipitation ceases completely, and the system reverts to a stable gas-oil two-phase region. This analysis demonstrates that the CPA, GMDH, and GEP models all effectively reproduce the characteristic patterns of asphaltene behavior under different pressure conditions. The quantitative evaluation using MSE shows that for crude oil A, the MSE values for the CPA, GMDH, and GEP models are 0.026, 0.021, and 0.056, respectively, while for crude oil B, the corresponding MSE values are 0.068, 0.001, and 0.071. The CPA model ensures strong physical consistency derived from its thermodynamic foundation; however, the GMDH model demonstrates superior predictive accuracy with the lowest MSE values, significantly outperforming the others, particularly for crude oil B. Furthermore, the GMDH model is designed as a white box, making it straightforward for other researchers to adopt and use. Collectively, these findings highlight the robustness and reliability of GMDH and CPA models in predicting asphaltene precipitation behavior for practical applications.Fig. 15Experimental measurements and model predictions for the asphaltene precipitation envelope of two different crude oils at various temperatures: (a) crude oil A, and (b) crude oil B.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was performed using Pearson, Spearman, and Kendall methods to assess the relative influence of each input parameter on the estimated amount of asphaltene precipitation based on the GMDH correlation. These techniques are widely employed to evaluate the degree of association among different parameters87. In cases where the data have a normal distribution, the Pearson method is a common choice, but for non-normally distributed data, the Kendall and Spearman methods are used87. The Spearman technique proves superior to the Pearson method when analyzing heavy-tail distributions88. In contrast to Pearson’s restriction to measuring linear relationships, the Kendall and Spearman approaches possess the ability to identify monotonic relationships as well, particularly in the presence of high skewness or outliers89. Due to the positive skewness of the parameters in this study, the Pearson correlation was not considered a particularly suitable choice for conducting the sensitivity analysis. Nevertheless, the performance of conventional methods was evaluated to determine their effectiveness in assessing how each input parameter influences estimated asphaltene precipitation. It should be emphasized that for all these correlation methods, the output is confined to a range from -1 to 1. The strength of the association is determined by how far the value is from zero (its absolute value), not by whether it is positive or negative90. These figures indicate that the parameters °API, P, and Pb demonstrate the most significant and consistent correlations with asphaltene precipitation. Outlier detection of the proposed GMDH model

The leverage approach provides a reliable way to establish the limits of a model’s validity and detect both questionable data points and those that fall outside the model’s influence within the dataset. In William’s plot, the leverage approach is utilized to pinpoint outlier data points, which are graphically represented at a significant distance from the primary data distribution91,92. This approach involves evaluating several factors and then using a scatter plot of standardized residuals (SR) versus hat values to visually define the range where the model is valid91,92. The SR value is derived from the formula presented below:52\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$SR = \frac{{z_{i} }}{{(MSE(1 - H_{ii} ))^{0.5} }}$$\end{document}

According to the formula, the MSE, the error of the ith data point (zi), and the hat indices (Hii) are the key elements. The calculation of the hat indices is based on the Hat matrix (H) shown below91–93:53\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H = X(X^{T} X)^{ - 1} X^{T}$$\end{document}

Here, XT is the transpose of the two-dimensional matrix X of size (m*n). Another factor under evaluation is the warning leverage (H*), calculated using this specific formula94:54\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H^{*} = \frac{3*(m + 1)}{n}$$\end{document}

Conclusions

This study introduces three white-box models (GMDH, GEP, GP) to predict asphaltene precipitation using 308 lab data points and eight input parameters including P, T, Pb, °API and SARA fractions. Their performance is benchmarked against a thermodynamically consistent CPA EoS. The database of 308 measurements from 25 crude oil samples may not fully represent all reservoir conditions or extreme thermodynamic states, and the models rely on static lab data without considering dynamic reservoir-scale effects like multiphase flow. Additionally, the predictive reliability of the white-box models is bounded by the training range of input variables.6) Future research should expand the experimental database to cover more crude oil types, pressures, and temperatures, while integrating dynamic production data and reservoir simulation. Hybrid models combining physics-informed constraints with symbolic regression, along with uncertainty quantification, can improve extrapolation and prediction confidence for industrial decisions.

6) Future research should expand the experimental database to cover more crude oil types, pressures, and temperatures, while integrating dynamic production data and reservoir simulation. Hybrid models combining physics-informed constraints with symbolic regression, along with uncertainty quantification, can improve extrapolation and prediction confidence for industrial decisions.
