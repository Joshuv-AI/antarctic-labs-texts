Fluorescence Detection of Alpha-Synuclein Aggregates in the Gut Using a Peptide Probe
Authors: Rachel Sim, Jeremy Lee, Joey Chieng, Ko Hui Tan, Grace Lim, Aaron Foo, Sunny Hei Wong, Kah Leong Lim, Kaicheng Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Parkinson’s disease (PD) is diagnosed clinically by motor symptoms, with no clinical molecular diagnostic test currently available. By the time motor symptoms manifest, significant irreversible neurodegeneration would have already occurred, limiting the effectiveness of the therapies. Recent identification of pathological α-synuclein (α-syn) aggregates in the gastrointestinal tract of prodromal PD patients offers a potential avenue for early diagnosis. This study aims to explore specific fluorescence labeling of α-syn aggregates in the gastrointestinal tract using a peptide-based probe for early indication of PD risk. We used primary hippocampal neuronal cells and wild-type mouse tissues with the addition of preformed α-syn fibrils (PFFs) to identify the most suitable probe (P1) for staining α-syn aggregates. We found that P1 labeled α-syn aggregates with high accuracy (87% in comparison to Serine129-phosphorylated α-syn antibody) and exhibited high labeling specificity for aggregated α-syn forms over monomeric forms. In a wide range of gastrointestinal tissues from PFF-injected mice and transgenic mice, P1 labeled α-syn aggregates across tissue layers (mucosa, submucosa, muscularis externa) and achieved comparable performance to antibody staining. A higher degree of probe labeling was found in older mice due to increased accumulation of α-syn aggregates with aging. α-Syn aggregates were readily detectable in the colonic mucosae using P1, even in colon samples that were briefly flushed with P1, indicating the potential use of this probe in colonic biopsy samples and intact colon imaging. These support further development of P1 as a specific fluorescent imaging biomarker for colonic α-syn aggregates for the early indication of PD risk.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disease that is characterized by the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and presence of intraneuronal Lewy bodies containing aggregated forms of alpha-synuclein (α-syn). PD is currently clinically diagnosed based on the presentation of motor symptoms on a Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) and there is no molecular-based diagnostic test available. Current treatments focus on symptom palliation through dopamine replacement therapies and are not protective against disease progression. Success in forestalling PD induced neurodegeneration has been largely limited by the severe extent of neuronal lossapproximately 50 to 70% of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta are lost by the time the disease is diagnosed. , Earlier diagnosis of PD is crucial for increasing the window in which disease-modifying treatments, especially neuroprotective therapies, can be tested. This would improve the success of clinical interventions and aid disease management.

In the prodromal phase, PD patients experience nonmotor symptoms such as idiopathic anosmia and gastrointestinal (GI) tract dysfunction. GI manifestations, including dysphagia, vomiting and constipation, can emerge up to two decades before clinical diagnosis of PD. Disruption of GI motility coincides with the emergence of α-syn pathology in the enteric nervous system (ENS) of the GI tract, including the esophagus, stomach, colon, and appendix. α-Syn exists physiologically as a soluble monomer but becomes misfolded in pathological states, leading to aggregation and Lewy body formation in cells. Braak and Hawkes have hypothesized that pathological α-syn propagates cranially in a prion-like manner along the vagal axis and affects the brainstem and cerebrum in an ascending fashion as the disease progresses. , Supporting this hypothesis, colonic mucosal biopsy and polypectomy specimens collected in prodromal PD patients demonstrated positive immunostaining for aggregated α-syn 2 to 5 years before the onset of motor symptoms. In 56% of prodromal PD patients, pathological phosphorylated α-syn was also found to have transgressed the mucosal and submucosal layers to seed the intestinal myenteric plexus, which is in contiguity with vagal nerve endings. These findings suggest that pathologically aggregated α-syn in the GI tract could be a clinical biomarker for the early detection of PD.

However, recent studies have provided additional evidence that the Braak hypothesis does not apply to a subset of PD patients, who demonstrate α-syn pathology originating from the brain. , McFleder et al. observed propagation of α-syn from the brain to gut in brain-first PD mouse models, while other studies observed bidirectional gut-to-brain propagation of α-syn pathology. The heterogeneity of clinical profiles between body-first and brain-first PD subtypes adds to the challenges of accurately detecting PD in the early stage.

Various strategies have been employed to detect α-syn aggregation in PD patients. Immunoassays such as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) use conformation-specific α-syn antibodies to quantify α-syn aggregates with high sensitivity. In terms of antibody-free approaches, seed amplification assays (SAAs) have shown promise for detecting α-syn aggregates in biological fluids such as cerebrospinal fluid , and blood. There is also increasing interest in developing fluorescent probes for imaging α-syn fibrils, with recent studies demonstrating utility in living neuroblastoma cells and PD brain sections. , In particular, luminescent conjugated oligothiophenes (LCOs) exhibit conformation-dependent fluorescence upon binding to aggregated α-syn forms in multiple system atrophy (MSA) and PD brain sections. ,

In this study, we develop a highly specific fluorescent imaging probe with a peptide sequence that can bind selectively to pathological aggregated forms of α-syn over functional monomeric forms. A peptide-based probe offers several advantages over antibodies, as it is cheaper and more scalable, while being less immunogenic for in vivo use. We validate the binding of our fluorescent probe to different forms of α-syn and demonstrate its specificity for labeling pathological aggregated α-syn over monomeric α-syn in cellular and animal models. We conduct an in-depth analysis of α-syn labeling in the GI tract, from the proximal (esophagus) to the distal end (colon), and through the different transmural tissue layers with a focus on quantification of surface mucosal expression. We envision that this peptide-based imaging biomarker can be explored for future clinical studies to evaluate GI tissues from biopsies or routine colonoscopy screenings. Higher levels of α-syn aggregate labeling in GI tissues may be used to indicate PD risk in patients in combination with lab-based assays, facilitating closer monitoring and subsequently more timely clinical interventions prior to the development of classic PD motor symptoms.

Results

BODIPY630/650 as the Fluorophore for Optimized Labeling

We synthesized a specific peptide-based fluorescent imaging marker to label pathological α-syn aggregates (Figure a). Cationic α-helical peptide PSMα3 was previously reported to bind toxic α-syn oligomers and amyloid fibrils with low nanomolar affinity, over the monomeric functional α-syn protein. We first chose Boron Dipyrromethene (BODIPY-FL) as the fluorescent tag for functionalization of PSMα3, due to its neutral charge, low molecular weight, and high quantum yield in conjugates. BODIPY-FL has been used for in vivo imaging applications and has been incorporated into a fluorescent probe undergoing clinical trial for detection of oral cancers. While BODIPY-FL-labeled P0 showed specific labeling of α-syn aggregates in cells (Figure S1a), BODIPY-FL fluoresces in the green channel that is prone to tissue autofluorescence, resulting in high background in tissue staining (Figure S1b). To avoid background from tissue autofluorescence, we explored using far-red dyes with longer excitation and emission wavelengths. The cyanine5.5 dye resulted in nonspecific labeling contributing to a high background signal and was deemed unsuitable for use (Figure S1c). BODIPY630/650 was the most suitable fluorescent tag, with minimal nonspecific background labeling, and the free dye demonstrating no detectable levels of staining in primary cells (Figure S1d). BODIPY630/650-labeled P1 was obtained after gel filtration chromatography with high yield of 90% (Figure S2a). A single peak corresponding to the expected mass of P1 in the liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) spectrum confirms that the peptide is labeled with BODIPY630/650 (Figure S2b). As the reaction is quick, single-step, and high-yielding, the synthesis of P1 can be rapidly scaled up for larger quantities needed in preclinical or clinical stages. The fluorescent and physiochemical properties of the probe can also be further fine-tuned if required, by modifications in the chemical structure of the BODIPY group.

P1 Shows High Labeling Specificity for α-Syn Aggregates Induced in Primary Hippocampal Neurons

We determined the labeling specificity of fluorescent probe P1 in an in vitro model established using primary hippocampal neurons prepared from postnatal mice pups to closely mimic a neuronal environment (Figure b). α-Syn preformed fibrils (PFFs) were generated from mouse recombinant α-syn and used to induce aggregate formation in primary neurons. The addition of PFFs causes endogenous α-syn in primary neurons to assemble into Lewy body-like aggregates that are hyperphosphorylated and have a filamentous ultrastructure. Primary neurons inoculated with PFFs for 7 days were subsequently treated with P1 for 5 min, for the labeling of α-syn aggregates in live cells. The cells were then fixed, and immunostaining was performed with antibodies for comparison. An antibody specific for phosphorylation at serine-129 (pS129) in α-syn aggregates [Abcam EP1536Y] was used as a gold-standard to visualize α-syn aggregates and an α-syn antibody [BD610787] was used to visualize total α-syn including monomeric and aggregated α-syn. In control cells that were treated with PBS, α-syn monomers were present (green), while there was no significant staining by P1 (red), indicating that the probe did not label α-syn monomers. In cells that were treated with PFFs, the presence of aggregated α-syn was confirmed by pS129 α-syn antibody staining (Figure c). P1 labeling was observed to have some nonspecific binding and more fluorescent signals compared to the pS129 α-syn antibody. This could possibly be due to P1 interacting with other forms of aggregated α-syn which were not phosphorylated. There was overall good colocalization between pS129 α-syn antibody staining (orange) and P1 staining (red), indicating that P1 probe labeled α-syn aggregates with high specificity over functional monomeric α-syn (Figure d). The positive predictive value (PPV) of the probe was determined by the number of true hits (labeled by both the probe and pS129 α-syn antibody, highlighted by green arrows in Figure d) over the number of positive hits (labeled by the probe). The average PPV was calculated to be 87.4%. The miss rate was determined by the number of false negatives (aggregates not labeled by the probe but labeled by the pS129 α-syn antibody, highlighted by pink arrows in Figure d) over the number of positive hits. P1 had a low miss rate of 8.7% (Figure e).

P1 Shows Preferential Binding to B* Oligomers and Fibrils over A* Oligomers and Monomers

After P1 was selected as the fluorescent probe with the best in vitro labeling activity, we investigated its binding specificity to different α-syn species to better understand the nature of P1 labeling. Prior work has demonstrated the ability of PSMα3 to bind toxic α-syn aggregates, including both oligomeric and fibrillar forms. To investigate if the incorporation of the BODIPY630/650 moiety in P1 affected the binding affinity of PSMα3 to toxic α-syn aggregates and to corelate with in vitro labeling, we characterized the interaction between P1 and various α-syn species. Various α-syn aggregates (nontoxic A* oligomers, toxic B* oligomers, PFFs) were prepared from mouse α-syn monomers according to literature − and their size and morphology were characterized by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Figure S3a,b).

We studied the general fluorescence properties of P1 by measuring the excitation and emission spectra of P1 in the presence of α-syn monomers, oligomers and fibrils (Figure a,b). The excitation peak of P1 increased by 1.8-fold and 7.6-fold in intensity upon binding to the B* oligomers and PFFs, respectively. Similarly, its emission peak also increased by 3-fold and 16-fold in intensity upon binding to the B* oligomers and PFFs, respectively. Fluorescence enhancement upon binding of the probe to the α-syn aggregates indicates that the probe can specifically detect aggregates over monomeric species. This phenomenon is most likely due to the PSMα3 peptide binding specifically to the hydrophobic β-sheet regions of B* oligomers and fibrils. This places the hydrophobic BODIPY moiety within the hydrophobic microenvironment of the β-sheets, where they form noncovalent interactions. These interactions result in restricted rotation of the fluorophore and shield the fluorophore from nonradiative decay of fluorescence from water molecules in the aqueous media, contributing to the increased fluorescence observed. Due to the lack of β-sheet regions in α-syn monomers and A* oligomers, the interactions of P1 with these species were weak, and lower excitation and emission peaks were observed.

By performing fluorescence titration against various α-syn species, we found that P1 binds to PFFs with a K D of 1.4 ± 0.26 μM (Figure c, Figure S4). This K D value is higher than the reported nanomolar binding affinity of PSMα3 for fibrils, determined directly by highly sensitive spectroscopic techniques, Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET) and fluorescence cross-correlation spectroscopy (FCSS). In our fluorescence titration assay, we relied on indirect measurements of BODIPY fluorescence gain when the probe is bound to the ligand. Insufficient fluorescence enhancement was observed at nanomolar concentrations of the probe; hence, we were unable to determine K D values below the micromolar range. Nevertheless, we demonstrated that P1 exhibited the strongest binding affinity to α-syn fibrils over other α-syn species.

We visualized probe-α-syn complexes using Native-PAGE to preserve the native conformation of the aggregates, as we found that the higher molecular weight aggregates were partly dissociated upon separation on SDS-PAGE (Figure S3a). In-gel fluorescence was observed corresponding to the B* oligomers and PFFs, indicating that P1 interacts specifically with the toxic aggregate forms (Figure d). The α-syn monomers and nontoxic A* oligomers showed no fluorescence, indicating that P1 did not bind to these nontoxic α-syn species. This shows that P1 exhibits a higher binding specificity for toxic α-syn aggregates containing β-sheet regions. We performed dot blotting using human recombinant α-syn monomers, oligomers, and PFFs, with P1 as a detection system (Figure e). We observed P1 had the strongest binding affinity for the oligomers and demonstrated concentration-dependent labeling of the oligomers and PFFs. The Native-PAGE gels and dot blots showed that P1 was able to bind with both mouse and human alpha synuclein oligomeric and fibrillar species.

P1 Detects α-Syn Aggregates in PFF-Injected Mouse Tissues and Post-mortem PD Brain Tissues with Low Background Signal

We next sought to evaluate the use of P1 in ex vivo staining using tissues obtained from PFF-injected wild-type mouse models (Figure a). PFFs were injected directly into the brain of wild-type mice to induce the formation of α-syn aggregates. A high degree of α-syn aggregation was observed in the hippocampal and cortex regions, labeled both by pS129 antibody and P1 (Figure b). No significant α-syn aggregation was observed in the control group, where the mice were injected with PBS (Figure S5). High background signal was observed in the green channel, which is attributed to tissue autofluorescence. When BODIPY-FL-labeled P0, which fluoresces in the green channel was tested, we found it challenging to distinguish between the probe staining and the tissue autofluorescence (Figure S1b). This highlights the importance of using a far-red dye to minimize background signal and avoid false positive results.

We evaluated the ex vivo tissue staining of P1 in gut-injected mouse models. Expression of α-syn is observed in the gut of a wild-type mouse (Figure S6). It is hence expected that injection of PFFs directly in the gut of wild-type mouse would induce the aggregation of endogenous α-syn. However, only a low degree of α-syn aggregation was observed in the duodenum tissue from gut-injected mouse (Figure c). This could be due to the maturation of α-syn pathology being age-dependent, as the mice used in this study were relatively young at 4 to 6 months old and expected to show more diffuse α-syn pathology compared to older animals. There was a low level of α-syn aggregates present in the mucosa layer, labeled by both pS129 α-syn antibody and P1.

We also evaluated the ex vivo tissue staining of P1 in brain tissue obtained from a PD patient post-mortem. P1 was found to label Lewy bodies and neurites in the substantia nigra of the PD brain, with a similar staining pattern compared to pS129 α-syn antibody (Figure d,e) P1 shows high specificity for staining α-syn aggregates within Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites in clinical samples.

P1 Highlights Differential Accumulation of α-Syn Aggregates across GI Tract in Transgenic Mice

To evaluate ex vivo labeling in the GI tract tissues and study the distribution of α-syn aggregation within the tissue layers, we employed transgenic PD mouse models. We selected two transgenic models demonstrating α-syn aggregation: (i) M83 transgenic mouse JAX004479 and (ii) double transgenic mouse JAX010799. JAX004479 mice were reported to have significant α-syn aggregates in the brain and onset of motor phenotype by 8–10 months of age. JAX010799 mouse were reported to have early gastrointestinal dysfunction, before the onset of motor symptoms. To evaluate probe staining, the esophagus, stomach, duodenum, jejunum, ileum, and colon tissues from JAX004479 mice were harvested for tissue section staining. By using a swiss-roll preparation and longitudinal sectioning for the harvested intestinal tissues, we could capture a large area of intestinal tissue and different portions from the proximal to the distal end for comprehensive histological assessment. α-Syn aggregation was observed across all the GI tract tissues investigated, with the highest intensity of staining observed in the esophagus and colon (Figure a,b). α-Syn aggregates were found in the mucosa layers, stained by both pS129 and P1 in a similar pattern. α-Syn aggregates were more prevalent in the extreme proximal and distal ends of the GI tract, with P1 staining accounting for 12.9% and 13.3% of the total tissue area in the esophagus and colon, respectively. Analysis using the Costes method revealed an average of 91.0% colocalization between the two channels (pS129 and P1), indicating that P1 staining was largely comparable to pS129 antibody staining (Figure c). Using CellProfiler software, objects labeled by both pS129 antibody and P1 were automatically counted and used to determine the probe positive predictive value (PPV). The PPV was generally consistent across all of the GI tract tissues, with an average of 87.8% (Figure d). This demonstrates that the labeling specificity of P1 was not affected by differences in the environments of the various GI tract tissues. The PPV in tissues was similar to the PPV in cells, indicating that the probe was able to label α-syn aggregates in both cells and tissues with similar sensitivity. The colon tissues were imaged at higher magnification, with immunostaining using antibeta III tubulin (TUBB3) antibody, to highlight the enteric neurons. P1 labeling of α-syn aggregates was observed in the enteric neurons of the colonic tissue (Figure e).

Accumulation of α-Syn Aggregation Increases with Age in Transgenic Mice

We evaluated the accumulation of α-syn aggregation within GI tract tissues by comparing young and old JAX010799 transgenic mice. The ages of the transgenic mice were chosen based on the presence of motor symptoms: 5-month-old mice presented no motor symptoms, analogous to early stage PD, while 15-month old mice presented motor symptoms such as tremors, analogous to late stage PD. Increased levels of total α-syn were observed in the brain and GI tissues of older transgenic mice. Higher molecular weight species of α-syn were also present in increased amounts in the older mice (highlighted by red arrows). There was generally lower abundance of α-syn in lysates obtained from duodenum and colon tissues, which may be due to difficulties in solubilizing α-syn aggregates in tissues or high levels of protein degradation in fresh GI tract tissues during protein extraction protocols. A 2-fold increase in total α-syn in the brain and 1.5-fold increase in the esophagus of 15-month-old mice indicated α-syn accumulation with age (Figure a,b). The presence of α-syn aggregates in the transgenic mice could be detected in dot blots of the tissue lysates, using P1 as the detection system (Figure S7b).

Comparison of α-syn aggregation between young and old mice was also performed with immunohistochemical staining and image analysis. Increased abundance of α-syn aggregates was observed in esophagus and colon tissues of older mice (Figure c,d). The degree of α-syn aggregation was quantified by assessing the ratio of probe P1 staining to total α-syn antibody staining, and the ratio of pS129 α-syn antibody staining to total α-syn antibody staining. Our findings consistently revealed a higher degree of α-syn aggregation across all GI tract tissues in older transgenic mice (Figure e). The trend was similar in both pS129 α-syn antibody-labeled and probe P1-labeled aggregation, demonstrating that our peptide probe shows potential as a diagnostic marker comparable to pS129 α-syn antibody.

α-Syn Aggregates Are Detectable in Colonic Mucosa

As endoscopic imaging is limited largely to the mucosal layers, we sought to determine the abundance of α-syn aggregation in colonic mucosa to determine the feasibility of endoscopic detection. α-Syn aggregates were abundant in the mucosa and labeled by both pS129 α-syn antibody and P1 (Figure a). The highest degree of α-syn aggregation was observed in the muscularis externa compared to the other tissue layers (Figure b), while >20% of α-syn present in the mucosal layer was found to be aggregated and labeled by pS129 α-syn antibody and P1. While α-syn aggregates were found in the lowest quantities in the mucosa compared to the submucosa and muscularis externa regions, they were still present and detectable in the mucosa by probe staining. The presence of quantifiable α-syn aggregates in mucosa indicates that α-syn aggregation is a potential biomarker for PD screening via the GI tract.

P1 labels α-syn aggregates in the surface mucosal layer.

To determine if P1 could be used as a rapid fluorescent dye applied topically to the mucosa in colonoscopy procedures, we evaluated the staining of the colonic mucosa after brief flushing of P1 in intact fresh colon tissues from transgenic mice. The lumen of the colon was flushed by P1 three times to mimic spraying of a contrast-enhancing dye in the GI tract commonly performed in clinical colonoscopy procedures. Thinly cut sections of the P1-stained colon tissue were then subjected to immunofluorescence staining with the pS129 α-syn antibody and aggregated α-syn antibody for comparison. P1 was found to stain only the surface mucosa up to a depth of around 30 μm deep using this quick flushing method (Figure c). As a result, α-syn aggregates present in the deeper submucosal and muscle layers of the tissue were not labeled by the probe. However, the surface mucosal P1 staining was specific and colocalized with antibody staining, demonstrating the utility of P1 for staining surface layers in scope applications.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate the use of a peptide-based fluorescent imaging marker to label α-syn aggregates with comparable specificity and sensitivity to an antibody specific for phosphorylated α-syn, in primary neurons, wild-type mouse, and transgenic mouse tissue samples. A range of cellular and tissue models were chosen, with consideration that α-syn aggregates are made up of a heterogeneous mix of oligomeric and fibrillar species, and that propagation and accumulation of α-syn aggregates differ across models. By using PFFs to induce α-syn aggregation in primary cells and wild-type mouse models, we observed specific labeling of aggregated α-syn over functional monomeric α-syn by probe P1. In brain-injected mice, some spread of α-syn aggregation was observed, with α-syn aggregates found in the cortex, away from the striatum, which was the site of injection. However, there was limited spread of α-syn aggregation in the gut-injected mice, possibly due to the young age of the mice used in the study. These PFF-injected models have some limitations as the recruitment of endogenous mouse α-syn by PFFs to form higher molecular weight aggregates occurs slower in younger animals and is also dependent on the seeding technique and site of injection.

To demonstrate the utility of the probe in disease models where α-syn aggregation is more sparsely distributed over a larger region, we used transgenic PD mice that overexpress α-syn. As the overexpression of α-syn in the transgenic models is not limited to the ENS or the GI tract, these mice may not entirely recapitulate the gut–brain pathway for α-syn propagation in PD. However, these transgenic mice display GI dysfunction before central nervous system dysfunction, and are still a relevant model for studying prodromal PD. We demonstrated high ex vivo labeling sensitivity in GI tract tissues of the transgenic mice, highlighting the potential of using fluorescence imaging in the GI tract as a detection method for diagnosis. While α-syn is believed to originate from enteric nerves, we also note other reports of α-syn expression being observed in the intestinal mucosa, , which corresponds with our observations of P1 labeling and α-syn immunostaining in the intestinal mucosa. The relatively higher prevalence of α-syn aggregation in the esophagus and colon suggests that gastroscopy and colonoscopy could be leveraged to access these GI tract regions for detection. We observed specific probe staining for α-syn aggregates in GI tract tissues of young transgenic mice that did not exhibit any obvious motor deficits. This suggests that the probe can be used for the detection of α-syn aggregates in the GI tract as an early indication of PD before symptom onset. Comparing GI tract images from young and old transgenic mice, we observed an increase in α-syn aggregation in older mice, which coincided with the manifestation of motor symptoms. This is in line with studies that observed an increase in α-syn aggregation in the gut of older wild-type animals, linked to the declining ability to clear α-syn aggregates from the cells with old age. , However, due to high levels of α-syn overexpression in the transgenic mice, this model is not representative of age-related decline in α-syn clearing mechanisms. In addition, although both models express human α-synuclein in neurons, the expression is not restricted solely to the enteric nervous system (ENS). This may contribute to added positive staining or certain staining patterns in intestinal tissues that may be less physiologically accurate, although not necessarily nonspecific background. Use of transgenic mouse models with ENS-restricted expression in future work will enable more anatomically precise hypotheses and better validate P1’s utility in detecting GI tract-localized α-syn pathology. Other studies investigating α-syn pathology in colonic biopsies have observed staining in preclinical stages of the disease and increased accumulation with disease progression. , Furthermore, our findings suggest the possibility of establishing quantification thresholds for probe staining at various stages of PD progression, using more sophisticated preclinical PD models and/or tissue samples from prodromal and symptomatic PD patient cohorts. Such thresholds could transform this imaging biomarker into a staging tool for assessing the disease severity or progression in clinical samples. However, this requires further validation through comparisons of probe labeling percentages in the GI tract between healthy controls and PD patients at various disease stages. It will be crucial to identify baseline levels of probe labeling and α-syn pathology in healthy controls to avoid having false positive results with this imaging biomarker.

Our peptide-based fluorescent probe offers advantages over the pS129 α-syn antibody, especially for further development as an imaging marker for diagnostic use. It can be used for live cell imaging at 1 μM, as demonstrated in the staining of primary neuronal hippocampal cells, as the peptide can be taken up into cells without the need for fixation and permeabilization. Peptide affinity is a well-known concept in probe development and may not be as sophisticated as new approaches promising structure-specific affinity. However, peptides continue to be remarkably useful in practice while being economical for manufacturing at scale, important considerations for a clinical use case, applied in a very large organ, and potentially screening large populations. The probe stains rapidly and demonstrates increased emission signal upon binding to the target aggregated α-syn species, allowing for the strong fluorescence labeling to be captured quickly by imaging. While thioflavin-T (ThT) is another important tool for amyloid detection, it is used at a higher molar concentration of 10 to 50 μM compared to P1, and also stains neuronal cell bodies and nucleoli. In addition, while SAAs have reported high sensitivities and specificities for distinguishing PD patients from control patients using cerebrospinal fluid samples, these assays are currently primarily qualitative in nature. Challenges persist in obtaining quantitative data due to inherent variability in aggregation processes and kinetics in different assay conditions, making it hard to establish standardized thresholds that correlate with disease severity and progression. In comparison, probe P1 labels the α-syn aggregates directly in the tissue without relying on amplification processes, potentially allowing for quantification thresholds to be determined and correlated to clinical phenotypes. We envision image-based approaches such as in vivo fluorescence labeling to be highly compatible with SAAs, where both could be used in tandem for a multipronged estimate of PD risk.

While our current study demonstrates the utility of our probe in mouse models, further work to validate probe staining in human tissue samples will aid clinical translation. The ability to visualize α-syn aggregates within the mucosal layers of GI tract tissues is promising and presents an opportunity to further develop the probe for use in GI screening methods. A survey of a late middle-aged population with a mean age of 60.3 years found that 11% of the subjects had more than two PD risk factors, and 9.7% had constipation. A probe specific for α-syn aggregates could serve as a surveillance tool to screen patients with nonmotor symptoms and deemed to be at risk of prodromal PD. The at-risk age for prodromal PD coincides with the recommended age for colorectal cancer screening (50–75 years), which presents an opportunity for the integration of PD screening into routine colonoscopy protocols. A recent study revealed that in intestinal biopsies of PD patients, the ratios of aggregated α-syn to total α-syn in the sigmoid mucosa were notably higher compared to healthy controls. However, the depth of field in endoscopic imaging may be limited to a more superficial mucosal layer, and the amount of submucosal tissue present in the typical colonic biopsies may not be sufficient to detect levels of aggregated α-syn reliably. Beach et al. has previously described challenges in distinguishing PD colon from controls based on immunohistochemical staining of pathological α-syn in archived colonic biopsy samples, as neuronal structures are sparse and staining intensities of α-syn are low. We have demonstrated labeling of α-syn aggregates on the surface mucosal layer by rapid flushing of P1 through the lumen of the colon, but this is limited to a tissue penetration depth of about 30 μm. Due to this key limitation, use of P1 in in vivo colonoscopy applications will be largely dependent on whether there is a significant presence of α-syn aggregates on the surface mucosal layer. Hence, more studies with the probe on clinical samples will be required to provide more insight on its ability to label α-syn aggregates with sufficient fluorescent signal in colonic biopsy samples or endoscopy procedures to identify PD patients.

Conclusions

We report a peptide-based fluorescent probe as a specific imaging marker to label α-syn aggregates in cellular and animal models of synucleinopathies. Probe P1 demonstrates a high positive predictive value of 88%, in comparison to antibodies staining for phosphorylated, aggregated α-syn in GI tract tissues. Being low-cost, rapid staining, and highly specific, P1 shows promise as a diagnostic tool to evaluate PD risk in GI tissue biopsies or routine endoscopic screening procedures. With further clinical validation, we envision that P1 can provide a means for PD surveillance in the older population and for classification of PD patients according to disease stages.

Methods

Synthesis of P1

Peptides (GenScript) were modified with a cysteine at the N-terminus. Tris­(2-carboxyethyl)­phosphine (TCEP) was added to PSMα3 (10 mg/mL in ultrapure water). After 15 min, BODIPY630/650-maleimide in DMSO was added, and the mixture was sonicated and left to shake at 1000 rpm at room temperature for 4 h. The BODIPY630/650-labeled peptide was purified on a desalting column. Fractions containing the labeled peptide were identified using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), and the fractions were combined and freeze-dried to give P1 as a dark blue powder.

Formation of Alpha-Synuclein (α-Syn) Oligomers and Preformed Fibrils (PFF)

Mouse α-syn monomers (Proteos, #RP-009) were dialyzed against ultrapure water at 4 °C overnight using microdialysis tubes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #69580) with 2–3 buffer replacements to remove the stock buffer solution. Monomers were frozen, lyophilized overnight, and stored in −80 °C until further use.

For type A* Oligomers Generation

The lyophilized α-syn monomers (6.08 mg/mL in PBS) were incubated with ten mol equiv of (−)-epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) for 48 h at 37 °C, in darkness, and without agitation. The solution was then subjected to six consecutive filtration cycles through 100 kDa centrifuge filters (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) to remove the excess compounds and unreacted monomers.

For type B* Oligomers Generation

The lyophilized α-syn monomers (11.6 mg/mL in PBS) were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C without agitation. The solution was subjected to four consecutive cycles of filtration through 100 kDa centrifuge filters. The final concentration of oligomeric solutions was determined by a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. The oligomers were kept at −80 °C until further use.

For Preformed Fibrils (PFF) Generation

The lyophilized α-syn monomers (5 mg/mL in PBS) were incubated in a thermomixer at 1000 rpm and 37 °C for 7 days. The solution was subjected to three consecutive cycles of filtration through 100 kDa centrifuge filters. The final concentration of fibrillar samples was determined by measuring the absorbance at 280 nm using a molar extinction coefficient of 7450 M–1 cm–1. Additionally, fibrils were sonicated with SONICS The Vibra-cell VCX-130 for 60 s with the following parameters: 1 s on, 1 s off, 20% amplitude, to generate fibrillar samples with a relatively homogeneous size distribution of small fibrils.

For Dot Blot Experiments

Human recombinant α-syn monomers (SPR-321C), kinetically stable oligomers (SPR-484C), and PFFs (SPR-322C) were purchased from StressMarq Biosciences and used without further modification.

Transmission Electron Microscopy

α-Syn oligomers and PFF samples were placed on glow-discharged grids for 1 min and grid blotted dry, followed by negative staining with 1% uranyl acetate for 1 min. The grid was left to dry completely before imaging. Images were acquired using an FEI Tecnai Spirit G2 transmission electron microscope.

Spectral Measurements of Probe

Two μM of probe (2 μM) was incubated with α-syn monomers, oligomers, and PFFs (10 μM) in a black, clear-bottomed 96-well plate (Grenier 655096), with gentle rocking for 30 min at RT to allow the probe-α-syn interaction to reach equilibrium. Excitation and emission spectra of the probe was measured on a fluorescence microplate reader (Tecan Infinite M200 Monochromator) at room temperature. The excitation scan was performed with λem at 735 nm, while the emission scan was performed with λex at 590 nm. Both scans utilized a step size of 1.

Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (PAGE)

Both denaturing SDS-PAGE and nondenaturing Native-PAGE were performed on 2 μg of purified monomers, oligomers, and fibrils using 4–15% Mini-PROTEAN TGX Stain-Free Precast Protein Gels (BioRad). SDS was not included in the sample, gel, and buffer of the Native-PAGE runs. For Native-PAGE, NativeMark Unstained Protein Standard (ThermoFisher) was used as a protein ladder, and samples were run for 1 h. For SDS-PAGE, Precision Plus Protein Unstained Standard (BioRad) was used as protein ladder, and samples were run for 3 h. No boiling step was included in both cases. To visualize proteins, Coomassie blue protein dye was used as stain.

Fluorescence Titration of Probe with α-Syn Species

0.5 μM of probe (0.5 μM) was incubated together with various α-syn species (0 to 10 μM) in a black, clear-bottomed 96-well plate, with gentle rocking for 30 min at RT to allow the probe-α-syn interaction to reach equilibrium. Each concentration was tested in triplicates. Fluorescence values were measured on a fluorescence microplate reader at room temperature with λex at 620 nm and λem at 655 nm with a step size of 5. The K D binding curve was obtained with Prism 9.5.1 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA), and data was fitted using a fluorescent ligand binding equation.

Primary Neuronal Culture

The protocol to harvest primary hippocampal neurons and add PFFs to induce α-syn aggregation was adapted from literature methods. , Brains of postnatal mice (P0–P3) were harvested and placed in dissection medium (HBSS with 1 mg/mL sodium pyruvate, 0.1% glucose, 10 mM HEPES) on ice. The hippocampus was dissected and washed with a dissection medium twice. The tissue was incubated with TrypLE solution in a 37 °C water bath for 20 min and a further 5 min at room temperature with the addition of DNase solution. The digested tissue was washed with plating medium (Neurobasal medium with B27 supplement, Glutamax, 50 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin, 10% FBS) twice and then triturated with a P1000 pipet to dissociate the cells. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 40-μm nylon mesh cell strainer. The cells were plated on poly-d-lysine-coated well-plates or glass slides for experiments at a density of 52,000 cells/cm2. The medium was changed to neuronal medium (Neurobasal medium with B27 supplement, Glutamax, 50 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin) 24 h after plating, and the cells were left to grow for 7 days. PFFs were added to the cells in 80% fresh neuronal medium (20% of medium in each well was retained), and the cells were left to culture for a further 7 days.

Control Mouse Tissue Preparation

4-month-old BALB/c nude mice were euthanised and organs were harvested. Organs were washed in PBS and fixed in 4% PFA for 15 min. Organs were sectioned to 50 μm using a VF-500-0Z microtome (Precisionary Instruments LLC, US).

PFF-injected Mouse Tissue Preparation

15 μL of 2 mg/mL α-syn PFF (StressMarq SPR-324) were sonicated in an ultrasonic bath sonicator for 2.5 h at the following conditions: sweep, 37 Hz frequency, 60% power. Water was changed at 30 min intervals to maintain the water temperature at 15 °C.

Injection in Brain

Ten μg of sonicated PFF (StressMarq) was injected into the left striatum of 4–6 months old C57BL6 mice using the following stereotaxic coordinates: ML + 2.0 mm, AP + 0.5 mm, DV −3.5 mm. 5 μL of 2 mg/mL of sonicated PFF was injected at a speed of 1 μL/min. 1-month postinjection, the mice were transcardially perfused with PBS followed by 4% PFA in PBS. The brain was harvested and kept in 4% PFA in PBS overnight. The brain was then kept in 15% sucrose in PBS for 24 h, followed by 30% sucrose in PBS for another 24 h, before embedding in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) medium. Cryosectioning was performed at a 30 μm thickness.

Injection in Gut

9–11 weeks old C57BL6 mice (9–11 weeks old) were injected with 6 μg of sonicated PFF (Proteos) at a total of 4 sites: 2 at the gastric pylorus and 2 at the upper duodenum (2 mg/mL, vol = 3 μL), totaling 24 μg of PFF. 2.5 months later, mice were transcardially perfused with PBS followed by 4% PFA in PBS. The stomach and upper duodenum were extracted and flushed with PBS to remove waste material. Tissues were kept in 4% PFA in PBS overnight, followed by 70% ethanol overnight. The stomach and upper duodenum were separately embedded in paraffin blocks. Paraffin sectioning was performed at a 5 μm thickness.

Transgenic Mouse Tissue Preparation

Two strains of transgenic mice were imported from the Jackson Laboratory: (i) M83 transgenic mouse JAX004479 and (ii) double transgenic mouse JAX010799. JAX004479 mice express full-length human α-syn carrying the A53T mutation under the control of the mouse prion protein promotor. JAX010799 mice harbor two independently inserted transgenes encoding human A53T mutant α-syn. JAX004479 mice were sacrificed at 10 months old (n = 2). JAX010799 mice were sacrificed at 5 months (n = 3) and 15 months old (n = 3). The tissues were harvested in this order from sacrificed mice: brain, esophagus, stomach, small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, and ileum), and colon. Intestinal tissues were flushed with PBS multiple times to remove waste material. The intestinal tissues were coiled in a swiss-roll manner on sponges in tissue embedding cassettes (Uni-Sci, C33118001PH) and fixed directly in the cassettes. Stomach was cut open and laid flat on the sponge in the tissue cassette. Tissues were fixed in 4% PFA in PBS overnight at 4 °C and then embedded in paraffin blocks.

For flushing with P1, 18 month old JAX010799 mice (n = 3) were sacrificed. The colon tissue was harvested and washed once with acetylcysteine (10 mg/mL) to remove waste material and mucus. Colon tissue was flushed with P1 (15 μg/mL, 1 mL) and washed with PBS (1 mL). The tissue was fixed with 4% PFA, then cut using a vibratome to 50 μm sections for immunofluorescence staining.

Immunofluorescence (IF) Staining

For Cells

Cells were treated with P1 (1 μM) for 5 min at 37 °C before washing in PBS. The cells were fixed with 4% PFA at room temperature. Immunofluorescence staining steps were performed as follows: Blocking with TBS-T with 2% horse serum for 1 h, incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washing with TBS-T (3 times), incubation with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h (see Table for list of antibodies). The cells were then incubated with Hoechst 33342 for 15 min, washed with TBS and fixed with antifade mounting medium (Abcam, #ab104135).

For Mouse Tissues

For fresh tissue staining: Fresh tissues were embedded in 2.5% agarose and sliced at a 50 μm thickness using a vibratome. The slices were mounted onto a polylysine-coated glass slide, air-dried, and used for subsequent staining. For Human Tissue

Unstained paraffin slides containing post-mortem substantia nigral brain sections from PD patients were obtained from Lee Kong Chian School of Medicine, NTU, deparaffinized, and used for staining as described previously.

Microscopy

Fluorescence images were acquired on an Olympus inverted fluorescence IX83 microscope and an Olympus FV3000RS confocal microscope. High content imaging was performed on a PerkinElmer Operetta CLS imaging system. Brightfield images were acquired with a Zeiss AxioObserver 7 Microscope.

Colocalization Quantification

The Manders colocalization, Costes colocalization, and Pearson’s correlation coefficients between pS129 staining (orange channel) and P1 staining (red channel) were determined using CellProfiler (v 4.2.1). The images were converted to 8-bit grayscale images, and illumination correction was applied. The channel images were aligned, then the correlation between the two channels were measured. The Manders colocalization and Costes colocalization coefficients were calculated based on methods described in literature. The Pearson’s correlation measurement is the normalized covariance, which is the covariance divided by the product of standard deviation of pixels in each image. Individual objects were automatically detected, with thresholding size set at a minimum of 3, and a maximum of 15 pixel units. Objects were counted in both channels, and objects found to touch and overlap each other were considered as colocalized. The probe positive predictive value is calculated by the number of objects colocalized in the orange and red channel (true positives), divided by the total number of objects detected in the red channel (probe positives).

Western Blot and Dot Blot

About 10 mg of tissue was flash frozen in dry ice. The frozen tissue was minced and homogenized with a mortar and pestle chilled on ice. Homogenized tissue was suspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 2% SDS, 1× Pierce protease, and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail). The mixture was sonicated for 5 s three times: 1 s on, 1 s off, 30% amplitude, at 4 °C. The samples were centrifuged at 12000g at 20 °C for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. Concentration of the protein sample collected was determined using the Bradford assay. For Western blotting, LDS sample buffer and reducing agent was added to 50 μg of protein lysate, and the samples were heated at 70 °C for 10 min. The proteins were separated on a 4 to 12% Bis-Tris gel using MES as a running buffer at 200 V for 30 min. The proteins were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane on an iBlot 2 gel transfer (dry blotting) system, at 20 V for 1 min, 23 V for 4 min, and 25 V for 2 min. The membrane was fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min and then washed with TBS and blocked with TBS-T containing 2% horse serum for 1 h. The membrane was incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and washed with TBS-T (3 times). The membrane was then incubated with fluorescent-labeled secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. The membrane was imaged on an iBright imaging system. Each sample was tested in triplicates.

For use in the dot blots, 10 μg of protein lysate per sample was manually spotted on a preactivated PVDF membrane and allowed to air-dry. The membrane was incubated with peptide probe P1 (0.5 μM) diluted in a blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature with gentle agitation. The membrane was imaged on an iBright imaging system, using excitation channel with a range of 608–632 nm and emission channel with a range of 675–720 nm.

Aggregation Quantification

The pS129-Ab-labeled aggregation and probe-labeled aggregation percentages were determined using CellProfiler (v 4.2.1). The images in each channel were converted to 8-bit grayscale images, and illumination correction was applied. The area where the signal was detected in each channel was calculated. The percentage of pS129-Ab- labeled aggregation was calculated by comparing the ratio of the area stained by ps129 antibody, over the area stained by total α-syn antibody. The percentage of probe-labeled aggregation was calculated by comparing the ratio of the area stained by probe P1, over the area stained by total α-syn antibody. Ten images were captured for each tissue (per biological replicate), and the average of the values calculated are plotted in Figure e,f.

Statistical Analysis

All values are expressed as the mean ± SEM. Differences between means were evaluated by a two-way ANOVA test (Prism, Graph Pad Software, San Diego, CA).
