Development and validation of the pulmonary rehabilitation Kinesiophobia scale for patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
Authors: Di Yang, Lu Wang, Guixing Liang, Pin Liu, Meizhu Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKinesiophobia is a significant barrier to pulmonary rehabilitation in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). However, there is currently a lack of a specific assessment tool for kinesiophobia in Chinese COPD patients. This study aims to develop the Pulmonary Rehabilitation Kinesiophobia Scale for Patients with COPD and to evaluate its psychometric properties.MethodsThis study was conducted in two stages. Stage I (instrument development): Based on literature review, qualitative interviews, and expert consultation, a preliminary version of the scale was developed. Stage II (item evaluation and psychometric testing): From April to September 2024, A total of 323 COPD patients from a tertiary hospital in Guangzhou completed the scale survey via convenience sampling. Data were analyzed for item selection, as well as reliability and validity testing. Reliability was assessed using internal consistency, test-retest reliability, and split-half reliability. Validity was evaluated through content validity, construct validity, and criterion-related validity. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) were performed to assess the scale’s structure. For criterion-related validity, the correlation was analyzed between scale scores and the Modified Medical Research Council Dyspnea Scale (mMRC), the COPD Assessment Test (CAT), and the 6-minute walk distance (6-MWD).ResultsThe scale comprises 17 items. EFA revealed a reasonable distribution of factor loadings across five factors, with a cumulative variance explained of 70.7%. This factor structure was further supported by CFA, and the model fit indicators were: χ²/df = 1.555, RMSEA = 0.053, GFI = 0.906, IFI = 0.958, TLI = 0.946, and CFI = 0.957. Reliability analysis showed a Cronbach’s α of 0.910, test-retest reliability of 0.844, and split-half reliability of 0.930. The content validity index I-CVI was 0.83-1.00, the S-CVI/UA was 0.824, and the S-CVI/Ave was 0.971. The correlation coefficients between the scale scores and mMRC, CAT, and 6MWD were 0.753, 0.731, and − 0.538, respectively.ConclusionsThe Pulmonary Rehabilitation Kinesiophobia Scale for Patients with COPD demonstrates good reliability and validity, which can be used to assess kinesiophobia in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12890-026-04522-6.

Background

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) presents a significant global public health challenge. The global prevalence of COPD among people aged 30–79 years was 10·3%, nearly 390 million patients. China has the largest number of COPD patients, accounting for one-quarter of all global COPD cases [1]. It is predicted that COPD cases will continue to rise, with COPD patients expected to reach 600 million by 2050 [2]. In addition to being highly prevalent, COPD has become the fourth leading cause of death worldwide [3], which places a significant burden on global health resources.

Pulmonary rehabilitation can significantly improve dyspnea, exercise capacity, and quality of life in patients with COPD [4]. It is also related to reducing the rate of hospital readmissions and the all-cause mortality over 1 year in COPD patients [5, 6]. Although COPD patients can benefit from pulmonary rehabilitation, real-world research has shown that their adherence to these programs is often suboptimal [7]. Adherence to pulmonary rehabilitation among COPD patients is influenced by multiple factors, with fear of exercise or physical activity being one of them.

Kinesiophobia is an excessive, irrational, and debilitating fear of movement and activity, resulting from a feeling of vulnerability to painful injury or re-injury [8]. Zhili Jiang’s research indicated that most COPD patients experience kinesiophobia, which affects their physical activity levels [9]. This kinesiophobia is closely associated with the respiratory limitations of COPD patients.

Currently, the most widely used tools for assessing kinesiophobia are the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia (TSK) and the Fear-Avoidance Beliefs Questionnaire (FABQ) [10], both of which apply to patients with chronic pain. Additionally, TSK has developed a series of derived scales, such as the Breathlessness Beliefs Questionnaire (BBQ) [11], the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia-Heart (TSK-H) [12], and the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia-Fatigue (TSK-F) [13]. These scales are respectively used to evaluate kinesiophobia in patients with respiratory, cardiac, and fatigue-related conditions. Although the BBQ can be used to assess kinesiophobia in patients with chronic respiratory diseases, the Chinese version of the BBQ has several limitations, including inadequate dimensional reliability, insufficient validation with an adequate sample size for COPD, and a lack of comprehensive psychometric evaluation. In summary, there is currently a lack of an appropriate tool for evaluating kinesiophobia in COPD patients within the Chinese context. Therefore, this study aims to develop a kinesiophobia assessment tool for COPD patients in the Chinese version and to evaluate its performance, which is beneficial to further research on kinesiophobia in COPD pulmonary rehabilitation.

Method

The scale was developed according to DeVellis’ Guidelines in Scale Development [14]. This study consists of two stages. Stage I: instrument development: items were extracted from the literature review and qualitative interviews, and revised through expert consultation. Stage II: item evaluation and psychometric testing: This stage is further divided into two sub-stages. Stage II A: The items were evaluated with some removed, and an exploration of the instrument’s preliminary structure was conducted. Stage II B: psychometric testing: validating the reliability and validity of the assessment tool.

Stage I instrument development

Item generation

Theoretical framework

This study is based on the common sense model of illness self-regulation [15] and the fear-avoidance model [16]. According to the common sense model of illness self-regulation, when individuals perceive an internal or external health threat, they usually generate cognitive and emotional representations. These two types of representations interact with each other, collectively mediating the coping strategies and ultimately leading to different disease outcomes. According to the fear-avoidance model, when confronted with pain or any other type of fear, individuals adopt two extreme responses of confrontation and avoidance, which result in different health outcomes. Furthermore, in previous studies on kinesiophobia, social support has been identified as a significant factor [17, 18]. We have incorporated social support into the theoretical framework. We hypothesize that the health threats serve as kinesiophobia stimuli, such as “dyspnea” and “pain”. When COPD patients perceive these stimuli, they develop cognitive and emotional representations that determine their coping strategies in the presence of social support. Therefore, the proposed theoretical framework includes the following components: kinesiophobia stimuli, cognition, emotion, social support, and coping strategies.

Literature review

We searched for literature related to the development and validation of kinesiophobia scales from PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and the Chinese Biomedical Literature. The search covered publications from their inception to November 2023. Using PubMed as an example, the search strategy was as follows: #1 “kinesiophobia”[Title/Abstract] OR “fear of Movement”[Title/Abstract] OR “fear of activity”[Title/Abstract] OR “movement phobia”[Title/Abstract] OR “movement fear”[Title/Abstract] OR “kinesophobia”[Title/Abstract] OR “Kinetophobia”[Title/Abstract]; #2 “assessment tool”[Title/Abstract] OR “assessment instrument”[Title/Abstract] OR “scale”[Title/Abstract] OR “questionnaire”[Title/Abstract] OR “evaluation tool”[Title/Abstract] OR “evaluation instrument”[Title/Abstract];#3 “development”[Title/Abstract] OR “validation”[Title/Abstract] OR “construction”[Title/Abstract] OR “reliability and validity test”[Title/Abstract]; #1 AND #2 AND #3。Inclusion criteria: (1) original studies related to the development, validation, or adaptation of the kinesiophobia scales; (2) participants aged 18 years or older. Exclusion criteria: (1) duplicate publications; (2) non -English or Chinese language publications; (3) unavailable full-text, incomplete, or ambiguous data; (4) publication types including conference, abstracts, or case reports. Two trained researchers independently screened the literature for inclusion and exclusion criteria and cross-checked their findings. In case of any disagreements, a third researcher decided whether to include the literature.

Qualitative interviews

Using purposive sampling, we conducted semi-structured interviews with outpatients and inpatients with COPD from a tertiary hospital in Guangzhou between December 2023 and February 2024. The interview outline was developed based on the theoretical framework and pre-interviews with two COPD patients, covering the following questions: (1) Do you regularly perform pulmonary rehabilitation? Could you tell me more about the exercises you do (e.g., breathing training, walking exercises)? (2) How do you feel about these exercises? (3) What kinds of support have you received during the exercise? The participant’s inclusion criteria: (1) meeting the guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of COPD [19]; (2) lung function is classified into stages II - IV according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease(GOLD) [20]; (3) age between 40 and 80 years; (4) those who are aware of their diagnosis; (5) conscious and without communication barriers; (6) able to provide informed consent and voluntarily agree to participate in this study. Exclusion criteria: (1) comorbid conditions such as unstable angina, severe arrhythmia, acute heart failure, uncontrolled hypertension, or severe hepatic, renal, or cerebral diseases; (2) those with limb mobility impairments or contraindications to exercise. Data extraction from interviews followed the principle of information saturation. We recruited 13 patients with COPD (mean age 70.8 ± 5.8 years). The interview was conducted by a trained clinical nurse, and the interview recording was transcribed within 24 h. Data analysis adhered to Colaizzi’s seven-step method [21].

Expert consultation

A consultation questionnaire was developed and sent via email or WeChat to 22 experts in relevant fields for a Delphi survey to revise the items and dimensions. These experts consisted of nurses, doctors, and professors who came from medical institutions or universities. The evaluation indicators for the consultation included: response rate, authority coefficient (Cr), and Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W). The expert inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) at least 10 years of professional experience in respiratory, rehabilitation, or psychology; (2) holds a mid-level or senior professional title; (3) has a bachelor’s degree or higher; (4) voluntarily participates in the research.

Stage II items exploration and psychometric tests

Participants and samples

A convenience sample of inpatients and outpatients with COPD was recruited from a tertiary hospital in Guangdong Province. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were consistent with those described in the qualitative study. A total of 355 participants were planned for recruitment. In stage IIA: Based on a fivefold calculation of the initial 24 items and a 10% dropout rate [14], the sample size was 133. In stage IIB, the sample size for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) is at least 200 [22]. Considering a 10% dropout rate, the sample size was 222.

Other measures

The Modified Medical Research Council Dyspnea Scale (mMRC) was used to assess the severity of dyspnea in patients with COPD, graded 0–4. Higher grades indicate more severe dyspnea [20]; The COPD Assessment Test (CAT) was used to evaluate the impact of COPD on patients’quality of life, with total scores ranging from 0 to 40. Higher scores indicate a more severe impact [20]. The 6 min Walk Test (6MWT) [23] was employed to assess exercise capacity in COPD patients, and the results were presented as 6 min Walk Distance(6MWD). A longer 6MWD distance reflects better cardiopulmonary function and exercise endurance.

Date collection

Data were collected in two stages. From April to May 2024, 133 questionnaires were distributed in stage IIA. From June to September 2024, 222 questionnaires were distributed in stage IIB, and participants’ mMRC, CAT, and 6MWD data were also collected. The questionnaire survey was conducted in outpatient clinics or quiet hospital rooms. The investigator explained the background and purpose of the study and obtained the participant’s informed consent before starting the survey. The questionnaire was administered face-to-face and was primarily self-administered, with patients reading and answering the questions independently. Researchers provided explanations using consistent and standardized language only when participants actively raised questions about a specific item, and did not interfere with the participants’ responses. The 6MWT was conducted in accordance with the expert consensus of the Chinese Medical Association, in a 30-meter straight, unobstructed indoor corridor free from pedestrian interference [23].

Date analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 27 and Amos version 28. Continuous variables were described by mean ± standard deviation (‾χ ± S)or median and interquartile range M(P25, P75). Categorical variables were presented using frequency(percentage). Data analysis was conducted using item analysis, pearson or spearman correlation analysis, exploratory factor analysis (EFA), and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Details of item exploration and psychometric tests are as follows.

Items evaluation and preliminary exploration

(1) Items were individually deleted to observe the Cronbach’s α coefficient. If the coefficient increased after deleting an item, the item was considered for removal [24]; (2) Participants were grouped based on their preliminary scale scores; a high-score group comprised the top 27%, and a low-score group comprised the bottom 27%. Items with a Critical Ratio (CR) < 3.0 or a significance level (P > 0.05) were evaluated for deletion [22]; (3) The correlation coefficient between each item score and the total score was calculated. Items with a correlation coefficient r < 0.4 or P > 0.05 were considered for removal [25]; (4) EFA was performed to preliminarily explore the factor structure, and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) ≥ 0.6 and P < 0.05 in Bartlett’s test of sphericity, indicating that factor analysis is appropriate. Factors were extracted by principal component analysis with orthogonal(varimax) rotation method. EFA was run stepwise, and items with factor loadings < 0.5 or cross-loadings less than 0.15 difference from an item’s highest factor loading were considered for deletion [22, 26–29].

Psychometric tests

Reliability analysis

Internal consistency: Cronbach’s α coefficient was calculated. Test-retest reliability (r): The correlation coefficient between scale scores obtained from 30 participants at a two-week interval. Split-half reliability (R): The scale items were split into two groups based on odd and even item numbers. The correlation coefficient between the scores of the two groups was calculated, and R was computed using the Spearman-Brown formula [14].

Validity analysis

Content validity

Six experts were invited to evaluate the relevance of items to the measured content. A 4-point Likert scale was used for scoring, ranging from 1 (not relevant) to 4 (very relevant). The evaluation indicators included the Item-Level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) and Scale-Level Content Validity Index (S-CVI). The S-CVI was further divided into Unanimity Agreement (S-CVI/UA) and Average (S-CVI/Ave) [30].

Construct validity

EFA and CFA were conducted to confirm construct validity. EFA was described in the preceding section. CFA model fit indices included χ²/df, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), Normed Fit Index (NFI), Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), Incremental Fit Index (IFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI) [22].

Criterion-related validity

Evaluating the correlation between scale scores and mMRC, CAT, and 6MWD to assess the criterion-related validity of the scale.

Ethical considerations

The ethics approval was conducted by the Ethics Committee at The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine (Guangdong Provincial Hospital of Chinese Medicine) with the ethics approval number YM2024-018. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. While interviewees provided written consent, others were exempted with the approval of the hospital’s Ethics Committee.

Results

Instrument development

A total of 1,423 articles were retrieved (with 6 additional articles supplemented through reference tracing). Following the removal of 232 duplicates, 1168 were excluded after reviewing titles and abstracts, and a further 12 were excluded after full-text reading. 11 articles were ultimately included, from which 8 items were retained. Five themes and 14 subthemes were extracted through qualitative interviews. The five themes are “multiple factors contribute to kinesiophobia”, “cognitions of pulmonary rehabilitation”, “emotional responses toward pulmonary rehabilitation”, “social support”, and “coping strategies”. Two rounds of expert correspondence consultations were conducted. In the first and second rounds, 19 (86.3%) and 20 (90.9%) experts responded, with Cr of 0.85 and 0.88, and W of 0.107 and 0.123 (both P < 0.05), respectively. Based on the first-round feedback, 9 items were revised, 3 items were deleted, and 3 items were added. Following the second round, 3 items were revised, and 1 item was deleted. Finally, a preliminary scale was developed, consisting of 5 dimensions and 24 items.

Items evaluation and preliminary exploration

The results of the item analysis are presented in Supplemental Table S1. Item B8 was considered for deletion because its removal increased the scale’s Cronbach’s α (0.924 to 0.926), while its absolute value of CR was < 3 and its item-total correlation was < 0.4. Four rounds of EFA were performed. In the first EFA, the KMO value was 0.880, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was also significant (χ2 = 1473.4, P < 0.001). Five factors (all eigenvalues > 1) were extracted. A total of 7 items were removed as a result of unreasonable factor loadings or excessively high cross-loadings, as detailed in supplemental Table S2-S4. In the final round EFA, the KMO value was 0.879, and Bartlett’s sphericity test was significant (χ²=1000.5, P<0.001). Five factors were ultimately extracted, explaining 70.70% of the cumulative variance (Table 2). Although the fifth factor had an eigenvalue of less than 1, it was retained by the research team based on the theoretical framework and qualitative interview findings. The factors were named “stimuli for kinesiophobia,” “cognition,” “emotion,” “social support,” and “coping strategies,” as shown in Table 2.

Reliability of the scale

Validity test

The content validity of this scale is represented by the CVI. The I-CVI ranges from 0.83 to 1.00, the S-CVI/UA is 0.824, and the S-CVI/Ave is 0.971. EFA and CFA were used to sequentially assess the scale’s structure validity. As detailed earlier, the EFA extracted five factors, accounting for 70.7% of the cumulative variance, with factor loadings showing a reasonable distribution. The CFA model fit indices were: χ²/df = 1.555, RMSEA = 0.053, SRMR = 0.048, GFI = 0.906, NFI = 0.890, IFI = 0.958, TLI = 0.946, CFI = 0.957 (Figure S1). Criterion-related validity was demonstrated by the correlation between total scale score and mMRC, CAT, and 6MWD, with coefficients (r) of 0.753, 0.731, and − 0.538, respectively (all P < 0.001) (Table 4).

Discussion

The COPD pulmonary rehabilitation kinesiophobia scale was developed to assess kinesiophobia in COPD patients. Based on the common sense model of illness self-regulation and the fear-avoidance model, it was constructed through a two-phase process of instrument development and psychometric validation, ultimately establishing a five-factor scale.

Factor 2 (kinesiophobia stimuli) reflects multiple causes of exercise-related fear in COPD patients, such as dyspnea, cough, falls or injuries, and concerns about exercise-induced exacerbations. Dyspnea is a threatening sensation that can induce anxiety and panic in COPD patients [31]. Most COPD patients experience exercise-related fear associated with dyspnea, and this fear may be linked to decreased gray matter in the cingulate cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala [17, 32]. Cough is a common symptom among COPD patients, and changes in airflow during exercise may contribute to coughing episodes, consequently leading to kinesiophobia. This may be related to their increased sensitivity to cough and airway reactivity [33–35]. The majority of COPD patients are elderly, whose declining physical function leads to impaired balance and increased susceptibility to falls and injuries [36]. Fear of falling often causes them to avoid physical activity or exercise. Exacerbations of COPD typically manifest as worsening respiratory symptoms such as dyspnea, cough, and sputum. Exercise may precipitate these symptoms, further promoting COPD patients’ concerns about exercise-induced worsening of their condition. Factor 1 (Cognition) reflects COPD patients’ perception of exercise, specifically their perceived decline in activity or exercise capacity, which may contribute to the development of an exercise avoidance attitude. Factor 3 (Emotion) reflects the emotional reactions of COPD patients toward exercise. Positive emotions can promote participation in exercise training, while negative emotions have the opposite effect. Some COPD patients suffer from negative emotions affected by their condition, such as anxiety and depression [37, 38], which lead to a pessimistic attitude toward exercise rehabilitation. Factor 4 (Social support) reflects the importance of social support in pulmonary rehabilitation for patients with COPD, and it may be associated with lower levels of kinesiophobia among these patients [17, 18]. A qualitative interview study of COPD patients also indicated that peer and professional support are key elements in maintaining regular exercise [39]. Factor 5 (Coping strategies) reflects the coping strategies employed by COPD patients when facing kinesiophobia, which are associated with their levels of kinesiophobia. High levels of kinesiophobia are typically linked to reduced activity or exercise levels among COPD patients [17].

Reliability represents the dependability of a measurement tool. The scale reliability analysis results show that the Cronbach’s α is 0.910, the test-retest reliability (r) is 0.844, and the split-half reliability (R) is 0.930(All values > 0.7), demonstrating that the scale possesses good internal consistency, reliability, and stability [22].

Content validity refers to the extent to which a scale reflects the content domain. It is generally accepted that an I-CVI above 0.78, an S-CVI/UA above 0.8, and an S-CVI/Ave above 0.9 indicate good content validity of the scale [30]. The results of this study indicate that the scale’s I-ICV ranged from 0.83 to 1.00, S-CVI/UA was 0.824, and S-CVI/Ave was 0.971. indicating that the scale items effectively reflect the measured content.

Structural validity is described using the results of factor analysis. The EFA indicated that the factor loadings of the five-factor scale were reasonably distributed. In CFA, it is generally considered that a model demonstrates good fit when χ²⁄df ≤ 3, RMSEA and SRMR ≤ 0.05 (the cutoff RMSEA can be relaxed to 0.08), and GFI, NFI, IFI, CFI, and TLI > 0.9 [22, 40, 41]. In this study, except for the NFI, which was slightly below 0.9, all other fit indices reached satisfactory or acceptable levels, revealing a good model fit and further validating the scale’s structure.

The mMRC, CAT, and 6MWD are important instruments for disease management in patients with COPD, and are widely used in clinical settings. Our study further analyzed the correlation between the scale scores and the mMRC grade, CAT score, and 6MWD to evaluate the scale’s criterion-related validity, and the correlation coefficients were 0.753, 0.731, and − 0.538, respectively (all P < 0.001), indicating a moderate to high correlation. Greater dyspnea severity is associated with higher kinesiophobia scores in COPD patients, and similar studies have also reported this positive correlation [18, 42]. This suggests that dyspnea may exacerbate kinesiophobia. Conversely, high levels of kinesiophobia may amplify the perception of dyspnea. Poorer health status in COPD patients is correlated with higher levels of kinesiophobia. This reflects that kinesiophobia is not merely an isolated psychological reaction, but is closely related to the overall disease experience. For individuals with high mMRC and CAT scores, in addition to focusing on comprehensive symptom management, it is essential to specifically assess kinesiophobia. Targeted psychological intervention strategies should be developed for those with high levels of kinesiophobia. The shorter the 6MWD walking distance, the higher the kinesiophobia score in COPD patients, suggesting that exercise capacity is limited not only by physiological factors such as cardiopulmonary function but also by psychological factors. Kinesiophobia may restrict the physical activity and exercise training of COPD patients [9, 43], which can promote the decline of exercise capacity. This decline in exercise capacity further exacerbates kinesiophobia, forming a vicious cycle. When designing pulmonary rehabilitation programs, this scale should be considered as one of the assessment tools to identify COPD patients with high levels of kinesiophobia. For this population, relying on exercise training only may have limited effects on improving exercise capacity, and psychological intervention should be integrated to promote the alleviation of their kinesiophobia. Overall, the correlations between this scale and the mMRC, CAT, and 6MWD collectively support its validity. This scale can partially reflect symptom perception and exercise capacity in patients with COPD, and may serve as a supplementary tool for designing pulmonary rehabilitation programs.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the sample was derived from only one tertiary hospital in Guangzhou, limited by the research conditions. Therefore, the generalizability of the scale requires further investigation. Future studies can expand the sample size to include participants from diverse regions and healthcare institutions, thereby enhancing the applicability of the scale. Secondly, the sample consisted predominantly of older and male patients, and the applicability for younger COPD patients and female COPD patients was inadequately validated. Subsequent research should increase the proportion of younger and female COPD patients to further validate the scale. Thirdly, our study did not evaluate the scale’s cutoff values, responsiveness, or ceiling and floor effects, which limits the scale’s applicability in clinical practice. Future studies should focus on establishing clinically meaningful thresholds, evaluating responsiveness to change, and validating the scale in more diverse populations. Fourthly, the questionnaire collection was not completely self-administered by the participants. This may have introduced bias into the study results. Sixthly, a methodological limitation of this study is its reliance on PCA. PCA focuses on maximizing the total variance explained in the observed variables rather than identifying the common variance underlying latent constructs. Consequently, it may incorporate unique and error variances into the extracted components. This not only compromises the interpretability of the factor structure but may also lead to an overestimation of its quality due to inflated cumulative variance explained. Future research could conduct EFA using Principal Axis Factoring (PAF) or Maximum Likelihood (ML) on a larger independent sample. Finally, we acknowledge that the retention of five factors was based on both theoretical considerations and the overall psychometric evaluation, rather than being unequivocally supported by the parallel analysis.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated the pulmonary rehabilitation kinesiophobia scale for Patients with COPD. This scale consists of 17 items and five factors: kinesiophobia stimuli, cognition, emotion, social support, and coping strategies. It employs a 5-point Likert scale (1–5 points), with total scores ranging from 17 to 85. Higher scores indicate a higher level of kinesiophobia. The scale demonstrates good reliability and validity for assessing exercise-related fear in patients with COPD.
