Health‐Related Quality of Life of Patients Undergoing Haemodialysis: A Structural Equation Modelling Approach
Authors: Moonja Kang, Younhee Jeong, Hyungran Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo develop and test a hypothetical model of the relationships between the health‐related quality of life of patients undergoing haemodialysis and associating factors including autonomy support from healthcare professionals, basic psychological needs, motivation, resilience, self‐management.DesignA cross‐sectional survey design.MethodsData were collected using a self‐report questionnaire at four haemodialysis units, including one hospital and three clinics in Seoul and Gyeonggi‐do, Korea, from March 2 to August 5, 2022. A total of 232 adults participated in this study, with data from 208 participants included in the analysis. The hypothetical model was examined using structural equation modelling.ResultsAutonomy support from healthcare professionals, basic psychological needs, motivation, self‐management, and resilience accounted for 50.4% of the variance in physical quality of life, while autonomy support from healthcare professionals, basic psychological needs, and resilience accounted for 39.2% of the variance in mental quality of life. Basic psychological needs were significantly associated with physical quality of life and mediated the relationship between autonomy support from healthcare professionals and physical quality of life. Basic psychological needs and resilience were significantly associated with mental quality of life. Moreover, basic psychological needs mediated the relationship between autonomy support from healthcare professionals and mental quality of life.ConclusionsAutonomy support from healthcare professionals and basic psychological needs, as well as patients' resilience, significantly impact the health‐related quality of life of patients undergoing haemodialysis.Implications for the Profession and/or Patient CareInterventional programs should be developed that promote autonomy support from health care professionals and enhance patients' basic psychological needs and resilience.Reporting MethodReported in adherence to the STROBE Statement for cross‐sectional studies.Patient or Public ContributionA total of 208 patients undergoing haemodialysis at a university hospital and three artificial kidney clinics participated in this study, and their responses were used in data analysis.Keywords: autonomy, basic psychological needs, haemodialysis, motivation, quality of life, resilience, self‐management

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) has become a growing health concern worldwide (Carney 2020; The Korean Society of Nephrology 2022, November 18). Although survival is the primary goal of treatment for patients with kidney disorders, prolongation of life through haemodialysis (HD) has led to health‐related quality of life (HR‐QOL) becoming an important indicator in the treatment of patients undergoing HD (de Brito et al. 2019; Janssen et al. 2015). Quality of life is the subjective perception of one's own physical, psychological, and social well‐being (Boelhouwer and Noll 2024). Patients with end‐stage renal failure undergoing HD face serious threats to their HR‐QOL because of the numerous symptoms of the disease as well as the physical and mental burden of HD (Pretto et al. 2020; Raoofi et al. 2023). Furthermore, their HR‐QOL is impaired by the psychological distress associated with physical symptoms (Kimmel et al. 2021; Ma and Li 2016). Therefore, it becomes imperative for health professionals to understand the HR‐QOL of patients undergoing HD and implement strategies to improve it.

Background

Previous studies have identified several predictors of the HR‐QOL of HD patients. Those predictors include perceived health status, sociodemographic factors (Engelbrecht et al. 2021; García‐Martínez et al. 2020), symptom experience, and self‐management (Engelbrecht et al. 2021; Jung et al. 2021). Resilience has also been found to play a crucial role in determining the HR‐QOL of such patients (García‐Martínez et al. 2020; Jung et al. 2021). Resilience is the psychosocial ability to attain positive outcomes in a challenging situation by overcoming adversity, adjusting to life, and achieving mental growth (Métais et al. 2022; Sisto et al. 2019). It plays an important role in adapting to chronic health conditions (Jin et al. 2023). It also boosts one's ability to psychologically cope with HD when one must change one's lifestyle majorly because of disease symptoms, diet, and pharmacological treatment (Chen et al. 2023; Izadi Avanji et al. 2021). Furthermore, meeting basic psychological needs has a positive impact on resilience (Liu and Huang 2021).

In addition, patients with chronic conditions, including those undergoing HD, can improve their health when they recognise that they are responsible for their own health and become the main agents of self‐management, thus highlighting the importance of self‐management in addition to treatment. Given that self‐management helps control the disease and prevent complications, patients undergoing HD who demonstrate good adherence to self‐management practices have fewer symptom experiences and better HR‐QOL (Lee et al. 2021). Autonomy and self‐control are two psychological factors strongly associated with successful self‐management among patients undergoing HD (Chen et al. 2018; Yasin et al. 2024), and providing motivation is key to facilitating continued self‐management compliance (Havas et al. 2016; Hussein et al. 2022). The self‐determination theory (SDT) is an important theory commonly employed to understand and predict human motivation and behaviour in various fields, including healthcare (Deci and Ryan 2009). It posits that basic psychological needs—autonomy, competence, and relatedness—influence autonomous motivation and are enhanced by professional support for autonomy (Deci and Ryan 2009).

Healthcare professionals' support of individuals' autonomy significantly affects the latter's basic psychological needs and behavioural outcomes by supporting them in autonomous decision‐making about their health behaviours (Ntoumanis et al. 2021; Ryan et al. 2008). Health professionals, such as physicians and nurses, meet patients' need for relatedness by communicating with and involving them in healthcare decisions. Moreover, when patients perceive high levels of support for their autonomy, their basic psychological needs are met, which in turn may improve both their HR‐QOL and physical and mental health (Chen et al. 2018; Gillison et al. 2019). Thus, when health professionals provide patients with information about the principles of treatment and clinical information, as well as engage in positive interactions that foster trust and psychological adjustment, patients' basic psychological needs (i.e., autonomy, competence, and relatedness) are met. This positively affects patients' mental and physical health (Ng et al. 2012; Ntoumanis et al. 2021).

Thus, autonomy support from healthcare professionals, meeting basic psychological needs, motivation, self‐management, and resilience may predict the HR‐QOL of patients undergoing HD. However, no study has considered resilience, self‐management, autonomy support from healthcare professionals, meeting basic psychological needs, and motivation based on the SDT as the predictors of HR‐QOL.

Aim and Objective

This study aimed to develop a hypothetical model of the relationships among the factors that affect the HR‐QOL of patients undergoing HD (Figure 1) based on the SDT and previous studies and test the hypothetical model with actual data.

Methods

Design, Participants, and Sample Size

In this cross‐sectional study, participants were convenience sampled among adult patients undergoing HD at four haemodialysis units in a hospital and three clinics in Korea. The inclusion criteria were: (1) being diagnosed with CKD by a nephrologist, (2) having regular HD treatments two to three times a week, and (3) being 20 years of age or older. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) unwillingness to participate in the survey, (2) irregular HD cycles, and (3) difficulty responding to the questionnaire due to communication or cognitive issues. The sample size was calculated using an a priori sample size calculator (Soper 2022). For structural equation modelling with an effect size of 0.3, a power of 0.9, six latent variables, and 22 measurement variables, the sample size was calculated to be 200. To account for a dropout rate of 10%, 223 participants were recruited. However, 15 responses were excluded from the final analysis due to incomplete responses. Consequently, the final analysis comprised data from 208 participants.

Data Collection

Data were collected using a self‐report questionnaire administered through a paper and pencil survey from March 2 to August 5, 2022. The eligibility of potential participants was first confirmed through a review of their electronic medical records (EMR). Prior to data collection, one (MK) of the researchers explained the purpose of the study to the participants and screened the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Those who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate were given the questionnaire to complete on their own. It took less than 30 min to complete the survey.

Similarly, disease‐related characteristics, except for the duration of haemodialysis, were also measured as categorical variables, as shown in Table 1. Both age and duration of haemodialysis were collected using an open‐ended question format.

Autonomy Support From Healthcare Professionals

Autonomy support from healthcare professionals was measured using the Korean version (Han and Shin 2010) of the Health Care Climate Questionnaire developed by Williams et al. (1996). This tool consists of 15 items, each rated on a seven‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicate greater perceived autonomy support from healthcare professionals. Reliability (Cronbach's α) of the tool was 0.95 at the time of development (Williams et al. 1996); moreover, it was 0.94 for the Korean‐adapted version (Han and Shin 2010) and 0.95 in this study.

Basic Psychological Needs

Basic psychological needs were measured using the Korean version (Lee and Park 2012) of the Basic Need Satisfaction Scale developed by La Guardia et al. (2000). This 21‐item tool consists of seven items for autonomy, six items for competence, and eight items for relatedness. All items are rated on a seven‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicate greater satisfaction with autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Reliability (Cronbach's α) of the tool was 0.90 at the time of development (La Guardia et al. 2000); moreover, it was 0.86 for the Korean‐adapted version (Lee and Park 2012) and 0.87 in this study.

Motivation

Motivation was assessed using the Korean version (Park 2018) of the Treatment Self‐Regulation Questionnaire that Williams et al. (1998) developed by modifying Ryan and Connell's (1989) Self‐Regulation Questionnaire to measure self‐control regarding health management. This tool consists of eight items for autonomous motivation and 11 for controlled motivation; each item is rated on a seven‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicate greater motivation. Reliability (Cronbach's α) of the tool was 0.80 for autonomous motivation and 0.86 for controlled motivation at the time of development (Williams et al. 1998); moreover, it was 0.72 and 0.79, respectively, for the Korean‐adapted version (Park 2018) and 0.85 and 0.87, respectively, in this study.

Resilience

Resilience was measured using the Korean version (Baek et al. 2010) of the Connor‐Davidson Resilience Scale developed by Connor and Davidson (2003). This 25‐item tool consists of five factors (hardiness, persistence, optimism, support, and spiritual nature), and each item is rated on a five‐point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (almost always). A higher score, from a total score of 0–100, indicates greater resilience. Reliability (Cronbach's α) of the tool was 0.89 at the time of development; moreover, it was 0.93 for the Korean‐adapted version (Baek et al. 2010) and 0.96 in this study.

Self‐Management

Self‐management was measured using the Self‐management Behaviour Scale developed by Song (2000) and modified and adapted by Cho and Choi (2008). This 35‐item tool consists of two items for medication, four items for exercise and rest, three items for social adjustment, six items for diet, 11 items for the management of physical problems, six items for fistula management, and three items for the management of blood pressure and body weight. Each item is rated on a five‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (generally not doing well) to 5 (doing very well), with a higher score indicating higher self‐management compliance. Reliability (Cronbach's α) of the tool was 0.85 at the time of development (Song 2000), 0.88 in the study by Cho and Choi (2008) and 0.94 in this study.

Health‐Related Quality of Life

Physical and mental QOL was measured using the Korean version (Han et al. 2004) of the Medical Outcomes Study Questionnaire Short‐Form 36 (MOS SF‐36) developed by Ware Jr. and Sherbourne (1992). Physical QOL was assessed using the physical component summary score, which summed the scores for four domains (physical functioning, role limitation‐physical, bodily pain, and general health) in the MOS SF‐36 and was converted into a norm‐based score for analysis. Mental QOL was assessed using the mental component summary score, which summed the scores for four domains (vitality, social functioning, role limitation‐emotional, and mental health) in the MOS SF‐36 and was converted into a norm‐based score for analysis. Higher scores indicate higher HR‐QOL. Reliability (Cronbach's α) of the tool was 0.88–0.92 at the time of development (Ware Jr. and Sherbourne 1992), and it was 0.93–0.94 for the Korean version (Han et al. 2004) and 0.91–0.92 in this study.

Data Analysis

Participant characteristics and the major study variables were analysed using descriptive statistics, namely percentage, mean, and standard deviation, and the sample's normality was examined using skewness and kurtosis. Correlations among the measurement variables were analysed using Pearson's correlation coefficient, and multicollinearity was tested. The measurement model's validity was evaluated using confirmatory factor analysis.

In structural equation modelling, it is recommended to use multiple indicators or variables rather than single variables to measure a latent variable. When the use of a single variable cannot be avoided, item parcelling is recommended to increase reliability, enhance normality, and reduce complexity (Coffman and MacCallum 2005). Item parcelling means aggregating individual items into one or more ‘parcels’, taking factor loadings into account, and using those parcels instead of items as indicators of the target latent construct (Bae 2021; Hall et al. 1999). In this study, the questionnaire for autonomy support from healthcare professionals consists of a single variable with 15 items, so item parcelling was performed to configure it into three measurement variables.

The structural equation model was analysed with maximum likelihood and bootstrapping. According to the recommendation of Shrout and Bolger (2002), the mediation effect was examined using phantom variables. The fit of the hypothetical model was tested using the chi‐squared test, Tucker‐Lewis index (TLI), comparative fit index (CFI), standardised root mean squared residual (SRMR), and root mean squared error of approximation (RMSEA). Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0 and IBM AMOS 25.0 (IBM 2017a, 2017b) software.

Informed consent was obtained from all participants since all participants completed and signed the consent form to participate in the study. The procedures and design of this study were consistent with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyung Hee University Hospital at Gangdong. (No. KHNMC 2022‐01‐022‐001). All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

Descriptive Statistics

A total of 223 participants were initially recruited for the study, with 15 subsequently excluded from the final analysis due to incomplete responses. The final analysis included data from 208 participants, resulting in a response rate of 93.3% and an attrition rate of 6.7%.

There were 139 (66.8%) men and 69 (33.2%) women, with a mean age of 61.85 (±12.96). Most participants were unemployed (70.2%) and lived with their spouses (63%), whereas 15.4% lived alone. Most participants had a monthly household income of ≥ 2400 US dollars (37.5%) and a high school diploma or higher educational achievements (81.7%; Table 1).

The most common cause of CKD was diabetic nephropathy (44.2%), followed by hypertensive glomerulosclerosis (21.2%), chronic glomerulonephritis (19.2%) and other causes (15.4%). The mean duration of HD was 4.88 (±6.13) years, and most patients (92.8%) underwent three HDs per week. More patients were without complications (53.8%) than with complications (46.2%; Table 1).

The absolute values of skewness and kurtosis were below 2 and 7, respectively, confirming that all measurement variables met the assumption of normality. Furthermore, the correlation coefficient among variables was below 0.8, and the variance inflation factor was below 10, at 1.17–2.35, confirming the absence of multicollinearity (Table 2).

Construct Validity of the Measurement Model

The measurement model's construct validity was evaluated using convergent and discriminant validity. The variables' convergent validity was measured using construct reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE), and it was established based on a CR of 0.81–0.98 and AVE of 0.57–0.92 (see Table S1). Discriminant validity was measured using the latent variables' correlation coefficients and a 95% confidence interval (CI). The CI of all correlation coefficients among the variables did not include −1 or 1, indicating that discriminant validity was established (see Table S2).

The most potent predictor of physical QOL in patients undergoing HD was basic psychological needs. Autonomy support from healthcare professionals affected physical QOL through the mediation of basic psychological needs. Resilience increased with increasing basic psychological needs. Although resilience did not directly affect physical QOL (β = 0.18, p = 0.331), it indirectly affected physical QOL through self‐management (β = −0.10, p = 0.008). Physical QOL increased with increased autonomy support from healthcare professionals (β = 0.29, p = 0.001), basic psychological needs (β = 0.68, p < 0.001), and resilience (β = 0.08, p = 0.012) as presented in Figure 2 and Table 4. However, the relationship between self‐management and physical QOL was more complex, showing a weak positive correlation but a statistically significant negative direct effect when controlling for other variables (β = −0.21, p = 0.019).

Mental QOL increased with increasing autonomy support from healthcare professionals, basic psychological needs, and resilience. Basic psychological needs (β = 0.38, p = 0.005) and resilience (β = 0.31, p = 0.002) directly affected mental QOL, whereas autonomy support from healthcare professionals affected mental QOL through the mediation of basic psychological needs (β = 0.25, p < 0.01). Motivation (β = −0.01, p = 0.519) and self‐management (β = −0.06, p = 0.489) did not significantly affect mental QOL.

Additionally, resilience mediated the relationship between basic psychological needs and self‐management (β = 0.44, p = 0.001 in physical QOL model; β = 0.44, p = 0.001 in mental QOL model).

Discussion

This study aimed to develop and test a hypothetical model for the predictors of the HR‐QOL of patients undergoing HD based on the SDT by Deci and Ryan (2009) and a literature review.

Our structural model showed that autonomy support from healthcare professionals affects the patients' basic psychological needs, subsequently affecting their motivation and self‐management, thus supporting Deci and Ryan's (2009) SDT. Additionally, autonomy support from healthcare professionals, basic psychological needs, motivation, self‐management, and resilience explained 50.4% of the variance in physical QOL, while autonomy support from healthcare professionals, basic psychological needs, and resilience explained 39.2% of the variance in mental QOL. These results are similar to the 24%–58.6% of variance in the QOL of patients undergoing HD, as explained by personal characteristics (Choi and Choi 2020; García‐Martínez et al. 2020), uncertainty (Choi and Choi 2020), self‐efficacy (Kang and Seo 2019), depression (Kang and Seo 2019), self‐management (Chae et al. 2020; Choi and Choi 2020), and resilience (Chen et al. 2023; Choi and Choi 2020; García‐Martínez et al. 2020). As a result of several meta‐analyses applying SDT in the health care setting, it has been proven that autonomous qualities of motivation and basic psychological need satisfactions are positively related to various health behaviours (Ryan et al. 2022). Therefore, our structural model, developed based on the SDT, showed a statistically significant fit in explaining the HR‐QOL of patients undergoing HD.

Physical and Mental QOL

In our study, participants had a mean physical QOL of 41.09 (±8.49) and a mean mental QOL of 43.19 (±11.32). These physical QOL scores were similar to those reported in a multicenter study of 6930 chronic HD patients from a different country (Rostami et al. 2013), which showed a mean physical QOL of 40.79 (±20.1). However, our mental QOL score was lower than the mean of 47.79 (±18.31) reported by Rostami et al. This discrepancy in mental QOL may be partially due to differences in general demographic characteristics, particularly age, as our study's mean participant age (61.85 ± 12.96 years) was higher than that in Rostami et al. (54.4 ± 17.1 years). Previous research suggests that aging decreases physical and mental functioning, which may impact QOL scores (Amarya et al. 2018; Grevendonk et al. 2021).

Basic Psychological Needs

Our findings indicate that basic psychological needs directly affect physical QOL in patients undergoing HD, while both basic psychological needs and resilience directly affect mental QOL. Addressing autonomy, competence, and relatedness is essential in healthcare settings to enhance QOL. Fostering an environment that supports these psychological needs can motivate patients to actively engage in self‐care and improve their health and well‐being (Ryan et al. 2008; Williams et al. 1998). Interventions focused on supporting these needs may strengthen both physical and mental QOL by promoting psychological well‐being.

Autonomy Support

Autonomy support from healthcare professionals was found to impact both physical and mental QOL, mediated by basic psychological needs. Patients undergoing HD often rely on supportive relationships from family, friends, and healthcare providers to cope effectively with their illness (Cha 2017; Jung and Kim 2020). According to Self‐Determination Theory (SDT), autonomy support enables patients to become more involved in their treatment, improving adherence and engagement in self‐care behaviours, which ultimately enhances QOL (Ryan et al. 2008). Therefore, training programs that educate healthcare professionals on providing autonomy support may positively influence QOL in HD patients.

Self‐Management

Autonomy support, basic psychological needs, and motivation significantly influenced self‐management adherence. However, in our study, self‐management did not positively impact mental QOL. Prior findings on self‐management and QOL have been mixed, with studies reporting positive, negative, or no significant effects (Cha 2015; Kang and Seo 2019; Keivan et al. 2023). Some research suggests that strict adherence to HD guidelines can create stress, impairing mental health (Cleary and Drennan 2005). Additional studies are needed to further investigate the factors that mediate or moderate the relationship between self‐management and QOL, especially in chronic illness management.

Resilience

Resilience was found to significantly impact mental QOL but not physical QOL, consistent with prior findings that emphasise resilience as a resource that primarily supports mental well‐being in patients with chronic conditions (Kukihara et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2020). The core elements of resilience, such as optimism, persistence, and social support, may more strongly influence mental health than physical functioning. Enhancing resilience through interventions like cognitive behavioural therapy or psychoeducation may promote mental QOL in HD patients (Liu et al. 2020). Nursing programs focused on resilience can support patients' psychological adjustment to chronic illness, improving HR‐QOL.

Implications for SDT‐Based Interventions

The significance of this study lies in its confirmation of Self‐Determination Theory (SDT) for HD patients and the exploration of resilience within an SDT‐based theoretical model. By identifying predictors of HR‐QOL and the pathways through which they influence HR‐QOL, this study provides empirical evidence for intervention strategies aimed at enhancing HR‐QOL in HD patients. Our findings suggest that autonomy support from healthcare providers positively affects patients' psychological needs, motivation, and self‐management, underscoring SDT's relevance in this context. Furthermore, the study demonstrates that resilience plays a critical role in supporting self‐management, providing a deeper understanding of the complex interplay of factors influencing HR‐QOL.

These results have practical implications for developing and implementing targeted interventions to enhance HR‐QOL in HD patients. Specifically, training programs for healthcare professionals to improve autonomy support could facilitate patient motivation and adherence, ultimately benefiting HR‐QOL. A meta‐analysis of 73 SDT‐informed studies similarly showed that interventions enhancing autonomy, competence, and relatedness lead to sustained health behaviour changes, reinforcing the potential of SDT‐based interventions in healthcare settings (Ntoumanis et al. 2021). This study thus provides a foundation for future nursing interventions informed by SDT and resilience to improve the well‐being of HD patients.

Strengths and Limitations of the Work

While this study contributes significant insights, it has certain limitations. As a cross‐sectional study, it cannot capture the impact of variables on HR‐QOL over time, limiting the understanding of causal relationships. Additionally, due to the use of convenience sampling, generalisability may be restricted. Future research would benefit from diversifying sampling methods and employing longitudinal designs to enhance the robustness of findings. Despite these limitations, the study is notable for its comprehensive theoretical framework, rigorous statistical analysis, and practical implications. It contributes to a nuanced understanding of the factors affecting HD patients' HR‐QOL, offering a basis for developing interventions and informing future research in the field of nursing care.

Conclusions

In this study, we established and tested a hypothetical model identifying the relationships among factors affecting the HR‐QOL of patients undergoing HD using structural equation modelling. The findings of this study suggest that autonomy support from healthcare professionals and patients' basic psychological needs have an important effect on the physical QOL of patients undergoing HD, whereas their mental QOL is influenced by autonomy support from healthcare professionals, basic psychological needs, and resilience. In line with Deci and Ryan's (2009) SDT, autonomy support from healthcare professionals, basic psychological needs, and motivation significantly predict self‐management; however, further research is needed on the relationship between self‐management and HR‐QOL. Additionally, our study highlights the role of resilience as a crucial mediating factor, emphasising the need to develop and maintain programs that enhance both basic psychological needs and resilience. By addressing these areas, we can provide better support to patients in managing their health conditions and improving their overall well‐being.
