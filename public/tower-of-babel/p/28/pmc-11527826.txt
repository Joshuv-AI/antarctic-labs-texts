Adherence and treatment discontinuation of oral semaglutide and once‐weekly semaglutide injection at 12 month follow‐up: Japanese real‐world data
Authors: Takeshi Horii, Chikako Masudo, Yui Takayanagi, Yoichi Oikawa, Akira Shimada, Kiyoshi Mihara
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdherence and treatment continuation rates of the glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonist (GLP‐1RA) semaglutide for both oral (O‐SEMA) and subcutaneous injection (SEMA‐SC) remain unknown in real‐world clinical practice. This retrospective observational study compared the 12 month adherence and treatment discontinuation of O‐SEMA and once‐weekly SEMA‐SC in patients with type 2 diabetes using a real‐world claims database. SEMA‐SC initiators were 1:1 propensity score‐matched to O‐SEMA initiators. Non‐adherence was defined as <0.8 of the proportion of days covered. SEMA‐SC had a significantly higher odds ratio (OR) for non‐adherence than O‐SEMA (OR: 1.39). The hazard ratio for treatment discontinuation, using O‐SEMA as the reference, was 1.45 for SEMA‐SC, although the discontinuation rate of O‐SEMA was higher during the early stage. O‐SEMA initiators showed significantly higher adherence and greater persistence in therapy than SEMA‐SC initiators at 12 months, which could lead to earlier initiation of GLP‐1RA treatment.

INTRODUCTION

According to the American Diabetes Association Standards of Care in Diabetes 2024 1 , glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RAs) are a feasible treatment option for type 2 diabetes because they effectively lower glucose levels and are associated with the highest potential for weight loss. Large‐scale randomized comparative trials have demonstrated that once‐daily liraglutide, once‐weekly semaglutide subcutaneous injections (SEMA‐SC), and dulaglutide reduce the incidence of cardiovascular events in patients with type 2 diabetes at high risk of cardiovascular events 2 , 3 , 4 . One factor hindering the proactive use of traditional GLP‐1RAs is the need for subcutaneous administration, given that some patients prefer oral medications to injectables 5 , 6 , and a reported decline in treatment adherence with injections 6 , 7 . Following the development of injectable formulations, oral semaglutide (O‐SEMA) was developed and approved for managing type 2 diabetes. The oral formulation is more beneficial than the injectable formulation owing to improved treatment continuation rates and adherence, providing a new option for the management of type 2 diabetes that could enable the early initiation of GLP‐1RA therapy. Although O‐SEMA offers the advantage of oral administration, the complexity of its unique dosing and administration methods may render daily use cumbersome. Once‐weekly administration of SEMA‐SC may achieve high adherence. Nevertheless, the superiority of O‐SEMA over SEMA‐SC in terms of treatment continuation rates and adherence in real‐world clinical practice is yet to be comprehensively established. Herein, we examined adherence and treatment discontinuation rates of O‐SEMA and SEMA‐SC using real‐world Japanese data.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This real‐world, retrospective cohort study was performed using administrative claims data between April 1, 2008, and December 31, 2022, from the Medical Data Vision (MDV) administrative claims database, a nationwide hospital‐based claims database. The observation start date was the date when O‐SEMA or SEMA‐SC was first prescribed. Following the standard dosage regimen, SEMA‐O was initiated at 3 mg, while SEMA‐SC was started at 0.25 mg weekly. Dosage adjustments for each medication were performed at the discretion of the physician.

Study population

The MDV database, comprising 4,702,688 registered patients, was used to extract data on patients diagnosed with type 2 diabetes (International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition (ICD‐10), code: E10‐E14). Figure S1 shows the flowchart of participant selection.

The study included new users (n = 12,280) of O‐SEMA (n = 6,140) and SEMA‐SC (n = 6,140).

Identification of outcomes

The primary outcome was the frequency of discontinuation of O‐SEMA and SEMA‐SC over a 12 month period. Additionally, we investigated factors associated with non‐adherence to semaglutide during the 12 month observation period.

Medication adherence was measured using the proportion of days covered (PDC) over a 12 month period. PDC is calculated using the formula: PDC = [number of days that the patient has access to semaglutide during the observation period (days)/observation period (days)]. PDC values ≥0.8 were defined as adherent; PDC <0.8 was deemed as non‐adherent 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 SEMA‐SC was calculated per single dose lasting 7 days.

Patient characteristics

Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, and body mass index (BMI), were obtained using data from billing records during the study period. Obesity was defined according to Japanese guidelines (BMI <18.5, 18.5 to <22, and ≥22 kg/m2) 12 .

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed data are expressed as mean ± SD and were analyzed using an unpaired t‐test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test and are expressed as absolute numbers or percentages. To minimize or eliminate potential confounding effects, differences in baseline characteristics (age, sex, BMI, and concomitant antidiabetic use) were adjusted using 1:1 propensity score matching with the nearest‐neighbor matching method 13 . The propensity score was 0.1314; therefore, the caliper coefficient for propensity score matching was set to 0.02628, which is a factor of 0.2. After matching, hazard ratios (HRs) for endpoints were analyzed using a Cox proportional hazard model. All statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 29.0.2; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

We performed a Kaplan–Meier analysis of the incidence of treatment discontinuation during the observation period in patients with type 2 diabetes using O‐SEMA or SEMA‐SC. SEMA‐SC was associated with a significantly higher cumulative incidence of treatment discontinuation than O‐SEMA (P < 0.001, log‐rank test). Using O‐SEMA as the reference, the HR for SEMA‐SC discontinuation (HR, 1.45) differed significantly (Figure 1).

We evaluated HRs for treatment discontinuation according to baseline clinical characteristics of patients using semaglutide (O‐SEMA and SEMA‐SC; Figure 2). For patients with a BMI <18.5 kg/m2 (HR: 2.00), the HRs for treatment discontinuation were significantly higher in the semaglutide group (Figure 2). DISCUSSION

Patients who were prescribed O‐SEMA exhibited lower treatment discontinuation rates and better adherence than those prescribed SEMA‐SC. The ease of administration may influence differences in treatment discontinuation rates and adherence. The O‐SEMA discontinuation rate was higher than that of SEMA‐SC during the early treatment stages. Population pharmacokinetic and exposure/response analyses revealed that the exposure range following O‐SEMA administration is wider than that of SEMA‐SC 14 , 15 , with significant individual variability in pharmacokinetics. Given that fluid intake and concomitant medications can greatly affect absorption 16 , unexpected side effects, or poor adherence may lead to treatment discontinuation during the initial administration period.

Background characteristics were most substantially associated with treatment discontinuation in patients with a BMI < 18.5 kg/m2. Semaglutide elicited the highest weight‐loss effect compared with other GLP‐1RAs, according to the ADA/EASD consensus 17 . Side effect‐related treatment discontinuation may frequently occur, as individuals with a lower body weight are more prone to semaglutide‐induced gastrointestinal side effects 18 . In patients with a low BMI at semaglutide initiation, the possibility of excessive weight loss may complicate continued therapy. The HR trends differed based on sex, age, and insulin use. The risk of side effects, such as nausea, due to sex differences has been poorly explored, and underlying reasons could not be clearly identified, indicating the need for further investigations. Patients using insulin or older individuals may have a greater likelihood of experiencing side effects such as nausea, owing to factors such as longer duration of diabetes and polypharmacy 19 , suggesting that differences in HR trends between O‐SEMA and SEMA‐SC could be attributed to interactions in these groups.

The OR for non‐adherence was significantly higher for SEMA‐SC, which could be attributed to the reduced treatment adherence when patients perceive treatment with injectable drugs as difficult or inconvenient 6 , 7 . Importantly, both oral and subcutaneous administration achieved an average PDC > 90%, which is higher than that reported previously 20 , 21 , suggesting that good adherence was maintained throughout the 12 month period from initiation.

An important limitation of this study was the unavailability of data regarding confounding factors, such as laboratory values and all concomitant medications that could impact outcomes, which were not considered in the present study. In addition to adverse effects, inadequate glucose‐lowering effects could underlie semaglutide discontinuation. However, as this study did not confirm the trend in HbA1c levels, the impact of these factors cannot be reasonably discussed. Given that O‐SEMA and SEMA‐SC reportedly exert equivalent glucose‐lowering effects 22 , HbA1c levels may not substantially impact treatment continuity. Conversely, previous studies 23 comparing the treatment continuity of metformin and DPP‐4 inhibitors have indicated that the risk of treatment discontinuation increases with higher HbA1c levels. Therefore, it is important to pay attention to this factor in the patient cohort targeted in the present study. Secondly, there is a marked reduction in the number at risk for treatment discontinuation, as shown in Figure 1, indicating the potential for substantial treatment disruption among patients who are not actually followed. The presence of dropout cases may influence the interpretation of the results, which is a limitation of this study design. However, this aspect could also be a strength of the study, as it may better reflect real‐world conditions. We intend to conduct additional investigations using a database with high continuity of observation.

O‐SEMA exhibited superior adherence and continuation rates to SEMA‐SC in Japanese patients with type 2 diabetes. Our findings may aid in guiding healthcare professionals when planning treatment strategies using semaglutide.
