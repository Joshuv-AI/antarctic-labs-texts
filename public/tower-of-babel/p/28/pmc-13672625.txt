Impact of kidney maturation on aminoglycoside exposure in term-born pediatric patients: an in silico study
Authors: Felix J Meigel, Ana Catalina Alvarez-Elías, Rasha Hussein, Doris H Fuertinger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKidney function determines aminoglycoside clearance in early life, but its maturation is insufficiently reflected in weight- and age-based dosing. Using in silico studies, we evaluate how kidney function maturation and growth influence aminoglycoside exposure and associated toxicity risks across pediatric development.MethodsWe performed an in silico pharmacokinetic study using a two-compartment model parameterized from pediatric data. Age-homogeneous virtual term-born pediatric cohorts (1 day to 12 years; total N = 10,000) were generated from WHO growth standards and reference values for measured glomerular filtration rates (mGFR). Primary analyses simulated guideline gentamicin dosing (4 mg/kg every 24 h in neonates, 7 mg/kg every 24 h in infants/children) and assessed peak (8–12, 15–20 mg/L) and trough (< 1, < 0.5 mg/L) targets on days 1–10. Amikacin and tobramycin were evaluated in secondary analyses.ResultsWeight and mGFR exhibited different maturation trajectories. Despite guideline-based weight-normalized gentamicin dosing, substantial variation in target attainment was observed. Peak target attainment increased from 34.2% to 70.0%. Trough target attainment increased from < 10% to > 90%, peaking around 2 years of age. Marked age-related heterogeneity persisted within infants: trough target attainment increased > 65% in one year. Sensitivity analyses indicated that exposure was more responsive to changes in glomerular filtration than to weight.ConclusionsGlomerular filtration maturation is a dominant driver of aminoglycoside exposure in early life. Standard weight-based dosing does not ensure target attainment across the pediatric age range. This supports the development of physiology-informed, model-based dosing strategies accounting for glomerular filtration maturation to improve efficacy while reducing toxicity risks.Graphical abstract A higher-resolution version of the Graphical abstract is available as Supplementary informationSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00467-026-07420-5.Keywords: Glomerular filtration rate, Nephrotoxicity, Treatment optimization, Aminoglycosides, Pharmacokinetics, In silico simulation methods

Introduction

Aminoglycoside antibiotics are cleared predominantly via glomerular filtration [1–3], making their systemic exposure highly sensitive to postnatal maturation of glomerular filtration function in early life. Although aminoglycosides, like gentamicin, amikacin, or tobramycin, are essential therapeutic agents in neonatal and pediatric infectious disease management for severe Gram-negative infections [4, 5], their use is tempered by well-documented risks of ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity [3, 6–8]. Aminoglycoside-associated acute kidney injury (AKI) has been reported in 5% to 15% of treatments [9, 10], and up to 70% of affected children have persistent kidney function impairment after six months of treatment [11]. In line with recent pediatric nephrotoxin stewardship initiatives such as Nephrotoxic Injury Negated by Just-in-Time Action (NINJA) and Baby NINJA [12–14], which emphasize prevention over management of nephrotoxic injury, maturation-informed aminoglycoside dosing represents an important opportunity to reduce avoidable nephrotoxic exposure during early childhood.

Aminoglycoside clearance mirrors the postnatal trajectory of glomerular filtration rate function maturation, which continuously increases during infancy before reaching a plateau in early childhood [15–18]. While tubular maturation influences intracellular uptake and toxicity through receptor-mediated endocytosis in proximal tubular cells, systemic clearance is predominantly driven by glomerular filtration rate (GFR) [7, 19]. Paracellular reabsorption within the tubules appears minimal for aminoglycosides [19]. Although the dependence of aminoglycoside clearance, and thus systemic exposure, on GFR changes is well established [1, 20, 21], its implications for age-stratified dosing remain underexplored. Despite a steep developmental gradient, conventional pediatric dosing guidelines, including the WHO Pocket book of hospital care for children [22], the Manual of Childhood Infections: The Blue Book [23], and the Red Book: Report of the Committee on Infectious Diseases [24], apply a uniform weight-based regimen after the neonatal period, without adjustment for maturational increases in GFR [25]. A recent study by Hollander et al. [26] highlights poor attainment of therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) target peak and trough levels in Dutch pediatric patients receiving gentamicin [26] with only 31 to 54% achieving therapeutic peak concentrations and 54 to 90% achieving trough concentrations below recommended thresholds.

Although aminoglycosides are widely used in pediatric care, age-stratified dose-optimization studies remain scarce [27], particularly during infancy when glomerular filtration rate maturation is most dynamic. In silico studies, increasingly recognized by regulatory agencies, especially in the context of drug development and labeling [28–31], also provide a framework to systematically isolate maturational effects on drug exposure and a controlled comparison of alternative dosing strategies without exposing vulnerable patients to experimental interventions. However, most existing simulation platforms, including widely used tools like Simcyp [32], rely on kidney function maturation models which are grounded on scarce empirical data during infancy [33, 34]. This presents an opportunity to develop improved physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) models that incorporate existing evidence of mGFR maturation trajectories across developmental stages [16–18, 35, 36].

In this study, we present an in silico pharmacokinetic modeling framework designed to systematically disentangle the impact of glomerular filtration rate maturation, somatic growth, and chronological age on aminoglycoside clearance and resulting exposure in pediatric patients. Employing a two-compartment pharmacokinetic model parameterized using real-world growth statistics and measured GFR trajectories, we simulated virtual, age-homogeneous cohorts ranging from birth to twelve years of age of term-born children. Dosing regimens were based on The Manual of Childhood Infections (‘Blue Book’) [23], produced with involvement of the Royal College of Pediatrics and Child Health and the European Society for Pediatric Infectious Diseases. To ensure direct comparability with the study by Hollander et al. [26] on TDM target attainment in Dutch pediatric cohorts, detailed dosing and monitoring specifications followed the Dutch Pediatric Formulary (DPF) [26, 37], which is consistent with major international dosing guidelines, like the Blue Book [23]; See Online Supplement Table S1. Our approach enables quantitative assessment of monitoring target attainment across pediatric subgroups and disentangles how variability in kidney function and body size influences drug exposure. Our framework addresses variability driven by glomerular filtration rate maturation in term-born children under typical inpatient care and does not explicitly account for rapidly evolving kidney dysfunction. By modeling maturation-dependent changes in GFR, our findings support the refinement of dosing strategies and help identify pediatric subpopulations at increased risk for therapeutic underexposure or toxicity. This maturation-aware approach bridges contemporary insights in pediatric nephrology with pharmacokinetic modeling, providing a stronger physiological basis for dosing recommendations.

Methods

Study design and data sources

We conducted an in silico pharmacokinetic study to evaluate TDM target attainment under guideline-based dosing across virtual pediatric cohorts. The primary objective was to quantify the independent effects of glomerular filtration rate maturation, chronological age, and somatic growth on gentamicin exposure in pediatric patients. Ten cohorts were created at age: 1 day, 7 days, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, 2 years, 4 years, 8 years, and 12 years. Each cohort comprised 1,000 term-born individuals with a strict 1:1 female-to-male ratio. Virtual subjects were stratified into clinically relevant age groups agreeing with WHO reporting standards: neonates (0–28 days postnatal age), infants (1–12 months), young children (> 12 months to ≤ 5 years), and older children (> 5 to ≤ 12 years), see Table 1. Growth characteristics were generated in accordance with World Health Organization (WHO) child growth standards [38], while age-dependent GFR was modeled based on mGFR trajectories from Smeets et al. [35] and Piepsz et al. [36]. The body surface area (BSA) was estimated using the Haycock formula [39]:

Exposure definition

Gentamicin administration was simulated as a once-daily intravenous infusion over 30-min, using dosing regimens specified in the ‘Blue Book’ and the DPF [23, 26, 37]: 4 mg/kg every 24 h for term-born neonates (gestational age ≥ 37 weeks) and 7 mg/kg every 24 h for infants and children older than 1 month. Implementation in agreement with the DPF allows comparison of the in silico study results with a recent clinical study [26]. A comparison on gentamicin dosing is included in the Online Supplement in Table S1. Dosing regimens for amikacin and tobramycin also follow the ‘Blue Book’/DPF recommendations. All simulations were conducted using fixed dosing schedules without adaptive dose adjustments.

Outcome definition

Peak and trough gentamicin concentrations were used as pharmacokinetic surrogates for efficacy and toxicity, respectively. Peak concentrations were assessed 30 min after the end of infusion, while trough concentrations were evaluated 23 h post-dose. Peak and trough concentrations depend on the age-dependent volume of distribution, which was captured through allometric scaling of distribution volumes (Online Supplement in the Supplemental Methods Sect. 3). Therapeutic target ranges were chosen consistent with those used in Hollander et al. [26]: for neonates, peak 8–12 mg/L and trough < 1 mg/L; for infants and children, peak 15–20 mg/L and trough < 0.5 mg/L. The primary study outcome was the proportion of virtual patients in each age group achieving peak or trough targets over 10 days of treatment.

Pharmacokinetic model

Aminoglycoside exposure was modeled using a two-compartment model with first-order elimination, which is the minimal viable model showing bi-phasic distribution kinetics; see Online Supplement in the Supplemental Methods Sect. 2. Simulations were deterministic and were conducted over 10 days of treatment. Simulations were implemented in Python [40]. Drug clearance was assumed to occur exclusively via glomerular filtration and to be proportional to mGFR, consistent with established evidence [1–3]. Accordingly, the framework specifically models maturation of glomerular filtration function as the determinant of aminoglycoside clearance. Aminoglycoside metabolism and plasma protein binding were considered negligible for all compounds [2, 3]. The volume of distribution in the central (Vc) and peripheral (Vp) compartments, as well as the intercompartmental clearance (Q), were scaled allometrically as power-law functions of body weight. Parameter estimates were derived from a structured synthesis of published pediatric popPK models; methodological details are provided in Online Supplement in the Supplemental Methods Sect. 3, including an illustration of parameter trends in Figure S1. Inter-individual variability in pharmacokinetic parameters was modeled as log-normal and fixed at the patient level.

Statistical analysis

Target attainment stratified by age group was assessed using descriptive statistics for peak and trough concentrations at treatment over 10 days of treatment. Sensitivity analyses were pre-specified to evaluate the impact of physiological variability on drug exposure. Sensitivity analyses were conducted for four reference ages: 1 day, 3 months, 2 years, and 8 years. All analyses were performed in Python [40].

Validation

Model validation was performed using two external real-world datasets. First, simulated target attainment rates were compared with clinical outcomes reported in Hollander et al. [26]. Second, predicted concentration-time profiles were evaluated against individual-level gentamicin data from a malnourished pediatric population reported by Seaton et al. [41]. Model performance was assessed by agreement of simulated percentile curves with observed data, and by computing the root squared mean logarithmic error (RSMLE). Validation procedures and comparative plots are presented in Online Supplement in the Supplement Methods Sect. 7, Supplemental Figures S2 and S3.

Results

Patient and simulation cohort characteristics

The virtual population comprised ten age-homogeneous cohorts, aggregated into four clinically relevant pediatric age groups: term-born neonates (N = 2000), infants (N = 4000), young children (N = 2000), and older children (N = 2000). Each cohort included an equal proportion of female and male individuals, and all subjects were assumed to be term-born (gestational age ≥ 37 weeks). Across developmental stages, median body weight increased from 3.3 kg in neonates to 32.9 kg in older children, while median height rose from 50.0 to 139.6 cm and median body mass index (BMI) values ranged from 13.2 to 16.6, consistent with WHO standards (Table 1; Fig. 1C). The assigned mGFR increased rapidly during early life, from 33.3 [23.5–42.3] ml/min/1.73 m2 (median [Q1–Q3]) in neonates to 65.2 [51.2–78.1] ml/min/1.73 m2 in infancy, plateauing thereafter at approximately 95 mL/min/1.73 m2 (Fig. 1C) in older children. These cohort characteristics align with normative developmental physiology [36, 38] and establish a physiologically representative foundation for the subsequent pharmacokinetic simulations.

Drug monitoring target attainment under standard dosing

Despite simulating weight-based dosing, substantial variability in gentamicin exposure was observed across pediatric age groups and treatment days (Fig. 2A; Table 2). Infants and young children exhibited particularly low peak target attainment, with only 43.0% and 43.9% of patients, respectively, within the therapeutic range on Day 3. Trough target attainment was also suboptimal: only 50.2% of infants and 87.7% of young children achieved the trough threshold on the same day. Older children achieved better peak levels (70.0% within range on Day 3), and 57.0% achieved trough levels below the target. Neonates achieved 69.2% and 54.7% target attainment on Day 3 for peak and trough levels, respectively.

Marked heterogeneity was observed within age groups (Fig. 2A), particularly among infants. At one month of age, only 5.5–17.3% of virtual patients met trough targets across treatment days 1 through 7. Trough level attainment improved with age: at 3 months, 42.8–59.0% met targets and by 2 years, 82.8–92.0% achieved trough concentrations below threshold. In contrast, peak target attainment remained relatively stable throughout infancy.

To further examine variability in early infancy, two-dimensional contour plots were generated (Fig. 2B), mapping the fraction of patients achieving peak and trough targets as a function of body weight (x-axis) and mGFR (y-axis), while accounting for inter-individual variation in BMI and pharmacokinetic parameters. From 1 to 6 months of age, the median developmental trajectories crossed the trough attainment contours, reflecting an age-related increase in glomerular filtration–mediated drug clearance. In contrast, the peak attainment contours remained closely aligned with the median trajectory, suggesting that peak levels are less affected by developmental changes during this period.

Peak target attainment exhibited minimal sensitivity to both weight and mGFR across all ages. Increasing GFR from the 25th to 75th percentile while keeping body weight constant altered peak attainment by 11.5% at both 1 day and 3 months of age, 18.5% at 2 years, and 10.8% at 8 years. Corresponding interquartile weight changes resulted in shifts of 8.2%, 8.5%, 8.7%, and 10.5%, respectively.

In contrast, trough concentrations were highly sensitive to mGFR, particularly during early life. At 1 day of age, variation in mGFR between the 25th and 75th percentiles changed trough attainment by 99.2%, compared to just 14.8% for weight. This disparity persisted at 3 months of age (mGFR: 56.7%, weight:13.5%). By 2 years of age, trough attainment became largely insensitive to either factor (mGFR: 9.6%, weight: 1.7%). At 8 years of age, sensitivity to mGFR remained modest (mGFR: 32.2%), while weight had a minor effect (weight: 8.1%).

Discussion

Principal findings

This in silico study provides evidence that glomerular filtration maturation is a key driver of variability in gentamicin clearance, which in turn shapes systemic gentamicin exposure across pediatric development. While current dosing strategies account for somatic growth through weight-based adjustments, our simulations reveal that inter-individual variability in mGFR exerts a greater influence on trough concentrations and an influence on peak concentrations comparable to body weight. Weight-based dosing assumes proportional clearance with body weight, whereas renal clearance scales nonlinearly with body size during childhood. Although dosing regimens for both term-born and pre-term are often highly stratified, dosing according to major guidelines for pediatric patients beyond the first month of life frequently relies on uniform weight-based adjustment across broad age ranges [22, 23, 34].

Our study demonstrates that trough target attainment was lowest and most variable in neonates and infants, with gradual improvement observed throughout early infancy. The improvement in trough target level attainment corresponds to ongoing postnatal glomerular filtration maturation, which accelerates drug clearance. In contrast, peak target attainment showed less variability across age groups but was notably higher in older children. This reflects the non-linear allometric scaling of the absolute volume of distribution with body weight, in agreement with findings from popPK studies in pediatrics [27]. Over the course of treatment, peak target attainment initially rose modestly before plateauing, while trough attainment mildly declined before plateauing. These temporal patterns reflect accumulation in slowly equilibrating tissue compartments modeled as peripheral compartment, see Fig. 1A.

Our findings were validated against real-world observations from mixed-age pediatric cohorts reported by Hollander et al. [26], reproducing the reported proportions of unmet peak and trough target attainment in a population-matched analysis (see Online Supplement in the Supplemental Methods Sect. 7 for details). With our model, we provide a mechanistic explanation for the frequently observed sub- and supra-therapeutic target attainment in the broad pediatric population and give motivation for further studies to explore if target attainment shifts with age. Unlike aggregated clinical data, our age-stratified simulation approach reveals that specific subgroups – particularly infants – systematically miss therapeutic monitoring targets under standard dosing. Grouping children aged 1 month to 12 years into a single dosing category obscures meaningful maturational differences in pharmacokinetics and masks age-specific dosing needs.

Supplementary analyses for amikacin and tobramycin confirm similar trends to those observed with gentamicin, including mismatches between peak and trough target attainment across ages, see Online Supplement Figures S4 and S5. However, due to broader therapeutic ranges defined in the DPF [26, 37], the impact on overall target attainment is less pronounced. As shown in Online Supplement Figure S6, the perceived adequacy of aminoglycoside therapy is sensitive to how monitoring thresholds are defined.

Our study highlights the importance of integrating our current knowledge of glomerular filtration rate maturation into pharmacokinetic modeling to bridge pediatric nephrology with clinical dosing practice that is physiologically informed yet clinically applicable. Recent advances in understanding how GFR evolves during infancy and childhood [16–18, 35, 36] provide physiologic context to guide drug exposure assessment. By grounding drug exposure predictions in physiologic maturation of GFR rather than weight alone, we show that maturational GFR changes are essential to understanding aminoglycoside safety and efficacy across childhood.

Strengths and limitations

This study is grounded in empirically characterized maturation of mGFR, rather than relying solely on age or body weight as proxies for kidney function. This enables a physiologically consistent representation of aminoglycoside disposition across pediatric development. By integrating published pediatric population pharmacokinetic studies across age groups with transparent parameter derivation (Online Supplement in the Supplemental Methods Sect. 3), the framework provides a coherent synthesis of existing evidence. The simulation-based approach allows separation of maturation-driven changes in renal clearance from the effects of somatic growth, enabling systematic evaluation of their respective contributions under controlled conditions.

As with all model-based analyses, the validity of our predictions depends on the availability and quality of underlying pediatric pharmacokinetic data. The model cannot capture the full spectrum of clinical variability, including comorbidities, deviations in drug administration, or prescriber decision-making. In addition, our analysis focuses on pharmacokinetic targets rather than clinical outcomes. Although trough concentrations are established surrogates for aminoglycoside-associated nephrotoxicity and underpin TDM in clinical practice [42, 43], toxicity is mediated by tubular uptake processes that may be developmentally regulated. Due to limited ontogeny data, these mechanisms were not explicitly incorporated.

The framework does not explicitly account for pathophysiologic alterations in kidney function, such as augmented renal clearance, AKI, asphyxia-associated changes, or extracorporeal support. While parameters were derived from pediatric popPK studies conducted in treated cohorts, reflecting typical clinical care, application to critically ill patients with pathophysiologic alterations in kidney function requires caution, and TDM remains essential for individual dose adjustment. Although accurate assessment of kidney function in critically ill children remains challenging, the maturation-informed modeling framework may help anticipate systematic age-related differences in drug handling. However, such approaches cannot replace patient-specific evaluation and must be complemented by TDM.

The study focuses on term-born children. Preterm infants were excluded, as renal development in this population follows distinct trajectories governed by gestational and postmenstrual age, for which dedicated models exist [44]. Simulations were based on dosing recommendations from the DPF [26, 37] and the ‘Blue Book’ [23]. While absolute target attainment rates may vary by guideline, developmental trends and associated dosing vulnerabilities are expected to be broadly generalizable.

Clinical implications

Aminoglycoside dosing in pediatric patients remains largely stratified by age and weight. While neonatal regimens are adjusted for developmental physiology, dosing recommendations for infants reflect weight alone, despite ongoing glomerular filtration maturation. Our findings suggest that this limitation may lead to systematic under- or overexposure, placing infants—especially those in early infancy—at elevated risk for nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity. These findings have direct clinical relevance for both initial dose selections and interpretation of TDM. In clinical practice, dose adjustments require balancing peak efficacy and trough toxicity thresholds, with priorities varying across clinical contexts. Within this framework, our model allows us to delineate the age-specific impact of dose adjustments on peak and trough target attainment.

The present study provides evidence to guide future research and clinical strategies aimed at integrating kidney function and maturation-aware pharmacokinetic modeling together with drug monitoring, offering a framework for strategies to improve pediatric care and prevent AKI associated with drug exposure. Our results complement current nephrotoxin stewardship programs in pediatric nephrology by quantifying how maturational glomerular filtration changes drive exposure patterns associated with nephrotoxic risk.

Outlook

Our findings highlight that pharmacokinetic variability is greatest in neonates and infants—the populations where accurate dosing is most critical. A more precise assessment of glomerular filtration rate maturation could significantly enhance therapeutic target attainment and reduce aminoglycoside-related toxicity in early life. However, clinical data on kidney function maturation in infancy remain limited [15, 17, 18, 35, 36], and current approaches to GFR assessment in infants are constrained by the invasiveness and logistical burden of measured GFR methods. Compounding this challenge, discrepancies between measured and estimated GFR values—driven by biomarker type, age, and underlying physiology—limit the reliability of standard estimation formulas in this population [15, 16, 45]. These limitations highlight the challenge of relying solely on direct or estimated GFR in early life, reinforcing the need for physiology-informed initial dosing and subsequent TDM-guided refinement. Given their predominant elimination by glomerular filtration and the routine use of TDM, aminoglycosides may serve as functional pharmacokinetic probes to infer glomerular filtration rate maturation and support dynamic dose refinement within a maturation-informed pharmacokinetic framework [1, 46, 47].

Clinical quality improvement initiatives such as Nephrotoxic Injury Negated by Just-in-Time Action (NINJA) and Baby NINJA [12–14] have effectively demonstrated that increasing clinician awareness and providing systematic notifications about drug-related nephrotoxic risks in pediatric subpopulations can enhance dosing safety [14, 48]. Our framework offers the possibility to complement such stewardship approaches by supporting dose selection through a physiologically grounded understanding of age-dependent exposure patterns. Nevertheless, efforts to increase guideline complexity must also consider the cognitive and operational burdens on clinical workflows, which may inadvertently increase mis-dosing [49].

Our framework provides a basis for the implementation of model-informed precision dosing (MIPD) frameworks that integrate seamlessly into routine care. MIPD tools for aminoglycosides are increasingly used in pediatric practice to guide individualized therapy [50]. “Virtual twin” models, combining patient-specific characteristics with pharmacokinetic simulations, offer a promising pathway. By quantifying the impact of glomerular filtration maturation across childhood, our framework provides a physiological basis to enhance parameter estimation for model selection and dose recommendations within such applications. Given the routine use of TDM in aminoglycoside therapy, exposure measurements could be harnessed to support individualized parameter estimation and individual dose adjustment without additional clinical workload. Future pediatric labeling and dose optimization studies will benefit from more granular age stratification to better capture maturational changes in heterogeneous pediatric populations. Development and validation of such MIPD tools in prospective pediatric studies represent the next step toward individualized aminoglycoside therapy.

Conclusion

This in silico study highlights glomerular filtration rate maturation as a critical determinant of aminoglycoside clearance and target attainment throughout pediatric development, using gentamicin exposure as a representative case. Current age- and weight-based dosing guidelines do not adequately capture the maturation-induced changes in aminoglycoside clearance, contributing to suboptimal therapeutic target attainment across pediatric subgroups. By simulating age-homogeneous, physiology-based virtual cohorts, this work provides mechanistic insight into developmental pharmacology and underscores the limitations of conventional dosing paradigms. Incorporating glomerular filtration rate maturation into aminoglycoside dosing is therefore a key opportunity to improve nephrotoxin stewardship and protect kidney health across early childhood. Our findings support the integration of physiologic maturation into pediatric dosing strategies, offering a pathway toward more precise, age-appropriate, and individualized drug therapy in children.
