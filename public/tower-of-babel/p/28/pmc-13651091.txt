Spatial-temporal epidemiological characteristics of influenza and its correlation with meteorological factors in Gansu province based on 24 solar terms
Authors: Minzhen Wang, Yiting Guo, Jia Jia, Mengmiao Zhao, Shuyu Liu, Xuxia Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo describe the spatial-temporal epidemiological characteristics of influenza outpatient visits in Gansu province, and to explore their correlation with temperature and humidity using the 24 solar terms (24-STs) as the time dimension.MethodsUsing influenza visit data from Gansu (2019–2024), spatiotemporal analysis employed gravity center shift, standard deviational ellipses, and seasonal decomposition. A distributed lag non-linear model (DLNM) assessed the effects of daily mean temperature (DMT, °C) and daily mean relative humidity (DMRH, %) on influenza visits, using a bivariate model to estimate joint effects.ResultsA total of 57,443 influenza visits were recorded. Visits peaked between Beginning of Winter and Winter Solstice, with Great Snow accounting for 10.62%. The northwest-southeast ellipse orientation was consistent with Gansu’s geography. DMT and DMRH exhibited significant nonlinear lag effects, with solar term-specific characteristics. The temperature risk window spanned Cold Dew to Frost’s Descent (6–16 °C; RR = 1.459, 95% CI: 1.246–1.709). Humidity risk increased above 50% in spring, peaking at 50% in winter (RR = 1.869, 95% CI: 1.582–2.208). Synergistic high-risk combinations and multiple visit peaks were identified across specific solar terms.ConclusionThe 24-STs may serve as a useful exploratory time frame for characterizing influenza activity. Daily mean temperature, daily mean relative humidity, and their joint effects are key meteorological factors associated with influenza visits, with notable differences across solar terms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28464-0.

Introduction

Influenza is an acute respiratory viral infection transmitted primarily through respiratory droplets and close physical contact. It features high transmissibility and rapid epidemic spread [1], circulating widely worldwide. Although most cases are mild or self-limited, influenza is among the diseases with the heaviest public health and economic burdens globally due to its high incidence and seasonal outbreaks. The World Health Organization (WHO) reports roughly one billion influenza infections globally each year, including 3 to 5 million severe cases and 290,000 to 650,000 influenza-related respiratory deaths annually [2]. Influenza epidemics impose a substantial burden on the healthcare system and the social economy [3, 4].

Influenza epidemics show significant seasonal and spatiotemporal characteristics. Regional differences in topography and climate shape the spatial aggregation and distribution range of influenza activity [5–7]. Influenza peaks are not randomly distributed, but align with cyclical seasonal climate changes. Previous studies have confirmed that temperature and relative humidity are key factors affecting influenza virus survival and transmission efficiency [8, 9]. The association between meteorological factors and influenza risk is not a simple linear relationship, but features both lagged responses and distinct nonlinear threshold effects [10]. To capture such complexity at finer scales, climatological studies have established mature frameworks for quantifying multi-scale regional heterogeneity in climate transition zones [11, 12], offering methodological references for future studies on meteorological drivers of influenza. Gansu Province is long and narrow with complex topography and diverse climate types. Against this background, this study explores the spatiotemporal distribution of influenza and its meteorological drivers in Gansu Province, providing evidence to inform local intervention strategies.

At present, studies on influenza spatiotemporal distribution and its correlation with meteorological factors mostly adopt Gregorian calendar years and four climatological seasons as the time framework [1, 13]. This widely used temporal division has limitations in capturing the detailed, continuous climatic and phenological rhythms of specific regions. The 24 solar terms (24-STs) were inscribed on the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2016 [14]. Based on the sun’s annual orbital trajectory, the system divides the ecliptic into 24 equal 15° segments, forming 24 yearly time nodes that reflect seasonal transitions, climate shifts, and phenological changes [15]. As a China-specific climate-aligned time classification system, the 24-STs framework offers an alternative perspective for identifying key meteorological change nodes linked to disease transmission [16]. Previous studies have explored correlations between 24-STs and mortality, sleep quality, acute epiglottitis, and other conditions [17–19], but no research has yet examined the association between influenza activity and 24-STs. Analyzing influenza activity from the 24-STs perspective can clarify its relationship with periodic climate rhythms, and provides a localized, fine-grained temporal framework for understanding disease spatiotemporal patterns.

In summary, this study uses influenza outpatient and emergency visit data from the Gansu Provincial Comprehensive Health Information Platform. Taking the 24-STs as the time framework, we analyze the spatiotemporal characteristics of influenza in Gansu Province and its associations with daily mean temperature (DMT, °C) and daily mean relative humidity (DMRH, %). This work aims to provide scientific evidence for targeted influenza prevention and control strategies that account for solar term variation and regional spatiotemporal disparities.

Materials and methods

Data sources

From February 4, 2019 to February 3, 2024, daily outpatient and emergency visit data covering five 24-STs cycles were collected from the Gansu Provincial Comprehensive Health Information Platform. Influenza cases (J10, J11) were extracted according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes. The county-level (district-level) administrative map of Gansu Province was sourced from the National Platform for Common Geospatial Information Services (Tianditu Cloud Service Center) (https://cloudcenter.tianditu.gov.cn/). DMT (°C) and DMRH (%) data were obtained from the National Meteorological Information Center (https://data.cma.cn/).

No missing data were identified for influenza visits. Daily mean temperature and relative humidity had 133 (7.3%) and 147 (8.1%) random missing days, respectively. Linear interpolation using adjacent valid values was applied for data imputation.

Research methods

Description of influenza outpatient and emergency visits and meteorological factors

We used 24-STs as units to describe the basic characteristics and time series of influenza visits and meteorological factors. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test the normality of the meteorological data. Results showed that the meteorological data across different solar terms did not all follow a normal distribution. Given the overall large sample size, mean ± standard deviation (x̅ ± s) was still used for statistical description.

Time series of influenza outpatient and emergency visits and meteorological factors

We constructed a daily time series at the 24-STs scale, calculated the mean daily influenza visit count for each solar term, and assigned this value to every day within the corresponding term. Seasonal-trend decomposition (STL) was then applied to the processed series.

Analysis of spatiotemporal evolution

The data of five 24-STs were combined, and the spatial-temporal distribution of influenza visits in different solar terms was analyzed by the gravity center shift model and standard deviational ellipses analysis [20]. The mean center reflects the core spatial location of influenza cases, and its trajectory tracks shifts in influenza’s overall spatial gravity. Standard deviational ellipses describe the directional spread, aggregation, and dispersion of influenza visits. Ellipse area indicates the degree of spatial clustering, while major and minor axes reflect the primary distribution direction and coverage scope.

Association between influenza outpatient and emergency visits and meteorological factors

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used to analyze the correlation between influenza visits and DMT and DMRH in each solar term, which was not limited by the assumption of normal distribution of the data.

The median daily influenza visits per solar term was 19.37 (IQR: 10.54–41.32), with an insufficient effective sample size for stable Distributed Lag Non-Linear Model (DLNM) fitting. We therefore grouped the 24-STs into four conventional seasonal groups (Table 1). Each analytical unit contains approximately 90 days of data, and the median daily visits increased to 24.22 (IQR: 14.11–52.41) after aggregation, ensuring reliable parameter estimation.

We constructed distributed lag non-linear models (DLNMs) to quantify meteorological effects on daily influenza visits across seasonal solar term subgroups [21]. Models adjusted for year, weekday, public holidays, and COVID-19 epidemic periods. Effects of DMT and DMRH on influenza outpatient and emergency visits

Fit statistics and convergence diagnostics for all DLNMs are provided in Table S2. All models converged successfully, with explained deviance ranging from 44.7% to 73.1%. No substantial residual autocorrelation was detected.

Cumulative and lag-specific effects of DMT and DMRH

Overall cumulative exposure-response curves for DMT across the four seasonal solar term groups are shown in Fig. 4. With median temperature as the reference, all four curves exhibited an approximately inverted V-shape. The effects in the spring and summer solar terms groups were not statistically significant. In the autumn solar terms group, risk peaked significantly at 10.4 °C (RR = 1.459, 95% CI: 1.246–1.709), followed by a gradual decline in RR. In the winter solar terms group, risk peaked at -0.8 °C (RR = 1.133, 95% CI: 1.000–1.282).

For lag patterns, the autumn solar terms group showed a high immediate risk at lag 0 within the 6–14 °C range, while the strongest effect in the winter solar terms group appeared at lags 13–14 days. Detailed lag-response structures for all seasonal groups are presented in Fig. S1.

Overall cumulative exposure-response curves for DMRH are shown in Fig. 5. In the spring solar terms group, DMRH showed positive associations with influenza consultations, with the relative risk rising continuously once humidity exceeded 50%. The summer solar terms group curve followed an inverted N-shape, with a significant protective effect in the 49–58% range and a risk effect in the 59–65% range. In the autumn solar terms group, the curve rose gently then declined, showing a protective effect at 54–62% and no significant association at higher humidity levels. Joint effect of DMT and DMRH

Bivariate response surface plots for DMT and DMRH across seasonal solar terms groups are shown in Fig. 6. In the spring solar terms group, the high-risk range mainly occurred under the combination of moderate temperature (4–8 °C) and high humidity (60–65%). With fixed DMRH, RR first increased then decreased with rising DMT, peaking at 5–7 °C. Under moderate DMT, RR increased gradually with higher DMRH. Risk remained low under both low-temperature/low-humidity and high-temperature/low-humidity conditions. In the summer solar terms group, high-risk areas were dominated by moderate temperature (15–17 °C) combined with high humidity (60–70%). At humidity above 60%, peak risk corresponded to the 15–17 °C temperature range; within this range, higher humidity was significantly associated with increased visit risk. In the autumn solar terms group, high-risk areas are concentrated in the combination of low temperature (6.5–10 °C) and moderate humidity (59–63%). Risk decreased with rising temperature regardless of the humidity level. At temperatures above 15 °C, risk remained low across humidity conditions, with no obvious humidity association. In the winter solar terms group, slightly elevated relative risk was observed under low-temperature conditions, but precise temperature cutoffs could not be reliably defined due to limited samples for extreme temperature-humidity combinations. A consistent protective trend appeared when temperatures exceeded 2 °C, independent of humidity.

Discussion

Using the 24-STs framework, this study characterized the spatiotemporal distribution of influenza outpatient and emergency visits in Gansu Province, and explored the associations of DMT and DMRH with influenza activity as well as the joint effect of the two meteorological factors. Seasonal epidemic patterns and meteorology-influenza associations across solar terms were systematically analyzed.

In terms of temporal distribution, influenza visit peaks were mainly concentrated in the winter solar terms group, from BW to Winter Solstice (WS), consistent with the typical winter influenza peak in northern China. A national study of seasonal influenza spatiotemporal patterns reported that influenza activity in northern China peaks from December to January [24], corresponding to the BW–WS interval in the 24-STs system. This peak coincides with sudden temperature drops and low humidity. These meteorological conditions, combined with increased indoor activity and interpersonal contact in cold weather, may facilitate influenza transmission [25, 26]. Besides the main winter peak, Waiting of Insects (WI) in the spring solar terms group, and CD, Frost’s Descent (FD), and BW in the autumn-winter transition formed secondary visit peaks. Around WI, rising temperatures and larger diurnal temperature variation may affect population immunity [22, 27], creating conditions favorable for viral variation and transmission [28]. Temperature and humidity also fluctuate sharply from CD to BW; such climate instability may disrupt the host-virus balance and partly explain the secondary peak in influenza consultations.

In terms of spatial distribution, standard deviational ellipse results were broadly consistent with temporal patterns. Ellipse areas were largest for winter solar terms such as SC, Light Snow (LS), and WS. Influenza activity in this period was not only temporally concentrated but also spatially widespread and dispersed. This may be linked to frequent winter cold air activity, increased inter-regional population mobility across the province [29], and the potential role of low temperatures in promoting viral transmission. Ellipse areas for the spring solar terms group were the second largest, corresponding to the secondary peak around WI. Influenza transmission range narrowed compared with winter, but still showed regional spread. These findings are consistent with a previous study in Fuzhou, which reported significant spatiotemporal clustering of influenza cases with winter-spring peaks, wide winter diffusion, and gradual spring contraction [30]. Notably, the ellipse areas for Beginning of Summer (BS2) and Grain Full (GF) in the summer solar terms group were also relatively large, contrary to the general perception of low summer influenza incidence. Although overall visit counts were low for these solar terms, cases were spatially dispersed. This pattern may be associated with increased population mobility (e.g., tourism and agricultural activities) as temperatures rise, leading to low incidence but wide spatial distribution [31].

Numerous studies have confirmed that temperature and relative humidity are key environmental factors influencing influenza virus survival and transmission, with specific window periods or optimal ranges for influenza activity [32]. DMT results indicate that the period from CD to FD is a key climatic window for influenza prevention. In the autumn solar terms group, DMT showed a significant risk effect with influenza activity in the 6–16 °C range. A 2022 multi-city study in China found that both low temperatures (< 9 °C) and temperatures of 10–15 °C increased influenza risk [33], consistent with the temperature characteristics of CD (8.15 ± 0.19 °C) and FD (6.37 ± 0.26 °C). Climatologically, these two solar terms mark the transition from cool to cold temperatures. This temperature range maintains influenza virus stability in experimental settings. Persistent low temperatures and humid weather are recognized as important climatic conditions for autumn influenza epidemics [34]. Falling outdoor temperatures reduce outdoor activities and increase indoor aggregation, while indoor ventilation decreases, all of which may elevate influenza transmission risk [35, 36].

In the winter solar terms group, temperature showed a significant risk effect only within the narrow range of -2 to 0 °C, roughly corresponding to early winter from BW (2.47 ± 0.34 °C) to LS (-1.87 ± 0.31 °C). Influenza virus maintains high environmental stability in this temperature range [37], while people still maintain routine daily activities such as commuting and schooling, while spending more time indoors [38]. The combination of favorable viral survival conditions and frequent interpersonal contact may drive this relatively high-risk period. Notably, when temperatures dropped further below − 2 °C (corresponding to solar terms such as GS and WS), influenza visit risk showed a downward trend. One explanation is that extremely cold weather may restrict unnecessary travel and outdoor exposure, reducing interpersonal contact frequency. This reduction in social contact may partially offset the transmission risk associated with improved low-temperature viral survival [39].

Compared with DMT, DMRH showed a more complex nonlinear association with influenza visits. In the spring solar terms group, influenza visit risk increased continuously with rising humidity when humidity exceeded 50%, corresponding to Rain Water (RW, 54.01%), Spring Equinox (SE, 50.03%), and Grain Rain (GR, 50.37%). Rising temperatures and increased precipitation [40] bring moderately high humidity, which has been linked to viral persistence in laboratory studies. A systematic review confirmed higher viral survival and transmission efficiency in both dry low-humidity and high-humidity environments [41]. Increased rainfall also reduces outdoor activities, and rebounding temperatures create favorable conditions for indoor virus spread.

In the summer solar terms group, relative humidity showed a significant risk effect in the 59–65% range, corresponding to Grain in Ear (GE, 56.26%) to Great Heat (GH, 63.09%). This finding aligns with existing review and laboratory evidence. Previous studies have confirmed that 40–60% indoor relative humidity accelerates inactivation of enveloped viruses, including influenza and SARS, inhibiting transmission [42]. In contrast, viruses maintain stronger infectivity at around 65% humidity [43]. From GE to GH, widespread air conditioning use may dry indoor air [44], and closed windows reduce natural ventilation, potentially increasing aerosol transmission risk in confined spaces [45].

In the winter solar terms group, humidity showed a risk effect at around 50%, and shifted to a protective effect above 57%. The risk peak humidity was close to levels in deep winter terms, such as WS (53.79%) and SC (52.52%). At around 50% humidity, winter conditions are cold and dry, and indoor heating further reduces air humidity. Virological studies show that extremely low absolute humidity benefits airborne influenza virus survival and spread [46]. The observed winter humidity risk peak may therefore be linked to the combined effect of outdoor cold-dry conditions and indoor heating-related dryness, which has been suggested to promote influenza spread [47]. Conversely, humidity above 57% is often accompanied by snow or rainfall, which may restrict outdoor activities and interpersonal contact, contributing to the observed protective effect.

The joint effects of DMT and DMRH on influenza activity were heterogeneous across seasonal solar terms groups. In the spring solar terms group, the highest risk occurred at 5–7 °C combined with 50–65% humidity, roughly corresponding to the period from WI to SE. WI marks rising temperatures and resuming social activities such as travel and school openings [15]. Temperatures return to a moderate range but are not yet high, while humidity rises gradually. Experimental studies indicate such conditions maintain viral activity in the environment, and increased social interactions may jointly facilitate influenza transmission [48]. In the summer solar terms group, the risk peak temperature-humidity interval covered the transition from GF to Summer Solstice (SS). Temperatures of 15–17 °C represent a transitional stage of summer warming, when high temperatures have not yet fully inhibited virus activity, providing conditions for humidity-related risk. Some studies suggest summer influenza activity is more closely linked to high humidity than high temperature [49]. When temperatures exceed 18 °C (e.g., 19.14 °C in GE), the inhibitory effect of high temperature on viral activity may counterbalance high humidity risk [50], leading to reduced overall risk. This also explains the non-significant temperature effect in the summer univariate analysis.

In the autumn solar terms group, influenza transmission risk was dominated by low temperature (6.5–10 °C) combined with moderate humidity (59–63%), matching the climatic characteristics of CD and FD. These two solar terms also feature sharp temperature drops [15]. In this temperature range, the humidity association was relatively weak, which appears inconsistent with the protective humidity association at 54–62% in the univariate analysis, but is actually coherent. Multiple studies have shown that temperature is a key driver of influenza spread; below a certain threshold, the role of low temperature in influenza risk becomes dominant [32]. Laboratory data indicate autumn low temperatures enhance airborne viral stability, increasing transmission risk and potentially masking the independent humidity association.

As temperatures drop further into deep winter, the independent humidity association re-emerges, reflecting seasonal shifts in dominant meteorological factors. In the winter solar terms group, overall risk was elevated under low-temperature conditions. Due to the limited sample size, finely segmented narrow risk windows lack sufficient statistical stability for detailed interpretation, so we only describe overall trends of the joint exposure-response curves. This period corresponds to solar terms from GS to WS, featuring cold weather and closed indoor environments [15]. Consistent with laboratory evidence, extremely low temperatures may prolong viral aerosol suspension [51]; combined with indoor crowding in heated spaces, these factors may facilitate influenza transmission [9]. At temperatures above 2 °C (e.g., BW), temperature showed a protective effect on influenza visits regardless of humidity. This may be linked to reduced viral activity at higher temperatures and increased ventilation, both of which lower transmission risk.

During 2020–2022, non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) for COVID-19 control, including mask-wearing, social distancing, and travel restrictions, substantially suppressed influenza activity [52], consistent with trends reported in other regions [53]. This period of abnormally low transmission may introduce bias in two main ways. First, the sharp decline in influenza visits reduces the statistical power for estimating meteorological effects. Second, NPI-altered transmission dynamics may attenuate or even mask the true exposure-response relationship between meteorological factors and influenza. The 2023–2024 influenza resurgence, potentially driven by accumulated immunity debt from prolonged reduced viral exposure [54], further adds complexity to the temporal pattern. Although all models included a COVID-19 period indicator to adjust for the overall pandemic effect, residual confounding may still exist due to seasonal variations in NPI intensity, and results covering this period should be interpreted with caution.

This study has several limitations. First, constrained by data volume, the six solar terms in each season were combined for analysis, which limits the ability to explore meteorology-influenza associations at the individual solar-term level and represents a departure from the high temporal resolution that the 24-STs framework aims to provide. Second, although the model controlled for long-term trends, seasonal fluctuations, day-of-the-week effects, holidays, and epidemic status, several potential confounding factors, including influenza vaccination coverage, ambient air pollution, socioeconomic characteristics, population mobility, school calendars, and healthcare-seeking behavior, were not included as independent covariates. Third, this study is an ecological study conducted at the population level, and causal inferences cannot be directly drawn. Future research could integrate individual-level and environmental covariates, adopt designs such as case-control studies, and extend the study period to enable single-solar-term analyses.

Conclusion

Based on the observed associations, influenza consultation peaks were mainly concentrated from BW to WS. Increased influenza visits were linked to distinct seasonal meteorological patterns: warm and humid conditions in spring (WI to SE), hot and humid conditions in summer (GE to SS), sharp temperature drops in autumn, particularly below 10 °C (CD to FD), and dry, cold environments in winter (GS to WS). These high-risk intervals can serve as reference windows for hypothesis-generating preventive strategies, which must be validated through subsequent intervention trials. The 24-solar-term framework offers a practical temporal tool to profile seasonal influenza dynamics. Further research is required to examine whether this framework outperforms traditional calendar seasonal divisions and whether the observed associations can be reproduced using longer surveillance datasets and individual-level study designs.
