Prevalence and factors associated with severe thrombocytopenia among hospitalized patients with malaria attending Kigeme District Hospital, Southern Province, Rwanda: cross-sectional study
Authors: Japhet Ndatumuremyi, Jean Marie Vianney Kagimbangabo, Isaie Nshimiyimana, Bizimungu Gaspard, Jean Pierre Sendegeya, Valens Nizeyimana, Celine Byukusenge, Charles Nsanzabera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThrombocytopenia is a common haematological abnormality in malaria patients, increasing the risk of mortality in both children and adults with Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax infections. Using simple random sampling, 124 patients were selected while ensuring confidentiality throughout data collection, analysis, and reporting. Data were gathered via a structured form in Kobo Toolbox and later processed in Microsoft Excel. Information on demographic, clinical characteristic and laboratory investigations considered were those collected during the time of patients admission to the hospital. Background

Thrombocytopenia is a haematological finding commonly encountered in daily clinical practice from asymptomatic clinic patients to critically ill intensive care unit patients and it is defined as platelet count < 150 × 103 per µl [1] while platelet count less or equal to 50 × 103 per µl is known as severe thrombocytopenia [2]. Severe thrombocytopenia increases mortality risk in both children and adults with Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax infections whereby patients having thrombocytopenia were over two times more likely to die from malaria than those without this infection [3]. The exact cause of thrombocytopenia among patients with malaria remains unclear yet it is believed to result from increased platelet destruction, reduced production, or both. Factors contributing to platelet depletion include immune-mediated destruction, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), sequestration, oxidative stress, and malaria-induced apoptosis [4]. The prevalence of thrombocytopenia among patients with malaria varies across different regions. For instance, in India a study among 130 patients with malaria infection has shown that thrombocytopenia was present in 108 (83%) cases [5], while this haematological disorder was reported in 73% among 120 malaria positive cases in Pakistan [6]. A study in Ghana among children with malaria has shown that 33(33%) patients has thrombocytopenia while it was 67% among adult patients suffering from malaria in Ethiopia [7] and 51% among malaria cases in Sudan [8].

Several significant factors were identified that contribute to the onset of severe thrombocytopenia, including the severity of illness, drugs, organ dysfunction, sepsis, septic shock, and renal failure [9]. Although the morbidity and mortality associated with thrombocytopenia are generally low in in patients with malaria, its presence frequently indicates a poor prognosis due to its association with underlying complications.

In Rwanda, there is a limited information on the magnitude of severe thrombocytopenia and its associated factors among hospitalized patients with severe malaria. Therefore, this study aims to determine the prevalence and factors associated with severe thrombocytopenia among hospitalized patients having severe malaria attending Kigeme District Hospital, Southern Province, Rwanda.

Methods

Study setting

This study was conducted in Kigeme District Hospital and this hospital is located in Nyamagabe District, Southern Province, Rwanda. Kigeme District Hospital serves 196,056 populations corresponding to 53% of district’s whole population and among these population, the majority are female (53%) [10]. These populations served by this hospital are distributed in 8 sectors namely Gasaka, Kamegeri, Mbazi, Kibilizi, Tare, Uwinkingi, Kitabi, and Cyanika.

The majority of malaria cases in the Kigeme District Hospital catchment area are managed by community health workers, health posts, and health centers, as established by the Ministry of Health in Rwanda [11]. However, complicated malaria cases or those with unclear diagnoses are referred to Kigeme District Hospital for further management.

From December 2023 to November 2024, Kigeme District hospitals received 5713 patients with sign and symptoms of malaria with 180(3.15%) of them tested positive for malaria blood smear examination and hospitalized for further management.

Study design

This study used a cross-sectional study design employing a quantitative approach based existing recorded information in patient’s file. It was conducted among patients diagnosed with severe malaria who were hospitalized at Kigeme District Hospital between December 2023 and November 2024. The study assessed concurrently presence of severe thrombocytopenia and exposure factors associated with severe malaria among the hospitalized patients. In this study, information on severe thrombocytopenia, demographic characteristics, clinical features, and laboratory investigations was collected at the time of patient admission to Kigeme District Hospital.

Study population

This study focused on patients who were hospitalized with a diagnosis of severe malaria for a period of one years from December 2023 to November 2024. During this period, a total of 180 patients were admitted to the hospital with severe malaria. These patients formed the basis of the study population. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) a patient with malaria infection is classified as severe case if one of the following clinical feature is met: impaired level of consciousness or coma, failure to feed, prostration, multiple convulsions, acidotic breathing, circulatory collapse, clinical jaundice with evidence of other vital organ dysfunction, haemoglobinuria, abnormal spontaneous bleeding, and/or pulmonary oedema. while on the side of laboratory finding a patient is considered to have severe malaria if the following laboratory information are noted: hypoglycaemia(blood glucose < 2.2 mmol/l or 40 mg/dl), metabolic acidosis (plasma bicarbonate < 15 mmol/l),severe normocytic anaemia (Hb < 5 g/dl), haemoglobinuria, hyperparasitaemia(> 2%/100000 per ul in low intensity transmission area or > 5%/250,000 per ul of high stable malaria transmission), and renal impairment (serum creatinine > 265umol/l(12).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients who were hospitalized for severe malaria with confirmed positive blood smear for Plasmodium species infection and having laboratory investigations as well as clinical information documented in his or her file were included in this study. Also, those who had missing laboratory information on malaria blood smear examination and other missing laboratory investigations as well as clinical information were excluded from this study. The ambulatory cases were also excluded from this study.

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined by using Yamen formula as follow

e = margin error equals to 5% (0.05) at 95% confidence interval; N = Population size (total number of patients diagnosed with malaria in Kigeme District Hospital; n=1801+1800.052=124, the sample size from this study was 124 patients with malaria attending Kigeme District hospital.

Sampling technique

The study used a simple random sampling technique to select participants for the study. First, a comprehensive list of all patients who had been diagnosed with severe malaria and admitted to Kigeme District Hospital between December 2023 and November 2024 was compiled. This list was entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, with each patient assigned a unique identification number. Using Excel’s random number generation function, a random number was assigned to each patient. The list was then sorted based on these random numbers in ascending order. The required number of study participants was selected from the top of this randomized list, ensuring that each eligible patient had an equal chance of being included in the study. This approach minimized selection bias and ensured a representative sample of the severe malaria cases admitted during the study period.

Study variables

Dependent variable

Severe thrombocytopenia.

Independent variable

Sociodemographic and clinical factors (gender, age, Fever, headache, vomiting, delay in seeking medical care, convulsion, acute kidney injury, coma status, convulsion, splenomegaly, jaundice and epistaxis).

Laboratory abnormalities such as C-reactive protein, Liver transaminase level, full blood count, kidney function tests (urea and creatinine).

Data collection and management

A data collection form was developed and adapted from previous studies related to the objectives of the study [11]. The data collection instrument consists of two sections. The sociodemographic information (age, sex, marital status, education level) is included in the first part. Second section contains clinical characteristics of the study participants which are fever, C—reactive protein, full blood count, time of consultation, liver transaminase level, kidney function tests namely urea and creatinine. Data collection authorization was obtained before the beginning of data collection process.

Data processing and analysis

Data was coded, entered, edited, and cleaned by using Microsoft excel and then exported into SPSS version 23 for analysis. Severe thrombocytopenia was confirmed by reviewing full blood count results recorded in patient’s files stored in electronic medical record system of in the hospitals. The density of malaria parasites was assessed semi-quantitatively using the standard plus (+) grading system, where a single plus (+) indicated 1–10 parasites per 100 high-power fields, two pluses (+ +) represented 11–100 parasites per 100 fields, three pluses (+ + +) denoted 1–10 parasites per single high-power field, and four pluses (+ + + +) were assigned when more than 10 parasites were observed per single high-power field.

Demographic, clinical and laboratory information were used as independent variables in the analysis. Binary logistic regression model was use to infer the association between the outcome and independent variables. In the bivariates analysis variables with P-value < 0.05 was included in the multivariable binary logistic regression. Odds ratio with 95% confidence level was computed and P-value < 0.05 was described as having a significant association.

Ethical consideration

This research received approval from Kigeme District Hospital Research committee. Since data were gathered from hospital’s registers, informed consent was not requested from participants. However, all records were anonymized, to ensure patient confidentiality.

Discussion

The study assessed the prevalence and factors associated with severe thrombocytopenia among patients with malaria attending Kigeme District Hospital from 2023 to 2024. The prevalence of severe thrombocytopenia among patients with malaria in this study was 27.4%, which is higher compared to a study conducted in Indonesia that reported a prevalence of 5.9% among patients with P. falciparum infection[3]. Similarly, prevalence was higher than the findings from a systematic review, which reported a prevalence of severe thrombocytopenia of 10.1% among patients with P. vivax infection [14]. Moreover, the results exceeded the prevalence of 12.3% reported in a cross-sectional study conducted in Ethiopia 4. However, the findings were lower than those of a study on haematological abnormalities in patients with malaria, which reported a prevalence of severe thrombocytopenia of 39.7% [7].

In this study, 55.6% of the study participants were febrile while the remaining proportion were afebrile. This is in line with the study conducted in district Balaghat which has reported that the slide positivity rate was 29% among afebrile patients [15]. Afebrile malaria contributed from 19.6% to 47.2% of the overall malaria burden in the Indian region [16]. Fever is known as main clinical manifestation among patients with malaria in non-immune individuals but afebrile cases are also reported in people with partial and complete immunity to disease in endemic area [17]. In the context of the present study, some patients with severe malaria were referred to the hospital for further management from health centers within the catchment area. Before being transferred, they had received antipyretics and other emergency resuscitation medications. Additionally, it is common for patients to self-medicate with antipyretics when experiencing a high body temperature. As a result, fever may be masked by the time of hospital admission and clinical examination.

Furthermore, a study conducted on severe malaria, which reviewed various WHO meetings on the topic, reported that hyperpyrexia (high grade fever) was removed from the WHO case definition of severe malaria in 1988 [18]; this indicate that severe malaria diagnosis should made not only based on the presence of fever but also in junction with other clinical and laboratory criteria.

This study found that severe thrombocytopenia in malaria patients was significantly associated with several clinical, laboratory findings and age of the respondents. Patients with age less or equal to 5 years old were 5.17 times more likely to have severe thrombocytopenia than people with 6 years above. This finding is in line with the study conducted in Indonesia which has reported that younger age was the risk to suffer thrombocytopenia than adulthood stage [3]. Another study conducted in Colombia has shown concordant findings to the present study whereby young age was shown to be associated with severe thrombocytopenia [13]. However opposite finding were observed in the study conducted in India whereby age was shown to be not significantly associated with severity of thrombocytopenia among patients with malaria [14].

Patients with a positive C-reactive protein (CRP) test were 3.45 times more likely to develop severe thrombocytopenia compared to those with a negative CRP result. This finding is supported by a study which has shown that C-reactive protein boosts antibody mediated platelet destruction and this protein was shown to be a novel pathogenic cofactor that enhances IgG-mediated phagocytic responses resulting in thrombocytopenia [15]. In another study it was shown that high CRP level correlated with antiplatelet IgG [22].

Similarly, individuals with jaundice had 2.88 times higher odds of experiencing severe thrombocytopenia than those without jaundice. This finding is in line with the study on clinical profile and complication of malaria hepatopathy, which has shown jaundice as a sign of hepatopathy was associated with thrombocytopenia among patients with malaria [23, 24]. In this study the patients with high level of aspartate aminotransferase was more likely to have severe thrombocytopenia than those with normal serum aspartate aminotransferase suggesting hepatic involvement among P. falciparum infections. Studies has shown in liver transaminases with aspartate aminotransferase (AST) included to be significantly elevated in falciparum malaria patients compared to their non-malaria counterparts which is an indication of defective liver function [26, 27] and available evidences has shown that thrombocytopenia correlated with the degree of liver severity[(28)]. In addition, study has shown that aspartate aminotransferase activity is increased in P. falciparum infection [29]. Additionally, another study has shown that liver function impairment among were common associated with thrombocytopenia in patients with malaria [12].

Moreover, patients with epistaxis were 1.84 times more likely to have severe thrombocytopenia compared to those without epistaxis. This finding was in line with the case report conducted in Cameroon which has shown that epistaxis was common feature characterizing malaria induced thrombocytopenia [30]. Another study has shown that epistaxis duration was associated with severe thrombocytopenia [24].

Patients with malaria having high serum urea levels were 1.73 times higher odds of severe thrombocytopenia than normal urea levels. This is suggested possible involvement of Plasmodium species in triggering kidney dysfunction. Plasmodium falciparum and P. vivax were shown to cause kidney damage whereby histological studies indicate the finding of thrombotic microangiopathy, characterized by endothelial injury, microvascular occlusion by platelets or fibrin thrombi and glomerular ischemia. In this process platelet are consumed in primary and secondary hemostatic process resulting to thrombocytopenia [19]. The study conducted Heng et al. has shown that serum urea was negatively correlated with platelet count (r = − 0.546, p < 0.01) [32]. However, other study has shown platelets are not only affected by kidney disease, but may also contribute to kidney disease progression by mediating inflammation and immune effects [26].

Conversely, malaria patients who were under coma status were less likely to suffer severe thrombocytopenia were associated with severe thrombocytopenia among patients with malaria. This is contrary to the report from the study in Uganda which has shown that thrombocytopenia was a common finding among patients with malaria [33]. Another study has shown that thrombocytopenia is likely possible among patients with cerebral malaria due to platelet activation possibly through an interaction between PfEMP1 on Plasmodium and CD36 on platelets [28]. Furthermore, a study in India has shown that thrombocytopenia was not significantly associated with cerebral malaria patients who were under coma status [29].

The finding from this study has shown that patients who were infected with P. falciparum were 3.4 times more likely to suffer for severe thrombocytopenia than patients who were infected by P. vivax. This finding is in line with the study conducted in Ethiopia, which has reported that the frequency of thrombocytopenia varies depending on the name of infecting Plasmodium species. with P. falciparum being more frequently associated with thrombocytopenia compared to P. vivax [37]. On other study has shown that patients who were having P. falciparum infection were 3.911 more likely to develop thrombocytopenia than people who suffered P. vivax infection and those who were having mixed infection [37]. Given the high burden of malaria in Rwanda, the finding from this study underscore early detection of malaria and monitor platelet count among patients who exhibit clinical and laboratory abnormalities highlighted in this study as independent predictors to severe thrombocytopenia among patients with severe malaria.

This study was a cross-section study design which has some limitations like inability to establish causal and effect relationship therefore longitudinal studies are need to assess temporal relationship. In addition, the study was conducted in one health facility, limiting the generalizability of the study findings to other population. In this study, preexisting haematological disorders were not extensively assessed, which may have influenced the observed association. Lastly as the study used existing recorded patient’s information, some patient’s record was missing.

Conclusion

This study found a high prevalence of severe thrombocytopenia among malaria patients attending Kigeme District Hospital, with a prevalence rate of 27.4%. The findings suggest that severe thrombocytopenia in malaria is significantly associated with factors such as young age group less or equal to 5 years old, elevated CRP levels, jaundice, high aspartate aminotransferase levels, epistaxis, and high serum urea levels. These findings underscore the importance of early detection among patients who are at high likelihood to suffer severe thrombocytopenia in this specific population. Furthermore, this study contributes valuable information that can support hypothesis generation for future longitudinal studies aimed at evaluating the risk and incidence of severe thrombocytopenia among patients with severe malaria.
