Enteric-colonized Cronobacter spp. can disrupt the central nervous system without translocation across the host barriers
Authors: Jillinda Yi Ling Toh, Sok Ching Tay, Zihui Lin, Evelyn Xiu Ling Loo, Jia Xu, Johan Gunnar Eriksson, Peter D Gluckman, Yap Seng Chong, Michael Meaney, Fabian Yap, Cleo Tay, Xiao Pan Ding, Dan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Cronobacter sakazakii is an opportunistic foodborne pathogen known to cause severe neonatal infections. However, its subclinical effects on human health remain largely unexplored. Here, we report that intestinal colonization by Cronobacter is potentially correlated with human neurobehavioral alterations in the absence of bacterial translocation across host barriers. Analysis of fecal microbiota from the children in GUSTO cohort revealed that Cronobacter abundance was significantly and positively correlated with their emotional reactivity and affective problem scores, suggesting an association between gut Cronobacter colonization and emotional dysfunction. Using a zebrafish larvae model, we demonstrated that enteric colonization by C. sakazakii ATCC 25944 triggered a distinctive bending phenotype and locomotor instability, indicative of central nervous system (CNS) disturbance. Multi-omics profiling revealed upregulation of genes related to circadian rhythm (e.g., per2, nr1d1, nr1d2) and neuropsychiatric markers (hint1), alongside metabolic dysregulation involving lipid classes linked to neurodegenerative stress. Random mutagenesis coupled with whole-genome sequencing identified modifier variants in genes associated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) biosynthesis and fimbrial adhesion, which attenuated the neurotoxic phenotype. LPS isolated from wild-type C. sakazakii, but not from mutants, reproduced the CNS impairment phenotype, highlighting its crucial virulence role. Collectively, our findings provide the first evidence that enteric Cronobacter colonization may affect human emotional development via gut-derived LPS signaling, emphasizing its underestimated role in the gut–brain axis and neurobehavioral health.Keywords: Cronobacter, gut–brain axis, social-cognitive-emotional performance, lipopolysaccharides, zebrafish

Introduction

Cronobacter is a newly described bacterial genus belonging to the family Enterobacteriaceae. Cronobacter genus contains 7 different species including C. condimenti, C. dublinensis, C. malonaticus, C. muytjensii, C. sakazakii, C. turicensis, and C. universalis. C. sakazakii, formerly known as Enterobacter sakazakii, is the most frequently clinically isolated species of the Cronobacter genus. 1 Cronobacter spp., particularly C. sakazakii, are capable of causing invasive diseases in all age groups, but are primarily associated with necrotizing enterocolitis, meningitis, and septicemia in neonates. The ability of C. sakazakii to invade the intestinal epithelium and brain endothelium is therefore a crucial step for its pathogenesis. As shown by Almajed & Forsythe, 2 C. sakazakii can invade and even translocate through cell lines like Caco-2 (intestinal epithelial cells) and HBMEC (human brain microvascular endothelial cells). Consequently, C. sakazakii infection is associated with high fatality rates and possible post-recovery neurological complications such as cerebral abscess and hydrocephalus, bringing C. sakazakii to global attention. 3 , 4

As an opportunistic pathogen, C. sakazakii is ubiquitously present in low-moisture foods (e.g. powdered infant formula, flour) and on household surfaces. It survives for extended periods under desiccating conditions 5 , 6 and exhibits remarkable resistance to environmental stresses including ultraviolet-C irradiation. 7 C. sakazakii carriage has been observed in healthy individuals with a well-functioning immune system. For instance, viable Cronobacter strains have been isolated from fecal samples of healthy adults and neonates without any clinical symptom. 8 It remains unknown whether the colonization of this pathogenic bacteria in human gut, without overcoming the intestinal and brain barriers, could cause any chronic harm to human health. Given that acute Cronobacter infections predominantly impact the central nervous system (CNS), we hypothesized a potential neurotropism of this bacterium and investigated the subclinical effects of enteric-colonized Cronobacter spp. on the CNS, particularly on the emotional and cognitive functions. Indeed, numerous evidence have shown that the gut microbiota consisting of diverse microbial communities residing in the gastrointestinal tract could affect the emotional and cognitive functions via the gut–brain axis. 9 , 10

We first examined the potential correlation between the Cronobacter reads in gut microbiota and the social-cognitive-emotional (SCE) performance of participants without any obvious infection symptoms from Growing Up in Singapore Towards Healthy Outcomes (GUSTO) study.

Next, we explored the suitability of studying the C. sakazakii-induced neurotoxicity with the use of zebrafish (Danio rerio) larvae. Zebrafish is a powerful animal model for studying host-pathogen interactions due to their optical transparency, genetic manipulability, and well-understood immune system. 11 , 12 As reviewed by Moradian et al. 13 , zebrafish is the most commonly used non-mammalian model in microbiota–gut–brain interaction studies. In this study, by combining real-time imaging with high-throughput transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling, we demonstrated the suitability of studying C. sakazakii-induced neurotoxicity with the use of zebrafish models. We also identified the potential virulence mechanisms associated with the neurotoxicity caused by enteric-colonized C. sakazakii through a combination of genetic mutagenesis and whole genome sequencing analysis.

Materials and methods

GUSTO study

The GUSTO cohort is a prospective study involving women recruited during their first-trimester of pregnancy in Singapore. 14 The detailed study design and methodology have been described by Soh et al. 14 Ethics approval was obtained from Singapore National Healthcare Group (DSRB approval number: D/2009/021) and SingHealth (CIRB approval number: 2018/2767). This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki ethical guidelines. The privacy rights of human participants have been observed and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before any study procedures were undertaken.

Fecal samples of the children were collected by parents using sterile containers and stored at −80 °C until analysis. 15 Total microbial DNA was extracted by MoBio PowerFecal DNA kit (Qiagen, Carlsbad, CA). The V4 region of 16S rRNA gene was amplified by polymerase chain reaction with modified 515F and 806R primers following the Earth Microbiome Project protocol (www.earthmicrobiome.org). The amplicon sequencing was performed on the MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) to generate 250 bp paired-end reads. Detailed data processing was described in the previous studies. 16 , 17 Briefly, paired-end 16S rRNA gene V4 reads were joined and demultiplexed in QIIME1.9. 18 Demultiplexed reads were quality-filtered using a Phred threshold of 19 and further filtered in USEARCH with a maximum expected error threshold of 1. Reads of 252–254 bp were retained, truncated to 251 bp, dereplicated, and singletons were removed before OTU delineation using USEARCH v9.2.64 (cluster_otus). 19 Reads were mapped back to representative OTU sequences at 97% identity to generate the OTU table. OTU representative sequences were taxonomically assigned using QIIME's BLAST-based parallel_assign_taxonomy_blast.py against the SILVA 123 SSURef Nr99 database. After data quality control and processing, 1223 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were obtained for downstream analysis. Alpha and beta-diversity analyses were performed as described previously. 20

The SCE variables were measured using the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL), 21 a parental-reported instrument widely used to evaluate emotional and behavioral problems in preschool children. We selected four CBCL subscales which were related with emotional problems, including Anxious or Depressed, Affective Problems, Anxiety Problems and Emotionally Reactive. Ultimately, data from 469 children who had at least one variable of interest were included.

Zebrafish maintenance, husbandry, and ethics statement

All zebrafish experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC; approval no. IACUC-BR23-1361). Wild-type adult zebrafish were housed in the aquatic facilities of the National University of Singapore under standard conditions, with a water temperature of 28 °C and a 14 h light/10 h dark photoperiod. Fertilized embryos were obtained by placing adult zebrafish in mating tanks and were subsequently maintained in Petri dishes containing E3 embryo medium (5 mM NaCl, 0.17 mM KCl, 0.33 mM CaCl₂, and 0.33 mM MgSO₄).

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

C. sakazakii ATCC 25944 was purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The bacterial culture was activated twice in the Luria-Bertani (LB) broth (OXOID, Basingstoke, UK) for 24 h at 37 °C before use.

C. sakazakii ATCC 25944 was transformed with a pGFPuv plasmid using Escherichia coli DH5-α as a host to enable the visualization in zebrafish larvae. The transformation was performed via electroporation at 3 kV using a 0.2 cm cuvette. The green fluorescent protein (GFP)-expressing C. sakazakii was cultured twice in LB broth supplemented with 150 ppm ampicillin sulphate (Sigma, Singapore) at 37 °C for 24 h before use.

Delivery of C. sakazakii into zebrafish larvae via static immersion

Overnight bacterial culture of C. sakazakii ATCC 25944 was centrifuged to pellet cells at 8000 × g for 5 min at room temperature (5810R, Eppendorf, Germany) and washed once with phosphate buffered solution (PBS, Vivantis Technologies Sdn Bhd, Malaysia). The pellet cells were then resuspended with E3 medium. Optical Density (OD) was measured using a microplate spectrophotometer (Epoch 2, Biotek Instruments, US) at a wavelength of 600 nm to obtain a concentration of approximately 8 log colony forming units (CFU)/mL.

Zebrafish larvae were monitored for phenotypic changes from 24 to 72 h post-infection (hpi) using a stereomicroscope (Flexacam i5, Leica Microsystems, Germany). At each time point, larvae were visually assessed and classified based on body-axis morphology. Larvae exhibiting a clear curvature of the trunk and/or tail, resulting in a characteristic C-shaped body axis, were classified as bent, while larvae with a straight body axis were classified as normal. The number of surviving larvae and those displaying the bending phenotype were recorded. The bending rate (%) was calculated as the percentage of bent larvae relative to the total number of larvae observed: Bending rate (%) = (number of bent larvae/total larvae observed) × 100. To account for baseline background levels, the normalized bending rate (%) was calculated by subtracting the bending rate of the negative control group from that of each treatment group: Normalized bending rate (%) = bending rate of treatment group (%) − bending rate of negative control (%). In addition, abnormal locomotor behaviour, such as unstable swimming or impaired posture, was qualitatively noted as a supportive observation but not quantitatively assessed. Images were consistently captured using the same stereomicroscope under the same settings.

Enumeration of C. sakazakii in zebrafish larvae

At 24 and 48 hpi, ten zebrafish larvae were randomly selected, pooled into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube, and washed twice with PBS. Larvae were euthanized by immersion in ice for 20 min, followed by homogenization in 1 mL sterile PBS using a FastPrep™ 24-5G tissue–cell homogenizer (MP Biomedicals, USA) at 6500 rpm for 15 s, for two cycles with a 60 s interval between cycles. The resulting homogenates were serially diluted in 0.1% peptone water (Oxoid), plated onto chromogenic Cronobacter isolation agar (Sigma-Aldrich), and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.

Localization of GFP tagged C. sakazakii in zebrafish larvae

To track the localization of C. sakazakii in the gut of zebrafish larvae, the larvae were anesthetized and mounted in 1.5% methylcellulose containing 0.2 mg/mL Tricaine, then positioned laterally on glass slides. GFP fluorescence was captured using a Nikon SMZ25 fluorescent stereomicroscope, with excitation at 400–520 nm and emission at 480–580 nm.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription‑quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‑qPCR) analysis

At 24 and 48 hpi, each 10 zebrafish larvae belonging to the same group (control, symptomatic or asymptomatic groups) were pooled as one sample in a 2 mL tube containing 0.5 mm yttria-stabilized zirconium oxide beads, Lysing Matrix Y (MP Biomedicals, USA) and euthanized by submerging the tube in ice for 10 min. The larvae were then homogenized with 700 mL of lysing buffer RLT in the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). The tubes were homogenized at 6500 rpm for 15 s with rest intervals of 60 s for 3 cycles by using Fast Prep™ 24-5G tissue cell homogenizer (MP Biomedicals, USA). The homogenates were centrifuged at 9000 × g for 5 min, and the total RNA was isolated using RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis reactions were performed with 100 ng of RNA using GoTaq® 2-Step RT-qPCR System (Promega, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The reaction mixtures were placed in a thermocycler (Bio-Rad T100™) with 25 °C for 5 min, 42 °C for 60 min, and 70 °C for 15 min.

The qPCR was conducted using 20 ng of cDNA with the GoTaq® qPCR Master Mix (Promega, USA) on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher, USA). The PCR cycling conditions were 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. The primers used for β-actin (forward: 5′- ATGGATGAGGAAATCGCTG-3′, reverse: 5′-ATGCCAACCATCACTCCCTG-3′) were according to the design of Yang et al. 22 , yielding a 140 bp amplicon. Primers for hint1 (forward: 5′-ATTCCGGCC AACATCATTTA-3′, reverse: 5′-GATCTGACTGATGGGCTTTC-3′) were designed using the PrimerQuest™ Tool (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA, USA) based on the zebrafish mRNA sequence (NCBI Accession No. NM_001005593), producing a 111 bp amplicon.

Illumina RNA sequencing and analysis

At 6 hpi, three biological replicates, each comprising 10 zebrafish larvae per experimental group, were collected and euthanized for RNA sequencing. Total RNA was extracted as described above and further purified using the GeneJET Gel Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). RNA quality was assessed by Nanodrop (Biodrop Duo, UK), with A260/A280 and A260/A230 ratios between 1.5 and 1.8, and by electrophoresis on 1% agarose gel before submission to NovogeneAIT Genomics Singapore Pte Ltd.

The mRNA was isolated from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. Following fragmentation, first-strand cDNA was synthesized with random hexamer primers, and second-strand cDNA was generated using dUTP. Directional libraries were prepared through end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, size selection, USER enzyme digestion, amplification, and purification. Library quality and concentration were evaluated using Qubit, real-time PCR, and a Bioanalyzer for size distribution. Libraries were pooled based on effective concentration and data requirements and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 150 bp paired-end reads (PE150).

Sequence analysis was performed on the Galaxy web platform (https://usegalaxy.org/). 23 Raw reads were assessed with FastQC and trimmed with Trimmomatic to remove adapters and low-quality sequences. 24 Cleaned reads were aligned to the zebrafish (Danio rerio) genome (GRCz11) obtained from Ensembl. Gene-level counts were quantified using featureCounts, 25 and differential gene expression was analyzed with DESeq2, with RUVSeq applied to remove unwanted variation. 26 , 27 Gene enrichment analysis was conducted using ShinyGO 0.73. 28

Ultra-performance liquid chromatography quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UPLC-Q-TOF-MS) analysis

At 24 hpi, 10 zebrafish larvae per experimental group, were collected in 2 mL microcentrifuge tubes, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until further analysis. Two biological replicates were collected, each with triplicate samples.

For metabolite extraction, the zebrafish larvae were homogenized in 400 µL methanol, 400 µL acetonitrile, and 200 µL ultra-pure water using a FastPrep™ 24-5G tissue homogenizer (MP Biomedicals, USA) at 6500 rpm for 15 s, with 60-s rest intervals, repeated for two cycles. After homogenization, the samples were incubated on ice for 1 h, followed by sonication (Company, Country) using three alternating cycles of 1-min sonication and 1-min incubation on ice. The samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was transferred to a fresh 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube and dried using a vacuum concentrator (Model, Company). The dried samples were stored at −80 °C until further analysis. For reconstitution, 500 µL of acetonitrile: ultra-pure water (1:1, v/v) was added to the dried samples, vortexed for 5 min, and filtered through a 0.22;µm PTFE filter. A total of 200 µL of the filtrate was transferred into a 2 mL HPLC vial for further analysis. For quality control, 100 µL from each sample was pooled, mixed thoroughly, and 200 µL of the QC mixture was added to each glass vial for UPLC-Q-TOF analysis.

Metabolomics profiling was conducted using a Xevo G2-X2-QTOF mass spectrometer (Waters, Milford, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source and coupled to an Acquity UPLC system. Chromatographic separation was performed on a CORTECS T3 C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 2.7 µm; Waters Pacific Pte Ltd, Singapore), maintained at 45 °C. The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B), delivered at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. Data acquisition was operated in both positive and negative ESI modes with a scan range of m/z 100–1000 and at a retention time range of 0.5–15 min.

Raw data files were processed using Progenesis QI software (v3.0, Waters), and the software performed retention time alignment with one of the QC samples as a reference. The adduct ions of [M + H]+, [M + NH4]+, [M + Na]+, [M + H − H2O]+, [M + K]+ were selected for position ionization analysis and [M − H]−, [M − H2O − H]−, and [M + K − 2H]− for negative ionization analysis. The samples were grouped into “bent” and “negative control” categories and subjected to “between-subject” design analysis and ANOVA (p < 0.05). Multivariate analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal projections to latent structures-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), were performed in EZinfo software (v3.0, Umetric, Sweden).

Key features of the identified components were searched against databases, including Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), 29 human metabolome database (HMDB), 30 METLIN MS/MS libraries, Chemical Entities of Biological Interest (ChEBI) 31 and National Library of Medicine (PubChem). 32 Putatively annotated compounds were classified using the NPclassifier tool. 32

Bacterial random mutagenesis

Overnight cultured C. sakazakii ATCC 25944 was transferred into LB broth supplemented with acridine orange (AO) concentration ranging from 0–800 µg/mL and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a shaking incubator (200 rpm, HCS Scientific and Chemical Pte Ltd, Singapore). The cultures were then gently centrifuged at 800 × g for 1 min at room temperature to remove the AO. The supernatant was then plated on LB agar and incubated overnight at 37 °C. A working concentration of 200 µg/mL AO was selected for downstream assays as it reproducibly reduced approximately 50% in viable counts while allowing complete bacteria recovery within 48 h. The colonies were picked individually and repeated with the above procedures three times before being delivered to the zebrafish as described above.

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) analysis of the mutants

Overnight bacterial cultures of C. sakazakii ATCC 25944 and the selected mutants were washed and pellet individually. DNA was extracted using GeneJET DNA extraction kit (Thermofisher, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. The quality of total DNA was validated by Nanodrop (Biodrop Duo, UK) with an A260/A230 and A260/A280 ratio of 1.5–1.8.

The genomic DNA was randomly sheared into shorter fragments (~350 bp), followed by end-repair, A-tailing, and ligation with Illumina sequencing adapters. The adapter-ligated products were size-selected and PCR amplified and purified using magnetic beads. The quality and quantity of each library were assessed using a Qubit fluorometer and quantitative PCR (qPCR), while size distribution was verified with a Fragment Analyzer. Pooled libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform using paired-end 150 bp (PE 150) reads. Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control to remove adapter sequences, low-quality bases and ambiguous reads. All samples had Q30 scores > 94%, GC content approximately at 57% and effective data rates above 98%, indicating high sequencing outputs.

The raw sequences of C. sakazakii ATCC 25944 were assembled into contigs using SPAdes (version 3.15.3) and annotated with Prokka (version 1.2.0). The assembled contigs and gene annotations of C. sakazakii ATCC 25944 were used as a reference in the subsequent analysis. The reads of the mutants were aligned to the reference genome using BWA (Burrows-Wheeler Aligner) (version 0.7.18). The sequence alignment/map (SAM) files generated from BWA were converted to binary alignment (BAM) format, sorted, and indexed for downstream analysis. Variant calling was performed using bcftools (version 1.21) to detect single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) of the mutants compared to the reference genome. Filtering was done to remove low-quality variants (Q score < 30). The resulting variant call format (VCF) files were generated for further annotation. The identified variants were annotated using SnpEff (version 4.3t), which was based on their potential impact on protein function. Since no high-impact variants were detected in the mutants compared to C. sakazakii ATCC 25944, the analysis focused on the modifier-impact variants. These variants, which may influence regulatory regions or non-coding sequences, were examined for their potential roles in gene expression differences and phenotypic variations observed in the zebrafish larvae.

Lipopolysaccharides (LPS) extraction and quantification

The extraction of bacterial LPS was conducted following Westphal & Jann method of using hot phenol-water method with some slight modifications. 33 , 34 Briefly, 150–200 mL of overnight bacterial culture grown in LB broth at 37 °C was harvested by centrifugation at 6000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was washed with endotoxin-free water and resuspended to reach 1 mg/mL concentration. Phenol crystals were preheated to 65 °C in a water bath, and an equal volume of phenol was added to the bacterial suspension. The mixture was then incubated at 65 °C for 30 min with vigorous shaking every 5 min. The emulsion was immediately cooled on ice and centrifuged at 8000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The aqueous (upper) phase was carefully collected and transferred to a fresh, weighted centrifuge tube. A second phenol extraction was performed on the pellet to ensure maximum recovery and the aqueous phases were pooled.

To remove the residual nucleic acids, the combined aqueous extract was treated with RNase A (100 µg/mL) and DNase I (100 U/mL) in a buffer with 5 mM MgCl2 and 0.5 mM CaCl2 by an incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. Protein contaminants were then digested by proteinase K (100 µg/mL) incubated at 55 °C for 1 h. LPS was precipitated by adding three volumes of cold 70% ethanol and incubating at −20 °C overnight. The precipitated LPS was collected by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C and washed sequentially with 70% ethanol. The LPS pellet from C. sakazakii ATCC 25944 and the selected mutants were air-dried under sterile conditions, resuspended in endotoxin-free water, and applied to zebrafish larvae at 3dpf in E3 water at a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. The analysis of zebrafish larvae was conducted as described above.

The concentration of extracted LPS was determined using the Pierce™ chromogenic endotoxin quant kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Singapore), according to manufacturer's protocol.

Statistical analysis

We conducted multiple linear regression analyses in R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2024) to examine the associations between Cronobacter abundance and the four SCE variables at 24, 36, and 48 months. The statistical analyses for the zebrafish study were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 9 (GraphPad software, USA). A paired t-test was used for data with two groups, one-way analysis of variance was used for data with more than two groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Correlation between Cronobacter reads from human gut microbiota with SCE variables

To examine whether Cronobacter in the human gut microbiome was associated with children's emotional problems, we performed multiple linear regression analyses adjusting for sex, mother's ethnicity, and father's ethnicity. Four behavioral subscales, including Anxious or Depressed, Affective Problems, Anxiety Problems and Emotionally Reactive, were assessed at 24, 36, and 48 months, yielding 12 behavioral outcome measures. Cronobacter abundance data at 24 and 48 months were not normally distributed but followed Pareto distributions; the data were therefore first transformed into a uniform distribution using the Pareto cumulative distribution function and then converted to a normal distribution via the quantile function. For each combination of Cronobacter abundance and behavioral measures, we fitted a model of the form: Behavior ~ Cronobacter + Sex + Mother's Ethnicity + Father's Ethnicity. To control for false discovery due to multiple comparisons, we applied Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction and report q-values.

The analysis revealed two significant associations after multiple comparison correction (q < 0.05). Higher Cronobacter abundance at 24 months was significantly associated with higher Affective Problems scores at 48 months (p = 0.0013, q = 0.0285). Similarly, higher Cronobacter abundance at 24 months was significantly associated with higher Emotionally Reactive scores at 48 months (p = 0.0024, q = 0.0285). The results suggest that elevated Cronobacter levels in early childhood may be associated with subsequent affective problems and emotional reactivity outcomes.

In addition, we assessed whether overall gut microbiome diversity was associated with behavioral outcomes. For alpha diversity, measured as Pielou's evenness, linear regression models showed no significant associations with any behavioral variables, with or without FDR correction. For beta diversity, evaluated using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) analyses also revealed no significant associations with any behavioral measures, regardless of FDR adjustment. Collectively, these results indicate that the observed associations are not attributable to global gut microbiome diversity, supporting the specificity of Cronobacter-related associations.

Empirical findings of C. sakazakii colonizing in the digestive tracts of zebrafish larvae and inducing a distinct bending phenotype

C. sakazakii ATCC 25944 was delivered to the zebrafish larvae by static immersion to simulate the fecal-oral route of bacterial exposure of C. sakazakii to human. Since we aimed to study the chronic harm of C. sakazakii intestinal colonization to human health, C. sakazakii suspensions were tested at varying concentrations of 7, 8, and 9 log CFU/mL on zebrafish larvae at 3 dpf, and the highest concentration of 9 log CFU/mL was excluded as it induced drastic mortality within 48 h (Figure 1A). Zebrafish larvae immersed in C. sakazakii suspensions at concentrations of 7 and 8 log CFU/mL maintained high survival rates above 80% up to 72 hpi (Figure 1A). In the meantime, it was observed that the zebrafish larvae immersed in C. sakazakii suspensions at 8 log CFU/mL, but not in suspensions at 7 log CFU/mL, demonstrated a distinct bending phenotype and unstable swimming behaviour since 24 hpi (Figure 1D) and the normalized bending rates remained consistently at 43% ± 7% at 24 hpi and 51% ± 3% at 48 hpi (p > 0.05; Figure 1B). The body bent near the swim bladder was referred to as the vestibular-induced bend reflex (VBR) and this behaviour was proven to be closely linked to neuronal activity in the brain and spinal cord. 35 Kalueff et al. 36 also indicated such abnormal behaviors and morphological phenotypes in zebrafish larvae may be indicative of disruptions in neuronal pathways and pathological brain function. Therefore, 8 log CFU/mL of C. sakazakii was selected to treat the zebrafish larvae for the downstream investigations.

This result suggests that C. sakazakii has successfully colonized in the digestive tract. Meanwhile, no systemic dissemination and infection of this bacterium were observed, indicating that the possible neuron disturbance was indeed caused by the enteric-colonized Cronobacter without penetrating the host barriers.

Validation of the CNS disturbance in zebrafish larvae caused by enteric-colonized C. sakazakii by multi-omics approaches

Whole-transcriptome profiling was performed on zebrafish larvae collected at 24 hpi among three groups: infected larvae exhibiting the bending phenotype, infected larvae without the bending phenotype, and uninfected negative control larvae. A total of 56 significantly expressed differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified when comparing bent C. sakazakii-infected larvae to uninfected negative controls, with 47 genes upregulated and 9 genes downregulated (adjusted p < 0.05, log₂FC > 1; Table S1). Among the DEGs, the genes associated with neurological activities are listed in Table 1, all of them have been listed as human orthologs by the Zebrafish Information Network (ZFIN).

The DEGs analysis individually suggested the potential disruptions of enteric-colonized Cronobacter in neuronal signaling, function, and neurodevelopment of the zebrafish larvae. Next, to gain a broader understanding of the biological processes and molecular functions affected by C. sakazakii colonization, GO enrichment analysis was conducted. GO analysis of the upregulated DEGs revealed significant enrichment in processes related to circadian rhythm regulation and locomotor behaviour (Figure 2A). The circadian rhythm is governed by a central pacemaker located in the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus, which orchestrates the body's 24-h cycle and influences critical functions such as the sleep–wake cycle, cognitive performance, and behavioral regulations. 49 , 50 These rhythms are highly sensitive to environmental stressors, including microbial infections. 51 Numerous studies have demonstrated that disruption of circadian rhythm can lead to impaired motor control, cognitive decline, and the onset or exacerbation of neurodegenerative diseases. Schurhoff et al. 49 reported that circadian rhythm disruption compromises blood–brain barrier integrity and brain homeostasis, ultimately contributing to the development of chronic neurological disorders. Patients with Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases frequently exhibit altered sleep–wake cycles, further supporting the link between circadian dysregulation and neurodegeneration. Moreover, circadian disturbances have been associated with microglial activation, neuroinflammatory responses, and behavioral abnormalities. 52 Therefore, the enrichment of circadian and locomotor-related GO terms provides a potential molecular basis for the bending phenotype and behavioral disturbances observed in C. sakazakii-colonized zebrafish larvae.

Despite the small number of downregulated DEGs, GO enrichment analysis revealed biologically meaningful terms associated with neurodevelopment and structural maintenance (Figure 2B). Key enriched processes included neuroblast proliferation, forebrain generation of neurons, and collagen fibril organization, all of which are critical for proper neuronal development, signaling, and brain matrix integrity. Disruption of these processes may impair neurogenesis, destabilize neuronal networks, and compromise extracellular matrix structure, potentially contributing to neurological dysfunction. 53-55

Untargeted metabolomics analysis was conducted using UPLC-QTOF-MS/MS in both positive (ESI+) and negative (ESI−) ionization modes. A total number of 1867 and 1657 compounds were identified in ESI+ and ESI− modes, respectively. Principal component analysis (PCA) plot was generated among the groups of infected bent larvae, uninfected negative control, and quality control (QC) samples. The QC sample cluster was close to the origin of the score plot, showing that the measurements were consistent and repeatable (Figure S1). Pairwise comparison between the infected and uninfected groups was validated through the construction of an orthogonal partial least squares linear discriminant analysis (OPLS-LDA) model (Figure S2). The infected and uninfected groups were not separated clearly in the PCA plots since PCA plot is an unsupervised method. A much clearer separation was observed in the OPLS-DA plot when features with low variance were excluded in the data analyses. For both ESI+ and ESI− modes, the model showed acceptable goodness-of fit (R2Y; ESI+: 0.76, ESI−: 0.88) and goodness-of-prediction (Q2; ESI+: 0.39, ESI−: 0.70). The compounds were then filtered with criteria of (VIP > 1, p < 0.05, max fold change > 1 and minimal fragmentation score of 10) were annotated and 70 and 48 compounds were annotated in ESI+ and ESI− modes respectively.

44 compounds were upregulated (Table S2) and 70 compounds were downregulated (Table S3) comparing bent C. sakazakii-infected larvae to uninfected negative controls. The annotated compounds were then processed with the NP Classifier to determine their subclasses. Metabolomic profiling of zebrafish larvae exhibiting bending phenotype revealed notable alterations in metabolites being associated with neurodegenerative processes or neuroprotection (Figure 2D). Upregulated metabolite subclasses including triterpenoids-, sphingolipids-, cyclohexylamine and piperidine-carboxylic acid derivatives were observed in the bent larvae. Previous reports suggested that these metabolites were neuroprotective exhibiting anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects, and reducing neuronal damage during stress conditions or infection. 56-58 However, the elevated triterpenoids levels observed in the infected larvae may also transit from protective to detrimental effects. Ling et al. 59 and Suzuki et al. 60 reported that excessive triterpenoids could disrupt cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis, leading to chronic inflammation and exacerbating neuronal dysfunction. The downregulation of several metabolites within the subclasses of amino acid, peptides, and analogues such as L-pyroglutamic acid, glutamyl-methionine, disulfide glutathione, 3-(carboxymethylamino) propanoic acid, and peptide derivatives (H-Thr-Glu-Tyr(PO₃H₂)-Val-al) might also suggest the effects of neuronal dysfunction. These metabolites are generally known to support brain health and signaling under regular physiological conditions. 61-63 For instance, glutathione disulfide (GSSH) is known to be essential in controlling oxidative stress and has been linked to neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases due to disruption of this metabolite. 64 Downregulation of these metabolites suggested that the infected larvae exhibiting bending phenotype might have been through compromised neurotransmission and signaling.

The integration of transcriptomics and metabolomic profiles provided additional insights into the molecular mechanisms of the distinct phenotype observed in C. sakazakii-infected larvae. Transcriptomics analysis revealed the upregulated genes associated with circadian rhythms were per2, nr1d1 and nr1d2 (KEGG Map04710), and numerous studies have reported that the disruption of circadian rhythm influenced metabolic pathways such as lipid metabolism. 65 These genes facilitated in lipid synthesis, storage, and breakdown in daily cycles, and disruption of the gene functions would lead to metabolic imbalance. 60 , 66 This result aligned coherently with the metabolomic data, where subclasses such as triterpenoids and sphingolipids were significantly upregulated. Several studies mentioned that the dysregulation of triterpenoids and sphingolipids could trigger cellular stress and apoptosis. 59 , 67 As reviewed by Pereira et al., 68 sphingolipids are crucial regulators of neuronal membrane integrity, the disruption of the circadian clock results in alterations in lipid homeostasis, which has been observed in patients with bipolar disorder.

Taken together, the transcriptomics and metabolomics alterations in the bent zebrafish with enteric-colonized C. sakazakii, especially the identified pathways of the circadian rhythm, locomotor behaviour, and upregulation of triterpenoids and sphingolipid derivatives, underscored that the enteric-colonized C. sakazakii indeed impaired the CNS of zebrafish larvae.

Mechanistic speculations of the neurotoxicity induced by enteric-colonizing C. sakazakii

AO is a commonly used chemical mutagen capable of inducing frameshift mutation through the insertion and deletion of nucleotides during replication. 69 , 70 In this study, AO was employed to induce random mutations in C. sakazakii ATCC 25944. Mutants generated through AO mutagenesis were subsequently screened in the zebrafish larvae model to identify improved phenotypic alternations that might be linked to attenuated virulence. This is to elucidate the bacterial virulence mechanisms responsible for the bending phenotype observed in the zebrafish larvae.

To generate mutants of C. sakazakii ATCC 29544, AO was tested at varying concentrations of 200, 400, 600, and 800 µg/mL. A dose-dependent cytotoxic effect was observed where higher concentration of AO resulted in increasing bacterial lethality. Among the test concentrations, 200 µg/mL AO produced a reproducible lethality rate of approximately 50%–60% (Figure 3A). Colonies surviving AO treatment at 200 µg/mL were subsequently screened in zebrafish larvae to identify candidates with altered virulence phenotypes.

A total of 89 mutants were screened for mitigated virulence using the zebrafish larvae model at 24 hpi (Figure 3B) and 48 hpi (Figure 3C). Mutants were considered as promising candidates if they induced ≥ 50% reduction of the normalized bending rates compared to the wild type C. sakzakii infection at 48 hpi. Seven mutants (C8, C13, C35, C61, C64, C75, C83) were selected in the initial screening round (Figure 3C) and validated with three independent tests (Figure 3D).

To determine whether the observed reductions in bending phenotype among the mutant strains was due to the decreased bacterial colonization, selective bacterial enumeration of Cronobacter was performed at 24 and 48 hpi. Among the seven mutants, and only C13 exhibited no significant difference in bacterial loads compared to the C. sakazakii ATCC 29544 at both 24 and 48 hpi (p > 0.05; Figure 3E). This suggested that the reduced bending rates in C13 colonized zebrafish larvae might be attributed to impaired virulence factors related to the neurotoxicity of C. sakazakii ATCC 29544. In contrast, the remaining mutants exhibited significantly lower colonized counts than C. sakazakii ATCC 29544 either at 24 or 48 hpi (p < 0.05; Figure 3E).

Comparative genomic analysis of was carried out using C. sakazakii ATCC 29544 as a reference. Variant annotations categorized the identified mutations into different impact levels, and we observed a substantial number of modifier-impact mutations in 124, 109, and 122 protein-coding genes in C8, C13, and C35 mutants, respectively. These mutations were primarily located in the non-coding or regulatory regions, such as promoters, untranslated regions, or intergenic sequences. Although variant modifier impact mutations does not change the protein structure but these variants can influence the gene expression levels, transcriptional timing and RNA stability thereby modulating phenotypic outcomes and influencing the severity of diseases. 71-73 We also observed some moderate-impact variants—missense mutations occurring within the coding regions and resulting in amino acid substitution which could alter protein function and structural confirmation. 74 There were 3, 1, and 2 protein-coding genes in C8, C13, and C35, respectively, with moderate-impact variants.

To understand the possible molecular basis of the reduced bending phenotype observed in the zebrafish larvae model, all 150 unique proteins detected in mutant C8, C13, and C35 strains were classified into different functional categories (Figure 3F) using blastp and domain annotations. Assignments were made using available databases, including UniProt, KEGG, NCBI protein database and relevant literature. The largest altered proteins belonged to the enzymes and metabolism category, which indicated a possible altered metabolic pathway in their persistence, spread of infection within the host, and reducing the survival possibility of bacteria. 75

Among the proteins with moderate and modifier variant impact, there were several proteins being associated with adhesion including inverse autotransporter beta domain-containing protein, fimbrial proteins, and fimbria/pilus outer membrane usher protein. Inverse autotransporter beta domain-containing protein is a large family of adhesins that are widely distributed in Gram-negative bacteria and export proteins across the bacterial outer membrane, and facilitate adherence to host cells. 76 , 77 Fimbrial proteins, including usher proteins like, YraH, YraJ1, and LpfD are crucial for the formation of a hair-like appendages that are proteinaceous structures on the bacterial surface that facilitate adhesion to host cells, movement, and biofilm formation. 78 , 79 Mutations in these components could reduce the bacterial ability to adhere to zebrafish larvae’s epithelial gut and thereby impair effective colonization and virulence. As shown in the comparative heatmap (Figure 3G), both C8 and C35 mutants exhibited higher modifier impact scores in the fimbrial protein and fimbria/pilus outer membrane usher protein than the C13 mutant. Correspondingly, the bacterial enumeration in zebrafish larvae revealed that only C13 did not exhibit a significant reduction in Cronobacter loads as compared to C. sakazakii ATCC 29544 (Figure 3E), suggesting that higher mutation impact in C8 and C35 mutants might have impaired their host attachment and colonization abilities, leading to reduced bacterial load in zebrafish larvae.

Next, proteins involved in LPS biosynthesis and homeostasis including Kdo (2) lipid IV (A) acyltransferase and metalloenzyme YejM were also noted among the proteins with moderate and modifier variant impact (Figure 3G). LPS is a major component of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria. Kdo (2) lipid A acyltransferases are crucial enzymes facilitating the biosynthesis of lipid A–a component of LPS. Both Garcia-Vello et al. 80 and Raetz et al. 81 highlighted that lipid A anchors the whole molecule in the bacterial outer membrane and is the main immunostimulatory component of LPS that could result in septic shock with large circulating amounts. Mutation of this enzyme would affect the integrity of LPS structure, biosynthesis of lipid A, and alter bacterial resistance to environmental stress, pathogenicity, and survival. 82 Metalloenzymes YejM play two pivotal roles, which are maintaining the integrity and permeability of the bacterial outer membrane as a defensive barrier and contributing to the regulation and abundance of LPS production. 83 According to Gabale et al. 84 , YejM could alter the outer membrane environment by influencing lipid compositions, enhancing bacterial resistance and survival during infection. Studies conducted by Fivenson and Bernhardt 85 and Gabale et al. 84 also demonstrated that YejM modulated LpxC turnover by FtsH-LapB to regulate LPS synthesis and showed that the deletion of YejM in E. coli and Salmonella led to a defective outer membrane barrier and reduced bacterial viability. The higher modifier impact score in C35, followed by C8 and C13, may indicate either defective LPS assembly or impaired outer membrane integrity. 83 Both could contribute to the observed reduction of the bending phenotype of the zebrafish larvae.

LPS is known to play a significant role in modulating the gut–brain axis and has been implicated in CNS damage. Upon dysbiosis or increased intestinal permeability (“leaky gut”), LPS can translocate into systemic circulation and activate peripheral immune responses via Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4). 86 This immune activation leads to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, which can cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) or signal through afferent nerves like the vagus, ultimately inducing neuroinflammation. 87 , 88 LPS has been shown to directly compromise BBB integrity and stimulate microglial activation within the CNS, contributing to neuronal damage and behavioral deficits. 89 Repeated or chronic exposure to LPS has been associated with neurodegenerative changes resembling those in Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases, highlighting its detrimental effect on CNS health through the gut–brain axis. 90 More importantly, recent studies suggested that LPS could also interact with the host via local gut-immune-neural circuits. LPS could activate intestinal TLR4 expressed on enteric neurons, epithelial cells, and resident immune cells, initiating localized inflammation and neuromodulatory signaling. 88 These signals may propagate through the vagus nerve or via circulating cytokines, affecting brain function and behavior without requiring LPS to enter systemic circulation. 91 As recently reviewed by Grebennikova et al. 92 , although zebrafish may lack a fully functional and responsive TLR4 signaling axis, they still exhibit robust responses to LPS through multiple TLR4-independent mechanisms. These include scavenger receptors (SRs), NOD1-dependent cytosolic sensing, peptidoglycan recognition proteins (PGRPs), Complement Component 3 (C3), and inflammasome activation mediated by the caspase-1-like protein Caspy2—each serving as functional analogs to their human counterparts. Therefore, zebrafish remain a valuable in vivo model for studying human immune responses to bacterial LPS.

Indeed, within the 56 significantly expressed DEGs identified by whole-transcriptome profiling, comparing bent C. sakazakii-infected larvae to uninfected negative controls at 24 hpi, cebpd, encoding CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein δ (C/EBPδ), is a well-established LPS-inducible transcription factor that operates downstream of NF-κB signaling and plays a critical role in amplifying inflammatory responses through the regulation of cytokines such as IL-6 and TNFα. 93 Similarly, fosab, a member of the FOS/AP-1 transcription factor family, represents a canonical immediate early gene rapidly induced upon LPS stimulation via MAPK-dependent pathways. AP-1 complexes, including FOS proteins, are known to cooperate with NF-κB to drive the expression of inflammatory mediators, positioning fosab as a key component of the early transcriptional response to endotoxin exposure 94 (Table S1). Metabolomic profiling further supports the activation of LPS-triggered inflammatory pathways, as evidenced by coordinated alterations in lipid signaling, redox balance, and immunometabolism (Tables S2 and S3). Notably, the upregulation of lysophosphatidylcholine (LysoPC) and ceramide species is consistent with well-established membrane remodeling processes during endotoxin exposure, where phospholipase activation and sphingolipid metabolism contribute to immune cell recruitment and NF-κB-mediated inflammatory signaling. LysoPC has been widely reported as a pro-inflammatory mediator that promotes monocyte chemotaxis and endothelial activation, 95 while ceramide accumulation is known to drive apoptosis and cytokine production in response to LPS. 96 In parallel, increased levels of phytosphingosine 1-phosphate further indicate activation of sphingolipid signaling pathways that regulate immune cell trafficking and inflammatory responses. 97 Concurrently, the elevation of succinate highlights a hallmark of LPS-induced metabolic reprogramming, as succinate accumulation stabilizes HIF-1α and enhances IL-1β production in activated macrophages. 98

To further validate the role of LPS from C. sakazakii causing impairment in the CNS of zebrafish larvae, LPS was extracted from C. sakazakii ATCC 29544 and the mutants C13 and C35. Surprisingly, the LPS levels quantified from the three strains were comparable, measuring approximately 6 log EU/mL (p > 0.05; Figure 3H). However, the LPS from the three strains demonstrated drastic differences when being applied to zebrafish larvae in causing the bending phenotype (Figure 3I). The zebrafish larvae at 3 dpf were immersed in the extracted LPS at concentrations of 5 log EU/ml of each strain. At 24 hpi, the bending rates of C13 group were lower than the ATCC 29544 but not in a significant way (p > 0.05; Figure 3I). LPS of C35, which exhibited the highest modifier impact in LPS biosynthesis modulating enzyme YejM as compared to C13 (Figure 3G), induced no fish bending phenotype at 24 hpi for all three independent trials (Figure 3I). At 48 hpi, LPS extracted from C13 and C35 resulted in significantly lower bending rates (7.1% ± 7.5% from C13, 7.1% ± 1.3% from C35) as compared to C. sakazakii ATCC 29544 LPS (39.9% ± 7.5%; p < 0.05; Figure 3I).

As noted by Rubin et al. 99 and Xiao et al. 100 , the formation of Kdo sugars contributes significantly to the structural diversity and functional complexity of lipid A. Kdo sugars are integral to the LPS core and engage with various acyltransferases that facilitate the addition of fatty acids, resulting in diverse lipid A structure. These structural variations can influence host immune recognition and inflammatory responses. Therefore, it explains why the LPS was quantified at comparable levels from the three strains whereas the mutants showed much diminished virulence in causing the bending phenomenon.

As reviewed by Di Lorenzo et al. 101 , despite over 70 y of research uncovering the role of LPS in host–microbe interactions, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying LPS–receptor interactions remain poorly understood. LPS is a structurally complex biomolecule with diverse and often enigmatic sugar residues, and the correlation between its chemical structure and biological function across different bacterial strains warrants further investigation using advanced analytical approaches. For instance, Santos et al. 102 reported that LPS from different pathogenic bacteria species including Vibrio spp., Photobacterium damselae, Aeromonas spp., and Tenacibaculum maritimum induce different mortalities in zebrafish. More interestingly, although LPS is widely recognized as a potent endotoxin that induces metabolic endotoxemia, recent studies have reported beneficial effects of LPS derived from several bacterial species. Carone et al. 103 found LPS from Segatella copri DSM 18205, a prominent member of the human oral and intestinal microbiota featured a unique chemical architecture, including a mannose- and glucose-rich oligosaccharide and a highly heterogeneous, hypo-acylated lipid A domain, and displayed attenuated TLR4 activation and weak pro-inflammatory activity. Garcia-Vello et al. 104 showed that Akkermansia muciniphila LPS lacks the O-polysaccharide repeating unit but consists of a complex glycan chain bearing two distinct undeca- and hexadecasaccharide units each containing three Kdo residues, generating an anti-inflammatory response and rationalizing its beneficial host interaction. All these evidence justified our assumption over the uniqueness of the LPS from C. sakazakii ATCC 29544 in showing neurotropism in zebrafish larvae.

Conclusions

C. sakazakii is a foodborne pathogen primarily associated with necrotizing enterocolitis, meningitis, and septicemia in neonates. As an opportunistic organism, C. sakazakii has also been detected in healthy individuals. This study demonstrated that colonization of the human gut by C. sakazakii might lead to chronic adverse effects on human health without translocating across the intestinal or blood-brain barriers, particularly on the CNS and SCE development. These findings provide novel evidence for how gut microbiota can influence the CNS and, in turn, cognitive development.

This study has several limitations that warrant further consideration in future work. First, gut microbiota profiling was conducted using 16S rRNA gene sequencing, which restricts taxonomic resolution to the genus level. This limitation arises from the use of short amplicons, conserved genomic regions among closely related taxa, and insufficient sequence variability to reliably distinguish species or strains. As a result, the specific Cronobacter species or strains driving the observed associations could not be resolved. In the zebrafish model, only a type strain of C. sakazakii was employed. Although C. sakazakii is the most frequently reported clinical species within the genus, future studies should incorporate multiple Cronobacter species and strains to better capture intra-genus diversity and identify those most strongly associated with SCE abnormalities. Second, the cohort design included only two sampling time points (24 and 48 months), resulting in relatively sparse temporal resolution. This limits the ability to delineate the timing, persistence, and potential critical windows of Cronobacter-associated effects on SCE development, as well as to distinguish immediate versus delayed impacts. Future longitudinal studies with more frequent sampling intervals are needed to provide a time-resolved understanding of these dynamics. Third, cross-species differences between zebrafish and humans may influence the interpretation of mechanistic findings, particularly in relation to LPS sensing, immune signaling pathways, and neurobehavioral regulation. While zebrafish offer a valuable in vivo model, differences in innate immune receptors, downstream signaling cascades, and neuroimmune interactions may limit direct translatability to human physiology. Finally, although statistically significant associations were observed between Cronobacter abundance and SCE-related outcomes, the clinical significance of the observed differences in behavioral scale scores remains to be further clarified. Future studies should aim to contextualize these effects in terms of clinical relevance and developmental impact, and to elucidate how Cronobacter-derived LPS influences human SCE-related behaviors through integrated structural and functional analyses, thereby defining its chemically and biologically distinct microbial signatures.
