Benzyl alcohol biosynthesis and its subcellular compartmentalization enable scent formation and salicylic acid production
Authors: Ji Hee Lee, Xing-Qi Huang, Matthew E Bergman, John A Morgan, Yi-Kai Liu, W Andy Tao, Natalia Dudareva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBenzyl alcohol, a simple aromatic alcohol found in more than half of seed plant families, plays important roles in plant-environmental interactions and serves as a precursor of benzyl benzoate, an intermediate in the phenylalanine-dependent biosynthesis of the phytohormone salicylic acid. Despite its importance, the enzyme responsible for benzyl alcohol formation in plants has remained unknown. Using a combination of classical biochemical, proteomic and genetic approaches, we demonstrated that two NADPH-dependent benzaldehyde reductases, differing by only three amino acids, are responsible for the formation of benzyl alcohol and its downstream derivatives, benzyl benzoate and methyl salicylate, in petunia flowers. Moreover, downregulation of benzaldehyde reductase expression substantially reduces salicylic acid levels in petunia stems upon pathogen infection. These findings provide direct evidence that benzaldehyde reductase is the last unidentified enzyme in salicylic acid biosynthesis via the benzyl benzoate intermediate. Moreover, the subcellular localization of benzaldehyde reductase homologs across flowering plants is species-specific, occurring either in peroxisomes or cytosol, suggesting that different species employ distinct compartmental organization for phenylalanine-dependent salicylic acid biosynthesis.Subject terms: Secondary metabolism, Biochemistry, Plant cell biology

Introduction

Benzyl alcohol, a simple aromatic alcohol with a mild, slightly sweet, and pleasant smell, is commonly found in flowers, fruits, and leaves of many plant species1–4. Although it occurs in more than half of the seed plant families, benzyl alcohol is typically present only as a minor constituent of floral volatiles, but it is produced at high levels in some species, including jasmine and apricot mei (Prunus mume) flowers and various tea plants3–6. Benzyl alcohol plays important roles in plant-environmental interactions, serving as a pollinator attractant that elicits antenna responses in hawk moths5 and acting as a defense compound, either directly through its repellent activity toward herbivores7,8 or indirectly via tritrophic interactions9. It is released in response to herbivory not only aboveground10–13 but also belowground from tomato roots following leaf infestation by Spodoptera exigua14. Benzyl alcohol also exhibits antifungal properties, contributing to plant resistance against pathogen infection15. In addition, it participates in plant-plant communication under drought stress by promoting leaf wilting in neighboring tea plants16, whereas its release by the plant microbiome promotes plant growth and development17.

Due to its low vapor pressure at ambient temperature, benzyl alcohol generally accumulates in large internal pools relative to its emission flux2,6. Within plant cells, benzyl alcohol can also undergo acylation by acyltransferases of the BAHD superfamily18, a modification that increases its volatility and diversifies volatile profiles. Transfer of an acyl group from coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA) to hydroxyl group of benzyl alcohol by acetyl-CoA:benzyl alcohol acetyltransferase (BEAT) results in the formation of benzyl acetate, a prominent floral volatile with characteristic sweet note emitted by Clarkia breweri, Prunus mume, and Jasminum sambac flowers19–21. Acylation of benzyl alcohol with a larger acyl moiety by benzoyl-CoA:benzyl alcohol benzoyltransferase (BEBT) or benzoyl-CoA:benzyl alcohol/2-phenylethanol benzoyltransferase (BPBT) leads to the formation of benzyl benzoate, a constituent of C. breweri and Petunia hybrida floral scents22,23. Besides being emitted as a component of the scent bouquet, benzyl benzoate has recently been identified as an intermediate in salicylic acid (SA) biosynthesis via a phenylalanine (Phe)/benzoyl-CoA-dependent pathway in rice, tobacco, and poplar24–27. In this pathway, unlike the isochorismate pathway found in a subgroup of the Brassicales order28, benzyl benzoate is first hydroxylated by benzyl benzoate oxidase (BBO), an endoplasmic reticulum-localized cytochrome P450 enzyme, to form benzyl salicylate, which is subsequently hydrolyzed by a cytosolic benzyl salicylate esterase (BSE) to produce SA. In addition, benzyl alcohol can be glycosylated to form non-volatile conjugates that are sequestered in the vacuole and released upon glycosidase-mediated hydrolysis, thereby enhancing scent emission during flowering or following tissue damage29,30. Beyond its biological roles, benzyl alcohol is a high-value industrial chemical widely used in soap, perfume, cosmetics, flavor, and pharmaceutical industries. Its low volatility and toxicity, combined with strong polarity, make it an effective solvent in the polymer and coating industries, including the production of inks, paints, adhesives, and automobile tires31. Therefore, its broad economic importance requires efficient production strategies, whether through chemical synthesis or metabolic engineering approaches32,33.

Benzyl alcohol, like most phenylpropanoid/benzenoid compounds, is synthesized from Phe23,34. Previous reports supported the interconversion between benzyl alcohol and benzaldehyde23,35, and feeding experiments with [2H8]-L-Phe in petunia flowers revealed that both compounds are derived from the β-oxidative pathway of benzoic acid biosynthesis35 (Fig. 1a). Feeding with 2H5-benzoic acid further demonstrated that it can be activated to benzoyl-CoA, which is the precursor for benzaldehyde and subsequently benzyl alcohol formation. Heterodimeric benzaldehyde synthases (BSs) consisting of α and β subunits and exhibiting strict substrate specificity towards benzoyl-CoA, have recently been isolated and characterized in petunia (Petunia hybrida), tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), almond (Prunus dulcis), and Arabidopsis thaliana35 and more recently in poplar (Populus trichocarpa)27. However, no gene responsible for benzyl alcohol biosynthesis in planta has been identified to date.

Using P. hybrida as a model system and a classical biochemical approach, we identified and characterized two petunia benzaldehyde reductases (BRs) and showed that they are involved in the formation of benzyl alcohol and subsequently SA via the benzyl benzoate intermediate. The petunia BRs identified here, along with their homologs from a wide range of plant species, form a distinct clade within the Class II cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD) multigene family. The BRs’ subcellular localization appears to be species-specific, suggesting that different species employ distinct compartmental organization for Phe-dependent SA biosynthesis.

Results

Identification of benzaldehyde reductase(s)

We previously showed that downregulation of PhBS genes in petunia flowers reduces benzyl alcohol and benzyl benzoate levels, whereas the reconstitution of the β-oxidative benzaldehyde biosynthetic pathway in N. benthamiana, together with Phe feeding, yields high levels of benzyl alcohol35. These results, together with feeding experiments with deuterium-labeled Phe and benzoic acid35 as well as with 2H7-benzyl alcohol showing reversibility of benzaldehyde conversion to benzyl alcohol23, suggest that benzyl alcohol is derived from benzaldehyde. Formation of benzyl alcohol from benzaldehyde could be catalyzed by aldehyde reductases or alcohol dehydrogenases. To date, phenylacetaldehyde reductases (PARs) catalyzing the reduction of phenylacetaldehyde to 2-phenylethanol have been characterized in tomato, rose, and poplar36–38. Although these enzymes exhibit substrate promiscuity and can use benzaldehyde as a substrate in vitro, their catalytic efficiency toward benzaldehyde in tomato was <3% of that toward phenylacetaldehyde, whereas rose PAR exhibited 47% of the activity observed with phenylacetaldehyde36,38. In addition to PARs, non-canonical CADs from Arabidopsis, cider gum (Eucalyptus gunnii), and alfalfa (Medicago sativa) have been shown to exhibit benzaldehyde-reducing activity in vitro39–43. Therefore, we searched for PAR and CAD homologs in the genome of Petunia axillaris, a parent that contributes ~96% to P. hybrida gene space44. Candidate genes encoding proteins with a cutoff of ≥70% amino acid sequence similarity were selected (Supplementary Table 1), and their expression profiles, along with other known petunia scent biosynthetic genes, were analyzed in RNA-Seq datasets from petunia corolla at two developmental stages, day −1 (bud) and day 2 post-anthesis as well as at three time points during a day/night cycle45 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Expression of PAR and CAD homologs was low to very low (< 25 Transcripts Per Million) at around peak emission (21:00 h) when compared with scent genes, except for Peaxi162Scf00016g02329, which showed high expression levels correlated with scent biosynthetic genes. Thus, the protein encoded by Peaxi162Scf00016g02329 was recombinantly produced in E. coli and its substrate specificity was analyzed (Supplementary Fig. 2). Biochemical characterization identified the enzyme as a bona fide CAD, designated as PhCAD1, which exhibited negligible activity towards benzaldehyde. Therefore, a classical biochemical approach was used to identify the enzyme(s) responsible for benzyl alcohol formation.

BR activity was analyzed using crude extracts from petunia petals collected at 21:00 h on days 1 and 2 post-anthesis. Efficient benzyl alcohol formation was detected in the presence of NADPH as the reducing cofactor, whereas only 9.4–11.4% of this activity was observed with NADH (Fig. 1b and c). Control reactions lacking those cofactors or containing denatured crude extract produced no detectable product, indicating that benzyl alcohol formation depends on a putative BR that catalyzes the predominantly NADPH-dependent reduction of benzaldehyde to the corresponding alcohol (Fig. 1b). Thus, BR was partially purified from crude protein extracts using ammonium sulfate precipitation (50–60% saturation; Supplementary Fig. 3) followed by MonoQ anion exchange chromatography (Fig. 1d), resulting in up to a 27-fold increase in specific activity with 8% recovery (Table 1). MonoQ fractions 18–23, with specific activities ranging from 42.6 to 2529.2 pKat·mg protein−1 (Fig. 1e), were then subjected to proteomic analysis. MS/MS spectra acquired on a trapped ion mobility spectrometry time-of-flight mass spectrometer (timsTOF MS) were searched against the protein sequences encoded in the Petunia axillaris genome, resulting in the identification of 2853 proteins. Comparison of their peptide abundance profiles with BR activity across fractions identified 45 candidate proteins showing correlated abundance patterns. Given the cofactor dependence of BR, these candidates were further screened for the presence of NAD(P)-binding domains, resulting in two proteins, Peaxi162Scf01125g00411 and Peaxi162Scf00956g00411. The corresponding cDNAs were obtained from Petunia hybrida and based on the results presented below, the encoded proteins were designated as Petunia hybrida benzaldehyde reductase 1 and 2 (PhBR1 and PhBR2), respectively (Fig. 1e). Interestingly, these proteins were among the CAD homologs identified through bioinformatic analysis (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1) and shared 72–74% sequence homology with AtCAD7 and AtCAD8. Moreover, five additional CAD homologs were detected in the fractions’ proteome (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1); however, they were present at very low abundance and showed no correlation to BR activity (Fig. 1e), suggesting that their contribution to benzyl alcohol formation is limited.

Purification of benzaldehyde reductase from petunia petals

PhBR1 and PhBR2 encode proteins of 39.5 kDa and 39.4 kDa, respectively, each containing an alcohol dehydrogenase GroES-like domain and an NAD(P)-binding Rossmann-fold domain. They share 99.2% identity with only three amino acid differences, Ser82, Trp97, and Thr327 in PhBR1, and Asn82, Cys97, and Ile327 in the corresponding positions in PhBR2 (Supplementary Fig. 4). Phylogenetic analysis revealed that PhBR1 and PhBR2 reside in Class II of the CAD multigene family, which belongs to the medium-chain dehydrogenase/reductase (MDR) superfamily (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6). In contrast to Class I bona fide CADs, which contain conserved motifs and active-site residues and function in monolignol biosynthesis46, Class II members possess amino acid substitutions within the substrate-binding pocket that result in broader or altered substrate specificity, generally higher Kms, and reduced catalytic activity toward monolignol substrates relative to Class I CADs47,48, suggesting that they perform diverse physiological functions in planta.

Biochemical characterization of PhBRs

Biochemical characterization of recombinant PhBR1 and PhBR2 produced in E. coli and purified by affinity chromatography (Supplementary Fig. 7) revealed that both enzymes catalyze the NADPH-dependent reduction of benzaldehyde (Fig. 2a). In the presence of NADH, PhBR1 and PhBR2 exhibited only 1.5 and 8%, respectively, of the activity observed with NADPH (Fig. 2a), which was comparable to levels detected in crude extracts (Fig. 1c). Given that PhBRs belong to the MDR superfamily which are capable of catalyzing reversible reactions, their alcohol dehydrogenase activities were assessed using benzyl alcohol as a substrate in the presence of NADP+. Oxidation activities of PhBR1 and PhBR2 were only 2.9 and 9.2% of their respective reductase activities (Fig. 2b), suggesting that these enzymes strongly favor the forward reduction reaction. Size-exclusion chromatography revealed apparent molecular masses of ~77 and ~73 kDa for PhBR1 and PhBR2, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 8), suggesting that these enzymes are active as homodimers. The enzymes exhibit slightly different pH optima, with PhBR1 showing peak activity at pH 7.5 and PhBR2 at pH 8.6 (Supplementary Fig. 9a) and both display maximal activity at 44 °C (Supplementary Fig. 9b).

As CAD enzymes exhibit substrate promiscuity, the reductase activities of purified recombinant PhBRs were examined using canonical CAD substrates (cinnamaldehyde, p-coumaraldehyde, coniferaldehyde, and sinapaldehyde) as well as phenylacetaldehyde, an aromatic aldehyde produced in petunia petals (Fig. 2c). In addition, hexanal, a saturated aliphatic aldehyde, along with several aromatic aldehydes structurally related to benzaldehyde and bearing various substitutions on the aromatic ring, including salicylaldehyde, 3-hydroxybenzaldehyde, 4-hydroxybenzaldehyde, m-anisaldehyde, and vanillin, were tested (Supplementary Fig. 10). Both PhBR1 and PhBR2 reduced all tested substrates except salicylaldehyde, 3-hydroxybenzaldehyde, 4-hydroxybenzaldehyde, and vanillin, while sinapaldehyde was reduced only by PhBR2, with benzaldehyde and cinnamaldehyde being the preferred substrates for both enzymes. Whereas PhBR2 showed its highest activity with benzaldehyde and 20% lower activity with cinnamaldehyde, PhBR1 displayed slightly higher relative activity with cinnamaldehyde (117% of its activity with benzaldehyde) (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 2). The largest differences in substrate specificity between the two enzymes were found for p-coumaraldehyde and phenylacetaldehyde. PhBR1 retained 73% activity with p-coumaraldehyde compared with benzaldehyde, whereas PhBR2 showed only 4%. For phenylacetaldehyde, PhBR2 exhibited 26% activity and PhBR1 only 2% (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 2).

Further biochemical characterization of purified recombinant PhBR proteins at pH 7.5 revealed that overall PhBR2 has higher affinity for benzaldehyde and cinnamaldehyde with apparent Km values of 72.4 ± 5.9 and 46.4 ± 5.7 μM, respectively, compared with 194.4 ± 10.6 and 131.3 ± 8.6 μM for PhBR1 (Table 2). Despite the ~2.8-fold higher Km value of PhBR1 for cinnamaldehyde, the apparent catalytic efficiencies (kcat/Km ratio) were similar for both enzymes due to the higher turnover number of PhBR1. In contrast, the catalytic efficiency of PhBR1 toward benzaldehyde was approximately half that of PhBR2. Overall, kinetic characterization of PhBR1 and PhBR2 revealed that, although they are less efficient than the bona fide Class I CAD proteins, their parameters are in line with those of Class II CAD members47,48. Kinetic parameters toward benzaldehyde and cinnamaldehyde were also determined for PhBR2 at its pH optimum (pH 8.6) (Table 2). Relative to pH 7.5, the apparent catalytic efficiencies were overall ~twofold to threefold higher mainly due to lower Km values with only minor changes in Vmax.

The apparent Km values for NADPH with benzaldehyde as a substrate determined at pH 7.5 were similar for both enzymes (80.3 ± 2.5 and 85.2 ± 8.7 μM for PhBR1 and PhBR2, respectively), resulting in comparable catalytic efficiencies. However, in the presence of cinnamaldehyde, PhBR2 exhibited a nearly fourfold lower Km for NADPH than PhBR1 (25.9 ± 3.0 versus 101.1 ± 6.3 μM) and a twofold higher catalytic efficiency (Table 2). PhBR2 Km for NADPH was even nearly two fold lower at pH 8.6 (14.1 ± 1.9 μM), resulting in ~two fold higher catalytic efficiency than at pH 7.5. Interestingly, PhBR2, but not PhBR1, was inhibited at NADPH concentrations above 500 μM, with a stronger inhibitory effect observed in the presence of benzaldehyde (Ki = 1.1 mM) than cinnamaldehyde (Ki = 4.6 mM) (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Similar NADPH inhibitory effects were maintained at pH 8.6 (Supplementary Fig. 11b). In contrast to PhBR2, PhBR1 efficiently reduced p-coumaraldehyde, however, it was a less favored substrate with a Km value of 744.5 ± 31.8 μM and a catalytic efficiency of 4.7 ± 0.1 mM−1 s−1, which is 4.7-fold lower than that for benzaldehyde (Table 2). Although PhBR2 showed measurable activity towards phenylacetaldehyde (Fig. 2c), it appeared to be a poor substrate (Supplementary Fig. 11a), suggesting that a different enzyme is likely responsible for 2-phenylethanol formation in petunia flowers.

Since PhBR1 and PhBR2 differ by only three amino acid residues, their structures were predicted using Boltz-249 to identify key contributing residue(s) for differences in substrate specificity. Among these substitutions, only the residue at position 97 is located within the putative substrate binding pocket (Fig. 2d). Therefore, site-directed mutagenesis was performed to swap these residues in PhBRs, generating Trp97Cys PhBR1 and Cys97Trp PhBR2 mutants. Substitution of Trp97 with Cys drastically reduced PhBR1 activity toward p-coumaraldehyde, making it nearly identical to that of PhBR2 (1.6 and 1.5 nKat·mg protein−1 for PhBR1 and PhBR2, respectively) (Fig. 2e). By contrast, the reciprocal substitution (Cys97Trp) increased PhBR2 activity to a level only 20% lower than that of PhBR1. Moreover, these substitutions altered activities toward other tested substrates, effectively converting PhBR2 into a PhBR1-type enzyme (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Table 2) and suggesting that residue 97 is a key determinant of substrate specificity in these two highly similar proteins.

Expression of PhBR1 and PhBR2 in petunia

To examine the involvement of PhBR1 and PhBR2 in benzyl alcohol formation, their spatial and developmental expression profiles were analyzed by quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) with gene-specific primers. PhBR1 and PhBR2 were expressed in all examined tissues, but at different levels, with the highest expression initially observed in stem and ovaries, followed by sepals and leaves (Fig. 3a). However, their expression was ~tenfold higher in corolla tissues at the bud stage and transcript levels of both genes decreased sharply after flower opening on day 1 post-anthesis and declined gradually thereafter (Fig. 3b). Unexpectedly, these developmental expression profiles did not coincide with those of known scent biosynthetic genes, which show an increase in expression from buds to day 2 post-anthesis23. Analysis of BR activity in crude protein extracts from petunia corollas over flower development revealed that enzyme activity did not closely parallel transcript abundance, with activity declining more gradually than transcript levels. Indeed, transcript abundance decreased by nearly 95% on day 1 post-anthesis, whereas BR activity declined by only ~25% and continued to decrease as the flowers senesced, retaining ~15% of maximal activity on day 7 post-anthesis (36.6 of 237.7 pKat·mg protein−1) (Fig. 3c) despite only 0.2% remaining mRNA levels (Fig. 3b). These results suggest that native PhBRs are mainly produced at the bud stage and remain relatively stable and catalytically active during flower development. Consistent with this, protein stability assays showed that PhBR1 retained 66% of its initial activity and PhBR2 retained 29% after 7 days at 21 °C (Supplementary Fig. 12). Moreover, no diurnal variations in PhBR activity were detected (Fig. 3c), indicating that, similar to S-adenosyl-L-methionine:benzoic acid/SA carboxyl methyltransferase (BSMT) which is responsible for methyl benzoate production in petunia flowers50, nocturnal benzyl alcohol production in petunia is regulated primarily at the level of substrate availability, as benzaldehyde levels fluctuate rhythmically and peak during the night23.

PhBRs are responsible for benzyl alcohol biosynthesis in petunia flowers

To investigate the role of PhBRs in planta, transgenic petunia plants with simultaneous downregulation of both genes were generated using an RNA interference (RNAi) approach. Three independent lines with the greatest PhBRs suppression were selected for further analysis. Since petunia contains a homolog of PhBR1 and PhBR2, Peaxi162cf00623g00411 (Supplementary Fig. 1) encoding a protein that shares 76.7% amino acid identity (85% homology) with PhBR1 and 77% identity (85.6% homology) with PhBR2, we examined its expression in transgenic RNAi lines by qRT-PCR with gene specific primers and found that its transcript levels remained unchanged in day 2 flowers (Supplementary Fig. 13). Reduction in PhBR1 expression by 52–71% and PhBR2 expression by 62–79% (Fig. 4a) resulted in 22–53% decrease in benzaldehyde reductase activity in crude petal extracts (Fig. 4b). Consistent with reduced expression and activity, benzyl alcohol emission and its levels in petals (hereafter referred to as internal pools) decreased in transgenic flowers by 50–92% and 35–87%, respectively, relative to wild-type and empty vector control flowers (Fig. 4c and d). Emission and internal pools of benzyl benzoate, which depends on benzyl alcohol as a substrate, were also reduced in transgenics by 29–55% and 18–38%, respectively (Fig. 4c and d). Recent studies have shown that SA is synthesized via the benzyl benzoate intermediate24–27. Coinciding with the reduction in benzyl benzoate production, the transgenic lines exhibited a 35–40% decrease in methyl salicylate emission, with no changes in its internal pool levels (Fig. 4c and d). In contrast, phenylethylbenzoate, which shares the benzoyl-CoA precursor with benzyl benzoate, displayed an increase in internal pools by ~53% in transgenics relative to controls with enhanced emission in only one of the lines (Supplementary Fig. 14a and b). Despite the observed changes in the emission of aforementioned volatiles, suppression of PhBRs did not alter total VOC emission relative to wild-type and empty vector controls, as benzyl alcohol, benzyl benzoate and phenylethylbenzoate with relatively low vapor pressures, together account for only ~5% of total emitted VOCs (Supplementary Fig. 14c). In contrast, these compounds possess substantially larger internal pools2 comprising ~27% of the total internal VOCs. Therefore, the reduction in their internal pools in PhBR-RNAi flowers led to a decrease in the total internal pools relative to controls (Supplementary Fig. 14d). Reduced benzyl alcohol production did not affect benzaldehyde emission or internal pools, which remained unchanged in transgenic plants (Fig. 4c and d).

PhBRs are involved in salicylic acid biosynthesis

In highly scented petunia flowers, methyl benzoate is the main emitted constituent, accounting for 67% of total VOC emissions, while methyl salicylate constitutes <0.1%. Both compounds are produced by the same enzymes, BSMT1 and BSMT2, which differ in three amino acids and exhibit apparent catalytic efficiencies (kcat/Km ratio) 40- to 75-fold higher with SA than with benzoic acid51. The low levels of produced methyl salicylate can be attributed to limited flux to SA caused by very low expression of BBO and BSE (Supplementary Fig. 1), which was alleviated by transient overexpression of BBO and BSE in petunia petals (Supplementary Fig. 15). Despite very low SA production in wild-type petunia flowers, downregulation of PhBRs further reduced methyl salicylate emission (Fig. 4c), indicating a decrease in the methyl salicylate biosynthetic flux and suggesting involvement of PhBRs in the recently discovered SA biosynthetic pathway via benzyl benzoate24–27.

As SA plays a central role in plant immune response and is highly inducible upon pathogen infection52, petunia leaves and stems were treated with Pseudomonas syringae pv. maculicola (Psm) and SA levels were analyzed at six time points over 48 h (Supplementary Fig. 16a). A stronger induction was observed in stems, where SA levels gradually increased, peaking at 21 h post-infection before declining. In contrast, leaves responded much earlier, peaking at 9 h post-infection, but the maximum pool of SA was threefold lower than in stem. In both tissues, SA glucosides increased following the peak accumulation of free SA (Supplementary Fig. 16b). Therefore, to examine the direct involvement of PhBRs in SA biosynthesis, stems of transgenic petunia plants along with empty vector controls were infected with Psm and SA levels were analyzed 21 h post-infection. Indeed, the reduction in expression of both PhBR1 and PhBR2 by 45–90% in the stem of transgenic plants (Fig. 5a), which was maintained after mock treatment and pathogen infection compared to empty vector controls (Fig. 5b) resulted in a 56–84% decrease in SA levels compared with the controls (Fig. 5c), and in a reduction in SA glucoside levels for PhBR-RNAi lines 28 and 73 (Supplementary Fig. 16c). To ensure that the observed phenotypes in transgenic lines are exclusively attributable to PhBR1 and PhBR2 downregulation, we analyzed the expression of Peaxi162cf00623g00411 in untreated petunia stem (Supplementary Fig. 17a and b), as well as after mock treatment and pathogen infection (Supplementary Fig. 17c). Although its expression was induced upon pathogen infection, it was not affected by the PhBR-RNAi construct in transgenic lines (Supplementary Fig. 17b and c) as observed in corolla (Supplementary Fig. 13). Therefore, taken together, these results support that PhBR is the last missing enzyme in Phe-dependent SA biosynthesis via the benzyl benzoate intermediate.

Comparative analysis of expression of genes involved in SA biosynthesis, PhBPBT, PhBSα, PhBSβ, PhBR1 and PhBR2, PhBBO, and PhBSE in petunia stem revealed that PhBRs exhibited the lowest expression, and, except for PhBR2, all these genes were transcriptionally upregulated to different degrees in response to pathogen infection (Fig. 5b and d). While PhBPBT and PhBBO exhibited the strongest responses (28- and 15-fold increase in mRNA levels, respectively), PhBR1 and PhBSβ showed only a modest increase in expression (1.9- and 1.3-fold, respectively). Additionally, PhBR1 induction was efficiently suppressed by the RNAi construct in transgenic lines (Fig. 5b).

Subcellular localization of PhBRs

Benzaldehyde, the substrate for PhBRs, is synthesized in petunia flowers by a peroxisomal heterodimeric BS that reduces benzoyl-CoA generated via the peroxisomal β-oxidative pathway of benzoic acid biosynthesis35,53–55, suggesting peroxisomes as the site of benzyl alcohol biosynthesis. However, DeepLoc 2.1, a language model-based protein sorting prediction algorithm that can differentiate between 10 different localizations and 4 different membrane associations56, predicted PhBRs to localize to the cytosol. To experimentally determine their subcellular localization, the PhBR1 and PhBR2 coding regions were fused to the C-terminus of the enhanced yellow fluorescent protein (EYFP) and transiently co-expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves with a mCherry-labeled peroxisomal marker protein. The fluorescence signals of EYFP-PhBRs showed typical cytosol localization and did not colocalize with the peroxisome marker signal (Supplementary Fig. 18), suggesting that, unlike benzaldehyde, benzyl alcohol in petunia is synthesized in the cytosol rather than in peroxisomes.

Species-specific compartmentalization of benzyl alcohol biosynthesis

To determine whether benzyl alcohol biosynthesis occurs in the cytosol in other plant species, we searched for the closest PhBR homologs in rice (Oryza sativa) and poplar, for which SA biosynthesis via benzyl benzoate was recently demonstrated24,26,27 as well as in N. tabacum, in which SA formation relies on the peroxisomal β-oxidative pathway of benzoic acid biosynthesis57. Two homologs from tobacco (LOC107792503 and LOC107777955, hereafter designated NtBR1 and NtBR2), one from rice (Os04g15920; OsCAD648, hereafter designated OsBR), and one from poplar (Potri.002G018300; PoptrCAD7, hereafter designated PtBR) were identified. NtBR1 and NtBR2 share 92/94% and 91/92% amino acid identity/similarity with PhBRs, respectively, whereas OsBR and PtBR exhibit 72/80% and 78/84% identity/similarity, respectively. Analysis of substrate specificities of the recombinant proteins encoded by these genes revealed that they, like PhBRs, indeed reduce benzaldehyde (Supplementary Fig. 19 and Fig. 2c), suggesting their potential involvement in benzyl alcohol formation. In addition, similar to PhBR1 (Fig. 2c), they use cinnamaldehyde and p-coumaraldehyde as substrates, but unlike PhBR1, they also accept sinapaldehyde, which is in agreement with previous characterization of OsCAD648.

Unexpectedly, DeepLoc 2.156 predicted different localizations for the PhBR homologs, with peroxisomal localization for OsBR and PtBR and cytosolic localization for NtBR1 and NtBR2. To experimentally verify their subcellular localization, the coding region of each homolog was fused to the C-terminus of the EYFP and transiently co-expressed in N. benthamiana leaves with peroxisomal mCherry-labeled marker protein. Consistent with the prediction, both NtBR1 and NtBR2, like the PhBRs (Supplementary Fig. 18), exhibited cytosolic localization, whereas OsBR and PtBR were detected in peroxisomes (Supplementary Fig. 20). Moreover, fusion of OsBR and PtBR to the N-terminus of the EYFP (OsBR-EYFP and PtBR-EYFP) blocked peroxisomal targeting signal, resulting in the protein relocalization to the cytosol (Supplementary Fig. 21). These results suggest that benzyl alcohol is produced in distinct subcellular compartments depending on the plant species: in the peroxisomes in rice and poplar and in the cytosol in petunia and N. tabacum.

Further analysis of BR homologs across diverse plant species with a cutoff of ≥ 80% amino acid identity revealed that these proteins cluster together forming a BR subgroup within Class II CAD proteins, which includes PhBRs, OsBR, PtBR, NtBR1, and NtBR2 (Fig. 6). Prediction of subcellular localization of proteins encoded by members of the CAD gene family using DeepLoc 2.1 showed that Class I bona fide CADs and Class III members are localized exclusively in the cytosol, whereas Class II members display mixed localization, mainly in the cytosol or peroxisomes (Fig. 6). Interestingly, expansion of the BR subgroup to 241 members revealed that ~50% of the proteins are predicted to localize to peroxisomes, with the remaining half localized in the cytosol (Supplementary Fig. 22).

The distinct subcellular localization of benzyl alcohol biosynthesis in plant species raises the question of whether benzyl benzoate biosynthesis is likewise species-specific. Recently, it has been reported that rice and tobacco BEBTs, which directly use the product of BRs and catalyze benzyl benzoate formation from benzoyl-CoA and benzyl alcohol, localize to peroxisomes24,57. In contrast, petunia PhBPBT23, according to the WoLF PSORT program, is predicted to localize to the cytosol and lacks a canonical peroxisomal targeting signal, PTS1 ([S/A]-[K/R]-[L/M/I]) at the C-terminus58 or PTS2. However, DeepLoc 2.1 predicted its peroxisomal localization. Despite being discovered almost two decades ago23, the PhBPBT subcellular localization has not been experimentally verified. Therefore, the PhBPBT coding region was fused to either the 5′- or 3′-end of the EYFP reporter gene and transiently co-expressed in N. benthamiana leaves with a peroxisomal marker. The N-terminal fusion protein EYFP-PhBPBT showed peroxisomal localization, while the C-terminal fusion protein PhBPBT-EYFP accumulated in the cytosol (Supplementary Fig. 23), confirming DeepLoc 2.1 prediction. Further DeepLoc 2.1-based predictions of BEBT homolog localization across 118 plant species revealed that, in contrast to the variable subcellular localization of BRs, they are predicted to localize almost exclusively to peroxisomes, with only seven exceptions (Supplementary Fig. 24).

Discussion

Benzyl alcohol is a constituent of floral scents produced by at least 50 plant families59, in addition to contributing to flavors of some fruits and teas4,60,61. The list of plant species producing benzyl alcohol has expanded substantially following the recent discovery, in vascular plants outside the Brassicaceae, of a Phe-dependent SA biosynthetic pathway via benzyl benzoate24–27 for which benzyl alcohol serves as an immediate precursor22,23,62. However, no gene responsible for in planta benzyl alcohol biosynthesis has been reported. Outside of scent and flavor-producing organs, benzyl alcohol usually accumulates only to very small, often undetectable levels, even during SA production in response to pathogen infection25,27. Although it was quantifiable in N. benthamiana bebt mutants after Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato (Pst) DC3000 infection25, its endogenous levels were ~sevenfold to 14-fold lower than those observed in petunia flowers. Therefore, we took advantage of the high levels of benzyl alcohol formation in petunia flowers and identified genes encoding PhBRs using a classical biochemical approach. The identified PhBRs and their homologs from diverse plant species form a distinct BR subgroup within Class II of the CAD multigene family (Fig. 6). Further biochemical and genetic analyses provide evidence that members of this subgroup are responsible for benzyl alcohol production (Fig. 4c and d) and participate in SA biosynthesis via the benzyl benzoate intermediate (Fig. 5a and c). Both petunia enzymes, which differ by only three amino acids (Supplementary Fig. 4), exhibit substrate promiscuity, preferentially reducing benzaldehyde and cinnamaldehyde (Fig. 2c and Table 2), with residue 97 (Trp in PhBR1 and Cys in PhBR2) being responsible for their slightly different substrate specificities (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Table 2). Interestingly, the developmental expression profile of PhBRs does not coincide with that of scent biosynthetic genes or benzyl alcohol emission, as transcript levels peak prior to flower opening (Fig. 3b). Although the physiological significance of such an expression pattern is currently unknown, it closely resembles the expression profiles of PARs responsible for 2-phenylethanol formation from phenylacetaldehyde in tomato and Persian musk rose, which likewise show peak mRNA accumulation at the budding stage of the flowers38,63. Nevertheless, the stability of the PhBR enzymes (Supplementary Fig. 12), and potentially post-translational modifications, enable them to retain enzymatic activity even as gene expression declines to nearly undetectable levels over 7 days post-anthesis (Fig. 3b and c).

Analysis of PhBR activities in petunia petals over the day/night cycle on days 1 and 2 post-anthesis revealed no rhythmic oscillations, and a slight developmental decline (Fig. 3c), suggesting that the rhythmic emission of benzyl alcohol62, like that of methyl benzoate50, is primarily regulated by the availability of its precursor, benzaldehyde, whose levels fluctuate rhythmically peaking at night23. PhBS and PhBRs function sequentially, with PhBS producing benzaldehyde substrate for PhBRs. Both enzymes exhibit comparable Vmax (PhBR1, 51.3 ± 0.9 nKat/mg protein; PhBR2, 41.6 ± 0.8 nKat/mg protein (Table 2); and PhBS, 63.5 ± 2.7 nKat/mg protein35). However, the catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) of PhBS (13.9 ± 1.8 mM−1 s−135) is 1.6–3.5-fold lower than that of PhBRs, suggesting that PhBS likely catalyzes a rate-limiting step of the two. Indeed, among the seven genes involved in SA biosynthesis that were analyzed in stems after pathogen infection, PhBRs showed the lowest expression levels, and PhBR2 was the only gene whose expression was not induced (Fig. 5b and d).

Suppression of PhBR1 and PhBR2 expression in petals reduced the emission and internal pools of benzyl alcohol and benzyl benzoate as well as the emission of methyl salicylate (Fig. 4c and d). Moreover, their reduced expression in stem decreased pathogen-induced SA levels by up to 84% (Fig. 5c), providing direct evidence that PhBRs are involved in benzyl alcohol formation and subsequently in SA biosynthesis via benzyl benzoate.

Unlike most flowering plants, which possess close PhBR homologs, members of the Brassicaceae family, including Arabidopsis thaliana and Brassica napus, harbor more distantly related homologs that form a distinct, isolated clade (Supplementary Fig. 22). This pattern is not observed in other families within the Brassicales order (e.g., Caricaceae, Carica papaya). Although the Brassicaceae family predominantly relies on the isochorismate pathway, which accounts for ~90% of pathogen- and UV light-induced SA production64,65, their genomes still retain genes encoding enzymes of the β-oxidative pathways for benzoic acid biosynthesis and BS24,35. However, genes encoding BRs, BBO, and BSE in the Phe-dependent SA biosynthetic route appear to have undergone accelerated sequence evolution and functional drift. Despite the low selective pressure, it is possible that Arabidopsis BRs, BBO, and BSE enzymes maintain their activities and contribute along with moonlight activities of other enzymes via the Phe-dependent pathway to the remaining 10% of SA production, although other scenarios are not excluded66–68.

In petunia, PhBRs exhibit cytosolic localization (Supplementary Fig. 18), unlike PhBS35 and the enzymes responsible for the side-chain shortening of cinnamic acid to benzoyl-CoA, e.g., cinnamate:CoA ligase53, cinnamoyl-CoA hydratase/dehydrogenase54, and 3-ketoacyl thiolase55, all of which are localized in the peroxisomes. Further prediction of the subcellular localization of other members of the BR subfamily (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 22), together with experimental validation of NtBR1, NtBR2, OsBR, and PtBR localizations (Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21), revealed that the site of benzyl alcohol biosynthesis is species-specific. While in some plants like rice and poplar, the synthesis of both benzaldehyde and benzyl alcohol occurs in peroxisomes, in petunia and tobacco (N. tabacum), these biosynthetic steps are compartmentally separated. In contrast to BRs, BEBT enzymes are predicted to localize almost exclusively to peroxisomes (Supplementary Fig. 24), consistent with the reported peroxisomal localization of rice and tobacco BEBTs24,57 and our experimental confirmation of petunia BPBT peroxisomal localization (Supplementary Fig. 23). Collectively, these results suggest that in petunia and tobacco, benzaldehyde synthesized in peroxisomes must leave the membrane-bound organelle to be reduced to benzyl alcohol in the cytosol, and benzyl alcohol needs to shuttle back to the peroxisomes to be used by BPBT for benzyl benzoate biosynthesis, while all these steps occur in peroxisomes in rice and poplar (Fig. 7). Interestingly, within the same genera, for example Nicotiana and Papaver, BRs appeared to be subjected to different selection pressures leading to the loss of peroxisomal localization due to mutations in the canonical PTS1 targeting signal. In the Nicotiana genus, while N. attenuata and N. benthamiana retain peroxisomal BRs, N. sylvestris and N. tabacum possess BRs that are localized in the cytosol (Supplementary Fig. 22). Indeed, a single substitution of the final C-terminal Leu residue in NbBR1 to Arg in NtBR1 likely redirected the enzyme from a peroxisomal localization in N. benthamiana to a cytosolic localization in N. tabacum (Supplementary Fig. 22). Moreover, when the same species, for example Andrographis paniculata, Amaranthus tricolor, Asparagus officinalis, and Triticum aestivum, among others, contains two or more BRs, these isoforms are often predicted to exhibit distinct subcellular localization, which typically results from one or more mutations in the PTS1 signal (Supplementary Fig. 22). The driving force and physiological significance of this divergent selection is currently unknown. However, the loss of the peroxisomal targeting signal may have co-evolved with increased benzaldehyde formation. When benzaldehyde is produced at low levels, only a small fraction is expected to reach the cytosol as benzaldehyde readily partitions into the peroxisomal membrane69, thus retaining positive selection pressure for the peroxisomal targeting of BRs. In contrast, in plants like petunia, N. sylvestris70 and N. tabacum flowers (Supplementary Fig. 25), which produce substantial amounts of benzaldehyde in the peroxisomes, this selection pressure is relaxed as benzaldehyde, en route out of the cell, also diffuses throughout the cytosol where it can be efficiently converted to benzyl alcohol by cytosolic BRs. In such a scenario, a portion of the benzyl alcohol is released from the cells while some is re-imported into the peroxisomes, likely by a transporter, especially in plants that produce high levels of benzyl benzoate, like petunia23. Even in the absence of cytosolic BRs, benzyl alcohol is produced in the cytosol by BSE as a co-product of SA (Fig. 7). The presence of a peroxisomal benzyl alcohol transporter would enable benzyl alcohol recycling and may support benzyl benzoate biosynthesis within peroxisomes. Although the isolation of BRs completes the Phe-dependent SA biosynthetic pathway via the benzyl benzoate intermediate, further studies are needed to identify the benzyl alcohol transporter, if any, and evaluate its contribution to SA formation, as well as to elucidate why different species have compartmentally separated benzaldehyde and benzyl alcohol biosynthesis. Additionally, the relationship between BR localization, benzaldehyde production, and pathway flux requires further investigation across diverse plant species.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Wild-type and transgenic P. hybrida cv. Mitchell diploid (W115, Ball Seed Co.) plants were grown under standard greenhouse conditions with a light period from 6:00 to 21:00 h. Given the high sequence identity of PhBR1 and PhBR2 (99.4%), the 300 bp (CDS nucleotides 727–1026) fragment from PhBR1, which has only two substitutions with PhBR2 was used to target both genes.

Before cloning, the fragment was verified to target only the target genes and not to lead to off-target interference using the Sol Genomics Network VIGS Tool (http://vigs.solgenomics.net/). The analysis was performed by comparing the PhBR1-RNAi target sequence against the P. axillaris and P. inflata (parental lines of P. hybrida)44 genomes using default parameters. This analysis predicted that no additional genes would be targeted by small interfering RNAs generated from the PhBR double-stranded RNAi trigger. The RNAi fragment was PCR amplified from P. hybrida bud cDNA using gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table 3), and the resulting amplicon was inserted into the pDONR/Zeo vector (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). After sequence verification, the RNAi fragment was then recombined into the destination vector pK7GWIWG2(II) using LR Clonase II (Invitrogen), resulting in an RNAi cassette under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter. The generated construct was transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain LBA4404, and transgenic P. hybrida plants were obtained via Agrobacterium-mediated leaf disc transformation using a standard transformation protocol71.

Partial purification of benzaldehyde reductases

Day 1 post-anthesis petunia flower limbs were collected from 21:00 to 22:00 h, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until protein purification. All purification procedures were carried out on ice or at 4 °C unless otherwise specified. Ten grams of petal tissue were ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen using a mortar and pestle. 45 mL of protein extraction buffer containing phosphate-buffered saline (137 mM sodium chloride, 2.7 mM potassium chloride, 10 mM sodium phosphate dibasic, and 1.8 mM potassium phosphate monobasic), 10% (v/v) glycerol, 10 mM dithiothreitol, and 1 mM phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride was added to the powder. The slurry was ground for an additional 10 min with a pestle and incubated on ice for 30 min with frequent mixing. The slurry was filtered through a double layer of Miracloth (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA), and the flow-through was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant (crude extract) was collected, and ammonium sulfate was added in 10% increments, starting from 40% saturation up to 70% saturation. After 20 min of gentle rotation at each step, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min. Protein pellets from each precipitation step were re-suspended in 10 mL of buffer A (20 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 10% (v/v) glycerol) and tested for benzaldehyde reductase activity (Supplementary Fig. 3). The 50–60% ammonium sulfate saturation fraction was collected and subjected to further purification after desalting on an Econo-Pac 10DG desalting column (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The desalted fraction was passed through a 0.45 μm filter and loaded onto an anion exchange column Mono Q 5/50 GL (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) pre-equilibrated with buffer A at the flow rate of 0.5 mL min−1 using an FPLC system (AKTA, GE Healthcare). The column was washed with 5 mL of buffer A, and the bound protein was eluted using a linear gradient (20 mL) from 0 to 500 mM NaCl in buffer A. Fractions of 0.5 mL were collected, checked for benzaldehyde reductase activity and analyzed with 12% SDS-PAGE followed by gel staining with Coomassie brilliant blue R-250. Six fractions from Mono Q chromatography (MonoQ 18–23) were subjected to proteomic analysis.

Proteomic analysis of MonoQ fractions

Proteins in the fractions (MonoQ 18–23) were denatured, reduced, and alkylated by incubating at 95 °C for 10 min with shaking in a buffer containing 12 mM sodium deoxycholate, 12 mM sodium lauroyl sarcosinate, and 100 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB), supplemented with 10 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine and 40 mM chloroacetamide. Following incubation, samples were diluted fivefold with 50 mM TEAB and digested overnight at 37 °C using MS-grade trypsin (Thermo Scientific) at an enzyme-to-protein ratio of 1:50 (w/w). Digestion was quenched by acidifying samples to a final concentration of 1% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid. To remove surfactants, an equal volume of ethyl acetate was added to the samples. The mixture was vortexed for 2 min and centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 2 min. The upper organic phase was discarded, and the surfactant removal step was repeated once. The resulting aqueous phase was dried by vacuum centrifugation, resuspended in 0.1% formic acid (FA), and subsequently desalted using in-house prepared C18 StageTips (Empore™ SDB-XC). Peptide concentrations were determined using the Pierce™ Quantitative Colorimetric Peptide Assay (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Equal volume aliquots of each fraction were dried for subsequent LC-MS/MS analysis.

Peptides were reconstituted in 0.1% FA and loaded onto Evotips Pure (EvoSep) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Chromatographic separation was performed on an Evosep One LC system coupled to a timsTOF HT mass spectrometer (Bruker) utilizing the 60 SPD method. Separation was achieved on a PepSep C18 column (8 cm × 150 μm, 1.5 μm; Bruker) connected to a CaptiveSpray source equipped with a 20 μm ZDV Sprayer emitter. Mobile phases consisted of 0.1% FA in water (solvent A) and 0.1% FA in acetonitrile (solvent B). The timsTOF HT was operated in dia-PASEF mode with variable window widths across an m/z range from 300 to 1200. The ion mobility range was set from 0.6 to 1.5 V cm−2. Accumulation and ramp times were set to 100 ms, resulting in a total cycle time of 1.38 s for 12 data-independent acquisition—parallel accumulation-serial fragmentation (dia-PASEF) scans. Collision energy (CE) was set from 59 eV at 1.6 V cm−2 to 20 eV at 0.6 V cm−2. The TIMS dimension was calibrated in positive mode using three Agilent ESI tuning mix ions 622.0289, 0.9848 V cm−2; 922.0097, 1.1895 V cm−2; and 1221.9906, 1.3820 V cm−2.

Raw LC-MS/MS data were processed using Spectronaut™ v18 (Biognosys) via the directDIA (library-free) workflow. MS/MS spectra were searched against the protein sequences encoded by the P. axillaris genome using the Pulsar search engine specifying trypsin/P as the digestion enzyme and peptide lengths of 5–52 amino acids. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, while protein N-terminal acetylation and methionine oxidation were included as variable modifications (five as maximum). False discovery rates for peptide-spectrum matches, peptides, and protein groups were set to 0.01. Retention time prediction was performed using local regression calibration, and identifications were validated via mutated decoy generation with a dynamic size set to 0.1 fractions of the library. Quantification was carried out at the MS2 level with cross-run normalization disabled, and data were filtered based on Q-values without imputing missing values.

Heterologous expression and purification of recombinant proteins

To verify the coding sequences of both PhBR1 and PhBR2 expressed in P. hybrida cv. Mitchell, a single pair of primers targeting both genes (Supplementary Table 3) was used to amplify their coding sequences (CDSs) from bud cDNA. After purification, the PCR product was cloned and sequenced, yielding two distinct sequences corresponding to PhBR1 and PhBR2. The coding sequences of PhBR1 and PhBR2 were then synthesized by Twist Bioscience (South San Francisco, CA, USA) with codon optimization for expression in E. coli. The coding sequences of PhCAD1 and the N. tabacum homologs of PhBRs (NtBR1 and NtBR2) were amplified from petunia petal or tobacco leaf cDNA, respectively, using corresponding gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table 3), while the coding regions of PhBR homologs in O. sativa (OsBR) and Populus trichocarpa (PtBR) were synthesized by Twist Bioscience. The resulting gene fragments were subcloned into the expression vector pMAL-c6T (New England Biolabs, NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA), which features an N-terminal His6-Maltose Binding Protein (MBP) tag, using a ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). After sequence verification, the desired plasmids were transformed into Rosetta2 (DE3) pLysS competent cells (EMD Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Single colonies were picked and cultured overnight in 3 mL LB medium containing 100 mg/L carbenicillin and 34 mg/L chloramphenicol at 37 °C. Overnight cultures were then diluted 100-fold with LB medium containing antibiotics and continued to culture at 37 °C until OD600 reached ~0.5. Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside was added to a final concentration of 0.1 mM, and cells were incubated at 21 °C with shaking for an additional ~17 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4 °C and lysed with BugBuster® Protein Extraction Reagent (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) supplemented with Benzonase® (MilliporeSigma). The lysates were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were used for further purification after filtering through a 0.45 μm filter. Protein purification was carried out using Amylose Resin (NEB) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Enzyme assays

Benzaldehyde reductase activity assays in crude extracts and ammonium sulfate precipitation fractions were carried out in a 100 μL reaction mixture containing 50 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.5), 1 mM benzaldehyde, and 1 mM NADPH. The reaction was initiated by adding a protein, incubated at 32 °C for 1 h, and terminated by adding 100 μL of ice-cold 100% methanol followed by vigorous vortexing. After centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 20 min, 5 μL of the supernatant was subjected to high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis using an Agilent 1260 Infinity II system (Santa Clara, CA) equipped with an Atlantis dC18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 3 μm, Waters) maintained at 30 °C. Products were separated using a 10-min linear gradient from 10% (v/v) acetonitrile in water to 100% acetonitrile at a flow rate of 0.4 mL min−1, and detected with a fluorescence detector (FLD) using 200 nm excitation with emission recorded at 301 nm. Benzyl alcohol was identified by comparing the retention time and emission spectrum with an authentic standard. Quantitation was achieved using a calibration curve generated with an authentic standard. Control assays included reactions (1) without protein extract, (2) without NAPDH but in the presence of benzaldehyde, and (3) with boiled protein extracts. Product verification was also performed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). The reaction was performed as described above, but the product was extracted with 200 μL dichloromethane (DCM) containing 1 μg naphthalene (internal standard). After centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 20 min, 2 μL of the bottom DCM phase was injected in GC-MS.

To determine the optimum pH, the activities of purified PhBR1 and PhBR2 recombinant proteins were assessed using three buffer systems, 50 mM MES with pH ranging from 5.5 to 6.5, 50 mM HEPES with pH ranging from 6.5 to 8.0, and 50 mM CHES with pH ranging from 8.0 to 10.0 in the presence of 1 mM NADPH and 1 mM benzaldehyde at 32 °C for 10 min. The optimum temperature for both PhBRs was analyzed at pH 7.5 and found to be 44 °C (Supplementary Fig. 9b); however, all further assays were conducted under more physiological conditions at 32 °C.

To test substrate specificities, purified MBP-tagged recombinant PhBR1, PhCAD1, NtBR1, NtBR2, OsBR, and PtBR were incubated with 1 mM NADPH and 2 mM aldehyde substrates. For PhBR2, reactions were performed with 0.5 mM NADPH. Reactions were carried out at 32 °C for 10 min and quenched by adding ice-cold methanol as described above. Formation of corresponding products was monitored by HPLC using the same method described above, with variable detection methods for different products. The FLD was used for detecting cinnamyl alcohol (200/317 nm, excitation/emission wavelengths), sinapyl alcohol (260/360 nm), and 2-phenylethanol (200/300 nm), and a diode array detector was used for detecting p-coumaryl alcohol and coniferyl alcohol at 260 nm. All alcohol products were identified by comparing the retention time and emission spectrum with an authentic standard. To quantify enzyme activities toward hexanal, salicylaldehyde, 3‑hydroxybenzaldehyde, 4‑hydroxybenzaldehyde, m‑anisaldehyde, and vanillin, spectrophotometric assays were used. NADPH consumption was monitored by measuring the decrease in absorbance at 340 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) and was calculated using the molar extinction coefficient of NADPH at 340 nm (ε = 6,220 M−1 cm−1) according to the Beer–Lambert law. Reactions containing heat‑denatured protein were included as controls to correct for background changes in absorbance.

To assess protein stability, purified PhBR1 and PhBR2 recombinant proteins were kept at 21 °C, and activity was measured every 24 h for up to 7 days. To evaluate cofactor preference, 1 mM NADH was tested in addition to NADPH. To determine the directionality of reactions, 1 mM NADP+ and 1 mM alcohol substrate were used. Reactions were carried out at 32 °C for 10 min.

For aldehyde Km determination, various substrate concentrations (benzaldehyde, 3.9 μM–2 mM; cinnamaldehyde, 7.81 μM–2 mM; p-coumaraldehyde 7.81 μM–4 mM, and phenylacetaldehyde 62.5 μM–2 mM) were used with 1 mM NADPH in the presence of 0.3 μg of purified MBP-tagged PhBR1, and 0.5 mM NADPH with 0.4 μg of PhBR2. For phenylacetaldehyde Km, 0.7 μg of PhBR2 was used in the presence of 0.25 mM NADPH. For determining the NADPH Km, NADPH concentrations ranged from 7.81 μM to 2 mM in the presence of 4 mM aldehyde substrates. The reactions and subsequent product detection by HPLC were performed as described above. All enzyme assays were carried out at an appropriate enzyme concentration to ensure that reaction velocity was proportional to the enzyme concentration and remained linear throughout the incubation period. Km and Vmax were determined by non-linear fit to the Michaelis–Menten equation using GraphPad Prism, v10.3.1. Triplicate assays were performed for all data points.

Site-directed mutagenesis

To introduce single amino acid substitutions, the entire pMAL-c6T expression vectors containing the wild-type coding region of PhBR1 or PhBR2 were amplified with primers containing the desired mutations (Supplementary Table 3) and recombined using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme). The resulting plasmids were verified by sequencing and transformed into Rosetta2 (DE3) pLysS competent cells (EMD Millipore) for protein expression, purification, and enzyme assays as described above.

Determination of molecular weight

To determine the apparent molecular weight of purified recombinant PhBR proteins, the His6-MBP tag was removed by incubating the proteins with His-tagged TEV protease for ~16 h at 4 °C. Following the incubation, Ni-NTA Agarose (Invitrogen) was used to remove the released His6-MBP, the TEV protease, and any undigested tagged proteins. The resulting tag-free recombinant proteins were concentrated using a Pierce protein concentrator (Thermo Scientific) with a 10 kDa cutoff and then subjected to size exclusion chromatography. A Superose 12 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare) was equilibrated and eluted with the buffer containing 20 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl at the flow rate of 0.4 mL min−1. The column was calibrated with protein molecular weight standard mix (Millipore Sigma) containing the following markers: bovine thyroglobulin (670 kDa), bovine gamma globulin (150 kDa), ovalbumin (44.3 kDa), ribonuclease A type I-A (13.7 kDa), and p-aminobenzoic acid (0.14 kDa).

RNA isolation and gene expression analysis by qRT-PCR

Total RNA from tissues and time points indicated in the text was extracted using the NucleoSpin RNA Plant and Fungi Mini kit (MACHEREY-NAGEL Inc., Allentown, PA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA (1 μg) was reverse transcribed to first-strand cDNA in a 20 μL reaction using the All-In-One 5× RT MasterMix with gDNA Removal (Applied Biological Materials Inc., Richmond, BC, Canada). Individual qRT-PCR reactions were performed in 10 μL using Fast SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) with gene-specific primers listed in Supplementary Table 3 in a QuantStudio 3 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). For relative quantification, Elongation factor 1-alpha (PhEF1α) was used as an internal reference gene, and calculations were performed using the 2−ΔΔCt method72. Absolute quantification of transcript levels was performed using standard curves generated from serial dilutions of purified CDS fragments for each target gene spanning the range observed in the experimental samples. Transcript abundance was calculated and expressed as copy number per microgram of total RNA. Primers used for absolute quantification of PhBR1, PhBR2, PhBSα, PhBSβ, PhBPBT, PhBBO, and PhBSE as well as for standard curve generation, are listed in Supplementary Table 3. Each data point represents average of two technical replicates.

Analysis of floral volatiles

Volatile emissions from petunia flowers were analyzed using a dynamic headspace collection method followed by GC-MS, as described previously73 with minor modifications. In brief, emitted volatiles were collected from a minimum of three day 2 post-anthesis transgenic and wild-type flowers between 18:00 and 21:00 h for each biological replicate. Absorbed volatiles were eluted from collection traps containing 20 mg of Porapak Q (80–100 mesh) (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) with 1 mL DCM supplemented with 1 μg of naphthalene as an internal standard. For analysis of total internal pools of volatiles, 100 mg of ground tissue was extracted at room temperature for 1 h with 1 mL DCM containing 1 μg naphthalene as an internal standard. The extracts were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min and the supernatants were concentrated to about 200 μL. Samples were analyzed on an Agilent 7890B-5977B GC-MS system using an analytical method translated from an established method with Agilent 6890N-5975B GC-MS system as previously described in ref. 74. Quantitation of different volatile compounds was performed according to calibration curves generated with authentic standards. N. tabacum floral volatiles were collected by a closed-loop stripping method35,75 from 11 “ready to open” cut flower buds at stage 1076,77 per biological replicate over 24 h using Porapak Q traps. Volatiles were eluted with 200 μL DCM and analyzed by GC-MS as described above.

Subcellular localization

The full-length coding regions of PhBRs, PhBPBT, and NtBRs were amplified from petunia petal or tobacco leaf cDNA using corresponding gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table 3), while those of OsBR and PtBR were amplified from gene fragments synthesized by Twist Bioscience. The resulting amplicons were cloned into binary vectors pCNHP-EYFP-C and pCNHP-EYFP35, which express fusion proteins with N-terminal and C-terminal EYFP, respectively. The sequencing-verified constructs, along with an empty vector, were transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101. Single colonies for each construct were picked and cultured overnight at 28 °C in 3 mL of LB medium supplied with 50 mg L−1 rifampicin, 50 mg L−1 gentamycin, and 50 mg L−1 kanamycin. Cultures were pelleted, washed, and resuspended in 10 mM MgCl2 containing 200 μM acetosyringone to a final OD600 of 0.6 for each construct, and then incubated for an additional 2 h at room temperature before infiltration into N. benthamiana leaves. Agrobacterium containing a plasmid expressing mCherry-labeled peroxisomal marker obtained from ABRC (ABRC stock: CD3-984) was co-infiltrated with EYFP fusion constructs. Seventy-two hours after infiltration, the fluorescent signals in leaves were acquired using a Zeiss LSM-880 laser-scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss, Thornwood, NY, USA). The excitation wavelength and emission bandwidth for each fluorescent protein, as well as for chlorophyll autofluorescence were optimized using the default settings in the ZEN 2.6 software (Zeiss) and were as follows: EYFP (excitation 514 nm, emission 519–583 nm), mCherry (excitation 561 nm, emission 580–651 nm), chlorophyll autofluorescence (excitation 633 nm, emission 652–721 nm).

Transient overexpression of PhBBO and PhBSE in petunia petals

To identify homologs of BBO and BSE in petunia, amino acid sequences of published rice enzymes involved in SA biosynthesis, OsBBH (Os09g0441400) and OsBSE (Os05g0410200)26, were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) and searched against the P. axillaris protein database. The coding regions of BLAST hits showing the highest sequence identities to OsBBH and OsBSE, Peaxi162Scf00599g00514.1 and Peaxi162Scf01312g00048.1, respectively, were amplified using gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table 3). The amplicons were cloned into the binary vector pCNHP and subsequently transformed into competent A. tumefaciens GV3101 cells. The preparation of Agrobacterium cells for infiltration was performed as described above and the suspension was infiltrated into petals of day 1 post-anthesis petunia flowers using a needleless syringe, after creating a tiny hole near the major vein of each lobe using a 26-gauge needle. Floral volatiles were collected two days after infiltration as described above.

Pseudomonas infection and SA analysis

The bacterial strain Pseudomonas syringae pv. maculicola (Psm) was cultured in King’s B liquid medium overnight at 28 °C. The following day, the bacteria were pelleted, washed once with 10 mM MgCl2, and resuspended in the same solution to a final OD600 of 0.02. Young petunia branches, including stem and leaves without any open flowers, were detached at 12:00 h, submerged in the Psm suspension, and subjected to a vacuum of 65 mm Hg for 3 min. The tissues were then blotted to remove excess liquid and placed in 3% sucrose solution and maintained under a day/night cycle until sample collection at the indicated time points. The collected plant tissues were ground in liquid nitrogen with mortars and pestles into fine powder. For SA and SAG extraction, a 100 mg aliquot of tissue was vortexed at 4 °C for 2 h in 500 µL 70% (v/v) methanol in water containing 400 ng/mL p-Fluoro-DL-phenylalanine as an internal standard. The mixture was then centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 20 min, and a 5 µL aliquot of the supernatant was analyzed by LC-MS/MS. Targeted analysis of SA and SAG was carried out using an HPLC-20 AD system from Shimadzu (Columbia, MD) coupled to a SCIEX QTRAP 5500 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer from AB Sciex (Redwood City, CA) equipped with an electrospray ionization source operating in negative mode, using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) for feature detection. Chromatographic separation was performed using a ZORBAX Eclipse XDB-C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 5 μm particle size; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) with mobile phases of 0.1% (v/v) acetic acid in water (solvent A) and 98% (v/v) acetonitrile in water (solvent B). Separation was achieved at a flow rate of 0.4 mL min−1 with the following gradient: 5–50% B (0–10 min), 50–100% B (10–12 min), 100% B (12–15 min), 100–5% B (15–17 min), and 5% B (17–25 min). Source parameters were set as follows: curtain gas, 25 psi; collision gas, low; ion spray voltage, −4.5 kV; temperature, 650 °C; ion source gas 1, 60 psi; ion source gas 2, 40 psi. The MRM transitions were monitored with a dwell time of 100 msec each: for SA, m/z 137.0 → 73.0; for SAG, m/z 299.0 → 137.0; and for p-Fluoro-DL-phenylalanine m/z 182.1 → 165.1. The final optimized settings for each compound were as follows: SA—declustering potential (DP) −50 V, entrance potential (EP) −11 V, CE −24 V, cell exit potential (CXP) −13 V; SAG–DP −60 V, EP −10 V, CE −16 V, CXP −10 V; p-Fluoro-DL-phenylalanine—DP −55 V, EP −13 V, CE −19 V, CXP −10 V. An authentic SA standard was used to generate an external calibration curve and to optimize detection in plant extracts. Data were processed using Analyst v.1.6.3 (AB Sciex).

Phylogenetic analysis

Amino acid sequences of PhBRs and PhBPBT homologs were obtained using the BLAST function of the NCBI website (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). The sequences were aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm in the MEGA12 package78. Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic trees were constructed using MEGA12 with suitable models for each tree inferred by “Find Best DNA/Protein Models (ML)” algorithm. Trees were graphed using TreeViewer v2.2.079.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. All experiments were performed with the sample sizes of at least three independent biological replicates. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized except for scent collection and SA analysis. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
