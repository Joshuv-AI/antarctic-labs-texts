High-potency MyoAAV capsids enhanced skeletal muscle correction in a mouse model of GSD IIIa
Authors: Kuo-An Liao, Sang-oh Han, Mercedes Barzi, Haiqing Yi, William Eisner, Beatrice Bissig-Choisat, Karl-Dimiter Bissig, Baodong Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Glycogen storage disease type IIIa (GSD IIIa) affects multiple tissues, including liver, heart, and skeletal muscles. We recently reported that an adeno-associated virus serotype 9 vector expressing pullulanase, a bacterial glycogen debranching enzyme, driven by an immunotolerizing dual promoter (AAV9-Dual-Pull), effectively decreased pullulanase-induced cytotoxic T lymphocyte response and corrected disease abnormalities in all major affected tissues in GSD IIIa mice. To reduce effective vector dosages for transgene delivery to skeletal muscles, we packaged the AAV-Dual-Pull vector into two muscle-tropic MyoAAV capsids, MyoAAV4A and MyoAAV4E. Six weeks after administration of the same dose vector (1 × 1013 vg/kg), both the MyoAAV vectors demonstrated remarkably greater transduction efficiency and glycogen clearance efficacy in the cardiac and skeletal muscles than the AAV9 vector, accompanied by the improvement of muscle function, reversal of liver abnormalities, and normalization of the disease biomarker, Glc4 in the urine. Furthermore, treatment with the MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull vector at a 10-fold lower dose (1×1012 vg/kg) achieved significantly better therapeutic outcomes in the skeletal muscles than the AAV9-Dual-Pull vector at a high dose (1×1013 vg/kg). Validation in human liver chimeric mice revealed that the MyoAAV vectors and the AAV9 vectors had a similar efficiency in transducing human hepatocytes, indicating increased translatability for clinical applications.

Introduction

Glycogen storage disease type III (GSD III) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the AGL gene, leading to the deficiency of glycogen debranching enzyme (GDE), a key enzyme responsible for glycogen degradation via its two independent catalytic activities, 4-α-glucanotransferase activity (EC 2.4.1.25) and amylo-α-1,6-glucosidase activity (EC 3.2.1.33).1,2 The disease has an estimated prevalence of 1:100,000. Genetic loss of GDE results in incomplete glycogen breakdown and accumulation of abnormally structured glycogen (limit dextrin) in multiple tissues, such as the liver, heart, and skeletal muscle. Two major GSD III subtypes are categorized based on the extent of tissue involvement: individuals affected by the most common subtype GSD IIIa (∼85%) display both liver and muscle diseases and those with GSD IIIb (∼15%) have only liver involvement. The other two subtypes, GSD IIIc (selective loss of glucosidase activity) and IIId (selective loss of transferase activity), are extremely rare.3 During infancy and early childhood, the clinical manifestations of GSD III mainly appear in the liver, including hepatomegaly, hypoglycemia, hyperlipidemia, and elevated hepatic enzymes. These liver symptoms usually turn milder with age, whereas progressive liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinomas can occur later in life.3,4,5 Muscle weakness manifests during adolescence and adulthood and becomes more severe as patients age. The muscle involvement is variable as most individuals present both myopathy and cardiomyopathy, whereas others exhibit only progressive skeletal myopathy without apparent heart involvement.6,7,8

Current treatments for GSD III are limited to dietary-based symptomatic management approaches, such as uncooked starch and high-protein diets.9,10,11,12 There is an unmet medical need for GSD III especially for the improvement of skeletal muscle function. Because GSD III is a monogenic inherited disorder, adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated gene therapy would be a feasible treatment for this disease. However, AAV gene therapy with human GDE is not applicable for GSD III due to the large size of the human GDE cDNA (4.6 kb) and the limitation of AAV packaging size (4.7 kb). To overcome this challenge, we previously developed a novel gene therapy approach using an AAV vector expressing a smaller bacterial GDE, pullulanase (EC 3.2.1.41) with 2.2 kb in cDNA size, allowing transitory liver and muscle corrections.13 To prevent bacterial-enzyme-induced cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses, we further developed an innovative dual promoter, which consists of an immunotolerizing liver-specific promoter (LSP) and the ubiquitous CMV enhancer/chicken β-actin (CB) promoter.14 We demonstrated that intravenous injection of an AAV vector (AAV-LSP-CB-Pull) expressing pullulanase driven by the dual promoter in adult GSD IIIa mice significantly decreased pullulanase-specific CTL responses, resulting in persistent expression of pullulanase and reduction of glycogen accumulation in all major effected tissues, including liver, heart, and skeletal muscles.14,15

AAV9 vector displays multi-organ tropisms and is a preferred capsid for systemic delivery of therapeutic transgenes to liver, muscle, and the central nervous system.16,17,18 To date, AAV9-based gene therapy has been tested in numerous clinical trials for inherited neuromuscular diseases with positive outcomes, but safety concerns have emerged after the reports of several patient deaths related to the high-dose AAV administration.19,20 Muscle comprises approximately 40% total body mass; sufficient transduction of natural AAV9 in muscle requires high dosing regimens, which can cause adverse events such as hepatotoxicity.21,22,23 This raises an urgent need for novel AAV capsids with enhanced efficiency and/or specificity toward skeletal muscles. Recently, Tabebordbar and colleagues developed a class of RGD-motif-containing MyoAAV capsids that transduce muscle with superior efficiency and selectivity over the parental AAV9 capsid in mice and nonhuman primates.24 In an effort to optimize the AAV-Dual-Pull gene therapy approach for GSD III, we here employed two lead MyoAAV capsids (MyoAAV4A and MyoAAV4E) and compared them with AAV9 for transduction efficiency and therapeutic efficacy in GSD IIIa mice.

Results

MyoAAV4A and MyoAAV4E capsids displayed significantly higher AAV transduction and pullulanase expression than AAV9 in skeletal muscles of GSD IIIa mice

To evaluate the in vivo transduction efficiency of MyoAAV4A and MyoAAV4E capsids, an AAV vector plasmid containing a codon-optimized CpG-free pullulanase cDNA under the control of the LSP-CB dual promoter was packaged into MyoAAV4A (MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull), MyoAAV4E (MyoAAV4E-Dual-Pull), and AAV9 (AAV9-Dual-Pull), respectively. Twelve-week-old GSD IIIa mice were intravenously injected with one of the AAV vectors at a dose of 1×1013 vector genomes (vg)/kg, and major affected tissues, including liver, heart, and skeletal muscles, were harvested 6 weeks later.

In the liver, high copy numbers of viral genomes were detected in all three AAV-treated groups (Figure 1A; Table S1). In heart and skeletal muscles, AAV genome copy numbers were significantly lower in the AAV9-treated mice than those in the MyoAAV4A-treated animals (Figure 1A; Table S1). Interestingly, MyoAAV4A treatment resulted in higher AAV genome copy numbers than MyoAAV4E in heart (4A: 1.59 ± 0.46 vs. 4E: 1.17 ± 0.15), diaphragm (4A: 1.43 ± 0.88 vs. 4E: 0.81 ± 0.19), quadriceps (4A: 0.99 ± 0.39 vs. 4E: 0.44 ± 0.43), and gastrocnemius (4A: 0.80 ± 0.51 vs. 4E: 0.35 ± 0.30), suggesting that MyoAAV4A is more efficient than MyoAAV4E in transducing muscles. Consistent with the AAV genome copy results, all three AAV treatments led to remarkably increased pullulanase expression and activity in the liver, in comparison with the untreated (UT) and wild-type (WT) groups; both MyoAAV4A- and MyoAAV4E-injected mice displayed remarkably higher pullulanase expression levels and activities than the AAV9-treated mice in the heart and skeletal muscles (Figures 1B and S1), and MyoAAV4A appeared to be slightly more effective than MyoAAV4E (Table S1). MyoAAV4A and MyoAAV4E capsids led to greater glycogen reduction than AAV9 in muscles of GSD IIIa mice

Pathological accumulation of glycogen in liver and muscle is the major disease phenotype in patients with GSD III and animal disease models.25,26,27 To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of our MyoAAV-Dual-Pull vectors, glycogen levels were measured in liver, heart, and skeletal muscles of MyoAAV-treated mice. Consistent with the pullulanase activity results, all three AAV treatments reduced glycogen content in the liver to near WT level 6 weeks after AAV injection. In heart and skeletal muscles, glycogen levels were decreased by 85%–95% in the MyoAAV4A- and MyoAAV4E-treated mice when compared with UT; in contrast, AAV9 treatment reduced glycogen content by 54%–57% in the heart and the diaphragm and by 22–25% in quadriceps and gastrocnemius muscles (Figure 2A; Table S1). To validate these biochemical assay results, periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining was performed to visualize glycogen in these tissues. As shown in Figure 2B, all three AAV treatments almost completely cleared glycogen accumulation in the liver. Both MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull and MyoAAV4E-Dual-Pull treatments showed profound glycogen clearance in heart and skeletal muscles, while AAV9 treatment showed inferior efficacy in these tissues. MyoAAV4A and MyoAAV4E vectors showed greater efficacy than the AAV9 vector in rectifying liver and muscle abnormalities and improving muscle function

To evaluate the therapeutic effect of MyoAAV-Dual-Pull treatment on liver and muscle functions, plasma activities of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and creatine kinase (CK) were measured. Six weeks after AAV injection, all three AAV treatments greatly reduced the plasma ALT and AST activities to levels comparable to those observed in WT control mice (Figures 3A and 3B; Table S1). Intriguingly, only MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull treatment significantly decreased the plasma CK level (−83%) when compared with UT (Figure 3C; Table S1). Glucose tetrasaccharide (Glc4) is a degradation product of glycogen, and elevated levels of Glc4 in urine have been associated with glycogen storage diseases including GSD II and GSD III.28,29 In this experiment, both MyoAAV vectors remarkably reduced urinary Glc4, in comparison with UT and AAV9-treated mice. MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull treatment reversed the Glc4 concentration to the WT level (Figure 3D; Table S1). Muscle functional tests revealed that all three AAV treatments significantly improved the grip strength (Figure 3E, Table S1). In the wire hang test, significant improvement was observed in both MyoAAV-treated groups in comparison with the UT and AAV9-treated groups (Figure 3F; Table S1). Taken together, these results demonstrate that both MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull and MyoAAV4E-Dual-Pull treatments are superior to AAV9-Dual-Pull in correcting disease symptoms in muscle of GSD IIIa mice, while MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull shows in general better therapeutic outcomes than MyoAAV4E-Dual-Pull.

MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull vector corrected biochemical abnormalities in tissues of GSD IIIa mice at lower doses

To determine the minimum effective dose of MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull, we next evaluated its potency and efficacy at lower doses. To this end, 12-week-old GSD IIIa mice were intravenously injected with MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull vector at doses of 1×1012 (low dose) and 3×1012 vg/kg (medium dose). At 6 weeks post-injection, muscle functional tests were performed, and major affected tissues including liver, heart, diaphragm, quadriceps, and gastrocnemius were collected.

We first assessed the abundance of vector genomes in MyoAAV4A-transduced tissues. At low dose, AAV genome copy numbers were low in liver (1.44 ± 0.81 vg/diploid genome) and heart (0.49 ± 0.11) and scarcely detectable in diaphragm, quadriceps, and gastrocnemius (0.03–0.06); at medium dose, AAV genome copy numbers were much higher in the liver (10.87 ± 2.89), moderate in the heart (2.08 ± 0.33), and undoubtedly detected (0.11–0.26) in skeletal muscles (diaphragm, quadriceps, and gastrocnemius) (Figure S1; Table S1).

We next measured the pullulanase activities and glycogen contents in these tissues. Although our MyoAAV-Dual-Pull vectors exhibit liver- and muscle-tropism in GSD IIIa mice, in vivo validation of these vectors in primary human cells is important for clinical translation. Discussion

Current AAV serotypes, particularly AAV9, transduce both liver and muscle; however, high dosing regimens of AAV9 required to transduce skeletal muscles can lead to unwanted adverse reactions, such as hepatotoxicity that subsequently eliminates the transgene from the liver, or even cause liver failure as observed in nonhuman primates and human patients.32,33 This underscores the need for the development of improved gene therapy with more potent AAV capsids for skeletal muscle diseases.

Progress in developing novel muscle-tropic AAV vectors via capsid engineering has been made in the past decade and most recently led to identification of two muscle-tropic AAV capsids, known as AAVMYO34 and MyoAAVs.24 These capsids were generated by inserting an RGD-motif to the surface of AAV9 capsid, which allows the vectors to interact with specific integrin molecules abundantly expressed in skeletal muscles and preferentially transduce muscle tissues while not affecting the transduction efficiency of AAV9 in liver. More importantly, the two lead MyoAAV capsids, MyoAAV4A and MyoAAV4E, have been shown to preserve its superior efficiency and high potency for skeletal muscle transduction in both mice and nonhuman primates following systemic administration,24 suggesting that they may be translatable to human as well. However, as far as we know, these capsids have never been tested in primary human cells.

Our laboratory has recently developed an innovative gene therapy for GSD III using an AAV9 vector expressing a bacterial pullulanase driven by an immunotolerizing dual promoter (AAV9-Dual-Pull).14 We demonstrated that intravenous injection of the AAV9-Dual-Pull vector into adult GSD IIIa mice effectively prevented pullulanase-induced CTL response and corrected disease symptoms in both liver and muscle.14,15 We postulated that packaging of the AAV9-Dual-Pull vector into the MyoAAV capsids would allow us to reduce the effective dose and ease the hepatotoxicity and immunotoxicity for gene therapy for GSD III. We first compared the transduction efficiency and therapeutic efficacy between MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull and MyoAAV4E-Dual-Pull using AAV9-Dual-Pull as control. Following systemic injection at the same dose (1 × 1013 vg/kg), both the MyoAAV-Dual-Pull vectors showed superior muscle transduction efficiency compared with their parental AAV9 vector. Importantly, the MyoAAV genomes were also detected in the liver, indicating that the MyoAAV capsids preferentially target muscle tissues but also transduce other tissues, especially those expressing high level of integrin αvβ6.24 These data suggest that MyoAAV capsids are ideal delivery vectors for the development of gene replacement therapy for GSD III, which involves both liver and muscle. This notion was further validated by our biochemical and histological data showing decreased glycogen contents in liver and muscle as well as corrections of liver and muscle phenotypes by MyoAAV-Dual-Pull treatment in GSD IIIa mice.

When we compared MyoAAV4A with MyoAAV4E vectors, there was no significant difference in most of the parameters but MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull seemed to have in general better therapeutic outcomes than MyoAAV4E-Dual-Pull. Although MyoAAV4E-Dual-Pull vector resulted in slightly lower transduction and weaker pullulanase activity in liver and muscle (Figure 1; Table S1), it significantly reduced the glycogen accumulation in liver and muscle to the similar levels observed in mice treated with MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull (Figure 2). In addition, both MyoAAV treatments effectively corrected GSD IIIa symptoms and restored muscle strength, suggesting that MyoAAV4A- and MyoAAV4E-Dual-Pull vectors have indistinguishable therapeutic efficacy at a dose of 1×1013 vg/kg for 6 weeks (Figure 3). It is interesting to note that both grip strength measurement and treadmill performance showed similar level of improvement between MyoAAV4A- and MyoAAV4E-Dual-Pull-treated mice (Figures 3E and 3G), whereas we observed that MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull treatment substantially increased the time of latency to fall in the wire hang test, but MyoAAV4E-Dual-Pull treatment had only small effect (Figure 3F). This difference may be due to more efficient transduction and correction of forelimb muscles by MyoAAV4A, which are more heavily involved in the wire hang test. Unfortunately, we did not collect and analyze forelimb muscles in this study.

Given hepatotoxicity is the most common adverse event following AAV administration, particularly in the settings of genetic myopathies that require high-dose regimens, we next sought to determine the minimum effective dose of MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull in GSD III. Our data demonstrated that MyoAAV4A-Dual-AAV treatment at low dose (1×1012 vg/kg) achieved much better therapeutic outcomes than AAV9-Dual-Pull at high dose (1×1013 vg/kg). We anticipate that the lower dosage requirement for effective corrections of liver and muscle manifestations in GSD III with this evolved myotropic AAV variant could provide a solution to reduce the high-dose vector-related hepatotoxicity, genotoxicity, and immunogenicity, which have long been recognized as major barriers in gene therapy. We did not observe any toxicities related to overexpression of pullulanase driven by the dual promoter in any treatment groups of mice in this study. However, in a previous experiment, treatment of GSD IIIa mice with a high dose (5 × 1013 vg/kg) of AAV9-MHCK7-Pull resulted in cardiac toxicity, which appeared to be associated with the extremely high level of pullulanase expression in the heart driven by the strong muscle-specific MHCK7 promoter (unpublished data). These findings highlight the importance of carefully regulating pullulanase expression by selecting appropriate promoters and AAV capsids to ensure both efficacy and safety in the development of this gene therapy strategy.

Preclinical evaluation of AAV vectors in animal models may not be translatable to humans. As GSD III patients present with both liver and muscle pathology, it is important to validate our gene therapy approach in primary human hepatocytes and myocytes. In this study, we took advantage of a well-established humanized mouse model to investigate whether MyoAAV vectors can transduce human hepatocytes repopulated in a chimeric mouse liver and compare the results with AAV9 vector, which has been used in numerous gene therapy clinical trials. Our histological data revealed the presence of the pullulanase-expressing human hepatocytes in the liver of all AAV-injected chimeric mice, indicating both MyoAAV4A and MyoAAV4E vectors successfully transduced human cells in vivo. Recently, a human skeletal muscle cell model of GSD III was developed using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing of human-induced pluripotent stem cells and was employed to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy and safety of a mini-GDE approach in a human cellular context.35,36 This cellular model provides a valuable platform for in vitro validation of MyoAAV-Dual-Pull vectors, enabling assessment of their transduction efficiency and therapeutic potential in human muscle cells.

One limitation of this proof-of-concept study is that treatment was evaluated only in male mice, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to female animals. To address this, we have recently completed two long-term studies to access the efficacy of MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull in both young adult and aged GSD IIIa mice. These studies included treatment cohorts of both male and female mice, and the results are currently being prepared for publication (manuscript in preparation).

In conclusion, we demonstrate that the use of LSP-CB dual promoter combined with myotropic AAV capsids effectively enhances the skeletal muscle correction and reduces the effective doses, suggesting that this improved strategy may represent a promising solution for a safe and efficient gene replacement therapy for GSD III in human. Our future studies will investigate the therapeutic efficacy of MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull in old GSD IIIa mice and GSD IIIa dogs.

Materials and methods

Sex as a biological variable

Our study exclusively examined AAV gene therapy in male mice; therefore, sex was not considered as a biological variable in this proof-of-concept study. We are testing the long-term efficacy of the MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull vector in both male and female GSD IIIa mice in our on-going experiment.

AAV vector construct and AAV viral packaging

To construct the new pAAV-Dual-Pull AAV vector plasmid, a 2.2-kb CpG-free pullulanase cDNA fragment linked with a hemagglutinin (HA)-tag sequence (5′-TACCCATACGATGTTCCAGATTACGCT-3′) at the 3′ end was synthesized at GenScript and cloned into the original pAAV-LSP-CB-Pull vector plasmid,14 to replace the CpG-rich pullulanase cDNA. Packaging plasmids for the top two MyoAAV capsids, MyoAAV4A and MyoAAV4E, were generated based on the published sequences of the RGD-containing peptides.24 The new pAAV-Dual-Pull vector was then packaged into MyoAAV4A (MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull), MyoAAV4E (MyoAAV4E-Dual-Pull), and AAV9 (AAV9-Dual-Pull), respectively, in HEK293 cells using calcium phosphate transfection method. Harvested cells were spun down at 3,000 rpm for 10 min, pellets were resuspended in AAV lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-Cl pH 8.5, 150 mM NaCl) and went through three cycles of freeze-thaw, followed by clearance by centrifugation. The AAV vector was purified using iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation method as previously described.37 Titers of AAV vector stocks were determined by quantitative real-time PCR using primers targeting the polyA tail.

Animals and viral vector administration

Animal care and experiment were conducted by following Duke University Institutional Animal care and Use Committee approved guidelines. The Agl knock-out mice (GSD IIIa mice) were generated by deleting exons 6–10 in the Agl gene as previously described.13 Twelve-week-old GSD IIIa male mice were divided into six groups: six mice each for MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull (1×1013 vg/kg), MyoAAV4E-Dual-Pull (1×1013 vg/kg), and AAV9-Dual-Pull (1×1013 vg/kg) groups; five mice for MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull (3×1012 vg/kg), six mice for MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull (1×1012 vg/kg), and five mice for untreated controls. In addition, five age-matched WT male mice were used for reference. Vectors were administrated intravenously through retro-orbital injection. After 6 weeks of treatment, GSD IIIa mice were sacrificed to collect tissues and blood following overnight fasting. Collected tissues were immediately frozen with dry ice and stored at −80°C or fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin.

For the humanized mouse model experiment, isolated human hepatocytes were transplanted into TIRF (Transgene-free, Il2rg−/−, Rag2−/−, Fah−/−) mice as previously described.30,31,38 In brief, 1×106 human hepatocytes (source: cadaveric cryopreserved hepatocytes from the same donor) in a volume of 50 μL were injected into the spleen of 2-month-old TIRF mice. Immediately after transplantation, selection pressure toward transplanted human hepatocytes was applied by withdrawing nitisinone from the drinking water. After 2 weeks, mice were again put on nitisinone for 3 days before a second withdrawal. Cycling was repeated until mice have >60% human liver chimerism, as assessed by the levels of human albumin in plasma (measured by ELISA; Bethyl Laboratories, Inc., Montgomery, TX, USA). Six humanized TIRF mice were injected into the tail vein with MyoAAV4A-Dual-Pull (n = 2), MyoAAV4E-Dual-Pull (n = 2), and AAV9-Dual-Pull (n = 2) at the same dose, 1.0 × 1013 vg/kg. One month after the treatment, livers were harvested and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for paraffin embedding.

AAV vector biodistribution analysis

AAV genome copy number was determined by quantitative PCR. Genomic DNA was extracted from frozen tissues using Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega). PCR was performed using SYBR Green (Roche) and following gene-specific primer pairs: forward 5′-GCCACTGGATGCCTACAACT-3′ and reverse 5′-CGTGCTGGTGCAGTGTATTG-3′ for pullulanase and forward 5′-AGAGGGAAATCGTGCGTGAC-3′ and reverse 5′-CAATAGTGATGACCTGGCCGT-3′ for β-actin. The pAAV-Dual-Pull plasmid DNA was used to create a standard curve for calculating the viral vector genome copy numbers.

Western blot analysis

Tissues were homogenized on ice in RIPA buffer containing protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Cell Signaling Technology), followed by centrifugation at 18,000 × g at 4°C for 15 min. The protein concentration was measured using bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Equal amounts of protein samples were separated in NuPage 4–12% Bis-Tris polyacrylamide gels (Invitrogen) and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked in phosphate-buffered saline with Tween-20 (PBST) containing 3% BSA and incubated overnight at 4°C with antibodies against HA-tag (rabbit, Cell Signaling Technology, 3724), β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich, A3854), and GADPH (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA5-15738-HRP). After incubation, the membranes were washed three times with PBST and incubated with horseradish peroxide (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, A0545) for 1 h at room temperature. After washes, blots were visualized by ChemiDoc Image System (Bio-Rad) using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Pullulanase activity assay

Expressed bacterial enzyme was measured using the Pullulanase Assay Kit (Megazyme, K-PullG6) following manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, frozen tissues were homogenized using tissue homogenizer (Caframo, RZR1) in deionized water on ice followed by 15 s sonication for three times (Qsonica Sonicator, Q500). After centrifugation at 18,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C, the supernatants were incubated with the substrate (4,6-O-benzylidene-4 nitrophenyl-63-α-D-maltotriosyl-maltotriose) for 10 min at 40°C. The reaction was terminated by adding the stop buffer (2% [w/v] Tris-Cl, pH 9.0), and the pullulanase activity was measured by reading the absorbance at 405 nm using a 96-well plate reader (PerkinElmer, Victor X3). The pullulanase enzyme standard provided in the kit was used to calculate the activity of pullulanase, and the data were normalized by the protein concentration of the homogenates measured by BCA assay.

Glycogen content assay

Glycogen content in tissues was measured indirectly as the glucose released from glycogen after total digestion with Aspergillus Niger amyloglucosidase (Sigma-Aldrich). Briefly, the tissue homogenates were boiled for 3 min and immediately cooled on ice for 10 min. The samples were then incubated with amyloglucosidase (final concentration 150 μg/mL in 25 mM potassium acetate pH 5.5) for 90 min at 37°C, and the reaction was stopped by boiling for 3 min. The glucose released from each reaction was determined by incubating the reaction mixtures with Pointe Scientific Glucose (Hexokinase) Liquid Reagents (Fisher Scientific) for 10 min at room temperature followed by measuring the resulting absorbance at 340 nm using a UV-VIS Spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1700, PharmaSpec). A glucose standard series was used for calculating glucose content in digested homogenates.

Histology

After primary immersion fixation, tissues were post-fixed with 1% periodic acid in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 48 h at 4°C. The samples were then washed with PBS, dehydrated with ascending grades of ethyl alcohol, cleared in xylene for 2 h, and infiltrated with paraffin. Sections of the paraffin-embedded tissue samples were stained with Schiff’s reagent (Sigma-Aldrich) as described previously.13,14 Briefly, slides were oxidized with 0.5% periodic acid for 5 min and rinsed with distilled water for 1 min followed by staining with Schiff’s reagent for 15 min. After 10 min of wash in distilled water, the slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, washed with tap water, incubated with bluing reagent for 1 min, and dehydrated with ethyl alcohol and xylene. Finally, all slides were mounted with Chemical Permount Mounting Medium (Fisher Scientific). The image was obtained with BZ-710 microscope (Keyence America).

Immunostaining of chimeric livers

Co-staining of HA-tagged pullulanase and hLDH was performed as described preciously.31 Paraffin-embedded liver samples were sectioned (5 μm thick), dewaxed, and rehydrated according to the standard processing procedure. The slides were then incubated in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 30 min for antigen retrieval followed by treating with hydrogen peroxide. After blocking with 2.5% horse serum, samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with rabbit anti-HA antibody (1:100 diluted, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-805) together with mouse anti-hLDH antibody (1:100 diluted, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-133123). After washing with phosphate-buffered saline buffer, samples were developed using ImmPRESS Duet Double Staining Kit (Vector Labs, # MP-7714-15) following manufacturer’s instructions. The image was obtained with BZ-710 microscope (Keyence America).

Plasma enzyme activity tests

The activities of plasma ALT, AST, and CK were measured using the Liquid ALT (SGPT), Liquid AST (SGOT), and Liquid Creatine Kinase Reagent Kit (Pointe Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, diluted plasma (1:5 with 0.9% [w/v] of NaCl) was incubated with the working reagent at 37°C, and the absorbance at 340 nm was recorded using a UV-VIS Spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1700) every minute for 5 min.

Measurement of Glc4 levels in urine

The concentration of urinary Glc4 was measured by stable isotope-dilution electrospray tandem mass spectrometry as previously described.39

Muscle function tests

Grip strength was measured using a grip strength meter (Bioseb). Three independent measurements of four limbs’ strength were recorded in each trial, and two trials were performed for each mouse. Maximum values of grip strength were presented.

Wire hang test was performed as described previously.13 A wire mesh was positioned 30 cm above a soft bedding and carefully inverted. The time mouse fell from the mesh wire or the maximum time (5 min) was recorded. Three independent measurements were recorded, and the maximum hanging time was used for comparison.

The treadmill exhaustion test was performed as previously reported13,14 using a treadmill (LE8709, Panlab Harvard Apparatus). Mice warmed up by running at a lowest speed, 5 cm/s, and 25 degrees of slope for 3 min. Then, mice were allowed to run at 8 cm/s for 3 min. The speed was increased by 4 cm/s every 3 min until mice were exhausted or the maximal speed (32 cm/s) was reached.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was used; for comparisons between two groups, unpaired Student’s t tests were applied. For normally distributed data, either an ordinary one-way ANOVA (for equal variances) with Tukey’s post-hoc test or Welch’s ANOVA (for unequal variances) with Dunnett’s T3 post-hoc test was used. For non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc test was used. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
