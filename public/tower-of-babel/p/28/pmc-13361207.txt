Defect-engineered N-CeO2/N-rGO heterostructures for visible-light photocatalytic degradation of 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid: the synergistic roles of ceria vacancies and N-doped graphene interfaces
Authors: Nguyen Hoang Hao, Nguyen Thi Thu Ha, Nguyen Ngoc Ha, Bui Cong Trinh, Nguyen Thi Nga, Le Minh Cam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Defect-engineered reduced graphene oxide heterostructures have attracted increasing attention for improving visible-light photocatalytic performance. In this study, nitrogen-doped cerium oxide supported on nitrogen-doped reduced graphene oxide (N-CeO2/N-rGO) was synthesized as an efficient photocatalyst for the degradation of 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D) under visible-light irradiation. Structural and spectroscopic analyses revealed that nitrogen incorporation into the CeO2 lattice increased the Ce3+ fraction and promoted the formation of oxygen vacancies, while nitrogen doping in the graphene framework generated additional defect sites and enhanced the electrical conductivity of the carbon matrix. The resulting N-CeO2/N-rGO composite formed a well-defined heterostructure in which defect-rich ceria nanoparticles were uniformly anchored on conductive graphene sheets. The hybrid catalyst exhibited enhanced visible-light absorption, a high specific surface area, and improved adsorption capacity compared with the individual components. Photocatalytic experiments demonstrated that N-CeO2/N-rGO achieved approximately 90% degradation of 2,4-D after 240 min of visible-light irradiation. Under identical experimental conditions kinetic analysis showed that the apparent rate constant (0.0105 × 10−2 min−1) was significantly higher than those of pristine CeO2 and other photocatalysts evaluated in this study. The enhanced photocatalytic activity can be attributed to the synergistic interaction between oxygen-vacancy-rich ceria and the conductive N-doped graphene interface, which promotes charge separation and accelerates interfacial electron transfer. These findings highlight an effective strategy for designing defect-engineered oxide–carbon heterostructures for the removal of persistent organic pollutants in water treatment.

Introduction

The increasing discharge of persistent organic pollutants (POPs) into water bodies has become a serious environmental concern because of their toxicity, stability, and resistance to conventional wastewater treatment processes.1 Among these pollutants, 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D) is a widely used herbicide that is frequently detected in surface and groundwater due to its high mobility and persistence. Its persistence and potential ecological risks necessitate the development of effective remediation technologies. In this context, heterogeneous photocatalysis has emerged as a promising approach for the degradation of refractory organic contaminants because of its environmental friendliness, cost effectiveness, and ability to mineralize pollutants under light irradiation with minimal secondary pollution.2,3

Cerium dioxide (CeO2) is a widely studied photocatalyst owing to its unique redox properties, high oxygen storage capacity, and intrinsic ability to generate oxygen vacancies that facilitate charge separation.4–7 However, pristine CeO2 typically exhibits limited visible-light activity and rapid recombination of photogenerated electron–hole pairs, which restrict its photocatalytic efficiency. Coupling CeO2 with reduced graphene oxide (rGO) is an effective strategy to overcome these limitations, as the high specific surface area and excellent electrical conductivity of rGO can promote the dispersion of CeO2 nanoparticles, accelerate charge transport, improve pollutant adsorption, and suppress recombination losses. Accordingly, CeO2-rGO composites have shown enhanced photocatalytic degradation performance compared with bare CeO2.8–13

In addition to physical coupling, nitrogen doping has been widely explored to tailor the electronic structure and surface reactivity of both oxide and carbon components. When introduced into CeO2, nitrogen alters the band gap and increases the concentration of oxygen vacancies, thereby enhancing catalytic activity.14,15 Nitrogen-doped reduced graphene oxide (N-rGO) is also a promising support material because nitrogen incorporation modifies the electronic structure and surface chemistry of rGO, leading to improved electrical conductivity, increased active sites, and stronger interfacial interaction with metal oxides.16–19 As a result, CeO2-N-rGO composites often exhibit better catalytic performance than pure CeO2 or nitrogen-free CeO2-rGO systems.

Recent studies have shown that nitrogen doping can improve the performance of CeO2-rGO-based materials by enhancing charge separation, accelerating electron transfer, increasing oxygen vacancies and Ce3+ concentration, and promoting the generation of reactive species under light irradiation.20–22 It has also been reported that nitrogen decoration in CeO2/rGO systems facilitates charge transfer through synergistic interaction between nitrogen and CeO2 on the graphene support,23 while defect-rich nitrogen-doped graphene can further enhance catalytic activity through the cooperative effects of nitrogen dopants and vacancy defects.24,25 Hybrid architectures in which nitrogen is simultaneously introduced into both the CeO2 lattice and the graphene framework have therefore attracted increasing interest.26

Despite these advances, most previous studies have mainly focused on systems involving undoped CeO2 combined with rGO or N-rGO. The simultaneous effects of nitrogen incorporation into both the CeO2 lattice and the graphene matrix on interfacial structure, Ce oxidation state, and oxygen defect density have not been systematically elucidated. Moreover, relatively few studies have addressed the photocatalytic degradation of herbicides such as 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid using nitrogen-doped CeO2 supported on N-rGO composites. Consequently, the synergistic roles of nitrogen doping in both phases in regulating charge transfer and photocatalytic activity remain poorly understood.

In this study, nitrogen-doped CeO2 nanoparticles supported on nitrogen-doped reduced graphene oxide (N-CeO2/N-rGO) were developed as photocatalysts for the visible-light degradation of 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D). By introducing nitrogen into both the CeO2 lattice and the graphene framework, the work aims to construct a defect-engineered heterostructure in which oxygen-vacancy-rich ceria is coupled with a conductive N-doped carbon matrix. Through systematic structural, surface, and photocatalytic characterization of N-CeO2/N-rGO and related reference materials, this study aims to clarify how nitrogen incorporation in the oxide and carbon phases influences defect formation, heterointerfacial properties, and photocatalytic activity toward the visible-light degradation of 2,4-D.

Experimental

Chemicals

All chemicals were of analytical grade and used as received without further purification. The reagents were purchased from commercial suppliers (Sigma-Aldrich). Deionized water was used in all experimental procedures.

Preparation of photocatalysts

Nitrogen-doped cerium oxide (N-CeO2), nitrogen-doped reduced graphene oxide (N-rGO), and the corresponding composite materials were synthesized to investigate the synergistic roles of nitrogen incorporation in both the oxide and carbon phases. In this work, nitrogen incorporation into CeO2 was achieved via a pyrolysis route using urea as the nitrogen precursor. Samples prepared with different urea/CeO2 feed ratios were denoted as x%U-CeO2, where x represents the nominal urea/CeO2 feed ratio (wt%) used during synthesis rather than the actual nitrogen content incorporated into the final material. The nominal urea/CeO2 feed ratio was varied as a synthesis parameter to regulate nitrogen incorporation and defect formation in the ceria lattice. Based on preliminary screening experiments (see SI, Section S1), the pyrolysis route using urea as the nitrogen precursor was selected as the effective method for nitrogen incorporation into the CeO2 lattice. Furthermore, a series of N-CeO2 samples prepared with different nominal urea/CeO2 feed ratios were screened to identify the most suitable precursor for composite fabrication. According to the structural and photocatalytic screening results (see SI, Fig. S1–S2), the sample prepared using a 25% nominal urea/CeO2 feed ratio exhibited the most favorable visible-light absorption and photocatalytic performance and was therefore selected for further study. It should be noted that this optimization was performed only for the N-CeO2 precursor. The optimal urea/CeO2 feed ratio may differ after coupling with rGO or N-rGO; however, a systematic optimization within the composite systems was beyond the scope of the present study. For simplicity, the selected sample prepared using a 25% nominal urea/CeO2 feed ratio is hereafter referred to as N-CeO2 throughout the the work. The selected N-CeO2 sample was subsequently integrated with rGO and N-rGO to construct the composite photocatalysts N-CeO2/rGO and N-CeO2/N-rGO. These materials, together with reference samples including CeO2, GO, and rGO, were used to investigate the influence of nitrogen doping and graphene-based support on defect formation, interfacial charge transfer, and photocatalytic activity. The composite materials were prepared to couple nitrogen-doped ceria nanoparticles with graphene-based supports for subsequent evaluation of defect structure, interfacial characteristics, and photocatalytic performance.

Detailed synthesis procedures for CeO2, N-CeO2, GO, rGO, N-rGO, and the composite materials are provided in the SI (Section S2).

Characterization

The morphology of the synthesized materials was examined by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, Hitachi S-4800) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) detector for elemental analysis and mapping. The microstructure and lattice features were further investigated using transmission electron microscopy (TEM and HRTEM). The crystal structure of the samples was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15418 nm). Surface chemical composition and bonding configurations were investigated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) using a Nexsa XPS system (ThermoFisher Scientific) with monochromatic Al Kα radiation (hν = 1486.6 eV). The binding energies were calibrated using the sp2 C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. The spectra were analyzed using XPSPEAK41 software, and peak deconvolution was performed using Gaussian functions. Optical absorption properties were evaluated by UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) using a Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrophotometer. Structural disorder and defect density were examined by Raman spectroscopy. Textural properties were determined by N2 adsorption–desorption measurements at 77 K using a TriStar 3000 V6 07 A analyzer.

Photocatalytic activity evaluation

The photocatalytic activities of the synthesized samples were evaluated using 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D) as the model pollutant. In a typical experiment, 0.075 g of photocatalyst was dispersed in 200 mL of 2,4-D aqueous solution with an initial concentration of 40.83 ppm (pH = 6.5). Prior to irradiation, the suspension was magnetically stirred in the dark for 60 min to establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium.

The photocatalytic reaction was conducted in a photoreactor equipped with a 300 W xenon lamp as the light source. The reactor temperature was maintained at 25 °C using a circulating water bath during the reaction.

Results and discussion

Morphology and crystal structure of the catalysts

The morphology and crystal structure of the synthesized catalysts were first investigated to elucidate the structural evolution toward the N-CeO2/N-rGO heterostructure.

FESEM images (Fig. 1) show that pristine CeO2 consists of quasi-spherical nanoparticles forming cauliflower-like agglomerates. After nitrogen incorporation, the overall morphology is largely retained; however, the particles become slightly smaller, less uniform, and more loosely aggregated, suggesting that nitrogen doping influences crystal growth and introduces structural disorder into the ceria phase.

FESEM images of: (A) N-rGO; (B) CeO2; (C) N-CeO2; and (D) N-CeO2/NrGO

In the composite catalyst, morphology changes markedly. In the N-CeO2/N-rGO sample, the oxide nanoparticles are distributed on wrinkled graphene sheets, suggesting that the N-rGO framework acts as a supporting matrix that improves nanoparticle dispersion.

TEM analysis (Fig. 2) further confirms these observations. Pristine CeO2 forms polyhedral nanoparticles with sizes of several tens of nanometers, which is consistent with the crystallite size estimated from XRD. After nitrogen incorporation, the nanoparticles become smaller and more irregular, indicating that nitrogen doping modifies the crystal growth process.

TEM images of (A) N-rGO; (B) CeO2; (C) N-CeO2; (D) N-CeO2/NrGO; and (E) HRTEM of N-CeO2/N-rGO.

In the N-CeO2/N-rGO composite, the oxide nanoparticles are uniformly anchored on thin graphene sheets, forming a well-defined oxide–carbon heterostructure. The HRTEM image reveals lattice fringes of 0.31 nm and 0.27 nm corresponding to the (111) and (200) planes of fluorite CeO2, respectively. These observations provide direct evidence of intimate interfacial contact between ceria nanoparticles and the graphene support, which is expected to be favorable for interfacial charge transfer during photocatalysis.

To further confirm the elemental composition of the synthesized materials, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analysis was performed for pristine CeO2 and N-CeO2 sample synthesized using a nominal urea/CeO2 feed ratio of 25%. The spectra reveal the presence of Ce and O as the dominant elements in the oxide sample, while a weak nitrogen signal is detected in the N-CeO2 catalyst, confirming the successful incorporation of nitrogen into the ceria structure. Quantitative analysis indicates a nitrogen content of approximately 0.6 wt% (≈2.7 at%), corresponding to an approximate composition of CeO1.92N0.08. This value represents the actual nitrogen concentration detected in the material and is substantially different from the nominal urea/CeO2 feed ratio used during synthesis.

Therefore, the sample designation (25%U-CeO2 in the SI, Fig. S2) refers only to the synthesis condition and not to the nitrogen content incorporated into the final solid. The relatively low intensity of the nitrogen signal is consistent with the expected low doping level and the detection limits of EDX analysis. Detailed EDX spectra and elemental mapping results are provided in the SI (Fig. S3).

These results support the coexistence of Ce, O, N in the synthesized catalyst and provide preliminary evidence of successful N incorporation, which will be further verified by XRD, Raman, and XPS analyses.

Defect structure and electronic states

The crystal structures of CeO2 and the modified catalysts were examined by XRD (Fig. 3). All samples exhibit diffraction peaks corresponding to the cubic fluorite structure of CeO2, indicating that nitrogen incorporation and composite formation do not alter the bulk crystal phase.

XRD patterns of CeO2; N-CeO2; N-CeO2/rGO; and N-CeO2/N-rGO.

Although the fluorite structure is preserved, subtle variations in peak position and width are observed after nitrogen incorporation. The (111) peak shifts slightly toward lower diffraction angles after nitrogen doping, indicating lattice expansion. This shift suggests the formation of structural defects such as oxygen vacancies and local lattice distortion induced by nitrogen incorporation. Such lattice expansion is consistent with defect formation in ceria, including oxygen deficiency and the partial reduction of Ce4+ to Ce3+.

Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 4) provides further evidence of defect formation. The characteristic F2g mode of CeO2 (∼464 cm−1) becomes broader after nitrogen doping, indicating increased lattice disorder. In the composite catalysts, the spectra display both the F2g mode of CeO2 and the D and G bands of graphene, confirming the formation of oxide–graphene hybrid structures.

Raman spectra of CeO2, N-CeO2, N-CeO2/rGO, and N-CeO2/N-rGO showing the F2g mode of fluorite CeO2 and the D, G, and 2D bands of the graphene framework.

The ID/IG ratio increases from 1.006 for N-CeO2/rGO to 1.055 for N-CeO2/N-rGO, indicating a higher defect density in the nitrogen-doped graphene framework. This result suggests that nitrogen incorporation modifies the oxide lattice and increases the structural disorder of the graphene support, thereby contributing to a more defect-rich heterointerface.

As summarized in Table 2, the Ce3+ fraction increases from 27.48% for pristine CeO2 to 38.47% for N-CeO2 after nitrogen incorporation. In the composite samples, the Ce3+ fractions remain relatively high, reaching 36.40% for N-CeO2/rGO and 32.01% for N-CeO2/N-rGO. Although slightly lower than that of N-CeO2, these values are still significantly higher than that of pristine CeO2, indicating that the defect-rich surface character generated by nitrogen incorporation is largely preserved after hybridization with graphene-based supports. Since oxygen-vacancy formation in ceria is accompanied by partial reduction of Ce4+ to Ce3+, the increased Ce3+ concentration is widely regarded as a direct indicator of oxygen-vacancy generation.

The O 1s spectrum of pristine CeO2 (Fig. 6a) can be deconvoluted into three components corresponding to lattice oxygen (OL, ∼529.1 eV), vacancy-related oxygen species (OV, ∼530.9 eV), and chemisorbed oxygen species (OC, ∼531.9 eV). Quantitative analysis reveals that the relative contribution of OV increases markedly from 17.1% in CeO2 to 36.8% in N-CeO2, whereas the fraction of lattice oxygen decreases from 74.8% to 53.2% (Table 2). The simultaneous increase in vacancy-related oxygen and decrease in lattice oxygen strongly suggest enhanced oxygen-defect formation following nitrogen incorporation. This observation is fully consistent with the increase in Ce3+ concentration obtained from the Ce 3d analysis.

High resolution XPS O 1s of (a) CeO2; (b) N-CeO2; (c) N-CeO2/rGO; and (d) N-CeO2/NrGO

The N 1s spectra (Fig. 7) provide further evidence of nitrogen incorporation. For N-CeO2, the N 1s spectrum can be resolved into components centered at approximately 396.7, 398.3, and 399.8 eV, assigned to Ce–N, pyridinic-N, and graphitic-N species, respectively.

High resolution XPS N 1s of (a) N-rGO; (b) N-CeO2; (c) N-CeO2/rGO; and (d) N-CeO2/N-rGO.

The presence of the Ce–N component indicates chemical interaction between nitrogen species and the ceria framework rather than simple surface adsorption. In N-rGO and the composite catalysts, pyridinic-N and graphitic-N species are also observed, confirming nitrogen incorporation within the graphene framework. Pyridinic-N can introduce defect-related active sites, whereas graphitic-N is generally associated with improved electronic conductivity of the carbon matrix.

To facilitate comparison among the samples, the quantitative XPS results are summarized in Table 2. Collectively, the Ce 3d, O 1s, and N 1s analyses consistently suggest that nitrogen incorporation modifies the surface electronic structure of ceria and promotes the generation of oxygen-vacancy-rich active sites, which are expected to contribute to the enhanced visible-light photocatalytic performance.

A comparison between EDX and XPS results further provides insight into the distribution of nitrogen in the N-CeO2 sample. EDX analysis indicates a nitrogen content of approximately 0.6 wt% (corresponding to ∼2.7 at%), whereas XPS reveals a surface nitrogen concentration of 18.12 at%. The substantially higher nitrogen content detected by XPS reflects the surface-sensitive nature of the technique and suggests that nitrogen species are preferentially enriched in the near-surface region of the ceria particles, where they can effectively influence the surface electronic structure and defect chemistry.

Overall, the XRD, Raman, and XPS results reveal that nitrogen incorporation induces lattice distortion, increases the Ce3+ fraction, and promotes the formation of oxygen-vacancy-rich ceria, while simultaneously increasing the defect density of the graphene framework. These structural and electronic features are central to the design of the N-CeO2/N-rGO heterostructure and are expected to facilitate interfacial charge transfer under visible-light irradiation.

Optical and surface properties

The optical absorption properties of the catalysts were investigated by UV-Vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (Fig. 8). The band gap energy (Eg) is determined from the Kubelka–Munk theory of the relationship between the absorption coefficient (α) and Eg:(α hν)n = A(hν − Eg)where A is a constant and hν is the photon energy. Pristine CeO2 shows an absorption edge near 400 nm, corresponding to a band gap of approximately 3.07 eV. After nitrogen doping, the absorption edge shifts toward longer wavelengths and the band gap decreases to 2.75 eV, indicating enhanced visible-light absorption due to defect-induced electronic states. This red shift is consistent with the formation of oxygen-vacancy-related states and the electronic perturbation introduced by nitrogen incorporation.

UV-vis DRS spectra of (a) CeO2, N-CeO2; and (b) N-CeO2/rGO, N-CeO2/N-rGO.

The composite catalysts exhibit even stronger absorption in the visible region. In particular, N- CeO2/N-rGO shows the broadest absorption (with Eg = 2.61 eV), reflecting the combined effects of defect-rich ceria and the light-harvesting capability of the graphene support. The enhanced optical response of the heterostructure is therefore attributable to the synergistic coupling between the modified ceria phase and the conductive N-doped carbon matrix.

N2 adsorption–desorption measurements show that the N-CeO2/N-rGO composite possesses a large specific surface area (∼247 m2 g−1), which originates from the porous graphene framework (Fig. S4 and S5 – SI). The high surface area and defect-rich structure provide favorable conditions for pollutant adsorption and photocatalytic reactions. Accordingly, the heterostructure combines three potentially advantageous features for photocatalysis: enhanced visible-light absorption, abundant accessible surface sites, and an intimate oxide–carbon interface that may facilitate charge transport.

Adsorption and photocatalytic degradation of 2,4-D

The adsorption behavior of the catalysts toward 2,4-D was first evaluated under dark conditions (Fig. 9).

Adsorption behavior of 2,4-D over the synthesized materials under dark conditions.

The adsorption capacities follow the order: N-rGO > N-CeO2/N-rGO > rGO > N-CeO2/rGO >> CeO2 ≈ N-CeO2. This trend correlates with the surface areas of the materials. Graphene containing samples exhibit higher adsorption due to the porous carbon framework, whereas the oxide samples show relatively low adsorption capacity. These results indicate that adsorption contributes to pollutant enrichment on the catalyst surface, but it cannot by itself explain the overall photocatalytic performance.

The photocatalytic degradation of 2,4-D under visible light is shown in Fig. 10.

(A) Decrease in 2,4-D concentration over time; (B) degradation efficiency of 2,4-D under visible-light irradiation.

After 240 min irradiation, the degradation efficiencies follow the order: rGO < N-rGO < CeO2 < N-CeO2 < N-CeO2/rGO < N-CeO2/N-rGO. Nitrogen doping significantly improves the intrinsic activity of CeO2.

The degradation efficiency increases from 54.1% for CeO2 to 76.0% for N-CeO2, which can be attributed to the increased concentration of oxygen vacancies and improved visible-light absorption. The composite catalysts exhibit further enhancement. In particular, N-CeO2/N-rGO achieves the highest degradation efficiency (∼90%), indicating a strong synergistic effect between defect-rich ceria and the conductive N-doped graphene support. Importantly, although N-rGO has the highest adsorption capacity, its photocatalytic activity remains limited, confirming that efficient 2,4-D removal requires not only surface adsorption but also effective photoinduced charge generation, separation, and interfacial transfer.

These results indicate that the activity enhancement follows the structural evolution of the catalysts: pristine CeO2 provides moderate photocatalytic performance, N-CeO2 benefits from defect-induced improvement, and the N-CeO2/N-rGO heterostructure delivers the highest performance through the combined effects of ceria vacancies and the N-doped graphene interface.

Nitrogen doping significantly increases the reaction rate. The rate constant increases from 0.0003 min−1 (rGO) to 0.0009 min−1 (N-rGO), indicating that nitrogen incorporation enhances the catalytic contribution of the carbon phase. For the oxide phase, CeO2 exhibits kap = 0.0037 min−1, which increases to 0.0064 min−1 after nitrogen doping, corresponding to a 1.7-fold enhancement associated with the higher Ce3+ fraction.

A further increase in the apparent rate constant is observed for the composite photocatalysts. N-CeO2/rGO exhibits a kap value of 0.0086 min−1, while N-CeO2/N-rGO shows the highest value (0.0105 min−1) among the photocatalysts evaluated in this study, corresponding to approximately 2.8 times the rate constant of pristine CeO2 and more than 16 times that of rGO. These kinetic results indicate that the enhanced photocatalytic performance of N-CeO2/N-rGO is not solely attributable to adsorption, but rather to the synergistic interaction between oxygen-defect-rich N-CeO2 and the conductive N-rGO framework, which facilitates charge utilization and promotes surface reaction kinetics.

Proposed adsorption–photocatalytic degradation mechanism of 2,4-D over N-CeO2/N-rGO under the employed irradiation conditions

Based on the combined structural, electronic, optical, adsorption, and kinetic results, a defect-assisted interfacial charge-transfer mechanism is proposed for the N-CeO2/N-rGO heterostructure.

First, the N-rGO surface facilitates the adsorption of 2,4-D molecules and dissolved oxygen, concentrating them near the catalytic sites. The large specific surface area of N-rGO and the presence of nitrogen functional groups facilitate pollutant adsorption and O2 activation.

Under the employed irradiation conditions, N-CeO2 absorbs photons and generates electron–hole pairs:CeO2 + hν → eCB− + hVB+

Nitrogen incorporation increases the Ce3+ concentration and oxygen-vacancy density, which introduce defect states within the band structure and enhance visible-light absorption. These oxygen vacancies also serve as electron-trapping centers, promoting charge separation.

The photogenerated electrons in the conduction band of N-CeO2 are rapidly transferred to the conductive N-rGO sheets:eCB− (N-CeO2) → e− (N-rGO)

This interfacial electron transfer is expected suppresses electron–hole recombination and may allow the transferred electrons to react with dissolved oxygen, forming superoxide radicals:e− (N-rGO) + O2 → ˙O2−

Meanwhile, photogenerated holes on the N-CeO2 surface may participate in oxidation reactions involving water or hydroxide ions, potentially leading to the formation of hydroxyl radicals:hVB+ + H2O/OH− → ˙OH

The generated reactive oxygen species (˙O2− and ˙OH) subsequently oxidize adsorbed 2,4-D molecules into degradation products. The exact degradation pathway and the nature of the resulting products require further investigation.

It should be noted that this mechanism is inferred from the observed structure–property–activity correlation rather than directly verified by reactive-species trapping experiments, transient spectroscopic measurements, or charge-carrier lifetime analyses. Therefore, the participation of specific reactive oxygen species and the exact charge-transfer pathway should be regarded as plausible rather than conclusively established.

Nevertheless, the results consistently indicate that the progressive enhancement in photocatalytic performance follows the designed structural evolution of the catalysts. Pristine CeO2 exhibits moderate activity because of its limited visible-light absorption and relatively low defect density. N-CeO2 shows improved activity associated with an increased Ce3+ fraction, enhanced oxygen-vacancy concentration, and stronger visible-light absorption. The N-CeO2/N-rGO heterostructure exhibits the highest activity, which is attributed to the combined effects of defect-rich ceria, improved pollutant adsorption, enhanced visible-light absorption, and the presence of a conductive nitrogen-doped graphene framework that may facilitate interfacial charge transfer. Therefore, the synergistic effect between ceria vacancies and N-doped graphene interfaces is proposed as a plausible explanation for the superior photocatalytic degradation performance observed in this study.

Conclusions

In this work, defect-engineered N-CeO2/N-rGO heterostructured photocatalysts were successfully developed for the visible-light degradation of 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D). Structural and spectroscopic analyses indicated that nitrogen incorporation into the CeO2 lattice increased the Ce3+ fraction and promoted the formation of oxygen vacancies, while nitrogen doping in the graphene framework introduced additional defect sites and was expected to improve the electronic conductivity of the carbon matrix.

The hybridization of N-CeO2 nanoparticles with N-rGO sheets resulted in a well-defined oxide–carbon heterostructure with intimate interfacial contact. The resulting heterostructure provided multiple advantages for photocatalysis, including improved visible-light absorption, increased surface area, and enhanced adsorption capacity for organic pollutants. Raman and XPS results further indicated that the heterointerface formed between defect-rich ceria and nitrogen-doped graphene was favorable for interfacial charge transfer.

Photocatalytic experiments demonstrated that the N-CeO2/N-rGO composite exhibited the highest activity for 2,4-D degradation under visible-light irradiation, achieving approximately 90% removal after 240 min. Kinetic analysis revealed that the apparent rate constant of N-CeO2/N-rGO (0.0105 min−1) was significantly higher than those of pristine CeO2 and the other reference catalysts, confirming the superior catalytic efficiency of the heterostructure.

The enhanced photocatalytic performance can be attributed to the synergistic interaction between oxygen-vacancy-rich ceria and the conductive N-doped graphene framework. Nitrogen-induced defects in CeO2 are associated with enhanced visible-light absorption, while the N-rGO sheets provide a conductive framework that may facilitate charge transport and reduce charge recombination. The resulting interfacial charge-transfer process is proposed to enhance the generation of reactive oxygen species responsible for the oxidative degradation of 2,4-D.

Overall, this study highlights the importance of simultaneous engineering defects in both the oxide and carbon components to construct efficient photocatalytic heterostructures. The strategy of coupling vacancy-rich ceria with nitrogen-doped graphene provides a promising pathway for designing high-performance visible-light photocatalysts for the removal of persistent organic pollutants in aqueous environments.
