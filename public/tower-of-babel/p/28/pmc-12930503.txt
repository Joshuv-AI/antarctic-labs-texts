Background

Intracranial hemorrhages (ICH) are potentially life‐threatening, acute pathologies that occur after trauma or spontaneously due to vascular or small‐ or large‐vessel disease. Timely diagnosis is important during treatment, and is usually performed by head computed tomographies (hCT) [1, 2]. An increase in the need for diagnostic imaging during both day and night hospital shifts, as well as a relative lack of (neuro‐) radiologists, further potentiate the risk of delayed or even missed ICH detection [3, 4, 5]. Machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), neuronal nets (NN), and their related commercial and customized methods and software, hereafter summarized under the umbrella term ML, have substantially changed radiological imaging within an extremely short period of time. By estimating the diagnostic accuracy of ML for signaling ICH on hCT as compared to any clinically and scientifically accepted reference standard, we will investigate if and how ML can assist doctors in identifying ICH, in particular if the identification of ICH missed on hCT can save patients' lives or provide them with the chance of tissue and functional recovery.

The cause and type of ICH vary significantly. The global prevalence for any ICH was 3.0 to 3.9 million in 2019 [6]. Incidence is strongly influenced by age and ethnicity of a population, and can fluctuate between 6 and 176 per 100,000 person‐years [7, 8, 9, 10, 11].

As a rule of thumb, the likelihood of surviving ICH or recovering with sufficient function to live independently is approximately 50:50. Although mortality has decreased over the years, it remains high: 21% to 55%, with rates slightly increasing with the time from onset of ICH [8, 10, 12]. A complex set of interacting factors determine patient prognosis, including demographic baseline features (e.g. age, gender, comorbidity, hypertension, obesity, smoking, medication, etc.), cause of ICH (e.g. ruptured intracranial aneurysm, stroke, head injury, etc.), diagnostic and therapeutic delay, accompanying injuries in the trauma setting, rapid referral to a proper hospital, individualized and guideline‐conform therapeutic intervention (e.g. neurosurgery or neuro‐interventional radiology, or both, etc.), intensive care, rehabilitation, and multiple others.

The global number of disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) due to ICH increased by 25% between 1990 and 2019 [6]. According to the World Health Organization, spontaneous ICH or hemorrhagic stroke caused 1279.5 DALYs and 71.61 deaths per 100,000 population in Germany in 2021 (91.89 million DALYs worldwide) [13]. DALYs lost by traumatic ICH are summarized among other injuries but have been estimated to amount to roughly 248 million worldwide between 1990 and 2013 [14]. Furthermore, the influence of ICH on global economies is significant. The value of lost welfare (VLW) for spontaneous ICH alone equaled USD 1095.17 billion (1,095,170 million) in 2019 worldwide [15]. As mentioned above, traumatic ICH is usually included among all injuries. However, in a Western country with only 5 million inhabitants (New Zealand), traumatic ICH created annual costs of USD 149 million for prevalent and new cases in 2010 [16].

Target condition being diagnosed

ICH is a serious condition affecting people of any gender and age, because of chronic and acute disorders and events, in high‐ and low‐income countries alike. Early referral to a high‐volume unit, immediate diagnosis of ICH (regardless of its cause) by hCT, and treatment conditional on findings increases the probability of the best possible outcome [17, 18, 19]. The problem is the burden on high‐volume units which, due to teleradiology and telemedicine, has increased in recent years.

When interpreting hundreds of hCTs on a weekend, even the best and most experienced radiologist may experience fatigue, leading to both false‐negative (FN) and false‐positive (FP) interpretations of images that result in under‐ or over‐treatment. In clinical practice, FN findings may play a greater role than FP, as patients urgently requiring intervention to save their lives or provide them with the chance of tissue and functional recovery may fall through the cracks. This is unacceptable from both an ethical and health‐economic point of view.

Index test(s)

The first ML algorithm to detect ICH on hCT received US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) clearance in 2018. Since then, many software products based on ML algorithms have been approved as medical devices in Europe (Conformité Européenne [CE] certification) or the United States (FDA certification) and are used in clinical practice. ML can assist radiologists during times of high workload, at best reducing the interval between detection of a significant finding and its proper treatment. Algorithms are designed to prioritize examinations through reordering radiology worklists by flagging abnormal scans, as a reading aid, or for discrepancy evaluation post‐reading to identify missed ICH.

To the best of our knowledge, there are no randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating the effectiveness of ML algorithms over the standard of care in diagnosing ICH on hCT scans. However, a multitude of scientists have performed analyses of various ML algorithms for the detection of ICH with lower levels of evidence. Most of these studies, like our own prior study [20], included a markedly large and diverse case‐mix and lacked truly comparable diagnostic accuracy measures. Systematic reviews reported sensitivities and specificities of above 90% for detecting ICH [21, 22, 23, 24]. However, the conclusions of the majority of these systematic reviews have only limited validity due to considerable heterogeneity in both sensitivity and specificity between the original studies.

A multitude of symptoms and medical histories entail indications of hCTs. Typical patients have either suffered significant head trauma or present with headaches or neurological symptoms (such as paresis, paresthesia, dysarthria, dizziness, or altered consciousness), or both. Possible results of hCT are confirmation or identification of ICH, exclusion of ICH, and identification or exclusion of other causes for clinical symptoms. Identification of ICH or any other intracranial pathology will result in further imaging using follow‐up hCT or MRI and treatment. Surgical, minimally invasive, endovascular, or non‐invasive treatment options are available for ICH and are dependent on various factors including the extent and cause of ICH, seriousness of neurological symptoms, and patient‐related factors (age, comorbidities, individual patient will). Thus far, guidelines for treatment of traumatic brain injury (TBI) and ICH associated with aneurysm rupture are more established [25, 26, 27, 28] than consensus about treatment of spontaneous ICH unrelated to macrovascular pathologies [29, 30, 31].

Prior test(s)

Prior to hCT, patients are evaluated clinically by trauma surgeons, neurosurgeons, and neurologists. Clinical scales used in the context of TBI and ICH are the Glasgow Coma Score (GCS) [32], Hunt and Hess or World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) grading system [33], modified Rankin Scale (mRS) [34], and the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) [35]. After clinical evaluation, patients are triaged to either receive no medical treatment and are discharged, observation with or without neuroimaging by MRI at a later stage, or immediate hCT depending on their symptoms and age. Clinical decision rules like the New Orleans criteria, the Canadian CT head rule, the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guideline for head injury, and the CT in Head Injury Patients rule can facilitate the assessment and reduce the number of hCTs (i.e. after TBI) [36]. Some pediatric patients with relevant trauma but mild or no symptoms might qualify for speedy MRI to reduce radiation exposure in the pediatric population [37].

Patients triaged to receive hCT are then referred and transported to the CT scanner to identify or rule out possible ICH and other causes of symptoms, such as ischemia and tumors.

Role of index test(s)

Numerous developers have addressed the problem of missed or delayed diagnosis of ICH on hCT. Even though the underlying technologies might be similar, scientific evidence of ML algorithm‐based tools to detect ICH differs between investigating sites. The reasons for implementing such tools are the need for prioritization of hCT with ICH to ensure timely treatment or even expedite existing workflows during times of increased workloads, relative and absolute shortage of staff interpreting hCT, and a deficit of specialized (neuro‐) radiologists [38]. Hence, hCT scans are usually exported from CT scanners to a local or cloud‐based platform for analysis by means of ML algorithms. Another option might involve analysis after export to picture archiving and communication systems (PACS). Positive findings of ML algorithms will then attract the attention of treating physicians. Currently, the ultimate decision regarding the correctness of automated analyses relies solely on human readers. Quick display of true‐positive findings by algorithms has the potential to refine the triage of patients and optimally lead to increased patient safety and outcomes. Before these ultimate outcome measures can be measured and evaluated, it is crucial that research focuses on determining the diagnostic accuracy of ML algorithms for ICH detection. In particular, the performance of these algorithms in comparison to human readers has yet to be determined with higher scientific evidence in order to substantiate the value and clinical usefulness of these tools.

Alternative test(s)

Before ML‐based algorithms for ICH detection were developed, detection of ICH exclusively relied on human readers with varying degrees of expertise, depending on local infrastructure and personnel. From the point of view of education and experience, reading by neuroradiologists should be considered as a gold standard for the interpretation of hCT [39, 40].

Rationale

ML‑based medical devices in clinical radiology have been evolving rapidly in recent years. The innovation spiral in this area is great, and there is a need to scientifically evaluate the diagnostic gains and benefits of this potentially disruptive healthcare technology. There is considerable debate across the clinical and scientific community about whether ML will replace doctors in the future, produce false‐positive findings, and impair healthcare providers’ capability to make good, patient‐centered decisions. With this first Cochrane review on ML‐based ICH detection on hCTs, we will appraise and synthesize evidence to inform the debate in this area.

Objectives

The primary objective of this review is to determine the diagnostic accuracy of any form of ML algorithm for detecting ICH in participants who underwent hCT. We will assess diagnostic accuracy by individual and pooled indicators such as sensitivity and specificity with 95% confidence intervals, positive and negative likelihood ratios, and the summary receiver operating characteristic curve.

Methods

Criteria for considering studies for this review

Types of studies

We will include prospective or retrospective cohort studies and RCTs. These must provide sufficient data to calculate sensitivity, specificity, and other indices of diagnostic test accuracy. Both comparative primary study designs and single‐test accuracy designs will be eligible for inclusion. Furthermore, we will consider ML algorithms both as standalone tools and as adjunct to human readers in calculating diagnostic accuracy measures. We will not include evaluations of test accuracy (e.g. based on artificial training samples) that are unlikely to be representative of the performance of the test in practice. Moreover, findings of only internally validated algorithms will not be taken into account. We will apply no restrictions with regard to time of follow‐up or other aspects.

Participants

The target population of this review is participants of any age and gender who were screened by means of hCT for the detection of ICH caused by any condition or disorder at a medical institution of any care level. Participants with and without ICH as the target condition who were receiving hCT scans are eligible for inclusion. Participants with a history of ICH, who are being evaluated for recurrence or resolution, will be excluded. By not limiting the participant population who undergo hCT for suspected ICH, we achieve the greatest possible representativity of our sample with regard to the use of the ML algorithm in routine clinical practice. In this way, a possible application scenario for ML algorithms can also be examined in which radiologists are supported in screening and flagging all scans for possible ICH during times of high workload without the need for preselecting hCT scans. To achieve this, typically all hCT are analyzed automatically with few or no restrictions, as ICH can be present even when not clinically suspected. Including participants without suspected ICH reflects real‐world clinical practice and enables the assessment of over‐call rates, specificity, negative predictive value (NPV) and positive predictive value (PPV), and helps to understand the diagnostic performance of ML algorithms and the potential for artificial intelligence (AI) fatigue [41].

Index tests

As index test, any type of ML algorithm for signaling ICH on hCT will be considered in this review. In particular, DL algorithms and their related commercial and customized methods and software are used to detect ICH from visual data. Among these, Random Forest (RF) [42], Conditional Random Field (CRF) [43], Support Vector Machine (SVM) [44], and Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) [45] are the best‐known ML algorithms. The basic requirement for the consideration of an algorithm is the binary classification into ICH present or not present. In order not to limit the generalizability of our results, we will not exclude any ML algorithm in our review. We will analyze the effect of different types of ML algorithms on diagnostic accuracy as part of our investigation of heterogeneity.

However, we will only consider ML algorithms that were externally validated by means of datasets that are representative of the scans seen in routine clinical practice. As previously reported, using unrepresentative datasets or inadequate validation methodology may result in biased conclusions [46].

Target conditions

The target condition is the presence or absence of ICH caused by any condition or disorder in participants of any age and gender.

A true positive (TP) refers to a case where the ML algorithm correctly signals 'ICH present' based on hCT, as confirmed by the reference standard (i.e. evaluation of hCT by a general radiologist, interventional or diagnostic neuroradiologist). A true negative (TN) occurs when the ML algorithm correctly signals 'ICH not present,' as confirmed by the reference standard. A false positive (FP) refers to a case where the ML algorithm signals 'ICH present,' but the reference standard confirms that ICH is not present, and a false negative (FN) occurs when the ML algorithm signals 'ICH not present,' but the reference standard confirms that ICH is actually present.

Reference standards

We define the reference standard as being the best available clinical method for identifying participants with ICH. This comprises general radiologists, interventional and diagnostic neuroradiologists evaluating the presence of ICH based on hCT without assistance from ML algorithms. Radiologists will need to be blinded to the results of the ML algorithm, meaning that they will be unaware of these results when evaluating hCT scans. Where available, the evaluations on the presence of ICH will also take into account follow‐up hCTs. Due to the predominantly lower level of other healthcare professionals in this area, reports from radiographers, stroke physicians, or emergency medicine physicians will not be accepted as a reference standard. If hCT scans are assessed independently by multiple healthcare professionals, the evaluations provided by the most highly specialized clinicians will be considered the reference standard.

Searching other resources

We will also use a 'snowball' procedure to identify related articles and articles cited in the reference lists of individual publications. If we require further data or explanation of study results, we will contact study authors by email or phone. We will guarantee that any data exchange will comply with International Council for Harmonisation ‐ Good Clinical Practice (ICH‐GCP) and individual rules and regulations for data safety and security. We will also search for grey literature in OpenGrey.

Data collection and analysis

At our institution, we employ standard operating procedures (SOP) for the selection of studies, data extraction, and recording in the context of a systematic review and meta‐analysis, which should guarantee transparency and adherence to Cochrane standards and other recommendations (e.g. those issued by the EQUATOR [Enhancing the QUAlity and Transparency Of health Research] group) [47]. While working on this review, we will adhere to ICH‐GCP [48], Good Epidemiological Practice (GEP) [49], and all other relevant rules and recommendations. We have trained personnel onsite to record, manage, and audit data, and our data storage complies with German legislation on data safety for research purposes.

Selection of studies

Two review authors (neuroradiologist and research associate) will independently screen the titles and abstracts of the study reports identified. We will assess studies for eligibility based on the defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. If it is not possible to make a decision based on title and abstract alone, we will obtain the full texts of studies deemed potentially relevant and assess them for inclusion in the review. Any disagreements between the two review authors regarding study selection will be resolved by a third review author. We will document the study selection process in a PRISMA flow diagram.

We will document details of the included studies in a predefined Excel spreadsheet. Two review authors will extract data from the original papers in duplicate, resolving any discrepancies by discussion, which will be moderated by a third review author.

Assessment of methodological quality

Two review authors (neuroradiologist and research associate) will independently assess the quality of primary studies using the QUADAS‐2 tool [51]. Any discrepancies will be resolved by discussion, as moderated by a third review author. QUADAS‐2 is the revised and updated version of the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies Included in Systematic Reviews (QUADAS) list, and includes four domains, namely: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. Each domain is assessed in terms of risk of bias and rated as 'low,' 'high,' or 'unclear.' Concerns regarding applicability are evaluated only for the first three domains and categorized as 'low,' 'high,' or 'unclear.' Signaling questions can be answered with 'yes,' 'no,' or 'unclear.'

In order to improve the reliability of the methodological assessment using the QUADAS‐2 tool, we have tailored QUADAS‐2 to our review question (see Table 1) and piloted it on the basis of selected primary studies. Statistical analysis and data synthesis

We will construct a 2 x 2 table for ICH detection by the ML algorithm based on the reported number of true positives, false negatives, true negatives, and false positives in the included studies. We will report the number of valid (e.g. adequate test result has been obtained, but the result is not clearly positive or negative) and invalid (e.g. caused by technical failures) inconclusive test results and group the valid inconclusive results with either the positive or negative results, depending on how these patients would be treated in the clinical context [52]. In rare instances where multiple scans on the same patient are available, only the first scan of a patient will be included in the analysis for calculating diagnostic accuracy. We will use follow‐up data to report the number and percentage of ICH cases detected exclusively by ML. For individual studies, we will calculate sensitivity and specificity with 95% confidence intervals (CI), tabulate pairs of sensitivity and specificity with CI, and depict estimates of coupled forest plots using RevMan [53].

We will estimate sensitivities and specificities across individual studies using the bivariate model, which is a robust statistical model taking the underlying relationship between sensitivity and specificity into account. We will fit the models in Stata [54] using binomial within‐study variance via the 'metandi' command. Graphical presentations will comprise summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) plots of sensitivity and specificity along with 95% confidence region and 95% prediction region, created in RevMan [53]. We will obtain likelihood ratios post‐estimation using the parameters of the bivariate model.

We will use the GRADE approach to evaluate the certainty of evidence [55] and present the key findings of the review in a summary of findings table. We will summarize the results of the search, characteristics of the included studies, and methodological quality assessment. We will endeavor to express the degree of statistical uncertainty associated with summary estimates of test accuracy and examine between‐study variability in detail.

Investigations of heterogeneity

We will assess heterogeneity visually by inspection of the coupled forest plots and plots of study results in the SROC space. We will also investigate possible sources of heterogeneity by adding single covariates to the basic bivariate random‐effects model. The bivariate model will be fit via the 'xtmelogit' command in Stata [56]. We will investigate if individual covariates affect the estimated sensitivity, specificity, or both [57]. Due to the expected small sizes of subgroups, we will interpret the results of heterogeneity analyses with caution and in a descriptive fashion.

For tests of heterogeneity, a minimum of 10 studies in total and at least four studies per categorical subgroup are advised [58]. Depending on the size of the subgroups, we will investigate the following potential sources of heterogeneity:

Sensitivity analyses

We will perform sensitivity analyses to investigate how individual QUADAS‐2 key domains (i.e. patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing) affect accuracy estimates. We will exclude each study that is considered to be at high or unclear risk of bias or judged to have high or unclear concerns of applicability in a respective domain from the domain‐specific analysis. By comparing the accuracy estimates of the sensitivity analysis of the remaining studies with those of the primary analysis that included the low‐quality studies, we will test the robustness of the primary analysis results. In a further sensitivity analysis, we will assess the impact of excluding inconclusive values on the accuracy estimates.

Assessment of reporting bias

If at least 10 studies are included in the meta‐analysis, we will explore potential publication bias by generating a funnel plot [59]. This will allow us to assess the potential for small‐study effects.
