Ternary solvation sheath reconfiguration drives sustainable cryogenic Li||Cl2 batteries
Authors: Qihao Liu, Guanzhong Ma, Ling Wei, Caili Zhang, Jiajun Geng, Junwei Han, Debin Kong, Linjie Zhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRechargeable Li | |Cl2 batteries hold promise for low temperature storage but suffer from interfacial instability due to electrolyte decomposition, particularly at cryogenic temperatures. We identify the failure mechanism as a complex solvation chemistry involving Li+, reactive SOCl2, and coordinating chloroaluminates that drives parasitic reactions. Here, we demonstrate a Ternary Solvation Sheath Reconfiguration strategy using lithium trifluoromethanesulfonate to engineer the solvation structure. By leveraging strong anion-cation interactions, this approach reconfigures the coordination environment to displace solvent molecules and directs the in-situ formation of a robust, bilayered positive electrode electrolyte interphase featuring an inorganic-rich inner layer. This engineered interphase effectively suppresses electrolyte degradation and enhances charge-transfer kinetics by lowering the Li+ desolvation barrier. Consequently, cells employing the Ternary Solvation Sheath Reconfiguration electrolyte sustain stable cycling at –40 °C (1000 mA g⁻1), maintaining capacity retention of 99.2% and Coulombic efficiency of 99.2% after 1100 cycles, while enabling reliable operation even at –80 °C. This work establishes a systematic framework for designing functional interphases via solvation chemistry for sustainable energy storage.Subject terms: Batteries, Batteries, Batteries

Introduction

The development of advanced electrochemical energy storage solutions offering both high energy density and cost-effectiveness is crucial for a sustainable energy future. However, developing such technologies often encounters substantial material and interfacial challenges1–7. Primary lithium-thionyl chloride (Li||SOCl2) batteries, for example, offer high theoretical energy density for niche applications, but their single-use nature is a significant drawback from a sustainability perspective, limiting resource circularity and contributing to waste8,9. Consequently, research has increasingly focused on rechargeable systems to enhance material utilization and minimize environmental impact, in line with broader sustainability goals10. Among these, rechargeable Li||Cl2 batteries are compelling, primarily due to their high theoretical specific energy (approximately 700 Wh kg−1) and the use of relatively earth-abundant constituent elements11,12. A typical Li||Cl2 cell comprises a lithium metal negative electrode, a chlorine-hosting positive electrode (often a porous carbon material), and an SOCl2-based electrolyte containing chloroaluminates (such as LiAlCl4) to facilitate Li+ conduction. The viability of these rechargeable cells hinges on the efficient and reversible conversion between LiCl and Cl2 at the positive electrode. Nevertheless, effectively controlling this electrochemical transformation while ensuring long-term interfacial stability under practical operating conditions presents considerable scientific and engineering hurdles12.

While efforts to enhance the reaction kinetics and cycling stability of rechargeable Li||Cl2 batteries have explored various approaches, much of the research has focused on the positive electrode11–17. Advances in positive electrode materials and architectures, for instance, by nanoconfining chlorine within metal-organic frameworks, have indeed enhanced reaction kinetics and capacity retention17. Despite its critical role in determining interfacial stability and long-term sustainability, systematic electrolyte engineering remains a comparatively neglected area of research18–21. Although some studies have reported benefits from tailored electrolytes with silicon negative electrodes20 or through additives in related alkali-metal—chlorine systems, such as iodide in Na||Cl2 batteries for improved reversibility21, a more comprehensive understanding of the electrolyte’s role, particularly its decomposition, is still needed. It is recognized that SOCl2-based electrolytes can decompose, and their byproducts interact with the electrode interface8,10,11. While some degree of electrolyte decomposition is common, this issue becomes particularly problematic during prolonged cycling or at low temperature, where high operating voltages can drive excessive and uncontrolled electrolyte reactions. The resulting detrimental effects of such excessive decomposition, including the formation of unstable and passivating interfacial layers, the consumption of active electrolyte species, and hindered ionic transport, are significant, yet their full implications are not thoroughly understood. Key questions persist regarding how the complex products from this excessive decomposition precisely shape the chemistry and microstructure of the positive electrode interphase. Indeed, the formation of this positive electrode interphase is intricately linked to the transport and reaction of key electrolyte components, including not only Li+ but also chloroaluminate species, like AlCl4−, which are essential for the LiCl precipitation and dissolution process. However, a fundamental challenge lies in the complex and often parasitic interactions involving the electrolyte’s three key components—Li+, the SOCl2 solvent, and chloroaluminate anions. This intricate solvation environment not only dictates the pathway of Li+ transport but, critically, also drives the continuous and unavoidable decomposition of the reactive SOCl2 at the positive electrode interface. This perpetual degradation, governed by the complex solvation chemistry, forms a chemically and structurally unstable positive electrode-electrolyte interphase, which is the core obstacle to achieving long-term cycling stability, particularly under cryogenic conditions22–25.

Herein, we address the challenge of precisely tuning the Li+ solvation environment by proposing and validating a ternary solvation sheath reconfiguration (TSSR) strategy. This TSSR approach, which leverages lithium trifluoromethanesulfonate (LiOTf) as a functional component, fundamentally redefines the Li+ coordination environment. Specifically, the TSSR mechanism leverages the distinct properties of LiOTf, particularly its high donor number (DN) and elevated highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy. These intrinsic characteristics not only enable Lewis-basic OTf− to effectively coordinate with Lewis-acidic chloroaluminates (AlCl4−/Al2Cl7−) but also directly contribute to modulating the involvement of SOCl2 within the primary Li+ solvation sphere. Through the TSSR strategy, OTf− reconfigures the Li+ solvation sheath due to its specific interactions with the ternary electrolyte components (Li+, SOCl2, and chloraluminates). This precisely reconfigured solvation environment subsequently directs the in situ formation of a distinct, anion-derived composite positive electrode-electrolyte interphase. This interphase exhibits a distinct bilayered architecture, comprising an inorganic-rich LiF inner layer and an outer organic layer featuring C–F functional groups. The successful implementation of the TSSR strategy, leading to this engineered interphase, further suppresses excessive electrolyte decomposition and stabilizes the positive electrode interphase, while simultaneously promoting Li+ desolvation and interfacial charge-transfer kinetics. These TSSR-driven improvements enable Li||CMK-3 cells to achieve stable cycling for over 1100 cycles at 1000 mA g⁻1 and –40 °C with nearly 100% coulombic efficiency, and to maintain stable stability with over 100 highly reversible cycles even at –80 °C. This work, therefore, proposes the TSSR strategy, laying an important foundation for developing more reliable and sustainable Li||Cl2 batteries capable of operating effectively in low temperature.

Results

Electrolyte-driven battery failure and additive screening

To understand the failure mechanisms in rechargeable Li||Cl2 batteries stemming from electrolyte degradation, especially in cells lacking advanced interfacial engineering, we began by systematically examining their electrochemical behavior and interfacial characteristics under typical operating and more demanding conditions (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Figs. 1–3).

At 25 °C, these conventional cells initially showed rapid degradation in electrochemical performance. As shown in Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1, cell capacity dropped sharply during early cycling, and the rapid capacity fade precluded practical application. This instability is attributable to irreversible side reactions and the loss of active materials within the battery. This poor performance is closely linked to severe instability at the positive electrode interphase. Excessive electrolyte decomposition is a primary driver of this interfacial instability. Under electrolyte-starved conditions, its detrimental impact is particularly exacerbated, leading to accelerated performance degradation that significantly affects subsequent cycling (Fig. 1b). Microscopic analysis of the positive electrode after cycling (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 2) confirmed these issues, showing that the positive electrode interphase typically had nonuniform, loosely-packed deposits, cracks, and structural damage, all hindering efficient Li+ transport and charge transfer. These findings indicate that uncontrolled products from electrolyte decomposition directly contribute to the degradation of the positive electrode interphase structural and chemical integrity. These electrolyte-driven failure modes worsen under cryogenic conditions. Specifically, low temperatures often lead to increased cell polarization and elevated operating voltage plateaus, which in turn are known to accelerate electrolyte decomposition rates (schematically shown in Fig. 1d). This accelerated decomposition results in a more detrimental positive electrode interphase, evidenced by severe morphological degradation of the positive electrode surface and significant electrolyte depletion (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3), leading to premature and complete battery failure at low temperatures. These characterizations clearly show that uncontrolled electrolyte decomposition and the resulting continuous degradation of the positive electrode interphase are fundamental bottlenecks. These issues limit the long-term cycling stability and reliable performance of conventional Li||Cl2 batteries, especially under demanding cryogenic conditions.

Recognizing that these fundamental bottlenecks stem from the detrimental participation of SOCl2 in the Li⁺ solvation sheath, particularly under cryogenic conditions, we have developed the TSSR strategy. The goal was to identify a component that could favorably modulate the Li+ coordination environment, reduce unwanted solvent activity at the interface, and consequently foster the formation of a more stable positive electrode interphase. Thus, we initiated a systematic screening of potential electrolyte functional components. Our systematic screening of components was therefore guided by key molecular descriptors essential for achieving our strategic goals: a high DN to ensure effective coordination with Li+ and chloroaluminates, a high HOMO energy to promote the formation of a stable positive electrode-electrolyte interphase through controlled decomposition, and an appropriate LUMO/HOMO gap for overall electrochemical stability (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Table 1)26. Among the candidates evaluated, LiOTf stood out due to its strongly polar and chemically robust S=O bonds. This triflate salt combines a high DN with a relatively high HOMO energy, enabling it to coordinate with Li+ via its Lewis basic sites and interact with Lewis acidic chloraluminates (AlCl4−/Al2Cl7−) in the electrolyte. Furthermore, its high HOMO energy facilitates its targeted decomposition at the positive electrode interface, a process that is instrumental in constructing a protective positive electrode-electrolyte interphase with favorable ionic transport properties24. These properties highlight its potential to initiate the desired solvation sheath reconfiguration by modifying the Li+ coordination, thereby potentially reducing the desolvation energy barrier (Supplementary Fig. 4). Electrostatic potential (ESP) analysis further supports this, showing that OTf− has a concentrated and elevated potential near the S=O and C–F bonds, suggesting a strong tendency for OTf− to both coordinate with Li⁺ and adsorb onto the electrode surface (Supplementary Fig. 5). This screening approach identified LiOTf as a prime candidate for achieving TSSR. This screening approach provides a rational design framework for selecting functional components, establishing a clear link between the properties of anionic functional groups and the goal of mitigating interfacial instability and improving battery performance under low temperature. This specific combination of properties in LiOTf not only promotes favorable anionic interactions for reconfiguration but also helps form a robust, F-rich interphase (Supplementary Fig. 6), offering a basis for a rational design framework for advanced Li||Cl2 battery electrolytes (Supplementary Fig. 7). The TSSR approach thus leads to a reconfigured, anion-rich solvation structure and, consequently, a protective interphase that minimizes side reactions and promotes homogeneous deposition (Fig. 1f). This outcome contrasts sharply with the uncontrolled interfacial reactions observed in the baseline electrolyte (Fig. 1d).

Electrolyte characterization and calculations

To understand the TSSR mechanism and the resulting electrolyte structures, we combined computational and spectroscopic analyses. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations first offered atomic-scale views of how 0.3 M LiOTf alters the Li+ solvation environment (Fig. 2a, b, Supplementary Figs. 8–10, and Supplementary Data 1)27,28. In the baseline electrolyte (Supplementary Table 2), SOCl2 molecules and AlCl4− anions primarily occupy the Li+ solvation sheath, creating a coordination that can hinder efficient ion transport and desolvation. In the TSSR electrolyte, however, a clear reconfiguration occurs: some SOCl2 is displaced from the primary solvation sheath, and OTf− accumulates near Li+ and AlCl4−. This ternary rearrangement forms an anion-enriched solvation structure. Radial distribution function (RDF) and coordination number analyses further confirmed that this structure significantly improves the Li+ desolvation rate29,30. Specifically, in the baseline electrolyte, Li+ interactions with SOCl2 (via O atoms) and AlCl4− (via Al centers) show broad distributions, indicating multiple competing coordination sites. In contrast, the TSSR electrolyte exhibits narrower, more distinct peaks for Li+–O(OTf−), Li+–O(SOCl2), and Li+–Al(AlCl4−) interactions, confirming greater anion enrichment in the reconfigured sheath (Fig. 2c, d). This reconfigured, anion-dominated solvation structure is therefore anticipated to facilitate the Li⁺ desolvation process and enhance ion transport, which would be particularly beneficial under demanding operating conditions.

Spectroscopic studies further confirmed these TSSR-induced structural changes. Combined 7Li and 27Al nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy showed how the TSSR strategy disrupts the competitive coordination involving AlCl4−/Al2Cl7− species. The NMR spectra indicate that in the TSSR electrolyte, some Li+ ions that were originally strongly coordinated with chloraluminate anions move into a more weakly bound, reconfigured solvation state (Fig. 2e, f)31,32. Raman spectroscopy also provided evidence for this reconfiguration. The characteristic SOCl2 coordination peak showed a clear blue shift after adding LiAlCl4 and LiOTf, and both the antisymmetric and symmetric S–Cl bond stretching vibrations were split (Fig. 2g)19,31,33. These spectral features confirm that OTf− interacts with both Al-Cl bridging bonds and S–Cl bonds. Such interactions support the reconfiguration driven by the TSSR strategy of the Li+ solvation structure, leading to faster Li+ desolvation and a changed Al-Cl anion coordination environment. Analysis of electrolyte solvation configurations from these studies also revealed that the TSSR strategy reduces solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIPs) and contact ion pairs (CIPs), while increasing aggregated (AGGs) solvation structures (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Table 3). This TSSR-driven shift towards AGGs structures not only improves the environment for ion transport but also helps form a stable, anion-derived positive electrode-electrolyte interphase. Theoretically, this shift toward AGG structures is beneficial for Li+ transport. In baseline electrolytes dominated by SSIP/CIP configurations, Li+ migration is often limited by the high energy barrier required for desolvation from a rigid solvent cage. In contrast, the anion-rich AGG structures provide a more dynamic coordination environment, offering low-energy pathways for Li+ to move between adjacent coordination sites without full desolvation. This mechanism enhances the intrinsic mobility of Li+, contributing to higher ionic conductivity and reduced polarization (Fig. 2i).

Mechanisms of TSSR-derived positive electrode-electrolyte interphase chemistry

Following our understanding of how the TSSR strategy impacts the Li+ coordination and bulk electrolyte properties (Fig. 2), we next investigated how this TSSR-modulated system guides the formation of a protective positive electrode-electrolyte interphase and influences its chemical makeup and operational mechanism. This understanding is key to addressing the persistent issue of electrolyte decomposition in Li||Cl2 batteries, a factor that significantly impacts their long-term sustainability. These factors promote excessive electrolyte decomposition, preventing complete reconversion and undermining reversibility, with the unrecovered fraction manifesting as parasitic losses. To counter these issues, our TSSR strategy is specifically designed to reconfigure the Li+ solvation environment. This approach aims to foster a more controlled interfacial chemistry and guide the formation of an effective interphase, thereby minimizing detrimental processes, improving the reversibility of key electrochemical reactions, and ultimately enhancing the overall sustainability of the battery system.

To further elucidate the chemical pathways underpinning these improvements, we used detailed time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) to map the spatial distribution of key species within the interphase. Three-dimensional TOF-SIMS reconstructions (Fig. 3a) compare the distributions of CF3⁻, LiF⁻, and LiCl⁻—all critical for positive electrode-electrolyte interphase construction—in the baseline and TSSR electrolytes. After 100 cycles in the baseline electrolyte, the positive electrode surface showed sparse, isolated CF3⁻ clusters and a relatively shallow LiF⁻ distribution, while LiCl⁻ deposits were widespread and disorganized. This pattern suggests that in the baseline electrolyte, chloride deposition is uncontrolled, leaving large positive electrode areas unprotected and prone to excessive electrolyte decomposition and further side-product buildup (Supplementary Fig. 11), a conclusion supported by anion fragment depth profiling (Supplementary Fig. 12). In sharp contrast, the TSSR electrolyte (Fig. 3a) exhibited a much denser and more uniform distribution of CF3⁻, CF2⁻, CF⁻, and LiF⁻. These species together form a composite positive electrode-electrolyte interphase featuring an outer layer of C–F groups above an inner LiF-rich layer, indicating the formation of well-structured fluorinated positive electrode-electrolyte interphase components (Supplementary Figs. 13–15)34–36. Concurrently, LiCl distribution within this engineered positive electrode-electrolyte interphase was reduced and more uniform, suggesting better control over its deposition, which was further corroborated by post-cycling X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of the positive electrode (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). Two-dimensional TOF-SIMS images also clearly visualized the distributions and relative amounts of these anionic fragments (Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19). Collectively, these results confirm that the TSSR strategy promotes the formation of a cohesive, F-rich positive electrode-electrolyte interphase on the positive electrode surface. This well-defined interphase, with its outer C–F layer and inner LiF layer uniformly integrated with the positive electrode, not only minimizes electrolyte decomposition but also regulates lithium salt deposition, ensuring a more stable and sustainable interface under harsh cycling conditions (Fig. 3b).

To further validate the chemical nature of the TSSR-derived positive electrode-electrolyte interphase, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted on cycled positive electrodes. The S 2p spectra (Supplementary Fig. 20) revealed multiple sulfur species in both charged and discharged states. In the baseline electrolyte, sulfur tended to transition unevenly towards higher oxidation states, resulting in a broader distribution of partially oxidized byproducts and increased parasitic reactions. In contrast, in the TSSR electrolyte, peaks for higher-valence sulfur species were more uniformly distributed, and Cl-bound intermediates were reduced, suggesting a more controlled and reversible redox process. The observed shielding and deshielding effects during charge and discharge cycles highlight how the TSSR strategy, by creating a TSSR-induced reconfigured local environment, effectively stabilizes sulfur-containing intermediates. This stabilization reinforces the positive electrode-electrolyte interphase’s protective function and lessens unwanted side reactions, aligning with the protective positive electrode-electrolyte interphase model suggested by our earlier analyses34,36. Depth-profile XPS analyses of the cycled positive electrode surface offered further detailed insights into the positive electrode-electrolyte interphase’s chemical composition and uniformity. The F1s depth profiles (Fig. 3c, d) show that in cells with the TSSR electrolyte, the positive electrode surface has -CF3 groups, which can repel the electrolyte. OTf− decomposition also generates -CF2 and -CF fragments that form an inner framework supporting the LiF-rich layer. Importantly, a clear LiF signal persists across multiple sputtering depths, confirming a continuous, inorganic-rich inner interphase. Additionally, depth profiles of Li 1s, C 1s, and Cl 2p (Supplementary Figs. 21 and 22) support the composite nature of this positive electrode-electrolyte interphase. The decreasing signal of side-products with more etching time shows that this interphase—resulting from the anion-directed decomposition pathway enabled by the initial solvation sheath reconfiguration—effectively suppresses electrolyte decomposition and protects the positive electrode. Temperature-dependent electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements (Supplementary Fig. 23) also support these XPS findings, highlighting this layered structure’s key role in reducing interfacial impedance and preventing further electrolyte degradation. Plotting the relative amounts of key anionic fragments versus sputtering time summarizes the interphase composition’s overall evolution (Fig. 3e). Consistent with linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) observations of electrolyte decomposition (Supplementary Fig. 24), the data indicate that in the baseline electrolyte, an effective positive electrode-electrolyte interphase barrier does not form. This allows more electrolyte or active chlorine species to undergo parasitic reactions at the electrode surface. This leads to increased buildup of sulfur-containing chloride byproducts and significant compositional variation through the interphase depth. In contrast, the TSSR electrolyte, with its TSSR-formed protective positive electrode-electrolyte interphase, maintains a high proportion of F-containing protective species (like LiF and C–F groups) throughout the interphase. This promotes uniform deposition, increases the exchange specific current (Supplementary Fig. 25), and leads to higher steady-state and initial currents (I0) (Supplementary Fig. 26). Consequently, a greater cumulative charge throughput is achieved (Supplementary Fig. 27). This translates to more efficient ion transport and interfacial charge transfer, enhancing interfacial stability and effectively suppressing further side reactions. These improvements ultimately lead to faster reaction kinetics, lower overpotentials, and potentially quicker reintegration of any benign electrolyte decomposition products during subsequent cycles34–36.

In summary, our comprehensive spectroscopic and electrochemical characterizations clearly show that the TSSR strategy yields a uniform, F-rich positive electrode-electrolyte interphase. This engineered interphase stabilizes the positive electrode interface by fundamentally changing Li+ coordination. This change directs the decomposition pathways of electrolyte components and suppresses parasitic reactions. Such an optimized interphase is crucial for improving ion transport and interfacial charge transfer. This enables the battery to cycle stably for over 600 cycles at 25 °C at a specific current of 2000 mA g−1 (Supplementary Fig. 28), substantially enhancing both reaction stability and cycling performance at ambient temperatures. These findings on the positive electrode-electrolyte interphase’s chemical composition and protective mechanisms lay a robust foundation for our subsequent examination of its specific microstructural and morphological characteristics.

Morphological characterization of the positive electrode and positive electrode-electrolyte interphase

Having established the chemical nature and TSSR-driven formation of the TSSR-derived positive electrode-electrolyte interphase (Fig. 3), we next used complementary surface characterization techniques to examine its physical structure and morphological changes during cycling (Fig. 4). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) first revealed nanoscale topographical details. In the baseline electrolyte, the positive electrode surface showed significant height variations and a highly uneven topography, indicating substantial parasitic deposits and increased polarization (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 29). In sharp contrast, in the TSSR electrolyte, the resulting positive electrode-electrolyte interphase dramatically smoothed the surface. This suggests the formation of a more uniform and protective positive electrode-electrolyte interphase that effectively limits uncontrolled electrolyte decomposition (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 30)37,38.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) provided further support for these AFM findings, offering higher-resolution microstructural details. In the baseline electrolyte, the interphase on the positive electrode was visibly thick, porous, and loosely bound, making it prone to cracking and delamination during electrochemical cycling (Fig. 4c). This unfavorable morphology remained even after 500 cycles under the same conditions, with the positive electrode exhibited a clearly heterogeneous interface (Supplementary Fig. 31). In contrast, cells with TSSR electrolyte consistently showed a notably compact and thin interphase, whether cycled for 100 or 500 times. Such a stable and well-defined interphase is key to reducing parasitic reactions and interfacial impedance (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 31). Importantly, even after 100 cycles at a demanding –40 °C, cells with TSSR electrolyte kept this thin and compact positive electrode-electrolyte interphase. This highlights the robust and consistent formation of the TSSR-derived interphase over a wide temperature range (Supplementary Fig. 32), a feature important for sustainable operation in varied climates. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images complemented the AFM and TEM findings, clearly showing the macroscopic differences in surface morphology. Positive electrodes cycled in the baseline electrolyte were covered with nonuniform, bulk-like deposits, typical of uncontrolled interfacial reactions and significant byproduct buildup (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 33). Even at –40 °C, where LiCl nucleation rates might change, substantial particulate side products still formed and gathered at the interface, adding to cell polarization (Supplementary Figs. 34 and 35). In stark contrast, the TSSR strategy ensured the positive electrode surface remained notably uniform and largely free of significant side-product deposits at both 25 and –40 °C. This confirmed the formation of a stable and protective positive electrode-electrolyte interphase (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Figs. 33 and 35), which is essential for reliable and sustainable battery function.

Collectively, these multi-scale morphological analyses clearly show that the TSSR strategy not only reconfigures the ternary solvation sheath but, critically, also directs the formation of a durable, uniform, and F-rich interphase (Supplementary Fig. 36). This engineered positive electrode-electrolyte interphase, stemming directly from the initial solvation sheath reconfiguration, plays a key role in reducing the Li+ desolvation barrier and improving ion transport. This is further substantiated by EIS results (Supplementary Fig. 37), which reveal a significant increase in interfacial resistance for the baseline cell after extended cycling, while the cells with TSSR electrolyte maintains a more stable and lower-resistance interface. These improvements contribute significantly to the enhanced cycling stability and reduced polarization seen in Li||Cl2 batteries, particularly under low temperature, paving the way for more sustainable and resilient energy storage37,39.

Electrochemical performance at low temperatures

The practical operation of Li||Cl2 batteries is often limited under low temperature, such as rapid cycling and very low temperatures. At these temperatures, slow interfacial charge-transfer and increased electrolyte decomposition severely hinder battery performance. Key kinetic bottlenecks include limited Li+ transport and desolvation at the electrode-electrolyte interface. Our investigations into the electrolyte’s solvation chemistry (Fig. 2) have shown that the TSSR strategy (via LiOTf) promotes an effective TSSR, leading to improved Li+ desolvation. This enhanced desolvation is anticipated to facilitate faster interfacial kinetics. The benefits of this reconfigured solvation environment are evident from the initial discharge cycle, where the nucleation overpotential is eliminated, thereby establishing a stable foundation for cryogenic cycling (Supplementary Fig. 38). Based on these findings, we then evaluated how this TSSR approach could support long-life, high-current cycling at low temperatures, a critical aspect for developing batteries with greater operational sustainability across diverse environmental conditions. Consistently, the TSSR electrolyte enabled significant improvements in both high-rate capability and cycle life at low temperatures (Fig. 5a). Specifically, when cycled at −40 °C under a demanding specific current of 1000 mA g⁻1, the TSSR electrolyte maintaining capacity retention of 99.2% and coulombic efficiency of 99.2% for over 1100 cycles. The initial capacity variation observed within the first ≈50 cycles is attributed to the electrochemical formation and stabilization of the positive electrode-electrolyte interphase. During this period, the OTf− anion gradually decomposes to form the protective, fluorine-rich interphase. Once this positive electrode-electrolyte interphase is fully established, a stable interface is provided for all subsequent reactions, leading to the stable capacity plateau. In sharp contrast, the baseline electrolyte cell began to show significant capacity decay and fluctuating coulombic efficiency after approximately 300 cycles under the same conditions (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Figs. 39 and 40). To further validate the robustness of the TSSR strategy, cycling tests were performed at a high reversible capacity of 1500 mAh g⁻1. The TSSR electrolyte demonstrated stable operation for over 200 cycles at −40 °C, a significant improvement over the baseline electrolyte cell which failed within 100 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 41). The stable cycling stability at low temperatures represents a notable step forward for Li||Cl2/Na||Cl2 battery technology operating in such environments, enabling extended operation with high coulombic efficiency even at high specific currents (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Table 4)11,14,20,21. Furthermore, the stability of the TSSR electrolyte was confirmed at a higher positive electrode loading of 4 mg cm⁻2, where it maintained stable cycling for 200 cycles at −40 °C, in contrast to the rapid failure of the baseline cell (Supplementary Fig. 42).

Crucially, during extended cycling, TSSR electrolyte showed substantially lower charge/discharge polarization (0.61 V at 100 cycles and 0.93 V at 900 cycles) than those with the baseline electrolyte (0.92 and 1.86 V, respectively) (Supplementary Figs. 43 and 44). Additionally, the charging plateau voltage lag, which often indicates increased polarization after low-temperature cycling, was significantly reduced from 0.29 V in the original system to just 0.08 V in the TSSR electrolyte (Fig. 5c). These improvements are due to the effective positive electrode-electrolyte interphase formed through TSSR and subsequent anion-directed decomposition. This robust interphase prevents continuous excessive electrolyte decomposition, thus maintaining a uniform interface free of excessive irreversible byproducts. Such an interface promotes better kinetics and a quicker start to the LiCl → Cl2 conversion, supporting our earlier mechanistic understanding.

The robustness of the TSSR electrolyte was further underscored by its performance at a low temperature of −80 °C. Even under these conditions, TSSR electrolyte delivered reliable cycling stability, maintaining consistent performance for over 100 cycles at a current of 200 mA g−1 with a specific capacity of approximately 400 mAh g−1 (Fig. 5d). This clearly demonstrates the potential of the engineered electrolyte for applications in severe cryogenic environments, enhancing the sustainability of energy solutions in such demanding settings.

Further insights into the internal reaction dynamics and the regulatory effect of the modified electrolyte on polarization and interfacial side reactions were gleaned from differential capacity (dQ/dV) analysis (Fig. 5e). In the TSSR electrolyte, the dQ/dV profiles revealed that the reversible electrolyte redox peak around 3.7 V remained stable over 500 cycles, exhibiting minimal polarization shift (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Figs. 45 and 46). In contrast, baseline electrolyte cells showed substantial polarization, with this reversible peak vanishing after only 200 cycles, indicative of inhibited SOCl2 reformation due to excessive electrolyte decomposition (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Figs. 43, 47, and 48). Moreover, at the high-voltage charging plateau, baseline electrolyte cells displayed prominent side-reaction peaks, confirming persistent electrolyte over-decomposition at high potentials. Conversely, the TSSR electrolyte maintained stable reaction peaks, providing clear evidence that the robust positive electrode-electrolyte interphase effectively suppresses excessive SOCl2 decomposition (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Figs. 45–48). Such stabilization supports the smooth and reversible conversion of active species during long-term cycling, thereby preserving capacity and reducing interfacial polarization, which are vital for achieving sustainable battery operation12,22,40. This stabilization is also reflected in the galvanostatic discharge profiles, stemming from a TSSR-driven capacity redistribution between the two competing redox pathways (Supplementary Fig. 49). The CV analysis confirms the TSSR strategy not only sustains the high-voltage S–Cl species pathway but also significantly reduces the polarization of the main Cl2 → LiCl reaction. This redistribution to the higher-potential S–Cl pathway enhances the overall energy efficiency.

Collectively, these analyses culminate in the interfacial mechanism summarized in Fig. 5f, rooted in the TSSR. The TSSR strategy initiates this reconfiguration by displacing SOCl2 from the primary Li+ solvation sheath and, through its interactions with Li+ and AlCl4−, establishing an anion-bridged, anion-rich solvation structure. This reconfigured environment markedly enhances Li+ desolvation and overall ion transport. Critically, following this TSSR, the high-HOMO OTf− undergoes preferential decomposition at the positive electrode, yielding a stable, bilayer composite positive electrode-electrolyte interphase. This positive electrode-electrolyte interphase effectively suppresses excessive electrolyte decomposition, thereby reducing interfacial polarization and significantly boosting long-term cycling performance. The TSSR-enabled Li||Cl2 system thus benefits from a synergistically reconfigured ternary solvation sheath where LiOTf, LiAlCl4, and SOCl2 interact dynamically. This TSSR-driven synergy not only reshapes solvation chemistry and lowers the Li+ desolvation energy barrier but also critically regulates the SOCl2 decomposition pathway (Fig. 5f). As a result, Li||Cl2 batteries employing this TSSR strategy show better cycling performance and much lower interfacial polarization under low-temperature conditions. This clearly validates our approach to additive selection and interfacial engineering, paving the way for more sustainable and reliable battery operation in harsh environments.

Discussion

We successfully addressed the critical challenge of interfacial instability in rechargeable Li||Cl2 batteries. This instability is largely driven by excessive electrolyte decomposition, a process exacerbated under demanding cryogenic conditions where increased cell polarization can lead to higher operating voltages, further promoting unwanted reactions and typically resulting in rapid performance decline. Our results demonstrate that a TSSR strategy, which leverages LiOTf as a functional component, fundamentally alters the Li+ coordination environment. This change directs the in situ formation of a uniform and fluorine-rich positive electrode-electrolyte interphase with a distinct bilayered architecture. The successful implementation of the TSSR strategy, which guides the formation of this anion-driven positive electrode-electrolyte interphase, is crucial for significantly reducing detrimental electrolyte over-decomposition and stabilizing the positive electrode interphase. These improvements enhance Li+ transport and charge-transfer kinetics, translating directly into electrochemical performance. Notably, TSSR electrolyte achieve over 1100 cycles with 99.2% coulombic efficiency at –40 °C (1000 mA g⁻1) and show robust operation even at –80 °C. Our findings establish TSSR as an effective strategy for overcoming longstanding interfacial and electrolyte stability issues in Li||Cl2 batteries, thereby enhancing their durability and paving the way for their sustainable application in low temperature. Beyond this specific system, engineering functional interphases by tailoring solvation structures offers a valuable approach for designing advanced and more sustainable battery chemistries capable of reliable high performance under harsh conditions. This TSSR strategy represents a platform concept. For instance, in high-voltage lithium metal batteries, a similar approach could deliver a functional anion to the positive electrode interface, forming a protective positive electrode-electrolyte interphase that shields the carbonate electrolyte from oxidation. This highlights the potential of solvation engineering for designing more durable high-voltage systems where electrolyte stability is a critical concern. This work, therefore, not only provides a practical solution for Li||Cl2 technology but also underscores that solvation sheath engineering via TSSR-like approaches offers a promising pathway for developing more durable high-voltage battery systems where electrolyte decomposition is a critical concern.

Methods

Materials availability

Thionyl chloride (SOCl2, 99%, Aladdin), lithium tetrachloroaluminate (LiAlCl4, 98.0%, Macklin), lithium trifluoromethanesulfonate (LiOTf, 99.0%, Aladdin) and lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI, DoDoChem) were used to prepare the electrolytes. All reagents were transported in argon-filled sealed packages, dried, and subsequently stored in an argon-filled glovebox where the H2O/O2 content was maintained below 0.01 ppm. Before use, LiFSI was further dried overnight in a vacuum oven at 100 °C. Ordered mesoporous carbon (CMK-3) was purchased from XFNANO. Commercial activated carbon (AC) was purchased from DoDoChem. Commercial lithium metal disks (diameter: 15.6 mm, thickness: 0.45 mm, purity: 99.9%) were purchased from Canrd. These pre-cut electrodes were stored in an argon-filled glovebox (H2O < 0.1 ppm, O2 < 0.1 ppm) and used directly without further surface treatment or rolling processes to minimize handling artifacts.

Preparation of electrolyte

Two types of electrolytes were prepared and used in this study. The baseline electrolyte (baseline) was prepared by dissolving 1.5 M LiAlCl4 and 0.1 M LiFSI in 1 mL of SOCl2. The TSSR electrolyte was prepared by adding 0.3 M LiOTf to this baseline electrolyte formulation. All mixtures were stirred for 20 min, and the resulting colorless supernatants were taken as the working electrolytes.

Preparation of positive electrode host

The positive electrode was prepared by mixing CMK-3 (XFNANO, 99.9%), Super P (Canrd, 99.9%), and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, PVDF-900) at a weight ratio of 8:1:1. The powders were dispersed in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, electronic grade, 99.9%, Aladdin) using a planetary mixer for 30 min to form a homogeneous slurry. The slurry was manually coated onto pre-cut 304 stainless-steel disks (diameter: 15.5 mm, thickness: 0.5 mm), which had been previously cleaned via ultrasonication in ethanol. Finally, the electrodes were dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 16 h without further cutting. The CMK-3 loading was 2.0 ± 0.2 mg cm−2 for standard tests and 4.0 ± 0.2 mg cm−2 for the high-loading experiments. The baseline positive electrode was prepared by mixing AC (DoDoChem, 99%), Super P (Canrd, 99.9%), and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, PVDF-900) at a weight ratio of 8:1:1. The powders were dispersed in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, electronic grade, 99.9%, Aladdin) using a planetary mixer for 30 min to form a homogeneous slurry. The slurry was manually coated onto pre-cut 304 stainless-steel disks (diameter: 15.5 mm, thickness: 0.5 mm), which had been previously cleaned via ultrasonication in ethanol. Finally, the electrodes were dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 16 h without further cutting. The AC loading was 2.0 ± 0.2 mg cm−2 (Supplementary Fig. 50).

Battery assembly

All cells were assembled inside an argon-filled glovebox with H2O/O2 levels maintained below 0.01 ppm. Coin cells (CR2032, 316 stainless-steel casing) were fabricated by stacking the pre-prepared CMK-3 positive electrode, one layer of commercial glass fiber separator (GF/D, Whatman; diameter: 19 mm, thickness: 675 μm, average pore size: 2.7 μm, porosity: ~90%), and a lithium-metal foil negative electrode. The potential for corrosion of the stainless-steel casing was evaluated via post-cycling analysis, which indicated that it was not a significant factor under the tested conditions (Supplementary Figs. 51 and 52). A total of 100 μL of electrolyte was added in two aliquots to thoroughly wet the positive electrode and separators.

Characterizations

XRD patterns were characterized by a Bruker-AXS D8 Advance. Morphology of the cycled positive electrode were investigated by using scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi Regulus 8230), transmission electron microscope (TEM, Thermo Scientific Talos F200X). Raman spectral was obtained by Raman (HORIBA HR Evolution) of 532 nm wavelength. Surface chemical bonding states of the cycled positive electrodes were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, escalab 250Xi). Depth profiling was conducted with a primary Bi3+ beam of 30 keV and a scanning area of 100 × 100 µm2. Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy was tested by scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi Regulus 8230). TOF-SIMS data were acquired from PHI nanoTOF II Time-of-Flight SIMS. The NMR data were obtained from a Bruker 600 M NEO liquid NMR spectrometer equipped with an iProbe BBFO 5 mm BBF/1H probe with a frequency range of 19F/31P-109Ag. The atomic force microscope (AFM) data were obtained from a Bruker Dimension ICON with an image resolution of 256 × 256. Pore structure characterization nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms were measured at 77 K using a BELSORP-mini II analyzer (MicrotracBEL, Japan) after degassing the samples at 200 °C for 12 h under vacuum. The specific surface area and pore size distribution were calculated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller method and the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda model, respectively. Electrochemical measurements were conducted at a strictly controlled temperature of 25 ± 1 °C inside an environmental chamber (MIHW-200-160CH-B, Neware), with the cells connected to a Land CT3002A battery testing system. For low-temperature testing, the cells were placed in an ultra-low-temperature freezer (MELNG DW-HW50, −40 °C ~ −86 ± 1 °C) while their leads remained connected to Land CT3002A test channels outside the freezer. Cyclic voltammetry (CV), LSV, Tafel analysis, and polarization I–t overpotential-deposition tests were performed on a Gamry Reference 5000 workstation. EIS measurements were performed in potentiostatic mode at the open-circuit voltage (OCV) with an AC perturbation amplitude of 10 mV r.m.s over a frequency range from 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz. A data density of 10 points per decade was used. Prior to each EIS measurement, the cells were rested at OCV for 30 min to strictly ensure a quasi-stationary state. For post-cycling characterizations (XPS, TOF-SIMS, SEM, TEM), positive electrodes were harvested from cells after a specified number of cycles and consistently disassembled in a fully discharged state. The harvesting and sampling processes were strictly performed in an argon-filled glovebox (H2O < 0.1 ppm, O2 < 0.1 ppm) maintained at 25 ± 1 °C. Prior to analysis, the retrieved positive electrodes were gently rinsed with high-purity dimethyl carbonate (DMC) to remove residual electrolyte salts and subsequently dried under vacuum overnight inside the glovebox. To prevent atmospheric contamination, the samples were sealed in a specialized vacuum transfer holder (for XPS/TOF-SIMS) or argon-filled airtight containers (for SEM/TEM) during transport from the glovebox to the analysis instruments. Similarly, for electrolyte characterizations, Raman spectroscopy measurements were conducted using samples sealed in airtight glass cuvettes inside the glovebox, and NMR samples were prepared in sealed NMR tubes to strictly exclude moisture and oxygen during testing.

Theoretical calculations

Density functional theory calculations were performed using the Gaussian 16 software package to obtain the HOMO and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energies, as well as the ESP. All structures were fully optimized at the B3LYP hybrid functional level using the 6–31++G(d,p) basis set for carbon (C), hydrogen (H), oxygen (O), sulfur (S), and fluorine (F) atoms. MD simulations were performed in GROMACS using the general amber force field (GAFF). Topology files and bonded and Lennard-Jones parameters were generated by using the Autoff, while the ADCH atomic charges from the Multiwfn3.8 program were used. The cutoff for the Lennard-Jones potential was set to 12 Å. The long-range coulombic interactions were counted by a particle-particle particle-mesh. The initial periodic systems were set up using PACKMOL. All ions and molecules were inserted in an initial cube box with 10-nm sides. For the TSSR electrolyte, the simulation box was constructed using 2754 SOCl2 molecules, 380 Li+ ions, 360 AlCl4− ions, 100 OTf− ions, and 20 FSI− ions. The simulation box for the baseline electrolyte contained 2754 SOCl2 molecules, 380 Li+ ions, 360 AlCl4− ions, and 20 FSI⁻ ions. In the equilibrium stage of the system, the energy of the simulated system was minimized by the conjugate gradient method first, then the equilibrium simulation was carried out under NPT ensemble for 10 ns. Finally, the simulation was continued for 20 ns with the NVT ensemble and data were collected. Only the final 10-ns trajectory was sampled for the analysis of the RDF and solvent-solute interaction environments. The optimized atomic coordinates and MD simulation structures are provided as Supplementary Data 1.
