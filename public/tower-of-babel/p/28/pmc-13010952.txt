Simultaneous extraction of photoelastic constants and strain profiles from picosecond ultrasonic echoes via spectral analysis
Authors: Motonobu Tomoda, Osamu Matsuda, Takehiro Tachizaki, Richard Wiltshaw, Hirotsugu Ogi, Oliver B. Wright
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present an echo spectral analysis method for picosecond ultrasonics that allows simultaneous determination of the complex photoelastic constant of an opaque isotropic thin film and its acoustic strain profile directly from optical pump–probe measurements with a normally incident optical probe beam. In contrast to previous approaches, which require separate evaluation of the photoelastic constant or iterative fitting procedures, the present method yields both the photoelastic constant and the strain profile from acoustic echoes through a purely analytical treatment. The analysis makes use of both the amplitude and phase of transient optical reflectance changes induced by acoustic echoes and requires only the complex refractive index and the longitudinal sound velocity. To illustrate the method, we apply it to simulated echo waveforms for representative opaque films and show that reliable reconstruction is maintained even in the presence of refractive-index errors, background variations, random noise, and frequency-dependent ultrasonic attenuation.

Introduction

The photoelastic effect—variations in refractive index with strain—can be studied through induced optical birefringence in isotropic transparent materials. This effect has been used in experimental mechanics to image static stress distributions in solids [1], [2]. A transparent specimen placed between crossed polarizers produces a fringe pattern in optical transmission, from which the stress distribution can be determined. In dynamic photoelasticity, the temporal evolution of such distributions can be imaged with millimeter spatial resolution, for example for longitudinal or shear acoustic waves [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. These techniques have a temporal resolution down to ∼μs. Optical phase retardations >2π are commonly obtained.

Similar techniques can be applied to detect stress (or strain) fields caused by surface acoustic waves on nanosecond timescales. In this case, the phase retardation is generally ≪2π, and specialized techniques are required. For example, by exploiting the photoelastic effect, Miyamoto et al. imaged shear-horizontal surface acoustic waves on an electrically-driven filter device at ∼0.8 GHz [8] whereas Yamazaki et al. and Saito et al. imaged surface acoustic waves caused by point-like optical excitation at ∼1 GHz, all of which achieved μm spatial resolution [9], [10].

At higher frequencies, up to ∼100 GHz and beyond, the detection of picosecond strain pulses is possible through the photoelastic effect by means of the methods of picosecond ultrasonics, which make use of the optical pump and probe technique [11], [12], [13], [14], with wide application to depth probing of thin films and nanostructures with sub-μm spatial resolution. In the basic configuration, one measures ultrafast reflectance changes when picosecond ultrasonic pulses return to the surface of the solid, detectable as acoustic echoes [12], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21]. Both the real and the imaginary parts of the relative reflectance change can be monitored, the latter proportional to the optical phase.

Monitoring the shape of picosecond strain pulses is useful for probing stress generation mechanisms (dependent on electron and thermal diffusion) and for characterizing propagation processes, such as frequency-dependent ultrasonic attenuation [22], [23]. Photoelastic interactions enable measurement of strain-pulse shapes in transparent materials by use of oblique optical-incidence techniques [24], [25], [26]. For normal incidence on opaque materials, reconstruction of the strain pulse shape from optical reflectivity variations mediated by the photoelastic effect was independently proposed by two groups [27], [28]. By use of a spectral sensitivity function, they were able to extract the strain pulse shape from the echo shapes. We later proposed a similar method to extract the strain pulse shape using a spectral filtering technique involving the measurement of reflectivity and phase changes [29]. However, all these methods rely on prior knowledge of the photoelastic constants. Gao et al. [30] and Lee et al. [31] used time-resolved x-ray diffraction to reconstruct longitudinal acoustic strain, providing direct access to the strain amplitude. However, such techniques require very cumbersome apparatus and are not easy to implement in a compact setting.

In this paper, we introduce an analytical method to extract the traveling longitudinal strain pulse profile inside an opaque isotropic solid from the transient relative reflectivity and phase changes associated with acoustic echoes, while simultaneously extracting the photoelastic constants required for this analysis at the probe-beam wavelength. The method requires an understanding of how the photoelastic interaction of a normally incident probe beam within the near-surface region distorts the acoustic echoes. With this knowledge, and by exploiting the broadband nature of the acoustic echoes, we show how both the real and imaginary components of the photoelastic constant and the strain profile of the acoustic pulse can be obtained. This obviates the need for an iterative fitting process to determine the complex photoelastic constant, thereby simplifying the subsequent calculation of the strain profile.

Theory

Following our previous approach [29], we describe longitudinal strain pulses as unidirectional plane waves. These pulses are typically optically excited by pump pulses and propagate perpendicular to the surface of an acoustically and optically isotropic opaque material. The material occupies the region z>0 (up to a certain depth) and is surrounded by air with a stress-free boundary condition.

In the case of reflection from a subsurface boundary (i.e., when the material is treated as a thin film), the incident (−z-propagating, ηA(t+z/v)) and reflected (+z-propagating, ηB(t−z/v)) strain pulses combine to yield a total longitudinal strain (1)η(z,t)=ηA(t+z/v)+ηB(t−z/v),which can be expressed as (2)η(z,t)=∫−∞∞A~(ω)e−iωz/v+B~(ω)eiωz/ve−iωtdω,in terms of the strain spectra A~(ω) and B~(ω), corresponding to strain propagating toward and away from the surface, respectively, where (3)A~(ω)=12π∫−∞∞ηA(t′)eiωt′dt′≡F(ηA(t)),where F denotes the temporal Fourier transform, and (4)ηA(t)=∫−∞∞A~(ω)e−iωtdω.Similar relations apply for B~(ω). In Eq. (2), v is the constant longitudinal sound velocity, ω is the acoustic angular frequency, and the tilde denotes complex values. We also assume lossless, non-dispersive propagation over the time interval corresponding to the typical propagation distance of the strain pulse.

Under the assumption of perfect strain reflection at the material surface with reflection coefficient −1, owing to the stress-free boundary condition, the relation ηB(t)=−ηA(t) holds. Noting in addition that ηA and ηB are real-valued, their spectra thus satisfy (5)A~(ω)=A~∗(−ω)=−B~(ω)=−B~∗(−ω),where ∗ denotes the complex conjugate.

Probe light pulses, normally incident in plane-wave form from z<0 and with a temporal duration much shorter than that of the strain pulse, are reflected at the sample surface (z=0), and the modulated intensity is detected as a function of delay time between the pump and probe, typically using an optical delay line. Defining the complex refractive index of the material as n~, and for small reflectance modulations, the transient relative reflectance change can be expressed as [13], [32], [33] (6)δr~(t)r~=2ik0P~121−n~2∫0∞η(z′,t)exp2in~k0z′dz′−2ik0∫0∞η(z′,t)dz′.where P~12 is the relevant photoelastic constant, k0=2π/λ is the optical wavenumber, λ is the optical wavelength in vacuum, and r~ is the complex reflectance of the unperturbed material.1 We assume the photoelastic constant to be independent of optical wavelength over the spectral bandwidth of the probe pulse. For a probe pulse duration ∼100 fs at a central wavelength of ∼400 nm for example, this optical bandwidth corresponds to ∼2 nm.

This treatment assumes that the thin-film thickness is large compared with both the spatial width of the strain pulse and the optical penetration depth of the probe light. Combining Eqs. (2), (5), Eq. (6) leads to the following expression [29]: (7)Fddtδr~(t)r~=4ik0vω2P~12(n~2−1)(ω2−4n~2k02v2)+1A~(ω)≡R~(ω)≡F~(ω)A~(ω),where F~(ω) plays the role of a filter function. In our previous approach [29], the strain profile was obtained from the spectrum A~(ω), which is calculated from the measured real and imaginary components of the relative reflectance change δr~(t)/r~—i.e., the complex echo profile—under the assumption that all other parameters that determine F~(ω), including the complex photoelastic constant P~12, are known. While the refractive index n~ can be determined using standard ellipsometry, measurement of the photoelastic constant requires specialized setups rather than off-the-shelf instrumentation. Consequently, P~12 has traditionally been extracted from acoustic echoes in δr~(t)/r~ using laborious iterative fitting procedures [34], [35].

Here, we demonstrate that the complex photoelastic constant P~12 can instead be determined directly from Eq. (7) by exploiting the property that the strain η(z,t) is a real quantity. Equivalently, this means that its spectrum A~(ω) must satisfy the symmetry condition given in Eq. (5).

We define the function C~(ω) as (8)C~(ω)≡(n~2−1)(ω2−4n~2k02v2)≡CR(ω)+iCI(ω),where CR(ω) and CI(ω) are the real and imaginary parts of C~(ω), respectively. By definition, C~(ω) is an even function, i.e., C~(ω)=C~(−ω). We further define the function D~(ω) as (9)D~(ω)≡C~(ω)R~(ω)4ik0v.Using Eq. (4), D~(ω) and D~∗(−ω) can be written in the form (10)D~(ω)=A~(ω)ω2(PR+iPI)+A~(ω)CR(ω)+iCI(ω),and (11)D~∗(−ω)=A~(ω)ω2(PR−iPI)+A~(ω)CR(ω)−iCI(ω),where use has been made of Eq. (5), and P~12≡PR+iPI.

By adding and subtracting Eqs. (10), (11), we obtain (12)D~(ω)+D~∗(−ω)=2ω2PRA~(ω)+2CR(ω)A~(ω), (13)D~(ω)−D~∗(−ω)=2iω2PIA~(ω)+2iCI(ω)A~(ω).The ratio of Eqs. (12), (13) can be used to define the real quantity Q(ω): (14)Q(ω)≡1iD~(ω)−D~∗(−ω)D~(ω)+D~∗(−ω)=ω2PI+CI(ω)ω2PR+CR(ω).Using Eq. (9), Q(ω) can also be written as (15)Q(ω)=1iC~(ω)R~(ω)+C~∗(ω)R~∗(−ω)C~(ω)R~(ω)−C~∗(ω)R~∗(−ω).Here, R~(ω) is obtained from the Fourier transform and time derivative of the echo transient reflectance change. Given that C~(ω) can be calculated from the known refractive index n~, Q(ω) can thus be evaluated from the complex echo profile. By rearranging Eq. (14), we obtain (16)Q(ω)CR(ω)−CI(ω)ω2=−Q(ω)PR+PI.By plotting Y≡[Q(ω)CR(ω)−CI(ω)]/ω2 vs. X≡−Q(ω), one can determine PR from the slope and PI from the intercept of the linear graph: (17)Y=PRX+PI.The complex photoelastic constant P~12=PR+iPI can therefore be extracted from the echo spectra. A minimum of two acoustic angular frequencies ω are required to extract PR and PI, but for better fidelity in the presence of noise it is advantageous to use a range of frequencies. This linear relation implicitly incorporates the Brillouin oscillatory response, which is intrinsic to the transient reflectance changes. The accuracy of the extracted slope depends on the available frequency span used in the analysis. Although in principle the acoustic strain profile and the complex photoelastic constant could be fitted directly in the time domain, the measured echoes depend on both quantities through integrals over depth, making such fitting cumbersome and time-consuming.

Calculation of the echo reflectance changes

To demonstrate the proposed method, we use analytical formulas for strain pulse propagation in an opaque solid film with a free surface (in practice, in contact with air or in a vacuum), assuming there is no ultrasonic attenuation in line with the proposed theory. The resultant relative reflectance changes are calculated using Eq. (6) for a synthetic bipolar strain pulse in the form ηb(t)=sgn(t)exp(−v|t|/ζ0), consisting of two decaying exponential parts of opposite sign with decay constant ζ0=43nm (0.11λ), and for a unipolar strain pulse in the form ηu(t)=−H(−t)exp(vt/ζ0), consisting of a single decaying exponential part with the same decay constant ζ0 (see Fig. 1(a) and (b)). The probe wavelength is chosen as λ=400nm, and the longitudinal sound velocity is v=4000m/s. Here, sgn(t) is the sign function and H(t) is the Heaviside step function. The bipolar form represents the idealized strain profile generated thermoelastically by an ultrashort optical pulse incident on the free surface of an opaque solid, neglecting diffusion [11], [12], [13], [14]. The unipolar form represents the idealized strain pulse generated at the interface between an opaque film and a transparent substrate when the two materials have identical acoustic impedances [36]. When such pulses arrive at a free planar surface of an opaque film—corresponding to the first acoustic arrival—the one-dimensional (1D) linear wave equation yields the following spatiotemporal forms: (24)ηb(z≥0,t)=sgnt+zvexp−v|t+z/v|ζ0−sgnt−zvexp−v|t−z/v|ζ0, (25)ηu(z≥0,t)=Ht+zvexp−vt+zζ0−Ht−zvexp−vt−zζ0.These apply to the bipolar and unipolar pulses, respectively. The first terms on the right-hand side of both equations represent the strain pulse propagating from deep inside the opaque solid film toward the surface. (As mentioned above, the opaque film is assumed to be much thicker than both the strain pulse width and the probe-beam optical penetration depth.) The center of the strain pulse arrives at the surface (z=0) at t=0. The second terms represent the inverted strain pulse produced after reflection from the free surface and propagating in the depth direction.

The calculated temporal variations of the real and imaginary components of the relative reflectance change, δr~/r~=ρ+iδϕ, are shown in Fig. 1(c) and (d).2 The relevant physical constants are chosen to represent a generic case for an opaque thin film [29]: probe refractive index n~=1.5+0.5i, corresponding to a probe optical absorption depth ζ=λ/(4πIm(n~))=64nm, and complex photoelastic constant dn~/dη=1+1.7i, giving P~12=(2n~)⋅dn~/dη=1.3+6.1i. The echoes exhibit damped oscillations at the Brillouin period τB=λ/(2Re(n~)v)=33.3 ps, with damping time ζ/v=16 ps. The step in δϕ in Fig. 1(d) results from surface motion while the unipolar pulse is being reflected, leading to a phase change in the reflected probe beam due to the associated change in optical path length.

In Eq. (21), both the numerator and denominator of Q(f) approach zero as f→0. Although the limit of Q(f) exists analytically and can be evaluated using L’Hôpital’s rule, doing so numerically would require estimating derivatives of R~′(f) with respect to frequency near f=0, which is highly sensitive to finite frequency sampling and noise in the present data analysis. For this reason, the very low-frequency region is excluded from the fitting procedure.

The effect is more pronounced for the bipolar strain pulse because its low-frequency spectral components are intrinsically suppressed by cancellation between the positive and negative parts of the pulse, which amplifies numerical errors in this frequency range. Nevertheless, the resulting errors in the extracted photoelastic constant remain extremely small (all below ∼0.1%), confirming the reliability of the method for determining P~12.

Certain values of the complex photoelastic constant lead to ill-conditioned behavior in the strain reconstruction procedure. In these cases, the information contained in the measured echo spectra is insufficient to uniquely reconstruct the strain profile. The origin and characteristics of these ill-conditioned cases are discussed in Appendix A.1. The influence of frequency-dependent ultrasonic attenuation on the present analysis is examined in Appendix A.2, whereas the robustness of the method as a function of variations in the probe optical absorption depth is discussed in Appendix A.3.

Influence of noise and background variations

In picosecond ultrasonics, echoes in the relative reflectance change often appear on a non-exponential time-decaying background arising from transient temperature changes caused by thermal or carrier diffusion in the sample. The background signal is usually subtracted in echo analysis using suitable fitting functions; however, such subtraction is not always perfect. Experimental data also include random noise. While this noise can be reduced by increasing the measurement time (e.g., through signal averaging or by using longer time constants in lock-in detection, where reducing random noise by a factor of 1/N requires a measurement time that is longer by a factor of N2), it cannot be eliminated completely. Here, we investigate these effects using three test cases: (i) background variations, (ii) random noise, and (iii) a combination of background variations and random noise. For brevity, only case (iii) is presented in the figures.

Figure 8(a) and (b) show the relative reflectance changes including background variations and random noise for the bipolar and unipolar pulses, respectively. As an example, background variations are modeled as a linear function ρ+iϕ=at, where a=0.001+0.001i ps−1. Any offsets are irrelevant because they are removed by the inherent time derivative. Random noise is introduced as ρ+iϕ=0.2randn(t), where randn(t) is a normally distributed random function with a standard deviation of 1. This represents white noise (i.e., with a flat spectrum). For comparison, the same random noise (using the same random seed) is applied to the bipolar and unipolar pulses in cases (ii) and (iii).

During signal processing, data smoothing over a 40-ps window is applied before taking the time derivative. This smoothing suppresses higher-frequency components, but such components are not used in the later fitting process because the dimensionless values X and Y converge to constants at high frequencies (|f|>100 GHz in this case). Conclusion

In conclusion, we have presented a method for analyzing picosecond acoustic echoes in opaque solids that also allows separate determination of the photoelastic constants, provided that both the real and imaginary components of the reflectance changes measured at normal optical incidence are available. Through spectral analysis, we demonstrated how to derive the photoelastic constants and subsequently reconstruct the strain pulse shape. The method only requires a knowledge of the complex refractive index at the probe wavelength (e.g., obtainable by ellipsometry) and the longitudinal sound velocity, which can be determined from the echo arrival times given the film thickness of the sample. Importantly, the method is shown to be robust even in the presence of errors in the refractive index, residual background variations, random noise or frequency-dependent ultrasonic attenuation.

Applying the method to experimental echoes presents a challenge for future work. Its implementation relies on accurate determination of both the real and imaginary components of the transient reflectance change, which requires careful calibration of the detection system, particularly the phase in the lock-in detection. The strength of the proposed approach lies in the broad frequency range available for echo analysis in the Fourier domain. Possible extensions include applying the method to opaque solids in contact with transparent media or to freestanding layers. The method may also provide an effective route for microscale imaging of photoelastic constants in thin films.
