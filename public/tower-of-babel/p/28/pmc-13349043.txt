Burst wave lithotripsy for the treatment of obstructive ureteroliths in cats
Authors: Adam Hunt, Adam D Maxwell, Jody P Lulich, Michael R Bailey, Kaizer Contreras, Ga Won Kim, Richard Hermes, Marissa Y Torre, Michael S Borofsky, Eva Furrow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUreteroliths are a major source of morbidity and mortality in cats, and limited non-surgical options are available for their treatment. Ultrasound-guided, burst wave lithotripsy (BWL) is approved by the Food and Drug Administration to fragment renal and ureteral calculi noninvasively in awake human patients.Hypothesis/ObjectivesAssess the safety and efficacy of a veterinary BWL device for the treatment of obstructive ureteroliths in cats.AnimalsEleven client-owned cats; 10 with unilateral ureteral obstruction and 1 with bilateral ureteral obstruction.MethodsIn this single-arm clinical trial, BWL was performed under general anesthesia in treatments ≤90 min. Outcomes included apparent fragmentation, ureterolith passage, decrease in pyelectasia or hydroureter, and decrease in serum creatinine concentration. Adverse events were characterized by severity and relationship to BWL.ResultsIn total, 12 ureters with 17 ureteroliths were treated with BWL. Five ureters received 1 treatment, 6 received 2, and 1 received 3, resulting in 20 total treatments. Ureterolith fragmentation was apparent in 11 of 12 treated ureters. Of 10 ureters with follow-up imaging, 8 had ureterolith passage and an accompanying decrease in ureteral dilatation. For the cats with ureterolith passage, the median serum creatinine concentration change was −29% (range, −56% to 28%) at 1-3 weeks. One serious BWL-related adverse event of worsening obstruction from residual fragments occurred, but was solved with retreatment.Conclusions and clinical importanceBurst wave lithotripsy shows promise as a minimally invasive treatment for obstructive ureteroliths in cats. With the system used in our study, more than 1 treatment was often necessary.Keywords: extracoporeal, renal/urinary tract, transcutaneous, ultrasonography, ureteral calculi, ureters, ureteral obstruction

Introduction

Obstructive ureteroliths are a major cause of morbidity and mortality in cats. Medical management with IV fluid therapy and drugs to promote ureterolith passage (eg, diuretics, alpha adrenergic antagonists) has a low success rate of 13%-31%.1–3 Minimally invasive techniques such as endoscopic laser lithotripsy and shock wave lithotripsy (SWL) are not options for cats because their ureters are too small for laser lithotripsy instrumentation and calcium oxalate uroliths in cats are relatively resistant to fragmentation by SWL.4 Placement of a subcutaneous ureteral bypass (SUB) device or ureteral stent is the current recommended treatment for cats that fail medical management.5 Although these treatments have advanced the management of ureteral obstruction, they carry a risk of perioperative mortality and of long-term complications (eg, device occlusion, bacteriuria).6–12

Burst wave lithotripsy (BWL) is a non-invasive transcutaneous (extracorporeal) ultrasound technology for the fragmentation of ureteroliths using sinusoidal pressure pulses that result in multiple small fragments along the urolith’s edge.13 This method uses a handheld ultrasound-guided transducer to deliver short harmonic bursts of ultrasound to produce cyclic stressing that leads to urolith fractures and fragmentation.14 In clinical trials in humans, BWL appears safe and effective for fragmentation of ureteroliths and nephroliths.15–19

In previous work, a BWL system was designed for cats and applied in vitro to fragment calcium oxalate uroliths from cats,14 the most common mineral composition of ureteroliths in cats (70%-94%).7,14,20–22 The BWL system fragments calcium oxalate uroliths into <1 mm pieces, which improves the probability they will pass and thereby relieve obstruction.3,14 Based on data using uroliths from humans and dogs, other compositions, such as calcium phosphate, struvite, and urate, are also amenable to fragmentation by BWL.23 The objective of our prospective single-arm clinical trial was to obtain preliminary data on the safety and efficacy of BWL. Our hypothesis was that BWL would safely treat obstructing ureteroliths in cats.

Materials and methods

Case enrollment and eligibility

Cats were enrolled in this single-arm clinical trial from June 2023 until August 2024 at the University of Minnesota (UMN) Veterinary Medical Center (see Appendix for consent and study approval information). Cats with confirmed ureteroliths were eligible. Although chronic ureteroliths were permitted, imaging (within 30 days) was required to document persistence. Screening diagnostic tests are listed in Table S1. Results of abdominal radiographs and ultrasonography were interpreted by a veterinary board-certified radiologist. Inclusion for treatment required an ultrasonographically-detected obstructing ureterolith measuring between 1 and 4 mm.14 Cats with major organ disease outside of the urinary system (eg, congestive heart failure, diabetes mellitus, liver failure, or neoplasia) were excluded.

BWL and post-treatment management

The BWL treatment occurred within 3 days of the screening visit. On the morning of treatment, cats underwent urinary tract ultrasonography to confirm persistence of the obstructing ureterolith(s) and obtain pretreatment measurements (see Appendix). Technical treatment details are provided in the Appendix and Figure S1.

After BWL treatment, the trial covered expenses of inpatient care and monitoring (see Table S1) for 3 days, based on the median hospitalization for cats after SUB device placement.6,10 The decision to shorten or extend this hospitalization and specific treatments provided were at the discretion of the managing veterinarian. Follow-up was scheduled for 2 weeks and, for the first 3 cases (Appendix), 12 weeks post-treatment (Table S1). Additional follow-up was determined by the managing veterinarians.

Outcomes

Efficacy outcomes included ureterolith fragmentation, ureterolith passage, decrease in renal pelvic height, decrease in proximal ureteral diameter, and a >25% decrease in serum creatinine concentration (which was defined as clinical success; see Appendix). Outcomes were determined at 3 timepoints: (1) immediate (at discharge), (2) short-term (1-3 weeks post-BWL), and (3) long-term (1-6 months post-BWL). Because of the progressive nature of chronic kidney disease,24,25 the long-term timepoint for serum creatinine concentration was selected as the last measurement within 6 months of treatment. For cats that received more than one BWL treatment, outcomes were determined after the final treatment.

Adverse events were recorded and classified as described in the Appendix.

Statistical analysis

The probability of event detection for 10 cats was calculated (see Appendix). For continuous variables, median was calculated with range, interquartile range (IQR), or both. For categorical variables, counts were determined. The Wilson score method was used to calculate 95% CIs.

Treatment description

BWL treatment

The site with the obstructing ureterolith(s) was treated once for 5 ureters, twice for 6 ureters, and 3 times for one ureter, resulting in 20 total treatments. Treatment duration was a median of 70 min (range, 50-90 min). Ureterolith positioning within the treatment acoustic window (Figure S1D) was sometimes difficult to maintain. Thus, the total exposure time of the ureterolith to BWL treatment could not be definitively determined and was estimated to be less than the treatment duration. For the cats that received multiple treatments, the median time between treatments was 4 days (IQR, 3-9 days; range, 1-27 days).

Other treatments

The cats were hospitalized for a median of 3 days (IQR, 2-5 days; range, 1-6 days) after BWL treatment. Post-BWL, all cats received IV fluid therapy (median rate, 56 mL/kg/day; range, 44-80 mL/kg/day). All cats also received ureteral relaxants (9 prazosin, 2 amlodipine) for medical expulsive treatment; in 9 of 11 cats, this treatment was continued beyond hospitalization. Other common treatments after the 20 total BWL treatments were antiemetic medications (8), appetite stimulants (5), and pain medications (17). Three cats received IV antimicrobial treatment (enrofloxacin [Baytril, antibacterial injectable solution 2.27%, Elanco, Indianapolis, IN]) in hospital for pyelonephritis (2 suspected, 1 confirmed based on renal aspirate cytology). Two of these cats were changed to PO treatment (marbofloxacin [Zenequin, Zoetis Inc., Parsippany, NJ]) at the time of discharge; antimicrobials were discontinued in the third cat because of negative urine microbial culture. An additional cat was treated empirically with antimicrobials (IV Baytril and ampicillin sodium and sulbactam sodium [Unasyn, Pfizer Inc., New York, NY]) followed by PO marbofloxacin (Zenequin, Zoetis Inc., Parsippany, NJ) for aspiration pneumonia. Four cats received corticosteroid treatment (dexamethasone sodium phosphate at 0.05-0.07 mg/kg IV q24) in hospital, based on limited data from human patients that it might promote ureterolith passage when used in combination with alpha blockers.26 One cat received its historically administered budesonide for chronic enteropathy.

Ureterolith and other imaging outcomes

Ureterolith fragmentation was observed at 11 of 12 BWL treated sites (95% CI, 65-99; Video S1 and Figure 2). No ureter had full clearance of ureterolith fragments during the BWL treatment. Six ureters had passage of the treated ureterolith(s) at the short-term timepoint (documented a median of 9 days after the final BWL treatment; range, 2-15 days). This response occurred after a single BWL treatment for 2 ureters and after 2 BWL treatments for 4 ureters. Two additional ureters had clearance of the obstructing ureterolith by the time of last follow-up (days 52 and 256 post-BWL treatment). Therefore, the overall rate of passage for obstructing ureteroliths by last follow-up was 8 of 10 ureters (80%; 95% CI, 49-94). One of the ureters with persistent obstructing ureteroliths was in a cat with intraluminal soft-tissue opacity material noted at the level of the obstruction before treatment (cat 3). This cat was euthanized 484 days after BWL treatment at a different clinic with a statement in the medical records that abdominal ultrasonographic findings were consistent with urothelial neoplasia of the ureter and kidney (images and radiology report not available; no necropsy). The other cat with persistent obstructing ureteroliths (cat 4) remained obstructed at last follow-up (409 days after BWL). If the 2 cats without outcome data (because of euthanasia [cat 1] and bilateral SUB device placement [cat 6]) are also assumed to be treatment failures, the overall ureterolith passage rate was 8 of 12 ureters (67%; 95% CI, 39-86).

Changes in renal pelvic height and proximal ureteral diameter were determined based on the last available imaging, which occurred at a median of 18 days (IQR, 13-63; range, 2-134 days) after the final BWL treatment. The medians and ranges for proximal ureteral diameter and renal pelvic height pre- versus post-treatment are provided in Table 2 for cats with successful passage of the obstructing ureterolith(s); individual data for all cats are presented in Table S2. The proximal ureteral diameter decreased from pretreatment in all 8 ureters that had passage of the obstructing ureterolith(s). The renal pelvic height also decreased on the ipsilateral side for 7 of these treated sites. The site with an improvement in the ureteral diameter but not the pelvic height was reimaged at only one week after BWL treatment, and full passage of the ureterolith did not occur until a later timepoint. The 2 ureters with persistent obstructing ureteroliths had an increase in both proximal ureteral diameter and renal pelvic height (cats 3 and 4).

Biochemical outcomes

Data for cats with successful passage of the obstructing ureterolith(s) are shown in Table 2, and individual data for all cats are presented in Table S2. All cats had immediate decreases in serum creatinine concentration after BWL treatment with a median change of -39% (IQR, −43% to −27%; range, −58% to −5%) at the time of discharge from the hospital. For the 8 cats with short-term outcome data, the overall median change in serum creatinine concentration was −22% (IQR, −43% to 4%; range, −56% to 195%). The serum creatinine concentration decreased relative to pretreatment in 6 cats, and 4 met the criteria for clinical success (>25% decrease) at this timepoint. All 6 of these cats were in the group with successful passage of the obstructing ureterolith(s). The other 2 cats had substantial increases in serum creatinine concentration (>25%). One of these cats had resolution of the obstruction but suspected pyelonephritis based on bacteriuria and pyuria (cat 9); a urine culture was not performed, and empirical antimicrobial treatment was administered by the primary veterinarian. The other cat had persistent ureteral obstruction with worsened hydroureter and pyelectasia compared with pretreatment (cat 4).

Long-term (1-6 month) follow-up data were available for 9 cats (Figure 1) at a median of 114 days (IQR, 88-152 days; range, 44-158 days). The overall median change in serum creatinine concentration was −17% (IQR, −49% to 3%). Serum creatinine concentration was lower than before BWL treatment for 6 cats, including 5 with successful passage of the obstructing ureterolith and 1 with persistent obstruction; 4 met the criteria for clinical success. The same 2 cats that had worsened at the short-term timepoint remained worsened at last follow-up. The final cat had a relatively static serum creatinine concentration (change of 3%).

Adverse events

Probable relationship to BWL

One cat had worsening of the ureteral obstruction 5 days after BWL treatment with progressive azotemia (serious event; cat 2). This cat had 3 ureteroliths at the treatment site. Ultrasonographic and radiographic evaluation showed evidence that fragments from the 2 more proximal ureteroliths had accumulated around the more distal ureterolith. After a second treatment at this site, all ureterolith fragments passed.

Unlikely-to-possible relationship to BWL

Eight cats had moderate-to-serious adverse events deemed unlikely or possibly related to BWL treatment. One cat was diagnosed with a urethral obstruction 2 days post-BWL treatment (serious event; cat 1). The cat had a history of cystoliths and a previous urethral obstruction 2 weeks before BWL treatment (obstruction spontaneously resolved without intervention). The cat was euthanized, and a necropsy determined that the BWL-treated ureterolith had fragmented into 2 pieces (Figure S2A-C) that were still present within the ureter (calcium phosphate carbonate composition). Ureteral pathology showed mild lymphoplasmacytic inflammation at the treatment site with no evidence of necrosis, stricture, or ureteral rupture. Similar inflammatory changes were noted in the untreated left ureter. Numerous pseudouroliths composed of dried solidified blood were present in the bladder (Figure S2D); no material was identified in the urethra. The cat additionally had a saddle thrombus, bilateral thyroid follicular adenomas, and concentric cardiomyopathy (suspected thyrotoxic).

Two cats were diagnosed with obstructing ureteroliths on the contralateral, untreated side (serious events). For 1 cat, this event occurred 4 days after BWL treatment (cat 6). Because evidence of persistent obstruction still was present on the treated side, the owner elected surgical placement of bilateral SUB devices. For the other cat, this event occurred 214 days after BWL treatment (cat 9), and euthanasia was elected.

One cat developed a new ureterolith at 14 days post-BWL treatment that was noted to have moved from the renal pelvis into the ureter on the treated side (moderate; possibly related to BWL treatment; cat 8). Another cat had suspected retrograde movement of the ureterolith fragments (mild; possibly related to BWL treatment; cat 9).

Two cats had suspected pyelonephritis during follow-up that resolved with antimicrobial treatment (moderate, unlikely related to BWL treatment; diagnosed at 18 [cat 9] and 135 days [cat 7]); one of these cats had a history of suspected pyelonephritis pretreatment (cat 7). One cat developed aspiration pneumonia that resolved with antimicrobial treatment (moderate, unlikely related to BWL treatment; cat 10).

All other adverse events were mild. These included subclinical bacteriuria (2, including the cat with bilateral SUB devices), transient elevation of the third eyelid after prazosin administration (3), decreased appetite (6, also present before treatment in all), and hematuria (3, also present before treatment in 2).

Discussion

In our trial, we found that BWL can safely and effectively fragment obstructing ureteroliths in cats. Ureteroliths were confirmed to pass in 8 cats, but more than 1 treatment was often necessary. Despite most cats having evidence that the obstruction was chronic, improvements in pyelectasia, hydroureter, and serum creatinine concentration were common. Although one serious complication occurred (worsening of obstruction from fragments), BWL did not appear to directly damage the ureter. These results indicate that BWL can be considered as a minimally invasive treatment for obstructing ureteroliths in cats.

The success of BWL in fragmenting ureteroliths in cats was high. Follow-up ultrasonography and radiographic imaging identified passage of the obstructing ureterolith in 8 of 10 ureters with available long-term data. These outcomes might not reflect those of BWL treatment alone because cats received at least short-term medical expulsive treatment with medications such as prazosin or amlodipine. These additional treatments might have contributed to urolith fragment passage. However, it is unlikely that passage would have occurred with medical expulsive treatment alone. Although the success of medical expulsive treatment is reported at 13%-31%, uroliths that pass are generally small (median, 1.1-1.9 mm in diameter) and distal.1–3 For comparison, the median ureterolith diameter in our study was 2.9 mm (range, 1.8-3.7 mm), and the location was distal for only 2 of 8 ureters with successful ureterolith passage.

An important factor in interpreting the success rate is that most obstructing ureteroliths (7 of 12) received more than 1 BWL treatment. Retreatment is also sometimes needed with SWL in dogs.27 This need for repeat BWL may reflect inefficiencies of the current system for treating large urolith burdens or technical difficulties maintaining the ureterolith at the target site throughout treatment. To address these issues, future studies could test a system with coregistered targeting that utilizes advanced ultrasound exposure strategies that improve the effectiveness in treating uroliths in human patients.28–30 Training in ultrasonography and, more specifically, practice maintaining the ureterolith consistently within the focal zone might also improve fragmentation rates.

In the cats with passage of the treated ureteroliths, imaging findings post-treatment showed a decrease in proximal ureteral diameter. The median post-treatment ureteral diameter and pelvic height measurements were higher than what is reported in cats with chronic kidney disease.31,32 Interpretation of these ultrasonographic findings is complicated by the variable timing of follow-up imaging with some cats re-evaluated only at short-term timepoints.

Serum creatinine concentrations decreased after BWL in all cats at the time of discharge and remained decreased at long-term follow-up in 6 of 9 cats with available data. Most ureteral obstructions in the cats in our trial were chronic. Thus, the capacity for improvement in pyelectasia, hydroureter, and indicators of renal function might be impeded by permanent damage sustained from the obstructive nephropathy.33 It is also possible cats had partial ureteral strictures.

Overall, BWL was well tolerated by the cats in our trial. No intra-procedural complications were observed. Additionally, follow-up ultrasonography did not identify evidence of soft tissue injury or damage attributable to BWL treatment. These findings are consistent with prior preclinical safety data on BWL in porcine models and data from clinical trials in human patients.14–19,34,35 Furthermore, because renal parenchymal damage in porcine models is minimal and complications are not reported with nephrolith treatment in humans,14–19,34,35 treatment of obstructing nephroliths (eg, located at the ureteropelvic junction) is likely safe in cats. The single major adverse event with a probable relationship to BWL treatment was worsening obstruction after initial treatment with retreatment needed to restore patency. This occurrence highlights the potential risk of partial fragmentation causing transient worsening. Obstruction from passage of urolith fragments is also a complication with SWL for treatment of nephroliths or ureteroliths in dogs.27 Given the risk for obstruction from urolith fragments, treatment of non-obstructing nephroliths is not recommended in cats, unless a stent is placed to maintain patency.

One cat in our study had suspected retrograde migration of ureterolith fragments post-BWL treatment. This event previously has been described in cats and dogs spontaneously and post-surgery.36 Retrograde movement suggests an abnormality with normal ureteral peristalsis,37 which we hypothesize occurred secondary to ureteral dilatation and injury from the obstructing ureterolith. In a different cat, the opposite situation occurred with movement of a nephrolith into the ureter post-BWL treatment, which was thought possibly to be related to BWL treatment because of pressure and flow changes in the renal pelvis. New ureteral obstructions from ureteroliths also were diagnosed on the contralateral side in 2 cats. Recurrences are a common feature of cats with ureteroliths. In cats undergoing unilateral placement of SUB devices, obstruction of the contralateral side occurs in 9% during long-term follow-up.12 In cats undergoing ureteral stenting or surgery, 16% had reobstruction from a ureterolith during the follow-up period.7 Thus, cats with previously treated obstructions from ureteroliths should be considered at risk for new ureteroliths.

The rate of post-treatment bacteriuria observed in our study was comparable to that seen in cats undergoing other surgical interventions. In cats treated with placement of SUB devices, ureteral stents, or ureteral surgery, bacteriuria was reported in 25%-32% at the time of hospitalization and in 14%-50% during follow-up.6,7,11 Urosepsis, a rare complication of SWL,38 was not observed here or in the BWL clinical trials in humans.15–19

A limitation of our study is that it was a small, single-arm trial. The small sample size and lack of a control group prevent conclusions regarding the comparative efficacy of BWL relative to medical or surgical treatments. The wide CIs surrounding efficacy metrics reflect these limitations. Furthermore, study enrollment might have been biased toward cats that were relatively stable at presentation, limiting generalizability to more critically ill patients. As described, ureteroliths were chronic in most of the study cats, which is expected to have negatively impacted return of renal function post-treatment. Additionally, the lack of follow-up data at some timepoints, particularly imaging, complicates interpretation of how clinical variables changed over time.

In conclusion, our study establishes foundational safety and feasibility data for BWL in the management of obstructive ureterolithiasis in cats. The rate of ureterolith fragmentation, tolerability of treatment, and observed clinical improvements warrant further investigation and development of the technology.

Study consent and approval

Written owner consent was obtained, and the study was approved by the UMN Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocols 1909-37415A and 2306-41166A).

Evaluation and events pre- and post-BWL

A timeline of diagnostic evaluations and other events is provided in Table S1. The trial was divided into 2 parts with 2 different funding sources. The first 3 cats were pilot cases, and the study protocol for those cats included a follow-up visit at week 12. The study protocol for cats 4-10 did not cover the expense for follow-up at a 12-week timepoint.

Renal pelvic and ureteral measurements

Measurements of the renal pelvic and ureteral diameters were obtained using ultrasonography, and images were reviewed by a single investigator (E.F.). Renal pelvic height was measured in the transverse plane from ceiling to where the proximal ureter starts.31,32 The proximal ureteral diameter was measured at the point of maximal dilatation.32

BWL system assembly and treatment details

The BWL system was assembled around a microconvex imaging transducer (CF4-9 USP-CF49F2A/WR, Samsung, Ridgefield Park, NJ; Figure S1A) to allow visualization of the ureterolith and accurate targeting during treatment. A silicone coupling membrane was secured over the assembled treatment and imaging transducers using a ceramic-composite ring. The space created between the membrane and transducers was filled with degassed (approximately 2-3 ppm oxygen), deionized purified water through ports.

Patients were placed under general anesthesia for BWL. Patients were positioned in dorsal or lateral recumbency, the haircoat was shaved, imaging gel was applied, and the BWL membrane was placed against the skin (Figure S1B and C). Treatment was applied with 800 kHz pulses (peak negative pressure = −9 to −10 MPa) or 1 MHz pulses (peak negative pressure = −9 to −11 MPa) with the ureterolith focused at a depth of approximately 4 cm from the imaging transducer (Figure S1C and D). Each pulse was 10 cycles in duration, which was applied at a rate of 20 pulses per second during the exposure. The ureterolith was centered on the screen using a vertical cursor line (Figure S1D). The BWL treatment was applied in periods of up to 10 min for up to 90 min of total treatment time or up to 150 min of anesthesia time, whichever was less. Early discontinuation of BWL (before the planned time limits) occurred if the cat was unstable under anesthesia, defined as hypotension, bradycardia, or other systemic disturbances that do not resolve with fluid and medical treatment.

Although the original protocol included a single BWL treatment, a protocol amendment was made and approved by the UMN IACUC after the second cat was treated. This amendment permitted cats to receive additional BWL treatments if the obstruction became worse (as determined by repeat ultrasonography interpreted by a veterinary board-certified radiologist) or persisted 3 or more days after the initial treatment.

Adverse event classification

The study investigators observed each cat throughout the trial for any unexpected events. An adverse event was defined as any observation in a study participant that was unfavorable, unintended, and occurred during or after BWL treatment. The relationships between adverse events and BWL treatment were categorized as probable if located within the BWL treatment region and not readily explained by concurrent disease (eg, ureteral rupture, hemorrhage, swelling), possible if they also could be explained by concurrent disease (eg, acute worsening of azotemia), or unlikely if the signs were caused by disease outside the BWL treatment region (eg, heart failure, aspiration pneumonia). If a cat died or was euthanized during the study, a necropsy was offered. Severity of adverse events was categorized as:

Event detection probability

An a priori calculation was performed to determine the probability of detecting at least one event if 10 cats were enrolled with an event probability (eg, adverse event or clinical success) of 20%. This was calculated using the basic probability of events formula, 1 − Pno event (1 − [1 − 0.20]n), and was found to be 89%.
