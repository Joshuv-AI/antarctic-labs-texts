Seeds of discord: Unravelling family conflict, parenting, and child externalizing behaviours
Authors: Rebecca J. Douglas, William L. Coventry, Sally A. Larsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExternalizing behaviours—aggression, defiance, and hyperactivity—are often reported in childhood and predict poorer academic, social, and mental health outcomes. Family influences such as interparental conflict and angry parenting shape these behaviours, yet maternal and paternal contrasts remain underexplored. This study used parent‐reported data from Waves 1–8 of the Longitudinal Study of Australian Children (N ≈ 10,000; ages 4–17) to test whether: (1) interparental conflict predicted child externalizing behaviours, (2) angry parenting predicted child externalizing behaviours, and (3) interparental conflict predicted angry parenting. Subscales of conduct, hyperactivity, and prosocial abilities were also examined. Random Intercept Cross‐Lagged Panel Models were estimated separately for mothers and fathers, enabling direct comparison of maternal and paternal pathways. Interparental conflict demonstrated little predictive value, suggesting it functions more as a stable background risk than a dynamic driver of change in child behaviours. In contrast, angry parenting and externalizing behaviours were reciprocally linked. Maternal effects emerged earlier and more strongly than paternal effects. Disaggregating parental roles offers new insights for intervention.

When children struggle with aggression, defiance, or impulsivity, the impact extends far beyond the home or classroom. These actions—collectively referred to as externalizing behaviours and hereafter described simply as externalizing—are disruptive in the short term but also forecast adverse adult outcomes, including academic underachievement, social and occupational difficulties, and justice involvement (Gornik et al., 2023; Odgers et al., 2007; Vergunst et al., 2023). Persistent childhood externalizing also predicts later mental health challenges and psychiatric disorders in prospective cohort studies (Reef et al., 2011). These risks are also evident in national Australian data. The Child and Adolescent Survey of Mental Health and Well‐being provides the country's most comprehensive picture of child mental health. The first survey (Sawyer et al., 2001) identified high rates of behavioural disorders, while more than a decade later the second survey (Australian Government Department of Health, 2015) found that 14% of children aged 4–17 met criteria for a mental disorder with externalizing features, consistent with global estimates (Polanczyk et al., 2015). These data continue to inform Australian policy, with a third survey now underway to update national estimates (Curtin University, n.d.). At the severe end, externalizing is also overrepresented among justice‐involved youth, underscoring its broader social and public health relevance (Malvaso et al., 2022).

EXTERNALIZING ACROSS DEVELOPMENT

Consistent with developmental taxonomies distinguishing adolescence‐limited and life‐course‐persistent patterns (Moffitt, 1993), cohort studies have identified multiple externalizing trajectories across childhood and adolescence. Latent‐class analyses show that while some children escalate, others remain low or decline across childhood (Kjeldsen et al., 2014; Roskam et al., 2013), with similar heterogeneity observed into late‐childhood (Brieant et al., 2025). Persistence of externalizing into adolescence has been linked to familial risks, such as interparental conflict, harsh parenting, and maternal depression (Bista et al., 2024), as well as broader vulnerabilities including socio‐economic disadvantage, difficult temperament, and neurodevelopmental risk (Pinquart, 2017). However, these factors do not fully account for all observed trajectories, highlighting substantial heterogeneity in behavioural development.

FAMILY PROCESSES IN CONTEXT

Within this broader ecological context, two family processes stand out as modifiable targets of intervention: interparental conflict and angry parenting. Interparental conflict refers to hostile or poorly resolved interactions between parents that can threaten a child's emotional security within the family system (Davies & Cummings, 1994). Angry parenting refers to emotionally reactive parenting characterized by anger, hostility, and coercive responses towards the child during discipline or conflict (Patterson, 1982). As family‐level processes, these influences are more proximal than distal factors such as socio‐economic status or child temperament. Longitudinal evidence consistently links sustained interparental conflict with heightened externalizing (Zemp et al., 2024), and harsh parenting with increased conduct problems over time (Speyer et al., 2022). Together, these processes (Paths 1 and 2; Figure 1) underpin this study's primary hypotheses.

Importantly, these processes are not merely predictors. A balanced view of family dynamics requires testing whether interparental conflict also fuels angry parenting (Path 3; Figure 1). Beyond the direct pathway from conflict to externalizing (Path 1), conflict may erode parental patience, warmth, and consistency, indirectly influencing externalizing through parenting (Paths 3 then 2) (Krishnakumar & Buehler, 2000; Lee & Lee, 2024). Considering these indirect effects adds depth and defines this study's third hypothesis.

THEORETICAL ACCOUNTS

Associations between interparental conflict, parenting, and externalizing can be interpreted through two prominent frameworks: the spillover hypothesis (Erel & Burman, 1995) and the transactional model of development (Sameroff, 2009). Spillover proposes unidirectional effects, as shown in Figure 1, whereas the transactional model emphasizes reciprocity, with externalizing also influencing parenting. From a transactional perspective, Path 2 operates bidirectionally, creating ongoing cycles of risk (see Figure 2).

Spillover theory

Spillover accounts explain how interparental conflict affects children indirectly through parenting (Paths 3 then 2; Figure 1) but offer little insight into the direct pathway from conflict to externalizing (Path 1). Other unidirectional frameworks address this gap. The cognitive‐contextual framework (Grych & Fincham, 1990) conceptualizes conflict as threatening from a child's perspective, prompting heightened threat appraisals and self‐blame, while the emotional security hypothesis (Davies & Cummings, 1994) emphasizes how conflict undermines a child's sense of security in family relationships. Both these accounts align with the spillover theoretical perspective since they propose that family processes directly precede externalizing behaviours. The cognitive‐contextual framework and the emotional security hypothesis provide theoretical explanations for why spillover effects might be expected when examining the effects of family‐level processes (including parenting) on child behaviour.

Some longitudinal evidence also supports spillover effects. Recent research shows that (1) sustained conflict is associated with elevated externalizing over time (Zemp et al., 2024), (2) harsher parenting predicts higher conduct problems, with some reciprocity (Speyer et al., 2022), and (3) couple conflict predicts heightened stress and less supportive parenting (Lee & Lee, 2024). Encouragingly, improvements in parenting have also been linked to reductions in externalizing, even in families facing multiple psychosocial risks (Zhang et al., 2020). This parent‐to‐child logic also remains influential in applied prevention contexts, where parent‐focused interventions commonly aim to reduce child behaviour problems by modifying parenting practices (Sanders, 1999).

Transactional accounts

By contrast, transactional perspectives (Sameroff, 2009) emphasize bidirectional effects, recognizing that child behaviour can also shape parenting (Path 2; Figure 2). Recent longitudinal analyses using both traditional Cross‐Lagged Panel Models (CLPM) and the newer Random Intercept Cross‐Lagged Panel Model (RI‐CLPM; Hamaker et al., 2015) suggest that reciprocity varies by age. In early childhood, parenting quality and externalizing predict each other across adjacent years (Ahmad et al., 2024; Demmer et al., 2018). In middle childhood, increases in parenting stress and externalizing continue to show reciprocal prediction (Van Dijk et al., 2022), and in late childhood advanced longitudinal analyses have also identified bidirectional links between conflict and externalizing (Aaron & Black, 2025). During adolescence, these reciprocal effects appear to strengthen further according to twin‐difference analyses (Kullberg et al., 2024). Collectively, these findings indicate that parent‐to‐child and child‐to‐parent pathways coexist, with relative strength shifting across development and context.

Coercion theory provides one mechanism through which such transactional patterns may become entrenched. From this perspective, child defiance and angry or reactive parental responses can mutually reinforce one another over time (Patterson, 1982). Accordingly, if bidirectional effects between angry parenting and externalizing are observed, coercion theory offers a useful account of how these parent–child cycles may consolidate across development.

LIMITATIONS OF PRIOR RESEARCH

Mother vs. father effects

Many studies combine mother and father effects, despite evidence that their influences differ in both strength and direction. Generalizing across parent roles risks obscuring important nuance. For instance, maternal inconsistent discipline—but not paternal—predicted higher conduct problems (Gryczkowski et al., 2010), and cross‐national evidence shows mothers' parenting behaviours exert stronger effects than fathers' (Lansford et al., 2018). Role‐specific differences also appear in research on parental sensitivity, defined as attuned, responsive caregiving. Externalizing predicted later declines in maternal sensitivity, whereas father–child relations were reciprocal. Maternal sensitivity appeared more influential in early childhood, with father–child effects strengthening later (Scott et al., 2018; Zvara et al., 2018). Despite this, few studies directly compare mothers and fathers, leaving a gap in contemporary research.

Methodological limitations

Beyond parent‐role differences, key questions remain about the timing and direction of family processes. Many studies rely on correlational, cross‐sectional or between‐persons designs, which risk confusing stable differences between families (e.g., some families consistently report more conflict) with actual within‐family change over time (Hamaker et al., 2015). Because family conflict cannot be ethically manipulated, longitudinal designs are essential for clarifying directionality. Research also shows that once stable family characteristics are controlled, effects often weaken or rebalance (Morelli et al., 2025; Zemp et al., 2018). Without methods that can distinguish enduring patterns from genuine developmental change, it is unclear whether observed associations reflect ongoing processes or long‐standing differences between families. To address this, contemporary longitudinal models provide clearer insight into temporal mechanisms. Murayama and Gfrörer (2024) identify the RI‐CLPM and the dynamic panel model (DPM) as the two most appropriate for testing causality in longitudinal data. Of these, the RI‐CLPM is particularly suited to psychological research, as it estimates time‐ordered, cross‐lagged pathways—linking one variable to another across time—which are further detailed in the Methods section. In contrast, the DPM affords only indirect estimates (Usami et al., 2019).

AIMS AND HYPOTHESES

This study examines how interparental conflict, parenting, and externalizing co‐develop over time, while directly comparing maternal and paternal influences. Australian prevention frameworks typically assume spillover effects, in which interparental conflict predicts externalizing either directly or indirectly through parenting practices (Australian Institute of Family Studies [AIFS], 2017). However, alternative developmental patterns are possible: reciprocal (transactional) effects may emerge, influences may operate in reverse across any of the three focal constructs—some beyond this study's scope—or no meaningful longitudinal associations may be observed. The RI‐CLPM can distinguish among these alternatives by modelling within‐family, time‐ordered effects (Hamaker et al., 2015). Nevertheless, given the strong policy emphasis on spillover frameworks, the present study specifies the following unidirectional hypotheses, aligned with Figure 1. At first glance, a tension appears between the unidirectional hypotheses specified in this study and the broader discussion of transactional perspectives in the preceding literature. This tension is intentional rather than inadvertent. While the literature review acknowledges growing empirical support for transactional models of parent–child influence, spillover‐based expectations continue to dominate prevention policy and intervention design. The present hypotheses therefore reflect the prevailing logic embedded in these applied frameworks, where parental behaviour is typically conceptualized as the primary driver of child outcomes. The analytic strategy was then selected to move beyond assumption‐based inference rather than reproduce it. By using a RI‐CLPM, this study is positioned to empirically adjudicate between spillover and transactional processes. In this way, transactional theory informs the interpretation of possible outcomes rather than the directional specification of hypotheses, allowing the study to evaluate whether policy‐dominant unidirectional frameworks adequately capture developmental processes within families.

METHOD

Participants

Data were drawn from the Longitudinal Study of Australian Children (LSAC; Australian Institute of Family Studies & Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2022), a nationally representative study of child development. In 2003, 10,090 families were recruited in two aged‐based cohorts: the Baby Cohort (B; n = 5107; 48.9% female), aged 0–1 year, and the Kindergarten Cohort (K; n = 4983; 49.1% female), aged 4–5 years. Eligible participants were identified through the Medicare Enrolment Database using stratified random postcode sampling (Soloff et al., 2005). Measures were collected biennially from 2004, using a cross‐sequential design with overlapping age coverage. The present study used data from both cohorts across Waves 1–8 (2004–2018; see Table 1) with samples collapsed together. Retention declined over time, with 72.4% of the original sample retained at Wave 7 and 61.1% at Wave 8 (Mohal et al., 2023).

The study sample was fairly representative of the Australian population. The children were 49% female, with an average age of 4.83 years (SD = 0.23) at the age 4–5 wave. Parent SES, as measured by the SEIFA deciles, averaged 6.00 (2.80) on the scale's range of 1–10, so slightly above the national decile average of 5.5. The prevalence of biological parenting at child‐age 4 was 99.6 and 96.7 for mothers and fathers respectively. The extent of non‐biological parenting at all waves was 3 and 10% in mothers and fathers respectively.

Analyses were anchored at age 4, the earliest point of overlap between the cohorts. Data from age 0 and 2 were excluded because identifiers to verify biological mother–father continuity were unavailable. Beyond this, we note two anomalies. First, while the age 4 wave was included in the analyses, because the child, but not parent, data were missing at this wave, it was the only wave to be included merely to enhance the longitudinal stability (Andersen, 2022). This was achieved by excluding this wave from just the cross‐lagged portion of the model. Second, at only age 16, the K but not B cohort contributed data. This reduced sample size meant only the mother‐report models converged. The father‐report models did not, so they were instead fitted without the age 16 data. This decision maintained acceptable model fit and mother–father comparability across the greatest number of waves.

In the current study, no a priori power analysis was conducted, as the pre‐existing sample size (N = 10,090) exceeds typical requirements for RI‐CLPMs. Access to the LSAC data were granted by the Department of Social Services via the Australian Data Archive [10 February 2025]. Study methodology was pre‐approved by the AIFS Human Research Ethics Committee (AIFS, n.d.). As this project involved secondary analysis of publicly available, de‐identified data, no institutional ethics approval was required.

Interparental conflict

Interparental conflict was measured using two scales, capturing both relationship quality and argumentative interactions. Relationship quality was assessed with the Hendrick Relationship Quality Scale (HRQS), a widely used unidimensional measure with demonstrated construct validity (Hendrick, 1988). Argumentative interactions were assessed with the Argumentative Relationships Scale (ARS), which captures hostility in couple interactions. Within the LSAC, the ARS shows a coherent factor structure and associations with family stress, harsher parenting, and child behaviour problems (Westrupp et al., 2015) and has also been used in AIFS research on child well‐being (Rioseco et al., 2020). Together, the HRQS and ARS provide complementary indicators of interparental conflict.

Angry parenting

Angry parenting was measured with the Angry Parenting Scale (APS), derived from the Ineffective/Hostile Parenting Scale created for the Canadian LSAC (Statistics Canada, 2006). Confirmatory factor analyses support a coherent, unidimensional construct with acceptable model fit across waves (Zubrick et al., 2014). Longitudinal correlations indicate moderate to strong stability across biennial assessments, suggesting reliable temporal consistency. Construct validity is evidenced by associations with child behaviour problems, interparental conflict, and family stress (Westrupp et al., 2015). While not internationally standardized, its demonstrated reliability and validity justify its use as an index of angry parenting in this study.

Child externalizing behaviours

Child externalizing was measured using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ; Goodman, 1997), a widely used 25‐item parent‐report measure with well‐established psychometric properties. The SDQ demonstrates robust construct validity, screening utility, and longitudinal stability across development (Goodman et al., 2000, Goodman, 2001; Hawes & Dadds, 2004; Stone et al., 2010). The SDQ Total Difficulties score served as the primary outcome, indexing overall behavioural adjustment. Consistent with the study's focus on externalizing processes, follow‐up analyses examined the Conduct Problems and Hyperactivity subscales, with the Prosocial Abilities subscale included as a contrasting indicator of positive social functioning, e.g., helping, sharing, cooperation (Goodman et al., 2010). Emotional and Peer Problems subscales were not analysed further, as they were beyond the scope of the present study.

Covariates

Covariates were included to control for alternative influences on child behaviour, ensuring that observed cross‐lagged effects were not artefacts of demographic instability or family structure changes. Selection was guided by research linking poorer child outcomes to parental disruption and separation (Amato, 2005; Lansford, 2009; Van Dijk et al., 2020). Included covariates were child gender, baseline age in weeks, socio‐economic status measured by the Socio‐Economic Indexes for Areas (SEIFA; Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2008), cumulative presence of biological parents, and cumulative exposure to step parent or single parent households. Step parent and single parent exposures were operationalized as the number of waves in which these family structures were present, providing conservative indices of family change over time.

Procedure

Raw data files were merged in SPSS across waves and cohorts using household identifiers, resulting in the n = 10,090. Variables of interest were identified from the LSAC Data Dictionary (AIFS, 2023), and scale scores were extracted separately for mothers and fathers. Only items administered in the same form across all waves (i.e., the same questions asked) were retained to ensure measurement continuity. RI‐CLPMs partition variance into stable between‐family components (random intercepts) and dynamic within‐family processes, enabling temporal associations to reflect family‐specific fluctuations across time. Because such within‐family inference depends on consistent measurement, it was essential that the same individual responded at each timepoint. Inconsistent reporters (e.g., alternating parents across waves) would confound true intra‐individual change. Therefore, families with inconsistent reporters across waves were removed from the sample. Further, families were also removed if we were able to identify a mother or father to be non‐biological, to maintain comparability with prior studies and reduce variability introduced by other caregivers. Although separated families remain an important focus for future research and were encompassed within the analysed sample, their low prevalence precluded independent modelling. Removing these various above‐noted families reduced the sample size by 624, resulting in a sample of 9466.

Missingness

Not all the 9466 families were analysed. Mplus listwise deletes across the exogenous variables, namely, all the covariates. So, families missing information on a single covariate were removed. This removed 3532 families, with 92% of these families (3247) missing on at least SES. This resulted in a remaining sample of 5934 with ‘complete covariate’ information. Families were further removed if they were missing on all the endogenous variables. The number removed here depended on the model but is the difference between the ‘complete covariates’ sample of 5934 and the ‘analytic’ samples reported in Table 3. The range was as follows. For the model of Angry and SDQ in the biological mothers and father respectively, there were 182 (5934–5752) and 948 (5934–4986) families missing on all the endogenous variables, and they were subsequently removed. The resulting analytic samples ranged from 5832 to 4986, at least in the models where we were able to include the covariates (see Model Fit paragraph below). In the models where we were not able to include the covariates, the analytic samples were understandably larger and ranged from 8540 to 6815. To be clear, these analytic samples still contained some missingness on the endogenous variables. However, the extent of this was not pronounced and ranged from 4 to 33% depending on the instrument, wave or parent reporting, with greater missingness on father than mother reporting, and attrition over time across the longitudinal waves of data. This was gradual and even across the waves. Depending on the instrument, it dropped by between 3 and 18% (mean of 11%) between the age 6 and age 12 wave. Ultimately, this missingness on the endogenous variables was managed by using the full information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimator.

Analytic approach

Model structure and rationale

RI‐CLPM analyses were conducted to test the directional hypotheses outlined in Figure 3. Models were estimated in Mplus 8.14 (Muthén & Muthén, 2017) using maximum likelihood with robust standard errors (MLR). Two model forms were estimated across the three hypotheses. Bipartite models tested associations between two constructs (e.g., angry parenting and total difficulties), whereas tripartite models extended this structure to include a third construct when testing hypotheses involving interparental conflict (e.g., relationship quality, argumentative relationships, and total difficulties). All models were run separately for mothers and fathers. To aid interpretation, Figures 3 and 4 present the analytic framework in two layers. Figure 3 outlines the conceptual structure across constructs, and Figure 4 illustrates the statistical implementation of the RI‐CLPM.

Conceptual illustration of the bipartite RI‐CLPM between angry parenting and SDQ Total. To understand the all‐important cross‐lagged paths (solid black arrows), we first explain the white circles. The white circles capture and remove the stable differences between families (e.g., average levels of parental anger at all timepoints), also referred to as the between‐person effects. Put simply, one parent may consistently express more anger across all waves, producing higher average scores than another. The cross‐lagged paths therefore represent how deviations from a parent's average anger at one time point (their within‐person variance) predict changes in total SDQ at the next. Cross‐lagged estimates also assume stability over time—that is, means and variances follow a consistent trajectory. Minor departures from this assumption (non‐stationarity) were corrected using the Andersen fix (Andersen, 2022). Beyond these pertinent parts of the RI‐CLPM, the white squares represent the measured (manifest) variables. The grey circles represent the within‐person latent variables. These contain the residuals left over after the average effects have been captured by the between‐person effects, and they capture the change over time, given the average effects have been removed. Covariates (not shown) were included as predictors of the manifest variables at the first timepoint (Mulder & Hamaker, 2021). The solid curved path represents the correlation between the two between‐person latent variables. The grey solid arrows represent the autoregressive coefficients. Finally, the dashed grey arrows represent the cross‐sectional correlations of the within‐person variables. For further detail on RI‐CLPM pathways, see Hamaker et al. (2015).

All the various paths of the RI‐CLPM are explained in the note below Figure 4, but in the Results, we only report the findings of the cross‐lagged paths (see solid black arrows; Figure 4), given they are the only paths relevant to the research question discussed here. The results of all the other paths were screened and produced sensible results.

Model fit

Model fit was assessed using standard SEM indices: the chi‐square goodness‐of‐fit statistic (χ2), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker‐Lewis index (TLI), and the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR; see Table 3). As expected with large samples, χ2 was statistically significant across all models. Because χ2 tests exact fit and is sensitive to sample size, interpretation focused on alternative fit indices (RMSEA, CFI, TLI, SRMR), which provide more practical benchmarks of fit. Consistent with best practice (Hu & Bentler, 1999), acceptable thresholds were RMSEA (< .06), CFI/TLI (> .95), and SRMR (< .08). All models demonstrated adequate‐to‐excellent fit, except for the tripartite father models (2 and 12), which produced elevated SRMR values when covariates were included. Removing covariates reduced SRMR to acceptable levels (.049), confirming them as the source of misfit (see Table 3). Further checks (e.g., testing covariates individually, moving them to intercepts, freeing residual covariances) did not improve SRMR. Covariates were therefore excluded in final tripartite analyses for both mothers and fathers to enable balanced comparison. Importantly, cross‐lagged effects remained non‐significant in both versions, supporting null findings regardless of the way the covariates were handled.

RESULTS

Overview

Prior to estimating the RI‐CLPMs, model assumptions were assessed in SPSS. Normality for each path was evaluated by running simple regressions, standardizing residuals, and examining their distributions using histograms and normal probability plots. Linearity was assessed using scatterplots with LOESS smoothing, and multicollinearity was tested across the predictor set. These procedures were applied to each developmental interval (e.g., 4–6 years, 6–8 years) for both parent–child and child–parent pathways. Most scales exhibited moderate negative skewness; however, given the large sample size and the use of MLR, this was not considered problematic. Residuals were broadly normal, linearity upheld, and no evidence of multicollinearity was observed. Overall, assumptions were sufficiently met to support structural modelling.

Analyses are organized by hypothesis (1–3). For hypothesis 2, examining angry parenting and child externalizing, the primary analysis of SDQ Total Difficulties is followed by supplementary analyses of subscales—Conduct, Hyperactivity, and Prosocial Abilities—to explore domain‐specific effects. Standardized effect coefficients are reported for all significant paths, with effect sizes interpreted against thresholds of small (β > .03), medium (β > .07), and large (β > .12) (Orth et al., 2024), which are specific to the cross‐lagged pathways of RI‐CLPMs. The full descriptive statistics and correlation matrices for study variables are presented in supplementary materials.

Hypothesis 1

Models 1 and 2 tested whether interparental conflict predicted SDQ Total Difficulties. For mothers, higher relationship quality at age 4 predicted fewer difficulties at age 6 (β = −.041, p = .047), indicating a small early protective effect. None of the remaining 19 pathways were significant for either mothers or fathers. Overall, interparental conflict—indexed by relationship quality and argumentative relationships—did not reliably predict child difficulties, providing little support for this hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2

Total difficulties

Models 3 and 4 tested whether angry parenting predicted SDQ Total Difficulties. For mothers, angry parenting significantly predicted later difficulties from age 8 onward, with effect sizes strengthening from small to large (β = .052, p = .029 at ages 8–10; β = .193, p < .001 at ages 10–12), and remaining large into adolescence. Medium reciprocal effects from SDQ Total Difficulties to later angry parenting were consistently observed across ages 8–14, forming a strong bidirectional cycle (see Figure 5a). For fathers, these associations were smaller, less consistent, and emerged later. Angry parenting predicted later difficulties with small‐to‐medium effects from age 10 onwards (β = .060, p = .050 at ages 10–12; β = .087, p = .004 at ages 12–14). Reciprocal paths from child difficulties to later angry parenting were also evident with medium effects from ages 10–14. Notably, effects from age 16 were unable to be studied, due to insufficient model convergence. Earlier lags, however, were non‐significant, indicating that bidirectional effects were weaker and emerged later than for mothers (see Figure 5b).

Having identified significant paths using SDQ Total Difficulties, questions remained about which domains accounted for these effects. To provide a more nuanced view of parent–child dynamics, follow‐up subscale analyses were conducted in Models 5–10. These examined conduct and hyperactivity, with prosocial abilities included as a contrasting indicator of positive social functioning.

Conduct problems

For mothers, angry parenting predicted later conduct problems across nearly all lags, with only one non‐significant path at ages 8–10. Effect sizes increased developmentally from small (β = .044, p = .039 at ages 6–8) to large (β = .206, p < .001 at ages 14–16). Medium reciprocal effects from conduct problems to later angry parenting were also consistent (e.g., β = .116, p < .001 at ages 8–10; β = .100, p < .001 at ages 10–12). Together, these results form an almost complete bidirectional pattern across childhood and into adolescence (see Figure 6a). For fathers, medium‐to‐large effects emerged later, with significant paths evident from ages 10–14 (see Figure 6b).

Strong bidirectional effects also emerged for hyperactivity, particularly from age 10 onward. For mothers, angry parenting predicted later hyperactivity, with effect sizes increasing from medium (β = .113, p < .001 at ages 10–12) to large (β = .171, p < .001 at ages 14–16). Reciprocal paths from hyperactivity to later angry parenting were also consistent from age 8, with only the earliest lag non‐significant (see Figure 7a). For fathers, associations were again weaker but remained consistent across the pre‐teen period into adolescence (see Figure 7b).

In contrast to the other subscales, Prosocial Abilities revealed an inverse pattern, namely, a negative rather than a positive association. For mothers, angry parenting predicted lower prosociality at later ages, with the largest effect in adolescence (β = −.198, p < .001). Reciprocal effects were less consistent, with only one significant path observed from prosociality to later angry parenting (see Figure 8a). For fathers, effects were comparable in strength but showed a stronger reciprocal pattern. Angry parenting at age 12 predicted reduced prosocial behaviours at age 14 (β = −.130, p < .001), while three consecutive paths from reduced prosociality to later angry parenting were evident from ages 8 to 14. Of the 72 pathways tested for this hypothesis, 44 were significant—most at stringent thresholds (31 at p ≤ .001 and 8 at p < .006). Taken together, these findings support Hypothesis 2 and highlight consistent bidirectional links between angry parenting and child externalizing outcomes across all examined behavioural domains.

Hypothesis 3

Models 11 and 12 tested whether interparental conflict predicted later angry parenting. Across 20 pathways tested for both mothers and fathers, none reached significance at any timepoint. This indicates that neither relationship quality nor argumentative relationships predicted changes in angry parenting over time, providing no support for this hypothesis.

DISCUSSION

This study tested three directional hypotheses using multi‐wave data from the LSAC and a RI‐CLPM designed to separate stable family differences from within‐family change over time. Hypothesis 1 (H1) proposed that interparental conflict would prospectively predict child externalizing; Hypothesis 2 (H2), that angry parenting would also predict externalizing; and Hypothesis 3 (H3), that interparental conflict would predict subsequent angry parenting. The following discussion interprets these findings, beginning with the reciprocal findings between angry parenting and child externalizing (H2), then the null effects for interparental conflict (H1 & H3). We summarize parent‐ and domain‐specific patterns and consider the theoretical, methodological, and practical implications for intervention and future research.

The findings in relation to H2 suggest that angry parenting and externalizing formed a self‐reinforcing loop that strengthened with age (Figure 9; Path 2). Across models, increases in parental anger predicted subsequent increases in child externalizing, which in turn forecast further elevations in parental anger. This pattern is consistent with transactional accounts of development, in which parent and child behaviours mutually adapt over time rather than operating through unidirectional influence alone (Sameroff, 2009). The strengthening of reciprocity across development suggests that these exchanges consolidate into increasingly stable interaction patterns as children grow.

Critically, the developmental timing of these reciprocal processes differed between mothers and fathers. Maternal angry parenting was more tightly coupled with externalizing earlier in development, whereas father–child exchange appeared to build more gradually across childhood. This developmental sequencing aligns with evidence that paternal reciprocity typically emerges later and with smaller magnitude (Scott et al., 2018; Zvara et al., 2018), possibly reflecting differences in caregiving time. These patterns suggest that while transactional processes operate for both parents, their expression may vary according to parental roles, exposure to daily child behaviours, and developmental context. Domain‐level analyses further highlighted these developmental differences. Maternal anger was reciprocally linked with both conduct problems and hyperactivity, whereas paternal effects were more selective and, in some cases, temporally delayed.

While these effects align with the transactional model's emphasis on reciprocal adaptation (Sameroff, 2009), coercion theory (Patterson, 1982) offers a complementary mechanistic account of how repeated cycles of aversive exchanges become entrenched over time. Within this framework, child defiance elicits parental anger, parental anger reinforces child defiance, and these reciprocal contingencies progressively narrow behavioural options for both parties. Longitudinal evidence indicates that such coercive cycles not only persist but also intensify across development, amplifying risk for chronic externalizing trajectories (Ahmad et al., 2024; Van Dijk et al., 2022). It remains possible that paternal effects may have strengthened further into adolescence had the final wave of data been suitable for inclusion in the present analyses.

Analyses of prosocial behaviour provided a conceptual counterpoint by testing whether positive child behaviour followed the same transactional contingencies as disruptive conduct. Results revealed asymmetric sensitivities across parents. Among mothers, higher levels of angry parenting predicted subsequent declines in child prosocial behaviour, but prosocial behaviour did not reliably attenuate maternal anger. In contrast, among fathers, lower levels of child prosocial behaviour more often preceded increases in paternal anger, while paternal anger did not predict later changes in prosociality. These contrasts echo evidence that mothers are typically more reactive to overt misbehaviour, whereas fathers may be more sensitive to the absence of positive engagement (Zvara et al., 2018). Such differences likely reflect distinct parental roles and interaction contexts, including mothers' greater involvement in daily caregiving and fathers' emphasis on cooperative interaction. Mothers, who often assume more direct caregiving responsibilities (Rioseco et al., 2020), may be attuned to immediate behavioural compliance, whereas fathers may gauge relationship quality through unity and shared activity (Scott et al., 2018). Together, these findings suggest that what fuels parental anger is not only the presence of misbehaviour but also the absence of positive relational exchange—a subtle yet powerful nuance that deepens understanding of how coercive cycles take hold.

In summary, these findings indicate that transactional associations between angry parenting and child externalizing differ systematically between mothers and fathers. Maternal pathways were characterized by earlier onset and greater consistency of reciprocal effects across development, whereas paternal pathways were fewer in number, emerged later, and were more domain‐specific. Although fathers also exhibited bidirectional coupling between angry parenting and externalizing, these effects were generally smaller and less stable across waves. Together, these patterns suggest that maternal and paternal influences operate through partially distinct transactional pathways shaped by differences in caregiving roles, interaction contexts, and developmental timing, rather than reflecting uniform parent effects.

When considered together, these findings highlight children as active agents in shaping their developmental environments. Parent–child exchanges operate not as one‐way transmissions of influence, but as dynamic systems evolving through ongoing mutual adaptation. Over time, these exchanges solidify into patterns that either amplify or mitigate risk, depending on how they are managed (Burke et al., 2008; Zvara et al., 2018). From an applied perspective, the intensification of reciprocity during middle childhood (approximately ages 8–10) suggests a particularly sensitive window for intervention. While evidence‐based programs such as Triple‐P appropriately target harsh and reactive parenting (Sanders, 1999), the present findings underscore the potential value of integrating child‐focused components that strengthen emotional regulation and prosocial engagement. In parallel with parent‐focused approaches, programmes that enhance children's emotional regulation could further buffer against parental anger and promote sustained positive exchanges (Ahmad et al., 2024). Addressing both sides of the transactional system may be critical for disrupting escalating cycles of anger and externalizing behaviour before they become established.

Importantly, the parent‐specific anger pathways identified here point to the need for tailored interventions. Because maternal effects emerged earlier and more strongly, programmes should prioritize emotional regulation, positive discipline, and support for managing stressors that heighten reactivity during the early‐school years. Paternal effects suggest that fathers influence children's development primarily through prosocial and self‐regulatory pathways—often cultivated through play and autonomy‐supportive interactions (Scott et al., 2018). Strengthening fathers' positive engagement may interrupt coercive cycles and foster more constructive behavioural development. Interventions acknowledging these role‐specific dynamics and addressing both mother–child and father–child relationships are likely to yield the most enduring benefits.

By contrast, and contrary to long‐standing spillover assumptions, interparental conflict exerted no reliable influence on either externalizing (H1) or angry parenting (H3) once stable family differences were accounted for. Neither the direct (Figure 9; Path 1) nor indirect (Paths 3 then 2) pathways operated reliably at the within‐family level, challenging theories that parental discord inevitably undermines child functioning (Davies & Cummings, 1994; Erel & Burman, 1995). Recent studies have continued to find support for spillover effects (Lee & Lee, 2024; Zemp et al., 2024); however, the present null findings offer a crucial counterpoint, suggesting that stability rather than contagion may better characterize family conflict.

Rather than sparking immediate disruption, conflict appears to settle into the emotional architecture of the home—a stable context rather than a shifting cause. After accounting for family stability, little variance remained to drive within‐family change, mirroring evidence that short‐term effects often dissipate when enduring traits are controlled (Morelli et al., 2025; Zemp et al., 2018). Conflict thus appears to operate as a background family climate rather than a moment‐to‐moment predictor, with its influence on children depending less on frequency and more on how it is expressed, managed, and repaired (AIFS, 2017). In this study, conflict effects may reflect enduring relationship tone rather than episodic tension, as the measures used capture relationship quality and argumentation rather than discrete conflict events. Despite this, although conflict may signal which families are at higher risk, it does not appear to be the mechanism through which that risk unfolds. Practically, couple‐focused support may enhance relational well‐being, but reducing externalizing risk is likely to require intervening where the dynamic risk emerged: angry and reactive parenting.

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS

This study's methodology provides a strong foundation for interpreting the observed family dynamics. Drawing on a large, nationally representative cohort spanning more than a decade, it applied longitudinal models capable of distinguishing enduring family characteristics from within‐family fluctuations. Separate analyses for mothers and fathers enabled cross‐informant contrasts, and domain‐specific models identified variation in reciprocity across behavioural dimensions. Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. The Angry Parenting and Argumentative Relationships scales, while validated within LSAC and used in other research (Gautam et al., 2024; Zubrick et al., 2014), are not internationally standardized, limiting cross‐cultural comparability. In addition, all subscales were completed by parents about their relationships and children. Common method variance can inflate associations between variables, even when different domains are captured (Lance, 2024). Incorporating child‐completed survey items can help resolve this issue as well as allowing children to participate more actively in research that concerns them.

The increasing proportion of missing data at each wave of the study is another limitation that should be highlighted. While the FIML estimator used in the analyses is robust to substantial percentages of missing data on outcome variables, we rely on an assumption that data are at least Missing At Random (MAR; Enders, 2013). If this assumption is not met, parameters may be biased to an unknown extent.

Although powered to detect medium‐to‐large effects, the study was not optimized for smaller ones (Murayama & Gfrörer, 2024). For example, some paternal and early conflict paths showed small coefficients (β ≈ .05–.07) that did not reach significance, likely reflecting limited power rather than true null effects. The RI‐CLPM is inherently conservative statistically (Mulder & Hamaker, 2021); however, model diagnostics confirmed expected distributions, plausible parameters, and stable associations, suggesting non‐significant paths reflect genuine stability rather than statistical artefact.

Several methodological critiques of the RI‐CLPM also warrant mention. The model cannot fully rule out stable, unmeasured family factors such as temperament or genetics (Murayama & Gfrörer, 2024), and omitted time‐varying influences may be absorbed into its stable component (Lüdtke & Robitzsch, 2021). These risks were reduced through covariates that reflected demographic stability and family structure. Some authors note that longer reporting intervals (≥2 years) may obscure slower developmental processes (Hamaker, 2023; Lüdtke & Robitzsch, 2021), meaning findings could differ if the LSAC's biennial design used shorter spacing. Although cross‐lagged effects can appear small, unstable, or reflect regression‐to‐the‐mean artefacts (Orth et al., 2021; Sorjonen et al., 2023), the large, multi‐wave design, repeated measurement, and consistency across parent models support confidence in the validity of the findings.

The present findings should be interpreted in light of the Australian social and policy context in which the data were collected, including the prevention and parenting frameworks discussed earlier. Australia is characterized by relatively high levels of social welfare provision, widespread access to parenting support services, and cultural norms that shape parental roles and expectations. These contextual nuances may influence both the expression of parental anger and the dynamics of the parent–child interaction over time. As such, caution is warranted when extending these findings to contexts with different family structures, caregiving norms, or policy environments. While the transactional processes observed here are theoretically consistent with broader developmental models, their timing, magnitude, and expression may vary across cultural and national settings. Future research using comparable longitudinal designs across diverse contexts would be valuable in assessing the extent to which these patterns replicate beyond Australia.

Building on this foundation, future work should extend the design using longitudinal data with shorter measurement intervals to capture more immediate family dynamics. Incorporating multi‐informant and observational data would reduce single‐reporter bias and strengthen confidence in the model estimates. Examining contextual moderators—such as parental mental health, family stress, child temperament, and co‐parenting quality—may clarify why coercive cycles persist in some families but not others. Collectively, such extensions could identify additional leverage points for intervention and further inform developmentally timed prevention strategies.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrated that the most dynamic forces of family change lie not between partners but within the everyday exchanges between parents and children. These findings challenge spillover assumptions and affirm that parent–child transactions—particularly those involving mothers—lie at the heart of child development. Recognizing this pattern shifts the lens of research and intervention alike, reminding us that meaningful change arises not from resolving partner conflict but from fostering connection and regulation within the parent–child relationship. While the interparental subsystem may define the emotional climate of a home, the parent–child dyad functions as the primary engine of influence within the family system.
