A “paradox of progress” shapes the social determinants of infectious diseases in Vietnam, 1975–2025
Authors: Nguyen Khoi Quan, Andrew W. Taylor-Robinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article discusses key social determinants of health and how they have shaped infectious disease patterns in Vietnam from 1975 to 2025. It suggests a “paradox of progress”: the country’s transition from post-war poverty to dynamic middle-income economy has driven a shift from diseases of deprivation to a more complex profile linked to development itself. Economic growth reduces malnutrition and poverty-related illnesses, yet rapid urbanization, lifestyle changes, and inequality fuel rising chronic diseases and some infection risk factors. Vietnam now faces a “triple burden” of communicable, non-communicable, and climate-related threats, underscoring the need for intersectoral approaches beyond biomedical interventions.

Introduction

In public health, it is a general rule that health varies by social gradient. Individuals in lower socioeconomic positions systematically experience worse health and have shorter life expectancies than those in higher positions1. This gradient does not exist just between the lowest versus highest income groups; it is a continuous phenomenon observed across the entire socioeconomic spectrum. As such, it is the primary mechanism through which social determinants of health (SDH) create unfair and avoidable health inequities1. For example, there is a staggering 18-year difference in life expectancy between high- and low-income countries2, and children born in the latter are 13 times more likely to die before the age of five1. These disparities are socially determined by decision-making processes, policies, and social structures that distribute power, money, and resources inequitably.

Vietnam’s journey from 1975 to 2025 marks one of the most significant development stories of the modern era, transforming the nation from a war-torn, impoverished country into a dynamic lower middle-income economy in a generation. This profound socioeconomic evolution has fundamentally reshaped the conditions in which its people live, work, and age, altering the primary drivers of disease and health3. The country’s health trajectory reflects a profound “paradox of progress”: the very progress that alleviated health burdens associated with absolute poverty has, paradoxically, generated new challenges linked to urbanization, inequality, and lifestyle changes. This perspective examines the fifty-year evolution of SDH in Vietnam and their impact on infectious disease trends (1975–2025). Specifically, it seeks to examine how major socioeconomic and political transformations — from the post-war subsidy era through the Đổi Mới reforms (1986) and into the present middle-income transition — have reshaped the underlying conditions of life and, in turn, influenced the epidemiology of key infectious diseases such as tuberculosis (TB), human immunodeficiency virus/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (HIV/AIDS), dengue, and acute respiratory infections (ARIs). These are among the leading infectious causes of morbidity and mortality in Vietnam over the past five decades, and each represents a distinct pathway through which SDH operate — poverty and economic stability (TB), social context and stigma (HIV/AIDS), built environment and urbanization (dengue), and environmental/health system factors (ARIs)3. These conditions also have robust data and high policy relevance, making them scientifically valid markers for temporo-spatial analysis and global comparison.

Ultimately, the goal is to demonstrate that addressing Vietnam’s “paradox of progress” requires intersectoral strategies that go beyond purely treating diseases, placing sustained focus on upstream determinants in order to build a resilient, equitable health system capable of meeting the challenges of a changing epidemiological landscape.

A nation in transition: Vietnam’s socioeconomic trajectory, 1975–2025

In order to understand the infectious disease dynamics in Vietnam, it is crucial to appreciate how the country has transformed so drastically and quickly in the last fifty years. The evolution of its political economy has fundamentally reshaped every SDH, creating a unique trajectory that has both solved old problems and generated new ones.

The post-war and subsidy period (1975–1985): a legacy of war and hardship

Following the end of the Second Indochina War (“Vietnam/American War”) and reunification in 1975, Vietnam faced a monumental task of reconstruction under conditions of extreme hardship. The decade from 1975 to 1985 was characterized by a centrally planned economy that struggled to overcome the devastating legacies of conflict and international isolation.

Economic context

The weak post-war economy led to Vietnam having one of the lowest average incomes globally, experiencing a sharp deterioration marked by production shortfalls, soaring inflation, and severe inefficiencies in distribution (known as thời bao cấp or “subsidy period”)5. By 1984, per capita annual income was merely $200 to $3005. This state-controlled system provided meager rations that were often insufficient to sustain the population. It is estimated that during this time, 70% of the Vietnamese population lived below the poverty line, with significant starvation6.

Social context

The war’s human toll was immense: 3 million Vietnamese killed and 4.5 million wounded7. The population was overwhelmingly young — over 42% under age 15 in 1979 — and prospects were bleak, with unemployment exceeding 20% by 19875. Food insecurity was widespread; average daily intake in the early 1980s was around 1,850–1940 kcal (below the 2300 kcal recommended minimum), and about one-quarter of children were malnourished5. These hardships were compounded by chemical warfare. The US military sprayed 45 million liters of the herbicide Agent Orange, containing 366 kg of the trace contaminant dioxin, across much of the country to defoliate forests and destroy crops7. An estimated 4.2–4.8 million people were exposed to this extremely toxic chemical, resulting in long-term burdens of chronic disease, cancers, and severe birth defects across generations7. Dioxin’s persistence also caused lasting environmental damage, accumulating in food chains and remaining in contaminated soils and sediments that still require remediation today.

Health system context

After 1975, the well-developed, state-run health system in Vietnam was united across the country8. This system was, in principle, a significant socialist achievement, providing free healthcare services from central to grassroots level through a highly effective network of community health stations9. However, like all other state programs during the first post-war decade, it was severely hampered by a lack of funds. The number of hospital beds and facilities failed to keep pace with rapid population growth, and crucial upgrades to water and sanitation infrastructure were delayed indefinitely. By the mid-1980s, the system was already in a state of crisis, unable to meet the population’s basic needs despite its robust organizational structure10.

The era of Đổi Mới (1986–2014): economic renovation and social consequences

The Sixth National Party Congress in December 1986 marked a historic turning point. Faced with economic collapse and widespread discontent, the government initiated a comprehensive political and economic renewal campaign known as Đổi Mới (“Renovation”).

Political economy of reform

Đổi Mới signaled a fundamental shift away from a rigid, centrally planned economy toward a “socialist-oriented market economy”. This pragmatic reform package combined state planning with free-market incentives. Key policies included: decollectivization of agriculture, which gave farmers control over their land and produce; opening of the economy to foreign direct investment; and deregulation of private business11.

Socioeconomic transformation

The results of Đổi Mới were dramatic and swift. The economy started to grow at an annual rate of over 7%, and by the late 1990s, poverty was nearly halved. Moreover, this transformation was seemingly little affected by the collapse and dissolution of the Soviet Union, then Vietnam’s closest political ally12. In a single generation, Vietnam catapulted itself from one of the world’s lowest income nations to achieving middle-income status in 201011. This economic boom fueled rapid urbanization, with the share of the population living in cities increasing from approximately 20% in 1990 to over 30% by 201413. Throughout this period, education remained a national priority, resulting in impressive achievements, including an adult literacy rate of over 90% by 200512.

Health system transformation

The health sector underwent its own parallel Đổi Mới reform3,14. The fully subsidized model was dismantled and market-oriented reforms were introduced. Public facilities began charging user fees, private medical practice was allowed, and rules on the sale of drugs in stores were reduced9. In 1992, a national health insurance program was started as a new way to finance health care15. This created a mixed public-private health system. While these reforms generated much-needed revenue and increased choice for some, they also fundamentally altered the basis of access to care. The financial burden shifted decisively from the state to individuals, leading to a surge in out-of-pocket (OOP) spending, which roughly accounted for over 50% of total health expenditure10. This engendered significant new inequities, with access to healthcare increasingly determined by a household’s ability to pay.

The modern era (2015–2025): navigating middle-income status and new complexities

As soon as Vietnam reached middle-income status, it transitioned into a new phase characterized by steady growth while faced with new, difficult challenges. From 2015 to 2025, the main goal has been to withstand these complicated times while also aiming to become a high-income country by 2045.

Economic context

Economic growth has remained robust, with gross domestic product (GDP) expanding at rates often exceeding 6% annually16. While structural problems have become more clear, there is concern that the economy could be stuck in a “low value-added trap”, relying too much on manufacturing by foreign companies and not enough on integration with local businesses17. The number of people living in extreme poverty has dropped below 3%, yet economic inequality has grown. The Gini Index has slowly but steadily increased, from 34.8 in 2014 to 35.7 in 2018, then to 36.1 in 202216. This raises concerns about social equity, access to health, and the sustainability of inclusive development18.

Social context

Urbanization has continued to accelerate, with the government setting an ambitious target of reaching an urbanization rate of 45% by 2025, then 50% by 203013. While cities are the engines of economic growth, this places immense strain on infrastructure and the environment, leading to escalating levels of air and water pollution in rapidly expanding metropolitan zones19. This period has also witnessed profound lifestyle changes. The traditional diet, rich in fresh vegetables, is gradually giving way to one that is higher in fatty and processed foods, while sedentary lifestyles are becoming more common, particularly in urban areas. These shifts are the primary drivers of a burgeoning epidemic of NCDs19.

Health system context

The government, once again, has made Universal Health Coverage (UHC) a key part of its national plan. Health insurance coverage has expanded dramatically, reaching an impressive 93% of the population by 202320. However, the system is now squeezed by the “double burden” of disease — needing to manage both persistent infectious diseases and the rapidly rising tide of NCDs. Public hospitals remain chronically overcrowded, often operating at 200% capacity21. There is also a lack of qualified health workers, especially nurses, and the constant need to update its infrastructure and equipment11. In response, in early 2024 the National Strategy for People’s Health Protection through 2030, with a vision toward 2045, was approved (Decision 89/QD-TTg)22. Its main goals are to improve the quality of medical human resources, strengthen primary care, and accelerate the digital transformation to create a “smart healthcare” system.

Vietnam’s health system evolved through path dependency, not clean breaks. The centralized, community-oriented public health system established before Đổi Mới was not erased by market reforms. Instead, market mechanisms were layered on top of this existing foundation, creating a unique and often contradictory hybrid system. Although under-resourced, the pre-1986 system built a nationwide network of commune health stations (CHSs) and fostered a societal expectation of state responsibility for health9.

Đổi Mới in the late 1980s–1990s introduced user fees, privatization, and insurance alongside the public system, driven by fiscal pressures and shifting ideology10. Today, a core tension persists whereby formal commitments to equity and UHC coexist with a de facto situation: market dynamics, high out-of-pocket spending, and commercialization of public hospitals23. Programs such as the Health Care Fund for the Poor attempt to mitigate, rather than fundamentally resolve, these gaps24. We acknowledge that this selection is not exhaustive. Other significant threats, most notably viral hepatitis (B and C), healthcare-associated infections (HAIs), and the escalating crisis of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), play increasingly critical roles in Vietnam’s evolving epidemiology. However, as these challenges are often driven by complex clinical and health system dynamics distinct from the broader social drivers analyzed here, they are beyond the scope of this article. Economic stability and disease burden: the tale of TB and HIV/AIDS

Tuberculosis (TB)

In the past, TB in Vietnam was a classic index of poverty because it mostly affected families on low incomes25. The causal pathways are well-established in Vietnam and globally: poverty leads to malnutrition, which weakens the immune system; crowded and poorly ventilated living conditions facilitate transmission; and financial hardship creates significant barriers to seeking and completing the long course of treatment.

Vietnam’s experience, however, shows how moderate economic growth can help to combat a disease that is caused by poverty. A landmark analysis of national prevalence surveys from 2007 and 2017 revealed a remarkable trend. During this decade of rapid and relatively equitable economic development — characterized by rising GDP per capita and a stable Gini coefficient — the burden of TB became significantly less concentrated among low income groups. The illness concentration index, which was negative in 2007 (indicating concentration in the lowest income households), shifted to neutral by 2017, signifying a more even distribution of the reduced TB burden across all socioeconomic groups25. This finding strongly suggests that broad-based development and social protection policies that lift entire communities out of poverty can be a potent “structural intervention” against TB, reducing its overall prevalence by addressing its root causes25.

Despite this success, TB remains a major public health threat in Vietnam, which ranks as the country with the 11th highest TB burden, recently exacerbated by coronavirus disease (COVID)-1926. The primary challenge has shifted from poverty as a cause to the economic consequences of the disease itself. The high cost of treatment and lost income due to illness impose catastrophic financial burdens on affected households, jeopardizing treatment completion and perpetuating a cycle of debt and disease26. In response, current public health strategies increasingly focus on integrating social protection with clinical care, piloting interventions that provide cash transfers, subsidized health insurance, and income-generation support to mitigate these economic impacts26.

HIV/AIDS

The HIV epidemic in Vietnam emerged, then erupted, in the decade following the Đổi Mới reforms, intricately linked to the social and economic disruptions of that rapid transition27. The epidemic remains concentrated among specific high-risk populations, primarily people who inject drugs (PWIDs), female sex workers (FSWs), and men who have sex with men (MSM). MSM, especially younger urbanite men, have become one of the communities in Vietnam most impacted by the HIV epidemic, with prevalence rising sharply from 9–11% in 2006 to 14–20% in key cities by 2009–201028. This pattern shows a shift in sexual transmission epidemiology toward MSM, especially younger city-dwelling men.

Economic instability is a powerful determinant of HIV risk. Limited economic opportunities, poverty, and periods of homelessness are strongly associated with engagement in high-risk behaviors, such as exchanging sex for money, drugs, or necessities like housing and food. The rise in illegal heroin use, unprotected sex, and prostitution that accompanied the economic opening of the 1990s created the perfect storm for the virus to spread rapidly. Drug injection was the primary driver for the doubling of reported HIV cases between 2000 and 200529.

Furthermore, MSM HIV rates in Vietnam must be understood in the context of lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, intersex, queer, and others (LGBTIQ + ) health vulnerability and socio-legal factors. The 2014 Marriage and Family Law lifted the ban on same-sex unions, tacitly accepting but not openly condoning couples married in a foreign country. However, the State still does not legally recognize same-sex marriage, leaving sexual and gender minorities without family protection, inheritance, spousal benefits, or other structural support that would enable them to receive health insurance and care associated with their HIV status30,31. Along with stigma, discrimination, and heteronormative standards, legal non-recognition leads to sexual identity concealment, delayed testing, and lower preventative program uptake. Community-based organizations have raised awareness of MSM and transgender issues, but structural hurdles like healthcare disclosure fears continue to determine risk patterns and limit HIV testing, pre-exposure prophylaxis, and treatment. Understanding why sexual transmission among MSM accounts for a growing proportion of new HIV infections in Vietnam requires consideration of these contextual elements.

HIV is now spreading from these concentrated groups to the general population, mainly via the MSM population, intimate partners of PWIDs, and clients of FSWs. This creates a particular vulnerability for women, whose risk is often determined not by their own behavior but by that of their male partners. The lack of routine HIV screening and persistent social stigmatization mean that the true prevalence among women may be significantly underreported.

Education, literacy, and health-seeking behaviors

Education is a cornerstone of public health, acting as a powerful protective factor against a range of diseases. Higher educational attainment is consistently linked to better health outcomes, in large part because it is a key determinant of health literacy — the ability to access, understand, and use health information to make informed decisions1. Vietnam’s investment in education has been a major development success, achieving a high adult literacy rate of over 90% and near-universal primary school enrollment since the early 2000s12.

This growth in education has direct effects on the control of infectious diseases in Vietnam. For TB and HIV/AIDS, which are shrouded in social stigma, education is a critical tool for change. Historically, TB was widely viewed as an incurable “social disease”, leading to the ostracization of patients and their families32. Similarly, for many years HIV/AIDS was officially framed as a “social evil”27,30, resulting in intense discrimination against those living with the virus. This stigma is a formidable barrier, preventing individuals from seeking testing and adhering to treatment for fear of social and economic repercussions. Public health experts believe that removing these barriers through targeted education and awareness campaigns can lead to more cases being reported, better treatment adherence, and eventually less transmission32.

Neighborhood and built environment: the rise of urban and vector-borne diseases

The main drivers of Vietnam’s economic growth — urbanization and industrialization — have profoundly reshaped the country’s landscape, in so doing inadvertently providing new opportunities for diseases to spread. This section illustrates the “developmental double-edged sword”, whereby the answer to one problem (poverty in rural areas) itself creates a new problem (health risks in crowded city environments).

Dengue

This mosquito-transmitted viral disease has emerged as a major and rapidly growing public health threat in Vietnam, flourishing in the very environments created by modern development. The disease is particularly prevalent in low income urban areas and peri-urban suburbs, with risk escalating during and soon after rainy seasons33,34. The key risk factors for dengue are intimately linked to the characteristics of the built environment, especially in the context of rapid and often unplanned urbanization:i.Housing and Sanitation: Case-control studies in Vietnamese cities have identified specific housing conditions as significant risk factors. Living in rented housing (often of lower quality and with less occupant control over the environment), in unhygienic conditions, near open sewers, or in homes that discharge wastewater directly into local ponds are all associated with a substantially higher risk of contracting dengue35.ii.Water Supply and Storage: The peridomestic Aedes aegypti mosquito, the primary vector for dengue transmission in southern and central tropical regions of Vietnam, breeds in standing water. The same is true of Ae. albopictus, which dominates the northern temperate region, notably in less urbanized areas, particularly in natural habitats but also adapted to peridomestic environments. A critical determinant of risk is the lack of reliable, piped tap water, which forces households to store rain water in vessels like jars, buckets, and tanks, in addition to its collection in empty cans, plant pots, and discarded tires. These containers become ideal mosquito breeding sites36.iii.Population Density and Infrastructure: The link between population density and dengue risk is complex, which highlights the importance of considering demographics and infrastructure quality together. Some of the highest rates of dengue are not in the most heavily populated urban cores, but in low- to moderate-density communities common in rural villages and peri-urban settlements that lack sufficient access to water. In these settings, the reliance on water storage containers leads to a high mosquito-to-human ratio, increasing individual risk36. In contrast, dense urban areas with good water supply and sanitation infrastructure may present a lower risk of virus transmission. This demonstrates that dengue in Vietnam should not be considered simply as a disease of poverty, but rather as a disease of a specific type of urbanizing environment in which infrastructure development has not kept pace with population growth33.

Living in rented housing (often of lower quality and with less occupant control over the environment), in unhygienic conditions, near open sewers, or in homes that discharge wastewater directly into local ponds are all associated with a substantially higher risk of contracting dengue35.

Moreover, it is important to note that in recent times climate change has become a more prominent contextual factor influencing dengue transmission in Vietnam. Rising temperatures shorten mosquito breeding cycles and accelerate virus replication in the vector, resulting in increased transmission potential even in places with traditionally lower seasonal limitations. At the same time, increased rainfall variability (characterized by heavy rains followed by periods of drought) creates erratic patterns of water storage and breeding site distribution, promoting mosquito proliferation in both urban and peri-urban areas. These climatic alterations, in addition to social determinant factors, have contributed to the steady spread of dengue risk to northern and highland regions, as well as longer or more unpredictable transmission seasons. While it is not the primary emphasis herein, recognizing these broader environmental constraints helps to explain why dengue incidence has increased and its geographic range has become less stable over time.

ARIs

These are a leading cause of illness and death in Vietnam, particularly among children under five years of age37. The determinants of ARIs are strongly tied to both the household and the broader environment. At the domiciliary scale, exposure to indoor air pollution from cooking with solid fuels is a significant risk factor for severe ARIs that require hospitalization38. At the macro level, the rapid urbanization and industrialization that have characterized Vietnam’s economic development have also led to a marked deterioration of ambient air quality, a major contributor to respiratory illness19. Furthermore, climate-related factors such as changes in humidity and precipitation patterns have been identified as predictors of respiratory virus circulation in Vietnam and the wider region37.

Social and community context: the enduring legacy of war and social norms

The community-spirited, family-centric fabric of Vietnam’s society, woven from its unique history and cultural norms, contains powerful SDH that persist to this day.

Legacy of war

The end of the Vietnam/American War in 1975 did not cease its influence on health. The Vietnam Health and Aging Study (VHAS), a major longitudinal project, provides compelling evidence of the war’s long-term “scarring” effects on the population. The study documents that higher levels of exposure to wartime violence and malevolent conditions are significantly correlated with worse health outcomes decades later, including a higher prevalence of chronic conditions, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), psychological distress, and functional limitations among the aging population39. This enduring burden of comorbidity and frailty creates a more vulnerable population, one that is likely to experience more severe outcomes when faced with infectious disease threats.

Gender norms and HIV

The social context powerfully shapes the experience of HIV, particularly for women. Stigma is not applied evenly; it is strongly differentiated by gender. Women living with HIV report facing stereotypes that link their status to perceived immorality or sex work, whereas men’s HIV status is more often attributed to drug use, a behavior that, while condemned, is often more culturally accepted40,41. This double standard, rooted in a patriarchal culture, means that in the case of married women they often face more direct discrimination, blame, and social isolation, even when they have contracted the virus from their husbands. This creates a profound psychological barrier to seeking testing, disclosing their status, and accessing consistent care40,41.

Community cohesion as a protective factor

In contrast to the negative impacts of stigma, the strength of community cohesion has emerged as a powerful positive SDH. A prime example is the success of Intergenerational Self-Help Clubs (ISHCs), a model based on Older People Associations that has been implemented across Vietnam42. These clubs are community-led and provide a holistic range of support that addresses multiple SDH simultaneously, including social connection, health screening and education, enabling economic opportunities through providing micro-loans, and offering peer support. Evaluation of ISHCs shows high levels of membership adoption and satisfaction, and an association with improved self-reported positive health42. This model demonstrates the immense potential of leveraging and strengthening existing community structures to build resilience and address health determinants from the ground up.

While community cohesion remains a vital asset, it is increasingly vulnerable to the disruptive forces of Vietnam’s rapid development. The very mechanisms of “progress” — industrialization and urbanization — are reshaping the social architecture that underpins this cohesion. Large-scale rural-to-urban migration is physically separating extended families, diluting the traditional multi-generational support networks that have historically absorbed health shocks. In urban centers, the “village culture” of mutual surveillance and support is often replaced by anonymity and social fragmentation, potentially isolating vulnerable populations like the elderly and migrant workers. Furthermore, while the digital transformation offers new modes of connection, it risks creating a “digital divide” that may alienate older generations from the evolving social fabric. If these traditional safety nets fray before formal social protections are fully matured, the population may face a precarious situation in which neither community nor state is fully able to buffer the health impacts of future crises.

Healthcare access and quality: double-edged sword of the hybrid system

Vietnam’s healthcare system acts as a distinct determinant of infectious disease outcomes, characterized by a dichotomy between broad physical coverage and persistent structural barriers. The legacy of the socialist era established a robust four-tiered administrative network of CHSs, ensuring basic primary care infrastructure reaches even remote communes. While this wide reach has supported high immunization coverage and near-universal health insurance enrollment ( > 89%), the system’s “hybrid” financing model creates friction14,43. High OOP expenditure — historically accounting for roughly 40% of total health spending — remains a barrier to early care-seeking, particularly for long-term infectious disease treatments like TB14,44,45.

Structurally, the system is challenged by a severe workforce imbalance that favors urban centers, leaving rural grassroots facilities understaffed14,43. Consequently, low public trust in local CHSs prompts patients to bypass primary care for central hospitals. Paradoxically, this centralization facilitates the spread of HAIs and respiratory viruses within overcrowded tertiary care hubs. Furthermore, the public sector’s continued reliance on manual, paper-based record-keeping hampers the real-time data integration necessary for effective infectious disease surveillance.

These structural deficits do not impact the population uniformly; rather, they act as a filter that concentrates infectious risk within specific pockets of vulnerability. Current surveillance systems are predominantly facility-based and lack integration with socioeconomic data, meaning that the precise pathways through which inequality drives disease remain partially obscured. For instance, migrant workers without permanent residency (“hộ khẩu”) face administrative barriers to health insurance, turning peri-urban zones into “invisible” reservoirs for transmission. Similarly, while malaria has retreated from cities, it remains entrenched among ethnic minorities in the central highlands, where physical isolation intersects with cultural barriers to care. Until surveillance systems are upgraded to capture these disaggregated realities, policy responses will remain reactive rather than addressing the root causes of these inequities.

A potentially decisive pivot is, however, now underway. The issuance of Circular 13/2025/TT-BYT mandated the nationwide implementation of electronic medical records by September 2025. Crucially, this transition is anchored by the VNeID national digital identity system, which now integrates “Electronic Health Books” (Sổ sức khỏe điện tử). This unified platform promises to bridge the digital divide, enabling the real-time, population-level surveillance required to finally expose and address these hidden health disparities.

Comparison with Southeast Asia region

Shared trajectories

Vietnam’s public health problems are closely linked to those in SEAR as a whole. The entire region is recognized as a global “hot spot” for emerging and re-emerging infectious diseases, driven by a common set of powerful forces: rapid economic growth; intense urbanization; high levels of population mobility, both within and between countries; and increasing impacts from climate change. Consequently, Vietnam, like its SEAR neighbors, is grappling with a formidable “triple burden of disease”: the unfinished agenda of controlling infectious diseases like TB and malaria, the explosive rise of NCDs, and the new health threats posed by climate change46. A common structural weakness across the region, including in Vietnam, is a pattern of relatively low government spending on health, which results in a heavy reliance on high OOP payments by households, risking impoverishment and creating barriers to care47.

Vietnam’s distinctiveness

Despite these similarities, Vietnam stands out in several respects. Its history of a strong, centralized state and a high degree of political stability has enabled a remarkable capacity for nationwide public health mobilization, as demonstrated by responses to the severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) epidemic in 2003 and the early phases of the COVID-19 pandemic48. Such a capacity for unified action is not as consistently identifiable across the more diverse political systems present elsewhere in the region. In large part, this reflects the inherent link between public health and politics; autocratic governments can often enact reforms faster than their democratic counterparts. Hence, by comparison with its neighbors, Vietnam’s success in achieving, at least in the initial decades of Đổi Mới, relatively equitable economic growth and poverty reduction was exceptionally swift20. Finally, the profound and lasting health consequences of the Vietnam/American War, including the effects of Agent Orange/dioxin and PTSD, represent a unique historical determinant that continues to shape the health of the country’s population.

Comparison with the LMIC archetype

Similarities

As a lower middle-income country, Vietnam shares many of the fundamental challenges faced by its economic peers globally. It continues to manage a significant infectious disease burden, faces the immense task of financing and implementing UHC, and must continually work to strengthen its primary healthcare system49. Like many LMIC, in order to better guide public health action Vietnam is also confronting the challenge of improving surveillance systems to detect pathogen emergence and disease outbreaks, transitioning from paper-based to online data collection, and integrating artificial intelligence into healthcare management processes50.

Differences

Vietnam diverges significantly from the “typical” LMIC profile. Its achievements in education are a standout success, with literacy and school enrollment rates that surpass many of its peers12. The government’s proven capacity to implement complex, nationwide programs — from mass vaccination campaigns to the rapid expansion of social health insurance — is a critical asset that is often weaker in other LMIC with less cohesive state structures51. Another striking difference lies in its urban landscape. While many rapidly urbanizing LMIC are characterized by the explosive growth of large, informal slum settlements, Vietnam has largely avoided this phenomenon. This has been attributed to a combination of unique housing policies that tolerate small plot sizes and incremental self-construction, cultural factors like inter-generational cohabitation, and a history of people-friendly socialist practices52.

Comparison with the HIC archetype

Divergent priorities

The gap between Vietnam and HIC remains vast, and their public health priorities reflect this. While Vietnam is still focused on building out and ensuring equitable access to fundamental infrastructure — such as sanitation systems and financial protection from healthcare costs — HIC are tackling different manifestations of SDH23. Their efforts are focused more on addressing health inequities rooted in complex issues like structural discrimination, the health effects of chronic stress, and ensuring equitable access to highly resourced but often fragmented healthcare systems4. The dominant infectious disease threats also differ; HIC are more concerned with challenges like AMR in advanced hospital settings, HAIs, and outbreaks within specific marginalized communities53.

Converging trends

Despite these differences, there are important areas of convergence. Both Vietnam and HIC are witnessing the rising importance of NCDs as a critical factor influencing susceptibility to infectious diseases. Comorbidities like obesity, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and cardiovascular disease, fueled by similar lifestyle shifts toward unhealthy diets and physical inactivity, are now understood to increase the severity of infections like influenza and COVID-19 in both settings53. Both are also at the forefront of confronting the profound health implications of two major global shifts: population aging and climate change, which together will place new and increasing demands on their respective health systems54.

Vietnam’s developmental journey positions it as a unique “hybrid” case study, one that does not fit neatly into the conventional categories of “developing” or “developed”. The nation exhibits characteristics of both. On the one hand, its remarkable educational outcomes, high UHC enrollment rates, and the strong implementation capacity of its state apparatus are features more commonly associated with HIC12. On the other hand, its economic profile as a lower middle-income country, its health financing structure characterized by low public spending and high OOP costs, and its ongoing struggles with basic environmental infrastructure like sanitation are classic LMIC challenges21. This hybridity is also reflected in its disease profile, as it simultaneously battles “old” diseases of poverty like TB, a rising NCD epidemic, and “new” diseases of development like dengue55. This complex, non-linear trajectory makes Vietnam an invaluable source of reference for the large cohort of nations currently in the midst of their own rapid transitions. A demonstrable lesson from Vietnam’s experience is that progress is not uniform across all determinants and that successfully addressing one set of problems can, paradoxically, create an entirely new set of challenges that require different strategies and a more sophisticated, intersectoral approach.

Future directions: double burden, climate change, and recommendations

As Vietnam looks toward the next decade and beyond to the mid-point of the 21st century, its public health landscape will be defined by the convergence of existing trends and the increasing likelihood of future shocks. The country is navigating a complex epidemiological transition under conditions of growing systemic pressure, the success of which will demand a more resilient and adaptive health system.

Compounding challenge of the double burden

Vietnam is experiencing a major shift in its epidemiology. While the country continues to tackle a significant infectious disease load, it is simultaneously facing a dramatic and accelerating surge in NCDs. Cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes, and COPD are now the leading cause of death in the country, collectively accounting for over 80% of all mortality55. The prevalence of key risk factors is rising alarmingly; between 2015 and 2021 alone, the prevalence of hypertension among adults rose from approximately one in five to more than one in four56.

This “double burden of disease” places an immense and multifaceted strain on a health system that was historically designed and structured to combat acute infectious illnesses. The system must now pivot to also manage chronic, long-term conditions, which requires a fundamental re-orientation from providing episodic, acute care to delivering integrated, continuous chronic disease management — a far more complex and resource-intensive endeavor57.

Crucially, these two burdens are not separate but deeply connected. A large and growing body of evidence, reinforced during the COVID-19 pandemic, shows that underlying NCDs significantly increase an individual’s susceptibility to infectious diseases and worsen their outcomes53. A population with a high prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, and lung disease is more vulnerable to severe illness and death from influenza, pneumonia, and novel coronavirus infections. This dangerous synergy is already at play in Vietnam and will become a more dominant feature of its public health challenges moving forward.

The economic consequences of this double burden are severe, which could undo Vietnam’s hard-won progress over the last 50 years. NCDs disproportionately cause premature death and disability among the populace in their prime working years, leading to significant losses in productivity and thus generating a substantial drag on economic growth46. For households, the financial impact can be devastating. The long-term nature of chronic care, combined with a health financing system characterized by high OOP spending, creates a high risk of catastrophic health expenditure, which can push families into poverty and hence reverse decades of progress10,17.

Future shocks: climate change and pandemics

On top of these already heavy loads, the risks of future shocks are growing. Vietnam is one of the countries that is most likely to be affected by climate change because it has a long coastline, large river deltas, and many smallholder farms. These impacts have direct consequences for health, including an elevated risk and expanding geographic distribution of vector-borne diseases like dengue, a higher incidence of water-borne diseases following more frequent and intense flooding, and threats to food security and nutrition due to damaging impacts on agriculture and aquaculture58,59.

Furthermore, the recent global experiences of SARS, avian influenza, and COVID-19 each provides a stark reminder of the ever-present threat of infectious disease outbreaks caused by emerging pathogens. While Vietnam’s response to these crises has often been lauded for its effectiveness, demonstrating strong state capacity, it has also highlighted the nation’s inherent vulnerability to global health security threats48. Future public health planning and system design must therefore have resilience to such shocks as a core, non-negotiable principle. Authorized by the Prime Minister, Decision 89/QD-TTg, the current National Strategy for “protection, care and improvement of the people’s health” provides a prime example22.

The convergence of these powerful forces — the double burden of disease, a rapidly aging population that will naturally increase healthcare demands, and the looming threats of climate change and pandemics — is creating an unprecedented “fiscal and systemic squeeze” on Vietnam’s health system. The demands on the system are growing exponentially and becoming more complex, in the face of which the current financing model, which places a heavy burden on individuals, is both inequitable and unsustainable. This elevates the central policy challenge for the 2025–2030 period beyond mere disease control. The critical task is one of fundamental health financing and system reform to manage this squeeze, ensuring the system can adapt and respond without collapsing or reversing the nation’s remarkable gains in health and equity.

Strategic pathways forward: recommendations for intervention design

This perspective of Vietnam’s past 50-year public health journey reveals a complex and evolving relationship between social determinants and infectious disease, beyond merely the biological ones. Consequently, the “paradox of progress” cannot be solved by vaccines and drugs alone. In order to address the challenges of the 2015–2025 decade and beyond, public health interventions must become more sophisticated, integrated, and responsive to these underlying drivers3.

Limitations

Data availability and quality were uneven across the 50-year period, particularly in the early post-war decades when Vietnam faced severe resource constraints. National capacity to systematically collect, manage, and analyze health and social determinant data was limited, resulting in fragmented and incomplete evidence. Although data systems improved with economic growth and international integration, many indicators remain inconsistently measured, under-reported, and poorly harmonized across time and space.

This study addresses the absence of a comprehensive temporo-spatial analysis linking social determinants to infectious diseases trends in Vietnam by consolidating diverse secondary sources into a single comparative framework. While this approach enables a unique longitudinal perspective, it also introduces potential biases related to differing definitions, measurement methods, and data quality across sources. Necessary simplifications to ensure comparability may understate the complexity of Vietnam’s social and health transitions. Despite these constraints, the synthesis provides a coherent overview of how evolving social conditions have shaped infectious disease patterns over time.

Conclusions

Over the past five decades, Vietnam serves as an example of the significant impact that social determinants of health exert on the development of infectious diseases. From the hardships of the post-war subsidy era, through the sweeping socioeconomic changes of Đổi Mới, to the complexities of the present middle-income transition, the country’s health challenges have consistently reflected the conditions in which people live, work, and age. Poverty, food insecurity, and malnutrition once drove the burdens of malaria, cholera, and TB. Later, unplanned urbanization, migration, and rising inequalities promoted escalating incidence of HIV/AIDS and viral vector-borne diseases. Today, rapid urban expansion, environmental degradation, and lifestyle transitions exacerbate the double burden of infectious and non-communicable diseases, while climate change and pandemics add new layers of risk.

Our perspective underscores that infectious disease control in Vietnam cannot be addressed solely through biomedical approaches. Instead, progress requires sustained attention to upstream determinants — economic stability, education, built environments, social context, and equitable access to healthcare. By synthesizing fragmented data into a temporo-spatial perspective, this article highlights both achievements and emerging vulnerabilities. As Vietnam looks toward 2030 and 2045, addressing the “paradox of progress” will depend on intersectoral strategies that integrate health into broader development policies, ensuring resilience and equity in the face of evolving threats.
