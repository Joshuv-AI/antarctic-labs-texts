Population Pharmacokinetic and Pharmacokinetic‐Pharmacodynamic Analysis for Clazakizumab in Patients With End‐Stage Kidney Disease Undergoing Dialysis
Authors: Venkateswari Y. Muthukrishnan, Thomas Kerbusch, Laura E. Strong, Huub Jan Kleijn, Marc Pfister, Anna Marie Chang, Milin Acharya, Partha Nandy, Jeannine S. McCune
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe inflammatory cytokine interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) plays a significant role in the progression of cardiovascular disease (CVD). We evaluated the impact of clazakizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting the IL‐6 ligand, upon high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein (hs‐CRP) as part of the dose‐finding phase 2b trial. In this randomized, double‐blind trial, adults with cardiovascular disease and/or diabetes receiving maintenance dialysis and having hs‐CRP ≥ 2 mg/L at baseline received clazakizumab (2.5 mg, 5 mg, or 10 mg, n = 32 per dose group) or placebo (n = 31) every 4 weeks. These data were used to develop a population pharmacokinetics (popPK) and a longitudinal PK‐pharmacodynamic (PK‐PD) model to characterize the relationship between clazakizumab and hs‐CRP to support Phase 3 dose selection. A 2‐compartment model with linear elimination well described clazakizumab PK. PK‐PD was best described by an indirect‐response inhibitory model, where clazakizumab concentrations inhibited the k in (zero‐order rate of production) of hs‐CRP. Simulations predicted that all doses (2.5, 5, and 10 mg IV every 4 weeks) would result in the majority (> 80%) of subjects experiencing at least an 80% decrease in baseline hs‐CRP (80.9%, 83.4%, and 88.1%, respectively). In addition, the proportion of subjects with hs‐CRP concentrations below 2 mg/L at Week 12 was predicted to be 67.7%, 76.7%, and 82.6%, respectively, for these 3 dose groups. These modeling and simulation results support the dose selection of clazakizumab 5 mg IV every 4 weeks in the phase 3 placebo‐controlled trial to evaluate the impact of IL‐6 inhibition via clazakizumab administration upon cardiovascular events.

Introduction

Patients with end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD) experience high morbidity and mortality rates despite receiving dialysis. In the general population, systemic inflammation is a major contributor to cardiovascular disease [1, 2]. Activation of the interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) pathway [3, 4] suggests a causal role. In dialysis patients, acute upregulation of proinflammatory cytokine transcription, including interleukin‐6, occurs both directly, by activation mediated by the extracorporeal circuit, and indirectly, mediated by thrombotic and infectious events, creating additional inflammatory stimulation [5]. Chronic inflammation may mediate cardiovascular disease among ESKD patients receiving dialysis. Inflammatory markers, including C‐reactive protein (CRP) and IL‐6, are usually elevated in ESKD and are associated with cardiovascular events in this population [6, 7]. Thus, targeting the IL‐6 pathway may reduce cardiovascular events and mortality in patients receiving dialysis. Clazakizumab is a high‐affinity, humanized monoclonal antibody that targets the IL‐6 ligand, thereby inhibiting downstream IL‐6 activity and mitigating IL‐6‐driven inflammation.

We hypothesized that clazakizumab could reduce high‐sensitivity C‐reactive (hs‐CRP) protein concentrations associated with major adverse cardiovascular events in patients undergoing maintenance dialysis. To test this, we conducted a double‐blind, randomized, placebo‐controlled trial combining a dose‐finding phase (phase 2b) with a cardiovascular outcomes phase (phase 3). This trial focused on maintenance dialysis patients with baseline serological evidence of inflammation, defined here as patients with an hs‐CRP over 2 mg/L.

Specifically, we performed a population PK (popPK) analysis of clazakizumab, a PK‐pharmacodynamic (PD) analysis of the relationship between clazakizumab concentrations and hs‐CRP concentrations in patients with ESKD who are undergoing maintenance dialysis. After the development of the popPK and the popPK–PD models, a modeling and simulation (M&S) analysis was conducted to support clazakizumab dose selection for the Phase 3 trial that is predicted to result in ≥ 80% of subjects attaining a ≥ 80% decrease in hs‐CRP from baseline and hs‐CRP levels < 2 mg/L at Week 12.

Methods

Study Design

The population PK and exposure–response analyses considered data obtained from patients treated with clazakizumab or placebo in the phase 2b component of POSIBIL6ESKD, which was a parallel‐group, double‐blind, randomized, placebo‐controlled trial (ClinicalTrials.gov registration: NCT05485961). Full details and results of the POSIBIL6ESKD study have been previously published [8]. In brief, 127 patients aged 18 years or older with a diagnosis of cardiovascular disease and/or diabetes with an hs‐CRP ≥ 2 mg/L and receiving treatment with dialysis were randomly allocated in a 1:1:1:1 ratio to placebo or clazakizumab at doses of 2.5 mg, 5 mg, or 10 mg every 4 weeks (Q4W) for up to 20 weeks. Randomization was stratified by hs‐CRP 2–6 mg/L or > 6 mg/L at screening to minimize the possibility of an imbalance in baseline hs‐CRP across groups. All subjects and investigators were blinded to treatment assignment. The prespecified primary efficacy outcome was the change from baseline to week 12 in serum hs‐CRP, expressed as the geometric mean ratio.

The study drug was administered as a 3‐min intravenous bolus via the return venous line of the hemodialysis circuit. Study drug administration began at least 1 h after the start of the dialysis session and no later than 1 h before its completion. The minimum treatment period was 12 weeks (three doses), and patients could continue treatment every 4 weeks for up to six doses, and followed until the last randomized participant received their third dose, which marked the end of the study. Therefore, all patients were expected to complete the 12‐week treatment period. An end‐of‐treatment visit occurred 4 weeks after the last dose of the study drug, followed by a safety follow‐up 8 weeks later.

A global executive committee and an independent data monitoring committee approved the protocol and reviewed progress, quality, and safety during trial conduct. Ethics Committees and Institutional Review Boards affiliated with each site approved the protocol. All subjects provided written informed consent.

Blood Sample Collection and Bioanalysis

Blood samples collected during Weeks 0 through 24 (nominal time) were used for the popPK and the longitudinal PK–PD modeling. The clazakizumab pharmacokinetic samples were obtained as follows: a predose sample was collected prior to dialysis at Weeks 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 13, 14, 16, 20, and 24; a postdose sample was collected 5–10 min after dosing on Weeks 0, 4, and 12. One blood sample was collected in a subset of subjects during dialysis at Week 24.

Serum concentrations of clazakizumab were determined using a validated quantitative enzyme immunoassay, which specifically measures unbound (free) clazakizumab. The plate was coated with a capture anti‐clazakizumab (9 cL 1) fragment and blocked. Standards or quality control (QC) samples diluted 1:50 in diluent buffer were added after another wash and incubated for approximately 1 h. The plate was then washed and incubated with a biotinylated rabbit monoclonal antibody (Biotinylated rb 1001) specific to clazakizumab. After a subsequent wash, NeutrAvidin‐HRP was added, followed by incubation with tetramethylbenzidine peroxidase substrate for approximately 5–10 min. The reaction was terminated with phosphoric acid stop solution and the color intensity, which was proportional to clazakizumab concentration, was measured at 450 nm with background correction at 620 nm. Concentrations were calculated using a four‐parameter logistic standard curve (optical density vs. concentration). Validation parameters, such as precision and accuracy (intra‐run, inter‐run, and inter‐analyst), dilutional integrity, and selectivity, met the following criteria: QC samples should not deviate no more than ±20.0% (± 25.0% at the upper limit of quantitation (ULOQ) and lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ)) from the nominal value at all concentrations. The validated assay range in human serum is 20.0–1250 ng/mL and was found to be selective in hemodialysis patient samples. These criteria were applied to the validity of clinical test data.

The hs‐CRP samples were obtained as follows: at screening (Day ‐14 to Day ‐1), a predose sample was collected prior to dialysis at Week 0, 1, 2, 4, 12, 13, 14, and 24; a postdose sample was collected on Weeks 0, 4, and 12. One blood sample was collected during dialysis at Week 24.

Serum hs‐CRP concentrations were measured within Roche Cardiac C‐Reactive Protein (CRP) (Latex) High Sensitive in vitro diagnostic test using Cobas c502 Chemistry Analyzer.

The validation followed the guidelines of the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) and the College of American Pathologists. Method performance characteristics assessed during this validation were in compliance with the subsections of CLIA regulation 42 CFR 493.1253, and testing procedures were generally based on the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute's method evaluation standards. The measuring principle of hs‐CRP is a particle‐enhanced immunoturbidimetric assay (particle‐enhanced immunological agglutination). Human CRP agglutinates with latex particles coated with monoclonal anti‐CRP antibodies. The precipitate is determined turbidimetrically. Validation results met the predefined criteria for repeatability (< 5%), reproducibility (< 10%), and accuracy of the assay (the bias between the observed mean and the theoretical value was ±15.0%). The analytical concentration range was within ±15.0% of the expected mean, except at LLOQ (±20.0%). Published reference range values were verified after ≥ 90.0% of values fell within the proposed reference range. Linearity confirmed as 0.15–20.0 mg/L. The extended analytical measuring range based on 1:15 (automatic) dilution was 0.2 to 300.0 mg/L.

Population Pharmacokinetic Modeling

Subjects were included in the popPK analysis if they received ≥ 1 dose of clazakizumab and had ≥ 1 measurable clazakizumab concentration with associated sampling time and dosing information. One‐, two‐, and three‐compartment models were evaluated. Nonlinear approximation for target‐mediated drug disposition (TMDD) was assessed using a Michaelis–Menten approximation.

The interindividual random effects on the parameters were introduced and retained if their inclusion did not cause model instability and if their estimates were not close to zero. They were modeled assuming a log‐normal distribution as given by the following expression: θki=θk×eηki where θ ki denotes the parameter value for the ith subject, θ k denotes the typical parameter value, and η ki denotes the interindividual random effect for the ith subject—assumed to have mean of 0 (zero) and variance ω k 2.

Collectively, the vector of random effects (across the parameters indexed by k) has the covariance matrix Ω. Covariance matrix structures, including diagonal and blocked diagonal structures, were evaluated after the inclusion of any covariate effects. If individual parameters needed to be restricted to certain values, a logit transformation was applied.

The residual error structure was assumed to follow an additive, proportional, or combined additive and proportional error model described by the following: Yij=Cij×1+ε1ij+ε2ij where Y ij is the jth observed concentration for the ith subject, C ij is the corresponding predicted concentration, and 𝜀1ij (proportional) and 𝜀2ij (additive) are the residual errors under the assumption that 𝜀~N (0, σ 2).

The residual error model was optimized until no trends were visible in residual plots (in particular, absolute values of IWRES vs. IPRED).

The covariates of interest for the popPK model were the baseline continuous covariates of body weight, age, and free IL‐6 concentration, liver enzymes (aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and alkaline phosphatase), bilirubin, and albumin, and the categorical covariate of sex and race on clearance (CL) and central volume of distribution (V) (Table S1). Covariates were assessed when they were available in at least 80% of subjects. For categorical covariates, a minimum of 15 subjects was necessary in each category. If covariates showed a correlation of > 0.5, only one of the correlated covariates (with the strongest influence or the most meaningful from a clinical, biological, or practical perspective) was included in the formal analysis. Continuous covariates were preferred over categorized covariates with the same meaning. Covariates that fulfilled these criteria were explored graphically and covariates displaying potential trends with clazakizumab PK were tested in a stepwise process.

All continuous covariates were incorporated into the population model using a scaled structure based on either the median population value or a standard value of the covariate. This ensured that covariate effects were relative to an individual in the middle of the population distribution. All categorical covariates were initially incorporated into the population model using a proportional structure with either the most common level of the covariate being the reference or a level specific to the analysis. This ensured that categorical covariate effects were relative to a reference group or category. The mathematical structures of the covariate models are shown below:

Where P ki is the population estimate of the parameter P k for subject i, X ij is the value of continuous covariate X j for subject i, or an indicator variable for subject i for categorical covariate X j with value 1 for the nonreference category and 0 for the reference category, M (X j) is the median of covariate X j in the analysis dataset, θ k is the typical value of the parameter P k, and θ j is a coefficient that reflects the effect of covariate X j on the parameter.

At the end of covariate testing, alternative variance–covariance structures for Ω were evaluated, including partial and full block structures. The model that resulted from this step of the model‐building process was considered the final model and included acceptable minimization for gradient methods, no estimates close to the boundary, relative standard errors (RSE) < 30% for fixed‐effects and < 50% for random effect parameters, and no unacceptable trends in goodness‐of‐fit plots.

The uncertainty of parameter estimates of the final popPK model was evaluated using the RSE or 95% confidence intervals (CIs) about the point estimates based on the estimated standard errors reported by NONMEM from the final popPK model. Shrinkage was reported for estimates of interindividual variability (IIV) and residual variability.

A nonparametric bootstrap analysis [9] was also conducted to evaluate the stability of the final popPK model. The bootstrap analysis was performed on 1000 replicates of the dataset, generated by random resampling of subjects from the original dataset with replacement. The final model was repeatedly fitted to bootstrap replicates of the dataset. CIs (95th CI) were calculated based on the distribution of the parameter estimates from the bootstrap runs.

Visual predictive checks (VPCs, 95th CI) were used to evaluate the predictive ability of the final popPK [10] performed with prediction correction (pc) where noted [11] using 1000 replicates.

Population Pharmacokinetic‐Pharmacodynamic Analyses

A longitudinal PK‐PD analysis was conducted to describe the relationship between clazakizumab exposure and hs‐CRP concentrations. Subjects were included in the longitudinal PK‐PD analysis if they had hs‐CRP measurement from ≥ 1 time point after study drug administration. Baseline hs‐CRP was defined by the sample immediately prior to receiving any study drug (predose sample on Day 1).

A sequential 2‐stage approach was employed for the PK‐PD analysis. Serum clazakizumab concentrations were predicted based on individual post hoc parameters as estimated in the PK analysis [12, 13]. Based on the exploratory PD analysis, delayed effects models were used to characterize the temporal relationship between serum clazakizumab and hs‐CRP. The exploratory analysis indicated a delay between measured effect and drug concentration and, therefore, effect‐compartment and indirect‐response model structures were evaluated. The covariates of interest for the longitudinal PK‐PD model were the baseline continuous covariates of free IL‐6 concentration, hs‐CRP, age, body weight, albumin, and Week 12 hs‐CRP and the categorical covariates sex and race on k in or IC50 (Table S2). Diagnostic plots during the development and evaluation of the final PK‐PD model followed the same methodology as was used for the popPK analysis, including 95th CI VPCs to evaluate the predictive ability of the final PK‐PD model [10].

Simulations

In order to calculate individual subject exposure metrics, the final popPK model was used to predict rich clazakizumab concentration‐time profiles for each subject based on posterior Bayes estimates. Monte Carlo simulations were performed using the final PK‐PD model to predict hs‐CRP concentrations following different dose regimens, with a virtual population of 1000 subjects generated by resampling individual post hoc parameters from the final popPK and PK‐PD models. The hs‐CRP profiles were simulated after 3 doses of 0, 2.5, 5, or 10 mg IV clazakizumab Q4W, with predicted hs‐CRP concentrations at Week 12 summarized. The goal of the simulations was to identify clazakizumab dosing regimens predicted to result in ≥ 80% of subjects attaining a ≥ 80% decrease in hs‐CRP from baseline and hs‐CRP concentrations < 2 mg/L at Week 12 based on results from the Phase 2b study to support dose selection for the Phase 3 study [8].

Additional simulations based on empirical Bayes estimates were performed for a virtual population of 1000 subjects generated using PK and PD parameter distributions sampled from the final variance–covariance matrix for each model and resampling of relevant covariate distributions from the PK‐PD dataset.

Software Used in Analysis

Datasets for the analysis were assembled using SAS (version 9.4). Population PK and longitudinal PK‐PD modeling were performed using nonlinear mixed‐effects modeling software (NONMEM; version 7.5.1, ICON, Hanover, MD, US) and Perl‐speaks‐NONMEM (PsN, version 5.3.1, Department of Pharmacy, Uppsala University, Uppsala, Sweden). The first‐order conditional estimation method of NONMEM with interaction (FOCE INTER) and the Laplacian estimation method were used during model development. R software (version 4.3.0) was used for data exploration prior to modeling, exposure generation, and postprocessing of results. VPC simulations were run in NONMEM with plots generated in R using the R package tidyvpc with binning using the Jenks method (popPK model) or binning based on nominal time (PK‐PD model).

Results

Population PK Analyses

The clazakizumab PK dataset consisted of 891 measurable PK observations from 95 subjects; the covariates are summarized in Table S1. The clazakizumab concentration vs. time stratified by dose suggests multicompartment disposition (Figure S1). Exposures across the 2.5–10 mg dose range were generally dose‐proportional, and target‐mediated drug disposition was not apparent. One‐, two‐, and three‐compartment structural models with first‐order elimination were tested; a 2‐compartment structure was preferred. The addition of a nonlinear CL component did not improve fit over the linear CL structure. Weight‐based allometric exponents estimated on central CL and central volume of distribution (V1) improved model fit. IIV was evaluated on all parameters but was not able to be reliably estimated on intercompartmental clearance (Q). Proportional and combined additive + proportional error models were evaluated, and the combined error model was selected. The structure of the variance–covariance matrix was refined by adding a correlation between CL and V1.

Baseline free IL‐6 on CL resulted in a significant improvement in objective function value (drop of 20 points, p < 0.001). Effects of sex on CL, V1, and peripheral volume of distribution (V2) were not retained because they were not significant at the p = 0.001 level. Any effects by sex are likely already described through the weight‐based allometry model. No other trends in eta distributions with covariates were noted.

Parameter estimates for the final clazakizumab popPK model are shown in Table 1. Goodness‐of‐fit plots and residual diagnostic plots for the final model are shown in Figures S2 and S3. The final popPK model parameters were within the 95% CI determined by a bootstrap analysis based on 806 successful runs of 1000 replicates, shown in Table 1. The final model predicted the observed clazakizumab concentrations with reasonable accuracy (Figure 1).

Population PK‐PD Analysis

The PD analysis population included 126 subjects; the covariates are described in Table S2. The PD analysis dataset included 1401 measurable hs‐CRP observations; 8.9% of all hs‐CRP observations after administration of the first dose of clazakizumab were BLQ; both exclusion of BLQ samples and imputation using the M3 method were investigated during model development. The absolute hs‐CRP concentrations, change from baseline, and percent change from baseline are shown in Figure 2. The distribution of baseline hs‐CRP was similar across treatment groups. At Week 12, the majority of subjects receiving clazakizumab had hs‐CRP concentrations < 2 mg/L and at least an 80% decrease from baseline, with no clear trends associated with the clazakizumab dose. Therefore, in order to better characterize the clazakizumab exposure to hs‐CRP relationship, changes in hs‐CRP over time (longitudinal modeling) were investigated.

Structures investigated for the PK‐PD model included direct‐effect, effect‐compartment, and indirect‐effect. A direct‐effect model structure was attempted, but only resulted in reasonable estimates of a half‐maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) if the 5‐ to 10‐min postdose samples taken during the dialysis procedure were omitted from the assessment. The hs‐CRP samples taken at 5 to 10 min postdose were similar to the predose concentrations despite clazakizumab concentrations being high, indicating that there is a time delay between serum clazakizumab exposure and suppression of hs‐CRP. An indirect‐effect model provided a better fit compared to an effect‐compartment model. Because of the mechanism of clazakizumab action as an anti‐IL‐6 antibody, and IL‐6 being a pro‐inflammatory cytokine, an indirect‐response model with clazakizumab inhibiting the zero‐order rate of hs‐CRP formation (kin) was chosen.

The PK‐PD model is described by the following equation: dEdt=kin*1−Imax*CpγIC50γ+Cpγ−kout*E where E is hs‐CRP concentration, k in is the zero‐order rate of hs‐CRP production, k out is the first‐order rate of hs‐CRP removal, C p is the concentration of clazakizumab in serum, I max is the maximum fraction of inhibition of k in, IC50 is the clazakizumab serum concentration associated with 50% of the maximum k in inhibition, and γ is a Hill factor describing the steepness of the exposure‐response relationship.

Prior to any clazakizumab being dosed, it was assumed that serum hs‐CRP concentrations are at steady state, that is, kinkout=E0 where E 0 is the hs‐CRP at time 0 (prior to any clazakizumab being administered), k in is the zero‐order rate of hsCRP formation, and k out is the first‐order rate of hs‐CRP removal.

The pcVPC plots for the initial PK‐PD model indicated a limited predictive ability with increased variability compared to observed values. The skewed baseline hs‐CRP values across the population, with many values falling close to the LLOQ once clazakizumab was administered, likely impacted the ability to precisely characterize IIV in the model as evidenced by the very wide η distribution for clazakizumab IC50. Therefore, models were fit to ln‐transformed hs‐CRP values, improving the model fit. The model was further refined to use the M3 method for imputation of BLQ samples [14], to include a Hill factor to better characterize the steepness of the E‐R response, and to include a Manly transformation [15] on the IC50 IIV to normalize the distribution of η values. These data transformations were crucial in obtaining a more precise estimate (RSE) for η‐IC50 (which remained large), and in turn were pivotal for the dose simulations.

The parameter estimates for the final PK‐PD model for serum hs‐CRP are presented in Table 2. Fixed‐effect parameters were estimated with good precision with %RSE values < 30% with the exception of IC50 and the shape parameter on IC50 IIV. The estimated IIV on IC50 was very large (> 1000%), indicating that the current dataset does not support a robust characterization of clazakizumab IC50 across the study population.

Discussion

The pharmacokinetics of IV clazakizumab were well characterized by a 2‐compartment model with linear elimination, estimated body weight‐based allometric components, and a covariate effect of baseline free IL‐6 on CL. The qualified popPK model and pcVPCs indicated an adequate description of the observed PK data, allowing accurate simulation of PK profiles for administered doses. Notably, clazakizumab has a molecular weight of 145 kDa [16], so hemodialysis is not expected to affect its renal clearance [17]. Data from biologics license application reviews and relevant literature indicate that impaired renal function can decrease the renal clearance of some therapeutic proteins and peptides with a molecular weight of less than 69 kDa [18]. Notably, the observed clearance represents both on‐dialysis and off‐dialysis clearance.

Clazakizumab administration led to a rapid decrease in hs‐CRP concentrations in ESKD patients receiving maintenance dialysis [8]. This time course is similar to that reported in patients with rheumatoid arthritis, in whom CRP concentrations rapidly decreased and remained low after receiving two doses of clazakizumab, administered at doses between 80 and 320 mg intravenously at week 1 and week 8, in combination with methotrexate [19]. An E‐R model using the hs‐CRP data was not feasible because all responses at Week 12 were at or near the maximum inhibitory effect of clazakizumab on hs‐CRP concentrations. Therefore, a longitudinal model was developed to use all available data, including the data demonstrating an early onset of hs‐CRP decline. This approach, instead of using more classical statistical techniques, enabled the use of the full time course of clazakizumab exposure and PD (hs‐CRP) effect across all clazakizumab doses to help predict the response at one dose group. The final PK‐PD model that best characterized the relationship between clazakizumab and hs‐CRP was an indirect‐response inhibitory model where clazakizumab concentrations inhibited the k in and a covariate effect of baseline hs‐CRP concentrations on k in.

Additional techniques, including ln‐transformation of hs‐CRP values, M3 imputation for handling of BLQ observations, fixing I max to 1, estimating a Hill factor to better characterize the steepness of the E‐R relationship, and applying a Manly transformation to normalize the IC50 IIV distribution, were also applied to better describe the data, as many of the hs‐CRP concentrations were near the LLOQ. Residual error was estimated as an additive term on the log scale, resulting in marked improvements in the model description of the PD profiles and improvement of the pcVPCs. However, some model misspecification was still observed. While the median observed PD profile was within the prediction interval, it was on the edge of the prediction interval (Figure 3). Because the observed 5th percentile and 95th percentile profiles from the model fell within the prediction intervals, the longitudinal PK‐PD model was deemed appropriately accurate for dose simulations of the administered dose treatment groups despite the large estimate for the IIV on IC50. Therefore, dose simulations for 2.5, 5, and 10 mg IV Q4W clazakizumab predicted a ≥ 80% decrease from baseline hs‐CRP at Week 12 (80.9%, 83.4%, and 88.1% of subjects, respectively) and hs‐CRP concentrations < 2 mg/L at Week 12 (67.7%, 76.7%, and 82.6% of subjects, respectively).

The trial has several limitations [8]. First, the sample size was modest and insufficient for conducting meaningful subgroup analyses. Additionally, hs‐CRP suppression at Week 12 was used for dose selection only 12 weeks, compared to the 52 weeks in the phase 3 trial. Adverse effects, such as infections, may occur more frequently with a longer duration of exposure to clazakizumab. Furthermore, data on residual kidney function were not collected.

In conclusion, collectively, the results of the clazakizumab popPK and the longitudinal PK‐PD model of clazakizumab and hs‐CRP in the POSIBIL6ESKD study support the selection of IV clazakizumab dose of 5 mg Q4W for the Phase 3 in subjects with a diagnosis of cardiovascular disease and/or diabetes with an hs‐CRP ≥ 2 mg/L and receiving treatment with dialysis. The rapid onset of action of clazakizumab on hs‐CRP resulted in hs‐CRP data that were less informative on clazakizumab dose–response. This is a common issue encountered between Phase 2 and Phase 3 clinical trials. In order to attain the required precision in exposure and dose–response, an advanced PK‐PD model was developed with methodological refinements to provide a dose–response with sufficient predictiveness and precision. Consequently, an appropriate dose selection for the clazakizumab Phase 3 study could be provided.
