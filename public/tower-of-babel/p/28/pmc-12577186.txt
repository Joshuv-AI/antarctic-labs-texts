From trash to results: shortcomings and benefits of paraphernalia-based drug checking in Finland
Authors: Teemu Kaskela, Janne Nahkuri, Aino Kankaanpää, Anna Pelander, Teemu Gunnar, J. Tuomas Harviainen, Jouni Tourunen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDrug checking is a harm reduction intervention that combines the identification of the chemical content of a drug with an information-sharing intervention concerning the risks of drug use. Despite growing evidence of the usefulness of this method, legal obstacles in many countries constrain the implementation of drug checking. The purpose of this Finnish study was to evaluate the usefulness of a paraphernalia-based drug-checking protocol.MethodsA total of 503 drug paraphernalia samples were submitted between April 2022 and July 2024 to 9 different harm reduction services. The samples were analysed monthly via chromatography‒mass spectrometry, and the visitors had the option to return to discuss the results after laboratory analysis. The questionnaire data were collected both during sample submission and the discussion of the results. Additionally, six thematic paired interviews were conducted with staff who performed drug checking at different service locations.ResultsThe concordance between the promised and detected contents varied greatly depending on the submitted substance. Over two-thirds of the samples were submitted by visitors who had never used substance use services before. The visitors returned for the laboratory results for only 208 samples. The interviewed staff believed that a long waiting time was the main reason for the low return rate. The staff also identified several advantages. The sample submission as well as the dissemination of the recent results via posters at the service points offered an easy way to approach people and discuss different topics related to drug use, harm reduction, and social and health issues.ConclusionThe protocol used was too slow for visitors to make preconsumption decisions. However, this shortcoming was not directly connected to the use of paraphernalia samples instead of actual drug samples, as the protocol could be accelerated with sufficient resources. The use of paraphernalia also caused uncertainties related to presubmission contamination. However, the protocol proved to be beneficial for initiating harm reduction discussions during the submission of the samples and the public dissemination of the results at the service locations, and should be considered as a starting point for drug checking when the use of actual drug samples is not possible.

Background

This Finnish study examines the usefulness of a drug-checking protocol using drug paraphernalia with trace amounts of drugs for harm reduction. The protocol was chosen to bypass legal obstacles. The results of this study provide new insight into the benefits and pitfalls of conducting drug checking in restricted legal environments.

Drug checking is a method of harm reduction in which drug samples are submitted by people who use drugs (PWUD) for an analysis of their chemical structure, the results of which, along with the effects and risks of the substance found, are discussed with the person who submitted the sample. Barratt and Measham [1] note that the aim of harm reduction, public collection of samples, and return of results to the person who submitted the samples, including information exchange and tailored intervention, should be features of every drug checking service (DCS). Brief intervention and motivational intervention techniques are mentioned as methods used in the interventions [2, 3], although the interventions vary across DCSs [1].

A variety of protocols fall under this definition of drug checking and differ from each other in terms of cost, speed of analysis, mobility, setting, reliability and accuracy [4, 5]. The analytical tools for detecting the composition of a drug vary from inexpensive, easy-to-use and fast test strips that can only detect the presence of a specific substance, to chromatography–mass spectrometry that detect different components and even concentration accurately and reliably, but are slower, more expensive, and require trained technicians [4]. Other methods fall between these options in terms of price, speed of analysis and accuracy [6]. In recent studies, multi-instrument approaches have been encouraged [4]. The time from sample submission to receiving results varies from minutes to weeks in different DCSs [5].

The choice of method is determined by the needs and financial resources of each particular setting. Historically, drug checking has been mostly oriented towards recreational drug use [6]. However, in recent decades, more DCSs have been designed for people at high risk of overdose, and are operating in settings such as needle exchange programs, supervised drug consumption sites and other harm reduction services [7, 8]. Additionally, third parties, such as friends and professionals, can submit samples on behalf of people who use drugs (PWUD) [9].

The most direct benefit at the individual level is the disposal of drugs in one’s possession if the sample is not what the individual believed it would be [10]. Several studies indicate that PWUD are willing to change their patterns of drug use after using DCSs, especially when the findings do not match their expectations (misrepresentations or adulterants) [11, 12]. DCSs visitors dose reduction intention is associated with lower odds of overdosing [13]. DCSs can also act as effective pathways to obtaining support for other needs [14].

In addition to benefits at the individual level, drug checking can impact policy, community, and the drug market levels [15]. Drug checking contributes to drug market monitoring by detecting new psychoactive substances, drugs of concern (e.g., highly potent opioids) and unexpected substances [13]. In particular, information about the concordance between what drugs are acquired as or expected to be and the detected chemical composition is difficult to obtain with any other monitoring method. Other sources (e.g. confiscations or surveys) typically yield information related either only to the buyer’s or user’s expectations, or to the chemical composition of the substance. At the community level, the information received from the DCS is shared with other PWUD and people who sell drugs [13, 16]. The sharing of information increases the accountability of the sellers in the drug market. PWUD are more aware of what substances they are purchasing and using, and sellers are able to check their own products [15]. One wider benefit is that DCSs are able to reach PWUD who have not received substance use-related support or care [10].

DCS facilities and services should be designed according to the local context and the target group. There is no “one size fits all” solution [17]. The location should be a familiar, trusted and private environment [16]. PWUD have identified low price, rapid test results, the small amount of substance required for a sample, and non-judgemental harm reduction messaging as important positive factors in their experience with DCSs [15]. In addition, peers and people with personal experience of DCSs can help create a safe environment for visitors [18].

Because the possession or handling of drugs is often illegal in most countries, DCSs often operate under different arrangements in a “legal grey area” [e.g., 4, 5] without comprehensive legislation or with only temporary permits [e.g., 5]. New Zealand was the first country to introduce permanent national legislation for drug checking in 2020 [19]. These legal restrictions are often obstacles when planning the best protocols for DCS in terms of harm reduction [14]. In some countries, the legal framework does not allow drug checking at all [20].

A 2022 survey found that two-thirds of Finns fully or partially accepted anonymous drug checking [21]. However, the legal framework for drug checking in Finland is challenging, as it seems de facto impossible to obtain a permit to receive and handle drugs acquired from the illegal market for harm reduction purposes. Instead, drug paraphernalia with trace amounts of drugs have been examined for harm reduction (e.g., needle exchange) [22] and research purposes [23], since trace amounts are not considered to be drugs in a legal sense.

The rate of drug-induced deaths caused by poisonings have been increasing in Finland for decades [24]. Compared with the Nordic [24] and European [25] contexts, young people are over-represented in death statistics in Finland. Therefore new methods are needed to reduce the harm caused by drug use.

The current study explores a two-year drug-checking project in which drug paraphernalia samples were used as a basis for chemical analysis. The project was based on experiences gained from a four-month pilot study conducted between 2018 and 2019 [26]. Similar paraphernalia-based drug checking protocols were also used in 2019 in a Canadian study that used both actual drugs and drug administration equipment as a source for drug checking. The study did not include much comparative analysis on the differences between these two types of samples. However, it noted that the analysed drug administration equipment could have been used multiple times with different substances, leading to incorrect interpretations of the analytic data [27]. Additionally, multiple studies have used paraphernalia, such as syringes [23] and plastic bags for storing drugs [28], as sources of information about substances in the drug market, without reporting the results to PWUD. These studies have proved to be good methods for the detection of new substances in local markets and for monitoring differences between geographical locations. However, since the paraphernalia was not collected directly from the users, these studies do not have data on what the substances were sold as. Additionally, it is unknown whether the paraphernalia had been used multiple times.

Context, data and methods

Aim and context

The overall aim of this study is to examine the usefulness of a drug paraphernalia-based drug-checking protocol for harm reduction. The questions are as follows:

Drug checking began in April 2022 and was conducted in services that were directed towards people who actively use drugs. In 2022, drug checking was conducted via three needle and syringe programs, two low-threshold meeting points without sobriety requirements directed for PWUD, and a 24/7 support point for young PWUD. At the end of 2023, the sample collection expanded to two housing first services and a shelter for PWUD. Sample submission remained open until July 2024. Initially, only the research project staff collected samples at each service location on different weekdays; however, after autumn 2022 the staff at the service locations were gradually trained to collect samples and discuss the results.

For legal reasons, drug paraphernalia samples (for storing, preparing or using drugs) were used instead of actual drugs to detect the chemical composition of the drug in people’s possession. The sample could contain only a trace amount of the substance: as a rule of thumb, it could not contain an amount that could be used for intoxication. Since the used laboratory methods (detailed description later under the heading “Laboratory analysis”) were very accurate, even hardly visible residues were sufficient for analysis. The chosen protocol did not restrict preconsumption drug checking. It was possible, for example, to create a paraphernalia sample by transferring a powder from a plastic bag to a new bag, and submit the original bag for analysis.

The samples were retrieved from the service locations once a month and transported to a laboratory for analysis. After the analysis, visitors had the possibility to return to the site and discuss the laboratory results of the sample they had submitted by using a pseudonym of their choice. Every four months, a summary report of the results was produced. A more detailed version was published on the project webpage and A3-sized posters of the main points were placed at the drug checking service locations. In the last four months of data collection, summary reports were produced once a month.

The protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethical Committee of the A-Clinic Foundation. Research permits were obtained from three NGOs and the city of Helsinki, which operate the services where the samples were collected. To ensure visitor privacy and the accuracy of the results, precise protocols were followed for data collection, the transfer of the samples and laboratory results, and the results submission.

Data

The data consisted of multiple different data collections: drug checking results, visitor questionnaire data, and paired interview data from the staff in charge of collecting the samples.

Drug checking results 503 drug paraphernalia samples containing trace amounts of drugs were collected, consisting of plastic bags (n = 382), mixing cups (n = 50), injection filters (n = 6), filter + cup combinations (n = 6), Eppendorf tubes (n = 14), folies (n = 11), blister bags (n = 7), and other miscellaneous paraphernalia (n = 18). Syringes used for injection could not be submitted, and the option to submit blister bags was eliminated during data collection, due to suspicions of presubmission contamination1 of the samples.

The visitor questionnaire data Visitors (persons who submitted a sample) were asked for descriptive information about the sample, suggestions on how the service could be improved, and, if the substance had been used, information on the effects and routes of administration. After the laboratory analysis, visitors had the opportunity to return to discuss the laboratory results of their sample under a pseudonym, including additional questions related to the results, ideas on how to improve the drug checking protocol, and a few optional demographic questions (gender, age group, education, employment status and history of substance use services and drug injections). After the first year of the study, it became possible to discuss the results via voice calls (phone, Session Signal, Wickr, Telegram and WhatsApp). The same pseudonyms or the pseudonym plus a running number were assumed to refer to the same participant. However, it is also possible that same person submitted samples multiple times under different pseudonyms.

Paired interviews for staff A total of six pair interviews were conducted at the six service locations created in 2022. The interviews were conducted between October and December 2024. When possible, the employees who had collected the most samples were prioritized in the selection of interviewees. Five of the interviewees were working in managerial positions, five as counsellors, and two as nurses. Ten of the 12 interviewees were women. The interviews were conducted by the project researcher and the project coordinator, who was responsible for the practicalities of drug checking. The interviews were led by the project researcher. Both interviewers had experience with sample collection and discussion of the results. On average, the interviews lasted for 58 min (45 to 78 min) and were recorded and transcribed. The interviews were divided into four themes: (1) Practical implementation of drug checking at the service location; (2) Drug checking as part of substance use work; (3) Communication of the findings; and (4) feedback.

Methods

Three methods were used in this study. The chemical compositions of the samples were analysed in the laboratory. The data from the laboratory analysis were merged with visitor questionnaire data into a dataset that was analysed via (mostly) statistical methodology. The interview data was analysed qualitatively.

Laboratory analysis The forensic toxicology laboratory of the Forensic Chemistry Unit of the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare analysed samples once a month via both ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) and high-resolution time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UHPLC-HR-TOF-MS). Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC‒MS) was used if needed. The methodologies have been described in detail elsewhere [23, 29]. The cut-off levels of both methods are in the low ng/mL range and are therefore more than sufficient for drug residue analysis. Mainly psychoactive components were reported, however, some nonpsychoactive substances typically found in illegal drugs were also reported. The substance identification was qualitative. However, in the case of multiple findings, the order of the amount present of the identified substances was reported approximately based on magnitude of the signal response. The concentration percentages could not be determined based on the chemical analysis.

Analysis of the questionnaire and laboratory data After the laboratory and questionnaire data were merged, the discrepancy between the chemical composition and what the sample was sold or acquired as was coded into a dataset. The samples were divided into six different categories based on the level of discrepancy: (1) Full concordance; (2) Other substances found, correct main finding; (3) Other substances found, incorrect main finding; (4) No concordance; (5) No psychoactive substances found; (6) Unclear what the sample was sold or acquired as. If the chemical composition did not match the expected content or if the visitor reported serious side effects, the questionnaire answers were analysed qualitatively to determine possible explanations for the findings. For example, there were samples in which the most likely explanation for discrepancy was presubmission contamination such as handling a sample with fingers or a spoon containing other drugs, or using the same plastic bag multiple times. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 29 [30] was used for the data analysis. Frequencies, means and crosstabulations are presented in the study. The chi-square test and an independent variables t test were used to analyse differences between groups.

Analysis of the interviews The interview data were analysed qualitatively via NVivo version 14 [31]. The qualitative analysis preceded the quantitative analysis presented above, as the purpose was to understand more deeply the findings of the quantitative analysis. All the transcribed data were first read through and, in the second reading, coded under 14 different codes. Six of the codes (benefits, ideas for improvement, dissemination of the findings, submission of the samples, discussion of the results and new visitor groups) were finally evaluated as relevant to this study. The six codes were reviewed multiple times, and notes were taken during the analysis.

Results

Concordance between laboratory findings and questionnaire answers

The samples (N = 503) were most often submitted as amphetamine (n = 124), alprazolam (n = 60), MDMA (n = 56) or cocaine (n = 49). There were also 44 samples submitted as different synthetic cathinones. A total of 225 samples were acquired by the visitor within a week before submission of the sample. However, 39 samples were acquired more than 2 months—and sometimes several months—before submission.

Amphetamine. Amphetamine was detected in 85% of the samples submitted as amphetamine; it was the only psychoactive substance in only 10 samples. Other substances most commonly found in the samples submitted as amphetamine were other stimulants commonly used as adulterants (e.g., caffeine, n = 79), or byproducts (e.g., DPIA, n = 51). However, some samples contained substances that are not typical for illegal amphetamine markets, such as methomyl, paracetamol, midazolam, buprenorphine and nicotine. There are a few possible explanations for these findings: (1) Substances had been added to the sample on purpose by the dealer; (2) the sample had been contaminated (e.g., by using the same bag multiple times or reaching into it with dirty fingers); (3) the sample had been mistaken for another similar sample before submission; and (4) the sample had been tampered with before submission. Especially concerning in one of the samples was the detection of methomyl, a pesticide associated with cocaine use poisoning in Italy [32]. The visitor who submitted the sample reported that two people had experienced serious poisoning symptoms when using the substance. Methomyl has not been previously reported in Finnish amphetamine samples.

Alprazolam. Samples acquired as alprazolam were common. Alprazolam (typically sold in illegal markets under the brand Ksalol) is widely available in Finland. Rumours of counterfeit tablets containing fentanyl have been circulating among PWUD, officials, and substance use treatment personnel, possibly because fentanyl analogues have been detected in alprazolam samples abroad [33]. However, there was no indication of fentanyl as an adulterant or replacement. A few samples were likely contaminated, as other common medical drugs (buprenorphine and methylphenidate) were found as secondary substances in the samples. There were also four samples that did not contain alprazolam, but contained other substances (bromazepam2, amitriptyline, desloratadine and prednisolone) that can imitate some features of alprazolam.

MDMA. MDMA was the main substance found in 52 of the 56 samples acquired as MDMA. In 45 samples, only MDMA was detected. Most of the other substances detected were other stimulants. MDMA samples typically came in the form of tablets, crystals, or powders. One sample was submitted after the police had issued a public warning for particularly dangerous grey owl-shaped tablets [34]. However, only MDMA was detected in the tablet. Later, the National Bureau of Investigation Forensic Laboratory confirmed that the tablets confiscated by the local police also consisted of only MDMA.

Cocaine. Forty-nine samples had been acquired as cocaine. Unadulterated cocaine was detected in almost half of the samples, and traces of cocaine were detected in another one-third of the samples. The majority of other compounds found in the samples containing cocaine were typical cocaine adulterants [35], such as levamisole, phenacetine and caffeine. In the samples in which cocaine was not detected, typical compounds were other stimulants (amphetamine and different synthetic cathinones).

The synthetic cathinones. A total of 44 samples were acquired as synthetic cathinones. More specifically, the samples had been acquired as “PV” (referring to synthetic cathinones in general, n = 28), alpha-PVP (n = 6), MDPV (n = 3), MMC (n = 3), alpha-PHP (n = 2), alpha-PVT (n = 1) and 2-CMC (n = 1). In Finland, synthetic cathinones are relatively common and are often detected in wastewater analysis [36] and postmortem toxicology [37]. In the samples where synthetic cathinones were detected as the main compounds, the most common main compounds were alpha-PHiP (13/16) during the first year of the data collection and alpha-PVP (10/16) in the samples submitted to the study after April 2023. There were also samples that contained other stimulants (caffeine, cocaine, fluoroamphetamine and amphetamine) instead of synthetic cathinones. There were a few samples in which no psychoactive substances were detected and a few samples containing pharmaceutical compounds such as hydroxyzine, buprenorphine and paracetamol.

Coverage of the drug checking model

A total of 503 samples were submitted under 279 different pseudonyms, which are here assumed to be different visitors. Some background information was obtained from the visitors. However, these questions were voluntary, and 12.9%−25.1% of the information was unobtained. Two-thirds of the visitors were men. A total of 28.2% were 30 years old or younger, 48.5% were between 31 and 45 years old, and 23.2% were older than 45 years. Half (50.9%) of the visitors were unemployed, 30.9% were employed, 5.2% were students, and 10.8% fell under the category “other”. One fourth (24.4%) had completed tertiary education, 37.8% had completed secondary education, 32.5% had completed elementary education, and few persons did not have an elementary education. A total of 58.1% of the visitors had injected drugs. 44.5% had injected drugs in the past month.

Three-fourths of the samples were collected via three syringe exchange programs. Only three samples were submitted to the two housing services and a shelter, which started drug checking at the end of 2023. There was a categorized question (6 categories, with the possibility of choosing many alternatives) asking why a visitor had selected the site for submission. The most common answers were “the location was suitable” (n = 249), followed by “I come here anyways” (n = 110) and “it was open today” (n = 102).

Although the services where the samples were collected were directed at people who use drugs intravenously, or those with social or health problems related to drug use, a considerable proportion of the samples (41.0%, 11.3% missing) were submitted by PWUD who had never used substance use services (SUSs) before. These visitors were more likely to have degrees in tertiary education (51.7% and 5.0%; p < 0.001) and more likely to be employed (62.8% and 9.1%, p < 0,001). On average, visitors without prior SUS contact submitted more samples per person than did visitors with prior SUS contact (2.4 and 1,4; p < 0.001). Only 10.7% of the visitors without prior SUS contact had ever injected drugs, whereas 88.0% (p < 0.001) of the visitors with SUS contact had injected drugs. There were also differences in the samples submitted between visitors with and without prior SUS contacts. Most samples submitted by visitors with prior SUS contacts were acquired as amphetamines (n = 61), alprazolam (n = 41), and “PV” (n = 20), whereas most samples submitted by visitors without prior SUS contacts were acquired as MDMA (n = 49), amphetamine (n = 48), and cocaine (n = 33). Visitors without prior SUS contact were also more likely to return to hear the result (60.4%) than were visitors who had used substance use treatment services in the past (21.8%; p < 0.001).

One fifth (n = 109) of the samples were submitted on behalf of someone else. 51 of these samples were submitted with a note clarifying on whose behalf the sample was submitted. 19 of these were submitted by a professional, 18 on behalf of a friend, eleven samples had been found (e.g., from a clinic), and three had been summitted by a peer.

Effects on pre-use decision-making

Only in the case of 208 samples (41.4%) did the visitor return to discuss the results. The return rate was higher for visitors without prior SUS contact (60.4%) than for visitors with prior SUS contact (21.8%, p < 0.001). The interviewed staff members believed that the long waiting time was the main reason for the low return rate. The time from submission to receiving results varied between 2 and 77 days (mean: 29.1, median: 27 days). Other reasons suggested by the interviewed staff for the low return rate were also connected to the long waiting time: visitors forgot that they had submitted a sample, staff forgot who had submitted the sample and were unable to remind them, visitors changed within a month, or no longer needed the results. As one interviewed staff member noted, it was sometimes possible for a visitor to determine the result of their sample from the summary reports or posters published every four months. In the visitor questionnaires, the speed of the results was also often highlighted as an area for development. After the first voice call contact (25.1.2023), 61.6% of the results were discussed remotely. However, the overall return rate did not increase after this option was introduced.

Of the 394 samples that were not submitted on behalf of others, only 121 had not been used before submission. The visitors with prior SUS contact (83.1%) had used the drug in the sample more often than had those without prior SUS contact (52.6%, p < 0.001). As the interviewed staff members explained, their customers usually only have their daily dose in their possession. Many interviewees noted that it might not even make a difference if the waiting time were a week instead of four weeks; the visitors wanted the results to be available instantly, as with screening tests for HIV, HVC or inflammation (CRP).I think that our visitors in particular want it [the results] right away. But those who came from outside of the services, they could wait and returned to ask for the results.

Upon discussing the results, the visitors were asked if the laboratory findings matched their expectations (n = 178). Also, the visitors were asked if they had used the drug they had submitted for analysis after they had received the result (n = 163). Among the visitors who submitted their own sample and answered both questions (n = 131), 61.4% speculated that they would use the patch in the same way as before, 7.9% would use the patch differently, 21.4% would not use the patch, and 9.3% might use the patch. In 61.5% of the cases (16.3% did not have expectations or had not answered), the laboratory findings matched the expectations of the visitor who returned to discuss the results. If the result matched the visitor’s expectations, 77.1% would use the patch in the same way as before, whereas only 31.4% (p < 0.001) of the visitors would use the patch in the same way if the result did not match their expectations (see Fig. 1). Changes in dosing and a need for additional information were the most common reasons cited by visitors who were either hesitant to use or who intended to use the patch differently.

Undirect advantages of the model

Surprisingly, the time when visitors returned for the results was not seen as an opportune moment for these discussions, as visitors were often in a hurry:I found out that so many people just want their results as quickly as possible, and then they just whoosh away.

Sample submission as a tool for harm reduction. In contrast, staff members expressed that they often had good conversations with visitors during the submission of samples. Even when employees and visitors already knew each other, the submission of the sample and the questions asked about the sample, the side effects, the methods of drug use, the simultaneous use of other substances, and the prior use of the SUS offered natural opportunities to discuss a variety of topics. Because the service locations were often busy and noisy, the submission of samples took place in a separate room, which offered an opportunity to discuss harm reduction practices, infections, and other needs for assistance in a relatively peaceful environment:[…For PWUD visiting the premises] help is not asked for until the need is really prolonged, whether it is wound care or guidance in social issues. In this [drug checking], you submit the residues of a bag, and we can chat while we fill out the questionnaire, and an infection or some other need for support can come up. It’s a pretty good tool.

Several interviewees also described the discussions during the submission as moments when the visitor had to “stop and think” about their drug use. In the context of drug checking, it was possible to discuss issues related to drug use in a way that was not considered patronizing but instead as a service.

It was interesting for staff to meet new visitors who used drugs recreationally, as it widened their understanding of drug use. The discussions with new visitors were viewed as meaningful and opened possibilities to evaluate their situation and provide advice for harm reduction:And then there were people who did not fit the profile [of the visitors in the service]. Their drug was cocaine and they were more educated. Information sharing at the services as a warning channel. Posters with current test results were mentioned by all six interviewees as convenient sources of information concerning harm reduction. The posters were mainly printed and displayed at those service locations that were part of the drug checking model. However, the interviewees thought that other SUSs, as well as other professionals, such as youth workers and staff working in emergency rooms or paramedic services, could also have benefited from the posters. Longer and more detailed online reports were also produced every four months but were not mentioned spontaneously in the interviews.

The presence of posters with drug-related information made it easy to approach visitors, initiate a discussion, and get straight to the point. A few interviewees mentioned that the discussions were not only one-sided, and that the staff also learned new things. Visitors were particularly interested in different types of cutting agents and impurities:We actually sat down and went through [the findings], and googled what the [laboratory] findings meant and in which drugs they can be detected. I feel that people were very interested in those different [findings] as they sounded odd, and all sorts of things were detected: I don’t even remember all [of the substances detected]. It was clearly interesting. I don’t know how that affected what they buy next time … However, at least we increased a certain awareness that when you buy cocaine, it is not [always] cocaine, but a variety of other substances.

Then, it [information] spread like a wildfire among visitors, as it instantly made you wonder how many could have had this pesticide or whatnot. It is really useful in such cases. The information [present in the service locations] itself might only reach a small group of people. But when it reaches the right verbally skilled, all-knowing mouth—someone who wants to spread their ‘doctor-level’ knowledge to everyone—then there are definite upsides to it.

Discussion

The usefulness of a drug-checking protocol based on trace amounts of drugs found in paraphernalia was examined in this study. The protocol used was too slow for drug checking visitors to make decisions before consumption. However, the protocol proved to be useful in other ways. Surprisingly, the time of sample submission—instead of the time when visitors returned for the results of the laboratory analysis—was perceived as better for discussing harm reduction. Posters with the latest results placed at drug checking locations increased awareness of situational availability of drugs in illegal markets and opened possibilities for discussing harm reduction. The protocol attracted people who used drugs recreationally and had not used SUSs before.

Shortcomings of the drug checking protocol used

The long waiting time [see also 38] was the main reason for the low return rate for getting the results, and the reason why people used the sample before knowing its chemical content. Notably, this shortcoming was only indirectly caused by the use of drug paraphernalia instead of actual drug samples. Even though the analysis of trace amounts excludes many rapid detection methods, the process could be substantially shortened with greater financial resources. Interestingly, new analytical methods, such as paper spray mass spectrometry [36], are being developed. These methods could offer comparatively reliable drug checking from smaller samples, while being faster and easier to use than traditional chromatography-mass spectrometry methods.

Although the use of drug paraphernalia as a source for analysis presented challenges, the use of trace amounts was not a problem for the qualitative detection of substances. A similar methodology has been used for the detection of much smaller quantities of psychoactive substances in matrices such as wastewater [39]. Instead, challenges were related to presubmission contamination (e.g., the use of plastic bags multiple times or the handling of paraphernalia with fingers containing residue from cannabis) and the accidental submission of wrong samples. When collecting paraphernalia, it was impossible for a visitor or a professional to visually detect whether the sample contained the drug for which they were providing information at the time of submission. Moreover, the use of trace amounts did not allow for detailed quantitative analysis, and the approximate order of amounts is also prone to errors caused by the use of trace amounts. Hence, the use of paraphernalia reduces reliability and accuracy, which have been found to be important for the visitors of the drug checking services [40].

Benefits of the drug checking protocol used

Especially for PWUD with high-risk use patterns and substance use disorders, the results should be provided on the same day to facilitate preconsumption decision-making, which our protocol did not offer. However, our study revealed other potential long-term benefits, such as changes in use patterns, information sharing and discussions to raise awareness, and the possibility of discussing other issues that are only loosely related to drug checking. If the process were faster, paraphernalia-based drug checking would have the potential to promote preconsumption behavioural changes. As was reported in prior drug checking studies in the context of high-risk drug use, the disposal of drugs was “less relevant” [40] and not always a realistic [41] option for this target group, even if the chemical content was known and does not match the desired substance.

Barratt and Measham [1] stress that drug checking is not just the chemical analysis of drugs, but includes information exchange and a tailored intervention. This study indicates that the benefits of drug checking for brief interventions are not limited to the time when results are provided. Therefore, the submission phase should be considered as an important opportunity for information exchange and intervention. Also, especially in the DCSs operating as part of harm reduction services, posters designed for the dissemination of information can be useful for initiating discussions with visitors.

Providing drug checking at existing harm reduction services likely decreased the threshold [42] for participation. These services had already established trust among visitors [18], were anonymous, and had professionals who were trained to interact with PWUD. Additionally, the context of drug checking provides a basis for trustful and respectful discussion [40] of individuals’ drug use, which can be otherwise often difficult amid the daily routines of the services. Although drug checking in this study was implemented through services directed at people with high-risk drug use, half of the people submitting the samples had never used a substance use service. This finding implies that it is also possible to reach people who use drugs recreationally through harm reduction services as long as the location is easy to access. However, we do not know if the profile of the drug checking sites was a barrier for some individuals [43, 44].

The finding that one-fifth of the samples were submitted by friends and professionals on behalf of others is in line with the study by Larnder et al. [9], and the use of outreach work [17] and peers [18, 40] has been suggested in other studies. These findings suggest that the use of proxies should be taken into account when designing future drug checking protocols. The need to use of such proxies might also imply that the threshold for using the service is too high for some PWUD. Since the information given by proxies is second-hand, some essential details may be missing.

The concordance between the acquired and detected contents varied greatly depending on what drug had been acquired. Synthetic cathinones were often not acquired as a specific substance; therefore, the concordance could not be determined. There were also a few cases, in particular a methomyl sample acquired as amphetamine, which showed that the protocol can benefit the wider warning system. As in a prior study [41], more detailed content, such as information on impurities and cutting agents, interested visitors. The dissemination of such information directly to PWUD and field-level professionals can raise awareness and provoke thought. In particular, simple posters designed for visitors of SUSs were an effective way to disseminate information.

Research limitations and future research

The benefits of the model are contextual [17], and the benefits and shortcomings depend on the group that a DCS attempts to target. The legal environment and financial resources also limit what kind of protocol is possible. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted in the Finnish context. However, some of the findings are useful in countries with similar challenges in conducting drug checking. We were not able to interview visitors of the services; their views were obtained in the questionnaires. Nevertheless, interviews would have provided different insights into the study.

Drug checking protocols are adjusted to fit national legislative frameworks. The results of this study suggest that some of the benefits of drug checking can be attained with paraphernalia-based drug checking models. There is a gap in the research on legal constraints in countries not yet implementing drug checking, and on the ways other countries have been able to bypass such challenges. An understanding of legal constraints on an international level is essential for implementing paraphernalia-based drug checking in other countries. There is also no systematic research on the association between the speed of the drug checking procedure, which this study found to be an important factor for visitors, and the visitors’ return rate to hear the results.

Conclusion

As in recent North American studies [39, 45], the results of this study suggest that the benefits of drug checking should be considered from a broader perspective than merely that of just drug disposal. The drug-checking protocol used proved to be too slow for PWUD, especially for people with high risk of drug use, to make preconsumption decisions based on the laboratory analysis. However, there were various benefits in terms of both individual harm reduction discussions and the wider community. Drug checking offered a context to reach people and have conversations that in many cases otherwise would not have happened. The protocol also reached PWUD whose drug use can be described as recreational and who had never used any services related to drug use. Risks particular to this group [6], such as insufficient knowledge and impurity of drugs, should be addressed. According to the staff who were conducting drug checking, the main benefits were discussions with visitors during the submission of the samples and in relation to the posters at the drug checking service locations, which offered opportunities to initiate discussions and disseminate the results.

The use of drug paraphernalia for drug checking is not an ideal solution. However, the findings of this study show that benefits linked to drug checking can be achieved even if only paraphernalia can be used. The greatest challenge—the time between submission and laboratory results—can be improved with better resources. Drug paraphernalia-based drug checking can be an option in legal environments that do not allow the use of drug samples. In these situations, some benefits of drug checking can still be obtained, and fears related to drug checking can be alleviated among the public and policy-makers.
