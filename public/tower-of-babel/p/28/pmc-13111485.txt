Embryonic exposure to environmental levels of roundup affects neurodevelopment in zebrafish
Authors: Rachel Lacroix, Valentina Almanza, Sophie McKenzie, Mataea Armstrong, Deborah Kurrasch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractN-(phosphonomethyl)glycine (glyphosate) is the most sprayed herbicidal chemical in the world. Although glyphosate is considered safe in humans and higher animals due to its targeting of the shikimate pathway found only in plants and microorganisms, recent in vivo and in vitro studies show evidence of toxicity across a range of systems, including the vertebrate brain. Given that developing brains with immature blood-brain barriers can be especially sensitive to environmental contaminants, here we test the effects of embryonic exposure to environmental levels of glyphosate or its commercial formulation, Roundup®. Embryonic zebrafish exposed to an environmental concentration of Roundup (10 µg/L acid equivalent) from 10 to 48 hours postfertilization (hpf) showed defects in morphology, respiratory capacity, and hatching at 48 hpf as well as changes in locomotion, light-startle response, and thigmotaxis behaviors at 5 days postfertilization (dpf), with some effects lasting in the juvenile. To understand neurodevelopmental changes underlying these behavioral abnormalities, we tested for changes to the timing of neurogenesis and conducted bulk RNA sequencing. We found increased neurogenesis and uncovered dysregulation of axonogenesis pathways, which was confirmed using immunohistochemistry. Finally, to test if axonal deficits might manifest as dysregulation of neuronal circuits, we observed changes in phospho-extracellular signal-regulated kinases levels and Ca2+ dynamics as indicators of activity disruptions at 5 dpf following embryonic exposure to Roundup. These results suggest that Roundup at current environmental levels may not be safe for organismal exposure, and glyphosate toxicity warrants closer attention.

N-(phosphonomethyl)glycine (glyphosate, CAS: 1071-83-6, C3H8NO5P) is a systemic, broad-spectrum herbicide first synthesized in 1950 [1]. Since commercialized by Monsanto in 1974, glyphosate has become the most-applied herbicidal chemical globally [2, 3], with usage still increasing year over year [3, 4]. Glyphosate is available widely, with hundreds of glyphosate-based herbicides (GBHs) now on the market [5]. Most GBHs are proprietary formulations, containing adjuvants such as surfactants that increase the uptake of glyphosate into the plant [6, 7]. Genetically modified Roundup Ready™ crops carry a glyphosate-resistant version of 5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase, glyphosate's target enzyme found only in plants and microorganisms [8]. Roundup Ready crops further contribute to an increased global prevalence of glyphosate due to increased food yield because of widespread weed elimination.

Human exposure to glyphosate is becoming nearly ubiquitous, with glyphosate observed in nonagricultural habitats [9] due to foliage and soil runoff, GBH spray drift, and/or uptake by other organisms [10]. Indeed, glyphosate is detected in aquatic environments worldwide [11], with surface water concentrations measuring from <2 ng/L [12] to 10 mg/L [13]. Glyphosate also reaches domestic environments, evidenced by detectable levels in human bodily fluids including urine [14, 15] and breast milk [16]. Some grains are harvested shortly after GBH application, including rye, barley, and wheat, which can lead to downstream contamination of food products such as cereals and crackers. Notably, some food residue levels exceed the maximum safe levels set by government agencies [17, 18]. Given glyphosate's pervasive presence, continued research into its potential effects on human health is needed to mitigate potential current and future risks.

Glyphosate is presumed safe in higher organisms due to the lack of its target enzyme, 5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase; however, off-target effects are observed across a variety of species [9]. In mammals, glyphosate is best studied for its carcinogenic and cytotoxic effects [19, 20], with emerging reports suggesting effects within the brain as well. In vitro, glyphosate can be toxic to neuronal cell lines [21] and impair an isogenic model of the human blood-brain barrier [22]. In vivo, relatively low levels of glyphosate in adult rats can impair neuroplasticity, synaptic functioning, and associated behaviors [23-25]. In humans, cohort studies show that exposure to GBHs increases the risk of autism spectrum disorder in children exposed prenatally [26] and causes memory impairment [27] and other neurological outcomes in exposed persons [28]. Recent attention has focused on the possibility that the commercial formulation of GBHs may be more toxic than glyphosate alone, with new reports showing coformulants themselves may amplify glyphosate effects or increase glyphosate's neurotoxic profile; for instance, 1 study shows GBH Roundup® Transorb impairs differentiation of neurons, an effect not appreciable in glyphosate alone [29]. Moreover, exposure to low doses of a GBH, but not glyphosate, prevents human neural stem cell differentiation in vitro [30] and drives apoptosis in adult mouse hippocampal cells in vitro [31]. In all, the impact of environmental levels of glyphosate on sensitive periods of brain development is unstudied.

Here we use zebrafish to assess the in vivo consequences of environmentally relevant levels of glyphosate or the GBH Roundup on neurodevelopment. We expose zebrafish to equivalent concentrations of either glyphosate or Roundup from 10 to 48 hours postfertilization (hpf), a critical embryonic period encompassing primary neurogenesis [32, 33], and investigate the impact on gross development as well as neurodevelopmental processes such as neurogenesis, axonogenesis, circuitry, and behavior. We find that low levels of Roundup during this restricted window of neurodevelopment lead to long-lasting effects on neuronal function and behaviors, adding to a growing body of literature suggesting GBHs are neurotoxic.

Material and methods

Zebrafish husbandry and chemical exposure

All protocols and procedures were approved by the University of Calgary Animal Care Committee (#AC22-0153). Tüpfel Long-Fin strain zebrafish were maintained at 28 °C for the entirety of the study. Glyphosate (44 521; Sigma) and Roundup Ready-to-Use Grass and Weed Control Non-Selective Herbicide With FastAct® Foam (PCP #22807; Bayer; herein referred to as Roundup) were diluted in 1x E3 embryo medium (NaCl, KCl, CaCl2·2H2O, and MgCl2·6H2O diluted in water and supplemented with 1% methylene blue) to working concentrations of 10 µg/L acid equivalent (a.e.; active ingredient as expressed in terms of glyphosate acid). Due to the proprietary nature of the Roundup formulation used, individual nonactive (inert) ingredients could not be tested as they are not reported; analyses were conducted on the full product mixture and on glyphosate alone, and results apply only to this specific formulation, as other Roundup products contain different active ingredients. For Roundup, we calculated this based on the provided active ingredient concentration of 7 g/L. Zebrafish embryos were exposed to control (vehicle), glyphosate, or Roundup at 10 hpf until 48 hpf, at which point embryos were washed with vehicle thrice. Solutions were replenished once every 24 hours. After wash-out, embryos were maintained in E3 medium until assays were performed. For assays performed past 5 days postfertilization (dpf), zebrafish were fed Gemma Micro-75 (#10818935; Skretting) food once daily commencing at 7 dpf. For juvenile assays (eg, 21, 45 dpf) fish were placed into tanks in our flow-through system until assessed. Across studies, larval or juvenile fish were used prior to the completion of sexual differentiation; thus separate sexes could not be determined in larval fish, nor could they be absolutely distinguished in behavioral studies of juveniles. For seahorse bioenergetics experiments, oligomycin, carbonyl cyanide p-trifluoro methoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP), and antimycin A stocks were prepared at 10 mM and diluted prior to the assay.

Lethal concentration at which 50% of embryos die, hatching, and morphological assessments

To assess mortality, zebrafish were exposed to a series of glyphosate or Roundup concentrations ranging from 1 ng/L to 1 g/L a.e. The ratio of zebrafish alive at the beginning of the exposure (10 hpf) compared to the end of exposure (48 hpf) per petri dish was used to calculate the lethal concentration at which 50% of embryos die (LC50). To assess hatching rates, the proportion of zebrafish hatched vs unhatched was counted at 48 hpf in control vs treatments. For morphological assessments, zebrafish were imaged at 5 dpf from a dorsal view using a Discovery V8 SteREO microscope (Zeiss), and body length, head diameter, and interoptic distance were measured using Fiji [34]. All hatching, mortality, and morphological assessments were performed at least in triplicate.

Seahorse bioenergetics assay

To assess mitochondrial dysfunction, we investigated in vivo bioenergetics in 2 dpf larvae using a Seahorse Mito Stress Test Kit (Agilent), which we previously showed predominantly measures brain neurometabolism given the high numbers of mitochondria in neural cells relative to the rest of the body [35]. Briefly, 2 dpf zebrafish were seeded in specialized microchambers in a 24-well islet microplate (Seahorse XFe24 FluxPak; #102340-100; Agilent) protected by a mesh islet capture screen approximately 30 minutes prior to loading into the XF24 Extracellular Analyzer instrument (Agilent; RRID: SCR_019539) at 2 dpf. Four wells were left blank to control for temperature fluctuations. Oxygen consumption rates (pmol O2/min) and extracellular acidification rates (pmol H+/min) were tested at basal levels and after exposure to 100 µM oligomycin, 37.5 µM FCCP, and 30 µM antimycin A. To record these steps, larvae were exposed to 27 cycles of 2 minutes of mixing, 1 minute of waiting, and 2 minutes of measurement, with drug injection points at cycle 7 (oligomycin), cycle 15 (FCCP), and cycle 21 (antimycin A). Parameters are calculated as described by Stackley et al [35].

Behavioral assays

Behavior was assessed in 5 dpf larvae and 21 and 45 dpf juveniles. To analyze behavior, larval zebrafish were tested in a ZebraBox apparatus with ViewPoint Software (ViewPoint Behaviour Technology, Montreal, Canada), while juveniles were tested in either the ZebraBox or ZebraCube. To assess locomotor and thigmotaxis responses, larval zebrafish were assessed in 48-well plates for 15 minutes in the light followed by 15 minutes in the dark, before testing responses to a light startle (5 successive cycles of 1 minute of light, 1 minute of dark). Active speed was calculated as the distance traveled at a speed >4 mm/s, with a speed <4 mm/s defined as inactivity. Freezing behavior was quantified as entry into inactivity speed. Total distance was calculated as all distance traveled regardless of speed. For juvenile (eg, 21 and 45 dpf) locomotor assessments, zebrafish were tracked in 6- or 12-well plates in the ZebraBox, with active speed calculated from any movement >10 mm/s. For the black-white preference test in larvae, we placed a black and white disc under each well of a 6-well plate such that each well was one-half white and one-half black. Black-white preference was quantified in terms of time spent in the black over the white region. For larval conspecifics analyses, we designed a 3-chamber tank that was manufactured by a local acrylic company (Acrylic Concepts, Calgary, Canada). We placed a sibling larva in the social region while the nonsocial region was blank; conspecifics preference was measured as proportion of time spent nearest the conspecifics chamber. For juvenile conspecifics analyses, we used larger tanks with a divider separating 2 halves and assessed conspecifics preferences as described previously. All behavior analyses were performed at least in triplicate (3 separate clutches).

Neurogenesis assessments

Control, glyphosate-, and Roundup-exposed embryos were pulse-labeled with 1 mM 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU; C10338; Molecular Probes) in vehicle or glyphosate or Roundup E3 buffer at either 9, 24, 48, 72, 96, or 120 hpf, as described previously [36]. Briefly, embryos were exposed to EdU for 15 minutes on ice, followed by 15 minutes at room temperature (RT) for a total time of 30 minutes. Embryos were washed thrice with vehicle and maintained at 28 °C until sacrifice at 5 dpf. For zebrafish pulsed with EdU at 24 hpf, washes were performed thrice with vehicle, glyphosate, or Roundup (depending on treatment group) to ensure zebrafish were continuously exposed to their respective treatment during the exposure window. Zebrafish were sacrificed by immersion in ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS overnight (O/N) at 4 °C. The following day, embryos were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose in diethyl pyrocarbonate water, embedded, and snap frozen on a dry-ice/alcohol slurry in Optimum Cutting Temperature medium (CA95057-838; VMR Scientific) and cryosectioned at 10 microns. For immunohistochemical processing, antigen retrieval was performed by immersion in citrate buffer for a total of 15 minutes, washed with 0.1% PBS-Triton-X100 (PBS-T), and then permeabilized with 1% PBS-T for 30 minutes at RT. Sections were blocked in 5% normal goat serum (NGS; G9023; Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.1% PBS-T for 1 hour. Following blocking, slices were incubated with a mouse monoclonal antibody detecting the human HuC/D neuronal protein (A-21271; Invitrogen; RRID: AB_221448) at a concentration of 1:200 in block O/N at 4 °C. The following day, after washing 4 × 10 minutes with 0.1% PBS-T, slices were incubated in secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor 488 goat α-mouse IgG (A11001, Molecular Probes; RRID: AB_2534069) for 2 hours at RT or O/N at 4 °C. After washing 4 × 10 minutes, slices were incubated in Click-iT EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (C10338; Invitrogen) cocktail for 2 hours at RT, covered from light. Slices were washed 3 × 10 minutes and then treated with Hoescht 33 342 (1:1000; #H3570; Thermo Fisher) for 1 minute, followed by 3 quick 0.1% PBS-T washes. Slides were covered with mounting media and imaged with a Zeiss LSM880 Observer confocal microscope. For image analyses, we employed arivis (Zeiss) software to quantify EdU+ and HuC/D+ cells across telencephalic slices. To count neurons in the optic tecta, whole-mount immunohistochemistry was used. In brief, zebrafish were sacrificed and fixed with ice-cold 4% PFA and washed thrice, and antigen retrieval was performed with 150 mM Tris-HCl (pH = 9) for 15 minutes at 70 °C. Fish were permeabilized in 0.3% PBS-T/1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)/1% bovine serum albumin (BSA)/0.1% Tween-20 for 2 hours and blocked for 1 hour in 0.1% PBS-T/1% DMSO/2% fetal bovine serum/1% BSA/0.1% Tween-20. Embryos were incubated in HuC/D primary antibody (1:500) O/N, washed thrice, and incubated in secondary antibody (1:500) for 2 hours at RT. Embryos were washed thrice with 0.1% PBS-T and stained with Hoescht 33 342 (1:1000) for 5 minutes. After washes, larvae were mounted ventral-side up in 1% low melt temperature agarose and Z-stacks of the entire head were acquired using a Zeiss LSM880 Observer confocal microscope. To prevent double-counting of cells, HuC/D+ cells were counted every fourth Z-slice and graphed from the average counts from at least 5 slices per fish.

Cell cycle marker (Phospho-Histone H3) immunohistochemistry

To assess phospho-histone H3 (pHH3) protein distribution and quantity, 5 dpf zebrafish were sacrificed and fixed by immersion in ice-cold 4% PFA O/N at 4 °C, washed by 3 × 15-minute washes in 0.1% PBS-T, then dehydrated in methanol and bleached as described previously. After 3 × 15-minute washes in 0.1% PBS-T, antigen retrieval was performed by incubation in 150 mM TrisHCl (pH = 9.0) at 70 °C for 20 minutes, which was followed by 2 × 5-minute washes in 0.1% PBS-T. Permeabilization of samples was done by incubation in 0.05% Trypsin-EDTA (#2530005; Thermo Fisher) on ice for 45 minutes, followed by 3 × 15-minute washes. Samples were blocked in 5% NGS/1% BSA/1% DMSO in 0.1% PBS-T for 1 hour, then incubated with an antibody detecting pHH3 (#06-570; EMD Millipore; 1:500; RRID: AB_310177) O/N at 4 °C. Larvae were then washed 3 × 5 minutes in 0.1% PBS-T and incubated in secondary antibody (1:500) O/N. Larvae were mounted and imaged as described preiously, and pHH3+ cells were counted across Z-stack projections.

RNA sequencing of 2 dpf heads

For transcriptomics analyses, we exposed zebrafish to vehicle or glyphosate as per the previous exposure paradigm. At 48 hpf, zebrafish were sacrificed and immersed in RNAlater solution (AM7020; Thermo Fisher) and kept at 4 °C O/N and then at −20 °C until use. Zebrafish heads were dissected using a stereoscope and pooled for RNA extraction at 15 heads/pool (5 pooled samples each of control and glyphosate treatments). RNA was extracted using TRIzol™ (#15596026; Thermo Fisher) followed by ethanol precipitation. Samples were treated with Turbo DNAse (#AM2238; Invitrogen) and purified using GeneJET RNA Cleanup and Concentration Micro Kit (#K0841, Thermo Fisher). Total RNA quality and quantity were confirmed using a TapeStation (samples with RIN > 8 were used for sequencing). Sequencing was then performed by the Centre for Health Genomics and Informatics (University of Calgary); in brief, NEB ultra II RNA directional library prep with poly-A capture was performed, with subsequent sequencing on an Illumina NextSeq 500 platform (75 bp cycles; high output; 400 M read pairs). Transcript counts were generated using Kallisto [37]; then DESeq2 was used to perform differential gene expression analysis between control and glyphosate groups. Differential expression was defined by transcripts with an adjusted P-value ≤ .01 (Benjamini-Hochberg method), which provided 38 differentially expressed genes (DEGs). To perform the Gene Ontology (GO) analysis, the 38 DEGs were entered into ShinyGO v0.77 [38] (South Dakota State University) and DAVID 2021 [39].

α-acetylated tubulin analyses

To investigate axons, immunohistochemistry was performed on 5 dpf whole-mount larval zebrafish. Samples were sacrificed by cold immersion and were fixed O/N in Dent's fixative at 4 °C (80% methanol/20% DMSO). Fish were then dehydrated in 100% methanol for at least an hour to O/N. Embryos were rehydrated in successive dilutions of methonal:0.1% PBS-T and then bleached in a solution of 3% H2O2/1% KOH in PBS-T until optical clarity. Zebrafish larvae were then washed 3 × 5 minutes with PBS-T and blocked in 1xPBS/0.5% Tween-20/1% DMSO/1% BSA/10% NGS for 1 hour at RT. Larvae were incubated in primary antibody, α-acetylated tubulin (T7451; Sigma; RRID: AB_609894) at a concentration of 1:250 in block O/N at 4 °C. The next day, larvae were washed 3 × 5 minutes in PBS-T and submerged again in block for 1 hour at RT. After blocking, larvae were incubated in secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor goat α-mouse 647 IgG2b and Hoechst 33 342 (1:1000) for 2 hours at RT or O/N at 4 °C. Whole-mount larvae were mounted and imaged as per the methodology described previously. To quantify axon properties (length, thickness, and immunoreactivity), Z-stack maximal projections were imported into Fiji [34] and analyzed with Analyze Particles, Local Thickness, and Skeletonize plugins.

Mitogen-activated protein kinase mapping of brain activity in 5 dpf brains

We utilized the method described by Randlett et al 2015 [39] for mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAP) mapping. Briefly, embryos were sacrificed and processed as per pHH3 assays previously, then incubated in primary antibodies p44/42 MAP 1/2 [extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERK); Cell Signaling #4696S; RRID: AB_390780] and phospho-ERK (pERK; Cell Signaling #4370S; RRID: AB_2315112) 1:200 in block O/N at 4 °C. The next day, after 3 × 15-minute washes, larvae were incubated in secondary antibodies 1:200 for 2 hours at RT or O/N at 4 °C. After 3 × 15-minute washes, zebrafish were mounted in 1% low melt temperature agarose and Z-stacks were acquired by confocal microscopy as previously described. Z-stacks were preprocessed in Fiji [34], registered to a reference image with the Computational Morphometry Toolkit [40, 41], with final analyses done in Matlab (Mathworks, USA). Each voxel was compared between groups using a Mann-Whitney test with a .005% false discovery rate threshold. MAP maps were created in Matlab using scripts provided by Randlett et al [42], highlighting significantly different voxels with green representing positive Z-scores and purple representing negative Z-scores.

Calcium imaging and analysis

To further test neural cell activity levels, we used a Tg(elavl3:Hsa.H2B-GCaMP6s)jf5Tg (herein referred to as gCAMP fish) transgenic line. We bred gCAMP+/− fish with wild-type fish and exposed them to vehicle, glyphosate, or Roundup as per the paradigm described earlier. Zebrafish were treated with 0.003% 1-phenyl 2-thiourea at 24 hpf for optical clarity, and at 2 dpf clutches were sorted, separating out fluorescing gCAMP+/− fish for subsequent activity analyses. At 5 dpf, time-series confocal images were obtained of zebrafish telencephalon (dorsal view) at a single z-plane with a Zeiss LSM880 Observer microscope. Time-series images were processed in Fiji [34] using Stardist [41] and Morpholib J [43] plugins to detect and label individual neurons. Mean gray values of neurons normalized to a basal mean grey value, and an R script was generated to calculate fluorescence variation measurements (ΔF/F0) using a threshold of ΔF/F0 > 2 to represent a calcium event, from which frequency and amplitude values were obtained for each neuron and averaged across each fish.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (v10.2.0). All data were tested for normality and lognormality, and outliers were removed with the ROUT method (1%). All normal (unless otherwise specified) data were tested by 1-way or 2-way ANOVA with post hoc Bonferroni test, whereas nonnormal data were tested by a Kruskal-Wallis test with post hoc Dunn's multiple comparisons test. All P-values are reported on their respective figures, unless otherwise noted.

Results

Embryonic exposure to roundup but not glyphosate causes gross defects

To determine the gross toxicological effects of glyphosate or Roundup exposure during early embryogenesis, we exposed zebrafish to vehicle, glyphosate, or Roundup from 10 to 48 hpf (Fig. 1A) at an environmentally relevant dose (10 µg/L a.e.), except for the LC50 experiments, where we used concentrations ranging from 1 ng/L to 1 g/L. In 48 hpf zebrafish, the LC50 of glyphosate was 544.9 mg/L, whereas the LC50 of Roundup was magnitudes lower at 30.5 mg/L (F(1, 41) = 46.42; P < .0001; Fig. 1B). These findings confirmed that the environmental concentration of 10 µg/L a.e. used throughout the remainder of the study was sublethal. Further, exposure to 10 µg/L a.e. of glyphosate had no effect on the rate of chorion hatching at 48 hpf, whereas Roundup exposure caused a significant increase compared to vehicle (P = .0363; Fig. 1C). Furthermore, embryonic exposure to 10 µg/L a.e. Roundup, but not glyphosate, caused a decrease in body length at 5 dpf (P = .0319; Fig. 1D and 1E). There were no significant changes in head diameter or interoptic distance for either exposure compared to control (Fig. 1F and 1G). Since others show glyphosate and GBH exposure can impact mitochondrial function [44], we performed a Seahorse Mito Stress Test on 48 hpf zebrafish exposed to glyphosate or Roundup (10 µg/L a.e.). We found that the maximal respiratory capacity was increased after Roundup exposure (P = .0356; Fig. 1H and 1I), but there were no changes in any other bioenergetic measures [44].

Embryonic roundup and glyphosate exposure causes behavioral changes

Next, we assessed behaviors later in life as a proxy for potential neurodevelopmental defects due to embryonic exposure to glyphosate or Roundup, employing the ZebraBox that tracks larval zebrafish movements in real-time (Fig. 2A). All larval behaviors were observed at 5 dpf. First, we measured locomotive behaviors in light and dark conditions and found that Roundup, but not glyphosate, caused a significant decrease in their active speed in the light conditions (P = .0498; Fig. 2B). Neither treatment altered the total distance traveled in the light (Fig. 2C), active speed in the dark, or distance traveled in the dark [44]. Second, we assayed anxiety-like behaviors using a thigmotaxis assay in the light and found that zebrafish exposed to glyphosate or Roundup traveled further distances (pGLY = 0.0161; pRU < 0.0001; Fig. 2D) and spent more time in the outer portion of the well (pGLY = 0.0129; pRU < 0.0001; Fig. 2E). This behavior continued at 7 dpf and 10 dpf [44]. We also used a light-startle assay, which measures the startle reflex after a sudden exposure to light, and observed a change in active speed across repeated light flashes in zebrafish exposed to Roundup embryonically (F(2, 217) = 6.776; Fig. 2F), perhaps indicating an anxiety-like response to the onset of light. There were no changes in distance traveled as a response to the light startle [44]. We also observed increased freezing behavior under basal light conditions (no startle or stimulus) with both treatments (pGLY = 0.0009; pRU = 0.0264; Fig. 2G). We retested for an anxiety-like response using a black-white preference test and found that neither Roundup- nor glyphosate-exposed zebrafish showed any changes in preference for the black or white side of the tank (Fig. 2H). Finally, we investigated behaviors in response to conspecifics. Although formal social behaviors do not begin to develop in zebrafish until later in life [45], we tested if embryonic glyphosate or Roundup changed behavioral responses to conspecifics at 5 dpf. We used a 3-chamber arrangement, with 1 of the periphery chambers kept empty, the opposing chamber housed with a sibling fish (unexposed), and the subject fish in the center chamber. We did not see changes in the regionality of swimming, and mean nonconspecific to conspecific region ratios were ∼0.5, suggesting no preference for nonconspecific vs conspecific regions in any treatment group, as expected (Fig. 2I). To further confirm these results, we formed 6-fish shoaling groups and observed no changes in interindividual or nearest neighbor average distance between fish [44], suggesting neither glyphosate nor Roundup act to alter behavior responses to conspecifics in larvae.

Embryonic glyphosate exposure causes changes in axon-related go terms in zebrafish heads

To identify potential neurodevelopmental programs impacted by embryonic glyphosate exposure, we performed RNA sequencing on 48 hpf dissected zebrafish heads. We found 38 DEGs between control and glyphosate (Fig. 3A). A principal component analysis (PCA) showed that while the control samples (C1-C5) clustered, the glyphosate head samples (G1-G5) were scattered, consistent with previous reports (Fig. 3B) [45]. A volcano plot of the DEGs show kcnh7, poc5, and gpd2 were especially upregulated in glyphosate-exposed zebrafish heads, whereas plxnb1b, zdhcc1, robo1, nup54, and ak4 were downregulated (Fig 3C). Finally, we conducted GO analyses using ShinyGO software [38] and observed terms related to neurogenesis and axon development (Fig. 3D), suggesting embryonic glyphosate exposure impacts these early neurodevelopmental programs.

Roundup impacts neurogenesis in the larval telencephalon

Given that neurogenesis is a potential program impacted by glyphosate exposure, we used EdU birthdating to test changes in neuronal birth. We gave 30-minute EdU pulses at 9, 24, 48, 72, 96, or 120 hpf and collected larvae at 5 dpf (Fig. 4A) and assayed the timing of neurogenesis in the telencephalon (Fig. 4B), a region implicated in sensory processing and anxiety-like behaviors [46-48] (as per Fig. 2) and thought to include the functional homologs of the mammalian neocortex, amygdala, and hippocampus [49] commonly implicated in human neurodevelopmental disorders [50]. We observed a higher proportion of newborn neurons to all neurons during Roundup exposure at 24 hpf (P = .0005; Fig. 4C) and at 72 hpf (P = .0004), with a lower proportion born at 48 hpf (P = .0198). Interestingly, glyphosate also appeared to affect neuronal birth rates, but only at 72 hpf (P = .0254). To test if increased neurogenesis at 24 hpf and 72 hpf led to more overall neurons in Roundup-exposed zebrafish, we quantified α-HuC/D+ cells, a pan-neuronal marker, at 5 dpf (Fig. 4D). We observed no significant changes in 5 dpf telencephalon neuron counts (Fig. 4E) but did see a significant increase in neurons in the optic tecta (pGLY = 0.0413; pRU = 0.0310; Fig. 4F). Increases in neurogenesis may indicate changes in the cell cycle dynamics, so we performed immunostaining with pHH3, a marker of late G2/M phase; no significant changes in pHH3+ cells were observed [44].

Glyphosate and roundup decreased α-acetylated tubulin-positive axons

We also examined changes in neuronal tracts since axon-related GO terms were highly enriched in our RNA sequencing results. We focused on the forebrain and optic tecta (Fig. 5A), where changes in neurogenesis were found. First, in the telencephalon, we observed a decreased immunoreactivity (integrated optical density) of α-acetylated tubulin (pGLY = 0.0288; pRU = 0.0019; Fig. 5B) but no change to its total area following embryonic glyphosate or Roundup exposure (Fig. 5C). Given that glyphosate and Roundup decreased axon marker immunoreactivity and morphology, we next tested whether these changes led to deficits at the circuit level by measuring brain activity. We used pERK as a marker of neuronal activity, as per the MAP mapping method [42] (Fig. 6A). We found that pERK levels were significantly increased across the forebrain of 5 dpf glyphosate- and Roundup-exposed fish compared to sibling controls (Fig. 6B and 6D), as well as the optic tecta and hindbrain. To further quantify the MAP map images, we integrated the optical density in individual 5 dpf postregistered Z-stack projections capturing the forebrain and whole brain for both pERK and tERK. Embryonic roundup exposure causes lasting effects on juvenile zebrafish behaviors

Next we investigated the longer-term impacts of embryonic exposure to glyphosate or Roundup by measuring juvenile behaviors. We found that embryonic Roundup exposure increased the active speed in 21 dpf zebrafish (P = .0210; Fig. 7A and 7B) and 45 dpf zebrafish when normalized to body length (P = .0368; Fig. 7C). We also found a decrease in the inside:outside ratio when measuring thigmotaxis behaviors in 21 dpf zebrafish exposed to both glyphosate and Roundup (pGLY = 0.0330; pRU = 0.0484; Fig. 7D), which was absent by 45 dpf (Fig. 7E). A light-startle assay in exposed 21 dpf and 45 dpf did not show any significant effects (Fig. 7F). Lastly, we examined conspecifics preference since zebrafish social behaviors are beginning to develop at these juvenile ages [46]. We observed no conspecifics preference in either treatment group (Fig. 7G and 7I), although all groups, including controls, appear to develop a social preference (mean social score >0.5; Fig. 7I) by 45 dpf as expected.

Discussion

Glyphosate, the most applied herbicide worldwide, is questioned for its environmental and human safety at current exposure levels. Our study focuses on the neurotoxicity of glyphosate and Roundup during a sensitive period of neurodevelopment, 10 to 48 hpf, during which primary neurogenesis, neuronal migration, and early synaptogenesis are occuring [47, 48]. Insults to neurodevelopment at this time may have permanent effects on brain function and behaviors throughout the lifespan. We chose 5 dpf as our primary endpoint since most brain structures have developed and larval zebrafish emerge as free-swimming animals capable of complex behaviors [49], demonstrating some level of brain maturation. We also investigated behaviors in the juvenile stages to determine whether these neurodevelopmental changes persist later into life. Overall, our aim was to determine the neural consequences of environmental levels of glyphosate or Roundup during a sensitive period of neurodevelopment.

Since glyphosate is used only as a GBH mixture, it is important to consider whether glyphosate itself is toxic and/or if the GBH coformulates contribute by acting either on their own or in combination with glyphosate. To address this question, throughout this study we tested both glyphosate and Roundup in parallel experiments, and repeatedly our results showed that Roundup is more harmful than glyphosate alone. For example, Roundup exposure across primary neurogenesis (ie, 10-48 hpf) causes a LC50 = 30.49 mg/L at 48 hpf, whereas glyphosate exposure has a LC50 = 544.5 g/L, nearly 20-fold higher. Consistently, other zebrafish studies that use slightly longer exposure paradigms (5-48 hpf glyphosate [50] or 0-48 hpf Roundup [51]) also report a lower Roundup LC50 concentration than for glyphosate (14.19 mg/mL vs 66.04 mg/L, respectively), further demonstrating a more harmful effect of the GBH mixture. Beyond lethality, hatching rate is another output indicative of developmental toxicity in zebrafish embryos, and here we observed that Roundup-exposed fish had a significantly higher hatch rate (∼90.70%) at 48 hpf than vehicle alone (∼67.07%), whereas glyphosate exposure had no effect. Our findings are inconsistent with previous results [52, 53], with 1 study showing Roundup exposure causes a hatching rate of ∼40% vs vehicle at ∼90% [54] (72 hpf), perhaps due to proprietary GBH formulations that may differentially effect chorion integrity, developmental programs, and/or lethality. We also found that embryonic Roundup exposure was more impactful on gross morphology than glyphosate, with an environmentally relevant dose of Roundup (10 µg/L a.e.), but not glyphosate, causing a decrease in body length at 5 dpf. Comparably, Bridi et al also observed decreases in body length in 7 dpf zebrafish exposed to Roundup (10 µg/L a.e.) from 3 dpf to 6 dpf, but they also reported that glyphosate alone causes a decrease [49], which we do not observe. This discrepancy might be because our exposure was earlier in embryonic development than theirs. Further, it is possible that the morphological phenotypes observed in this study are partly attributable to earlier hatching in Roundup-exposed fish, as accelerated hatching may result in less developmental time prior to measurement and consequently shorter body length. Combined, these findings contribute to a growing body of evidence that GBHs might be more harmful that glyphosate itself.

Mechanistically, the molecular target of glyphosate in animal cells is unclear, with mitochondrial bioenergetics as a proposed pathway [55, 56]. We used a Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Agilent) on 48 hpf zebrafish following glyphosate or Roundup exposure and found no significant changes in mitochondrial function except for in maximum respiratory capacity after Roundup treatment. It is unexpected that only 1 mitochondrial parameter would be altered if GBHs were acting through a bioenergetics mechanism, suggesting that additional studies are needed to reveal the molecular target of glyphosate and/or GBHs in embryonic zebrafish.

Although glyphosate and GBH exposure have been shown to cause behavioral changes in zebrafish and other species [57-59], our study uses a lower, environmentally relevant concentration and a shorter exposure window than previously described. Our most striking phenotype is the impact of embryonic glyphosate or Roundup exposure on anxiety-like tasks at 5 dpf, including thigmotaxis, response to flashing light, and freezing behavior. Both embryonic glyphosate and Roundup exposure cause a decrease in inside-to-outside speed and distance ratios, suggestive of an increased anxiety-like response. These findings are consistent with a previous study in adult male mice, whereby intranasal GBH exposure (96 mg/mL) increased thigmotaxis [60]. Relatedly, parental glyphosate exposure (1 ppm to parents) increased thigmotaxis behaviors in the offspring [61], suggesting a potential generational effect of this chemical on anxiety. We also used the visual startle response assay [62, 63] to test if glyphosate or Roundup exposure disrupts hyperactive behaviors following a sudden onset of light and observe hypoactivity and a notably “flat” light-startle response in Roundup-exposed zebrafish when compared to vehicle- or glyphosate-exposed zebrafish, consistent with an anxiety-like phenotype in animals exposed to GBHs. Further, both glyphosate- and Roundup-exposed larvae show a disruption in freezing behaviors, a phenotype associated with stressed larvae [64]. Finally, we did not observe any changes in black-white preference in larvae. When considered with changes in the other assays mentioned earlier, this suggests that the observed behavioral effects are selective rather than global, affecting specific components of anxiety-like behavior rather than all anxiety-related measures. Notably, black-white and conspecific preference assays rely more strongly on visual processing, which may engage distinct neural and sensory mechanisms compared with other anxiety assays. Combined, these data demonstrate a functional impact of embryonic exposure to GBHs on zebrafish behaviors later in life, consistent with a developmental disruption in neural circuit formation from these chemicals.

The underlying neurodevelopmental program that might be targeted by glyphosate or GBH coformulants is unclear. Reis et al use birthdating to show a decrease in the number of EdU+ neuroepithelial cells following treatment with Roundup (2.5 mg/L) but not glyphosate [30], suggestive of a dampening of cell birth following exposure to GBHs. Here, we show that embryonic Roundup exposure disrupts primary neurogenesis by increasing the number of neurons born at 24 hpf and decreasing the number of neurons born at 48 hpf. After primary neurogenesis concludes, we observe increased neuronal birth again, 24 hours later, at 72 hpf. This temporal switch in response to GBH chemicals between mid- and late neurogenesis is intriguing, and initially we speculated a disruption in neural progenitor cell cycle times; however, we did not observe any changes in cell cycle markers. Ojiro et al report that glyphosate and GBH exposure in female dams from early gestation changes neurogenesis transcripts in their offspring [31], consistent with a sensitivity of neurogenic programs to these chemicals although not revealing of potential target(s). We conducted bulk RNA sequencing on glyphosate-exposed 48 hpf heads to identify candidate pathways and uncovered a remarkably small subset of DEGs, only 39 in total, including robo1 and plxnb1b that are involved in neurogenesis [65, 66]. Our PCA of DEGs also shows considerable heterogeneity across glyphosate-exposed samples but not controls, suggesting variability between exposure groups and not a technical issue. These findings are consistent with Babich et al, who found only 105 differentially expresssed transcripts and variability in PCA distribution in 72 hpf whole embryos exposed to 10 µg/L glyphosate [67], and Terrazas-Salgado et al, who used the same dose exposed to 96 hpf and uncovered only 10 DEGs [45]. Taken together, and perhaps consistent with glyphosate having a limited toxicity profile in the developing embryo, exposure to glyphosate technical appears to impact a limited number of genes. Future studies should examine the transcriptomic changes in zebrafish heads following embryonic exposure to Roundup.

Despite the low number of genes impacted by embryonic glyphosate exposure, their functions clustered tightly around axon-related GO terms. While this enrichment of axonogenesis-related GO terms may be partly due to sample collection timing, which coincided with axonogenesis (∼16 hpf) and is established ∼28 hpf [60, 61]; this timing does not fully explain the phenotype, as several other transcripts of additional neurodevelopmental programs appear unaffected. Experimentally, we show significant decreases in acetylated α-tubulin immunoreactivity in the telencephalon and optic tecta in Roundup-exposed embryos, suggesting that either (1) less axonogenesis is occurring or fewer axons are present in 5 dpf zebrafish exposed to Roundup from 10 to 48 hpf or (2) the stability of existing axons is altered such that less acetylated α-tubulin marker is present in the system. In support of the first option, we observe a decrease in total axon length in the intertectal commissure following embryonic Roundup exposure, although consistent with the second possibility, Díaz-Martín et al show that 50 µg/mL of glyphosate (exposed 1.5-96 hpf) reduces the ratio of acetylated α-tubulin protein to total α-tubulin protein in zebrafish [62], suggesting microtubule instability or poor axonal maturation. In vitro, glyphosate exposure causes shorter, unbranched axons [23], suggesting less axonogenesis; however, this result could be due to microtubule disassembly as was shown previously in Xenopus [63]. In future work, the ratios of acetylated α-tubulin to total α-tubulin can be compared to understand if our exposure paradigm is shifting microtubule stability. In the meantime, our findings and those of others suggest that axonogenesis is sensitive to embryonic Roundup exposure.

Finally, we explored whether embryonic glyphosate and/or Roundup have lasting effects on neuronal activity at 5 dpf. We employed the MAP mapping method [42] that quantifies spatial changes in pERK relative to ERK levels across whole zebrafish brains as a readout of neural activity since ERK phosphorylation increases in depolarized neurons [64, 68]. We observe increases in brain activity in both glyphosate- and Roundup-exposed zebrafish compared with controls, with the Roundup group showing a stronger phenotype. This result was consistent with our live-cell imaging measuring calcium fluctuations in living zebrafish telencephalon, where we observe an increase in calcium signaling events after embryonic Roundup exposure. These data demonstrate changes in neuronal activity at 5 dpf following embryonic GBH exposure and are consistent with Cattani et al, who show activation of the upstream pathways phosphorylating ERK in Roundup treated rat hippocampal slices as well as an induction of calcium influx [69]. Given the observed defects in axonogenesis following embryonic GBH exposure, we predicted a concomitant decrease in neuronal activity, but instead, we observed an increase. One explanation might be that neurodevelopment continues past the 10 hpf to 48 hpf window that zebrafish embryos are exposed to glyphosate or Roundup. It is possible that circuits become reorganized postexposure, leading to strengthened activity in key regions by 5 dpf. Another possible reason could be local changes in synaptic activity. Indeed, acute glyphosate and Roundup treatment decreases gamma-aminobutyric acid-A receptors in C. elegans, affecting inhibitory circuits [70] and potentially leading to maintained excitatory signaling. Likewise, acute Roundup exposure increases the release of glutamate into the synapse, perhaps contributing to long-term excitotoxicity [69]. These findings start to reveal candidate programs that might underlie the mechanistic impact of GBH exposure on neurodevelopment.

A limitation of this study is the inability to determine whether GBHs permit glyphosate toxicity or whether GBH components themselves are more toxic. Additionally, some newer Roundup formulations contain active ingredients beyond glyphosate; therefore, the findings of this study are limited to the specific formulation tested and may not be generalizable to other products. Although the exact GBH mixture used in this study is proprietary, many GBHs include surfactants that facilitate cellular penetration of glyphosate, the primary herbicidal active ingredient. Consequently, it remains difficult to disentangle whether (1) the surfactants in Roundup permeabilize the chorion as well as embryonic cells, permitting a higher glyphosate exposure for developing zebrafish and thus, greater toxicity or (2) Roundup coformulants themselves are neurotoxic independent of glyphosate. Without knowledge of the composition of this Roundup formulation, it will be hard to empirically test the impact. Further, human embryos are protected in utero by the placenta, making it unclear how much glyphosate and/or Roundup coformulants are transferred to the fetus and complicating the translation of the neurodevelopmental defects. Another pitfall to this work is that our transcriptomics analysis was conducted using only glyphosate exposure and not Roundup. Our focus with this experiment was to identify glyphosate-specific effects, and in hindsight, it would have been more useful to investigate the effects of Roundup exposure in tandem, as these results may be more applicable to environmental exposure.

Conclusions

Glyphosate is an herbicidal chemical that is ubiquitous across domestic and natural environments, exposing humans and many animals in daily life. Here, we show that an environmentally relevant concentration of glyphosate technical or the GBH Roundup affects neurodevelopment in a zebrafish model, causing changes in neurogenesis, axonogenesis, neuronal activity, and ultimately behaviors. These data not only implicate glyphosate and/or Roundup as neurotoxic but suggest that current “safe” environmental levels of glyphosate need closer examination.
