Blunt abdominal trauma in a 6-year-old boy and need for imaging
Authors: Salim Al Masroori, Sarah Redhwan, Joe Nemeth, Suzanne Beno
Published: 2025

A 6-year-old boy presents to the emergency department after a motor vehicle collision at 90 km/h. The child was properly restrained, had no loss of consciousness, and experienced 1 episode of emesis at the scene. His vital signs are normal for age and his physical examination is unremarkable, with normal mental status and no signs of trauma.

How common is intra-abdominal injury in children?

Intra-abdominal injury occurs in 5% to 15% of pediatric blunt thoracoabdominal traumas.1–3 Solid organ injuries (liver, spleen, and kidney) comprise most intra-abdominal injuries in pediatric blunt abdominal trauma, primarily due to incomplete skeletal shielding, weaker musculature, and reduced adipose tissue compared with adults.1 Although potential for substantial hemorrhage exists, most solid organ injuries are successfully managed nonoperatively in pediatric trauma centres.1 Presentations of intra-abdominal injury in children can be subtle and overlooked. Classic injury patterns that raise concern for clinically important intra-abdominal injury requiring intervention include the presence of a seat-belt sign or handlebar injury. Handlebar injuries, indicated by history or bruising, are caused by focused energy impact and are associated with solid organ, pancreatic, and hollow viscus injuries, often requiring surgical intervention despite an initial clinically benign examination.4 A seat-belt sign — linear bruising across the abdomen — after a motor vehicle collision is associated with a 1.6-times increased relative risk of intra-abdominal injury and warrants laboratory investigations (complete blood count, lipase level, and liver profile), consideration for abdominal computed tomography (CT) imaging, and surgical consultation.5 Even in the absence of abdominal pain or tenderness, 2% of children with a seat-belt sign have clinically important intra-abdominal injuries.5

What is the role of focused assessment with sonography for trauma (FAST) in pediatric blunt trauma?

Focused assessment with sonography for trauma is used to identify free fluid in the abdomen or precordium, with extended FAST used to assess for pneumothorax and hemothorax. The utility of FAST in evaluating pediatric blunt trauma for intra-abdominal injury is limited, with lower sensitivity than in adults. A meta-analysis of 8 prospective studies (n = 2135) revealed a pooled sensitivity of 35% and specificity of 96% for detection of pediatric intra-abdominal injury using FAST.6 A key limitation is that more than one-third of intra-abdominal injuries in children do not manifest free peritoneal fluid; thus, a negative FAST does not rule out intra-abdominal injury.6 The role of FAST in children is evolving, with higher sensitivity for thoracic evaluation using extended FAST and serial examinations in combination with clinical parameters. Contrast-enhanced ultrasonography can be used to augment solid organ vascular imaging in real time, which is a promising approach for assessment of pediatric intra-abdominal injury.7

Which children require abdominal computed tomography after blunt trauma?

Computed tomography is the reference standard for diagnosing hemodynamically stable intra-abdominal injury. Pediatric abdominal trauma poses a diagnostic challenge, balancing the need to rule out clinically important intra-abdominal injury against the risks of radiation exposure, given children’s increased sensitivity to radiation-induced malignancies.8 Fortunately, several clinical decision instruments exist to assist clinicians. The Pediatric Emergency Care Applied Research Network (PECARN) instrument uses bedside variables to identify children at very low risk of intra-abdominal injury requiring intervention, thus not requiring an abdominal CT scan, with an excellent negative predictive value of 99.9%.1 The Pediatric Surgery Research Collaborative (PedSRC) instrument further includes chest radiography and laboratory values, increasing sensitivity for assesment of all intra-abdominal injuries and not just those requiring acute intervention.2 Both instruments are safe, are effective, and have been externally validated (Table 1).9–11 In the Canadian pediatric trauma context, a quality improvement initiative incorporating these clinical decision instruments has resulted in sustained reduction in rates of abdominal CT imaging for children at low risk for intra-abdominal injury without missing serious injuries.3 If clinicians are uncertain about the need for imaging, they should consult a pediatric emergency or trauma provider for further guidance.

Case revisited

The patient reported no abdominal pain and had no signs of abdominal wall injury (e.g., seat-belt sign) or abdominal tenderness on examination. Chest radiography and FAST exams were both negative. Laboratory results revealed normal hematocrit lipase, and aspartate aminotransferase levels, and a normal urinalysis. Using the aforementioned clinical decision instruments, the clinician determined the patient was at very low risk for intra-abdominal injury, despite the dangerous mechanism of injury, and abdominal CT imaging was not indicated. On re-evaluation, the patient was playful, tolerated oral intake, and was able to be discharged home.
