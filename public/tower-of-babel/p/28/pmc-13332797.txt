Nanomedicine for Depression: From Blood–Brain Barrier Delivery to Neuroimmune–Barrier–Plasticity Network Reprogramming
Authors: Xiong Wang, Liping Shen, Qiang Mo, Xiaofei Wang, Bing Wang, Junhao Chen, Yuanyin Teng, Zhongsong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDepression is a heterogeneous and recurrent brain disorder in which neuroinflammation, blood–brain barrier dysfunction, oxidative and mitochondrial stress, and impaired neuroplasticity interact within the neurovascular–glial–neuronal unit. This mechanism-oriented integrative review examines how engineered nanosystems may move beyond brain entry toward lesion-directed modulation of the neuroinflammation–barrier–neuroplasticity axis. We first synthesize the pathological nodes that sustain depression-related network dysfunction and then classify current nanotherapeutic strategies into three categories: small-molecule nanodelivery systems, nucleic acid nanocarriers, and functional nanoplatforms, including lipid and polymeric nanoparticles, inorganic and nanozyme-based systems, biomimetic membrane-coated nanoparticles, and engineered extracellular vesicles, including exosomes. Unlike previous nanosynthesis-focused or catalogue-style nanocarrier reviews, this review organizes the field around a disease-mechanism framework rather than material type alone, emphasizing barrier-state navigation, glial–neuronal–subcellular targeting, stimulus-responsive release, and coordinated modulation of inflammation, vascular integrity, redox homeostasis, and synaptic plasticity. We further argue that nanoplatforms should be evaluated not only by brain accumulation but also by patient stratification, engagement of defined pathological nodes, multimodal biomarker evidence of network-level modulation, manufacturability, and safety under repeated administration. Major translational bottlenecks include insufficient subtype-specific patient selection, limited human relevance of current stress- and inflammation-based models, uncertain biodistribution and long-term neurotoxicity, constraints in scaling up nose-to-brain delivery, batch-to-batch variability, cargo instability, immunogenicity, and unclear regulatory classification of complex biologic or combination products. Finally, we propose a pathological-network-guided precision nanomedicine framework that integrates blood–brain barrier status assessment, liquid biopsy and imaging biomarkers, human-relevant validation models, and scalable quality control to guide future platform design and clinical translation. This review provides a disease-mechanism-centered roadmap for transforming nanomedicine for depression from delivery optimization into precision network-oriented intervention.

Introduction

Depression is one of the most common and disabling mental disorders worldwide and is characterized by heterogeneous clinical manifestations and a prolonged, recurrent course.1,2 It is associated with an increased risk of suicide, impaired social functioning, and multisystem comorbidities.3 Although the monoamine hypothesis has substantially shaped the standardized management of depression over the past decades, current antidepressant therapies remain limited by delayed onset of action, incomplete efficacy, treatment resistance, and adverse effects that compromise adherence.4,5 These limitations indicate that depression cannot be explained simply as a chemical imbalance caused by deficiency of a single neurotransmitter. Instead, depression is increasingly viewed as a complex brain network disorder involving multiscale interactions among molecular, cellular, and regional processes.6 These processes include sustained neuroinflammatory amplification, impaired blood–brain barrier homeostasis, oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, abnormal glia–neuron interactions, and reduced neuroplasticity, which are interconnected and mutually reinforcing.7–9 However, the recognition of these complex mechanisms has not yet been matched by equally successful clinical translation. Most nanomedicine strategies for depression remain at the stage of material design, cellular validation, or animal-model proof of concept, and no nanoformulated antidepressant has yet become part of routine clinical practice for depression. Major translational gaps include insufficient patient stratification, limited human relevance of existing stress- or inflammation-based models, incomplete characterization of brain biodistribution and long-term neurotoxicity, uncertainty regarding repeated intranasal administration, formulation scalability, batch-to-batch reproducibility, cargo stability, immunogenicity, and regulatory classification of complex combination products. Therefore, an early discussion of these translational limitations is necessary to frame why a purely delivery-centered or materials-centered summary is insufficient for the field.

In this context, the role of nanomedicine in depression is being re-evaluated.10–12 Initially, nanodrug delivery was introduced into psychiatric research primarily to improve drug solubility, stability, and pharmacokinetic behavior, particularly by attempting to overcome the blood–brain barrier, a longstanding obstacle to central nervous system drug delivery.10,11 However, as the field has advanced, a more critical issue has become apparent: for depression, simply “entering the brain” is not the ultimate goal.12,13 What truly determines the disease-modifying potential of nanointerventions is not whether drugs cross the blood–brain barrier, but whether nanoplatforms can reach the appropriate brain regions at the right spatiotemporal window, target the relevant cell populations and subcellular lesions, and ultimately intervene in the key pathological amplifiers that drive disease persistence and refractoriness.13,14 Accordingly, nanomedicine for depression should be evaluated not only by improved brain exposure, but also by its ability to engage defined pathological nodes, generate measurable biological effects, and satisfy translational requirements related to safety, manufacturability, and clinical feasibility. In other words, the value of nanomedicine in depression is shifting from merely increasing brain exposure to reshaping pathological networks through engineered delivery strategies.12

In recent years, basic and translational studies have further supported this concept.15,16 Accumulating evidence indicates that depression has prominent neuroimmune features, including chronic stress, elevated peripheral inflammatory mediators, abnormal glial activation, enhanced inflammasome signaling in the brain, synaptic damage, and impaired neurogenesis.16,17 Together, these processes form an inflammation–plasticity imbalance that contributes to depression-related pathology. The blood–brain barrier (BBB) is no longer viewed as a passive anatomical barrier but as a dynamic pathological interface linking peripheral inflammation, neurovascular-unit homeostasis, and impaired central nervous system plasticity.18,19 Furthermore, oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, lysosomal/autophagic dysfunction, and excessive activation of the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome shift attention to organelles and signaling complexes, highlighting the multiscale nature of depression from molecular and cellular alterations to brain-network dysfunction.6–8,20 In this review, we define the neuroimmune–barrier–plasticity network as a coupled pathological system in which peripheral and central immune activation, BBB and neurovascular-unit dysfunction, redox and mitochondrial injury, glial–neuronal dysregulation, and impaired synaptic remodeling mutually reinforce one another. This framework does not describe a single linear pathway; instead, it provides an organizing structure for understanding disease persistence, poor therapeutic response in some patients, and potential sites at which nanomedicine may exert mechanism-based effects. Therefore, if nanomedicine is viewed only as a means of delivering drugs into the brain, its mechanistic value and therapeutic scope will remain limited. Only by integrating drug delivery with the pathological continuum of neuroinflammation, barrier dysfunction, and impaired neuroplasticity can the engineering advantages of nanosystems be translated into mechanistic and, ultimately, clinical benefits.21,22 Accordingly, current advances suggest that nanomedicine for depression is evolving from a central drug-delivery tool toward a strategy for modulating pathological networks, thereby opening new avenues for antidepressant intervention.

Although the number of reviews on nanomedicine for depression has increased in recent years, most discuss inflammation, blood–brain barrier injury, oxidative stress, or neuroplasticity in isolation and lack an integrated framework that connects these processes. Consequently, there remains insufficient discussion of why nanomedicine may exert disease-modifying effects in depression and how true precision translation can be achieved. To improve methodological transparency, this article was designed as a mechanism-oriented integrative review rather than a formal systematic review or meta-analysis. We focused on peer-reviewed studies related to depression pathophysiology, blood–brain barrier and nose-to-brain delivery, neuroinflammation, oxidative and mitochondrial stress, neuroplasticity, small-molecule nanodelivery, nucleic acid nanocarriers, biomimetic membranes, nanozymes, and engineered extracellular vesicles. Evidence was interpreted with priority given to studies that linked nanoplatform design with defined pathological mechanisms, behavioral or biological outcomes, and translational considerations such as safety, reproducibility, and clinical feasibility. In this review, we use the continuous pathological axis of “neuroinflammation-barrier-neuroplasticity” as the central framework to examine how nanomedicine has evolved from blood–brain barrier drug delivery into a tool for pathological network reprogramming. Specifically, we integrate the key pathological mechanisms of depression and examine small-molecule re-delivery, nucleic acid delivery, and functional nanoplatforms to show how nanostrategies evolve from improving brain exposure to targeting lesions and reshaping pathological networks through blood–brain barrier penetration, cellular and subcellular targeting, stimulus responsiveness, and multifunctional coordination. By placing translational bottlenecks and methodological scope at the beginning of the review, we aim to provide a clearer rationale for why depression nanomedicine should move from material-centered optimization toward pathology-guided, biomarker-supported, and clinically translatable precision intervention. Overall, this review aims to provide a conceptual and translational framework for future basic research, platform optimization, and clinical development of nanomedicine for depression.

Pathological Network of Nanomedicine Intervention in Depression

Traditional antidepressant strategies have largely been developed around the neurotransmitter imbalance hypothesis, yet increasing evidence suggests that depression is better understood as a multiscale pathological network involving neuroinflammation, barrier dysfunction, oxidative and organelle stress, and impaired neuroplasticity. Within this framework, the value of nanomedicine lies not only in improving brain delivery but also in enabling targeted intervention at specific pathological nodes across molecular, cellular, and regional levels. Therefore, before discussing individual nanoplatforms and delivery strategies, it is necessary to clarify the interconnected pathological basis of depression, because this provides the mechanistic rationale for how nanomedicine may move from drug transport toward network-oriented modulation.

Here, we use the term “network reprogramming” in a specific and cautious sense and distinguish it from simple multi-target delivery. Multi-target delivery generally refers to the simultaneous transport of one or more therapeutic cargos to multiple molecular targets or pathological pathways. By contrast, network reprogramming implies a higher-order therapeutic goal: coordinated modulation of causally connected pathological nodes, through which self-reinforcing interactions among neuroinflammation, barrier dysfunction, oxidative and mitochondrial stress, glial–neuronal dysregulation, and impaired neuroplasticity may be redirected toward a more homeostatic state. Therefore, a nanoplatform should not be considered a network-reprogramming system simply because it carries multiple drugs or alters several biomarkers. Stronger evidence would require spatially and temporally controlled delivery, engagement of defined pathological nodes, coordinated correction of multiple interconnected pathological processes, and functional recovery supported by behavioral, molecular, cellular, and preferably imaging-based or longitudinal biomarker evidence.

Neuroinflammation and Glial Cell Abnormalities

Crossing the blood–brain barrier is one of the most apparent technical challenges in nanomedicine for depression. However, the pathological relevance of this process depends not merely on whether a drug enters the brain, but on whether it effectively targets the glial-cell network altered by neuroinflammation. Therefore, neuroinflammation and glial abnormalities should be viewed not simply as entry points for nanomedical intervention, but as pathological hubs linking peripheral immune activation, barrier dysfunction, and impaired neural plasticity (Figure 1).23,24 To avoid treating neuroinflammation only as background pathology, this section focuses on the correspondence between inflammatory lesions and nanoscale intervention strategies: glial activation creates cell-specific, region-specific, and microenvironment-responsive targets that free drugs often cannot address efficiently. Severe depression is increasingly recognized as a systemic disorder with prominent neuroimmune features. Chronic stress, elevated peripheral inflammatory cytokines, altered blood–brain barrier permeability, and activation of brain immune cells collectively contribute to disease persistence and treatment resistance.23,24,26,27 In this process, microglia and astrocytes act as central pathological amplifiers. Reactive microglia can release IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and inflammasome-related mediators, thereby disrupting glutamate homeostasis, weakening brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) signaling, inhibiting neurogenesis, and impairing synaptic plasticity.28–30 Astrocytes further participate in inflammatory amplification, glutamate uptake, lactate metabolism, trophic-factor secretion, and blood–brain barrier/neurovascular-unit maintenance.26–29 Thus, the key therapeutic issue is not only how to suppress inflammation, but how to deliver therapeutic signals to pathological glial states while minimizing nonspecific systemic exposure. Nanomaterials address this problem through several complementary mechanisms. First, nose-to-brain delivery can reduce systemic exposure and increase central accessibility of anti-inflammatory or neuroprotective cargos. Second, ligand modification and biomimetic membrane coating can enhance recognition of activated glia or inflamed brain regions. Third, inflammation- or ROS-responsive systems can release cargos preferentially in pathological microenvironments. Fourth, nucleic-acid and extracellular-vesicle platforms can intervene in upstream inflammatory regulatory networks rather than only blocking downstream cytokines.31–33 For example, Luo et al developed a DNA tetrahedron-based miR-22-3p delivery system that inhibited the PTEN/PI3K-AKT/NLRP3-associated inflammatory axis and improved depression-like behavior in an LPS-induced model.34 Ju et al reported that intranasally administered synergistic amine lipid nanoparticles delivered si-circATF7IP to the hippocampus, reduced CD11b+CD45dim microglia and decreased TNF-α and IL-6 levels; the correlation between circATF7IP and HAMD-24 scores also suggests potential value for biomarker-guided stratification.32 Engineered exosome systems provide another route for coordinated regulation. RVG-BDNF-Exos crossed the blood–brain barrier, targeted hippocampal and prefrontal neurons, reduced microglial and astrocytic activation, and enhanced BDNF/TrkB/AKT signaling.35 Similarly, exosome-encapsulated ROS-responsive nanogels achieved nose-to-brain delivery and combined antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and PACAP/PAC1-associated plasticity-promoting effects.31

More targeted designs further illustrate how nanomaterials can match specific inflammatory lesions. The microglial-membrane-coated PDA-Mem@M system combined blood–brain barrier penetration, microglial accumulation, polydopamine-mediated ROS scavenging, and on-demand memantine release, thereby increasing BDNF and reversing dendritic spine loss.33 Biomineralized silk fibroin nanoparticles, CaSF@TB, promoted microglial M2 polarization, reduced inflammatory mediators, increased AQP4 expression, and enhanced hippocampal BDNF expression in an LPS model.36 Stimulus-responsive nose-to-brain nanoparticles also improved depression-like behavior through ROS scavenging.37 Taken together, these studies show that glial abnormalities provide a direct design logic for nanomedicine: the pathological problem is localized, cell-state dependent, and dynamically inflammatory; the corresponding material solution is targeted delivery, biomimetic recognition, responsive release, and multifunctional anti-inflammatory/plasticity-restoring regulation. Nevertheless, most evidence remains preclinical, and future studies should verify whether glial targeting leads to reproducible target engagement, long-term safety, and clinically meaningful improvement beyond short-term behavioral rescue.24,33,35

Chronic stress and inflammatory signals can disrupt tight junction proteins, endothelial homeostasis, astrocytic endfeet, and neurovascular-unit function, thereby increasing BBB permeability and facilitating the entry of inflammatory mediators, immune cells, and oxidative stress signals into the brain.19,38,40 Clinical and population-based studies have begun to support this view, including evidence of regional BBB permeability changes in severe depression and associations between barrier-related markers and inflammatory changes.41,42 Animal studies also show that chronic stress can reduce Claudin-5 and ZO-1 expression and increase tracer extravasation, supporting a link between stress, barrier disruption, and depressive phenotypes.43,44 For nanomedicine, the practical question is therefore twofold: how to cross or bypass the barrier, and how to avoid worsening or ignoring barrier pathology itself. Nanomaterials address barrier-related problems through two major strategies. One is active BBB transport, using ligand modification, receptor-mediated transcytosis, biomimetic coating, or stimuli-responsive release to improve brain entry and local exposure. The other is nose-to-brain delivery, which partially bypasses the blood circulation route and may reduce systemic exposure while improving access to olfactory and trigeminal pathways.45,46 Intranasal polymeric and lipid nanocarriers can improve nasal retention, mucosal transport, and brain delivery by tuning particle size, surface charge, and mucoadhesion.45 This route may be particularly useful for unstable cargos, proteins, nucleic acids, and natural products with limited oral bioavailability or poor BBB permeability.46 Representative depression-related studies illustrate this pathology–material correspondence. Exosome-encapsulated ROS-responsive nanogels achieved rapid brain enrichment through the nose-to-brain route and simultaneously reduced oxidative stress, inflammation, and PACAP/PAC1-related synaptic impairment in a perimenopausal depression model.31 Quercetin-loaded intranasal thermosensitive gel improved depression-related behavioral outcomes and reduced hippocampal c-fos expression, suggesting that nasal retention and brain-targeting optimization can amplify the effect of bioactive small molecules.47 BDNF/quercetin alginate nanogels enabled intranasal delivery of BDNF and enhanced antidepressant-like effects associated with BDNF/TrkB, PI3K/Akt, and glutamatergic signaling.48 Biomineralized silk fibroin nanoparticles showed low systemic distribution after intranasal administration while regulating AQP4, microglial polarization, inflammatory mediators, and BDNF expression.36 These examples indicate that the barrier problem should not be reduced to “whether the nanocarrier enters the brain”; rather, the desired material function is controlled access, pathological-site enrichment, and restoration of barrier–immune–plasticity coupling.49

However, barrier-targeted nanomedicine remains far from clinically resolved. Nasal mucociliary clearance, human–animal anatomical differences in the olfactory region, dose scalability, long-term mucosal safety, formulation reproducibility, and quantitative biodistribution remain major translational barriers.45,46 Thus, BBB and nose-to-brain strategies should be evaluated by more than brain accumulation alone; future studies should include pharmacokinetic profiling, regional biodistribution, repeated-dose toxicity, mucosal safety, and evidence that barrier delivery translates into disease-relevant target engagement.19,36,45,46

Oxidative Stress, Mitochondrial Dysfunction, and Imbalance of Redox Homeostasis

Oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction provide another major pathological interface for nanomedicine in depression. Elevated ROS, lipid peroxidation, impaired mitochondrial bioenergetics, altered antioxidant defenses, and ferroptosis-related injury have been associated with depressive pathology in both peripheral and central contexts.50–53 These changes can amplify neuroinflammation, damage endothelial and neuronal cells, impair synaptic plasticity, and contribute to persistent emotional-circuit dysfunction.50–52 Because ROS, lipid peroxidation, and mitochondrial injury are not only pathological outcomes but also microenvironmental signals, they are particularly suitable for nanomaterial-based intervention.52

Building on this view, Larrea et al proposed that mitochondrial abnormalities in depression are reflected not only in disordered ATP production, but also in broader disturbances involving the tricarboxylic acid cycle, the electron transport chain, mtDNA homeostasis, and neuronal metabolic flexibility. These alterations may further promote ROS generation, forming a self-reinforcing cycle of energy deficiency, oxidative damage, and functional decline.50 Ciobîcă-Bătcu et al described this process as a mitochondrial hub in depression, emphasizing that mitochondria are not isolated energy-producing units, but are embedded at the intersection of psychological stress, neuroendocrine signaling, immune activation, and inflammatory amplification. Accordingly, any nanotherapeutic strategy intended to achieve deep disease modification in depression is unlikely to bypass intervention at the level of redox and mitochondrial homeostasis.51 Furthermore, Liu et al noted in their review of the ferroptosis–mitochondria axis that a positive feedback loop exists among lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial damage, metabolic imbalance, and ferroptosis-related neuroinflammation. This makes the sequence of ROS accumulation, mitochondrial damage, inflammatory amplification, and further ROS production one of the most notable mechanistic chains in recent depression research, and it provides a direct theoretical basis for developing nanoplatforms with both antioxidant and anti-inflammatory functions.53 Several studies illustrate these strategies. BDNF/quercetin alginate nanogels combined antioxidant protection with neurotrophic support, protected BDNF from oxidative degradation, improved brain accessibility through intranasal delivery, and regulated BDNF/TrkB, PI3K/Akt, and glutamate-related pathways.48 Polydopamine nanoparticles acted closer to a “material-as-drug” system by using intrinsic free-radical-scavenging capacity to reduce inflammation, synaptic loss, and depression-like behavior in an LPS-induced model.54

ROS-responsive self-destructing nanocapsules enabled synergistic delivery and release of serotonin and catalase, suggesting that ROS can be used as both a pathological target and a release trigger.55 GLX351322-loaded nanoparticles inhibited hippocampal oxidative stress and ferroptosis-related injury through the NOX4/ROS axis and lipid peroxidation regulation.56 Brain-targeted quercetin lipid nanoparticles, intranasal quercetin thermosensitive gel, and nanocurcumin further demonstrate that nanodelivery can enhance the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neurotrophic effects of natural molecules.47,57,58 Therefore, the correspondence between redox pathology and nanomaterial design is relatively direct: excessive ROS can be neutralized by nanozymes or antioxidant materials; oxidative microenvironments can trigger drug release; mitochondrial and ferroptosis-related injury can be targeted through carriers that improve local exposure and protect neuronal redox balance. At the same time, these findings should not be overinterpreted as proven disease modification, because most evidence still relies on animal behavioral tests and selected oxidative markers. Future studies should include quantitative brain biodistribution, mitochondrial target engagement, ferroptosis-specific validation, long-term clearance, and repeated-dose safety assessment before redox-regulating nanomedicine can be considered clinically translatabl.55,56,58

Application of Nanomedicine Delivery System in Depression

Building on this pathological framework, the therapeutic relevance of nanomedicine in depression can be more clearly appreciated at the level of delivery design and functional intervention. Rather than acting simply as passive carriers, nanodelivery systems are increasingly engineered to match the biological complexity of depression by enabling selective cargo transport, expanding accessible therapeutic targets, and integrating intrinsic bioactivity with delivery performance. The following sections therefore highlight these emerging directions through small-molecule re-delivery systems, nucleic acid delivery approaches, and functional nanomedicine platforms such as nanoenzymes, biomimetic membranes, and engineered exosomes (Table 1). Representative Nanomedicine Delivery Systems for Depression: Cargo, Design Strategy, Mechanism, and Therapeutic Outcomes

Small Molecule Redelivery System

In nanomedicine for depression, small-molecule nanoredelivery is attractive not only because it is technically feasible, but also because it addresses an important clinical reality. Current treatment still relies heavily on small-molecule drugs, yet these agents are often limited by delayed onset, insufficient brain exposure, first-pass metabolism, peripheral adverse effects, and poor patient adherence. Therefore, the value of nanoformulation lies not simply in changing dosage forms, but in reshaping how drugs enter the brain, persist in brain tissue, and interact with the pathological microenvironment, thereby expanding the pharmacological potential of existing small molecules.47,63,67,68

A representative example is provided by Vitorino et al, who used a quality-by-design (QbD) strategy to optimize intranasal lipid nanoparticles for depression treatment. Their formulation-development process incorporated key quality attributes, including particle size, drug release, nasal mucosal permeability, stability, and cellular compatibility.67 They demonstrated that nanostructured lipid carriers had greater permeability across porcine nasal mucosa, better stability, and improved cellular compatibility compared with nanoemulsions, and that the optimized platform produced antidepressant-like and anxiolytic-like effects in mice. This work is significant because it advances small-molecule nanoredelivery for depression from proof of concept toward an engineering-oriented and scalable formulation-development framework.67 From a clinically relevant perspective, particularly the redesign of marketed antidepressants, venlafaxine-loaded poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles reported by Cayero-Otero et al provide a recent example. The system was prepared using a double-emulsion solvent-evaporation method and showed a particle size of approximately 190–210 nm, an encapsulation efficiency of 55%–65%, a polydispersity index (PDI) below 0.2, and a zeta potential of approximately −20 mV.63 These nanoparticles showed efficient endocytosis and good cytocompatibility in human olfactory neuroepithelial cells.63 More importantly, intranasally administered venlafaxine-loaded PLGA nanoparticles reversed reduced sucrose preference and abnormal tail-suspension behavior in depression models within 7 days. Their behavioral effects were superior to those of free venlafaxine, suggesting that this strategy may address the slow onset associated with first-line monoaminergic drugs. This result is important not only because it demonstrates improved nose-to-brain delivery of venlafaxine, but also because it suggests that, for classical serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI) drugs, nanoredelivery may not alter the endpoint pharmacology itself. Instead, it may shorten the time required to reach an effective pharmacological state in the brain and alter the exposure route through which that state is achieved, which represents an important source of translational value.63,68 More broadly, Upadhyay et al noted in their review of FDA-approved antidepressants delivered through the nasal route that the main goals of intranasal nanodelivery for selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), SNRIs, and other centrally acting small molecules are to reduce systemic exposure, enhance brain targeting, and accelerate therapeutic onset. This rationale is consistent with the venlafaxine study discussed above.68

In addition to classical antidepressants, the nanodelivery of naturally derived or repurposed small molecules has become an important focus in recent years. This direction better illustrates how nanosystems may amplify therapeutic effects by engaging multiple depression-related pathological processes. Quercetin provides a representative example. For instance, Elkomy et al developed a quercetin-loaded intranasal thermosensitive in situ gel. This system significantly improved depression-related behavioral phenotypes in animal models. Its value lies not only in improving the central accessibility of quercetin, but also in using a transfersome-based thermosensitive gel system to prolong nasal residence, enhance mucosal penetration, and concentrate the intrinsic antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective effects of quercetin within the central pathological microenvironment.47 Xu et al further developed brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)/quercetin alginate nanogels, representing a more integrated delivery strategy. This system used quercetin nanogels as carriers for protein delivery, rapidly entered the brain after intranasal administration, and achieved nearly 50-fold higher bioavailability than orally administered quercetin. In corticosterone-treated mice, stress-exposed mice, and chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) rats, this platform improved despair-like behavior and anhedonia, increased hippocampal BDNF levels, reduced interleukin-6 (IL-6) and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), and revealed regulatory mechanisms involving the glutamatergic system, phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) signaling, and the BDNF/tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB) pathway through integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses. These findings indicate that advanced small-molecule nanoredelivery is not simply a means of transporting small molecules into the brain, but a strategy for enabling antioxidant protection and synergistic therapeutic activity (Figure 3).48 Consistent with this concept, Chen et al reported that brain-targeted lipid nanocarriers enhanced the antidepressant efficacy of quercetin. Another common feature of this research direction is that it moves beyond merely increasing the amount of drug entering the brain and instead emphasizes reshaping the pathological context in which the drug acts. For example, Jiang et al reported PDA-Mem@M, a microglial membrane-coated polydopamine nanoparticle system loaded with memantine. This design enables BBB penetration, microglial homing, free-radical scavenging, and on-demand drug release. In this platform, memantine is not used solely as a classical N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor-modulating small molecule, but as part of an integrated strategy combining anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and synaptic-restorative actions. As a result, PDA-Mem@M produced stronger behavioral improvement and greater recovery of BDNF expression and dendritic spine integrity than free memantine. This study illustrates how nanodelivery can reposition established small-molecule drugs as mechanism-oriented interventions more closely aligned with pathological network modulation.33

Similarly, Khan et al’s mucoadhesive eugenol nanoemulsion developed, together with the andrographolide-loaded lipid nanocarriers reported by Lapmanee et al in the same year, demonstrates that for small molecules with anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective potential, nanoscale formulation not only improves solubility and absorption, but also significantly enhances their effects on stress-related behavior, hippocampal injury, and neuroinflammatory markers.49,69 In other words, the nanosystem amplifies both pharmacokinetic advantages and multitarget pharmacological effects.49,69 From the perspective of clinical research and translation, small-molecule redelivery is currently the most realistic and feasible path because it maximizes the reuse of existing safety knowledge, pharmacological foundations, and clinical experience with approved indications. In this regard, it offers a clearer translational route than novel nucleic-acid therapeutics or complex biological agents, which still face greater regulatory uncertainty.63,68,70 However, it should be acknowledged that, to date, no nanoformulated antidepressants for depression have entered routine clinical practice.68,70 This means that small-molecule nanodelivery in depression still remains largely at the stage of high-quality animal studies and formulation development.68,70 However, this does not mean that the pathway lacks translational promise. On the contrary, intranasal esketamine has demonstrated that a non-oral, rapidly central-accessible, brain-centered drug-delivery strategy is clinically feasible for treatment-resistant depression. Both recent reviews and real-world studies have supported its effectiveness and overall manageable safety in TRD, thereby providing a practical reference point for the future development of more refined nose-to-brain nanoplatforms.70–72

Therefore, small-molecule redelivery should not be regarded as a minor modification of old drugs, but rather as a pragmatic and far-reaching translational strategy. Starting from classical antidepressants, repurposed drugs, and natural small molecules, it uses nanosystems to improve brain delivery, shorten onset time, reduce peripheral burden, and convert single agents into treatment units more closely aligned with the pathological network linking neuroinflammation, barrier dysfunction, and neuroplasticity. At the current stage of development of nanomedicine for depression, this is likely to be the route closest to clinical practice.63,70–72

Nucleic Acid Delivery: Opening Up New Druggable Targets

Nucleic-acid delivery broadens the range of molecular targets that can be therapeutically addressed in depression, whereas small-molecule nanoredelivery mainly optimizes existing treatments. This distinction is important because many circular RNAs, microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and inflammation-related transcriptional regulatory networks involved in depression onset and progression are not readily accessible to conventional small molecules. Nanodelivery systems provide a feasible means of targeting these difficult-to-drug molecular nodes in the brain, specific brain regions, and distinct cell types, thereby moving nanomedicine for depression beyond pharmacokinetic optimization toward network-oriented pathological modulation.32,34,73,74

Current research suggests that nucleic-acid delivery in depression extends beyond mechanistic validation and is beginning to form an evidence chain that links target discovery, delivery implementation, pathological correction, and potential clinical stratification. A representative study by Ju et al showed that circATF7IP was significantly elevated in the plasma of patients with MDD and positively correlated with 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-24) scores. The authors then constructed synergistic amine lipid nanoparticles (SALNPs) for the intranasal delivery of si-circATF7IP. These nanoparticles reached the hippocampus and improved depression-like behavior in a lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced model, while reducing the proportion of CD11b+CD45dim microglia and decreasing tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels. The key significance of this study is that it links a measurable peripheral molecular signal with central neuroinflammatory correction and behavioral improvement, rather than merely demonstrating successful delivery. This finding suggests that nucleic-acid nanomedicine may support a dual strategy combining biomarker-guided stratification with therapeutic intervention.32 In parallel with siRNA-based strategies, miRNA delivery represents another approach with broader network-level regulatory potential. For instance, Luo et al reported a DNA tetrahedron-based system that delivered miR-22-3p using DNA tetrahedral nanostructures. In an LPS-induced model, this system improved depression-like behavior and reduced the expression of inflammation-related factors. This effect was associated with regulation of the phosphatase and tensin homolog/phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B/NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (PTEN/PI3K/Akt/NLRP3) inflammatory axis. This study suggests that DNA nanostructures can enhance the in vivo stability and cellular uptake of miRNAs and may convert short, degradable, and potentially off-target nucleic acids into intervention tools with central therapeutic potential.34

More broadly, multiple studies now regard miRNAs as one of the most promising classes of nucleic-acid targets in depression because they can simultaneously regulate pathways related to neuroinflammation, neurotransmission, and neuroplasticity.74,75 For example, Kaurani et al noted in their clinical review that abnormal peripheral miRNA expression has been repeatedly observed across multiple cohorts of patients with depression,74 whereas Choudhary et al further summarized that miRNAs exert cross-regulatory effects on neuroplasticity, neuroinflammation, and neurotransmission, making them especially suitable as network-level therapeutic targets.75 However, the major barriers to clinical translation remain insufficient delivery efficiency, poor in vivo stability, and a high risk of off-target effects, all of which are areas in which nanomedicine may provide practical solutions.74,75 In addition to directly loading miRNAs or siRNAs, exosome- or extracellular-vesicle (EV)-mediated nucleic-acid delivery is becoming an increasingly important direction in depression research because these vesicles possess good biocompatibility, potential to cross the BBB, and natural RNA-carrying properties. He et al pointed out that extracellular-vesicle miRNAs show substantial dual value as both mechanistic mediators and biomarkers in common mental disorders.76 Chamakioti et al further emphasized in their recent review that stress-related extracellular vesicles and their miRNAs can influence neurogenesis and neuroinflammation and may participate in the development of psychiatric disorders such as depression.77 Together, these findings provide pathological support for the development of engineered extracellular-vesicle systems for therapeutic nucleic-acid delivery. More importantly, clinical interest in EV-associated miRNAs is increasing. Tunset et al’s systematic review analyzed 46 studies of blood-derived EVs in psychiatric disorders and found that miRNAs were the most extensively studied molecular class. Although no stable and universally accepted clinical biomarker candidate has yet emerged, these studies have linked inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and brain-derived signals to EV cargo. This suggests that, if peripheral EV-based nucleic-acid stratification can eventually be integrated with engineered EV-based nucleic-acid therapy, nucleic-acid nanomedicine for depression may move closer to precision medicine.78

From the perspective of delivery routes, nose-to-brain delivery remains the most realistic route for administering nucleic-acid therapeutics for depression because, compared with systemic administration, the nasal cavity can partially bypass the BBB and reduce peripheral exposure and degradation of nucleic acids. Qiu et al stated clearly that the nose-to-brain route offers the possibility for large molecules such as siRNAs to reach affected brain regions directly.46 Li et al likewise emphasized in their review of brain-targeted intranasal biomolecule delivery that intranasal administration is particularly suitable for the early development of gene therapy and other biomacromolecules for central nervous system disorders. These observations strongly support original studies such as SALNP-mediated delivery of si-circATF7IP.79 However, despite these promising prospects, the clinical translation of nucleic-acid therapy still requires caution and realism. Seyhan et al pointed out that the field of miRNA therapeutics remains at an early stage, with few candidates having advanced into clinical development and none yet reaching Phase III trials.80 Brillante et al further noted that when miRNA therapy moves from preclinical studies toward clinical application, the major challenges remain delivery, safety, immunogenicity, and off-target effects.81 Therefore, although nucleic-acid nanodelivery in depression has shown encouraging mechanistic depth and therapeutic efficacy in animal models, more rigorous evaluation of pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, long-term safety, and manufacturing consistency is still required before it can be translated to patients.80,81 Therefore, the use of nucleic-acid delivery to open up new druggable targets is not merely the addition of a new formulation strategy. It also means that depression treatment may move beyond the passive limitation of repairing only the monoaminergic system and gain the ability to intervene directly in inflammatory amplifiers, epigenetic regulatory nodes, and upstream neural plasticity networks. From SALNP-mediated delivery of si-circATF7IP, to DNA tetrahedron-based delivery of miR-22-3p, to extracellular-vesicle miRNAs as dual-purpose platforms for diagnosis and therapy, current evidence shows that this pathway is not only scientifically sound, but may also mark a turning point at which nanomedicine for depression evolves from delivery improvement toward genuine mechanistic innovation.

Functional Nanomedicine Delivery System: Nanoenzymes, Biomimetic Membranes, and Engineered Exosomes

Traditional nanodrug delivery mainly addresses the problem of how drugs enter the central nervous system. Therefore, the key to advancing nanomedicine for depression through functional nanosystems lies in whether they can address a more important question after entering the brain: can they reprogram disease processes at the network level along the pathological axis linking neuroinflammation, the BBB, and neuroplasticity? Taking nanoenzymes as an example, Fu et al prepared bovine serum albumin-coated cerium oxide nanoclusters (CeO2@BSA), which functioned not merely as carriers but as ROS-scavenging nanomedicine in depression models. This system showed favorable brain distribution in mice, reduced oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, and improved depression-like behavior, suggesting that oxidative stress may serve as a direct target for catalytic intervention in depression.83 This approach has been further refined in recent studies. For example, the N-doped carbon-dot nanoenzyme (CDzyme) reported by Jia et al exhibited antioxidant enzyme-like activity, reshaped gut microbiota composition, improved hippocampal neurotransmitter levels, and alleviated behavioral abnormalities in a stress-induced depression model. These findings suggest that the therapeutic effects of nanoenzymes are not limited to ROS clearance in the brain, but may also involve broader systemic regulation through the gut-brain axis and inflammatory signaling.84 A further mechanistic advance was reported by Fang et al, who developed Fe3O4@CS nanoenzymes with tryptophan hydroxylase-like activity. After nose-to-brain delivery, these nanoenzymes catalyzed the conversion of tryptophan to 5-hydroxytryptophan in stressed neurons using endogenous ascorbic acid and H2O2, thereby restoring in situ 5-HT synthesis, increasing brain 5-HT levels, and improving depression-like behavior. This study moves nanoenzymes beyond the clearance of damaging factors toward the correction of neurotransmitter biosynthetic defects, thereby providing a new paradigm for precise metabolic intervention in depression.85 In the field of exosomes and extracellular vesicles, Hu et al co-loaded PACAP and estradiol into ROS-responsive hyaluronic-acid nanogels and coated the system with exosomes to generate HA-NGs@exosomes. In a perimenopausal depression model, this system efficiently entered cells, crossed the BBB, and achieved responsive drug release in a high-ROS microenvironment, thereby reducing inflammation and oxidative damage simultaneously. This finding suggests that extracellular vesicles are not only natural carriers, but can also be combined with stimuli-responsive materials to create an engineered interface for multimodal therapeutic amplification.31 Further work on engineered exosomes has begun to target core neurotrophic pathways involved in depression. Liu et al incorporated RVG brain-targeting peptides and BDNF into exosomes to construct RVG-BDNF exosomes. In LPS-induced depression models, this system crossed the BBB, preferentially targeted hippocampal and prefrontal neurons, increased local BDNF levels, promoted neurogenesis, and improved depression-like behavior. These results suggest that engineered exosomes may serve as next-generation antidepressant platforms with brain-region selectivity and the capacity to deliver biologically active cargo.35

Importantly, the value of extracellular vesicles in depression is not limited to their role as drug-delivery tools, because their intrinsic cargo can also participate in depression-related pathological networks. Costa-Ferro et al found that EVs derived from human umbilical-cord mesenchymal stem cells alleviated depression-like behavior in chronically stressed rats, possibly by regulating pathways associated with neuroinflammation and synaptic integrity.86 Yu et al suggested that extracellular-vesicle miR-24-3p contributes to neuronal injury and BBB dysfunction, and that inhibition of this miRNA can reduce BBB permeability and improve behavioral abnormalities.87 These findings suggest that future engineered exosomes may exert therapeutic value by removing pathogenic cargo or enriching protective cargo, rather than simply serving as drug-loading vehicles.86,87 From the perspective of clinical translation, the major bottlenecks extend beyond proof of concept to scalable manufacturing, standardized membrane and extracellular-vesicle sources, consistent cargo loading, defined in vivo pharmacokinetics, long-term immune safety, and robust quality-control systems.88,89 In other words, the promise of functional nanodelivery systems in depression lies not only in more efficient brain delivery, but also in their potential to integrate BBB crossing, inflammation control, neurotransmitter restoration, and synaptic plasticity repair into engineered, modular treatment units suitable for precision medicine. However, the available evidence remains dominated by preclinical rodent studies, and standardized clinical application in patients with depression will require further advances in manufacturing and regulatory science.82,88,89

Different Targeting Strategies in Nanomedicine

In depression, targeting strategies are central because they determine whether nanomedicine can engage the hierarchical organization of disease pathology. The therapeutic challenge is not simply to deliver agents into the brain, but to guide them across pathological barriers, toward relevant cellular effectors, and ultimately to subcellular sites where inflammatory amplification, metabolic dysfunction, and impaired plasticity converge. From this perspective, targeting is a core design principle, rather than a secondary optimization step, that defines the translational value of nanosystems for depression. BBB Penetration Strategy: Ligand Modification, Membrane Camouflage, and Light/Heat Assistance

In depression, crossing the BBB is not merely a drug-delivery step but a prerequisite for determining whether networks involving neuroinflammation, barrier homeostasis, and neural plasticity can be therapeutically modulated. In recent years, growing evidence has shown that chronic stress and inflammation associated with depression can alter tight junctions, transporters, and neurovascular-unit function. Therefore, precise BBB-penetration design represents a key strategy by which nanotherapy for depression may move beyond increasing brain exposure toward intervention in pathological networks.38,39,100

At present, ligand modification is one of the most mature and promising engineering strategies. Its principle is to use ligands targeting the transferrin receptor (TfR), nicotinic acetylcholine receptor-related pathways, or other brain-targeting molecules to trigger receptor-mediated transcytosis and improve brain-delivery efficiency without disrupting barrier integrity.38,101 In one representative depression-related study, Yu et al constructed a borneol-modified graphene oxide nanocarrier (GO-PEG-BO) for delivery of ginsenoside Rg1. In vitro BBB models and in vivo distribution studies showed that this system enhanced BBB penetration and increased brain accumulation by transiently regulating tight junctions and inhibiting efflux systems. In depressed rats, Rg1/GO-PEG-BO improved stress-induced anhedonia, despair-like behavior, and anxiety-like behavior, suggesting that combining a ligand or penetration enhancer with a nanocarrier may amplify the central efficacy of natural antidepressant molecules beyond simple pharmacokinetic optimization.102 Following a similar receptor-mediated BBB-crossing strategy, He et al reported transferrin-modified carboxymethyl chitosan nanoparticles that bound TfR at the BBB and enhanced brain-targeting efficiency under depression-related neuroinflammatory and neuronal-injury conditions.62 Previous reviews have noted that this BBB-crossing strategy relies on receptor-mediated transport and shows favorable safety and biocompatibility in both in vitro and in vivo settings. These findings support the translational potential of the TfR axis for BBB-targeted delivery in depression.99 Another ligand-based strategy involves modification with neuron-affinity peptides such as RVG29. Tan et al used RVG29-modified black phosphorus nanosheets loaded with hypericin to construct a brain-targeted nanoplatform. Under 808 nm near-infrared irradiation, this system more effectively recognized and crossed the BBB and promoted drug delivery to affected brain regions, thereby enhancing the therapeutic effect of hypericin in a depression model.61 This design integrates brain-targeted ligand recognition with light- and heat-assisted BBB facilitation within a single platform, representing a combined BBB-penetration strategy that has gained increasing attention in recent years.103 A similar RVG-based strategy has also been applied to engineered extracellular vesicles. For example, RVG-BDNF exosomes constructed by Liu et al crossed the BBB and targeted hippocampal and prefrontal neurons, increased local BDNF levels, regulated BDNF/TrkB/Akt signaling, and improved neurogenesis and depression-like behavior in LPS-induced depression models. These findings suggest that ligand modification is not limited to synthetic nanoparticles but can also improve the brain-targeting performance of natural biological carriers.35

Compared with ligand modification, membrane-camouflage strategies place greater emphasis on making nanoparticles resemble components of the pathological environment itself, thereby simultaneously providing advantages such as immune evasion, inflammatory homing, and BBB penetration.82 An important example of this strategy is the memantine-loaded polydopamine nanomedicine encapsulated within a microglial cell membrane, PDA-Mem@M, reported by Jiang et al, this system achieved BBB penetration and enrichment in the brain and hippocampus through homologous adhesion and inflammation-associated tropism conferred by the microglial membrane. In chronically stressed mice, fluorescence signals in the brain and hippocampus were higher than those of unmodified particles, and the system outperformed memantine monotherapy even at lower doses and reduced dosing frequency.33 At the same time, this system reduced hippocampal ROS and pro-inflammatory factors, increased anti-inflammatory mediators, and restored BDNF and markers of synaptic plasticity. These findings indicate that the value of membrane camouflage lies not only in facilitating entry into the brain, but also in directing carriers to regions in which inflammatory and plasticity-related imbalance is most pronounced.25,33 Similarly, Zhou et al proposed a cell-liposome delivery system based on neuroinflammation-guided targeting of the amygdala to alleviate LPS-induced depression-like behavior. This study further suggests that, in depression, where marked brain heterogeneity and uneven distribution of inflammatory hotspots are common, the significance of biomimetic cellular or cell-liposome systems lies in their ability to use endogenous inflammatory cues to achieve lesion-proximal delivery rather than uniform average brain exposure.104 As for light- and heat-assisted BBB penetration, its core value lies in providing a controllable spatiotemporal window that shifts the entry of nanomedicine into the brain from passive, opportunity-dependent transport toward externally triggered and spatially precise delivery. Li et al demonstrated, using molecularly targeted gold nanoparticles, that intravenous injection combined with transcranial picosecond laser stimulation could reversibly and incrementally increase BBB permeability, mainly through enhanced paracellular diffusion rather than irreversible vascular damage. This finding provides an important methodological basis for subsequent integration of photothermal materials into brain-delivery strategies for depression105 Meanwhile, Chang et al developed aptamer-functionalized near-infrared (NIR)-responsive artificial synaptic vesicles loaded with 5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP) and IR780 for depression treatment. The aptamer enhanced blood–brain barrier (BBB) targeting, while NIR-triggered photothermal effects promoted BBB permeability and rapid 5-HTP release, leading to improved depressive-like behaviors and increased hippocampal brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression in chronic unpredictable mild stress mice. However, from a clinical translation perspective, several practical barriers still limit the application of these strategies in patients with depression. First, ligand-modified systems may lose targeting efficiency after formation of a protein corona, and the actual in vivo transport efficiency of receptor-based strategies such as TfR targeting is often lower than predicted from in vitro studies. Second, membrane-camouflage systems and engineered exosomes face manufacturing challenges related to source standardization, batch-to-batch consistency, cargo controllability, and long-term immune safety. Third, although light- and heat-assisted BBB opening can be controlled in time and space, stricter animal and preclinical evaluation is still needed to avoid brain-tissue thermal injury and ensure the safety of repeated treatment.99,100,105,107 Therefore, in nanomedicine for depression, the most promising future direction for BBB-penetration strategies is not simply stronger penetration, but modular integration of receptor-mediated transcytosis, biomimetic inflammatory homing, and exogenous physical triggering. Such integration may allow drugs to reach the right brain region and cell type at the appropriate time and in the appropriate manner, thereby supporting systematic reprogramming of the neuroinflammation, barrier, and neuroplasticity network.

Cell Targeting Strategies: Microglia, Neurons, and Glial Cells

The central question in cell targeting is which cell populations should be prioritized after a therapeutic agent enters the brain. This question is particularly important in depression because the disease is not neuron-autonomous; rather, it involves a cellular network comprising microglia-driven inflammatory amplification, impaired neuronal plasticity, and astrocyte-mediated disruption of synaptic, metabolic, and barrier homeostasis. Therefore, redesigning nanodelivery systems according to cell type can shift brain targeting toward pathological cell-state targeting.25,88,108

For microglial targeting, Jiang et al developed PDA-Mem@M, a biomimetic microglia-targeted polydopamine nanomedicine loaded with memantine, which represents one of the most informative preclinical examples. This system uses microglial-membrane encapsulation to achieve homologous recognition and enrichment in inflammation-related brain regions, while synergistically regulating neuroinflammation and neuroplasticity through the ROS-scavenging activity of polydopamine and the pharmacological action of memantine.33 The original study and subsequent reviews indicate that PDA-Mem@M can cross the BBB, enhance microglial targeting, increase BDNF levels, reduce hippocampal ROS and pro-inflammatory factors in chronic stress models, and outperform memantine monotherapy even at lower doses and reduced dosing frequency. These findings suggest that microglia are not merely bystanders of inflammation, but therapeutic entry points that can be preferentially engaged by nanosystems.82,99

For neuronal targeting, one of the most compelling recent examples is the RVG-BDNF engineered exosome platform reported by Liu et al This platform uses RVG peptides to confer neuronal affinity on exosomes, allowing them to preferentially reach hippocampal and prefrontal neurons after crossing the BBB, increase local BDNF levels, promote neurogenesis, and alleviate LPS-induced depression-like behavior.35 We think The significance of this work is that it moves beyond simply delivering drugs to brain tissue and instead focuses directly on precise reconstruction of neurotrophic support within the damaged cell populations central to depression, thereby truly coupling nanodelivery with repair of neural plasticity. Furthermore, research on extracellular vesicles suggests that vesicles derived from different cellular sources may possess intrinsic cell-type preferences and regulatory capacities. For example, Liu et al reported that bone-marrow-mesenchymal-stem-cell-derived extracellular vesicles attenuated LPS-induced depression-like behavior, accompanied by decreased pro-inflammatory factors and reduced neuroinflammation.109 Although such systems may not achieve the same degree of single-cell specificity as engineered extracellular vesicles, these findings suggest that cellular source is an important design parameter because it shapes targeting properties relevant to nanotherapy for depression.

For other glial cell types, particularly astrocytes, the evidence base has expanded significantly over the past two years. On the one hand, Luarte et al showed that astrocyte-derived extracellular vesicles (ADEVs) are involved in immune signaling and influence neuronal function and the synaptic environment in stress-related emotional disorders, suggesting that astrocytes are not passive support cells, but pathogenic nodes worthy of individual targeting.108 On the other hand, the astrocyte-targeted estrogen biomimetic nanoparticle hydrogel reported by Chu et al advances this concept directly to the level of functional drug delivery. This system targets abnormal astrocytes in perimenopausal depression and achieves targeted delivery and therapeutic amplification of estradiol through inhibition of the P2X7 receptor, indicating that glial-cell targeting has begun to move from mechanistic inference toward verifiable drug-engineering implementation.110,111 It is also worth noting that the translational value of cell-targeting strategies is reflected in their natural compatibility with liquid biopsy and patient stratification. Xie et al observed a highly inflammatory astrocytic state in serum astrocyte-derived extracellular vesicles from patients with MDD,112 whereas Xu et al further found that after ECT in patients with treatment-resistant depression, GFAP, S100β, CD81, and several inflammatory markers in ADEVs decreased significantly. These findings suggest that glial-cell-related EV markers may become companion biomarkers for efficacy monitoring and target selection.113 Similarly, Zhang et al proposed that vitamin D-binding protein in plasma microglia-derived extracellular vesicles may serve as a potential biomarker for MDD,114 whereas Li et al detected changes in ADEV-associated BDNF and VEGF related to neuroplasticity and vascular or barrier regulation in two studies respectively.115 Together, these population-level data suggest that future nanomedicine for depression may not only direct drugs to specific cell types, but may also evolve into a closed-loop strategy in which EV profiles are first used to identify dominant pathological cell populations and then guide personalized cell-targeted interventions.114,115

Overall, microglial targeting is particularly suitable for interrupting inflammatory amplification and immune imbalance. Neuronal targeting serves synaptic remodeling and neurotrophic recovery more directly, whereas targeting astrocytes and other glial cells may simultaneously address metabolic support, glutamate homeostasis, and repair of the BBB microenvironment. The most promising next-generation nanosystems for depression are therefore unlikely to rely on a single strategy. Instead, they may require sequential or combinatorial designs that incorporate multilevel targeting modules for microglia, neurons, and glial cells according to the patient’s dominant pathological phenotype, thereby enabling network-reprogramming therapies more closely aligned with precision psychiatry.88,99

Subcellular Level: Mitochondria, Lysosomes, and Inflammasomes

The value of subcellular localization depends on whether treatment can reach the most persistent amplifiers within the pathological network of depression, including mitochondrial damage, lysosomal/autophagic imbalance, and excessive activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. In other words, nanomedicine may move drug delivery toward precise modulation of the coupled axis of neuroinflammation, barrier dysfunction, and neuroplasticity by extending intervention to organelles and signaling complexes.8,100,116

In recent years, multiple studies have suggested that mitochondria are not passive victims of injury in depression, but central hubs linking impaired energy metabolism, ROS accumulation, mtDNA leakage, and inflammatory amplification. Available evidence suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired mitophagy may contribute to depression by amplifying oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, whereas exosomes and other nanoscale carriers may serve as both delivery systems and platforms for detecting mitochondrial injury.8,117,118 Specifically, Mendes-Silva et al reported in human population data that the deletion rate of exosome-associated mitochondrial DNA (EX-mtDNA) was increased in patients with late-onset depression and was associated with inflammatory receptors and cognitive outcomes, suggesting that mitochondrial damage in depression may be detectable through nanoscale vesicles. This finding provides evidence for pathological stratification and offers a translational entry point for designing nanoscale intervention strategies for patients with mitochondrial damage.118 Direct mechanistic evidence linking mitochondrial repair with inflammasome inhibition is also increasing. Liu et al found that AdipoRon inhibited NLRP3 inflammasome activation by improving microglial mitophagy and mitochondrial homeostasis in a mouse model of depression, thereby alleviating depression-like behavior. Although this work is not a classical nanomedicine study, it defines subcellular pathological targets that future nanocarriers should prioritize. These targets are not generic inflammatory pathways, but rather inflammasome activation driven by defective mitochondrial quality control within microglia.119 In line with this concept, Han et al further summarized that activation of the microglial NLRP3 inflammasome in depression is not an isolated event, but is coupled to mitochondrial ROS, release of damage-related molecules, and reduced autophagy–lysosome clearance capacity.120 Therefore, effective nanotherapeutic strategies may need to target oxidative stress, mitochondrial homeostasis, and inflammasome clearance simultaneously, rather than merely blocking downstream inflammatory factors.120 From an empirical nanomedicine perspective, the Fe3O4@CS nanoenzyme with tryptophan hydroxylase-like activity reported by Fang et al is particularly informative. Although its main design goal was to restore 5-HT synthesis, its mechanism relies on endogenous ascorbic acid and H2O2 to catalyze the in situ conversion of tryptophan to 5-hydroxytryptophan in neurons, thereby extending the therapeutic interface to the subcellular scale of the intracellular redox microenvironment and neurotransmitter biosynthesis.85 These studies suggest that future mitochondria-targeted nanomedicine may not be limited to conjugation with classical groups such as TPP, but may also indirectly modulate mitochondria–neurotransmitter coupling by manipulating local ROS, cofactors, and metabolic flux.85

If mitochondria represent a major source of energy failure and danger signaling in depression, the lysosome-autophagy system functions as a key clearance machinery that determines whether pathological stress can be resolved in a timely manner. Recent studies have begun to reveal its causal involvement in emotional regulation. In a corticosterone-induced depression model, Zhang et al demonstrated that excessive neuronal autophagy consumed BDNF and impaired adult hippocampal neurogenesis, thereby exacerbating depression-like behavior. This finding suggests that increased autophagy is not necessarily beneficial and that nanocarriers designed to regulate autophagy must avoid shifting protective autophagy toward an opposite extreme characterized by depletion of neurotrophic factors.121 Mo et al further reported that the lysosomal TFEB-TRPML1 axis in astrocytes can regulate depression-like behavior. This finding suggests that lysosomal Ca2+ signaling and its associated transcriptional program are not merely housekeeping processes, but may influence emotional phenotypes through glial metabolic support, neurotransmitter homeostasis, and remodeling of neural-network excitability. This discovery is particularly relevant to nanomedicine because future lysosome-targeting delivery systems may be designed not only to facilitate intracellular trafficking and endosomal escape, but also to modulate glial-cell function through TFEB activation, restoration of lysosomal acidification, or ion-channel regulation.122 Lysosomal dysfunction is also closely linked to inflammasome activation. In a Chronic Unpredictable Mild Stress (CUMS) model, Li et al demonstrated that lysosomal dysfunction was associated with NLRP3 inflammasome activation and depression-like behavior. When cells fail to clear damaged mitochondria and inflammatory complexes through the autophagy-lysosome pathway, NLRP3 signaling may shift from transient defense to persistent pathogenic activation.20 This pathological sequence helps explain why engineered exosomes and biomimetic nanosystems increasingly emphasize organelle-level intracellular trafficking within microglia and other glial cells after endocytosis. For example, Lv et al constructed a macrophage-derived-exosome-functionalized PLGA nanocarrier (CMC-EXPL) that inhibited M1 polarization and promoted M2 polarization in a post-stroke depression model. Although this study did not directly measure NLRP3 assembly, its effects on iNOS/CD86, Arg-1/CD206, and brain inflammation suggest that exosome-based nanohybrids may deliver drugs to inflammatory cells and modulate their subcellular inflammatory programs.123

From a clinical-research and translational perspective, the greatest value of subcellular targeting lies in providing a more actionable level of precision medicine than targeting by brain region or cell type alone. Mitochondrial damage may be stratified through peripheral or EV-related mtDNA, oxidative-stress indices, and energy-metabolism markers; inflammasome activation may be reflected by NLRP3- and IL-1β-related signals; and lysosomal or autophagic status may increasingly be evaluated dynamically through EV cargo, autophagy markers, and multi-omics profiles. However, three major bottlenecks remain: achieving stable but not excessive organelle localization in vivo, avoiding biphasic effects caused by long-term intervention in autophagy or lysosomal pathways, and establishing reproducible systems for evaluating subcellular efficacy.20,118,122

Response Release and Multifunctional Nano-Strategy Collaboration

ROS Response

ROS should no longer be viewed solely as a marker of elevated oxidative stress, but rather as a pathological trigger that can be therapeutically exploited. On the one hand, excessive ROS contributes to neuroinflammation, dysregulated 5-HT metabolism, impaired neurogenesis, and reduced synaptic plasticity. On the other hand, redox abnormalities enriched in the lesion microenvironment provide a design rationale for responsive nanosystems that release cargo after reaching the target site, clear ROS during release, and enhance neuroprotection after ROS removal. Therefore, the significance of ROS-responsive strategies in depression lies not only in controlled release itself, but also in transforming pathological features into therapeutic switches.124,125

Liu et al reported a representative study in which dextran was modified with PBAP to construct ROS-responsive nanoparticles. They further enhanced nose-to-brain delivery using hexa-arginine (R6) and co-loaded olanzapine with the hydrogen-generating molecule ammonia borane into a thermosensitive hydrogel to form an Olz/RDPA in situ thermosensitive hydrogel. In the CUMS model, this system entered the brain through the nose-to-brain pathway. A high-ROS environment triggered cleavage of boronic ester bonds to promote drug release while simultaneously releasing H2 to scavenge ·OH. This reduced ROS burden, improved oxidative-stress-related 5-HT dysfunction, and alleviated depression-like behavior. Together, these findings demonstrate a therapeutic loop integrating ROS-responsive drug release, antioxidant therapy, and enhanced brain delivery37 In the context of perimenopausal depression, Hu et al extended this concept to a more complex biomimetic interface. They constructed HA-NGs@exosomes, a composite system in which hyaluronic-acid nanogels served as the core, PACAP and estradiol (E2) were co-loaded, and an exosomal membrane was coated on the outer layer to confer BBB penetration and ROS-responsive drug-release capability. This platform showed efficient cellular uptake and favorable brain-delivery performance in both in vitro and in vivo studies. After intranasal administration, it produced rapid antidepressant-like effects in mice subjected to ovariectomy combined with CUMS. This finding suggests that ROS-responsive design is not limited to single-drug release, but can also be coupled with dual hormone and neuropeptide cargoes and exosome-mimetic shells to create a multifunctional nanotherapeutic module aligned with pathological network modulation.31 Beyond ROS-triggered drug release, another important approach is to design the nanosystem itself as a ROS scavenger or nanoenzyme. The CeO2@BSA nanoclusters reported by Fu et al represent an important example in this field. Rather than relying on conventional antidepressant loading, this system used the enzyme-like antioxidant activity of nanoceria as the therapeutic core to reduce oxidative stress and neuroinflammation in the brain and improve behavioral outcomes in depression models. This suggests that, for some depression phenotypes dominated by redox imbalance, ROS itself may become a primary intervention target, and nanoplatforms may shift from drug-delivery tools to catalytic therapeutic entities.83 This approach was further extended by Hu et al, who constructed Pd-CeO2@RBC. In this system, Pd-CeO2 nanosheets provided SOD- and CAT-like activities and scavenged O2−, H2O2, ·OH, and certain reactive nitrogen species, while red-blood-cell-membrane encapsulation enhanced biocompatibility and BBB penetration. The resulting platform reduced the activation of microglia and astrocytes, alleviated neuroinflammation, and improved depression-like behavior. This suggests that, when ROS-responsive or ROS-clearing strategies are combined with membrane camouflage, they may expand from simple redox intervention into a multilevel synergistic approach integrating barrier crossing, antioxidant effects, and anti-inflammatory activity.126

Similarly, the melanin-like polydopamine nanoparticles proposed by Zhu et al are not traditional passive carriers. Their work showed that polydopamine nanoparticles could exert anti-inflammatory effects and rescue synaptic loss in inflammatory depression, suggesting that organic nanomaterials with free-radical-scavenging potential can also serve as components of ROS-related therapies. Their value lies in simultaneously suppressing inflammatory amplification and protecting synaptic structures, rather than merely reducing a single oxidative index.54 Recently, ROS-responsive strategies had become more closely coupled to specific injury and cell-death pathways. Wang et al encapsulated the NOX4 inhibitor GLX351322 into mPEG-PCL nanoparticles and found that the resulting GLX NPs increased drug accumulation in the brain, inhibited NOX4 expression, reduced ROS and lipid peroxidation, activated the Nrf2/HO-1/GPX4 axis, and alleviated neuronal ferroptosis in CUMS mice, thereby improving depression-like behavior. In principle, such systems may also reduce systemic exposure and off-target drug release. However, the available evidence remains almost entirely limited to rodent preclinical studies. Key issues still need to be resolved before clinical translation, including the ROS activation threshold, the efficiency of nose-to-brain delivery, batch-to-batch consistency of exosome- or membrane-encapsulated formulations, long-term immunological safety, and the possibility that excessive antioxidant activity may disrupt physiological redox signaling across different models. Therefore, the most promising future direction may lie not in a single ROS-responsive mechanism, but in a modular system that integrates ROS-triggered drug release, ROS scavenging, BBB penetration, immune regulation, and neuroplasticity repair. Such an approach could be combined with patient stratification based on oxidative stress phenotypes. pH- and Inflammatory-Microenvironment-Responsive Strategies

Unlike ROS-responsive systems, which rely primarily on redox imbalance as a release trigger, pH- and inflammatory-microenvironment-responsive strategies convert another set of key abnormalities in depression-related lesions, including local inflammatory amplification, endocytic acidification, glial activation, and receptor-related signaling, into cues for drug delivery and therapeutic amplification. However, original studies that directly validate this concept in depression still focus mainly on inflammatory-microenvironment responsiveness, whereas evidence for strictly defined pH-responsive systems remains limited. Therefore, this section should be understood as focusing primarily on inflammatory-microenvironment responsiveness, with pH responsiveness discussed as an emerging complementary direction.82,90,99

The neuroinflammation-guided cell-liposome delivery system developed by Zhou et al is a representative example of exploiting the inflammatory microenvironment for delivery. This system does not rely solely on passive diffusion, but instead uses neuroinflammatory states to target the amygdala and improve behavioral outcomes in LPS-induced depression-like models, suggesting that the inflammatory microenvironment can serve as a pathological cue for the spatial selectivity of nanosystems.104 In line with this concept, Jiang et al reported a microglia-biomimetic, memantine-loaded polydopamine nanomedicine that integrates inflammation recognition and multifunctional therapy within a single platform. Microglial-membrane camouflage confers tropism toward inflammation-related brain regions and activated glial cells, whereas the polydopamine framework helps clear oxidative and inflammatory signals, producing stronger antidepressant-like effects than free memantine in depression models. This suggests that responsiveness to the inflammatory microenvironment does not necessarily require bond-cleavage-mediated drug release, but may also involve inflammatory homing, inflammation-associated enrichment, and inflammation-driven pharmacological amplification.33 In inflammatory depression, the melanin-like polydopamine nanoparticles developed by Zhu et al provide another line of evidence supporting microenvironment-regulating nanomedicines. In an LPS-induced model of inflammatory depression, PDA nanoparticles crossed the BBB and, through their phenolic-hydroxyl-rich structure, scavenged free radicals and reduced neuroinflammation while rescuing synaptic loss and depression-like behavior. This suggests that, when the inflammatory microenvironment constitutes the core lesion, the nanoplatform may function not only as a carrier, but also as an active agent for modulating the pathological microenvironment.54

Similarly, consistent with inflammation-microenvironment-oriented and functionally synergistic design, Hu et al constructed an exosome-coated ROS-responsive nanogel for the inflammatory and oxidative-stress microenvironment associated with perimenopausal depression. In this system, hyaluronic-acid nanogels were used to co-load PACAP and estradiol, whereas the exosomal outer layer improved cellular uptake and BBB penetration, producing rapid antidepressant-like effects in an ovariectomy-plus-CUMS model. This work suggests that inflammatory-microenvironment responsiveness in depression often overlaps with ROS, hormonal imbalance, and barrier abnormalities, and that future systems may need to respond to more than one pathological signal.31 In more complex forms of secondary depression, the macrophage-derived-exosome-functionalized PLGA nanocarrier CMC-EXPL, designed by Lv et al, is also noteworthy. This system was designed for post-stroke depression and achieved neuroinflammation-targeted delivery through macrophage-derived exosome functionalization and dual-drug co-loading. It also alleviated depression-like symptoms by regulating microglial polarization, suggesting that inflammatory-microenvironment-responsive strategies may offer particular advantages in subtypes with a high inflammatory burden, such as post-stroke depression.123 In contrast, evidence for pH responsiveness in depression remains closer to a transferable design principle than to a fully validated therapeutic module. At present, direct evidence that pH-responsive nanoplatforms improve depression-related outcomes through lesion-specific acidic-microenvironment targeting remains limited. For example, Zhao et al’s pH-responsive PEG-coating strategy enhanced the accumulation of small extracellular vesicles in acidic lesion areas,128 whereas Meng et al reported that a pH-responsive polyethylene glycol strategy improved the brain-delivery efficiency of PEGylated nanomedicine (Figure 8).129 Importantly, Figure 8 is included as an extrapolated CNS nanomedicine example illustrating a general BBB transcytosis and pH-triggered release strategy, rather than as direct evidence for depression treatment. Although these studies mainly address other brain-disease settings, they collectively demonstrate the technical feasibility of exploiting endosomal or lysosomal acidification to achieve surface deshielding, carrier exposure, or subsequent release. They therefore provide an engineering template for pH-responsive strategies that are not yet fully developed in depression.128,129 Therefore, such systems should currently be regarded as design templates for future depression-oriented nanomedicine development rather than as validated depression-specific therapeutic platforms. Future studies are needed to determine whether pH-responsive BBB-shuttle strategies can achieve safe, reproducible, and pathology-relevant delivery in depression models and whether such delivery translates into meaningful antidepressant-like effects. This conclusion is also indirectly supported by recent reviews. Whether considering reviews of functional delivery systems for depression or summaries of nose-to-brain nanotherapy and biomimetic nanodelivery, the repeatedly discussed and preclinically validated strategies are still dominated by ROS-responsive systems, membrane camouflage, exosome engineering, and inflammation-targeting approaches, whereas pH responsiveness remains largely at the stage of conceptual potential and transferable design.82,90,99 This means that the next step in this direction is not simply to increase materials complexity, but to establish a true closed loop among lesion-acidification mapping, drug-release validation, and efficacy correlation in stress-based or inflammatory depression models. It is also worth noting that the latest study by Chu et al reported that an astrocyte-targeted estrogen biomimetic nanoparticle hydrogel improved perimenopausal depression by inhibiting the P2X7 receptor. From another perspective, this finding suggests that inflammatory-microenvironment responsiveness may in the future be further refined into inflammatory-receptor-responsive or glial-state-responsive delivery.110 In other words, future systems may not necessarily rely on pH reduction or bond cleavage alone to release drugs, but may instead depend on inflammatory receptor-expression profiles, glial activation states, and the local ionic or metabolic environment to achieve higher-resolution conditional delivery. Therefore, the key conclusion is not that pH responsiveness has matured, but that inflammatory-microenvironment responsiveness has already become a practical mainline, whereas pH responsiveness is gradually emerging as a refined auxiliary tool that can be coupled with exosomes, membrane camouflage, and brain-delivery modules. Once the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of brain acidification, glial activation, and inflammatory receptor expression can be translated into quantifiable delivery parameters, such strategies may truly move from smart materials toward precise psychopharmacology.110,128,129

Photothermal and Exogenous-Stimulus-Responsive Strategies

External-stimulus-responsive strategies provide spatiotemporal control over when and where release occurs and how strongly regulation is applied, while shifting drug delivery, barrier regulation, and neural-circuit remodeling from passive transport toward active regulation. When light, ultrasound, or magnetic fields are used as triggers for nanosystems, the therapeutic target is no longer limited to a single molecule but extends to the interconnected network linking neuroinflammation, the BBB, and neuroplasticity.61,130,131

As an example of photothermal responsiveness, Li et al constructed near-infrared (NIR)-responsive conjugated polymer nanoparticles that combined the pharmacological effects of fasudil with nanoscale photothermal transduction, thereby enabling remote regulation of the depression-related Kv7.4 ion channel through local temperature elevation. This work suggests that external stimuli are useful not only for promoting drug release but also for modulating abnormal electrophysiological activity through thermosensitive gating, thereby transforming the nanocarrier into a nanoscale neural regulator and providing a new paradigm for circuit-level intervention in depression130 In parallel, Lu et al used superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIOs) combined with mild magnetic stimulation in CUMS mice to selectively enhance local magnetic responsiveness in the left limbic cortex. After 5 days of intervention, depression-like behavior improved, accompanied by increased BDNF levels and attenuation of HPA-axis hyperactivity. These findings suggest that exogenous physical fields can use nanomaterials to achieve more focused cortical stimulation, thereby linking neural-plasticity recovery with normalization of the stress system.131

However, because depression is characterized by chronic low-grade inflammation and synaptic fragility, stimulation alone is unlikely to be sufficient. More effective systems may need to combine anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and plasticity-promoting functions. For example, the melanin-like polydopamine nanoparticles (PDA NPs) reported by Zhu et al, although not triggered by external irradiation, possess intrinsic free-radical-scavenging and immunoregulatory properties that reverse LPS-induced depression-like behavior, reduce peripheral and central inflammatory factors, inhibit microglial activation, and rescue synaptic loss.131 This result suggests that, to maximize their value in depression, exogenous-stimulus-responsive systems may need to cooperate with endogenous pathology-responsive modules to cover the core pathological chain linking inflammation and synaptic injury.131 This concept is further illustrated by Liu et al, who designed stimulus-responsive nanoparticles delivered through the nose-to-brain pathway for broad-spectrum ROS clearance and alleviation of depression-like behavior. This system improved behavioral outcomes and reduced neuroinflammation and oxidative-stress-related brain injury. These findings suggest that, for patients with fragile BBB function and sensitivity to systemic adverse effects, combining exogenous route guidance with lesion-microenvironment responsiveness may be more practical than relying solely on an intravenous BBB-crossing strategy.37

One of the clearest recent examples of the clinical potential of precise release is the study by Wu et al on low-intensity focused ultrasound (LIFU)-triggered ketamine-loaded nanodroplets (Ket NDs). The authors administered Ket NDs with high encapsulation efficiency and stability by intravenous injection and used transcranial LIFU to achieve region-specific drug release in the lateral habenula (LHb). This approach produced antidepressant-like effects at lower systemic doses in a mouse model and was intended to reduce the risk of ketamine dependence and systemic adverse effects. This design is particularly important because it integrates a rapid-acting antidepressant, deep-brain targeting, and physically triggered drug release within a single nanodroplet platform.132 In this respect, it represents an important step from BBB-crossing delivery toward on-demand therapy at the brain-network level. From a broader technological perspective, focused-ultrasound-mediated BBB opening has been regarded as one of the exogenous-stimulation strategies closest to clinical translation in recent years.133,134 Its advantages include reversibility, focality, image guidance, and compatibility with platforms such as liposomes, nanodroplets, and polymeric particles. Zhao et al noted in their review that focused-ultrasound-mediated BBB opening has advanced rapidly in drug delivery for neurodegenerative diseases, with major challenges including standardization of acoustic parameter windows, consistency across brain regions, safety of repeated procedures, and clarification of carrier-ultrasound synergistic mechanisms.133 Durham et al further summarized current clinical studies and showed that ultrasound-mediated BBB opening has entered multiple stages of human investigation, with an emphasis on verifying safety, reproducibility, and enhancement of drug delivery.134 Although applications in mental disorders are still at an early stage, this clinical foundation suggests that once depression-related nanosystems demonstrate controllable release and network-level benefits in deep affective circuits such as the LHb and the prefrontal-limbic system, the translational threshold may be reduced.134 Davidson et al also included depression as a key indication in their psychiatric focused-ultrasound roadmap, emphasizing the future use of FUS for neural modulation, BBB opening, and ultrasound-triggered drug-release pathways in combination with imaging, biomarkers, and patient stratification. This perspective clarifies the potential clinical direction of external-stimulus-responsive nanomedicine for depression. This direction is not generalized drug delivery to the brain, but coordinated modulation of the drug-inflammation-plasticity triad at monitored, reproducible, and quantifiable brain-network nodes.135

Therefore, the evidence discussed in this section suggests that the true significance of photothermal and other exogenous-stimulus-responsive strategies is not to add another superficially sophisticated module to nanodrug delivery, but to enable nanoplatforms to intervene in key pathological processes of depression within a spatiotemporally closed loop. Upstream, physical fields may help overcome or bypass the BBB; midstream, they may synchronously suppress ROS and microglia-driven inflammatory amplification; and downstream, they may reshape neural plasticity through ion-channel regulation, recovery of BDNF signaling, and synaptic reconstruction. To achieve future clinical translation, additional solutions will be needed to address transcranial energy attenuation, long-term biosafety, tissue tolerance to repeated stimulation, patient stratification in the context of heterogeneity, and large-scale GMP-compatible manufacturing of complex multifunctional nanomaterials. Nevertheless, existing research is sufficient to show that exogenous-stimulus responsiveness is not a marginal supplement to nanomedicine for depression, but a core engine driving its transition from delivery technology toward network therapy.61,130,131

Clinical Translational Challenges and Future Directions

Challenge

Although recent research has documented the rapid development of nanomedicine for depression, substantial barriers remain before current findings can be translated into clinical practice. The central challenge lies not simply in converting drugs into nanoparticles, but in enabling nanomedicine to achieve durable and verifiable modulation of the neuroinflammation–barrier–neuroplasticity network in a disease as heterogeneous, dynamically evolving, and multiscale as depression. In other words, the core challenge has shifted from simply improving BBB transport to establishing a causal and verifiable translational framework that connects patient stratification, barrier-state assessment, brain-cell-specific delivery, long-term safety, and scalable manufacturing.19,41,125

First, depression is not a single biological entity. Patients with inflammatory, stress-vulnerable, metabolic, or vascular/barrier-damage-associated phenotypes may respond very differently to the same nanointervention. At present, most nanodrug-delivery studies still rely on CUMS, CRS, LPS, or corticosterone models, which differ substantially from human MDD in etiology and clinical heterogeneity, thereby directly limiting the extrapolation of preclinical findings.43,125,136 Second, the BBB is not a static barrier, but a dynamic interface shaped by disease stage, brain region, sex, and inflammatory burden. For example, Shang et al used DCE-MRI to show, for the first time, that patients with MDD exhibit regional increases in BBB permeability, which correlate with symptom severity in regions including the hippocampus and thalamus.41 Dion-Albert et al and Sun et al further suggested, on the basis of stress models and human brain tissue, that claudin-5 downregulation, vascular remodeling, and TNF-α infiltration are not merely accompanying phenomena, but may actively drive vulnerability to depression.43,137 This means that enhanced penetration may not always be the correct goal, because in some patients the barrier is already abnormally open or dysregulated, and further increasing permeability may expand the window for nonspecific exposure and neurotoxicity.137,138 Third, the real challenge of targeting within the brain is not simply reaching brain tissue, but reaching the correct cells, subcellular compartments, and disease time windows. Depression-related inflammation is not limited to microglial overactivation. Astrocytes, brain microvascular endothelial cells, pericytes, and neuron-derived extracellular-vesicle signaling also participate in pathological remodeling.112,139,140 Peripheral-accessible EV markers may help identify populations suitable for barrier- or inflammation-targeted nanotherapy, but such markers still lack standardized isolation procedures, reference ranges, and repeated validation across independent cohorts.112,139,140 In addition, a well-designed nanosystem does not necessarily mean that particle fate is controllable in vivo. Once nanoparticles enter the bloodstream, the protein corona rapidly reshapes their biological identity, thereby altering pharmacokinetics, receptor recognition, clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system, and brain distribution.94,141,142 At every stage, from hemodynamics and endothelial uptake to transcytosis and diffusion within the brain parenchyma, BBB-crossing nanomaterials face potential mismatch among particle size, surface charge, shape, ligand density, and the disease microenvironment.94,141,142 Therefore, observing fluorescent signals in mouse brains is far from equivalent to achieving effective, predictable, and clinically translatable brain drug delivery.

Fifth, efficacy evaluation still leans heavily toward behavioral endpoints and lacks an integrated readout that can simultaneously capture inflammation, barrier function, and plasticity. For example, many studies have shown that nanomedicine can improve sucrose preference, forced-swim performance, or tail-suspension behavior, yet evidence remains incomplete as to whether these interventions truly repair endothelial tight junctions, reduce TNF-α and IL-1β entry across the barrier, restore BDNF and synaptic-protein expression, and reshape the temporal causal dynamics of prefrontal-hippocampal circuits. This leaves an explanatory gap between antidepressant-like effects and genuine network reprogramming.41,125,143 Although some proof-of-concept studies are encouraging, they have also exposed the high threshold for translation. Issues such as manufacturing consistency, cargo stability, batch-to-batch variation, immunogenicity, long-term safety under repeated administration, and regulatory classification as drugs, biologics, or combination products remain far from resolved. This is especially true for EV formulations, which still lack industry-wide standards regarding source cells, purification methods, potency definition, and quality control.144,145 Therefore, what is most needed in the next stage of nanomedicine for depression is not simply the addition of stronger brain-penetrating ligands or higher loading rates, but the establishment of a research paradigm based on patient stratification, guided by BBB status, directed toward the glial-vascular-neuronal unit, and supported by multimodal biomarkers and causal-mechanism validation. Only when a nanoplatform can answer five questions at the same time—who should receive it, when it should be used, where it should be delivered, which pathological chain it rewrites, and whether it can be safely scaled up for production—will its clinical prospects in depression truly move from an advanced concept to a reliable treatment.45,146

Future Research Directions

At present, nanomedicine for depression has not yet reached the stage of precision clinical translation. The key question for the next stage is how BBB -crossing drug delivery can be advanced to achieve durable and verifiable modulation of the pathological coupling among neuroinflammation, barrier damage, and neuroplasticity imbalance in depression. Based on the current body of evidence, the most important future direction is no longer simply to pursue higher brain uptake, but to establish an integrated paradigm that encompasses patient stratification, barrier characterization, brain-cell-specific delivery, and network-level functional readouts.11,147

Large-sample studies have shown that depression and anxiety do not reflect a single pathway imbalance, but can instead be quantified as brain-circuit biotypes with distinct treatment-response profiles. Stolfi et al and Perna et al further noted that precision psychiatry guided by multi-omics and biomarkers is moving from a conceptual framework toward practical implementation. This provides a realistic basis for stratified development of nanomedicines tailored to inflammatory, barrier-damage, or plasticity-deficient subtypes.148–150 Specifically, future research should shift from blind BBB crossing to measurement and navigation of BBB status. Shang et al observed regional increases in BBB permeability in human MDD using DCE-MRI, whereas Xie et al and Burrows et al suggested, from astrocyte-derived EV inflammatory features and abnormalities in neuronal-enriched EV miR-93, that depressed patients already exhibit barrier-glia-neuron signals that can be captured by liquid biopsy.11,41,140 Therefore, a more promising strategy is to combine imaging-based BBB characterization with EV molecular fingerprints in order to identify which patients require enhanced delivery and which would benefit more from barrier repair and limitation of abnormal leakage.11,41,140 Correspondingly, the design goal of future nanoplatforms should move beyond simply delivering drugs into the brain and toward rebuilding neurovascular-unit homeostasis. Many studies suggests that future efficacy evaluation should not focus solely on behavioral improvement, but should also incorporate BBB tight-junction integrity, glial inflammation, changes in ADEV and NDEV cargo, and recovery of neuroplasticity-related molecules within the same evaluative framework. In this way, network reprogramming may become a verifiable endpoint rather than a rhetorical concept.115,151

At the level of delivery tools, engineered EVs and nucleic-acid nanomedicines are likely to represent some of the most promising directions for future breakthroughs. For example, the RVG-BDNF engineered exosomes constructed by Liu et al achieved targeted delivery to hippocampal and prefrontal neurons in an LPS-induced depression-like model, accompanied by improvement in BDNF/TrkB-related plasticity.35 Garcia-Martin et al’s study of miRNA sorting sequence codes further illustrates that EVs are not merely natural carriers, but are likely to become programmable loading platforms.152 By designing EXOmotif and CELLmotif sequences, researchers can control whether miRNAs are retained in cells or enriched into EVs, thereby providing a basis for constructing EVs with preferred brain-region distribution, cell-type specificity, and cargo selectivity. This opens substantial methodological space for the design of psychiatric nanomedicines.35,152 However, for such platforms to enter clinical practice, humanized, high-throughput, and disease-relevant screening systems must be developed in parallel. This is precisely where BBB-on-a-chip and organoid or organ-on-a-chip technologies are valuable. Therefore, we believe future nanomedicine research in depression should particularly incorporate stress- or inflammation-induced BBB chips, patient-derived iPSC cerebrovascular-unit models, and glia-neuron co-culture organoids in order to reduce the extrapolation bias introduced by single animal models.153,154 Furthermore, the value of artificial intelligence and machine learning should not be limited to auxiliary optimization at the literature level, but should become an integral part of the future nanomedicine research-and-development process. AI can be used for nanomaterial-formulation screening, drug-carrier matching, identification of biomarkers for brain disorders, and personalized treatment design.155 For depression, this means that a model could eventually integrate a patient’s inflammatory profile, EV cargo, circuit imaging, BBB-permeability parameters, and prior treatment response in order to infer the most suitable carrier type, ligand density, nucleic-acid cargo, and dosing window. Such a shift would move the field from an experience-driven approach, in which particles are designed first and indications sought later, toward a data-driven strategy in which pathological subtypes are defined first and particles designed accordingly.149,150,155 Therefore, the most competitive future research route for nanomedicine in depression should involve defining treatment targets through precise subtyping, selecting delivery strategies through BBB imaging and EV-based liquid biopsy, achieving cell-specific regulation through engineered EV or nucleic-acid carriers, validating efficacy in BBB chips and patient-derived models, and ultimately enabling clinical translation through manufacturability, reproducible quality control, and interpretable algorithms. Only in this way can the transition from BBB-crossing drug delivery to reprogramming of the neuroinflammation-barrier-neuroplasticity network truly evolve from a set of basic discoveries into a new paradigm for the treatment of depression.35,41,153,155

Conclusion

Current evidence indicates that nanomedicine can improve key technical and biological dimensions of antidepressant intervention, including brain or nose-to-brain delivery, protection of unstable cargos, controlled release, cellular and subcellular targeting, and local modulation of inflammation, oxidative stress, and neurotrophic signaling. Lipid and polymeric nanoparticles, nucleic acid carriers, nanozymes, biomimetic membrane-coated systems, and engineered extracellular vesicles have shown encouraging effects in preclinical models by reducing neuroinflammatory activation, attenuating oxidative and mitochondrial injury, improving barrier dysfunction, and restoring synaptic plasticity-associated markers. These findings suggest that nanoplatforms can be engineered to engage multiple pathological processes relevant to depression, rather than functioning only as passive carriers. However, current evidence does not yet demonstrate durable disease-modifying network reprogramming in patients with depression. Most available studies still rely on rodent stress- or inflammation-based models, short-term behavioral assays, and selected molecular readouts. Therefore, claims of network-level repair should be made cautiously and should require causal pathway validation, longitudinal assessment, multimodal biomarkers, and evidence from human-relevant models. Important uncertainties remain regarding negative or neutral findings, interlaboratory reproducibility, sex- and subtype-specific responses, protein corona formation, brain and peripheral biodistribution, long-term immune effects, nanomaterial clearance, repeated-dose toxicity, and dose–exposure–response relationships. Thus, the field has generated strong proof-of-concept evidence but has not yet reached the translational certainty required for clinical application. Before nanomedicine for depression can advance toward clinical translation, several minimum evidence requirements must be met. First, future studies should demonstrate reproducible efficacy across clinically relevant models and should not rely solely on forced swim, tail suspension, or sucrose preference outcomes. Second, pharmacokinetic and biodistribution studies should quantify tissue accumulation, persistence, clearance, and potential brain, immune, hepatic, renal, or mucosal toxicity after repeated administration. Third, mechanistic studies should verify that behavioral improvement is linked to defined pathological target engagement rather than nonspecific sedation, stress reduction, or systemic anti-inflammatory effects. Fourth, translational development should incorporate patient stratification, blood–brain barrier status assessment, imaging or liquid biopsy biomarkers, dose scalability, good manufacturing practice-compatible manufacturing, batch-to-batch reproducibility, sterility and stability control, and early regulatory classification. Only after these criteria are addressed can nanomedicine progress from promising preclinical delivery systems to clinically testable precision interventions for depression. The conceptual promise of network reprogramming should also be clearly distinguished from the evidence currently available. At present, nanomedicine for depression has established proof-of-concept evidence for improving brain delivery and modulating several pathological readouts, including inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress markers, blood–brain barrier-related proteins, and neuroplasticity-associated signals. However, these findings do not yet demonstrate durable disease-modifying network reprogramming. Future studies should therefore move beyond multi-target biomarker changes and establish causal target engagement, inter-node coordination, longitudinal durability of therapeutic effects, reproducibility across models, and validation using human-relevant biomarkers or clinical samples. Only with this level of evidence can network reprogramming be considered more than a conceptual framework.

Overall, the future value of nanomedicine for depression will depend less on producing increasingly complex nanostructures than on generating rigorous, reproducible, and clinically interpretable evidence. A mature translational framework should connect material design with disease subtype, target engagement, safety profile, manufacturing feasibility, and regulatory pathway definition. Such a framework will help the field move beyond proof-of-concept optimism and identify which nanoplatforms are truly capable of improving antidepressant therapy in patients.
