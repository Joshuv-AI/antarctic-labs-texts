Improved virus concentration methods for wash waters from decontamination of permeable and non-permeable surfaces
Authors: Brittany N. Hurst, Asja Korajkic, Adin Pemberton, Brian R. McMinn
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Surface decontamination is a method of using wash water to decontaminated surfaces preventing transmission of biological contaminants that can pose potential health risks to responders and the public. However, the risks associated with handling used wash water are largely unknown due to the lack of effective methodology to screen for pathogenic microorganisms present in these samples, especially viral pathogens. This study adapted the dead-end hollow-fiber ultrafiltration (D-HFUF) system to wash waters, including a separate procedure for recovering particle attached viruses. Simulated wash water was created using dechlorinated tap water containing a mild surfactant (0.05 % Tween 80). To determine virus recovery efficiencies, measured amounts of somatic and F+ coliphage were spiked into 2-liter volumes of wash water under the following scenarios: (1) wash water was amended with a measured amount of sterile river sediment with no sediment separation prior to filter concentration; or (2) sediment added to wash water was allowed to settle prior to filter concentrating clarified liquid portions, while precipitated sediment was subjected to viral extraction techniques to recover particle attached virus; and (3) the optimized method was deployed on non-porous and porous surfaces to simulate a decontamination clean-up event. Separation of sediment prior to D-HFUF significantly increased recovery of coliphages, (P = <0.0001) versus filtration of sediment and liquids simultaneously. A tryptic soy broth (TSB) elution solution was significantly more effective (P = ≤0.010) for recovery of both somatic and F+ coliphage, (108 ± 9 % and 92 ± 9 %, respectively), compared to elution buffers containing various surfactants (sodium hexametaphosphate, Tween 80) for recovering particle attached virus. Simulating a biocontaminate clean-up event (using the optimized sediment separation and elution protocol) resulted in coliphage recoveries of 75–96 % (permeable surface) and 71–92 % (non-permeable surface). This procedure can be used to effectively detect viruses in used wash waters aiding in reducing risks to human health during site decontamination.

Introduction

Contaminated surfaces resulting from both natural (floods, hurricanes, etc.) and man-made incidents (bioterrorism, accidental waste discharge, etc.) have caused illnesses through exposure of humans to biological agents and toxins (Raber et al., 2001). Following a contamination incident, exposed surfaces can be decontaminated using a wash water (Gallardo et al., 2019; Grundmann, 2014; Krauter et al., 2011). Wash water is the blanket term referring to water that has been amended with various concentrations of surfactants and anti-microbial agents and can vary widely depending on situational requirements (Grundmann, 2014). Wash water is used to spray surfaces to remove or dilute contaminates present, although if the wash water doesn’t contain the appropriate disinfectant (i.e., sodium hypochloride), further decontamination might be required (Michael et al., 1999). Used wash water may then be collected, disinfected, and properly disposed of to prevent the release of active biological agents that represent a risk to human health (Krauter et al., 2011; Department of Homeland Security, 2009). Through these processes (surface decontamination and used wash water collection), exposure risks to first responders can occur through inhalation of aerosolized droplets or through primary skin contact with contaminated liquids. Characterizing biological agents, in these instances, is important to give insight as to the safe handling and disposal of wash water during site remediation processes, as well as initiating proper treatment for exposed individuals.

Determining the presence of viral agents is especially important when their infectious dose needed to cause illness is low. Viruses deposited on surfaces also have the propensity to persist for extended periods of time (Hoglund et al., 2002; Wanjugi et al., 2016), and are resistant to natural degradative processes such as sunlight, desiccation, heat (McMinn et al., 2020; Bae and Schwab, 2008), and disinfection (Fujioka and Yoneyama, 2002; Hoff and Akin, 1986; Sauerbrei et al., 2009) compared to pathogenic bacteria. Human enteric viruses (enteroviruses, norovirus and adenovirus) are important etiological agents of waterborne gastroenteritis and diarrheal diseases worldwide (Jiang et al., 2007; Hlavsa et al., 2015; Leclerc et al., 2002). During natural disasters, human waste can contaminate structural surfaces, this is especially true in situations where flood waters may contain untreated wastewater (ten Veldhuis et al., 2010; Taylor et al., 2011). Unfortunately, efforts to determine exposures and risks associated with handling used wash water have been hampered by the lack of validated methods for concentration, recovery and detection of viral agents present in these sources (Gallardo et al., 2019). Since viruses are typically found in low concentrations, wash waters following surface decontamination in the aftermath of a natural or intentional contamination incident require the use of a concentrating filter to allow for detection of dilute viruses present (McMinn et al., 2017). Hollow-fiber ultrafiltration (HFUF) can be used to concentrate human enteric viruses from liquids and has previously shown effectiveness for both wastewater and environmental waters (McMinn et al., 2017; Kahler et al., 2015; Morales-Morales et al., 2003; Olszewski et al., 2005). Due to the risks associated with working with infectious pathogenic viruses in laboratory settings, viruses such as coliphages, which share similar morphologies with that of viral pathogens, can be used in laboratory studies as pathogen surrogates. Coliphage have been safely used to develop and evaluate methods that have been successfully applied to pathogenic virus strains (Stetler, 1984; Rajal et al., 2007) since they pose no danger to human health, rely on simple culture-based assays for quantification, and can produce high infectious titers under routine laboratory conditions.

Particulates from soil and other debris are commonly collected as surfaces are washed during decontamination procedures. Infectious virus attached to particulate matter in liquids can shield viruses from both physical and chemical degradative forces (Gerba et al., 2018; Cromeans et al., 2010). Hollow-fiber ultrafiltration in a dead-end setup (D-HFUF) has shown effectiveness in recovery of viruses from a variety of challenging environmental matrices, although sediment can impact the ability of this filtration system for recovery of viruses present (McMinn et al., 2017; Morales-Morales et al., 2003; Hill et al., 2009; Liu et al., 2012). A challenge in working with used wash waters is that they may contain various amounts of particulate matter which can foul filters while samples are being concentrated. Viruses can become bound to sediment particles present in environmental samples, justifying the need for effective virus recovery methods for both liquid and particulate portions of samples (Rodriguez et al., 2009; Filippini and Middelboe, 2007; Whitman et al., 2009). Since the D-HFUF-SAL system has been extensively evaluated for concentrating viruses from large volumes (1–1000 L) of environmental waters and wastewaters (McMinn et al., 2017; EPA, 2018a,b; McMinn et al., 2023), our research focused on adapting this methodology, along with a separate procedure for recovering particle attached viruses (McMinn et al., 2017; Morales-Morales et al., 2003; Olszewski et al., 2005). Appropriate virus detection methods suitable for used wash waters need to include steps to effectively concentrate viruses in both liquid and solid fractions of a sample (Kitajima et al., 2020; Maranger and Bird, 1996a).

The goals of this study were to (i) evaluate the D-HFUF for concentrating viruses from liquid portions of wash water (with and without sediment), (ii) identify and optimize methodologies to extract infectious viruses from sample particulates, (iii) combine and evaluate both methods (liquid concentration and particulate extraction) to recover spiked virus from used wash water following a simulated clean-up event from both permeable and non- permeable surfaces.

Methods and materials

Virus stock

Somatic and F+ coliphage were used as viral surrogates during experimentation. Primary wastewater influent, collected from a wastewater treatment plant in the Cincinnati, Ohio area, was used to isolate and propagate somatic and F+ coliphages, using methods described below. Upon arrival at the laboratory, primary influent samples were filter sterilized through a 0.22 μm syringe driven disc filter (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Filtrates (5 mL) were then added to 100 mL of tryptic soy broth (TSB) (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) containing bacterial host under mid-log growth (CN-13 ATCC-700609 for somatic coliphage or Famp ATCC-700891 for F+ coliphage), and appropriate antibiotic (100 μg/mL nalidixic acid for CN-13 E. coli and 15 μg/mL of both streptomycin/ampicillin for Famp E. coli). The suspensions were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to allow for viral propagation. The suspensions were then centrifuged at 3600× g for 10 min to remove cellular debris, and the resulting supernatant was passed through a 0.22 μm sterilizing disk filter. Coliphage levels in each filtrate were titered using a single agar layer method (SAL). Coliphage densities between 400 and 600 plaque-forming units PFU/mL were targeted for use during spiking experimentation.

Single agar layer (SAL) method

The SAL method was used to quantitate F+ and somatic coliphage during experimentation as previously described (McMinn et al., 2017; EPA, 2018a, 2018b). Briefly, 100 mL samples were amended with a 500 μl of a 4 M solution of MgCl2 (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) before adding equivalent volumes of molten 2X tryptic soy agar (TSA) (tempered to 50 °C), 10 mL of the log-phase bacterial host and 2 mL of appropriate antibiotic solution, as described above. The mixture was gently swirled to mix and poured evenly over five 150 mm petri plates (Fisher Scientific, Pittsburg, PA). Plates were allowed to solidify prior to incubation at 37 °C for 16–18 h. Positive (30–40 PFU virus spike) and negative controls (sterile TSB) were performed alongside samples for each experiment to ensure the assay performance was acceptable and no contamination was present during the study. Viral plaques were enumerated, and data were adjusted and expressed as PFU/L.

Simulated wash water

Simulated wash water was composed mirroring components used in similar studies, strategies, or materials (Gallardo et al., 2019; Krauter et al., 2011; Buttner et al., 2004; Calfee et al., 2013). Wash water solution consisted of a tap water base (dechlorinated with 0.1 g/L sodium thiosulfate) containing a mild surfactant solution (0.05% Tween 80). Sediment was added to the wash water to represent particulate matter that would be washed off an environmental surface during decontamination. Sediment used for experimentation was collected from a local riverbank and allowed to dry uncovered for 2 days before autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min to sterilize it. Sterility of sediment was assessed by adding 2 g of heat sterilized sediment to 2 L of sterile wash water, mixing for 3 min and analyzing replicate 100 mL samples (n = 4) using the SAL assay. No evidence of contamination was observed during sediment sterility analysis. During experimentation, sterilized sediment was added to samples at a ratio of 2 g/L, which created a sample turbidity of approximately 50 NTU, which was measured using a YSI ProDSS digital water quality meter (YSI, Yellow Springs, OH).

Concentration method

Dead-end hollow-fiber ultrafiltration (D-HFUF) was used for all virus concentration experimentation following previously published protocols (McMinn et al., 2017; EPA, 2018a). Briefly, 2 L of wash water were filtered through a single use 15 S Asahi Kasei Rexeed ultrafilter (Dial Medical Supply, Chester Springs, PA) using a peristaltic pump powered by an onsite electrical outlet. Following sample filtration, filters were eluted using 200 mL of an elution solution (0.01% Tween 80, 0.01% sodium hexametaphosphate, 0.001% Antifoam Y-30) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). The resulting eluates were divided evenly and analyzed for somatic and F+coliphage by the SAL method described above.

Three experimental designs (Fig. 1) were tested. For Experiment 1, wash water was filtered by D-HFUF without addition of sediment. For Experiment 2, wash water was amended with sediment, mixed, and immediately processed through D-HFUF before settling could occur. By contrast, for Experiment 3, wash water was amended with sediment, mixed, and sediment was allowed to gravity settle prior to D-HFUF concentration. For all experiments wash water was spiked with coliphage, sediment was added (where required), and solution was mixed for three minutes prior to experimentation. Wash water with sediment (no gravity settling) was filtered immediately after the 3-minute mix, while wash water with particle gravity settling sat undisturbed for 10 min prior to filtration. Residual sediment particles not filtered, were collected, and processed separately through the sediment extraction method described below.

Sediment extraction protocol optimization

To aid virus detachment from sediment particles and stabilize viral particles present, various viral suspension solutions were evaluated for use in sediment extraction procedures. Since viruses can be sensitive to solutions containing surfactants, coliphage stability was assessed in three different sediment elution solutions (1) D-HFUF elution solution, (2) sediment elution solution (SED) (0.06 % sodium hexametaphosphate), and (3) 3 % TSB media. For each solution (in triplicate), coliphage were spiked into the solution (100 mL), incubated at room temperature for 1 h, and was then processed using SAL (Table 1) to assess viral sensitivities.

Physical disruptive forces are effective in dislodging attached microorganisms from sediment; however, these forces can influence the viability of viruses present (Maranger and Bird, 1996a,b; Slava and Epstein, 1995; Danovaro et al., 1994). To determine the sensitivities of coliphage to these commonly used agitative forces (vortex and sonication), a series of experiments were conducted to determine coliphage inactivation rates. For this testing, known concentrations of both somatic and F+ coliphage were seeded into measured volumes of 3 % TSB and subjected to either sonication (Qsonica Sonicator Q125, Fisher Scientific) set at an amplitude of 20 % for 30 s intervals (repeated 6 times) or were subjected to vortex at max setting (for 15 second intervals repeated 6 times). Coliphage were quantified from sample replicates (n = 3) for both the sonication and vortex treatments using SAL separately.

Optimized sediment extraction protocol

Residual, gravity settled sediment collected during experimentation (Fig. 1, experiment 3) was resuspended in a TSB solution and vortexed for 15 ss, and then subjected to sonication for 30 s while samples were incubated on ice, this entire process was repeated for a total of 6 times following procedures detailed in Danovaro et al. (2001). Next, samples were centrifuged (3600 rpm at 23 °C) for 10 min to pellet suspended particulates. Liquid supernatant was collected and analyzed by SAL for the presence of somatic and F+ coliphage.

Method deployment

To simulate a contamination clean-up event of a permeable surface, a heavily trafficked office carpet was used as an experimental substrate to which the optimal concentration method and sediment extraction protocol was applied. Wash water was used to fill the clean water solution tank of a Hoover Floormate 3-in-1 (Model # FH40010B) (Hoover Inc., Charlotte, NC) wet/dry vacuum. Using the gentle wash setting (following manufacturer’s instructions), wash water was automatically applied and mechanically scrubbed into the floor and then suctioned from the carpet. Used wash water was collected within the vacuum’s water collection tank (pre-sterilized using 10 % bleach soak and with sodium thiosulfate dechlorination) and was transferred into a sterile collection carboy for laboratory analysis.

Used wash water was aliquoted into 2 L volumes and spiked with both somatic and F+ coliphage and mixed for 3 min. Due to high particulate matter collected during sample collection (ranging from 330 to 354 NTU), spiked carpet wash water samples were allowed to sit for 10 min to promote sediment settling prior to D-HFUF filter concentration. Liquid portions of carpet wash samples (2 L) were then filtered through the D-HFUF, and the resulting sediments (gravity settled prior to filtration) were collected processed using the optimized sediment extraction protocol and assayed by SAL as previously described.

To simulate an impervious surface clean-up event, the wet/dry vacuum was deployed as described above on an impervious laboratory floor. Due to low particulate matter collected (20–35 NTU) during wet/dry vacuum cleaning, it was not necessary to perform the sediment extraction protocol to the used wash water collected from this surface. Instead, used wash water was collected, spiked with coliphage, mixed for 3 min, and immediately processed by D-HFUF. Filter eluates were then directly assayed by SAL.

Prior to each sampling event, experiments were conducted to determine if endogenous levels of coliphage were present in either the permeable or non-permeable surfaces tested. Following procedures listed above, aliquots of 100 mL (n = 3) of the 2 L of used (non-virus spiked) wash water collected as described above for each surface type (permeable and non-permeable) and were analyzed to determine if background coliphage levels were present. During this analysis, no evidence of endogenous coliphage were observed.

Data analysis

Percent of coliphage recovery (PR) was calculated using the following formula: PR=C/SC × 100 where C = coliphage concentrations in sample or filter eluate determined by SAL, and S = initial spiked coliphage concentrations added to experimental matrices. Percent recovery data was arcsine square root transformed prior to statistical analyses. GraphPad Prism software (version 8.1.2 for Windows, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) was used for all statistical analyses. Two-tailed paired and unpaired t-tests, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparison test were used to assess the effectiveness of method(s) to recover somatic and F+ coliphage from variety of test matrices (α = 0.05 for all tests).

Results

Baseline D-HFUF coliphage recovery in unamended wash water

Performance of the D-HFUF filtration procedure was evaluated to generate baseline data for recovery of somatic and F+ coliphages from simulated, unamended wash water (Fig. 1-Experiment 1). Under these conditions, coliphages were spiked into 2 L volumes of wash water without addition of sediment and filtered using D-HFUF. During this experimentation, recoveries of F+ coliphage averaged 57 ± 12 % and somatic at 55 ± 27 % (Fig. 2). During this experimentation, the baseline method was equally effective in recovery of both somatic and F+ coliphage, as no statistically significant differences in recoveries between coliphage types were observed.

Impact of sediment on recovery of coliphage in wash water using D-HFUF

Sediment (2 g/L) was added to coliphage spiked wash water samples, mixed and concentrated using D-HFUF without gravity settling. During this experiment (Fig. 1-Experiment 2), lower levels of both F+ and somatic coliphage were recovered (20 ± 5 % and 27 ± 13 %, respectively) (Fig. 2) using the D-HFUF method, compared to baseline measurements (wash water containing no sediment) of 57 ± 12 and 55 ± 27 %, respectively (Fig. 1-Experiment 1). Further analysis revealed that presence of sediment significantly reduced the recovery of both somatic (P = 0.0203) and F+ coliphage (P = <0.0001) compared to the baseline method.

Recovery of coliphage from wash water using sediment separation

Sediment significantly impacted the efficacy of the D-HFUF-SAL to concentrate and detect coliphages from used wash water, to counter this effect, a sediment separation and processing step was initiated (Fig. 1-Experiment 3). The separation of sediment (by gravity for 10 min) allowed for increases in recoveries of both F+ (28 ± 12 %) and somatic coliphages (35 ± 8 %) (Fig. 2) compared to experimentation without sediment separation (20 ± 5 % and 27 ± 13 %, respectively). This difference was significant for F+ (P = <0.0001), but not for somatic coliphage.

Sediment elution solution optimization

Steps of the sediment virus elution protocol were optimized to enhance recovery of coliphage attached to particulate matter in wash water samples. Sediment elution relies on collecting particulates via gravity settling, resuspending sediment in a virus elution solution, and then subjecting the particulates to various physical disruptive forces to release attached viruses. To ensure optimal coliphage recovery, various steps of the sediment extraction procedure were modified to recover the highest levels of seeded coliphage, these included different viral elution solutions, and various physical disruptive forces. For these experiments, coliphage were seeding into unused wash water (without sediment), where each physical disruptive force was separately applied to series of experimental replicates. During these experiments, F+ coliphages were recovered at an average of 90 ± 4% for vortexed samples, while for samples exposed to sonication, an average of 100 ± 12 % of seeded F+ were recovered (Table 2). For somatic coliphage, an average of 31 ± 5% of seeded virus was recovered when samples were vortexed, compared to 31 ± 3 % for samples undergoing sonication. When looking at each individual coliphage, neither physical disruptive force significantly outperformed the other for somatic (P=0.8135) or F+ (P=0.6424), though somatic coliphage were more sensitive to both physical disruptive forces evaluated (P value range: 0.0154–0.0002). Due to these observations, both sonication and vortex were incorporated as part of the optimal method and used in all subsequent experimentation.

Further optimization was conducted to evaluate the effects of three different sediment elution solutions, where coliphage spikes were added directly to either a sediment elution solution (SED), D-HFUF elution solution or to TSB media (Table 1). For the sediment elution solution (SED), 88 ± 2 % of F+ and 25 ± 2 % of somatic coliphage were enumerated from the original spike titer, while for the elution solution (D-HFUF), 77 ± 10 % of F+ coliphage and 25 ± 7 % of somatic coliphage were recovered. TSB media used as a viral suspension was observed to outperform all other solutions tested for both coliphage types, with 92 ± 9 % of seeded F+ coliphage recovered and 108 ± 9 % of seeded somatic coliphage. Both SED and the HFUF solutions were found to significantly impact (P = <0.0001) the recovery of somatic coliphage compared to TSB. For F+ coliphage, use of TSB media significantly outperformed (P = 0.010) the D-HFUF solution but was not significantly different to the SED solution.

Method deployment

The D-HFUF concentration system combined with the optimized sediment extraction method (TSB with vortex and sonication) were deployed on two surfaces, carpet (permeable) and a laboratory impervious floor (non-permeable). Since high levels of particulate matter were present in used wash water collected from the carpet (NTU 330–354), sediment gravity separation was required prior to D-HFUF filtration, while the settled sediment particles were processed through sediment extraction procedures. Lab floor wash water samples contained negligible levels of particulates (NTU 34–36), so these samples only utilized D-HFUF concentration, not requiring a separate sediment processing step.

For permeable surface experimentation, the D-HFUF method recovered 75 ± 32 % of F+ coliphage and 96 ± 19 % of somatic coliphage from liquid portions of the sample, while the sediment extraction method recovered an additional 9 ± 10 % of seeded F+ coliphage and 8 ± 4 % of somatic coliphage present (Fig. 3). For the non-permeable laboratory floor, the D-HFUF-SAL method recovered 92 ± 4 % of spiked F+ coliphage and 101 ± 9 % of somatic coliphage. There was no statistical difference in recovery of coliphages using D-HFUF-SAL method between the permeable and non-permeable surfaces, despite the 10-fold difference in turbidity observed between the two sample types.

Discussion

A challenge in working with used wash waters is that they may contain various amounts of particulate matter which can interfere while samples are being filter concentrated. Viruses can become bound to sediment particles present in environmental samples, justifying the need for effective virus recovery methods for both liquid and particulate portions of samples (Rodriguez et al., 2009; Filippini and Middelboe, 2007; Whitman et al., 2009). While bacterial contaminant assessments have been successfully conducted in other wash water use scenarios (i.e., vegetable washes, biocontamination clean-up) (Gallardo et al., 2019; Farkas et al., 2018; Magana et al., 2013), effective methodological procedures to assess viral presence are still needed. Since the D-HFUF-SAL system has been extensively evaluated for concentrating viruses from large volumes of environmental waters and wastewaters (McMinn et al., 2017; EPA, 2018a,b; McMinn et al., 2023), our research focused on adapting this methodology, along with a separate procedure for recovering particle attached viruses (McMinn et al., 2017; Morales-Morales et al., 2003; Olszewski et al., 2005). A sediment extraction protocol was developed to collect and physically detach seeded viruses from particulates present in wash water samples (Kearns et al., 2008). Since viruses can be sensitive to certain experimental conditions during recovery procedures (pH, physical forces, elution solution constituents, etc), both the efficacy of the elution solution and the effects of physical disruptive forces were evaluated (Pinto et al., 2010; Su et al., 2010).

Solutions typically used for extracting viruses from surfaces or particulates typically fall under two classes of solutions; media (e.g., RPMI, beef extract, or tryptose based broths) or saline solutions (e.g., ringers, phosphate buffered saline, or saline). According to the Centers for Disease Control, solutions promoting viral stabilization should be neutral in pH, contain antimicrobial/antifungal agents, buffering agents (amino acids), and a source of protein (Prevention, 2020). During our evaluation, the TSB elution solution statistically outperformed both the D-HFUF and SED elution solutions for coliphage recovery from sample particulates. Since TSB media has a neutral pH and source of amino acids, and proteins, our results support CDC recommendations for a viral stabilizing solution with those types of characteristics.

To aid in detaching viruses from sample particulates, various physical disruptive forces were applied. Little is known about the effect of these forces on infectious virus stability, so both sonication and vortex were evaluated (Jones et al., 2009; Rekrut et al., 1985; Arens et al., 1988). There was no significant reduction in culturable coliphage between these two treatments, so both were used for the optimized viral sediment extraction protocol. During experimentation using the sediment extraction protocol, coliphage recoveries from sediment reached as high as 30% of total virus spike. The physical disruptive forces deployed during experimentation visibly broke-up large particles present, suggesting that viruses attached to these particles would be released at higher efficiencies compared to approaches lacking these treatments (Rekrut et al., 1985; Arens et al., 1988). Overall, sediment separation and the sediment extraction protocol enhanced the ability to use the D-HFUF for processing larger volumes of used wash water, while reducing sample turbidity, allowing better visualization and quantification of viral plaques during the SAL analysis. Virus signal detected in sediment could supplement viral signal recovered from liquid portions of samples, resulting in improved method sensitivity, thus increasing our ability to detect low level viral targets present in challenging wash water matrices (Filippini and Middelboe, 2007; Whitman et al., 2009; Danovaro et al., 2001).

During the simulated decontamination clean-up event, seeded coliphage were recovered at rates ranging from 75 % to 101 % despite varying levels of particulate matter between the surfaces tested (permeable and non-permeable). Comparing our results to those in literature is complicated due to a vast majority of studies report only presence/absence data (Julian et al., 2011). Those reporting quantitative viral data, recovered 13–76 % of seeded virus from non-permeable surfaces, while 6–53 % were recovered from permeable surfaces (Julian et al., 2011; Yeargin et al., 2015; K. Scherer et al., 2009; Carducci et al., 2002). Differences in viral recoveries observed in our study with those in literature could be due to differences in methodological approaches, specifically, most studies have focused on recovering viruses seeded onto small coupons (ranging from 5 to 9 cm2) of stainless steel, PVC plastic, or textile surfaces using pre-treated cotton swabs. While recovery of MS2 (culturable phage and RNA signal) was not significantly impacted by the type of eluant (media or saline solution) used in the Julian et al. (2011) and, Carducci et al. (2002), other studies found using media-based solutions (beef extract) were optimal for recovery of Hepatitis C virus from surfaces (Julian et al., 2011; Carducci et al., 2002). The findings of these studies suggest that approaches using media-based solutions yield quantifiable levels of both culture-based and molecular viral signals (PCR and qPCR), an important criterium for broad application of any viral concentration technique. While our study focused on developing methodologies to recover and maintain the infectivity status of viral seeds, it is assumed that our approach would be applicable to molecular-based assays, as it shares many of procedural steps successfully used for concentration and detection of viral nucleic acids in complex environmental systems (McMinn et al., 2023, 2016; Korajkic et al., 2022). The conclusions of our study indicate a significant advantage of using media as opposed to saline solutions for coliphage recovery under our experimental conditions. It is important to note that our approach was unique in that the experimental substrates (permeable and non-permeable surfaces) were used to generate the medium (used wash water) used for virus seeding virus and concentration. While our approach was artificial (mimicking a biocontaminant cleanup event), it highlights the successful application of a proven virus concentration methodology (D-HFUF) when paired with a substrate collection and wash procedure for infectious viral recovery from large volumes of liquids containing particulates (McMinn et al., 2017; Kahler et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2012; McMinn et al., 2023). Though samples were challenged with 10-fold higher sample turbidity (permeable surface), the use of D-HFUF-SAL was not significantly impaired when a sediment extraction procedure was implemented compared to samples (non-permeable surface) with lower turbidity readings. This data highlights the robustness of the D-HFUF-SAL method for dealing with challenging matrices, and paired with the sediment extraction procedure, this method further enhances our ability to detect viruses attached to particulate matter within these matrices.

This study demonstrates that the D-HFUF-SAL and sediment extraction protocol are practical options for recovering infectious virus surrogates from wash water samples. The concentration method and the sediment extraction protocol can be combined or used independently for viral recovery depending on the sample particulate matter load present. This study provides valuable information applicable to the establishment of effective methods to assess contaminated wash water for the presence of pathogenic viruses and characterizing the presence of viral pathogens in contaminated wash water. Additionally, effective wash water processing techniques will be vital not only in viral agent identification, but in determining the extent of contamination, evaluation of ongoing decontamination efforts, as well as meeting site remediation clearance to ensure proper handling and disposal practices are deployed to safeguard human health (United States Environmental Protection Agency, 2022).
