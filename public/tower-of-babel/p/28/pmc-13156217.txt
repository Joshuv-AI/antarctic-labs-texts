Cycling to TNFi vs. Switching to IL-17Ai Among Patients with Psoriatic Arthritis and Axial Spondyloarthritis: Real-World CorEvitas PsA/SpA Registry Data
Authors: Alexis Ogdie, Nicole Middaugh, Maya Marchese, Jessica A Walsh, Natalia Bello, Marcus Ngantcha, Andris Kronbergs, Elsie L Grace, Philip J Mease
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTumor necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFis) are a common first-line treatment option for patients with psoriatic arthritis (PsA) or axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) with persistent disease activity despite conventional treatment. There is limited real-world evidence available to inform selection of therapy after first TNFi failure.MethodsThis study compares the effectiveness of cycling to a second TNFi versus switching to an interleukin-17A inhibitor (IL-17Ai) at 6-month follow-up, among patients with PsA and axSpA who discontinued a first TNFi. Patients were stratified into two groups: those who (1) initiated a second TNFi (cyclers) and (2) initiated an IL-17Ai (switchers). Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was used to account for baseline differences between cyclers and switchers. Separate models were fit for the PsA and axSpA cohorts.ResultsThe PsA cohort included 277 (59%) cyclers and 194 (41%) switchers. Prior to IPTW, switchers demonstrated greater improvement (− 4.1 [95% confidence interval (CI) − 5.6, − 2.5]) in the primary outcome of change from baseline (CFB) in the Clinical Disease Activity Index for Psoriatic Arthritis (cDAPSA) versus cyclers. Post IPTW, switchers showed greater improvements across most secondary outcomes, and significantly greater improvements in Physician Global Assessment (PhGA) of arthritis and psoriasis (− 5.0 [95% CI − 9.3, − 0.8], p = 0.021) and non-work activity impairment (− 4.7 [95% CI − 9.2, − 0.2], p = 0.039).The axSpA cohort included 119 (69%) cyclers and 53 (31%) switchers. Prior to IPTW, switchers demonstrated greater improvement (− 0.7 [95% CI − 1.2, − 0.2]) in the primary outcome of CFB in Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI) versus cyclers. Post IPTW, switchers showed significantly greater improvement in patient fatigue (− 9.4 [95% CI − 15.8, − 3.1], p = 0.004) and numerically greater improvements across remaining secondary outcomes.ConclusionSwitching to IL-17Ai vs. cycling to another TNFi provided similar or numerically improved outcomes in both cohorts, underscoring the importance of considering alternative mechanisms of action for these patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03531-5.Keywords: Psoriatic arthritis, Axial spondyloarthritis, TNFi, IL-17Ai, Effectiveness, Real-world evidence

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Treatment guidelines recommend both tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFis) and interleukin-17 inhibitors (IL-17is) as options for patients with psoriatic arthritis (PsA) or axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA).TNFis are a common first-line treatment; however, there is little evidence available to inform selection of therapy after failure with TNFi treatment.The aim of this study is to compare the effectiveness of a second TNFi (cycling) versus switching to an IL-17Ai among patients with PsA and axSpA who discontinued a first TNFi.What was learned from this study?The findings from this real-world analysis suggest that switching from a first TNFi to an IL-17Ai results in similar or numerically improved outcomes compared to cycling to a subsequent TNFi in both PsA and axSpA cohorts.These results underscore the importance of considering alternative efficacious mechanisms of action such as IL-17Ais as suitable options for patients with PsA or axSpA discontinuing a first TNFi therapy.

Introduction

Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) and axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) are chronic inflammatory diseases. PsA is an inflammatory arthritis associated with cutaneous psoriasis and involves musculoskeletal manifestations, including dactylitis and enthesitis [1]. Patients with PsA may also have axial involvement (termed axial PsA). AxSpA primarily affects the axial spine and sacroiliac joints [2]. PsA (and axSpA in some studies) is associated with cardiovascular and metabolic comorbidities and both conditions are associated with depression and anxiety [3–6]. PsA and axSpA are associated with significant pain and disability and have a considerable impact on quality of life [7]. With persistent disease activity despite conventional treatment, the first choice of biologic therapy for PsA and axSpA is often a tumor necrosis factor inhibitor (TNFi) based on availability [8, 9]. However, a proportion of patients either do not adequately respond or experience a loss of response or adverse reaction necessitating treatment modifications or alternative treatment strategies with the aim to reach the target of remission or low disease activity [10]. Patients who experience inadequate response despite TNFi therapy can cycle to either a different TNFi or switch to a biologic agent with an alternative mechanism of action (MOA) [9, 11].

Clinical trials have shown interleukin-17A inhibitors (IL-17Ais) to be efficacious in patients with PsA and axSpA who were naïve to treatment with biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (bDMARDs) [12, 13] and who had an inadequate response to a TNFi [14–16]. Ixekizumab has demonstrated greater efficacy than adalimumab in improving skin outcomes in patients with PsA [17]. In a randomized head-to-head comparison of patients with PsA, ixekizumab was also superior to adalimumab in achieving the simultaneous improvement of joint and skin disease [18]. Other studies comparing PsA treatments to adalimumab had mixed results [19]. However, the evidence on the effectiveness of switching to an IL-17i versus cycling to a second TNFi, after discontinuation of a first TNFi, among patients with PsA and axSpA is limited. A 2017 systematic literature review of both observational and interventional studies found that among patients with axSpA who received a TNFi as first bDMARD, 97% cycled to TNFi and 3% switched to IL-17i [20]. One observational study of patients with axSpA suggested that after prior withdrawal from a TNFi, switching to an IL-17Ai is comparably effective to cycling to another TNFi [21]. However, comparative data is scarce and there is a pressing need to better understand and provide evidence on the efficacy in cycling vs. switching mechanism of action in treating patients with PsA and axSpA. The aim of this study is to compare the effectiveness of a second TNFi (cycling) versus switching to an IL-17Ai among patients with PsA and among patients with axSpA who discontinued a first TNFi. We hypothesize that switching from a TNFi to an IL-17i provides improved therapeutic response compared to cycling to a subsequent TNFi in patients with PsA and axSpA.

Methods

Study Design

This study is a secondary analysis of data from a longitudinal observational cohort study of patients with PsA and axSpA enrolled in the CorEvitas PsA/SpA Registry from May 2013 to January 2024. The CorEvitas PsA/SpA Registry is a prospective, multicenter, non-interventional registry, launched in March 2013, for patients with PsA, ankylosing spondylitis, and radiographic and non-radiographic axSpA. Patients included in this study were diagnosed with PsA or axSpA and initiated a second TNFi or IL-17Ai at or after CorEvitas enrollment following discontinuation of a first TNFi. Diagnosis was determined by the treating rheumatologist based on their clinical judgement. Patients with concomitant axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) diagnosed by the treating healthcare provider were excluded from the PsA cohort. This exclusion applied to axSpA specifically, not axial PsA. Patients with PsA could self-report spinal pain VAS and nocturnal pain. Patients were enrolled in the registry at any point following their diagnosis. As the registry is observational, there is no protocol-defined treatment regimen and medication discontinuation may occur for any reason. Approved TNFi included adalimumab, etanercept, certolizumab pegol, golimumab, infliximab, and TNF biosimilars (infliximab-dyyb, etanercept-szzs and adalimumab-atto). In this study, by the time of the analysis, approved IL-17i in the USA included ixekizumab and secukinumab, both of which are IL-17Ais.

The index date was defined as the date on which an approved TNFi or IL-17Ai was initiated as second therapy following discontinuation of a first TNFi. The baseline period was defined as the period 4 months prior to the index date up to 1 month after the index date and was used to define the baseline variables. Study design is shown in Fig. S1, and inclusion/exclusion criteria are shown in Table S1 in the supplementary material. The baseline characteristics and variables included in the propensity scores (PS) were those collected at the study index visit, defined as the registry visit at which initiation of a second TNFi (cycle) or an IL-17i (switch) occurred.

Outcomes

At treatment index date, sociodemographics, history of comorbidities, PsA/axSpA disease characteristics, disease activity measures, patient-reported measures, and treatment characteristics were reported. A list of these characteristics and outcomes are detailed in supplementary material (Table S2). Separate analyses were performed for each indication. The primary outcome for the PsA cohort was change from baseline (CFB) to 6 months follow-up in Clinical Disease Activity Index for Psoriatic Arthritis (cDAPSA) and for the axSpA cohort was CFB to 6 months follow-up in Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI) as outlined in Table S3. Secondary outcomes for the PsA cohort included achievement of cDAPSA low disease activity (LDA), remission, achievement of minimal disease activity (MDA), achievement of very low disease activity (VLDA), and achievement of body surface area (BSA) < 3% in patients with BSA ≥ 3% at baseline. A full list of PsA secondary outcomes is shown in supplementary material (Table S4). Secondary outcomes for the axSpA cohort included achievement of BASDAI50, achievement of Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Score with C-reactive Protein (ASDAS-CRP) LDA and inactive disease (ID), and achievement of Assessment of Spondyloarthritis international Society (ASAS) 20/40. A full list of axSpA secondary outcomes is shown in supplementary material (Table S4). All achievement outcomes were conducted among the subset of patients not already in the threshold state at index; for example, patients in cDAPSA remission at baseline were excluded from remission achievement analysis (Table 1). All secondary variable definitions and measurements are outlined in supplementary material (Table S4). All outcomes were evaluated 6 months (3–9 months following the index date, the registry visit closest to 6 months) after initiating the second therapy (index date).

Sensitivity Analysis

For both PsA and axSpA cohorts, two sensitivity analyses were conducted. The first sensitivity analysis restricted the study populations to patients who initiated therapy less than 6 months after discontinuation of the first TNFi to evaluate the effect of cycle vs. switching within a subgroup of patients with similar timing of medication changes. Patients who have a gap in treatment longer than 6 months may have different outcomes than patients who cycle or switch shortly after discontinuation of the first TNFi. This is important as the timing of medication change might be associated, directly or indirectly, with effectiveness endpoints. The second sensitivity analysis restricted the study population to line of therapy, including patients who discontinued a first-line TNFi, excluding patients with a history of a non-TNFi biologic. This sensitivity analysis was deemed important as second-line TNFi or IL-17Ai after first-line TNFi use may represent a different patient population with different responses to medication changes versus patients on later lines of therapy.

Statistical Analysis

Separately for PsA and axSpA cohorts, characteristics at baseline were summarized using means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Differences in baseline characteristics between cyclers and switchers were evaluated with standardized differences (d; d > |0.1| indicating a potentially meaningful imbalance) as recommended in the literature [22]. Propensity scores for the predicted probability of initiating an IL-17Ai (switcher) vs. a second TNFi (cycler) were computed for each patient using a logistic regression model with treatment (switcher vs. cycler) as the dependent (outcome) variable and a set of covariates as independent variables. PS variables included sociodemographics, disease characteristics, clinical assessments, patient-reported outcomes, comorbidities, and treatment history (detailed in Supplementary Figs. S5–S10). Despite comprehensive adjustment, some covariates remained imbalanced (PsA: MDA, IBD, uveitis; axSpA: concomitant therapy, depression, insurance). These were included in outcome models. After the propensity scores were generated, they were used to derive inverse probability of treatment weights (IPTW) to create groups balanced with respect to baseline characteristics. IPTW methodology is an accepted strategy to mimic randomization in observational research [23, 24].

Regression models—linear for continuous outcomes and Poisson for binary outcomes—were fitted to each primary and secondary outcome to estimate the association between cycling/switching status and outcome at the 6-month follow-up registry visit using the IPT-weighted population. Regression coefficients were estimated from the linear models and relative risks were estimated from Poisson models with a robust error variance. P value for a difference in the outcome between groups was reported for the primary outcome and 95% CIs for point estimates were reported for all secondary outcomes. Analysis was conducted using STATA Version 16 (StataCorp, LLC, College Station, TX) and R Version 4.0.3 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna Austria).

Missing patient characteristic data at treatment baseline date or the follow-up registry visit was relatively small; thus, no methods to address missing data were planned. The frequency and percentage of patients with missing information for study variables was reported in all baseline tables. No multiple testing adjustment was applied, as this is exploratory research with pre-specified hypotheses. P values indicate evidence strength rather than definitive tests; emphasis should be on effect magnitude and confidence intervals.

Results

Overall, 471 patients were eligible for the PsA analysis and 172 patients were eligible for the axSpA analysis. There were 277 (59%) cyclers and 194 (41%) switchers in the PsA cohort and 119 (69%) cyclers and 53 (31%) switchers in the axSpA cohort as shown in Fig. 1 and Table 1. Results presented are stratified by the cycler and switcher groups within each disease cohort. TNFis were the most frequently initiated first-line biologic therapies among both cyclers and switchers, consistent with the study inclusion criteria. Detailed baseline characteristics for both cohorts are presented in Table 1. Within each disease cohort, cyclers and switchers had consistently similar disease characteristics at baseline. Although PS models cannot adjust for all baseline covariates (Supplementary material, Figs. S5 and S6), some clinically meaningful differences are discussed below.

For the PsA cohort, switchers were, on average, younger than cyclers (cyclers (mean) 55.8 years, switchers (mean) 53.8 years), less likely to be female (cyclers 59.1%, switchers 52.6%), and less likely to be White (cyclers 91.0%, switchers 87.6%). Other demographics were similar among switchers and cyclers, and the total number of comorbidities did not differ meaningfully between the groups (Table 1). However, anxiety and fibromyalgia were more prevalent among switchers, whereas more cyclers had a history of malignancies, uveitis, and IBD. Switchers displayed higher disease activity than cyclers, including higher mean Physician Global Assessment (PhGA) of arthritis and psoriasis Visual Analogue Scale (VAS; cyclers 31.0, switchers 35.4) and mean cDAPSA (cyclers 17.7, switchers 19.5), with most of the patients in the moderate-to-high cDAPSA category. A greater percentage of cyclers had clear skin as opposed to switchers (IGA [0–4], 35.4% vs. 24.6%, respectively). Switchers showed a higher disease burden relative to cyclers across several patient-reported outcomes (PROs), including mean Patient Global Assessment (PtGA) VAS (cyclers 41.9, switchers 45.7), PtGA of arthritis and psoriasis VAS (cyclers 41.5, switchers 46.0), patient pain VAS (cyclers 47.6, switchers 51.1), patient spine pain VAS (cyclers 32.9, switchers 36.5), and morning stiffness VAS (cyclers 46.5, switchers 50.3).

For the axSpA cohort, the mean age at index was 51.4 and 50.2 years for cyclers and switchers, respectively. Switchers were mostly female (cyclers 52.1%, switchers 61.5%), nonsmokers (cyclers, 58.8%, switchers 66.0%), less likely to be Hispanic (cyclers 6.0%, switchers 2.1%), and less likely to have a high number of comorbidities (2+) (cyclers 43.7%, switchers 35.8%) (Table 1). Fibromyalgia and IBD were more prevalent among switchers, whereas most other comorbidities had a higher occurrence among the cyclers. Disease characteristics were similar between groups, except for mean duration of axSpA disease (cyclers 8.6 years, switchers 5.5 years). Switchers had higher disease activity at baseline, demonstrated with a mean BASDAI (cyclers 4.9, switchers 6.0), ASDAS-CRP (cyclers 2.7, switchers 3.2), and predominantly distributed by high and very high disease activity (cyclers 47.1 and 23.0; switchers 44.7 and 39.5, respectively) rather than inactive disease (cyclers 11.5, switchers 0.0). Switchers showed a higher disease burden relative to cyclers across several patient-reported outcomes (PROs), including higher mean PhGA VAS (cyclers 32.8, switchers 39.8), Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (BASFI; cyclers 4.1, switchers 4.6), and BSA (cyclers 0.1, switchers 5.6). Switchers showed a higher disease burden compared to cyclers across several PROs, including mean PtGA VAS (cyclers 50.6, switchers 58.7), patient pain VAS (cyclers 52.9, switchers 68.5), patient spine pain VAS (cyclers 47.2, switchers 64.3), nocturnal spine pain VAS (cyclers 45.3, switchers 57.7), patient fatigue VAS (cyclers 50.1, switchers 59.3), and morning stiffness VAS (cyclers 53.0, switchers 62.4).

A significant proportion of the overall patient population (> 80%) had a high BMI (> 25.0), and obesity was among the most prevalent comorbidities. In both cohorts, switchers more frequently used ≥ 2 conventional DMARDs previously and initiated treatment as monotherapy, whereas prior non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) use was higher among the cyclers (Table 1). Regarding treatment patterns for PsA initiators, cyclers (by definition) received a second TNFi as the subsequent therapy, of which 120 (43%) discontinued; switchers received an IL-17Ai as their next therapy, of which 68 (35%) discontinued, prior to or at 6-month follow-up, with approximately two-thirds citing efficacy reasons (Table 2). Among the axSpA initiators, 35 (29%) cyclers and 22 (42%) switchers discontinued their second TNFi/IL-17Ai therapy prior to or at the 6-month follow-up, with up to half discontinuing due to other reasons (Table 2).

Sensitivity Analysis

The first sensitivity analysis (restricted to patients who initiated second-line therapy less than 6 months after first TNFi discontinuation) included 349 patients in the PsA cohort (cyclers, n = 208; switchers, n = 141) and 136 patients in the axSpA cohort (cyclers, n = 97; switchers, n = 39). The second sensitivity analysis (restricted to patients with no prior non-TNFi biologic use before first-line TNFi discontinuation) included 388 patients in the PsA cohort (cyclers, n = 231; switchers, n = 157) and 161 patients in the axSpA cohort (cyclers, n = 111; switchers, n = 50). Results from both sensitivity analyses (PsA: Tables S9 and S11; axSpA: Tables S10 and S12) showed similar trends as in the primary analyses. However, some variables remained out of balance after IPTW in the PsA and axSpA cohorts in the sensitivity analyses (Supplementary material: Figs. S7–S10). To account for these residual imbalances, outcome models in PsA were adjusted for smoking status and overall work impairment in the first sensitivity analysis and depression in the second sensitivity analysis, as these were the variables demonstrating the largest imbalance. Similarly, outcome models in axSpA were adjusted for concomitant therapy in the first sensitivity analysis and patient pain in the second sensitivity analysis. Although this analysis ensured more comparable treatment patterns between groups, the reduced sample size limited statistical power.

For PsA cohort, a greater mean improvement was estimated in cDAPSA for switchers compared to cyclers (IPT-weighted estimate = − 2.0; 95% CI − 4.8, 0.8, p = 0.153) in the first sensitivity analysis (Supplementary material: Table S9). For PsA second sensitivity analysis (first-line TNFi only), IPT-weighted CFB in cDAPSA was 0.0 (95% CI − 2.1, 2.0, p = 0.977). No significant differences were observed across most secondary outcomes for both groups of patients (Supplementary material: Table S11). In the axSpA cohort, switchers had a 3.3 times higher probability to achieve BASDAI50 (IPT-weighted RR = 3.3; 95% CI 1.3, 8.6, p = 0.013) as compared to cyclers (Supplementary material: Table S12), and significantly greater improvement in patient fatigue (IPT-weighted estimate = − 10.3; p = 0.003).

To summarize overall results (Tables S5, S6, S11, and S12), switchers showed numerically greater improvements across most outcomes, with statistically significant differences for PhGA of arthritis and psoriasis (PsA), non-work activity impairment (PsA), patient fatigue (axSpA), and BASDAI50 achievement (axSpA).

Discussion

Treatment guidelines recommend TNFis and IL-17Ais as options for patients with PsA or axSpA with persistent disease activity despite conventional treatment [8, 9, 23–25]. TNFis are commonly used first-line treatment in PsA and axSpA [26, 27]. There is little real-world evidence available to provide support for switching to a different drug class vs. cycling to the same drug class, thereby informing selection of therapy after TNFi treatment failure [9]. For patients with axSpA, the Assessment of Spondyloarthritis International Society (ASAS) and the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) have recommended a change in mechanism of action after failure of the first TNFi, IL-17i, or Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitor [23]. Similar recommendations have been established for patients with PsA by EULAR and the Group for Research and Assessment of Psoriasis and Psoriatic Arthritis (GRAPPA) [8, 9]. The growing number of drugs with diverse mechanisms of action leads to questions about how to pick the best treatment for the individual patient in the real-world and a resulting evolution in treatment strategies.

Cycling within the same drug class, particularly TNFis, has been reported to provide limited benefits to patients with PsA and axSpA in several studies that have highlighted the need for treatments with other mechanisms of action than TNF inhibition [27–33]. Cycling preference may reflect historical prescribing patterns, insurance requirements for sequential trials within drug classes, and prescriber familiarity with TNFis. The switching rates observed in our study (41% PsA, 31% axSpA) suggest a shift in clinical practice toward switching to newer biologic classes after TNFi failure. No randomized clinical trial has been published with TNFi in patients failing a first-line TNFi [34]. Observational data suggest that a second-line TNFi can be efficacious in patients, although the level of efficacy may be lower than that with the first TNFi [31]. Switching to a different drug class is recommended and practiced by clinicians [35–37], with reports in the literature of up to 8% of patients being treated with ≥ 3 DMARDs (biologic or targeted synthetic) within 3 years’ follow-up [35]. Nonetheless, the evidence base for directing switching from one mechanism of action to another is limited, with most switches based on clinician observation or experience [10].

In our analysis, unadjusted primary outcomes in both PsA and axSpA cohorts showed numerically greater improvements among switchers relative to cyclers. In other words, patients who initiated an IL-17Ai (switch) demonstrated greater numerical improvements in the primary outcome of CFB in the cDAPSA (PsA cohort) and CFB in BASDAI (axSpA cohort). After applying IPTW, switchers in both cohorts, PsA and axSpA, generally demonstrated numerically greater improvements across clinical outcomes compared to cyclers. The IPT-weighted binary measures showed that among patients with axSpA, switchers were more likely to achieve BASDAI50 than cyclers, and among patients with PsA, switchers were more likely to achieve BSA < 3%. PROs are important in the evaluation of rheumatic diseases [38], particularly pain and fatigue, clinically present in about 25% and 50% of patients, respectively [7]. In our analysis of the IPT-weighted cohorts, statistically significant improvements were observed in favor of switchers for several PROs such as PhGA of arthritis and psoriasis VAS, non-work activity impairment, and fatigue.

These findings should be interpreted in the context of observational study methodology, where potential regression to the mean cannot be entirely excluded, as physicians preferentially select alternative mechanisms for patients with more severe disease. Furthermore, the distinction between primary non-response and secondary failure to first TNFi is a clinically important factor for subsequent treatment decisions. Our study only partially addressed this with the first sensitivity analyses (restricted to therapy initiation within 6 months). Approximately 40% of patients had time on first TNFi ≤ 6 months, suggesting primary non-response, and showing similar patterns between both cohorts (PsA: Table S9; axSpA: Table S10).

Disease severity and comorbidity burden make the management of PsA and axSpA challenging, and baseline patient characteristics are pertinent to treatment patterns. Although the propensity score methodology in this study could not balance all variables due to the limited sample sizes, we were able to make the following observations with regards to efficacy and the presence of comorbidities and their possible influence on the choice of treatment and ultimately the results of our analysis. Both cyclers and switchers among patients with PsA and axSpA had high disease activity at baseline, with more severe disease among switchers. Clinical disease activity scores were predominantly in the moderate-to-high range (cDAPSA) for patients with PsA, and in high/very high disease activity state (ASDAS-CRP) for patients with axSpA. Similarly, both cohorts had high disease burden observed by PROs. Despite starting with worse disease relative to cyclers, switchers demonstrated greater improvements in both joint (cDAPSA) and skin outcomes (BSA) at 6-month follow-up. IL-17A’s central role in skin and joint inflammation may partially explain the observed superiority across both disease domains [44] and improvements in PROs. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating patient perspectives into treatment decisions, aligning with GRAPPA and EULAR recommendations that advocate for shared decision-making to optimize individualized care in psoriatic disease. Clinically, our observational findings support switching to IL-17Ai after a first TNFi failure, particularly for patients with prominent skin involvement (PsA), significant fatigue (axSpA) or inadequate disease activity control.

Patients in both cohorts also had a high prevalence of comorbidities that may have influenced the treatment patterns observed, including obesity in the overall population of both PsA and axSpA, a comorbidity known for exacerbation of disease activity [39] and a factor to diminish the response to biologics [40]. Patients with PsA and axSpA were seemingly switched to an IL-17Ai if they had anxiety or fibromyalgia. Other comorbidities observed in this study include uveitis and IBD, with a higher number in the cycler cohorts. Adalimumab was a drug of choice for specific PsA subpopulations such as those with comorbid uveitis and IBD [41]. As such, patients with a high prevalence of uveitis and IBD were cycled to receive adalimumab in the PsA and axSpA cohort, consistent with the recommendations for TNFi for patients with concomitant IBD or uveitis. Patients with axSpA who had a lower prevalence of comorbidities, and a lower incidence of multiple comorbidities were mostly switched to an IL-17Ai therapy. Overall, 41% of patients with PsA and 31% of patients with axSpA switched to an IL-17Ai after discontinuing a first TNFi therapy, which are higher switching rates than previously published for patients with PsA and axSpA [20, 42].

This analysis provides novel contributions: simultaneous evaluation of PsA and axSpA using standardized methodology; comprehensive validated outcome measures; robust IPTW adjustment; contemporary treatment patterns (2013–2024) when IL-17Ais were widely available; and substantially higher switching rates (41% PsA, 31% axSpA) than historically reported. Our findings reiterate the treatment recommendations of EULAR, GRAPPA, and ASAS to consider each patient’s prior treatment history, disease activity, and comorbidities when choosing a biologic to provide the optimal therapy [8, 9, 23]. Screening and managing of any relevant comorbidities may facilitate the treatment response target of remission or low disease activity in PsA and axSpA [43]. Some previously published studies for PsA and axSpA support switching in general [40, 41], although other studies have found no difference between cycling and switching [18]. Our real-world findings suggest that switching from a first TNFi to an IL-17Ai results in similar or numerically improved outcomes compared to cycling to a subsequent TNFi in patients with PsA and axSpA, although no statistically significant difference was observed in the vast majority of outcomes.

It is important to consider the results of this study within the context of its limitations. First is the potential for confounding. While IPTW achieved balance for most covariates, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded and may have contributed to observed treatment effects. Our observational study does not account for potential provider biases that are independent of the patient characteristics accounted for in propensity model. Additionally, due to the characteristics of the data collected, information regarding the dosing for each drug included in the TNFi and IL17Ai cohorts was unavailable, precluding any adjustments for this in our analysis. Some variables could not be included in the propensity score model due to small number of patients or because their clinical meaning was not considered impactful on the objective studied in this manuscript (e.g., axSpA primary analyses: lumbar flexion). Therefore, a more parsimonious model was prioritized (with fewer variables). The small number of switchers in the axSpA cohort limited the number of variables that could be included in the outcome model to control for residual confounding. Furthermore, sample sizes varied substantially across outcomes due to missing data, with composite measures requiring complete component data. For example, cDAPSA was calculable for approximately 93% of PsA cohort. This reduced statistical power and may introduce selection bias if missingness relates to disease severity or response. We did not analyze time-to-discontinuation as a primary outcome. Future analyses examining treatment persistence would provide valuable complementary information about response durability. The 6-month visit used in this study includes visits that occurred within a window of 3–9 months after the index date, meaning that the results presented here represent a range of treatment windows ranging from 3 to 9 months for each group compared. While the CFB to 6 months for each subject could be under- or overestimated depending on the timing of their 6-month follow-up visit, the average treatment effect estimates should not be impacted. This is an inherent limitation of real-world clinical practice, where office visits happen with some variability. Lastly, findings derive from a USA-based registry and may have limited generalizability to systems with different treatment access and insurance. Prescribing patterns vary internationally based on guidelines, reimbursement, and physician training. However, the clinical decision-making regarding alternative mechanisms of action after inadequate response to a first biologic may be relevant across healthcare settings.

Conclusion

Findings from this real-world analysis suggest that switching from a first TNFi to an IL-17Ai results in similar or numerically improved outcomes compared to cycling to a subsequent TNFi in both PsA and axSpA cohorts. These results underscore the importance of considering alternative efficacious mechanisms of action such as IL-17Ais as suitable options for patients discontinuing a first TNFi therapy.
