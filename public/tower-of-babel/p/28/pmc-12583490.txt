Self-referencing ultrafast wide-field pump-probe microscopy
Authors: Rihan Wu, Yaqing Zhang, Md Shahjahan, Elad Harel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ultrafast pump-probe microscopy reveals spatial heterogeneity in material properties including charge and energy transfer, electron-phonon coupling, and vibrational dynamics. At high spatial resolution, weak nonlinear signals often require point scanning with significant signal averaging, limiting studies of non-repeatable processes or systems prone to photodamage from prolonged laser exposure. To overcome these limitations, we introduce a wide-field pump–probe approach that integrates Parallel Rapid Imaging with Spectroscopic Mapping (PRISM) and a self-referencing method, suppressing noise by more than two orders of magnitude. Our technique exploits spatial correlations within the field of view to denoise and suppress laser intensity fluctuations without a reference detector, enabling acquisition of over one million pump–probe traces in under a second. This capability supports high-throughput material screening, real-time ultrafast spectroscopy, and investigations of non-reversible dynamics. Applied to few-layer WSe₂ and monolayer WSe₂/MoSe₂ heterostructures, the method resolves vibrational modes and substrate-induced coupling previously hidden by laser noise.

Introduction

Coherent pump-probe microscopy is a widely utilized technique for investigating ultrafast electronic and vibrational dynamics in solids. It provides a direct means of capturing excited-state dynamics in various materials, which reflect carrier relaxation and recombination processes, and vibrational dynamics that report on structure and electron-phonon coupling1,2. The microscopic imaging capability reveals intrinsic material heterogeneity, which significantly influences these dynamic properties. Despite its strengths, the technique often faces challenges related to low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) due to the limited sample volume and localized probing, which inherently reduces signal strength3,4. This issue is especially pronounced in materials with low optical density, such as dielectrics, biological samples, and 2D materials, where pump-induced probe changes can be as low as 10−2 to 10−3, with vibrational signals typically one to two orders of magnitude smaller5–7. As a result, effective denoising in both temporal and spatial domain is crucial for isolating and analyzing these weak signals.

Noise in pump-probe experiments stems from various sources, including instrumental noise such as electronic readout and laser fluctuations, and environmental noise such as mechanical vibrations and temperature drift8,9. To reach the shot noise limit, techniques such as lock-in amplification, reference detection, high-repetition-rate lasers, and faster acquisition schemes have been developed3. However, each technique has its limitations. While lock-in detection enhances signals at a specific frequency and effectively suppresses broadband noise, achieving high SNR requires long acquisition times, which makes the method vulnerable to slow environmental drifts and laser fluctuations. Moreover, prolonged exposure can damage sensitive materials such as biological tissues, soft polymers, 2D materials, and nanoscale structures, introducing artifacts or altering measurement conditions. High-repetition-rate lasers improve SNRs through rapid averaging and high-frequency detection, but they can introduce significant thermal load on the sample, potentially leading to heating, drift, or damage that degrades measurement accuracy and repeatability. Additionally, these methods cannot effectively suppress coherent noise that results from the pulses themselves and cannot be removed through simple averaging. To address some of these challenges, balanced detection is commonly used to suppress common-mode noise in optical signals. By subtracting signals from two matched photodetectors, it effectively rejects noise that is equally present in both beams, such as laser power fluctuations9. While effective, this technique is highly sensitive to mismatches in detector response, optical alignment, and environmental fluctuations10. Minor discrepancies in optical paths, detector sensitivities, or phase stability can introduce residual noise, compromising overall performance. Furthermore, maintaining precise balance often requires complex optical setups and continuous monitoring. These limitations not only increase system complexity but also reduce robustness, particularly when dealing with low-signal samples or long-term measurements.

In this work, we introduce a self-referencing, high-sensitivity, wide-field coherent imaging technique based on the previously developed Parallel Rapid Imaging with Spectroscopic Mapping (PRISM) method11. By capturing wide-field images that included both sample and reference regions, we established spatiotemporal correlations for effective noise suppression without requiring a separate reference detector, thereby eliminating issues related to detector imbalance, calibration drift, and sensitivity variability between detectors. Using a high-speed camera (256 × 320 pixel) operating at 20,000 frames per second (fps), together with an advanced denoising scheme, we acquired over one million spectra per second and constructed high-quality images of electronic decay dynamics and vibrational coherences in few-layer tungsten diselenide (WSe₂) with acquisition times as short as 50 ms. The enhanced SNR also enabled the detection of exceptionally weak signals in monolayer tungsten diselenide /molybdenum diselenide (WSe₂/MoSe₂) heterostructures with acquisition times of only 600 ms, representing a speed improvement of more than two orders of magnitude compared to conventional methods at comparable SNR1. While high-speed acquisition reduced the impact of low-frequency noise and minimized sample exposure, the self-referencing denoising scheme enabled reliable signal recovery from measurements with inherently weak responses. This combination enabled measurement of both spectral and temporal information that are often difficult to access with conventional methods, and to our knowledge, it is one of the first techniques capable of capturing full spectral profiles and decay dynamics of such low-signal samples with sub-second acquisition. These capabilities expanded the utility of PRISM for a wide range of applications, including rapid material screening, real-time monitoring of dynamic processes, and the study of irreversible ultrafast phenomena, particularly in sensitive systems such as biological samples and ultra-thin materials.

Results and discussion

The principle of PRISM is the synchronized acquisition of time-resolved image sequences using a high-speed camera, enabling real-time, frame-by-frame observation of pump-induced signal changes. The experimental setup (Fig. 1a) consisted of pump and probe beams that were gently focused to illuminate a relatively wide field of view (FOV) of ~80 × 80 μm². The timing between the pump and probe pulses was controlled using a high-speed voice coil (VC) stage, which oscillated sinusoidally at a frequency of 10 Hz along the probe beam’s optical path. This motion continuously varied the time delay between pump and probe pulses while the camera captured frames at a rate of 20,000 fps. The acquired data was stored as a three-dimensional matrix, where the first two dimensions corresponded to spatial coordinates, and the third represented time, encapsulating the transient electronic and vibrational responses induced by the pump excitation. To conceptualize the data analysis process, we present a simulated dataset in Fig. 1b that illustrates three distinct regions, each exhibiting different electronic and vibrational responses. Electronic state dynamics typically follow multi-exponential decay behavior, while vibrational dynamics appear as coherent oscillations. To efficiently separate these two components, the three-dimensional dataset was first flattened into a two-dimensional matrix combining spatial and temporal information. Global analysis was then applied to fit the data, recovering multiple exponential decay functions and their corresponding amplitudes12. The one-dimensional amplitude data were then reconstructed back to a two-dimensional matrix representing two spatial axes and displayed as decay constant maps. These maps revealed spatial heterogeneity in carrier relaxation dynamics across the sample. To isolate vibrational contributions, the fitted exponential decay components were subtracted from the original data, leaving only the oscillatory signals associated with vibrational modes. Fourier transformation of these oscillatory signals in the time domain then yielded the vibrational spectra and their corresponding hyperspectral maps. Simultaneously imaging both electronic and vibrational responses enhanced sensitivity in distinguishing structural heterogeneity and revealed distinct regions that remained hidden in single-modality imaging, highlighting the power of correlation-based analysis in capturing fine spatial details and coupled state dynamics.

In our previous study, we demonstrated ultrafast measurements of multi-layer WSe₂ using PRISM, achieving a maximum acquisition speed of 50 ms per complete scan11. However, for samples with inherently weak signals, a single scan without averaging often resulted in noise-obscured signals due to laser and environmental fluctuations, necessitating multiple scans and averaging to achieve sufficient SNR. To suppress low-frequency noise from environmental fluctuations and 1/f noise, we implemented an intensity modulator operating at half the frame rate in the pump optical path9. By blocking the pump pulses in alternating frames, we subtracted the frames with and without pump excitation. However, this method was less effective against higher-frequency noise components that overlapped with the signal in the frequency domain. In this work, we took advantage of spatial correlations across pixels in the wide-field image and the inherent redundancy in spatial information to suppress noise that overlapped with the signal in the spectral region, enhancing the SNR by as much as two-orders of magnitude.

We utilized few-layer WSe₂ samples to demonstrate the principle of the denoising scheme. Due to the thin nature of the sample (estimated 8–10 layers, see SI) and the transmission heterodyne detection scheme, the pump-induced signal was significantly weaker than the transmitted probe light. Without averaging multiple scans, the time trace and vibrational spectrum were heavily corrupted by noise. However, we observed that the noise in the sample region was highly correlated with that in the reference region without the sample, as shown in Fig. 2b. While the correlation matrix effectively captured spatial and noise relationships across the FOV, its practical implementation posed computational challenges. Due to the high spatial resolution of PRISM (81,920 pixels), the full correlation matrix had dimensions of 68,281 × 13,639 and, when stored as 32-bit floating-point values, required ~3.5 GB of memory. To address this, we investigated two sampling strategies to reduce the matrix size while preserving essential correlations: (1) Random sampling, where reference points were randomly selected from the reference region (green dots in the top inset of Fig. 2e); (2) Sequential sampling, where reference points were selected in a predefined spatial order to systematically expand coverage (bottom inset of Fig. 2e). We note that, reference pixels were always selected exclusively from the background region (i.e Fig. 2c black region).

As the number of sampling points increased, the effectiveness of noise suppression was expected to improve across different sampling strategies. This is because a larger set of reference pixels enabled more accurate modeling of shared noise features while averaging out pixel-specific intensity variations. To evaluate the impact of sampling density on denoising performance, we examined the time domain traces and corresponding vibrational spectra under varying numbers of reference points, as shown in Fig. 2d. Each spectrum was normalized to its highest peak to allow easier comparison of spectral shape rather than absolute amplitude. It can be seen that with fewer reference points, denoising was less effective, particularly in the low frequency region (below 1 THz), where noise appeared to require more sampling points for effective suppression. When the number of sampled points exceeded 1% of the total reference pixels, noise suppression improved significantly, revealing a previously obscured low-frequency interlayer breathing mode near 0.3 THz, indicative of a sample thickness corresponding to 8 to 10 layers14,15, as well as the 7.5 THz A1g vibrational mode of WSe2. In sequential sampling, the SNR gradually improved as the number of sampling points increased. In contrast, random sampling exhibited a sharp increase in SNR once the number of sampling points exceeded 1% of the total reference points. This was due to highly correlated reference points being distributed relatively uniformly across the sample region, rather than being confined to specific areas. As a result, random sampling was the more efficient and robust method to improve computational efficiency while maintaining a high SNR. For both sampling methods, the SNR stabilized when approximately half of the reference region was utilized in constructing the correlation matrix. The implementation of correlation-based denoising improved the SNR from 3.3 without denoising to ~700, representing an improvement of over 200-fold. This improvement demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed approach in enhancing signal fidelity.

We note that this referencing strategy assumed the presence of a background region within the FOV that did not contain pump-probe response and could serve as a clean reference for estimating and removing common-mode noise. This might limit applicability in samples that fully occupy the imaging area, such as continuous roll-to-roll materials. In such cases, alternative referencing approaches such as spatial filtering, masking a small region to block the pump, or using an auxiliary detector, could be employed to isolate and subtract shared noise components from the measurement.

Based on the previously discussed denoising scheme, we compared the results of a single scan, a denoised single scan, and an averaged result over 19 scans to evaluate the effectiveness of the denoising approach. Figure 3a presents the spatially averaged time trace over the entire sample area, the isolated oscillatory signal, and the corresponding spectrum for these three cases. The denoised single scan exhibited a significantly enhanced SNR, resulting in more clearly resolved vibrational oscillations compared to both the unprocessed single scan and the 19-scan averaged result. Closer inspection of the unprocessed spectrum showed that noise was particularly prominent above 7 THz, obscuring the vibrational peak near 7.5 THz. For example, a peak near 8 THz appeared in the single-scan spectrum but was absent after denoising, suggesting that it was a noise artifact rather than a true vibrational mode. Even in the 19-scan averaged spectrum, noise remained significant, indicating that limited averaging did not effectively suppress background fluctuations. This result highlighted the effectiveness of the correlation-based denoising approach in enhancing signal quality while reducing the need for multiple scans, thereby improving measurement efficiency. In the decay constant map, the relatively strong electronic state signal allowed the single scan to capture the primary decay component, characterized by a 2.5 ps decay time constant which is attributed to decay of exciton through radiative or nonradiative recombination16,18. In the single-scan hyperspectral map, the 7.5 THz A1g mode was completely obscured by background noise. After applying the denoising scheme, this vibrational mode became easily resolved.

With the substantial improvement in SNR and imaging quality achieved through the correlation-based denoising scheme, we next investigated a CVD grown WSe₂/MoSe₂ heterostructure at an acquisition time of 600 ms (12 scan averages). Due to the monolayer thickness of the sample, the signal was inherently weak, making traditional measurement techniques reliant on long integration times with lock-in detection or extensive signal averaging, often requiring several hours to acquire a complete set of hyperspectral maps. Our approach enabled the direct acquisition of high-fidelity ultrafast dynamics without the need for time-consuming methods, which increase the risk of photodamage and may also alter material properties due to prolonged laser exposure.

Figure 4c presents a representative decay constant map alongside hyperspectral maps of the vibrational modes. The extracted decay constant for the heterostructure was ~0.3 ps, faster than that of few-layer WSe₂, likely due to an increased density of surface states. Moreover, despite the distinct material properties of WSe₂ and MoSe₂, their respective regions exhibited similar lifetimes, which may be attributed to their closely matched A-exciton binding energies and density of states, leading to comparable ultrafast carrier dynamics23. As observed in few-layer WSe₂, the effect of denoising on the decay constant map was relatively minor, as the strong electronic signal provided sufficient SNR even in the unprocessed data. However, the impact was pronounced in the hyperspectral map, where denoising significantly enhanced weak vibrational signals, particularly the interlayer coupling mode and the MoSe₂ A1g mode, both of which were otherwise indistinguishable in the raw data.

After denoising, the spatial distribution of these vibrational modes became clearly resolved. The interlayer coupling mode was confined exclusively to the overlapping WSe₂/MoSe₂ region, confirming its origin from interlayer interactions. Similarly, the 7 THz mode was localized solely within the MoSe₂ region as expected. However, the 7.5 THz mode which was expected across the entire WSe₂ layer was instead confined to the overlap region and exhibited a notable suppression outside this area. This attenuation suggested a significant substrate influence on the vibrational response, likely due to a remote phonon effect from the SiO₂ substrate24,25. In this process, surface optical phonons from SiO₂ interacted with charge carriers and phonon modes in WSe₂ through long-range Coulomb coupling, effectively damping out-of-plane vibrational modes and reducing their intensity in regions where WSe₂ is in direct contact with the substrate. This effect had been previously observed in various 2D materials, where substrate-induced long-range Coulomb interactions more strongly influence carrier and phonon behavior in materials with weaker dielectric screening26–29.

We observed that within the heterostructure region, interlayer coupling mitigated the substrate effects, leading to an enhancement of the phonon oscillation signal. This enhancement may result from strong interlayer interactions between WSe₂ and MoSe₂, which could modify phonon dispersion and restore vibrational coherence by facilitating energy transfer between layers and enhancing dielectric screening30,31. Phonon hybridization within the heterostructure might minimize remote phonon scattering, thereby preserving out-of-plane vibrational modes32. Additionally, enhanced dielectric screening could weaken long-range Coulomb interactions, further mitigating substrate-induced phonon damping.

Further examination of the hyperspectral maps revealed spatial variations in the interlayer coupling strength. Some regions exhibited weaker coupling signals, likely due to local morphology variations such as folds, wrinkles, or strain-induced defects. These variations were also evident in the 7.5 THz hyperspectral map. The correlation between the coupling mode intensity and the enhancement of the WSe₂ vibrational mode further supported our hypothesis that interlayer interactions mitigate substrate-induced quenching effects, leading to stronger vibrational responses in the heterostructure region. Understanding and controlling interlayer phonon interactions and substrate effect are crucial for thermal management in 2D materials where phonons serve as primary heat carriers. By mitigating remote phonon scattering and preserving intrinsic vibrational modes, interlayer coupling can enhance or tune thermal conductivity, which is essential for efficient heat dissipation in nanoelectronics, optoelectronics, and thermoelectric devices33.

In this work, we introduced a high-sensitivity, wide-field coherent imaging technique based on PRISM for investigating ultrafast electronic and vibrational dynamics. By utilizing a self-referencing denoising approach that takes advantage of spatial correlations within the FOV, our method effectively suppressed noise without requiring a secondary reference detector, lock-in detection, or extensive signal averaging. This improvement addressed a key challenge in coherent spectroscopic imaging, enabling the detection of weak vibrational signals that are often obscured by background noise. Applying this technique to few-layer WSe₂ and MoSe₂/WSe₂ heterostructures, we achieved high-speed acquisition while maintaining high SNR. The denoising process enhanced the detection of weak vibrational modes and interlayer coupling effects, while also enabling the observation of substrate-induced suppression and its recovery within the heterostructure region, which are otherwise difficult to resolve due to background noise and signal limitations. These findings highlighted the advantages of PRISM as a powerful tool for ultrafast spectroscopy. By combining high measurement speed with exceptional sensitivity, this approach enables real-time observation of dynamic processes and irreversible events that are otherwise difficult to capture, while also providing fundamental insights into ultrafast energy dissipation, charge transfer, and vibrational dynamics in complex material systems.

Methods

PRISM Setup: Ultrafast pump and probe pulses were generated using two second-harmonic noncollinear optical parametric amplifiers (2H NOPAs), pumped by a 1030 nm femtosecond laser (Light Conversion CARBIDE). The pump beam (Light Conversion ORPHEUS-N-2H) was tuned to 760 nm to match the A-exciton resonance of WSe₂ and MoSe₂, while the probe beam, generated by a custom-built 2H NOPA, was set to 700 nm. Both beams operated at a repetition rate of 200 kHz with pulse durations of ~32 fs. To minimize dispersion and preserve pulse duration, both beams were directed into the microscope using low group-delay-dispersion mirrors and focused using reflective optics. The pump beam entered at an incidence angle of ~10°, while the probe was aligned along the optical axis. Both beams were focused onto a common area of ~70–80 µm² at the sample plane. The transmitted probe light was collected using a 50×/0.42 NA objective (Mitutoyo Plan Apo NIR) and imaged onto a high-speed camera (Phantom S710). The FOV at the sample was 57.5 × 46 µm, captured at a resolution of 256 × 320 pixels and a frame rate of 20 kHz. Image sequences were read out by a frame grabber (Euresys Coaxlink Octo). Temporal delay between pump and probe pulses was controlled by a voice coil stage (Physik Instrumente V-522), oscillating sinusoidally at 10 Hz to provide a scanning range of 0–7 ps. The stage position was monitored in real time using a HeNe laser interferometer. The interference signal was detected by a photodiode (Thorlabs APD410A) and digitized with a high-speed digitizer (AlazarTech ATS9146). Precise timing control was achieved through hardware synchronization of all components, including the laser sources, modulator, stage, camera, and digitizer. Additional details on the experimental setup and synchronization scheme are provided in the SI.

Denoising scheme: To enable noise suppression through self-referencing, a high-speed optical intensity modulator (Thorlabs OM6NH) was placed in the pump path to alternate frames with and without pump excitation. This allowed the acquisition of reference frames where the pump-off frames were used to compute pixel-wise correlations across the FOV, enabling construction of a correlation matrix between reference and signal regions. This matrix was then applied to the pump-on frames to isolate and subtract common-mode noise, particularly fluctuations from laser intensity and optical background. The data processing steps, including background isolation, reference point sampling, and correlation matrix computation, were implemented in MATLAB. For a full FOV dataset with 1000 delay time points, the total computation time was ~3–4 s. This time could be further reduced by lowering the number of sampled reference points as discussed in main text.

Sample Preparation: Few-layer WSe₂ flakes were mechanically exfoliated from bulk crystals and transferred onto glass substrates using the Scotch tape method. The layer thickness was estimated from the spectrum obtained from PRISM measurements. Monolayer WSe₂ and MoSe₂ (SixCarbon Tec.) for heterostructure samples were grown separately via chemical vapor deposition on glass substrates. The MoSe₂ monolayer was then transferred onto the WSe₂ layer using a dry-transfer technique to form the stacked heterostructure.
