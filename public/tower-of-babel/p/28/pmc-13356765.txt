Machine learning–driven identification of PIM2 kinase inhibitors through QSAR modeling and molecular dynamics simulations
Authors: Aamir Fahira, Muhammad Shahab, Zaheer Ud Din, Xiaoan Li, Xuemin Jian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The proto-oncogene serine/threonine kinase PIM2 is a critical regulator of cell proliferation, survival, and tumor progression and represents an attractive therapeutic target for several cancers. In this study, an integrated machine learning–guided computational pipeline was developed to identify potential PIM2 inhibitors by combining quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) modeling, virtual screening, molecular docking, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and pharmacokinetic prediction. Bioactivity data for PIM2 inhibitors were retrieved from the ChEMBL database, yielding 5953 compounds. After data cleaning, structural standardization, and removal of duplicates and invalid entries, a curated dataset of 1584 compounds was obtained for QSAR modeling. To address dataset imbalance, the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied before model development. Twelve molecular fingerprint descriptors were generated and used to construct 180 QSAR models using five machine learning algorithms, including Random Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Support Vector Regression (SVR), k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP). Among these models, the Random Forest–fingerprint model demonstrated the best predictive performance, achieving a mean R2 of 0.971 with low prediction errors (RMSE = 0.271; MAE = 0.125) across training, testing, and cross-validation datasets. The optimized model was subsequently applied to virtual screening of multiple chemical libraries, including FDA-approved drugs, natural product databases, and commercial compound collections. Several promising candidates were identified, including TCMBANKIN000009 (emetine), Amb28533044 (4,6′-Anhydrooxysporidinone), NPC170963 (Lysophosphatidylcholine (15:0)), NPC262768 (Endosulfan), and NPC469603 (8-hydroxyircinialactam A). Molecular docking showed that these compounds bind within the ATP-binding pocket of PIM2 kinase, forming interactions with key residues such as Lys62, Asp125, Asp128, and Glu168. Subsequent molecular dynamics simulations confirmed the stability of selected complexes, demonstrating reduced residue fluctuations, stable protein compactness, and persistent intermolecular interactions during the simulation. Furthermore, ADMET prediction suggested favorable pharmacokinetic and toxicity profiles for several compounds. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of the identified molecules as promising PIM2 inhibitor candidates, providing valuable leads for future experimental validation and anticancer drug development.

Introduction

The serine/threonine kinase PIM2, one of three members of the PIM proto-oncogene family, is a constitutively active enzyme that promotes the survival, proliferation, and metabolic fitness of malignant cells across hematologic and solid tumors.1, 2, 3, 4 Unlike most AGC/CMGC-like kinases, PIM isoforms lack regulatory domains and exhibit activity that scales with expression, enabling persistent signaling in oncogenic contexts.2, 4 PIM2 phosphorylates key apoptotic and translational checkpoints, most notably BAD and 4E-BP1, and intersects with MYC stability and output, thereby suppressing apoptosis and sustaining cap-dependent translation under cellular stress.2, 3, 4 Collectively, these features position PIM2 as a compact, druggable signaling node whose inhibition promises broad antitumor impact.

Clinically, PIM2 overexpression is recurrent in multiple myeloma (MM) and diverse leukemias/lymphomas, where it contributes to aggressive phenotypes, therapy resistance, and inferior outcomes.5, 6, 7 In MM, PIM-driven crosstalk with JAK/STAT, PI3K/AKT, and NF-κB augments tumor survival while exacerbating osteolytic bone disease by tipping the osteoclast/osteoblast balance.3, 5, 6 Recent mechanistic work demonstrates that selective PIM2 blockade activates the integrated stress response and NOXA, rendering plasma cells acutely MCL1-dependent, and reveals a rational co-targeting strategy with MCL1 inhibitors.8 These convergent survival, translational, and bone-microenvironment effects underscore why PIM2 inhibition is therapeutically compelling in MM and beyond.

Translating this biology, multiple PIM inhibitors have reached the clinic. The pan-PIM inhibitor AZD1208 demonstrated robust preclinical activity but modest single-agent signals in early trials; nevertheless, it confirmed on-target pathway modulation and remains a useful pharmacologic probe.9, 10 PIM447 (LGH447) reduced tumor burden and protected bone in myeloma models, supporting disease-modifying potential.11 First-generation chemistry, such as SGI-1776, was curtailed by hERG-mediated QT prolongation in Phase I studies, illustrating the need for improved selectivity and safety.12 Fortunately, PIM kinases possess idiosyncratic structural features, most notably a proline-rich, hydrogen-bond-poor hinge that widens the ATP site, offering routes to isoform-selective designs and better therapeutic windows.13, 14 Together, these insights motivate renewed efforts to discover potent, selective PIM2 inhibitors with optimized liability profiles.

Rigorous QSAR modeling,15 grounded in curated data16 balanced classes, external validation, and applicability-domain control have matured into a dependable predictive tool for triaging vast chemical spaces.16, 17 Several computational studies have attempted to model or discover PIM inhibitors, including statistical QSAR studies on limited PIM1/PIM2 inhibitor series, 2D/3D-QSAR and docking analyses of PIM2-focused scaffolds, and more recent machine-learning or graph-attention models for PIM-family activity prediction.18, 19, 20, 21 However, these studies were based on relatively small or congeneric chemical series, focused on a single scaffold family, or addressed PIM kinases collectively rather than on developing a broad PIM2-specific screening workflow. In addition, a recent PIM2-focused study combined energy-based virtual screening, XGBoost, DeepDock, MM/GBSA rescoring, MD simulation, and biological testing, confirming the value of computational prioritization for PIM2 inhibitor discovery.22 Nevertheless, that workflow was primarily structure-based and rescoring-driven. In contrast, a ligand-based, ChEMBL-derived, multi-descriptor QSAR framework linked to diverse library screening and MD-based dynamic validation remains less explored for PIM2.

When combined with targeted library selection and repurposing strategies, QSAR-guided virtual screening can efficiently identify novel chemotypes and prioritize actives at scale. In this work, we therefore assembled diverse screening sets spanning FDA-approved drugs, natural products (NPASS),23 ZINC ultra-large purchasable subsets, and Traditional Chinese Medicine resources (TCMBANK),24 and aligned cheminformatics descriptors with our trained model to ensure schema fidelity before prediction and activity-thresholding.

To provide structural plausibility and dynamic verification beyond ligand-based predictions, top QSAR hits were subject to structure-based docking and explicit-solvent molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Docking provides binding poses and preliminary affinity rankings; subsequently, an AMBER-based MD simulation assesses complex stability, key interactions, and solution-phase energetics over time.25, 26 Although previous computational studies have explored PIM2 inhibitor discovery using QSAR, docking, machine learning, or structure-based virtual screening approaches, most were limited to specific chemical series, smaller datasets, or individual modeling strategies. In contrast, the present study integrates a curated ChEMBL-derived dataset, comparative machine-learning QSAR modeling, virtual screening of diverse chemical libraries, molecular docking, MD simulation, and ADMET assessment into a unified workflow. This multi-tier computational pipeline provides a resource-efficient strategy for prioritizing potential PIM2 inhibitor candidates for future experimental validation.

Method

Data retrieval and processing

The PIM2 bioactivity dataset used in this study was obtained from the ChEMBL database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl, ID: CHEMBL4523), a publicly available repository of bioactive molecules with experimentally validated pharmacological properties.27 Data retrieval was carried out programmatically using the ChEMBL Web Resource Client in Python. Data comprised all experimentally measured bioactivity records associated with the target, including inhibitory concentrations (IC50), assay information, compound identifiers, canonical SMILES representations, and relevant publication metadata.

The raw bioactivity data underwent extensive preprocessing to ensure consistency, quality, and suitability for QSAR modeling (Fig. 1). Briefly, duplicate entries based on compound ChEMBL IDs were removed, and compounds lacking a canonical SMILES or valid bioactivity values were excluded. Invalid chemical structures were further removed using the RDKit cheminformatics toolkit.28 Bioactivity values measured in nanomolar units were converted to a negative logarithmic scale (pIC50) to normalize the measurement range and improve interpretability. Concisely, for activity-class annotation, compounds were categorized using IC₅₀-based thresholds before QSAR modeling. Compounds with IC₅₀ ≤ 1000 nM were classified as active, compounds with 1000 nM < IC₅₀ < 10,000 nM were classified as intermediate, and compounds with IC₅₀ ≥ 10,000 nM were classified as inactive. These thresholds correspond approximately to pIC₅₀ ≥ 6 for active compounds, 5 < pIC₅₀ < 6 for intermediate compounds, and pIC₅₀ ≤ 5 for inactive compounds. To enhance predictive modeling performance, the high-dimensional descriptor space was optimized through systematic feature selection and filtering. Initially, descriptors with near-zero variance across compounds were removed to eliminate uninformative features. Highly correlated descriptors were further excluded by calculating pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients, with a threshold of |r| ≥ 0.90 to minimize redundancy. Finally, recursive feature elimination (RFE) was applied using model-derived feature importance to select the most informative molecular descriptors for QSAR modeling. The optimized descriptor dataset was standardized using z-score normalization, ensuring that all features contributed proportionally during machine learning.

An exploratory data analysis (EDA) was conducted to understand structural diversity, the distribution of molecular properties, and the relationship between descriptors and bioactivity outcomes. Descriptor distributions were visualized, outliers were identified using interquartile range (IQR) thresholds, and drug-likeness was assessed based on Lipinski's rule of five.29 Correlation heatmaps were generated to evaluate collinearity among descriptors and guide further dimensionality reduction, ensuring that only non-redundant features were retained for predictive modeling.

Given the inherent imbalance between active and inactive compounds in the dataset, a data-balancing strategy was applied using the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) implemented via the imbalanced-learn library.30 Before oversampling, multiclass bioactivity labels were encoded, and the numeric descriptor matrix was separated from compound identifiers and SMILES strings. SMOTE was used to generate synthetic minority-class samples in the descriptor space, effectively balancing the class distribution and mitigating bias during model training. As originally described by Chawla et al.,30 SMOTE generates new minority-class instances by interpolating between neighboring samples in feature space. Consequently, the generated samples represent artificial positions in descriptor space rather than newly generated chemically valid molecular structures. Similar limitations of descriptor-space interpolation have been discussed in cheminformatics and QSAR modeling studies, where synthetic feature vectors may not always correspond to physically realistic molecules or valid combinations of molecular fragments.31, 32 To preserve molecular interpretability, each synthetic vector was mapped back to the closest real compound within the same activity class using a nearest-neighbor search based on Euclidean distance in the standardized descriptor space. The resulting balanced dataset contains both real and mapped synthetic compounds. Thus, SMOTE was used only as a model-training strategy to reduce class imbalance and was not interpreted as a chemical-structure generation method. Nevertheless, nearest-neighbor remapping may reduce but cannot eliminate local oversampling bias around minority-class chemical regions; therefore, model reliability was assessed using independent test-set prediction and ten-fold cross-validation rather than training performance alone.

Molecular descriptors computation

Molecular descriptors and fingerprints were computed to numerically represent chemical structures and capture relevant molecular properties for machine learning analysis. Descriptor generation was performed using RDKit, in which a comprehensive set of 12 distinct molecular fingerprints and structural representations was calculated. These included AtomPairs2D and AtomPairs2DCount fingerprints, EState descriptors, CDK and CDKextended fingerprints, CDK graph-only descriptors, Klekota-Roth fingerprints and counts, MACCS keys, PubChem fingerprints, and substructure-based descriptors.33 In addition to fingerprint representations, physicochemical properties such as molecular weight, partition coefficient (logP), topological polar surface area (TPSA), molar refractivity, hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, and rotatable bond counts were extracted. All SMILES strings were sanitized and standardized to ensure valid molecular representations before descriptor computation. The resulting descriptor matrix was merged with bioactivity data for model development.

Machine learning-based QSAR modeling

A multi-model QSAR modeling framework was developed to evaluate the predictive performance of several machine learning algorithms, including Random Forest (RF),34 Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost),35 Support Vector Regression (SVR),36 K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN),37 and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) neural networks.38 All models were implemented in Python using scikit-learn and XGBoost libraries. For final QSAR model construction, the model-ready descriptor dataset was generated after fingerprint-level filtering to improve separation between high- and low-activity compounds. Compounds with pIC₅₀ ≥ 7.0 were retained as active compounds, whereas compounds with pIC₅₀ ≤ 5.5 were retained as non-active compounds; compounds with 5.5 < pIC₅₀ < 7.0 were excluded to reduce boundary ambiguity. The resulting model-ready descriptor dataset contained 2904 instances and was split into training and testing sets with an 80:20 ratio, yielding 2323 instances for model training and 581 instances for independent testing. The split was performed using a random partitioning strategy with a fixed random seed of 42 to ensure reproducibility. Ten-fold cross-validation was employed on the training set to assess model generalizability. To further enhance model performance, hyperparameter optimization was conducted using Optuna, an advanced Bayesian optimization framework that efficiently searches the parameter space using a Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE).39 For each machine learning algorithm, a customized search space was defined, including the number of estimators, maximum tree depth, learning rates, and regularization terms for XGBoost, kernel functions and penalty parameters for SVR, number of neighbors for KNN, and network architecture parameters for MLP. Each optimization trial evaluated candidate hyperparameter sets based on R2 scores from cross-validation, and the best-performing hyperparameters were used to retrain the final models on the full training dataset. Model performance was evaluated using multiple statistical metrics, including the coefficient of determination (R2), root mean squared error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE), providing a comprehensive assessment of predictive accuracy and consistency.

Following optimization, a composite score was calculated as an internal model-ranking index to compare the overall reliability of each model–descriptor combination. This score was not intended to represent a universal QSAR validation equation, but rather a transparent multi-metric ranking strategy for the present comparative modeling workflow. The rationale for this approach is supported by QSAR validation literature showing that model selection should not rely on a single statistical parameter, because goodness-of-fit, prediction error, robustness, and external predictivity describe different aspects of model quality.16, 40, 41, 42 Rácz et al. specifically emphasized the need to compare QSAR models using multiple performance parameters and model-ranking strategies, while Roy et al. highlighted that R2-based metrics alone may be misleading because they can depend on the range and distribution of response values in the test set.40, 41 Concisely, the Composite Score was first calculated to quantify the overall predictive reliability of each model–descriptor combination by integrating both goodness-of-fit and prediction error parameters (Eq. 1). Specifically, the numerator includes the mean values of the coefficient of determination R2and the average coefficient of determination AverageRM2, which reflect model explanatory power and predictive consistency. The denominator incorporates prediction error metrics, including root mean square error RMSE, mean absolute error MAE, and the difference between training and test predictive performance ΔRM2, which collectively measure model uncertainty and potential overfitting:(1)Composite Score=15×MeanR2+MeanAverageRM2Mean RMSE+MeanMAE+Mean∆RM2

A higher composite score indicates improved predictive accuracy, reduced prediction error, and stronger generalization capability across datasets.

To further ensure robust model comparison, an average rank metric was calculated by assigning independent rankings to each model according to five evaluation criteria (Eq. 2): R2, AverageRM2, RMSE, MAE, and ΔRM2. The final ranking score was obtained as the arithmetic mean of these individual rankings:(2)Average Rank=RankR2+RankAvrRM2+RankRMSE+RankMAE+Rank∆RM25

Lower average rank values indicate better overall model performance. This ranking strategy minimizes bias associated with any single evaluation metric and provides a balanced assessment of prediction accuracy and model stability. Concisely, the composite score and average rank were used to identify the optimal descriptor–model combination for subsequent applicability domain analysis and virtual screening procedures.

All analyses were conducted in a Jupyter Notebook environment using Python 3.10 on a Windows 10 workstation. The computational environment included the following libraries: pandas v2.0, NumPy v1.26, RDKit v2023.09, scikit-learn v1.4, imbalanced-learn v0.12, Optuna v3.5, XGBoost v2.1, matplotlib v3.8, and seaborn v0.12.

Virtual screening

The optimized QSAR model was used to guide a structure-based virtual screening pipeline aimed at identifying novel PIM2 kinase inhibitors from multiple chemical libraries.43 Following the evaluation of various machine learning models, the best-performing QSAR model was selected based on cross-validation performance, external test set accuracy, and stability across descriptor sets. To facilitate the identification of promising bioactive compounds, a virtual screening strategy was applied across several curated compound libraries, including FDA-approved drugs, NPASS (Natural Products Atlas),23 subsets of the ZINC database,44 and Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) compounds (https://tcmbank.cn/)24 and Ambinter (https://www.ambinter.com/). These diverse chemical collections were selected to maximize structural diversity and chemical space coverage while maintaining drug-likeness potential.

Before prediction, all compounds were processed through the same cheminformatics pipeline used during model development to ensure feature consistency and compatibility with the optimized QSAR model. Molecular descriptors were computed using the same protocols and feature sets as the training phase. Descriptor matrices were standardized by aligning their feature spaces to the trained model through zero-filling of missing features and reordering of columns to match the input schema employed during QSAR development precisely. This ensured that all descriptors were correctly positioned relative to the model's learned feature weights, preventing input bias during prediction.

The optimized QSAR model was then applied to each compound in the screening datasets to predict their inhibitory potency, expressed as pIC50 values. A cutoff threshold of pIC50 > 5.0, corresponding approximately to IC50 < 10 μM, was applied to identify compounds with potential biological activity against PIM2 kinase.45 This threshold reflects a commonly accepted benchmark for moderate potency during the early stages of drug discovery. Compounds meeting this criterion were prioritized for further evaluation, and the top-scoring candidates from each library were selected for downstream molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations to validate their binding affinities and interaction stability within the PIM2 active site.

Molecular docking

The compounds prioritized from the virtual screening campaign were subjected to molecular docking to predict their binding affinity and interaction patterns with the PIM2 kinase active site. The crystal structure of human PIM2 kinase in complex with compound 1 s was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 4X7Q) and used as the receptor structure for molecular docking. This structure was selected because it represents an experimentally resolved PIM2–inhibitor complex reported in the structure-based optimization study by Ishchenko et al..46 The presence of the co-crystallized ligand provided a reliable experimental reference for defining the inhibitor-binding pocket, docking-grid placement, and interaction analysis. Therefore, the ligand-bound 4X7Q conformation was considered suitable for docking the candidate PIM2 inhibitor. The three-dimensional structure of human PIM2 kinase was prepared using AutoDock Tools (v1.5.7) by removing crystallographic water molecules, adding missing hydrogens, assigning Gasteiger charges, and optimizing bond geometries. Energy minimization was performed using the MMFF94 force field to relieve steric clashes and stabilize the overall structure.47 Ligand molecules selected from the top-ranked QSAR-predicted hits were prepared using RDKit and OpenBabel to ensure correct stereochemistry, tautomeric forms, and protonation states at physiological pH. They were energy-minimized using the MMFF94 force field with a convergence criterion of 0.001 kcal/mol·Å.

Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina (v1.2.5), focusing on the ATP-binding pocket of PIM2 kinase as the target binding site.48 The docking grid was defined based on key catalytic residues within the active site, ensuring comprehensive coverage of the binding cavity. An exhaustiveness parameter of 8 and an energy range of 3 kcal/mol were used for flexible ligand docking. For each compound, ten docking poses were generated and ranked based on their predicted binding free energies (ΔG). The pose with the lowest binding energy and correct orientation within the active site was selected as the representative docking conformation.

To validate the docking protocol, the co-crystallized ligand (compound 1 s) from PDB ID: 4X7Q was redocked into the ATP-binding site using the same docking parameters. The redocked pose closely matched the experimental conformation, with an RMSD of 0.033 Å, confirming the reliability of the docking setup (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Protein–ligand interaction profiles were further analyzed using the Protein–Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP) to identify the complete non-covalent interaction pattern of each docked complex, including hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, salt bridges/electrostatic interactions, halogen bonds, and π-cation interactions.49 Furthermore, PyMOL (v2.5) and Discovery Studio Visualizer were used for structural visualization and figure preparation. The top-ranked protein–ligand complexes from docking were then used as input for molecular dynamics simulations to investigate their structural stability and conformational dynamics under near-physiological conditions.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were carried out using the AMBER 22 software package to evaluate the dynamic stability, conformational flexibility, and binding energetics of the top-scoring docked complexes.50 The ff14SB force field was used to parameterize the protein,25 while ligand topologies were generated using the general AMBER force field (GAFF2),51 and atomic partial charges were assigned using the AM1-BCC method in Antechamber.52 Each docked complex was solvated in an explicit TIP3P water box extending 12 Å beyond the protein surface in all directions, and counterions were added to neutralize the system, with NaCl concentration set to 0.15 M to mimic physiological ionic strength.53

Energy minimization was performed in two stages to relax unfavorable stereotypes of steric clashes and optimize the system geometry. In the first stage, 5000 steps of steepest descent minimization were performed with positional restraints of 10 kcal/mol·Å2 applied to the protein backbone, followed by 5000 steps of conjugate gradient minimization without restraints. The minimized system was then equilibrated into two phases. The first phase involved a 500 ps NVT ensemble simulation to gradually increase the temperature from 0 K to 300 K using the Langevin thermostat, and the second phase consisted of a 500 ps NPT ensemble simulation to stabilize the system pressure at 1 atm using the Berendsen barostat.

Following equilibration, a 200 ns production MD simulation was performed using a 2-fs integration time step under periodic boundary conditions. All covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the SHAKE algorithm, and long-range electrostatics were computed using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method with a cutoff distance of 10 Å for both van der Waals and Coulombic interactions. System coordinates and energies were saved every 10 ps for trajectory analysis.

Post-simulation analyses were conducted using the CPPTRAJ module in AMBER to examine the structural stability and conformational dynamics of the protein–ligand complexes. Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) values were calculated to assess the overall stability of the protein backbone and ligand throughout the simulation, while root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) values quantified residue-level flexibility.54 The radius of gyration (Rg) was evaluated to monitor changes in protein compactness during the simulation.54 Hydrogen bond analysis was performed to identify persistent intermolecular interactions, and the molecular mechanics Poisson–Boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) method implemented in MMPBSA.py was used to estimate binding free energies.55 Visualization of the simulation trajectories and structural transitions was performed using PyMOL and VMD to interpret key molecular interactions and conformational dynamics.

Free energy landscape (FEL) analysis was performed to further characterize the conformational stability and dominant energetic states of the selected PIM2–ligand complexes during MD simulation. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) obtained from essential dynamics analysis were used as reaction coordinates.56 The relative Gibbs free energy was estimated from the probability distribution of sampled conformations using the equation G = −kBTlnP, where kB is the Boltzmann constant, T is the simulation temperature, and P is the probability density of each conformational state.56, 57 The resulting FEL maps were used to identify dominant low-energy conformational basins and to compare the conformational stability of the simulated complexes.58

Prediction of pharmacokinetic and toxicological properties

The pharmacokinetic and safety parameters of the prioritized compounds were evaluated using the Deep-PK web platform (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/deeppk/prediction).59 Deep-PK applies deep-learning models trained on experimentally validated datasets to estimate key ADMET parameters, including absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity. Absorption was evaluated using simulated permeability through Caco-2 and MDCK cell models, as well as predictions of intestinal absorption and oral bioavailability. Distribution properties were assessed by blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability and volume of distribution, while metabolic behavior was analyzed through predicted inhibition and substrate potential for major cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes. Clearance rate and half-life were used to estimate excretory performance, and toxicity models included mutagenicity (AMES test), hepatotoxicity, and carcinogenicity. All results were generated at the default confidence settings of Deep-PK and classified into favorable or unfavorable categories to provide a rapid in silico assessment of each compound's drug-likeness and safety profile.

Results

Data preprocessing

From ChEMBL, programmatic retrieval for the human PIM2 target returned 5953 compound records with associated SMILES and activity annotations. After removing duplicates, entries lacking canonical SMILES or standard activity values, and structures that failed RDKit sanitization, a curated working set of 1918 compounds was retained. Subsequent standardization (identifier harmonization and unit checks) yielded the final pre-modeling dataset containing 1584 chemically valid compounds for exploratory analysis and downstream descriptor preparation.

To correct the imbalance while preserving chemical interpretability, we applied SMOTE in multiclass mode to the numerical descriptor matrix, using the default sampling strategy that upsamples minority classes to the majority class size. Given the majority class contained 1404 actives, the balanced training set therefore contained 1404 samples per class, active 1404, intermediate 1404, and inactive 1404, for a total of 4212 training instances. Each synthetic vector was mapped back to its nearest real compound within the same class in descriptor space so that every resampled row carried a valid ChEMBL ID and canonical SMILES, yielding a chemically coherent, class-balanced dataset for downstream descriptor processing. For the final binary QSAR model construction, an additional fingerprint-level filtering step was applied to improve class separation: compounds with pIC₅₀ ≥ 7.0 were labeled as active, compounds with pIC₅₀ ≤ 5.5 were labeled as non-active, and compounds with 5.5 < pIC₅₀ < 7.0 were excluded from binary model training to reduce boundary ambiguity.

Machine learning models construction and optimization

A total of 60 model–descriptor combinations were generated by combining five machine learning algorithms, including Random Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Support Vector Regression (SVR), k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), with twelve molecular fingerprint descriptors. These combinations were evaluated across training, independent testing, and ten-fold cross-validation datasets, resulting in 180 performance assessments. Because the objective of this study was to predict continuous pIC₅₀ values, model performance was evaluated using regression-specific metrics, including the coefficient of determination (R2), root-mean-square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and model robustness indicators such as Average RM2 and ΔRM2.

R2 was used to quantify the proportion of variance in experimental pIC₅₀ values explained by the model, whereas RMSE and MAE were used to measure prediction error. Average RM2 and ΔRM2 were used to assess model robustness and consistency between calibration and external prediction performance, with smaller ΔRM2 values indicating improved stability and reduced susceptibility to overfitting. Detailed heatmap-based comparisons of model performance across algorithms, descriptors, and validation datasets are provided in Supplementary Figs. S2–S5, including R2 (Supplementary Fig. S2), RMSE (Supplementary Fig. S3), RM2 (Supplementary Fig. S4), and reverse RM2 (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Since predictive performance estimated from a single metric or a single data partition can be misleading, a normalized composite scoring approach was adopted to integrate goodness-of-fit, prediction error, and model stability into a single interpretable ranking index. For each model–descriptor combination, the mean R2, RMSE, MAE, Average RM2, and ΔRM2 values were first computed across all three evaluation settings. These metrics were normalized using min–max scaling, where R2 and Average RM2 were treated as benefit metrics, while RMSE, MAE, and ΔRM2 were treated as cost metrics. Molecular docking and MD simulation

To evaluate the structural plausibility and dynamic stability of the top-ranked virtual hits, molecular docking followed by all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations was performed. Docking provided insight into binding poses, intermolecular interactions, and preliminary binding affinities, while MD simulations probed the temporal stability and conformational behavior of ligand–protein complexes under near-physiological conditions.

Residue-level flexibility analysis using RMSF

To assess the impact of ligand binding on the structural dynamics of PIM2 kinase at the residue level, Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) analyses were conducted with over 200 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation trajectories for the reference inhibitor and five QSAR-identified hits. Fig. 4 (a–f) presents the RMSF profiles for all systems, quantifying the average positional deviations of each residue's Cα atom from its mean position during the simulation. Lower RMSF values indicate restricted atomic fluctuations and enhanced local rigidity, typically associated with stabilizing ligand–protein interactions. The reference system (Fig. 4a) displayed moderate flexibility throughout the structure, with pronounced fluctuations (>20 Å) observed at the N- and C-terminal regions, a common trait for unstructured terminal loops. Core residues spanning the ATP-binding pocket exhibited moderate flexibility (∼10–15 Å), indicating stable but dynamic interactions with the native ligand. In contrast, the RMSF profiles of the ligand-bound systems revealed varied degrees of flexibility modulation. TCMBANKIN000009 (Fig. 4b) induced a global reduction in residue fluctuations across the protein backbone, particularly within residues 40–120, encompassing segments of the β-sheet core and hinge region. This suggests strong, stabilizing interactions within the active site. Similarly, Amb28533044 (Fig. 4c) and NPC262768 (Fig. 4e) exhibited substantial rigidity across the central domain (residues 50–200), with RMSF values consistently below 12 Å, highlighting their potential for tight binding and reduced conformational entropy. NPC170963 (Fig. 4d) showed moderate flexibility across residues 60–160, although a localized peak near residue 250 suggests partial destabilization in the solvent-exposed region, possibly due to surface-exposed ligand moieties. Interestingly, NPC469603 (Fig. 4f) retained elevated fluctuations near the C-terminal (residues 270–310), yet significantly stabilized residues around the ATP-binding site, implying a unique binding mode that favors partial structural rigidity and dynamic adaptability at the termini. Notably, across all systems, conserved catalytic residues such as Lys62, Asp125, Asp128, and Glu168 displayed reduced RMSF values when bound to the predicted inhibitors, reinforcing their central role in mediating stable ligand engagement. Among the test compounds, NPC262768 and TCMBANKIN000009 consistently demonstrated the lowest fluctuation amplitudes within the functional core, suggesting superior stabilization of the active site architecture and potentially higher binding affinity and residence time. Overall, the RMSF data complement docking and MM/PBSA results by confirming that top-ranked ligands not only occupy the catalytic pocket effectively but also dampen the flexibility of key functional residues, thereby supporting their candidature as potent PIM2 inhibitors. Compactness assessment via radius of gyration (Rg)

To evaluate the conformational compactness and structural integrity of PIM2–ligand complexes during molecular dynamics simulations, the radius of gyration (Rg) was monitored over a 200 ns timescale. Rg reflects the overall distribution of atomic mass around the center of mass and is widely used as a metric to assess the folding state, stability, and global fluctuations of protein–ligand complexes. Fig. 5 presents the Rg profiles for the reference system and each of the five docked ligands. The reference complex (Fig. 5a) displayed noticeable fluctuations in Rg, rising from approximately 4 Å to over 11 Å by the end of the simulation. This substantial increase suggests a degree of unfolding or global structural relaxation in the absence of stabilizing interactions beyond the core catalytic site. In contrast, NPC262768 (Fig. 5e) maintained a remarkably consistent Rg profile, stabilizing between 6 and 7.5 Å throughout the simulation. This indicates a compact and stable protein architecture, likely due to the extensive interactions observed with hinge and distal catalytic residues, including Asp183 and Glu168. TCMBANKIN000009 (Fig. 5b) showed a steady but modest increase in Rg from 3 Å to around 8 Å, suggesting intermediate flexibility while maintaining structural cohesion. NPC469603 (Fig. 5f) also demonstrated a similar Rg trend, rising gradually from 3 Å to ∼9 Å, which may be attributed to its extended binding mode that partially engages solvent-exposed residues. Notably, Amb28533044 (Fig. 5c) and NPC170963 (Fig. 5d) exhibited the most significant Rg increases, both exceeding 10 Å by the end of the simulation. This implies comparatively lower conformational stability, possibly due to limited interactions anchoring the ligand within the catalytic cleft or partial disengagement during simulation. Overall, Rg trends across all systems are consistent with the interaction complexity observed in docking and RMSF analyses. Compounds such as NPC262768 and TCMBANKIN000009, which formed multiple hydrogen bonds and engaged both hinge and catalytic site residues, yielded the most stable and compact complexes. In contrast, ligands with fewer stabilizing contacts showed greater global flexibility and higher Rg values. Ligand-induced changes in solvent accessibility of PIM2

To evaluate the solvent-exposed surface dynamics and folding behavior of PIM2 in response to ligand binding, solvent accessible surface area (SASA) was computed over 200 ns molecular dynamics simulations for each complex. SASA quantifies the surface area of the protein accessible to solvent molecules and is closely associated with conformational rearrangements, ligand-induced compactness, and protein hydration shell behavior. Fig. 6 presents the time-resolved SASA profiles for the reference and the top five virtual screening hits. The reference complex (Fig. 6a) exhibited a progressive increase in SASA, rising from ∼16,000 Å2 at 0 ns to over 20,000 Å2 by 200 ns. This trend indicates a gradual unfolding or expansion of the protein surface, potentially due to limited long-range stabilization offered by the native ligand. In contrast, several screened hits, particularly NPC262768 (Fig. 6e) and TCMBANKIN000009 (Fig. 6b), maintained relatively stable SASA values in the range of 17,500–18,500 Å2, suggesting compactness and limited solvent exposure throughout the simulation. The restrained SASA variability in these systems further reinforces their structural stabilization observed via Rg and RMSF analyses. Amb28533044 (Fig. 6c) and NPC170963 (Fig. 6d) showed moderate increases in SASA, stabilizing between 18,000–19,500 Å2. These intermediate fluctuations are consistent with partial surface relaxation, likely driven by moderate interaction strength and less extensive anchoring across the catalytic cleft. Meanwhile, NPC469603 (Fig. 6f) initially demonstrated a rapid SASA rise to ∼20,000 Å2 by 80 ns, followed by mild fluctuations, indicating an early-stage surface expansion possibly due to the extended ligand conformation that interacts with solvent-facing residues. Overall, SASA trends across all systems reveal that NPC262768 and TCMBANKIN000009 provide greater surface stability and limit conformational exposure, consistent with their favorable binding profiles. Compounds that stabilize the core without causing substantial unfolding or surface relaxation are likely to confer better thermodynamic stability, lower desolvation penalties, and more favorable binding energetics. Discussion

The present study demonstrates the effectiveness of integrating machine learning–based QSAR modeling, structure-based docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and pharmacokinetic prediction for the identification of potential PIM2 kinase inhibitors from chemically diverse compound libraries. By combining data-driven predictive modeling with structural and dynamic analyses, this workflow provides a comprehensive strategy for prioritizing candidate molecules for experimental validation. Similar integrative computational pipelines have increasingly been adopted in modern drug discovery to accelerate early-stage hit identification while reducing experimental screening costs.60, 61

The use of a composite score helped avoid overinterpretation of any single validation metric. This is important because a model with high R2 may still show larger prediction errors or weaker external stability, whereas error-based metrics alone do not fully describe explained variance or robustness. Therefore, combining normalized goodness-of-fit, error, and stability measures provided a more balanced basis for selecting the Random Forest FingerPrinter model for downstream virtual screening. This interpretation is consistent with QSAR best-practice recommendations that emphasize internal validation, external predictivity, applicability, and multi-parameter model assessment.16, 40, 41, 42

Among the evaluated machine learning algorithms, Random Forest combined with molecular fingerprint descriptors demonstrated the most robust predictive performance across training, testing, and cross-validation datasets. Although certain XGBoost models achieved slightly higher training R2 values, their increased RMSE, MAE, and ΔRM2 values on independent datasets indicated reduced generalizability. Ensemble learning methods such as Random Forest are well known for their robustness in high-dimensional descriptor spaces because bootstrap aggregation and random feature selection reduce model variance and mitigate overfitting.34 Previous QSAR studies have similarly reported that Random Forest models frequently outperform other machine learning approaches when modeling bioactivity datasets with heterogeneous structural descriptors.62, 63 Our findings, therefore, support the continued applicability of ensemble tree-based models for predictive modeling in cheminformatics.

Another key observation was the superior performance of fingerprint-based descriptors compared with other descriptor classes. Molecular fingerprints encode structural fragments and topological patterns that capture essential features governing molecular recognition, including pharmacophoric motifs, ring systems, and functional group connectivity. Numerous studies have shown that fingerprint representations are highly effective for modeling biological activity because they preserve substructural information relevant to ligand–protein interactions while remaining computationally efficient for large datasets.33, 64 The strong predictive performance observed in the present study further confirms the importance of structural fingerprints for QSAR modeling of kinase inhibitor datasets.

The structural interpretation of predicted inhibitors was further supported by molecular docking analyses, which revealed consistent interactions between the prioritized compounds and residues located within the ATP-binding pocket of PIM2 kinase. Notably, several compounds formed interactions with residues such as Lys62, Asp125, Asp128, and Glu168, which are known to play important roles in ligand stabilization within the catalytic pocket. Structural studies of PIM kinases have highlighted the importance of hinge-region interactions and catalytic loop contacts in determining inhibitor binding orientation and potency.65 In particular, interactions involving acidic residues within the catalytic pocket have been associated with enhanced ligand anchoring and improved binding affinity in previously reported PIM kinase inhibitors.66 In addition, hydrophobic contacts with residues such as Leu39, Val47, Phe127, and Ile182 suggest that hydrophobic packing may also contribute to stable ligand accommodation within the PIM2 binding pocket. The docking poses observed in this study are therefore consistent with known structural determinants of PIM kinase inhibition.

While docking provides valuable insights into ligand binding modes, it represents a static approximation of protein–ligand interactions. To evaluate the dynamic stability of predicted complexes under physiologically relevant conditions, molecular dynamics simulations were performed. RMSF analysis revealed that several compounds, particularly NPC262768 and TCMBANKIN000009, reduced residue-level fluctuations within the catalytic region of PIM2, suggesting stabilization of the active site architecture. Reduced flexibility of catalytic residues upon ligand binding has been widely associated with increased binding affinity and improved complex stability.54, 67, 68 Complementary analyses of radius of gyration and solvent-accessible surface area further indicated that these ligands maintained a compact protein conformation throughout the simulation, suggesting that they contribute to structural stabilization of the kinase domain.

Hydrogen bond analysis also provided insights into ligand–protein interaction stability. Persistent hydrogen bonds observed in complexes containing NPC170963 and NPC469603 suggest strong polar interactions that may enhance ligand retention within the catalytic pocket. Hydrogen bonding plays a critical role in determining binding affinity and specificity in kinase inhibitors, particularly when interactions occur within the hinge region or catalytic loop.69 In addition, principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that several predicted inhibitors restricted large-scale conformational motions of the PIM2 kinase backbone, indicating stronger conformational locking compared with the reference ligand. Such restriction of protein dynamics is often associated with longer ligand residence times and improved inhibitory potency.54 Consistently, FEL analysis using PC1 and PC2 as reaction coordinates showed low-energy conformational basins for the ligand-bound complexes. The more restricted low-energy regions observed for TCMBANKIN000009 and NPC262768 further supported their stabilizing effects on PIM2 and strengthened their prioritization for experimental validation.56, 57

Beyond binding affinity and structural stability, the pharmacokinetic suitability of candidate compounds represents an important consideration in early drug discovery. In this study, Deep-PK predictions indicated that several prioritized compounds possess favorable ADMET properties, including acceptable absorption profiles and low predicted toxicity. Deep learning–based ADMET prediction tools have recently gained attention because they can capture complex nonlinear relationships between chemical structure and pharmacokinetic behavior, providing more accurate predictions than traditional rule-based methods.59 Although such predictions remain computational approximations, they provide a useful preliminary filter before experimental testing.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although SMOTE was used to address class imbalance in the training dataset, synthetic oversampling may introduce artificial patterns that do not fully reflect the true chemical distribution of minority classes. Second, the QSAR models rely primarily on two-dimensional molecular descriptors, which may not fully capture three-dimensional conformational effects influencing protein binding. Recent studies have suggested that integrating graph neural networks or 3D descriptors can further improve predictive accuracy in drug discovery pipelines.60 Third, the docking and MD simulations provide computational evidence of potential binding but cannot fully substitute for experimental validation. Therefore, biochemical kinase inhibition assays and cell-based experiments will be required to confirm the predicted activity of the prioritized compounds.

Overall, the results highlight the value of combining machine learning–based QSAR modeling with structure-based and dynamics-based validation approaches for the discovery of kinase inhibitors. The Random Forest–fingerprint model developed in this study demonstrated strong predictive performance and successfully guided the identification of candidate PIM2 inhibitors from diverse chemical libraries. The structural and dynamic analyses further confirmed that several predicted compounds can stabilize the catalytic pocket of PIM2 kinase and maintain favorable interaction networks during simulation. These findings provide a promising starting point for future experimental validation and medicinal chemistry optimization aimed at developing novel PIM2-targeted therapeutics.

Despite the promising results obtained from this integrated computational approach, several limitations should be considered. The QSAR models were developed using bioactivity data aggregated from the ChEMBL database, which may contain variability arising from different experimental conditions and assay protocols. Although SMOTE helped reduce bias caused by the highly imbalanced activity distribution, its use in cheminformatics requires caution. SMOTE operates by interpolating between minority-class samples in descriptor space and therefore may generate synthetic vectors that do not directly correspond to chemically realistic structures. The nearest-neighbor remapping strategy used in this study minimized this issue by linking each synthetic vector back to the closest real compound within the same activity class, thereby preserving valid molecular identifiers and SMILES strings. However, this approach cannot eliminate the risk of local oversampling bias or artificial expansion of sparsely represented chemical regions. For this reason, the balanced dataset was used only for model training, while model selection emphasized test-set and cross-validation performance to reduce overinterpretation of training-set improvements. Additionally, the QSAR models primarily relied on two-dimensional molecular fingerprints, which encode molecular topology and substructural patterns but do not fully capture three-dimensional conformation, stereochemistry, ligand flexibility, or protein-induced binding effects. Therefore, future models incorporating 3D descriptors, conformer-based features, or graph-based molecular representations may improve predictive reliability. Furthermore, molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations provide theoretical approximations of binding behavior and may not fully reproduce complex biological environments. Docking scores should be interpreted cautiously because commonly used scoring functions estimate binding affinity using simplified energetic terms and may not reliably distinguish true binders from false positives in all chemical series.70 Although docking was followed by MD simulation to evaluate dynamic stability, conventional MD simulations remain limited by accessible simulation timescales and may not capture rare conformational transitions, ligand unbinding events, or slow rearrangements that could be better explored using enhanced sampling approaches. Finally, the pharmacokinetic and toxicity profiles predicted using in silico tools require experimental validation. Most importantly, the inhibitory activities of the prioritized compounds have not yet been confirmed using biochemical PIM2 kinase assays or cellular IC50 measurements. Therefore, NPC262768, TCMBANKIN000009, and other prioritized candidates should be considered preliminary computational hits that require experimental IC50 determination, selectivity profiling against other PIM isoforms and kinases, and cell-based anticancer validation before being advanced as confirmed PIM2 inhibitors.

Conclusion

In this study, an integrated computational pipeline combining QSAR modeling, virtual screening, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and ADMET prediction was developed to identify potential PIM2 kinase inhibitors. The Random Forest–based QSAR model using molecular fingerprints demonstrated strong predictive performance and successfully guided the identification of several promising candidate compounds. Structural and dynamic analyses further supported the stability of these ligands within the PIM2 catalytic pocket. Among the prioritized candidates, NPC262768 and TCMBANKIN000009 showed the most consistent dynamic stability and should be prioritized for further biochemical IC50 validation, kinase selectivity profiling, and cell-based anticancer evaluation. These findings provide valuable leads for future experimental validation and optimization toward the development of novel PIM2-targeted therapeutics.
