Social isolation risks and preventive strategies for residents in long-term care facilities in China: an interview-based study with staff in facilities
Authors: Weitong Li, Meng Wang, Wenjing Tu, Yayi Zhao, Yulei Song, Wei Yang, Ziqi Mei, Zichun Ding, Guihua Xu, Yamei Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial isolation is a significant challenge for residents in long-term care facilities. This study investigates the risk factors and predictive indicators of social isolation in these settings, aiming to enhance social connections and improve well-being.MethodsThis was a qualitative study. In-depth semi-structured interviews were conducted with participants from 12 long-term care facilities, and the data were analyzed using NVivo 10 software. Inductive analysis was used to identify key themes, which led to the development of a social isolation risk warning model and proposed prevention strategies.ResultsA total of 23 participants were interviewed, including 14 managers, 4 head nurses, 2 qualified nurses, and 3 healthcare assistants. In the risk warning model, social isolation risks were categorized into three domains: (1) Social isolation risks and influencing factors: Participants described limited social relationships, restricted social participation, and emotional distress as key risks, further compounded by insufficient family and peer support and weak social interaction skills. (2) Risk consequences and early warning interventions: Social isolation was perceived to contribute to declines in mental well-being and quality of life. Participants emphasized the need for risk assessment, focusing on anxiety、depression and emotional support level. They also highlighted training/education, including theoretical study and contingency plans, and suggested preventive measures such as family members’ support and organizing group activities to foster social inclusion. (3) Challenges and solutions: Participants identified concerns about caregiver quality and scarce geriatric care resources as key barriers. They suggested human resource development and national policy support to strengthen workforce capacity and institutional care services.ConclusionsSocial isolation risk assessment plays a pivotal intermediate role in risk prevention. Evaluating relevant risk factors, comprehensive preventive measures could be developed and implemented to reduce the incidence of social isolation. This approach not only improves the psychological well-being of older adults but also enhances the safety and overall quality of care within long-term care facilities.Clinical trial numberSQ2024049. Introduction

With the rapid aging of populations, social isolation has become a critical issue for residents in long-term care facilities (LTCFs). Studies indicate that approximately 5.8% of older adults in Japan, 18.3% in South Korea, and 27.5% in urban China experience social isolation, which is associated with adverse physical and mental health outcomes [1–3]. Although social isolation is a prevalent issue in LTCFs worldwide, its causes and management strategies vary significantly across regions. In East Asia, institutional constraints play a major role in shaping social isolation experiences in LTCFs. China’s LTCFs are highly regulated, with strict institutional policies that often limit residents’ external interactions and autonomy [4]. While Japan and South Korea face similar demographic challenges, they have increasingly incorporated AI-assisted monitoring and robotic companionship as part of their intervention strategies [5, 6]. By contrast, Western LTCFs place greater emphasis on social engagement through community-based programs, structured social activities, and volunteer participation, aiming to integrate older adults into broader social networks. Despite these regional differences, social isolation remains a widespread issue in East Asian LTCFs. As traditional family caregiving models decline, increasing numbers of residents are transitioning into LTCFs, a trend similarly observed in Japan and South Korea. However, despite these shared demographic patterns, there remains a lack of research examining the intersection of institutional policies, caregiving constraints, and social isolation in China’s LTCFs [7].

Within LTCFs, several institutional factors further exacerbate social isolation among residents [8, 9]. At the facility level, strict policies governing time and space limit older adults’ daily activities, reducing their autonomy and opportunities for external social engagement [10]. Moreover, administrative priorities often overshadow the individualized physical and psychological needs of residents, resulting in inadequate environmental and service support [11]. These institutional restrictions collectively restrict older adults’ ability to make personal decisions, limits participation in external social activities, and forces their social networks to diminish. At the level of caregiving staff, due to understaffing and heavy workloads, most of their time and energy are dedicated to meeting the basic physical needs of older adults, including assistance with daily living and medical care. Consequently, there is a significant deficit in both resources and systemic support, which are necessary to cater to the emotional well-being and social engagement needs of older adults. This prolonged neglect not only limits opportunities for meaningful social interactions but also exacerbates feelings of social isolation, further affecting their overall well-being [9, 12]. In summary, these factors contribute to significant social isolation among residents in LTCFs, leading to profound adverse outcomes.

Studies have demonstrated that social isolation markedly elevates the risk of depression [13], cognitive decline [14], cardiovascular diseases [15, 16], multiple chronic conditions [17] and even mortality [18] among older adults, establishing it as a critical determinant of their physical and mental health. Hence, it is imperative to conduct early assessments of social isolation risks faced by residents in LTCFs to effectively mitigate these negative impacts and improve their quality of life. The analysis and evaluation of social isolation risk factors, along with an increased awareness of risk prediction and the reduction of these risks, are essential for improving the quality and safety of care in LTCFs [19, 20]. Social isolation risk management has emerged as a vital approach in geriatric care, emphasizing the importance of early identification of potential risk factors before they escalate into more serious physical and mental health issues [21]. This proactive approach involves the continuous identification, evaluation, and mitigation of social isolation risks, ensuring that older adults remain emotionally and psychologically supported.

NJ Donovan [22] have demonstrated that early identification and seamless management of social isolation risks could enhance the overall quality of care. By addressing these risks early, LTCFs could lower the incidence of social isolation, alleviate negative emotional states in older adults, and improve the relationships and satisfaction levels between caregivers and older adults. Through the analysis of risk factors and the implementation of early warning systems, facilities could ensure the safe operation of care services, ultimately leading to better health outcomes for residents. By implementing early detection and intervention strategies, such as regular assessments and personalized care plans, safer and more supportive environments could be created, improving the overall quality of life for older adults. However, there is still a lack of comprehensive understanding of the specific factors contributing to social isolation in China’s long-term care environment. Despite increasing awareness of the issue, interventions specifically targeting the social and emotional needs of residents remain limited.

This study aims to explore the risks of social isolation and their influencing factors in LTCFs, with a particular focus on developing a social isolation risk warning model. Semi-structured interviews were conducted to identify existing risks, underlying causes, and potential solutions. By developing this model, the study aims to enhance the understanding of social isolation risks and provide a basis for effective preventive strategies. Ultimately, this will contribute to fostering a culture of safety, improving care quality, and enhancing the well-being of residents in China’s LTCFs.

Methods

Study design

This study is a qualitative descriptive study to explore the issue of social isolation among residents in LTCFs. In-depth semi-structured interviews were conducted with managers, head nurses, registered nurses, and nursing assistants working in LTCFs. Based on a systematic analysis of the interview data, a risk warning model for social isolation was developed, aimed at identifying key risks, assessing potential impacts, and providing theoretical support for intervention measures and institutional practices, ultimately reducing the risk of social isolation among older residents in LTCFs.

Setting and sample

Research subjects at LTCFs in Nanjing, China (nursing homes and the Geriatrics department of a hospital) were contacted via invitation letters and phone calls. We approached 17 LTCFs that had expressed a preliminary interest in participating in our research program. Two nursing homes either did not respond to the researchers or declined due to understaffing, sickness within the management team, and recent participation in research activities. Three other nursing homes were not included in the study after theoretically adequate data was available. Ultimately, 23 participants (14 managers, 4 head nurses, 2 qualified nurses, and 3 healthcare assistants) from 12 LTCFs were interviewed and the data were analyzed (Fig. 1). From qualitative analysis, the data obtained from the 23 staff members proved to be adequately rich to reach theoretical saturation, with additional interview responses not yielding any further unique perspectives. The respondents were anonymously represented by P and number [23].

Inclusion and exclusion process of interviewees

Data collection

A combination of convenience sampling and theoretical sampling was used to obtain the final sample of 23 participants. Convenience sampling was used at the outset of the study. Theoretical sampling was used after a conceptual model was established. The researchers contacted the interviewees, and an appointment was made for the visit. The sample size was based on theoretical saturation which was assessed using the criteria proposed by Francis JJ [24], whereby data collection could cease when no additional codes emerge from three consecutive interviews following the analysis of at least ten interviews.

The researchers introduced the purpose and general content of the study to the interviewees and then performed face-to-face interviews at specified quiet spaces according to the predetermined interview outline (Table 1). Each interview was approximately 45 min in duration and conducted by the same research. The interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and proofread. The interview was analyzed by combining the audio recording with the researchers’ on-site record.

Data analysis

The data were managed and analyzed using NVivo 10. The transcripts were read and initially coded inductively and then deductively, and specific themes and codes were categorized. The data were analyzed inductively to ensure important themes were not lost due to deductive data analysis, and until no new themes were identified during inductive analysis. All transcripts were analyzed by one author and independently reviewed by another author to ensure the reliability of the coding. Any discrepancies were discussed and resolved, and the content of each code was discussed with all authors.

Rigor and trustworthiness

In the collected qualitative data process, the researchers reading all the participants’ answers to the same question first and then analyzed the data item by item, to assure the trustworthiness. In the data analyzed process, open codes that emerged as new themes were added to the coding framework in the thematic coding. The researchers then followed normal coding procedures, underlining key words or phrases and making marginal notes that highlight key elements in each response. Coding and qualitative data analysis were conducted by using software NVivo 10. After collecting an interview data, the data were analyzed immediately. To determine dependability, there was more than one researcher coded the data. All transcripts were analyzed by one author and independently reviewed by another author to ensure the reliability of the coding. Any discrepancies were discussed and resolved, and the content of each code was discussed with all authors. At the end of the coding process, all interviews were reread with the final codebook to ensure completeness. The transcripts were read and initially coded inductively and then deductively, and specific themes and codes were categorized. Data were sorted according to comparable criteria at first, keeping the categories large and then subdivided into smaller groups after they become “cluttered” and lengthy. Defining each category and providing a synthesizing statement about each category and subclass [25].

A risk warning model of social isolation in LTCFs has been developed, consisting of three key components, which is visually represented in Fig. 2. The first component is Identifying social isolation risks and factors, which includes social isolation risks and Influencing factors. The second component is Evaluating risk consequences and implementing early warning intervention measures, encompassing Consequences, Risk assessment, Training/education, and Preventive measures. The third and final component is Addressing challenges and proposing solutions, which focuses on Existing challenges/obstacles and Solutions. This comprehensive approach aims to improve care quality and enhance the well-being of residents in LTCFs.

Risk warning model of social isolation in long-term care facilities

Domain I: social isolation risks and influencing factors

Social isolation risks

Social relationships

Participants frequently mentioned that weakened social relationships, especially infrequent family visits and the loss of friendships, significantly contributed to social isolation among residents in LTCFs.

“Some older adults only see their families during the holidays. They often express, ‘It feels like we’ve been forgotten.’” (Healthcare Assistant 2, Female, Site 12).

“After moving into the facility, many lose touch with old friends. They’ve said, ‘I want to reconnect, but I don’t know how.’” (Manager 6, Male, Site 3).

Social participation

A lack of engaging social activities emerged as a notable contributor to social isolation among older adults in LTCFs. Staff noted that repetitive and monotonous activities quickly lead to disengagement and loss of interest, limiting opportunities for meaningful interaction.

“The same repetitive activities make older adults lose interest quickly.” (Manager 3, Female, Site 4).

“Mobility issues make it hard for some older adults to leave their rooms, further limiting their social participation.” (Head nurse 2, Female, Site 5).

Emotional state

Staff observed that loneliness and depression were common emotional outcomes of isolation, often manifesting as feelings of being unnoticed or unvalued by others.

“An older adult told me, ‘It feels like I’m invisible; no one even notices if I’m here.’” (Healthcare Assistant 1, Female, Site 1).

“Hopelessness is frequent. One individual said, ‘Why even bother trying? It doesn’t make a difference.’” (Manager 11, Female, Site 8).

Influencing factors

Lack of family support

Participants noted the emotional toll of limited family engagement, emphasizing its profound impact on the mental well-being of older adults.

“Some families only visit during emergencies. An older adult once said, ‘Why don’t they come anymore? Did I do something wrong?’” (Healthcare Assistant 3, Female, Site 3).

“Family visits are critical. Without them, many feel unconnected and unsupported.” (Manager 4, Female, Site 12).

Lack of friend support

Moving into care facilities often disrupts long-standing friendships, as older adults are separated from their familiar social circles and communities. This sudden change leaves many struggling to maintain or reestablish meaningful connections, with some expressing feelings of starting over socially in an unfamiliar environment.

“They’ve told us, ‘I miss the people I used to talk to; it feels like I’m starting over.’” (Qualified nurse 2, Female, Site 3).

“Not everyone feels confident enough to build new friendships, which worsens isolation.” (Manager 13, Male, Site 9).

Weak social interaction skills

Many older adults struggled to initiate conversations or participate in activities, often due to a lack of confidence or fear of rejection. This reluctance limited their opportunities for interaction and reinforced feelings of isolation, making it harder to rebuild social connections.

“Some want to join, but they’re afraid of being rejected or judged.” (Healthcare Assistant 2, Female, Site 5).

“Consistent encouragement is key, especially for those who are naturally reserved.” (Manager 4, Female, Site 12).

Lack of recreational amenities

Participants noted insufficient facilities for communal activities, highlighting that the lack of suitable spaces often prevented older adults from gathering and engaging socially. This limitation not only restricted opportunities for interaction but also hindered the organization of meaningful group activities, further exacerbating feelings of isolation.

“The lack of suitable spaces makes it hard for older adults to gather socially.” (Manager 12, Female, Site 11).

“Without proper amenities, it’s challenging to organize activities that everyone can enjoy.” (Manager 9, Female, Site 8).

Domain II: risk consequences and early warning intervention measures

Consequences

Decline in mental well-being

Social isolation was closely linked to worsening mental health, often leading to heightened anxiety, depression, and a diminished sense of purpose. Participants noted that this emotional decline reduced motivation to engage in activities, further perpetuating the cycle of isolation.

“One person told me, ‘I feel trapped in a loop of loneliness with no escape.’” (Healthcare Assistant 1, Female, Site 2).

“When they withdraw emotionally, it becomes harder to support them effectively.” (Qualified nurse 2, Female, Site 3).

Quality of life of older adults

Isolation diminished older adults’ participation in daily activities, as the lack of social interaction and emotional support eroded their motivation and engagement. This decline not only limited their involvement in meaningful routines but also contributed to a sense of purposelessness, ultimately leading to lower life satisfaction.

“An older adult said, ‘I used to enjoy painting, but now it feels pointless.’” (Manager 1, Female, Site 5).

“When they disengage, they stop caring about themselves, affecting their overall health.” (Qualified nurse 2, Female, Site 3).

Disease prognosis

Prolonged isolation delayed recovery and worsened health conditions by reducing adherence to treatment and diminishing motivation for health management, ultimately exacerbating existing issues and compromising well-being.

“One patient said, ‘What’s the point of taking medicine if no one cares?’” (Qualified nurse 2, Female, Site 3).

“Post-surgery patients often lack motivation without emotional support.” (Healthcare Assistant 2, Female, Site 2).

Cognitive decline

The absence of mental stimulation accelerated memory loss, as older adults lacked opportunities to engage in activities that challenge cognitive functions. This decline was often compounded by limited social interactions, which further reduced mental engagement and hastened the progression of cognitive deterioration.

“Someone told me, ‘I can’t even remember what day it is anymore.’” (Manager 13, Male, Site 7).

“Regular engagement helps retain cognitive abilities, but isolated individuals decline faster.” (Head nurse 4, Female, Site 6).

Loss of self-worth

Isolation eroded older adults’ confidence and sense of purpose, leading to withdrawal from activities and reduced engagement with others, further deepening their sense of disconnection and diminishing well-being.

“One older adult said, ‘I used to feel important, but now I just exist.’” (Healthcare Assistant 1, Female, Site 6).

“When they lose their sense of value, it impacts their willingness to engage in even basic activities.” (Qualified nurse 2, Female, Site 3).

Domain III: challenges and solutions

Existing challenges

The most significant challenges included staffing shortages and low awareness of social isolation risks, limiting efforts to provide emotional support and proactively address the issue.

“There aren’t enough staff to provide meaningful interactions. Everyone is stretched thin.” (Manager 3, Male, Site 4).

“Families often underestimate the emotional toll of isolation on older adults.” (Healthcare Assistant 3, Female, Site 3).

Solutions

Participants emphasized the need for training to enhance emotional care, policy support for resources, and improved facilities to foster social engagement among older adults.

“Training programs should focus on emotional support, not just physical care.” (Manager 2, Female, Site 6).

“Government funding is essential for hiring more staff and upgrading facilities.” (Manager 13, Male, Site 9).

Discussion

Through interviews with long-term care staff, a social isolation risk assessment model was developed to identify critical risk factors and consequences of social isolation among older adults, while offering targeted, personalized interventions. The model comprises three key components: identification of risks and factors, evaluation of consequences and implementation of interventions, and addressing challenges with proposed solutions.

Identify social isolation risks and factors

This study identifies and distinguishes the risk and the influencing factors of social isolation. The risk factors for social isolation include the lack of social relationships, reduced social participation, and the absence of emotional support. Among elderly residents in LTCFs, these risk factors directly increase the likelihood of experiencing social isolation. In China’s LTCFs, older adults often hide their emotional needs to avoid burdening their children or reducing family stress. Additionally, the locations of these institutions, often distant from urban centers, contribute to a lack of intimate emotional support, which is similar to the situation in many East Asian countries [26, 27]. These factors share common impacts across different cultural contexts, making interventions targeting these factors widely applicable in long-term care settings in East Asia and other developing countries. In contrast to direct risk factors, influencing factors affect the severity of social isolation in indirect ways. Factors such as insufficient family support, lack of friends, and weakened social interaction skills, although not directly causing social isolation, exacerbate its intensity. Particularly in developing countries, older adults often lack necessary social skills and emotional support due to health issues, economic hardship, or social isolation. For elderly residents in LTCFs, emotional isolation makes them more susceptible to feelings of helplessness and loneliness, thereby intensifying social isolation.

Evaluate consequences and implement intervention measures

The consequences of social isolation, risk assessment, education and training, and preventive measures form the core of the phase dedicated to evaluating consequences and implementing intervention measures. By exploring these measures in depth, we can more effectively mitigate the negative impacts of social isolation on the physical and mental health of older adults. The effects of social isolation on older adults are primarily reflected in three areas: mental well-being, quality of life, and disease prognosis. The decline in mental well-being is a significant consequence of social isolation. The lack of social interaction amplifies feelings of loneliness and emotional isolation, leading to psychological issues such as depression and anxiety, particularly in older adults within LTCFs. In East Asia, the cultural suppression of emotional expression makes feelings of loneliness even more pronounced, thereby exacerbating psychological distress [28]. Secondly, social isolation significantly reduces the quality of life of older adults, particularly in terms of life satisfaction. The loss of social interactions makes life monotonous and devoid of joy, further aggravating loneliness and depressive feelings. As social support diminishes, older adults’ motivation to engage in daily activities wanes, and positive emotions and satisfaction noticeably decrease. In the context of diminished family support and changes in social structures, many older adults face even more severe social isolation, leading to a further decline in life satisfaction. Finally, social isolation hinders the recovery process of older adults from illnesses. The lack of emotional support and social interaction reduces their motivation for rehabilitation, slowing down recovery. Social interaction not only improves mood but also promotes recovery by enhancing immunity. The absence of family companionship and social activities makes it difficult for older adults to actively participate in treatment, thus impacting their health recovery. On this basis, risk assessment becomes crucial. Evaluating the anxiety, depression symptoms, emotional support levels, and social participation ability of older adults can assist caregivers in early identification of social isolation risks. Anxiety and depression are often early signals of social isolation, and through these evaluations, caregivers can accurately identify the occurrence of social withdrawal and take timely and effective intervention measures. Additionally, assessing emotional support helps identify emotionally isolated older adults and provide them with necessary emotional assistance. Regular social isolation risk assessments help caregivers dynamically adjust care plans, effectively reducing the negative impact of social isolation. Education and training are key to enhancing caregivers’ ability to identify and intervene in social isolation. During the training process, theoretical study provides caregivers with a foundation on social isolation theories and intervention methods, enabling them to better understand the long-term impacts of social isolation on older adults’ health. Through contingency plan training, caregivers can adjust strategies flexibly according to the individual needs of elderly residents, ensuring effective intervention in various situations. Communication skills are also a core part of the training. Caregivers should learn how to establish positive interactions with older adults, use effective communication methods to provide emotional support, and guide them in participating in social activities, thereby helping reduce feelings of isolation and promoting social participation and emotional exchange. Finally, preventive measures play an indispensable role in the intervention of social isolation. LTCFs should regularly organize various social activities, such as group entertainment, interest groups, and outings, which provide older adults with opportunities to interact with others, effectively strengthening social connections and slowing down the process of social isolation. Family support also plays a critical role in alleviating social isolation. Regularly encouraging family members to participate in the daily care and social activities of older adults enhances their emotional support and promotes social participation. Utilizing modern communication technologies (such as video calls and phones) is also an effective preventive measure. Especially when face-to-face interactions are limited, technology can help older adults maintain contact with family and friends, alleviating loneliness, which is consistent with the findings of Célia’s scope review [29]. Through these comprehensive assessment and intervention measures, we can effectively slow down the negative effects of social isolation on the physical and mental health of older adults, enhancing their quality of life and well-being.

Address challenges and propose solutions

Despite the effective social isolation intervention strategies proposed in this study, LTCFs still face significant challenges, particularly in the context of East Asia and other developing countries. First, the quality and quantity of caregivers is a common issue, especially in resource-scarce regions. In China and other East Asian countries, with the increasing aging population, the demand for elderly care is gradually rising [30]. However, the shortage of caregivers and their low professional level prevents LTCFs from providing adequate emotional support and social activities for older adults, directly exacerbating the issue of social isolation. The lack of high-quality caregivers, particularly professionals who can identify social isolation risks and provide emotional support, makes it difficult for older adults to receive personalized care and effectively alleviate feelings of loneliness and psychological distress. Secondly, low risk awareness is another important issue faced by LTCFs. Many caregivers and managers focus only on the physical care of older adults, often neglecting their social needs, resulting in a lack of targeted interventions and effective social isolation risk management. This low risk awareness leads to the delayed identification and ineffective intervention of social isolation issues, which negatively impacts the quality of life of older adults. To address these challenges, this study proposes the following solutions: in terms of human resource development, it is essential to strengthen the professional training of caregivers, particularly in East Asia and developing countries, where caregivers should be trained to help older adults overcome social isolation issues according to their changing needs and capabilities based on different backgrounds and cultures. In addition, policy support is also crucial. Governments should introduce more detailed policies, increasing financial support for LTCFs, particularly in areas such as caregiver training, staffing, and improving care conditions. Through policy guidance, governments can encourage social forces to participate in elderly care services, thus alleviating the issue of resource scarcity. With these measures, LTCFs will be better equipped to provide older adults with emotional support and opportunities for social participation, effectively reducing the negative impacts of social isolation. This not only helps improve the quality of life and mental health of older adults but also provides a guarantee for building a healthier and more harmonious society, especially in the elderly care environment of East Asia and other developing countries, where it holds significant practical value.

Strengths and limitations

This study has both strengths and limitations. It provides a structured analysis of social isolation risks in LTCFs by incorporating diverse perspectives from managers, nurses, and healthcare assistants. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on the consequences of social isolation, this study develops a risk warning model tailored to the institutional and policy context of China, offering a systematic approach to early identification, assessment, and intervention. Additionally, the findings highlight key institutional challenges and propose targeted strategies, which may serve as a reference for improving elderly care both in China and in other countries facing similar demographic and caregiving constraints. However, several limitations should be considered. First, the study was conducted in LTCFs in Nanjing, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other regions with different cultural and regulatory frameworks. Future research should expand to a broader geographic scope to enhance applicability. Second, the study primarily focuses on staff perspectives and does not directly incorporate the lived experiences of residents in LTCFs, which may overlook certain aspects of social isolation. Further studies including elderly residents’ voices would provide a more comprehensive understanding. Third, the study was conducted in the post-COVID-19 period, a time when institutional policies and social dynamics may have been atypical. Future research should examine whether these findings hold in a long-term post-pandemic context.

Conclusion

There are various risk factors contributing to social isolation among residents in LTCFs, with social isolation risk assessment playing an important intermediary role in the prevention and control of adverse outcomes. Social isolation risk prevention and control could be guided by the early warning model developed for LTCFs, which includes risk factor identification, risk assessment, preventive measures, current challenges, and corresponding solutions. Based on the identified social isolation risk factors, comprehensive measures could be formulated and implemented, including policy support, enhancing multidisciplinary team cooperation, improving supervision, timely and dynamic risk assessment, prompt response to risk events, strengthening continuing education, and optimizing hardware and software facilities. These measures, collectively referred to as the early warning model, aim to reduce the incidence of social isolation risk and promote the safety and well-being of residents in LTCFs.
