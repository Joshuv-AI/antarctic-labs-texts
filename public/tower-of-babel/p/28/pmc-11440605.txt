Rigorous Models for Vapor Diffusion in Polymers with Immobilization and Chemical Potential Gradients
Authors: Brandon L Foley, Sylvie Aubry, Maxwell Murialdo, Elizabeth A Glascoe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwo key phenomena—immobilization and concentration-dependent mixing free energies—simultaneously alter the sorption thermodynamics and diffusion of vapors in materials. This interrelation is leveraged to fit a unified model simultaneously capturing both sorption dynamics and the equilibrium isotherms. This transport model incorporates quasi-equilibrated immobilization reactions and considers Fick’s law rigorously in terms of chemical potential gradients rather than concentration gradients. Five material case studies are discussed with varying characteristics, including fillers that provide sites for surface sorption, pores for capillary condensation, and apparent clustering or pooling at high vapor concentrations. Each case study illustrates that intrinsic diffusivity is constant, while the effective diffusivity changes predictably because of immobilization or changing free energies of mixing.

Introduction

Modeling the transport of vapors in materials is critical for evaluating source/sink behavior,1 solving long-term systems engineering problems regarding the aging and degradation of materials,2 and assessing the permeability of a material for separations.3 These phenomena are important in a range of applications, including art conservation,4 airbag longevity,5 microelectronic delamination,6 and metal corrosion.7 Each of these problems rely on accurate quantification of both the sorbed species concentrations and the dynamic time scales. Dynamic vapor sorption, an in situ method where sorption isotherms and dynamics are assessed by stepwise changes in sorbate partial pressures, is a reliable approach for investigating these phenomena.8−12 However, a key challenge is developing robust models that apply to a wide range of materials, do not require repeatedly solving partial differential equations to obtain parameter estimates, and are easy to implement. In this work, we describe a robust method based on fundamental mechanisms for modeling the diffusion-controlled vapor sorption and equilibrium in materials exhibiting a wide range of sorption-transport behaviors.

Equation 1 captures diffusion driven by both concentration and mixing free energy gradients, the latter of which varies with sorbed concentration. Equation 1 is generalized further by considering that not all sorbed species are mobile. Instead, a distinct population of immobile species, such as those adsorbed on filler surfaces, also contribute to the total concentration within a material. In these cases, the left-hand side of eq 1 must consider the accumulation of both mobile (cm) and immobile (ci) species while the diffusion term on the right-hand side considers only mobile species, such that eq 1 generalizes to eq 2.13−15

The chemical potential is a function of the thermodynamic activity (a) and standard state chemical potential (μvo) in eq 3.

Finally, the equilibrium concentrations of mobile and immobile species are both functions of the activity (cm = cm(a); ci = ci(a)). Substitution of these relationships and eq 3 into eq 2 gives eq 4.16,17

Equation 4 assumes that mobile and immobile species are in local quasi-equilibrium with each other. This assumption is valid when diffusive time scales are much longer than time scales for chemical interconversion between species,17 and we assume here that slow kinetic processes that may result from material changes (e.g., swelling) are negligible.18 In eq 4, the ratio of the mobile concentration (cm) to the activity is related to the free energy of mixing of the sorbate in the polymer, ΔGmix (eq 5).

When the mobile concentration is linear in activity, the mixing free energy is constant and diffusive flux is proportional to the concentration gradient. When cm is nonlinear, gradients in mixing free energies also contribute to diffusion, as is accounted for in eq 4.

Concentration-dependent mixing free energies often result in concave-up sorption isotherms and decreases in the effective diffusivity at high activities. This decrease is often captured in models by defining diffusivity as a function of concentration,19,20 however this is likely not necessary unless the effective diffusivity varies by several orders of magnitude due to plasticization of the polymer.21 Rather, the need to include concentration-dependent diffusivity is caused by defining flux in terms of concentration gradients rather than rigorously in terms of chemical potential gradients.

Equation 4 is a partial differential equation in terms of activity that is solvable if cm(a) and ci(a) are known. Determining these two functions is the final hurdle in developing a model for describing equilibrium and sorption dynamics of the solute. Herein, we detail a generalized approach for elucidating mobile and immobile concentrations by fitting eq 4 to experimental dynamic data. In this procedure, the total sorbed concentration and sorption dynamics are assessed individually as a function of activity and temperature by measuring the dynamic vapor uptake in response to stepwise changes in the activity. The versatility of this approach is demonstrated on five case studies with materials of varying intrinsic behaviors that are described well by eq 4. These five case studies are chosen for their characteristic properties and unique functional forms for cm and ci. In these case studies, we capture a wide range of dynamic sorption behavior without the need to define diffusivity as a function of concentration.

Estimation of Effective Diffusivities

Effective diffusivities were determined by fitting Deff in the one-dimensional Fick’s law equation (eq 6) to each humidity step at each temperature, where c' is the relative concentration change c′ = (c(t) – c0)/(c∞ – c0), where c0 and c∞ are the initial and final concentrations for that humidity step, respectively. The boundary condition is specified by the real-time experimental relative humidities, and the center of the slab is assigned a no-flux boundary condition. The Deff is found by minimizing the sum of the square errors between the solution to eq 6 and the measured relative mass change, m', (eq 7) using the bisection method. Upper or lower bounds on Deff are found by increasing or decreasing Deff by factors of 10 until the average residuals change sign. Parameter Estimation Methods

The isotherm and effective diffusivities at all temperatures were fit simultaneously by minimizing the sum of the square errors using the fminunc Matlab function. One hundred initial guesses were generated using the Latin hypercube27 method. Each initial guess was optimized to find the local minimum fit. Increasing the number of initial guesses resulted in the same optimal fit. Dozens of the initial guesses converged to the same optimal fit. The best of the fits was deemed the global minimum fit.

The weighting factors for least-squares fits should be the inverse of the relative variances for the data. We approximate the relative variances for isotherm data by the square of the median of the isotherm, while the relative variance for effective diffusivities were the square of the median of the effective diffusivities at a given temperature multiplied by a factor of 100. This ensures that each data type is normalized by its magnitude and the less accurate effective diffusivity data are weighted 100× less than the more accurate isotherm data. The effective diffusivities often contain outliers that deviate from the rest of the trend by several orders of magnitude. These data were removed from the fit by assigning a weight of zero to them. A discussion on uniqueness-of-fit and a model-agnostic approach for elucidating mobile and immobile concentrations is provided in Section S1 of the Supporting Information. A brief overview on the use of the Matlab codes are provided in Section S2 of the Supporting Information and the code is available to download for free from https://github.com/foley352/polymer-diffusion.

Results and Discussion

Generalized Model Derivation

Equation 4 succinctly describes sorption thermodynamics and diffusive transport with immobile populations and chemical potential gradients. However, finding the global minimum fit of a partial differential equation to dynamic data has many challenges. Fitting analytical equations to experimental data is preferred because finding global minimums are comparatively facile, unbiased objective functions are easier to define, and equilibrium and dynamic data can be individually weighted, yielding better fits with lower computational time. We achieve this by approximating eq 4 to reduce the number of unknowns to a single effective diffusivity parameter (Deff) (eq 8). where c′, c0, and c∞ are the relative, initial, and final concentrations for a given humidity step, respectively. The substitution of c′ for a assumes is constant for a given humidity step, which is a good approximation for small humidity steps. A stepwise derivation of eq 8 is found in Section S3 of the Supporting Information.

Equation 8 is fit to each humidity step to determine Deff as a function of humidity and temperature. This procedure is illustrated in Figure 1a, where the Deff fits are shown for two of the humidity steps. The size of the humidity steps are assumed sufficiently small to approximate Deff as constant for each step. Thus, from the dynamic experiments we obtain two measured quantities: the total equilibrium concentration (Figure 1b), ctot, which is the sum of the mobile and immobile concentrations at equilibrium, and the effective diffusivity, which is a function of cm, ci, and D per eq 8. Appropriate model equations for cm and ci are substituted into eq 8 and fit to the experimentally determined Deff and ctot by least-squares methods. The fitting approach is discussed in detail in the Methods section.

In the following sections, we consider five case studies where we apply eq 8 to model the equilibrium thermodynamics and diffusive transport of water in several materials. These materials were specially selected because they each require different functional forms of cm and ci and provide instructive examples for using this approach in any other material-sorbate system.

Case I: Henry’s Mode Only Sorption

The simplest sorption isotherms are linear in relative pressure of the sorbate. The dynamic sorption for these materials is concisely described by eq 9, where the mobile concentration is linear in activity (a), the immobile concentration is zero, and the effective diffusion coefficient is equal to the intrinsic diffusion coefficient.

Water sorption in polysulfone exhibits Case I sorption with isotherms and dynamics described by eq 9. As shown in Figure 2a, the water sorption isotherms are linear in the relative humidity and nearly independent of temperature, indicating that the mixing enthalpy is ∼0. The measured effective diffusivity is independent of the sorbed concentration but exhibits Arrhenius dependence on temperature, as shown in Figure 2b. The model fits of eq 9 to the experimental data are shown as solid lines in Figure 2, with excellent agreement over the entire range of relative humidities and temperatures. The best-fit parameters are found by fitting the model to the experimental data in Figure 2, and are reported for all materials in Section 3.7. These parameters are used to simulate the dynamic sorption profile in Figure 3, which closely matches the experimental data. Residual plots for the model simulation in Figure 3 and for all following samples are reported in Section S4 of the Supporting Information.

Case I is the simplest sorption isotherm because the mixing free energy is independent of the sorbed concentration and there are no immobilized species populations. Both the Henry’s coefficient (Hm) and the diffusivity (D) are intrinsically dependent on temperature via van’t Hoff and Arrhenius relationships, respectively. In the remaining case studies, we explore the effect of nonideal sorption and immobilization on the sorption equilibrium and dynamics, and we demonstrate how a range of sorption behaviors are described by the generalized sorption-diffusion model.

Case II: Henry’s Sorption with Langmuir Immobilization

Filler particles are often added to polymers to alter their mechanical properties, but they also contribute to the sorption isotherm and alter the transport dynamics by providing surfaces for molecules to adsorb onto. For monolayer surface coverages, the sorption thermodynamics are described by the Langmuir isotherm. Species that adsorb on filler particles are necessarily immobile because they must desorb to transport through the material.

This phenomenon is demonstrated in the sorption dynamics and isotherms of a silica-filled silicone (Figure 4). At low relative humidities, the sorption isotherm is concave down, a characteristic feature indicating the presence of Langmuir-like sorption on the filler particles. The Langmuir sorption decreases the effective diffusion because these immobile modes are filled simultaneously with the mobile species without increasing the intrinsic diffusion rate into the material. As the Langmuir sorption modes become saturated, the effective diffusivity increases and plateaus as it approaches the intrinsic diffusivity, as observed for the filled silicone (Figure 4b).

(a) Isotherm and (b) effective diffusivities for water sorption in a silica-filled silicone from 30–60 °C. Sorption and desorption data sets overlay for both the isotherms and the effective diffusivity data.

At high relative humidities, the Langmuir sorption mode becomes saturated, and the total sorption isotherm becomes linear (Figure 4a), suggesting that the mobile sorption isotherm is only Henry’s mode sorption. The shared parameters between the effective diffusivity and mobile and immobile concentrations illustrate the intimate connection between the sorption isotherms and the sorption dynamics. The effective diffusivity is found by eq 8.

Equation 10 is fit simultaneously to the sorption isotherm and the effective diffusivities to estimate the thermodynamic and diffusivity parameters. The model fit of eq 10 to the experimental data is shown as solid lines in Figure 4. Case III: Henry’s Sorption with Mobile Pooling

Nonlinear sorption isotherms can occur due to two phenomena: (i) an immobilizing sorption mode with nonlinear sorption, as observed for the silica-filled silicone in Section 3.3, or (ii) dependence of the mixing free energy on sorbed concentration. For water sorption in the urethane adhesive, a concave-up sorption isotherm is observed at high relative humidities (Figure 6a) with a coinciding decrease in the effective diffusivity (Figure 6b). This upward concavity is often referred to as the “clustering”29 or “pooling”17 mode, which is the nonrandom distribution of the sorbate in solution or accumulation in multilayers on surfaces or in pores. We refer to this mode as “pooling” hereinafter. This pooling mode can be assigned to mobile or immobile concentrations, with no impact on the sorption isotherm. However, the mobility of the pooling mode impacts the effective diffusivities. Thus, we need to evaluate the fit of both models to determine whether pooling in the urethane adhesive is best described as mobile or immobile.

Case IV: Langmuir Immobilization with No Concave Down Isotherm

When assigning sorption modes to an isotherm, the apparent shape of the isotherm can be misleading. The water sorption isotherms for Kapton H tape exhibit what appear to be Henry’s mode sorption with pooling at high relative humidities (Figure 8a).23 However, the measured effective diffusivity increases with relative humidity with a similar shape to that observed in materials with Langmuir isotherms, such as the filled silicone in Case II (see Figure 9a). To test whether Langmuir sorption modes were present, two models were fit to the experimental data. Both models included mobile Henry’s and pooling modes, but one model included immobile Langmuir sorption (eq 13) while the other model omitted Langmuir sorption (eq 11).

(a) Kapton H water sorption isotherms for models with and without immobile Langmuir mode sorption. The fits are nearly indistinguishable. (b) Comparison of model fits with and without Langmuir mode sorption in the 0–20% RH region for the 30 °C sorption isotherm.

Effective diffusivity as a function of temperature and relative humidity for Kapton H. (a) Model fits with immobile Langmuir and mobile Henry’s and pooling sorption modes. (b) Model fits with only mobile Henry’s and pooling sorption modes. The 70 °C effective diffusivity data was omitted from fitting because of the noisiness in the data set.

Both model fits capture the sorption isotherms and are nearly indistinguishable (Figure 8a). A closer view of the fits in the 0–20% relative humidity range (Figure 8b) shows that the 30 °C isotherm has subtle downward concavity which is captured better by the model with Langmuir sorption, but this is insufficient evidence that Langmuir sorption modes are present in Kapton H, given that this fit includes three additional parameters (b, L, and ΔHb). However, fits to the effective diffusivity clearly benefit from the inclusion of the immobile Langmuir sorption mode, as illustrated in Figure 9. The model without Langmuir sorption predicts much faster dynamics than observed experimentally at low relative humidities. This is highlighted in Figure 10, which shows that the model without Langmuir sorption reaches equilibrium too quickly, while the model with Langmuir sorption captures the sorption dynamics more accurately.

Case V: Langmuir Immobilization with Capillary Condensation Hysteresis

In Cases I–IV, pooling sorption modes were described by power law terms and the sorption isotherms exhibited no hysteresis. However, the model (eq 4) is agnostic to the functional form of the mobile and immobile concentrations. In this final material case study, we consider a porous filled silicone which exhibits sorption hysteresis at relative humidities greater than 50% (see Figure 11a), a hallmark of capillary condensation.30 The differing isotherms for sorption and desorption lead to hysteresis in the effective diffusivities in the capillary condensation regime (Figure 11b). At the onset and termination of capillary condensation, there is a discontinuity in the effective diffusivities. These changes in the sorption dynamics are accurately described by changes in immobile pooling concentrations in eq 8.

(b) Effective diffusivity as a function of relative humidity during sorption and desorption. Discontinuities occur at the onset and termination of sorption hysteresis. Experimentally measured data are shown as points and model fits are shown as lines.

To capture this isotherm behavior, the power law immobile pooling term is multiplied by a logistic function to endow smooth “on–off” functionality. The pooling parameters differ for sorption and desorption, while immobile Langmuir, mobile Henry’s, and intrinsic diffusion parameters are shared during sorption and desorption. The model equations for mobile and immobile concentrations are summarized in eq 14. The derivative in the effective diffusivity is not evaluated in eq 14 due to space constraints. Because pi, ni, k, and a0 are fit separately for sorption and desorption, the equilibrium concentrations and effective diffusivities both exhibit hysteresis.

Equation 14 is fit simultaneously to the isotherm and effective diffusivity data in Figure 11, with best fits shown as solid lines. The sorption-diffusion model captures the hysteresis in both the sorption equilibrium and the effective diffusivities. These fits demonstrate that the intrinsic diffusivity is constant and that the change in dynamics is caused by varying capillary condensation immobilization. During both sorption and desorption, the onset of capillary condensation is associated with a discontinuity in the effective diffusivities, which is well-described by the diffusion model. Conclusions

The physics of vapor transport and sorption equilibrium are intertwined via immobilization reactions and the mixing free energies of mobile species. In the model discussed heretofore, the null hypothesis is that the intrinsic diffusivity is constant, and changes in the observed sorption kinetics are primarily driven by the physics of sorption phenomena. This is not to suggest that intrinsic diffusivity is never concentration-dependent, as plasticization can lead to many order-of-magnitude changes in the diffusivity,21 but including such terms in a transport model should be an action of last resort after the null hypothesis has been rejected.

The difference between the models discussed in this work and employing concentration-dependent diffusivities may seem trivial since both approaches may provide reasonable fits to dynamic vapor sorption measurements. However, a goal of model development is to extrapolate from simple stepwise sorption experiments on thin films to varying geometries, new conditions, and multimaterial systems, where properly capturing the physics is paramount to accurate simulations. Furthermore, concentration dependence of intrinsic diffusivity can be added to the models discussed here, but this does not loosen the requirement that immobilization and chemical potential must be considered when modeling the physics of vapor transport through materials.

Finally, connecting equilibrium thermodynamics to diffusion kinetics enables elucidation of the sorption and transport mechanisms. The sorption modes that are present in a material, and whether they are mobile or immobile, become apparent in the goodness-of-fit of various model constructions. Incorrect assignment of sorption modes can have profound impacts on the expected vapor transport. For example, immobile species are irrelevant to steady-state permeability of a material, and thus it is critical that the model captures mobility/immobility correctly. Modeling vapor transport using the equations discussed in this work is straightforward, fast, and applies to a wide array of materials. Moreover, having a consistent model across many materials makes it easy to construct multimaterial simulations.
