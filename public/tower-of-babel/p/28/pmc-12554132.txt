Acinetobacter towneri harbouring blaNDM-1 and blaOXA-58: genomic characterization of the first human isolate from Brazil
Authors: Daiana Cristina Silva Rodrigues, Bruna Ribeiro Sued-Karam, Melise Chaves Silveira, Orlando Carlos da Conceição-Neto, Bruno Rocha Pribul, Ana Paula D'Alincourt Carvalho-Assef, Cláudio Marcos Rocha-de-Souza
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

The genus Acinetobacter encompasses Gram-negative bacteria inhabiting diverse environments, from natural habitats to hospital settings. Whilst A. baumannii complex remains the most clinically significant group owing to its association with healthcare-associated infections and pronounced antimicrobial resistance, other species including A. pittii, A. nosocomialis, A. lwoffii, and A. johnsonii have been increasingly documented in clinical contexts [1].

Acinetobacter towneri represents a lesser-characterised member of this genus, predominantly associated with veterinary and environmental sources, including swine faeces, wastewater, and soil. Human isolates remain rare, highlighting a considerable knowledge gap regarding its epidemiology and clinical significance [2].

We describe the first isolation of A. towneri from a human clinical specimen in Brazil. The organism was recovered from urine of a 43-year-old hospitalised female patient in Rio de Janeiro in January 2024. Initial identification was performed using MALDI-TOF MS and confirmed through WGS.

PCR analysis detected carbapenemase genes blaNDM-1 and blaOXA-58. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted using disc diffusion following EUCAST/BrCAST guidelines, as well as broth microdilution employing the Sensititre™ Gram Negative MDRGNX2F plate. The isolate demonstrated resistance to tetracycline, nalidixic acid, trimethoprim-sulphamethoxazole, clindamycin, ciprofloxacin, ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, tigecycline, meropenem/vaborbactam, imipenem, fosfomycin, ceftolozane-tazobactam, ceftazidime-avibactam, meropenem and cefiderocol.

The A. towneri isolate exhibited an alarming resistance profile encompassing virtually all major antibiotic classes, including complete resistance to recently introduced β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations. This resistance pattern mirrors those previously described for carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii, in which susceptibility to these agents is uncommon. In a substantial Taiwanese collection of 136 imipenem-non-susceptible isolates, ceftazidime-avibactam demonstrated activity against merely 1.5 % of strains, whilst meropenem/vaborbactam showed no activity whatsoever [3]. The pronounced resistance observed may be attributed to the combined presence of NDM-1, unaffected by vaborbactam, tazobactam, or avibactam, and OXA-58, similarly exhibiting poor inhibition by these compounds. This combination could account for, at least partially, the complete loss of activity of these novel agents in our isolate. The concurrent resistance to cefiderocol observed in our strain further compounds this concerning scenario, restricting therapeutic alternatives to last-resort agents such as colistin.

Genomic DNA was extracted with the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit. Illumina and Oxford Nanopore libraries were prepared with the DNA Prep and Rapid Barcoding Kits, respectively, and sequenced on MiSeq and MinION platforms. Long reads were assembled de novo with Flye and polished using Polypolish.

The blaNDM-1 gene was located on a 42,021 bp plasmid (pCCBH37868_NDM-1) within truncated Tn125 transposon, flanked upstream by ISAba14–aph(3′)-VI–ISAba125 and downstream by bleMBL. Core genes typically present in complete Tn125 structure, such as groES, groEL, cutA and second ISAba125 copy, were absent, highlighting genetic variability amongst Acinetobacter species. The plasmid carries conjugation-related genes (virB, traA, traD) and the partitioning gene parA. However, no identifiable rep genes were detected. The plasmid pCCBH37868_OXA-58 (127,754 bp) was identified carrying the blaOXA-58 gene, located downstream of an ISAba3 insertion sequence and upstream of a truncated ISAba3-like element. This plasmid also harbored multiple accessory genes, including insertion sequences and macrolide resistance genes, such as mph(E) and msh(E). The rep gene of pCCBH37868_OXA-58 belongs to the R3-T21 subgroup of the R3 family, linked to structural diversity and carbapenem resistance spread [4] (Fig. 1). The coexistence of blaNDM-1 and blaOXA-58 in a clinical A. towneri isolate is exceptionally rare, with previous reports limited primarily to environmental or livestock strains. This finding may suggest gene exchange events occurring outside clinical environments. The observed multidrug resistance profile, combined with dual carbapenemase presence, mirrors patterns described for hospital wastewater and animal sources, underscoring the potential role of A. towneri as a reservoir for clinically relevant resistance genes. Overall, findings underscore the critical human-animal-environment interface in resistance gene dissemination, reinforcing the importance of One Health approaches.

The report reinforces the importance of precise identification of Acinetobacter species beyond A. baumannii in routine laboratory workflows. Given horizontal gene transfer and environmental persistence, monitoring A. towneri remains relevant to broader antimicrobial resistance surveillance.

This is the first Brazilian report of a clinical A. towneri isolate harbouring both blaNDM-1 and blaOXA-58. Ongoing genomic and epidemiological studies are essential in elucidating clinical significance and resistance gene dissemination amongst non-baumannii Acinetobacter species.

The complete chromosome and plasmid sequences of Acinetobacter towneri CCBH37868 have been deposited in the NCBI database under BioProject PRJNA677881 and BioSample SAMN50794198.
