Rationally Designed InhA Inhibitors: A Comparative Anti‐Tubercular Activity Study of Sulfonate Esters of Isoniazid Hydrazones and Their Structurally Flexible Benzyl Analogues
Authors: Mukanda Gedeon Kadima, Sahil Mishra, Gobind Kumar, Pule Seboletswe, Françoise Roquet‐Banères, Maëlle Foubert, Laurent Kremer, Rajshekhar Karpoormath, Parvesh Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMolecular hybridization of isoniazid with hydrophobic aromatic moieties represents a promising strategy for the development of novel anti‐tubercular therapeutics. In this study, a series of hybrid molecules (5a–i) was synthesized by linking isoniazid with aromatic sulfonate esters via a hydrazone bridge. Molecular docking studies revealed that these compounds interact effectively with the catalytic triad of the InhA enzyme (Y158, F149, and K165), suggesting their potential as InhA inhibitors. To enhance molecular flexibility and improve binding interactions with both NADH and the catalytic residues, a second generation of derivatives (8a–k) was designed and synthesized. All synthesized compounds were structurally characterized using spectroscopic techniques, including nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (IR), and high‐resolution mass spectrometry. As anticipated, these new compounds exhibited enhanced anti‐tubercular activity compared to their precursors. Notably, compound 8b demonstrated significant potency with an MIC of 0.078 μg/mL, approximately twofold more active than its precursor 5b (MIC = 0.156 μg/mL) against Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb). However, both generations of compounds (e.g., 5a, 5b, 8a, 8b, 8c, and 8 k) lost activity against INH‐resistant Mtb strains harboring katG mutations. Importantly, no cytotoxicity was observed for these compounds in THP‐1 human monocytic cells at a concentration of 10 μg/mL. The structural integrity of the lead compound 8b was confirmed via 1H NMR stability studies. The ADME/T parameters (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity) were also explored to determine their drug likeness and safety profile. Collectively, these hybrid molecules present valuable scaffolds for further optimization in the pursuit of new anti‐tubercular agents.

Introduction

Tuberculosis is the leading cause of human death globally from a single infectious agent (Glaziou et al. 2014). It remained the second deadliest infectious disease after COVID‐19 in the second decade of the 21st century, with a mortality rate surpassing those of AIDS and COVID‐19 combined (Trajman et al. 2022; Vilcheze and Kremer 2017). TB primarily affects the lungs (pulmonary TB) but can also involve other organs such as the spine, kidneys, and brain (extrapulmonary TB). It is estimated that one‐third of the global population harbors latent TB, in which the bacterium remains dormant and asymptomatic. If left untreated, TB can become highly resistant to conventional therapies because of its ability to persist within host cells by switching between latent and active states and acquiring mutations (Rodriguez‐Takeuchi et al. 2019). This adaptability undermines the host immune response and has led to the emergence of multi‐drug‐resistant TB (MDR‐TB), extensively drug‐resistant TB (XDR‐TB), and totally drug‐resistant TB (TDR‐TB), posing significant challenges to treatment and public health (Migliori et al. 2020).

According to the Global Tuberculosis Report 2023, an estimated 1.2 million deaths occurred among HIV‐positive individuals, and approximately 240,000 deaths were reported among HIV‐negative individuals infected with tuberculosis (Bizuneh et al. 2024). Thus, addressing this global health threat is imperative, as tuberculosis continues to hinder global development and prosperity. The current frontline anti‐TB agents, isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol, and fluoroquinolones (Figure 1), have significant limitations, including prolonged treatment durations and the emergence of drug‐resistant strains, which compromise their long‐term efficacy (Laurenzi et al. 2007).

Current commonly used anti‐TB drugs.

Isoniazid (isonicotinic acid hydrazide, INH), an age‐old drug, still serves as a cornerstone in modern TB drug treatment. Mechanistically, INH has to be activated to an isonicotinoyl radical by the catalase‐peroxidase enzyme KatG to exert its inhibitory power on the enoyl‐acyl carrier protein (ACP) reductase (InhA) (Unissa et al. 2016). However, long and persistent use of INH has developed mutations in the katG and inhA genes, rendering the drug ineffective (Bollela et al. 2016).

Drug re‐engineering has yielded fruitful results regarding newer versions of older antibiotics (Reddy et al. 2022). Researchers studying anti‐TB medications have also used this tactic and molecular hybridization to create quick and effective compounds, bypassing target mutation‐related hindrances (Figure 2) (Kaur et al. 2021). Numerous publications in the literature describe INH‐based hybrids whose activity has been enhanced by modifying the INH structure (Figure 3) (Mishra et al. 2024). The free amino group in the INH structure can be readily maneuvered to create new molecules containing spirocyclic, hydrazone, and azole (oxazole, azole) linkages (Gao et al. 2018). There are few reports of sulfonate‐based anti‐TB drugs/prodrugs (Pradhan and Sinha 2018) in the literature, although sulfonate as a scaffold plays an important role in medicinal/organic chemistry.

Re‐engineering of old TB drugs.

Recent literature reports on anti‐TB hybrids on the basis of INH and sulfonate linkage.

Koçak Aslan et al. (2022) have previously hybridized INH with some para‐substituted benzene sulfonated esters, displaying activity against Mtb H37Rv and INH‐resistant clinical isolates carrying mutations in katG and inhA. To expand the scope of these compounds in anti‐TB research, we designed a series of INH‐hydrazone hybrids containing para‐substituted benzene sulfonate esters 5 (a–i) as depicted in Figure 4. On the basis of the docking interactions of compounds 5 (a–i) within the InhA active site, we replaced the rigid sulfonate ester with flexible groups, which led to significantly improved interactions of compounds 8 (a–k) with surrounding residues and NADH. Designed compounds were then synthesized, and their structural characterization was determined using different spectroscopic techniques, followed by their anti‐TB evaluation against Mtb strains susceptible or resistant to INH because of mutations in katG. Further, the cytotoxic activity of the most potent compounds was assessed using human THP‐1 macrophages, and their stability was investigated using NMR.

Results and Discussion

Molecular Docking Studies

Different computational methods, such as molecular docking, have made it possible to delve inside the enzyme crystal coordinates, investigate the enzyme‐substrate interactions, and hence help design new chemical entities (Agu et al. 2023; Deb et al. 2021). The substrate‐binding site of InhA is characterized by a large, hydrophobic cavity, comprising key residues such as P99, F97, K118, I202, A198, Y158, and F149. This environment favors the accommodation of hydrophobic scaffolds, enabling critical hydrophobic interactions between the enzyme and potential drug candidates. Accordingly, a strategy was devised to hybridize isoniazid (INH) with various hydrophobic and rigid/flexible moieties, aiming to enhance binding affinity. The resulting compounds, 5 (Scheme 1) and 8 (Scheme 2 ), were subsequently evaluated and their ligand–enzyme interactions compared with those of the native INH–InhA complex. Six representative compounds bearing rigid sulfonyl 5 (a–f) and flexible benzyl groups 8 (a–f) were flexibly docked using an induced fit molecular docking algorithm (IFD) in the InhA active site (pdb id: 4TZK).

Because Y158, F149, and K165 represent a catalytic triad that plays an important role along with NADH in the oxidoreductase activity of the enzyme (Rozwarski et al. 1999), it was decided to surround this triad and NADH with hydrophobic and flexible moieties. Consequently, the rigid sulfonyl linker in series 5 was replaced with a more flexible methylene group to design its structural analogues, series 8, most of which are novel and have not been previously reported in the literature. Molecular docking results demonstrated that series 8 compounds exhibited stronger and more favorable interactions within the active site of InhA compared to 5 (Figure 5b). Specifically, compounds 8a (docking score: −11.649 kcal/mol), 8b (−9.942 kcal/mol), 8c (−9.699 kcal/mol), 8d (−9.852 kcal/mol), 8e (−9.058 kcal/mol), and 8f (−10.454 kcal/mol) formed multiple hydrogen bond interactions within the active site. Notably, these compounds interacted with Y158 via their phenyl and benzyloxy moieties, and also showed H‐bond interactions with F97, M98, P99, P156, and R43 (Figure 5b), indicating enhanced binding affinity relative to series 5. Compound 8b additionally exhibited π‐cation hydrophobic interactions with the nicotinamide moiety of NADH, further contributing to its binding affinity. On the basis of these promising docking results, compounds 5 and 8 were synthesized (Schemes 1, 2) and subsequently evaluated for their anti‐TB activity against both isoniazid (INH)‐sensitive and INH‐resistant strains of Mtb.

Chemistry

The synthetic strategies adopted for the preparation of target compounds 5 (a–i) and 8 (a–k) are illustrated in Schemes 1 and 2. Compound 3 was initially prepared by refluxing INH 2 and 4‐hydroxybenzaldehyde 1 in ethanol for 1 h in the presence of a catalytic amount of acetic acid. The target compounds 5 (a–i) were then obtained from compound 3 by its O‐sulfonylation using the corresponding sulfonyl chlorides 4 (a–i) in the presence of potassium carbonate in dry DMF (Scheme 1). For the synthesis of compounds 8 (a–k), 4‐hydroxybenzaldehyde was initially O‐alkylated with substituted benzyl bromides 6 (a–k) using K2CO3 in dry ACN under reflux to produce compounds 7 (a–k). Subsequently, compounds 7 (a–k) were refluxed with INH 2 in ethanol, using a catalytic amount of glacial acetic acid, to afford the final hydrazide‐hydrazone compounds 8 (a–k) (Scheme 2).

The structures of the synthesized compounds were confirmed through NMR (1H and 13C), IR, and HRMS techniques. As per the 1H and 13C NMR analysis, both series of compounds existed as a mixture of E and Z isomers. However, the appearance of a singlet for the = CH proton in the range 8.41–8.47 ppm for series 5 (a–i) and 8 (a–k) compounds was in accordance with the E‐isomer as reported in the literature (Munir et al. 2021; Kumar et al. 2025). For instance, compound 8b displayed two singlets at 11.99 and 8.41 ppm for CONH and = CH protons, respectively (Figure S1). In compound 5b, these two singlets appeared relatively downfield at 12.19 and 8.47 ppm (Figure S1). In the 13 C NMR spectra, fewer carbon signals were observed than expected because of peak overlap in the aromatic region, which resulted in the merging of multiple carbon resonances.

The presence of the N‐acyl hydrazone moiety in compounds 8a–k and 5a–i was further confirmed by characteristic 13 C NMR signals, with carbonyl carbons appearing in the range of162.1 to 161.88 ppm and iminic carbons in the range of149.8 to 147.11 ppm (Figure S1). The IR spectra of compounds 5a–i exhibited characteristic SO 2 stretching bands at 1368–1358 cm−1 and 1150–1147 cm−1, along with absorption bands at 3400–3200 cm−1 and 1657–1650 cm−1, corresponding to the N‐H and C=O groups, respectively. Similarly, compounds 8a–k showed absorption bands for N‐H (3400–3200 cm −1), C=O (1670–1650 cm −1), and C=N (1603–1590 cm−1). These spectral features collectively confirm the presence of the N‐acyl hydrazone moiety in both compound series 5 and 8. 1H NMR, 13C NMR, FTIR, and HRMS data of the final compounds are provided in the Supporing Information.

Biological Studies

In Vitro Activity Against INH‐Susceptible M. tuberculosis

Compounds 5 (a–i), 8 (a–k), and 7 (a–k) were biologically evaluated for their in vitro activity against Mtb mc26230 in Middlebrook 7H9 medium supplemented with 10% OADC, 109 μM pantothenic acid, and 0.025% tyloxapol at 37°C using INH as a reference drug. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values are summarized in Table 1. For the 5 (a–i) series, compounds 5a, 5b, 5c, 5d, and 5f exhibited an MIC value of 0.15 μg/mL, whereas compounds 5e, 5 g, and 5i showed a ~2‐fold decrease in their anti‐TB activity (MIC of 0.313 μg/mL). Compound 5 h, with a MIC value > 200 μg/mL, was the least active among the tested compounds containing a rigid sulfonate ester linked to INH‐hydrazone.

The importance of INH‐hydrazone linkage in the anti‐TB therapeutic design can also be aptly viewed by comparing the MIC data for compounds 7 (a–k) (Table S1 of supporting data) and 8 (a–k). The MIC values indicate that the INH‐hydrazone associated with flexible substituted benzyls 8 (a–k) displayed better activity than their precursors 7 (a–k). Overall, the flexible INH hydrazone‐benzyl hybrids 8 (a–k) interacted more efficiently with the sizeable hydrophobic cavity of InhA, resulting in improved anti‐TB activity compared to their precursors 7 (a–k) and INH hydrazone sulfonate ester hybrids 5 (a–i) (Table 1).

Structure–Activity Relationship (SAR) Analysis

A detailed examination of the biological results generally indicates that the anti‐TB activity of the proposed hybrids depends on electron density, bond flexibility, and hydrazone linkage (Figure 6).

SAR summary of 5 (a–i), 7 (a–k), and 8 (a–k).

In the case of the precursors 7 (a–k), both electron‐donating and electron‐withdrawing groups, regardless of their point of attachment on the phenyl ring (Figure 6), displayed moderate to good activity with MIC values ranging from 25 to 100 μg/mL. For example, compounds 7b, 7c, 7f, and 7 g, having bromo, chloro, methyl, and fluoro groups, respectively, at the para position, displayed MIC values of 50 μg/mL. Compound 7k, having a meta bromo substitution, displayed a moderate MIC of 50 μg/mL, similar to its para‐substituted homolog 7b. However, compound 7d (MIC = 100 μg/mL), possessing an ortho di‐substituted chloro substituent, displayed poorer activity than compound 7j (MIC = 50 μg/mL) bearing ortho fluoro para bromo substitutions, suggesting that para substitution is more favorable than ortho substitution. In contrast, compounds 7h and 7i, having electron‐donating thio‐methyl and electron‐withdrawing trifluoro substitution at the para position, were the most active ones (MIC = 25 μg/mL). Overall, the electron‐donating or withdrawing groups preferred the para position over the ortho or meta position (Table S1, Supporting Information).

In the case of compounds 5 (a–i), the strong electron‐withdrawing substituents such as nitro (5e) and fluoro (5 g), substituted at the para position on the phenyl ring (Figure 6), bridged by the sulfonate ester, showed lower activity when compared to those bearing electron‐donating groups (Table 1). For instance, compound 5f, bearing an electron‐donating methyl group, showed higher activity (MIC = 0.156 μg/mL) than compounds 5e and 5 g with MIC values of 0.313 μg/mL. Similarly, compounds 5b and 5c bearing bromo and chloro substituents displayed superior activity (MIC = 0.156 μg/mL) to their halogen counterpart (5 g) substituted with the most electronegative halogen fluorine (MIC = 0.313 μg/mL). Compound 5d, with multiple electron‐donating substituents, also showed an equivalent activity (MIC = 0.156 μg/mL) to their monosubstituted analogues substituted with electron‐donating groups. In contrast, compound 5 h, bearing an electron‐donating methoxy group, emerged as the exception, showing the least activity (MIC > 200 μg/mL).

In the case of INH‐hydrazone hybrids 8 (a–k), a multifold improvement in their anti‐TB activity was observed as compared to their precursors 7 (a–k) and compounds 5 (a–i) attributed to INH coupling via hydrazone linkage as well as the additional flexibility offered by the benzyl moiety, which in turn probably enabled them to penetrate within the long hydrophobic active site of InhA. For example, compound 8b, having a bromo substitution at the para position, showed an improved MIC value of 0.078 μg/mL compared to compound 5b (MIC = 0.156 μg/mL) and a drastic improvement with respect to its precursor 7b (MIC = 50 μg/mL). The meta‐substitution of the bromo group slightly decreased the activity in 8 k (MIC = 0.078 μg/mL). Hence, the para position was observed to be favored for the different substituents regardless of their nature. For instance, compounds 8c, 8e, and 8 g, having electron‐withdrawing chloro, nitro, and fluoro substitutions, showed equivalent activity (MIC = 0.078 μg/mL or 0.156 8e) to 8b. Similarly, the substitution of electron‐donating groups, such as methyl and thiomethyl in compounds 8f and 8 h or dichloro substitution (8d), also did not compromise their activity, showing a MIC value of 0.078 μg/mL. Their analogues bearing no substituent (8a) or substitution of different halogen substituents (8j) showed a 2‐fold decrease in the activity with a MIC value of 0.156 μg/mL. A summary of the most essential SAR features is illustrated in Figure 6.

Stability Studies of Compound 8b

The stability of the imine bond is often a concern, as it is influenced by factors such as solvent environment, acidity, and basicity (Emon et al. 2023). Upon hydrolysis, the imine bond can revert to its precursor molecules, potentially leading to misinterpretation of biological activity results. To ensure that the observed anti‐TB activity was not due to the release of the INH moiety from hydrolysis, we investigated the stability of the most active compound, 8b, in DMSO using 1 H NMR spectroscopy.

For the NMR stability study, 10 mg of compound 8b was dissolved in DMSO‐d 6 and subjected to 1HNMR analysis. Spectra were recorded at 0 (t0), 4 (t4), 8 (t8), and 24 (t24) hours. The overlay of the 1H NMR spectra (Figure S2) showed no change in chemical shifts or the appearance of new proton signals over the 24‐h period. These results confirm that compound 8b remains stable in DMSO, supporting its structural integrity during anti‐TB assays and validating the reliability of the biological activity data.

In Silico ADME/T Studies

Drug discovery uses a specific criterion called ADME/T (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity) to ensure an effective drug development process by assessing the pharmacokinetics and safety profile of the drug. These properties are critical in early‐stage drug discovery and development because high‐quality drug candidates must have strong efficacy against a therapeutic target as well as suitable ADME/T features at a therapeutic dose. Compounds of flexible benzylated series 8a, 8b, 8c, and 8k were subjected to ADME/T and drug likeness profiling along with standard drug INH via SwissADME and pkCSM, and the results are presented in the Supporting Information (Tables S2 and S3). As shown in Table S2, all these compounds, including INH, were within the standard range, indicating their good bioavailability.

Furthermore, the comparison of ADME/T properties for compounds 8a, 8b, 8c, 8 k, and INH using pkCSM (Table S3, Supporting Information) reveals that compound 8b shows a high intestinal absorption (94.07%) while moderately crossing the blood–brain barrier (log BB 0.15). It also gets metabolized by the most important Cytochrome P450 enzyme, CYP3A4, and shows interactions with other CyP enzymes. Additionally, 8b shows no AMES cytotoxicity, suggesting no genotoxic effects. Compounds 8c and 8 k also exhibited similar ADME/T properties with high intestinal absorptions (94.14% and 94.18%, respectively), minimal blood–brain barrier penetration (log BB ranging from 0.15 and 0.13), and multiple interactions with Cyp enzymes, particularly Cyp 3A4, 1A2, and 2C9. They also exhibited no AMES toxicity. Compound 8a was found to possess the highest intestinal absorption rate (95.09%) and moderate blood–brain penetration (0.19), along with interactions with CyP 3A4, 1AC, and 2C19, and no AMES toxicity. In contrast, INH shows moderate intestinal absorption (75.70%), with no CyP interactions, but shows AMES toxicity, which indicates potential genotoxicity concerns. Overall, the flexible benzylated compounds 8a, 8b, 8c, and 8 k possess favorable ADME/T properties and reduced AMES toxicity compared to INH.

Conclusion

More effective anti‐TB agents 8 (a–k) were developed through an in silico docking‐guided modification of 5 (a–i). These were tested against wild‐type Mtb mc26230 and three KatG mutants. Amongst compounds 8 (a–k), six (8b, 8c, 8d, 8 g, 8 h, and 8 k) demonstrated improved anti‐TB activity but, like INH, lost their activity against the mutant strains. This highlights the dependency of these hybrid molecules on KatG bioactivation, which can be considered a limitation in their use against KatG‐mutated clinical strains. Interestingly, the introduction of flexibility in 8 (a–k) rendered them capable of interacting with the surrounding residues and NADH through H‐bonds and hydrophobically. This is evident in their low MIC values (0.78 μg/mL for compounds 8b, 8c, 8d, 8 g, 8 h and 8 k), which compare favorably with other INH‐derived hybrids such as those reported by Koçak Aslan et al. (2022) (0.31–0.62 μg/mL) and Alcaraz et al. (2022) (0.25–0.50 μg/mL), for example, 8 when compared to the other reported hybrids in the literature They also displayed poor toxicity against human macrophages. Overall, it can be inferred that these molecular hybrids have the potential against Mtb, and can be further structurally manipulated to develop new anti‐TB drugs with optimal pharmacological profiles.

Materials and Methods

Chemistry

Experimental

All the chemicals utilized to synthesize the molecular hybrids were purchased from Merck, Sigma Aldrich, and CRD and were further used without any purification. TLC 60 F254 silica gel plates were used to monitor the reaction progress. A Bruker Avance‐III spectrometer (600 MHz) was used to record 1H and 13C (150 MHz) NMR spectra of the compounds. The multiplicities are reported as follows: singlet (s), doublet (d), doublet of doublets (dd), multiplet (m), triplet (t), and broad singlet (brs) and coupling constant (J) in Hz. Infrared (IR) spectra were obtained on Perkin Elmer Spectrum BX FT‐IR (Beaconsfield, UK) and reported in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 using an ATR accessory (Pike Technologies) with a diamond crystal. The high‐resolution mass analysis spectra were obtained using a Waters Micromax LCT Premier TOF‐MS instrument using a 1–2 ppm concentration of the sample. Column chromatography was performed on silica gel (60–120 mesh).

General Procedure for the Synthesis of 5 (a–i) (Procedure A)

Isoniazid 2 (1 mmol) and 4‐hydroxybenzaldehyde 1 (1.3 mmol) were mixed in 50 mL of absolute ethanol and refluxed for 5 h in the presence of a few drops of glacial acetic acid. Solid product 3 obtained was filtered under vacuum, washed with 10 mL of warm ethanol and 50 mL of water, and dried under vacuum. Compound 3 (1 mmol) was then dissolved in dry DMF (50 mL), to which oven‐dried K2CO3 (3 mmol) was added. Further, different sulfonyl chlorides (1.3 mmol) 4 (a–i) were added dropwise to the DMF solution of compound 3, and the reaction was then stirred for 7 h at room temperature. After completion of the reaction (TLC), 10 mL of ice‐cooled water was added to the reaction mixture and extracted with ethyl acetate (4 × 25 mL). The combined organic layers were then washed with water and brine solution and dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate. The organic layer was concentrated under vacuum, and the crude product was purified through column chromatography using hexane‐ethyl acetate as eluent to procure products 5 (a–i).

General Procedure for the Synthesis of 8 (a–k) (Procedure B)

4‐hydroxy benzaldehyde 1 (1 mmol) was dissolved in dry ACN (50 mL) to which oven‐dried K2CO3 was added (1.2 mmol). Further to this solution, substituted benzyl bromides 6 (a–k) (1.2 mmol) were added. The reaction was stirred for 4 h under reflux. After completion of the reaction (TLC), 20 mL of ice‐cooled water was added. Precipitates formed were filtered under vacuum, washed with water and brine (50 mL each), and left to dry to give compounds 7 (a–k).

Isoniazid 1 (1 mmol) and compounds 7 (a–k) (1.3 mmol) were refluxed with 2 drops of glacial acetic acid in 50 mL of absolute ethanol for 5 h. After completion of the reaction (TLC), the reaction mixture was cooled, and the precipitated solid was filtered and washed with 10 mL of warm ethanol and 10 mL of water. The precipitates were left for drying to give compounds 8 (a–k).

Biological Assays

Culture Conditions

The pantothenate‐auxotrophic Mtb mc26230 strain and the mutant resistant against INH Mtb mc26230 M1, Mtb mc26230 M2, and Mtb mc26230 M3 were grown in Middlebrook 7H9 broth supplemented with 10% OADC, 0.025% tyloxapol, and 109 μM pantothenic (complete 7H9) at 37°C without agitation (Sambandamurthy et al. 2006).

The pantothenate‐auxotrophic Mtb mc26230 strain and the Mtb mc26230 M1, Mtb mc26230 M2, and Mtb mc26230 M3 were grown in Middlebrook 7H9 broth supplemented with 10% OADC, 0.025% tyloxapol, and 109 μM pantothenic (complete 7H9) at 37°C without agitation (Sambandamurthy et al. 2006).

Drug Susceptibility Testing

MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of compound inhibiting bacterial growth, at which no change in turbidity was observed, and performed in 7H9 broth supplemented with 10% OADC, 0.025% tyloxapol at 37°C without agitation. For the experiment, 109 μM pantothenic acid was added to all experiments. MIC determination was done using the broth dilution method. Briefly, a log‐phase culture was diluted to an OD600 = 0.05 in 7H9 medium and deposited in 96‐well plates. Compounds were then directly added (in general, 2 μL/well of a 20 mg/mL or 10 mg/mL stock solution) to the first‐row wells containing 198 μL of bacteria. Serial 2‐fold dilutions were then completed, starting from the first row. Plates were wrapped in parafilm and then placed in a 37°C incubator and inspected visually after 6 days. Control wells included DMSO as vehicle control, in which bacterial growth was not inhibited (as for untreated wells). INH was included as a reference drug.

Cytotoxicity Assay

Human THP‐1 monocytes were grown in RPMI medium supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (Sigma‐Aldrich) and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. Cells were differentiated with 20 ng/mL Phorbol Myristate Acetate in a 96‐well plate (2.104 cells/well) for 48 h and exposed to decreasing concentrations (ranging from 100 to 0.78 μg/mL) of the tested compounds. Following incubation (24 or 72 h), 10% v/v resazurin was added to each well and left to incubate for a few hours at 37°C with 5% CO2. Data were measured using a fluorescent plate reader (excitation 540 nm, emission 590 nm). Results are the mean of two independent experiments done in duplicate.

Computational Studies

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was conducted using the Schrödinger suite (2022–1 release) with calculations on the basis of the OPLS4 forcefield. Firstly, the structure of Mtb enoyl‐acyl carrier protein reductase (InhA; PDB ID: 4TZK) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank. This protein was then modified by adding missing hydrogens, side chains, and loops using Prime (Bhachoo and Beuming 2017). Epik (Shelley et al. 2007) was used to generate tautomers and heteroatomic states at pH 7.0 ± 2.0 and delete water molecules that are 5 Å away from the binding cavity. The hydrogen network was then optimized and minimized to an RMSD of 0.3 Å. Subsequently, the binding site was minimized using Prime. Compounds for docking were drawn, saved as ChemDraw files, imported into the workspace, and prepared using the LigPrep module to generate 3D models with the lowest energy conformation. The Induced Fit Protocol (IFD) module was employed to dock these compounds into the active site of InhA; PDB ID: 4TZK. This process involved defining the ligand box by selecting a box using the centroid of selected residues with dimensions under 20 Å and performing conformational sampling within a 2.5 kcal/mol energy range. Ligands were docked with Glide, and poses were refined by adjusting side chain conformations using Prime. Poses with energies up to 30 kcal/mol were redocked, and the top 10 were selected for evaluation. The best pose for each protein‐ligand complex was chosen on the basis of docking scores, IFD scores, Glide e‐model, and Glide energy.
