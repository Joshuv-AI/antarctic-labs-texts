Boreolithothamnion acuminatum sp. nov., B. homoglobosum sp. nov., B. jejuensis sp. nov., and B. japonium comb. nov. (Hapalidiales, Rhodophyta) from Korea: Expanding diversity and biogeographic range in the Northwest Pacific region
Authors: So Young Jeong, Boo Yeon Won, Suzanne Fredericq, Tae Oh Cho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe non‐geniculate coralline genus Boreolithothamnion currently includes eight species with a Northern Hemisphere distribution. To date, only a few studies have focused on the systematics of Boreolithothamnion species. Here, we performed morphological and phylogenetic analyses, based on rbcL, psbA, and SSU rRNA gene datasets, on Boreolithothamnion from Korea as well as DNA‐sequenced type material representing nine names. The analyses supported the proposal of three new species of Boreolithothamnion from Korea: B. acuminatum sp. nov., B. homoglobosum sp. nov., and B. jejuensis sp. nov. Boreolithothamnion acuminatum is distinguished by its encrusting to warty growth form, acuminate to cylindrical protuberances, and raised to low‐domed tetra/bisporangial conceptacles having bordered pore canals with three to four roof cells. Boreolithothamnion homoglobosum is characterized by a fruticose or unattached rhodolith and simple to bifurcated protuberances. Boreolithothamnion jejuensis is distinguished by an encrusting to undulate or knobby growth form with unbranched protuberances. Phylogenetic analysis of the holotype of Lithothamnion japonicum (TRH C16‐3267) based on the psbA gene strongly supported its transfer to the genus Boreolithothamnion, B. japonicum comb. nov. Eight other type specimens of Lithothamnion were also observed to cluster within the Boreolithothamnion clade and were resolved with previously accepted species. This study expands the biogeographical range of Boreolithothamnion in the Northwest Pacific.Keywords: Boreolithothamnion, coralline algae, morpho‐anatomy, phylogeny, psbA, rbcL, SSU rRNA, taxonomy

INTRODUCTION

The genus Boreolithothamnion was established from Lithothamnion based on morphological characteristics and DNA sequencing data (Gabrielson et al., 2023). This taxonomic revision followed the sequencing of Lithothamnion muelleri, the type species of Lithothamnion, by Jeong et al. (2021). Using DNA sequences from type specimens, certain species previously classified under Lithothamnion were identified as distinct and transferred to the newly established genera Boreolithothamnion and Roseolithon (Coutinho et al., 2021; Gabrielson et al., 2023). Boreolithothamnion was proposed for species in the Northern Hemisphere that exhibit morphological similarities to Lithothamnion, with B. glaciale designated as the generitype (Gabrielson et al., 2023). In Boreolithothamnion, the rosette cells are aligned with the surface, whereas in Roseolithon, these cells are situated in distinct depressions due to the gradual degradation of the filaments lining the pore canal cells (Coutinho et al., 2021; Gabrielson et al., 2023). To correctly assign species to Boreolithothamnion, Roseolithon, or Lithothamnion based solely on morphological characteristics, specimens must possess multiporate conceptacles and exhibit some epithallial cells with flared walls (Gabrielson et al., 2023).

Boreolithothamnion is characterized by a monomerous and non‐coaxial thallus structure, flared epithallial cells, elongate intercalary meristematic cells, cell fusions, rosette cells aligned with the surface, and multiporate tetrasporangial conceptacles (Gabrielson et al., 2023). The genus currently includes eight species that were transferred from Lithothamnion: B. corallioides, B. giganteum, B. glaciale, B. lemoineae, B. phymatodeum, B. sonderi, B. soriferum, and B. tophiforme (Gabrielson et al., 2023; Peña et al., 2025). Type materials for these species have been sequenced for the rbcL gene and/or psbA gene (Adey et al., 2015; Gabrielson et al., 2023; Hernández‐Kantún et al., 2015; Peña et al., 2021). In Korea, besides L. japonicum, six additional Lithothamnion species have been reported based on their morphological characteristics: “L. corallioides,” “L. giganteum,” “L. indicum,” “L. lemoineae,” “L. muelleri,” and “L. pacificum” (Jeong et al., 2018; Lee, 2008; Lee & Kang, 2002). Despite Gabrielson et al. (2023) reclassification of L. giganteum, L. lemoineae, and L. pacificum into the genus Boreolithothamnion, DNA sequencing has not yet verified the existence of these Lithothamnion species reported in Korea. Specifically, L. japonicum was first described from Muroran in Iburi Subprefecture, Hokkaido, Japan (Foslie, 1900a), and is considered an endemic species of the Northwest Pacific, including in Korea. Jeong et al. (2018) conducted a comprehensive morphological and anatomical study of L. japonicum, examining the holotypes of both L. japonicum and L. fretense, the latter now regarded as a synonym of L. japonicum.

From 2014 to 2024, we collected unidentified non‐geniculate coralline samples from Korea that resembled Lithothamnion‐like species and compared them with type materials at herbarium TRH (Norway). Based on these analyses, we have described three new species of Boreolithothamnion along with a new combination and provided evidence of their phylogenetic relationships based on sequencing analyses of the rbcL, psbA, and SSU rRNA genes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Specimen collection

A total of 33 samples of non‐geniculate coralline algae were collected from intertidal to subtidal zones in Korea between 2014 and 2024. The specimens were gathered manually with a hammer and chisel or by collecting stone and shell substrata containing visible mixtures of corallines from the intertidal zone or via scuba diving and dredging in the subtidal zone. The samples, which included both individual algae and substrata with coralline mixtures, were cleaned, air‐dried, and preserved with silica gel to maintain their integrity for future analysis. Additionally, the study benefited from examining nine type specimens of Lithothamnion on loan from herbarium TRH, Norway (Thiers, 2025). The voucher specimens from this comprehensive survey have been cataloged and are now stored in the herbaria of CUK and MABIK in Korea (Thiers, 2025).

Morpho–anatomical analyses

Only DNA confirmed specimens were used for the morpho‐anatomical studies. External morphology was observed under a stereomicroscope. Morpho‐anatomical observations were conducted using two approaches with a light microscope and a scanning electron microscope. For light microscopy (LM), silica‐dried samples were first decalcified in 0.6 M nitric acid, rinsed with distilled water, and then stained using a 3:7 mixture of 1% aqueous aniline blue and glycerin solution (10% glycerol to 90% absolute ethanol) for 30–120 min (Jeong et al., 2019). Thallus sections, 5–20 μm thick, were prepared using an embedding matrix (O.C.T.; CellPath, Newtown, Wales, United Kingdom) and a freezing microtome (Shandon Cryotome FSE; Thermo Shandon, Loughborough, United Kingdom). Sections were then treated with Mayer's hematoxylin (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, Pennsylvania, United States). Micrographs were taken using an Olympus microscope (BX51TRF; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an Olympus DP71 digital camera. Permanent slides were mounted in 70% Karo corn syrup. For scanning electron microscopy (SEM), selected fragments of dried samples were mounted on aluminum stubs using double‐sided adhesive carbon tape (Nisshin EM, Tokyo, Japan) and coated with 18–20 nm of gold using a digital ion sputter coater (SPR‐20, COXEM, Korea). Samples were examined in a COXEM EM‐30 PLUS SEM (Mini SEM; COXEM, Korea) at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. Anatomical and morphological terminology followed Chamberlain (1990) and Woelkerling (1993). For cell measurement, length refers to the distance between primary pit connections, whereas diameter indicates the maximum width of the cell lumen at right angles to length. Conceptacle measurements were conducted according to Adey and Adey (1973). Measurements were conducted using ImageJ analysis software.

DNA extraction, amplification, and phylogenetic analyses

DNA was extracted from 42 silica gel‐dried specimens, including 33 newly collected samples and nine type materials. Samples were cleaned with autoclaved seawater under a dissecting microscope to remove epiphytes, and endophytes and collected by scraping the thallus surface with a single‐edge razor blade. DNA extractions from both CUK field‐collected specimens and the type specimens were performed following the method described by Jeong et al. (2019), and these extractions were carried out at the Chosun University laboratory.

Amplifications and sequencing of the materials used two primer sets: F57/R1150 and F993/RrbcStart for the rbcL gene (Feshwater & Rueness, 1994) and G01/G08 and G04/G07 for the SSU rRNA region, following the protocol of Jeong et al. (2022). Primer psbAF1/psbAR2 (Yoon et al., 2002) was used for the psbA gene according to the protocol of Kim et al. (2012). These primers were also used in cycle sequencing. Sequencing of specimens extracted was performed by Macrogen (Seoul, South Korea). Sequences were deposited in GenBank (Table S1). Sequences were determined for both forward and reverse strands using an ABI prism 3100 Genetic Analyzer (Life Technologies, Seoul, Korea). The sequences were aligned using ClustalW (Thompson et al., 1994) and manually edited using Geneious Prime (v.2023.0.1; Biomatters Ltd., Auckland, New Zealand).

Phylogenetic trees were inferred from the rbcL gene, psbA, and SSU rRNA gene sequence data. All sequences used in the analyses are listed in Table S1. The dataset of concatenated sequences (rbcL + psbA + SSU rRNA) was aligned, with unavailable rbcL, psbA, or SSU rRNA gene sequences treated as missing data in our analysis. Additionally, we constructed phylogenetic trees for the rbcL, psbA, and SSU rRNA gene datasets. Sporolithon tenue and S. ptychoides were selected as outgroups. The sequences were aligned using ClustalW (Thompson et al., 1994) and manually edited using Geneious Prime (v.2023.0.1; Biomatters Ltd., Auckland, New Zealand). To determine the best partition scheme and model evolution for the concatenated dataset of the rbcL, psbA, and SSU rRNA genes, we used PartitionFinder 2 (Lanfear et al., 2016). The selected model for all datasets was the general time‐reversible (GTR) model of nucleotide substitution with invariant sites and gamma‐distributed rates for the variable sites (GTR + Γ + I). The maximum likelihood (ML) analysis was estimated by the GTR + Γ + I model with 1000 bootstrap replications, using RAxMLGUI v1.5 (Silvestro & Michalak, 2012). Bayesian inference (BI) was conducted using MrBayes 3.2.6 (Ronquist et al., 2012), employing Metropolis‐coupled Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMCMC). To assess posterior probabilities, two runs were executed, each with four chains (one cold chain and three heated), for 2,000,000 generations, with tree sampling occurring every 1000 generations under the GTR + Γ + I evolutionary model. A burn‐in value of 25% was employed to eliminate suboptimal trees from the final consensus tree. Interspecific pairwise distances were estimated using the p‐distance model in Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis version 11 (MEGA11; Tamura et al., 2021).

RESULTS

Taxonomic results

Based on the phylogenetic analyses of the rbcL, psbA, and SSU rRNA gene sequences and morpho‐anatomical observations, three new species and a new combination of Boreolithothamnion are proposed.

Holotype: MABIK AL00101052, 06.xi.2014; intertidal, leg. S.Y.Jeong, T.O.Cho & D.B.M.

Type locality: Ha‐dong, Gapa Island, Daejeong‐eup, Seogwipo‐si, Jeju, Korea (33°09′49.85″ N, 126°16′22.69″ E)

Etymology: The specific epithet “acuminate” refers to acuminate protuberances of thallus.

Habitat and geographic distribution: Plants occur in the high to the low intertidal zone, ranging from sheltered to wave‐exposed areas. They are epilithic on rocks, small boulders, or pebbles. It is confirmed through DNA sequencing that they are found in the southern (Jeju) to the eastern (Busan, Pohang, Ulleung Island) regions of Korea.

Description: Plants non‐geniculate encrusting to warty, with short and terete individual protuberances. Protuberances less than 0.2 cm high, unbranched, acuminate or cylindrical in shape, up to 0.6–1.2 mm long and 0.7–1.0 mm wide, with rounded apex (Figure 1a–c). Thallus margins firmly to slightly loosely attached with white lines, but no free margins. Surface texture matte and granular due to the presence of abundant conceptacles. Epithallial cells in surface view form thickened calcareous ridges that surround central concavities (Figure 1d).

Vegetative anatomy: Thallus margins composed of plumose medullary filaments (Figure 1e). Thalli monomerous, dorsiventrally organized in the crustose portion (Figure 1f), and radially organized in protuberances. Haustoria absent. Thalli 91–268 μm thick and composed of medulla and cortex. Medulla plumose (non‐coaxial), comprising about seven to 14 cell layers, 25–57 μm thick, and usually less than 21%–32% of the mature thallus thickness. Medullary cells rectangular to elongated with rounded corners, 11–16 μm long, and 3–5 μm wide. Downward curving medullary filaments terminate in more or less triangular‐shaped cells (Figure 1g). Cortex comprises the bulk of thallus thickness, 67–203 μm, and usually constitutes over 68%–79% of thallus thickness, except near the margins. Cortical cells ovoid to rectangular with rounded corners, 3–5 μm long and 4–6 μm wide (Figure 1h). Subepithallial initials (intercalary meristematic cells) rectangular with rounded corners, as long as or longer than the cells immediately subtending them, 3–6 μm long, and 3–5 μm wide (Figure 1h). Epithallial cells arranged in a single layer flattened and flared, 1–3 μm long, and 3–5 μm wide (Figure 1h). Cell fusions abundant among cortical and medullary cells, frequently occupying most of the walls of adjoining cells (Figure 1i). Secondary pit connections and trichocytes not observed.

Reproductive anatomy: Tetrasporangial conceptacles multiporate, slightly raised to low‐domed, 47–74 μm, lacking rims, not fused with adjacent conceptacles, above surrounding thallus surface (Figure 2a,b), 141–318 μm in external diameter. Pore plates rounded and approximately 165–186 μm in diameter, with up to five to 12 pores (Figure 2c). Pores irregularly distributed on the pore plate (Figure 2c), surrounded by a rosette of six to 12 cells, and neither protudent nor sunken in relation to the cells of the surrounding roof surface (Figure 2d). Tetrasporangial conceptacle chambers rounded to elliptical, 136–227 μm in diameter, and 59–136 μm in height (Figure 2e). Conceptacle primordia develop from a small group of subepithallial initials located in the dorsal region of the thallus, consisting of five cell layers or fewer (Figure 2f). Tetrasporangia scattered across the chamber floor or periphery to the wall, zonately divided, 19–52 μm in diameter and 50–118 μm in height (Figure 2g,h). Roofs of tetrasporangial conceptacles 13–19 μm in thickness and composed of pore canals, pore canal lining filaments, and roof filaments (Figure 2i). Pore canals of mature conceptacles blocked by apical plugs, 4–6 μm in diameter and 9–14 μm in height (Figure 2i). Pore canal lining filaments composed of three to five rounded to slightly elongated cells, of which the second ones are longer than the upper or downward cells. Senescent tetrasporangial conceptacles not buried in the tissue, infilled with irregularly arranged cells (Figure 2e). Gametangial plants not observed.

Phylogenetic analyses

A total of 25 sequences were obtained for the rbcL gene (1351 bp), 31 sequences for the psbA gene (845 bp), and 19 sequences for the SSU rRNA gene (1702 bp with gaps) from samples collected in Korea (Table S1). Additionally, we obtained one rbcL and eight psbA gene sequences from type materials loaned from TRH. The phylogenetic tree, constructed from the concatenated dataset (rbcL + psbA + SSU rRNA), revealed that our samples nested within the genus Boreolithothamnion in the family Hapalidiaceae (Figure 5). The phylogenetic tree resolved 12 supported lineages in Boreolithothamnion. Four new lineages within Boreolithothamnion were recognized as distinct species from congeners: B. acuminatum sp. nov., B. homoglobosum sp. nov., B. jejuensis sp. nov., and B. japonicum comb. nov. Although there were slight differences in the detailed phylogenetic relationships of some species, the phylogenetic tree derived from the concatenated dataset showed overall consistency with the individual rbcL, psbA, and SSU rRNA gene sequences (Figures [Link], [Link]). Interspecific sequence divergences were observed as follows: Between B. acuminatum sp. nov. and B. homoglobosum sp. nov., divergences ranged from 10.7% to 11.1% for the rbcL gene, 7.9% to 8.1% for the psbA gene, and 2.3% to 2.5% for the SSU rRNA gene; between B. acuminatum sp. nov. and B. jejuensis sp. nov., divergences ranged from 10.9% to 12.2% for the rbcL gene, 9.5% to 9.7% for the psbA gene, and 1.5% for the SSU rRNA gene; and between B. homoglobosum sp. nov. and B. jejuensis sp. nov., divergences ranged from 10.8% to 10.9% for the rbcL gene, from 7.3% to 7.4% for the psbA gene, and were 1.3% for the SSU rRNA region. Additionally, sequence divergences for the rbcL, psbA, and the SSU rRNA genes between B. acuminatum and its congeners ranged from 7.9% to 12.2%, 7.6% to 9.7%, and 1.0% to 2.5%, respectively; between B. homoglobosum and its congeners, from 8.6% to 11.3%, 6.0% to 8.1%, and 1.3% to 2.5%; between B. jejuensis and its congeners, from 8.5% to 2.6%, 6.9% to 9.7%, and 0.7% to 1.5%; and between B. japonicum and its congeners, from 1.8% to 11.1%, 0.8% to 8.0%, and 0.1% to 2.3%, respectively.

We successfully obtained sequences from various types of materials of Lithothamnion taxa. The 266 bp rbcL gene sequence from the holotype of Lithothamnion intermedium f. nanum (TRH B8‐2155) clustered with Boreolithothamnion glaciale (Figure 5 and Figure S1). Additionally, psbA gene sequences were successfully obtained: 455 bp from the lectotype of L. apiculatum f. connatum (TRH B20‐2669), 845 bp from the lectotype of L. dehiscens (TRH B22‐2727), 845 bp from the holotype of L. fornicatum f. sphaericum (TRH B26‐2789), 845 bp from the lectotype of L. fruticulosum f. fastigiatum (TRH B25‐2777), and 832 bp from the lectotype of L. ungeri f. corymbiforme (TRH B27‐2804). All these sequences clustered with B. glaciale (Figure 5 and Figure S2). The 524 bp psbA gene from the lectotype of L. fornicatum (TRH B21‐2712) grouped with B. soriferum, whereas the 425 bp psbA gene from the holotype of L. japonicum (TRH C16‐3267) and the 527 bp psbA gene from the holotype of “L. fretense” (TRH C15‐3228) were grouped within the B. japonicum comb. nov. clade (Figure 5 and Figure S2). Boreolithothamnion japonicum, known as an endemic species in the Northwest Pacific, is closely related to B. phymatodeum, with sequence divergence values of 1.8%–3.2% for the rbcL gene, 1.0%–1.6% for the psbA gene, and 0.1% for the SSU rRNA region.

DISCUSSION

Based on molecular (rbcL, psbA, and SSU rRNA genes) and morphological data, we have recognized three new species and one new combination within Boreolithothamnion from Korea: B. acuminatum sp. nov., B. homoglobosum sp. nov., B. jejuensis sp. nov., and B. japonicum comb. nov. The genetic divergence values for the rbcL, psbA, and SSU rRNA genes among Boreolithothamnion species supported their identification as distinct taxa, with the newly described species exhibiting divergences of 7.9%–12.6% (rbcL gene) and 6.0%–9.7% (psbA gene) from other species within the genus. These results align with previous studies (e.g., Coutinho et al., 2021; Gabrielson et al., 2011; Torrano‐Silva et al., 2018), which reported interspecific variation in rbcL (1.1%–10.9%) and psbA (1.0%–13.7%) genes among other coralline algae. These results highlight the critical role of molecular tools in resolving taxonomic uncertainties of non‐geniculate coralline algae.

In our morphological observations, Boreolithothamnion acuminatum was distinguished from other species within the genus by its encrusting to warty growth form, short acuminate to cylindrical unbranched protuberances, and lack of buried tetrasporangial conceptacles infilled with irregularly arranged cells (Table 1). It also features irregularly scattered pores on the tetra/bisporangial conceptacle pore plate and bordered pore canals with three to five roof cells. Boreolithothamnion homoglobosum was distinguished by a fruticose and unattached rhodolith with simple to bifurcated claviform protuberances (Table 1). Boreolithothamnion jejuensis was characterized by an encrusting to undulating or knobby growth form without protuberances (Table 1). Although a few tetra‐ and bisporangial conceptacles were observed and carposporophytes were absent, these new species share important morphological traits that support their classification within Boreolithothamnion, including flattened and flared epithallial cells, subepithallial initials that are as long as or longer than their immediate inward derivatives, and rosette cells that are flush with the surface in tetrasporangial conceptacle (Gabrielson et al., 2023).

Recent advancements in methods for extracting DNA from older specimens have significantly aided in resolving taxonomic challenges in non‐geniculate coralline algae, particularly for type specimens (Adey et al., 2015; Basso et al., 2015; Gabrielson et al., 2018, 2023; Hernandez‐Kantun et al., 2015; Hind et al., 2015; Richards et al., 2017; Sissini et al., 2014). In this study, we successfully amplified rbcL or psbA gene sequences from nine type specimens, allowing for species‐level and sub‐species‐level identifications. Our phylogenetic analysis of the rbcL gene revealed that the holotype of Lithothamnion intermedium f. nanum (TRH B8–2155), which has been considered a synonym of Lithothamnion ungeri f. nanum based on morphological data by Foslie (1898) and Woelkerling et al. (2005), is Boreolithothamnion glaciale. Similarly, the lectotype of L. ungeri f. corymbiforme (TRH B27–2804) clustered within B. glaciale in the psbA gene analysis, despite being sunk under L. ungeri based on morpho‐anatomical evidence (Athanasiadis, 2016). Additionally, the lectotype of L. dehiscens (TRH B22‐2727) also grouped with B. glaciale in the psbA gene analysis. Although Foslie (1900b) synonymized L. dehiscens with L. formicatum based on morphology, our phylogenetic analysis indicates that L. dehiscens is a distinct species from L. formicatum and is a junior heterotypic synonym of B. glaciale. Furthermore, the psbA gene analysis showed that the lectotype of L. apiculatum f. connatum (TRH B20‐2669), the holotype of L. fornicatum f. sphaericum (TRH B26‐2789), and the lectotype of L. fruticulosum f. fastigiatum (TRH B25‐2777) all clustered within B. glaciale, confirming previous results based on rbcL gene sequences reported by Peña et al. (2021). Our psbA gene analysis also indicated that the lectotype of L. fornicatum (TRH B21‐2712), which had been reassigned to B. soriferum (Gabrielson et al., 2023; Peña et al., 2021), clusters with B. soriferum. These results are consistent with our molecular analysis of the psbA gene (Figure S2).

The discovery of four Boreolithothamnion species in Korea significantly expands our understanding of their geographic distribution across the Northern Hemisphere (Figure 6). Boreolithothamnion japonicum is widely distributed along the coasts of Korea, Japan, and China, whereas B. acuminatum occurs primarily in southern and eastern regions of Korea. Boreolithothamnion homoglobosum and B. jejuensis are confined to Jeju Island, indicating highly localized distributions (Figure 6). Although B. giganteum (as Lithothamnion giganteum), B. lemoineae (as L. lemoineae), and B. phymatodeum (as L. pacificum) were previously documented as Lithothamnion species in Korea (Lee, 2008; Lee & Kang, 2002), their presence in Korea was not verified in this study. Most Boreolithothamnion species have been previously documented mainly in cold‐water habitats in the Pacific United States, Pacific Mexico, Atlantic Canada, Greenland, Norway, the United Kingdom, Germany, and Ireland (Gabrielson et al., 2023; Peña et al., 2021), except B. corallioides, which has cold and warm water habitats (Melbourne et al., 2017). The presence of Boreolithothamnion species in Korea suggests a wider ecological range than previously thought, pointing to their potential adaptability across various marine environments. This result highlights the significance of the Korean marine ecosystem in promoting biodiversity and raises questions about the biogeographic patterns and dispersal mechanisms that might enable such distribution shifts.
