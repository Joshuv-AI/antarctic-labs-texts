Variation in seed and kernel oil characteristics of wild Litsea cubeba influenced by major ecological factors across southern China
Authors: Guoxiang Liao, Xiaoyi Pan, Xiaodan Ning, Guorong Fan, Shangxing Chen, Lu Zhang, Zongde Wang, Yuling Yang, Jiawei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLitsea cubeba is an important industrial plant valued for its pericarp essential oil and kernel oil. However, variation in seed traits and kernel oil characteristics among wild populations and their relationships with ecological factors remain insufficiently understood. Wild germplam from 21 provenances across eight provinces in southern China was evaluated for seed traits, kernel oil content, and fatty acid composition, and variance analysis, correlation and path analysis, and multivariate ecological analyses were used to characterize phenotypic diversity and examine its associations with environmental variables.ResultsAnalysis of variance showed that the investigated provenances differed significantly in seed traits and kernel oil characteristics (P < 0.05). Provenances ZJ-LY and SC-KJ exhibited relatively high kernel oil content (≥ 35%), whereas YN-XC, GZ-ZY, and SC-KJ showed higher seed yields (SY, ≥ 1000 g.tree−1). In addition, kernel oil from Yunnan provenances was characterized by relatively high lauric acid content (C12:0, ≥ 60%). Variance partitioning analysis revealed that provenance effects accounted for the majority of variation in seed and kernel oil characteristics. Seed and its kernel oil yield showed greater diversity than its oil content and components. Based on the RDA analyses, annual average relative humidity, annual average rainfall, soil pH, soil maximum water-holding capacity, soil total potassium, soil water content, and longitude were identified as the main environmental correlates of seed and kernel oil characteristics. Greater kernel oil accumulation was associated with higher annual minimum temperature, higher soil total potassium, and higher latitude, whereas less favorable fatty acid composition was associated with higher soil water content and longitude; in addition, greater C12:0 accumulation was associated with higher annual maximum temperature and higher soil water content.ConclusionsThese findings reveal substantial variation in seed traits and kernel oil characteristics among wild L. cubeba populations in southern China. Provenances ZJ-LY and SC-KJ are promising candidates for breeding programs targeting high kernel oil content. Moreover, the identified associations of kernel oil accumulation and fatty acid composition with annual minimum temperature, soil water status, and soil nutrients provide useful guidance for germplasm selection and cultivation management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08907-x.

Introduction

Litsea cubeba (Lour.) Pers., a dioecious aromatic plant species belonging to the Lauraceae family, is widely distributed across southern China and Southeast Asia [1, 2]. This economically important species is valued for its fruits, which are rich in both essential oil and kernel oil [3, 4]. The essential oil of L. cubeba has diverse applications in food flavoring [5], pharmaceutical [6, 7], cosmetics [8], and pest control [9]. L. cubeba kernel oil (LCKO) is abundantly rich in medium-chain saturated fatty acids (MCFAs), especially lauric acid [10]. Its value can be comprehensively summarized in three main aspects: health functionality, food industry applications, and potential as bioenergy or chemical feedstock. Regarding health functionality, it has been reported that MCFAs contribute to the regulation of glycolipid metabolism, the balance of gut microbiota, the control of obesity, and neuroprotection [11–14]. Additionally, the distinctive fatty acid composition of LCKO supports its potential application in food processing, for example, in the preparation of oleofoams and edible oils [15, 16]. Furthermore, its favorable fatty acid constitution and fractionation characteristics render it a promising raw material for the production of bio-lubricants and biodiesel [17, 18].

Seed and kernel oil characteristics are extremely important phenotypic traits to estimate the potential production and quality of LCKO [19]. These economically important phenotypic traits of trees are shaped by a complex interplay between provenance-related differences and environmental factors [20, 21]. In general, phenotypic variation may reflect underlying biological differences among provenances as well as environmental influences [22–24], which determines morphological traits and secondary metabolite production. Tree phenotypes can also be affected and selected by various habitat factors [25]. From an evolutionary perspective, the diversification of plant species such as L. cubeba is driven by the interaction between provenance-related variation and selective pressures exerted by the environment [26]. The combination of long-term environmental exposure and geographic isolation has likely contributed to provenance differentiation [27, 28]. Phenotypic differentiation observed across populations of L. cubeba may reflect the combined influence of provenance-associated differences and environmental heterogeneity [29, 30]. These rich phenotypic variations, including variations in seed and kernel oil characteristics of wild L. cubeba, can be exploited to breed fine cultivars that produce large amounts of kernel oil. Understanding the relationships between phenotypes and environmental factors will also provide a theoretical basis for breeding programs and for optimizing the cultivation conditions for L. cubeba [31]. Previous studies on L. cubeba have surveyed a range of ecological resources [32], explored the processing and utilization of kernel oil [33] and essential oil [34–36], and uncovered aspects of its molecular biology [37, 38]. However, systematic evaluation of seed traits and kernel oil characteristics across wild L. cubeba provenances remains limited, and quantitative analyses of their relationships with major habitat factors are still lacking. Previous studies have reported significant variations in leaf and fruit morphology among different provenances, and have analyzed the essential oil composition of the pericarp, but almost no attention has been paid to the seed and kernel oils [31, 39]. Previous studies have shown that environmental factors such as available moisture, temperature, latitude, and elevation significantly affect the formation and accumulation of fatty acids in various plants, for example, Trigonella foenum-graecum, Salvia hispanica L., Lallemantia iberica L., and Cuphea (Lythraceae) [40–43]. Therefore, it is important to determine how habitat and environmental factors affect the characteristics of seed and kernel oils in wild L. cubeba populations. Research on the phenotypic diversity of seed and kernel oil characteristics among populations is crucial for an accurate assessment of the potential to cultivate this plant and fully utilize its oils and fatty acids.

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive survey of 21 L. cubeba provenances from eight provinces in China, and characterized their seed and kernel oil characteristics. The phenotypic diversity of seed and kernel oil characteristics of wild L. cubeba were compared among provenances, and the relationship between environmental factors and phenotypic traits was explored by correlation analysis, multiple stepwise regression analysis, and redundancy analysis (RDA). Accordingly, the objectives of this study were to evaluate variation in seed and kernel oil characteristics among wild L. cubeba provenances across southern China and to identify the habitat factors that most strongly influence this variation. The results provide new information for germplasm conservation, elite provenance selection, and the commercial cultivation of L. cubeba for kernel oil production.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The survey was conducted from August 1 to 30 in 2020 and 2021, and included 21 provenances of Litsea cubeba (Lour.) Pers. in eight representative provinces in China [Anhui (AH), Fujian (FJ), Guizhou (GZ), Hunan (HN), Jiangxi (JX), Sichuan (SC), Yunnan (YN), and Zhejiang (ZJ)] (Fig. 1). For each of the 21 investigated provenances, 25 wild female individuals meeting the sampling criteria were randomly selected, giving a total of 525 sample individuals (Table S1). All sampled trees were female individuals in good growth condition and free from visible disease symptoms. The sampled trees were generally located in relatively open sites without obvious shading, and obviously unhealthy, poorly growing, or strongly shaded individuals were avoided as far as possible. The distance between individuals was more than 100 m to avoid collecting clones [31]. From the 525 trees, 134 individuals were selected to evaluate tree growth and harvest whole fresh fruits to determine the fruit yield and the seed and kernel oil characteristics. In addition, L. cubeba essential oil (LCEO) samples were prepared from fresh fruits gathered from 103 individual trees representing 16 provenances. The five provenances FJ-LC, GZ-ZF, YN-MG, YN-SM, and YN-XC were excluded from the LCEO analysis because the quantity of fresh fruit samples collected from these provenances was insufficient for essential oil extraction. Therefore, only the 16 provenances with available LCEO data were included in analyses involving LCEO to ensure data matching and analytical consistency. The LCEO was extracted from the fresh fruit of each individual by steam distillation according to previously reported procedures [39]. The botanical identification of all Litsea cubeba (Lour.) Pers. samples was confirmed by Dr. Jiawei Wang and Prof. Shangxing Chen. Voucher specimens were deposited at the College of Forestry, Jiangxi Agricultural University (JXAU), China. These specimens are preserved there and can be accessed for reference purposes (e.g., collector number GZ-ZY-360).Fig. 1Schematic representation of the sampling locations for 21 provenances across eight provinces in southern China

Seed characteristics

Seed-related characteristics were derived from fruit phenotypic data previously reported by our research group [31] and were used in this study to support kernel oil yield estimation and subsequent ecological association analyses. Fruits were individually harvested from each selected tree at full maturity for seed trait determination. After removal of fruit stalks, 50 fruits per individual were randomly selected to measure seed moisture content (SMC) and seed-to-fruit ratio (SFR), with each measurement performed in triplicate.

Based on these seed-related measurements, seed yield per plant (SY) was calculated using fruit yield and fresh flesh rate. To account for variation in tree size among individuals, seed yield per unit basal diameter (SY/BD) was calculated by normalizing SY to the basal diameter of each tree. These yield-related parameters were then used to evaluate seed productivity and biomass allocation efficiency across different provenances.

Kernel oil determination and extraction

The kernel oil content was determined using a nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) oil content analyzer and expressed on a dry weight basis. Kernel oil (LCKO) was extracted from dried kernels via Soxhlet extraction with petroleum ether (boiling range 60–90 °C) as the solvent, following the method of Zhuang et al. (2018) with minor modifications [44]. Based on our preliminary optimization experiments (Supporting Information, Method S1), approximately 10 g of dried kernel powder was extracted at a solid-to-solvent ratio of 1:14 (g.mL−1) for 180 min under reflux conditions. The solvent was subsequently removed under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator, and the residual oil was dried to constant weight. The obtained kernel oil was then used for fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) preparation prior to fatty acid composition analysis. All kernel oil-related analyses were performed with three biological replicates.

Fatty acid composition of kernel oil

To characterize the kernel oil composition of L. cubeba, the fatty acids in the extracted oil samples were initially converted into fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs). Next, 150 µL of each sample was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate to remove any residual moisture. Subsequently, 1 mL of the resultant mixture was carefully transferred into a vial with a 2 mL headspace using a sterile syringe fitted with an organic solvent-compatible needle filter (13 mm × 0.22 µm pore size) for gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis.

The GC–MS platform employed in this study was based on the setup described in our previous study [39], with modifications made to the oven temperature program. Helium served as the carrier gas. The oven temperature was programmed as follows: it was initially set to 90 °C and held for 1 min (with a solvent delay of 3 min), then increased to 120 °C at a rate of 5 °C.min−1 and held for 1 min, and finally raised to 240 °C at a rate of 10 °C.min−1 and maintained for 1 min. The GC–MS conditions were as follows: ion source temperature of 230 °C, electron energy of 70 eV, GC–MS interface temperature of 250 °C, and a scanning range of 50–650 m.z−1. Three biological replicates were analyzed for each LCKO sample to determine its composition. Components were identified by comparing the retention times of LCKO constituents with those of authentic standards obtained from the Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (http://www.aladdin-e.com) and further validated against the NIST 17. L Mass Spectrometry Library. Fatty acid composition was quantified as the relative percentage of total fatty acids.

Ecological factors

The geographical features of the sampling points were recorded using the 2bulu softwar (https://www.2bulu.com). Climatic data were sourced from the China Meteorological Administration (http://www.cma.gov.cn), which provided the best available estimates for each site based on extensive regional data. The climatic variables obtained encompassed average annual temperature (AAT), average annual maximum temperature (AAMaxT), average annual minimum temperature (AAMinT), average annual relative humidity (AARH), and average annual rainfall (AAR). A comprehensive summary of the geographical characteristics and climatic conditions for each provenance collection site is presented in Table S1.

In this study, Rhizosphere soil physicochemical data corresponding to the 21 provenance sites were derived from a previously published dataset generated by our research group [39]. The rhizosphere soil samples were collected concurrently with the fruit sampling at the same provenance sites. Fan et al. (2023) described in detail the soil sampling procedures and measurement protocols, including soil water content (SWC), soil bulk density (SBD), soil maximum water holding capacity (SMaxWHC), soil minimum water holding capacity (SMinWHC), soil porosity (SP), soil pH (SpH), soil total organic carbon (STOC), soil total phosphorus (STPC), soil total nitrogen (STNC), and soil total potassium (STKC). These soil variables were integrated in this study only to explore their associations with the variation in seed and kernel oil characteristics.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to evaluate 22 major phenotypic characteristics (including seed and kernel oil characteristics) across all samples. Statistical calculations, including mean, standard error (SE), standard deviation (SD), range (maximum-minimum), coefficient of variation (CV), and Shannon–Weiner diversity index (H′), were performed using R software (https://www.r-project.org). The CV was utilized to assess the degree of phenotypic trait dispersion, as outlined by Zhang et al. [45]. The Shannon–Weiner diversity index, which provides a measure of phenotypic diversity within each plant population, was calculated as described by Xin et al. [46]. A nested analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by multiple comparison analysis (LSD test, P ≤ 0.05), and hierarchical clustering based on Euclidean squared distances were conducted to discern patterns among the 22 primary phenotypes of L. cubeba. In addition, we applied multiple regression path analysis, Pearson’s correlation analysis, detrended correspondence analysis (DCA), Redundancy Analysis (RDA), and variance partitioning analysis (VPA) to examine the relationships between 18 environmental factors and the 20 major phenotypes. All statistical analyses were performed within the R 4.4.0 environment.

Results

Diversity characteristics on seed and kernel oil of wild L. cubeba

The survey of L. cubeba germplasm resources revealed substantial phenotypic diversity. The results show that L. cubeba exhibits a wide range of trait variations, especially in kernel oil composition, with significant differences between the minimum and maximum values for most phenotypic traits, except for the relative proportions of C12:1, C14:0, and C18:0 (Table S2). Decenoic acid (C10:1), myristoleic acid (C14:1), and palmitoleic acid (C16:1) were detected in only a limited number of provenances. The CV for the 22 phenotypes of L. cubeba ranged from 9.24% (SFC, saturated fatty acid content) to 230.36% (LCKOY). The lower CV for SFC than that of other traits (Table S2) suggests relatively limited numerical variation. In contrast, its comparatively high Shannon–Wiener index indicates greater richness and evenness in the graded distribution of this trait. Among the 22 phenotypes examined for each provenance, LCKOC, C12:1, C14:0, unsaturated fatty acid content (UFC), and SFC showed higher diversity (H' ≥ 2.00), while SY exhibited the lowest diversity (H' = 0.78) (Table S2). The value of H' appeared to be somewhat related to sample size, highlighting the importance of appropriate sample selection in studies on germplasm resources. The nested ANOVA revealed significant differences among the 22 phenotypes within the population. Additionally, SY/BD, C10:0, C12:1, and UFC showed significant variation within the population (Table S3).

The distribution of trait values for L. cubeba did not show any clear regional patterns. No significant global spatial autocorrelation was detected for LCKOC, UFC/SFC, or C12:0 at the provenance level based on Moran’s I (all P > 0.05), further suggesting that geographically adjacent provenances did not consistently exhibit more similar values for these key traits. Across all provenances, the average LCKOC was 26.95%. The LCKOC ranged from 13.13% (GZ-DF) to 43.98% (SC-KJ) and was low in GZ-TJ (13.86%). Ecological factors affecting seed and kernel oil characteristics

The original data of phenotypic characteristics were subjected to a DCA. As summarized in Table S9, the gradient lengths for all sorting axes were less than 3 (DCA 1 = 1.668, DCA 2 = 0.931, DCA 3 = 0.534, and DCA 4 = 0.611), indicating that the data were appropriate for RDA with a linear model. We used 19 phenotypic characteristics of L. cubeba as response variables and included 17 environmental factors and the soil factor SpH as explanatory variables. The RDA model results were significant (p = 0.001) and indicated that most of the environmental factors were associated with the phenotypic diversity of L. cubeba. Based on the constructed RDA model, we found that the three factors SBD, SMinWHC, and SP were not significant. Collectively, 15 environmental factors explained 47.97% of the variation in phenotypic characteristics. The first axis accounted for 21.95% of the variation, and the second axis explained an additional 7.30% (Table S10). Thus, the first two axes in the RDA effectively captured the relationship between environmental factors and the phenotypic characteristics of L. cubeba.

According to the correlations between environmental factors and phenotypic characteristics of L. cubeba, we applied a forward selection method to identify the key environmental variables contributing to phenotypic variation among provenances (Fig. 5). The first RDA axis (RDA1) was positively correlated with variables such as SpH, AARH, UFC, UFC/SFC, C18:1, C18:2, C16:0, and C18:0, and negatively correlated with longitude, SWC, SMaxWHC, and AAR. The second axis (RDA2) was strongly positively associated with STNC, C14:0, STOC, and SMC, and strongly negatively associated with LCKOC, STKC, SY/BD, LCKOY, SY, FY, and SWC. Discussion

Seed phenotypic characteristics of wild L. cubeba

The fruit of L. cubeba is an important economic trait contributing to the commercial value of this species [47]. Previous studies have reported substantial variation in fruit-related traits among diverse L. cubeba provenances [48]. Our findings further confirm pronounced phenotypic diversity in seed- and oil-related traits among wild provenances from southern China. The relatively low Shannon–Wiener index suggests that phenotypic diversity in wild L. cubeba populations may be somewhat constrained compared with that of other fruit crops [45]. Moreover, the marked variation observed in yield- and oil-related traits indicates substantial provenance differentiation and highlights the potential value of these wild resources for germplasm conservation and selective utilization. These results are generally consistent with those reported by Fan et al. [39]. In addition, the major fatty acid composition of LCKO is similar to that of palm oil (Elaeis guineensis), suggesting its potential application value as an edible oil resource [49]. However, despite this overall similarity, the fatty acid composition of LCKO still showed considerable diversity among provenances, with decanoic acid, myristic acid, and palmitic acid detected only in certain provenances. The extensive diversity of seed and kernel oil observed under different environmental conditions illustrates the high degree of adaptability exhibited by L. cubeba in complex habitats [31].

The geographical distribution range of L. cubeba encompasses multiple ecological regions within China [1]. We detected notable differences in SY/BD, C10:0, C12:1, CD, TH, and LCEOC across the distribution range, which could be indicative of evolutionary divergence because of geographical isolation, with different provenances growing in substantially different microenvironments. Significant trait variations across different geographic regions have been documented in other plants, such as Nicotiana glauca [50], Populus euphratica [51], and Carya illinoinensis [52], suggesting that evolutionary divergence within species is a common response to geographic and environmental heterogeneity. Previous studies have also reported that the phenotypic diversity of L. cubeba is largely influenced by the geographical location [53]. In addition, other studies have shown that specific environmental conditions can lead to variations in key traits [54]. Future research integrating molecular approaches will be needed to further clarify the joint roles of provenance-related differences and environmental heterogeneity in shaping phenotypic diversity.

Fan et al. (2023) explored the geographical distribution pattern of the essential oil content and its components in L. cubeba fruits. Interestingly, no statistically significant correlation was detected between essential oil content and kernel oil content and their composition [39]. This might be because there are different biosynthetic pathways for kernel oil and essential oil [55, 56]. The primary constituents of essential oil are terpenoids, which are derived from the mevalonate pathway and the non-mevalonate pathway [57, 58], whereas the predominant components of kernel oil are fatty acids, which are synthesized via fatty acid synthesis pathways [59].

Main ecological factors affecting seed phenotypic characteristics

The interaction between environmental factors and genetic variation plays a pivotal role in the development of plant phenotypic traits, resulting in notable diversity under varying environmental conditions [60]. Phenotypic diversity is frequently indicative of a species’ capacity to adapt to complex environments, and thus, it serves as a reliable indicator for assessing adaptability [61]. A similar study on Populus trichocarpa found that geographical location and environmental selection pressures are the primary factors influencing phenotypic differentiation among populations [62]. The results of our survey reveal close correlations among the morphological variations observed in different geographical locations of L. cubeba, suggesting that geographical factors play a crucial role in the formation of adaptive phenotype differentiation [63]. As elucidated by Gong et al. (2023), climatic factors strongly influence plant secondary metabolites [64]. Furthermore, the physical and chemical properties of soil have been demonstrated to significantly influence plant growth, development, and phenotypic variation [65, 66].

In the current study, the 15 environmental variables included in the RDA explained only 47.97% of the total phenotypic variation, suggesting that the measured habitat factors captured an important, but incomplete, component of phenotypic differentiation. The remaining unexplained variation may reflect the effects of unmeasured environmental variables, spatially structured factors, or stochastic processes that were not fully captured by the present model [67, 68]. It may also be associated with additional ecological variables not included in the present study, such as rhizosphere microorganisms, light availability, and belowground biotic interactions [69, 70]. Furthermore, genotypic variation and local adaptation are likely important drivers of phenotypic differences among provenances [71], and some degree of localized historical human disturbance cannot be completely excluded [72]. Therefore, the present RDA should be interpreted as identifying the major environmental correlates of phenotypic variation rather than exhaustively explaining all sources of variation.

Environmental factors collectively explained a substantial proportion of the phenotypic variation in seed and kernel oil characteristics of wild L. cubeba. Among them, AARH, SpH, SWC, longitude, SMaxWHC, AAR, and STKC appeared to be the key variables, indicating that climatic, soil, and geographic conditions jointly contribute to provenance differentiation in these traits. Path analysis further clarified the direct and indirect associations between these environmental factors and the representative kernel oil traits. A supplementary PLS-SEM analysis further supported this overall pattern at a broader structural level (Fig. S2). In this exploratory framework, geographical factors were more strongly associated with soil and climatic factors than directly with seed and kernel oil characteristics, whereas soil factors showed the strongest direct association with seed and kernel oil traits and were also positively associated with growth characteristics.

Previous studies have examined the relationship between environmental factors and fruit phenotypes, particularly in terms of lipid accumulation [73, 74]. The positive correlation between STKC and LCKOC suggests that potassium availability may be important for kernel oil accumulation in L. cubeba [75, 76]. This relationship is unlikely to be limited to a single step of lipid metabolism. Potassium is essential for the activity of several enzymes involved in primary carbon metabolism, such as pyruvate kinase, and may therefore influence the supply of pyruvate and acetyl-CoA for fatty acid biosynthesis [77, 78]. Moreover, adequate potassium supply has been associated with enhanced photosynthate transport and improved carbon allocation to sink tissues, which could favor storage lipid accumulation [79]. Potassium may also indirectly affect TAG assembly, as phosphatidic acid phosphatase-mediated diacylglycerol production is a necessary step in TAG biosynthesis [80, 81]. Therefore, STKC may represent an important environmental correlate of kernel oil accumulation in wild L. cubeba, although the precise molecular mechanisms remain to be clarified. The SWC was observed to restrict the ratio of unsaturated to saturated fatty acids in LCKO. Soil water status may influence photosynthetic carbon assimilation, assimilate transport, and carbon allocation to developing seeds, thereby affecting oil accumulation and fatty acid composition [82]. Drought stress can reduce the quantity and quality of oil in seeds, according to a study on dragon’s head (Lallemantia iberica) [83]. Soil physical properties may also influence the distribution of L. cubeba germplasm by affecting root growth, soil aeration, water availability, and habitat suitability [84]. In addition, although all sampled individuals were selected from naturally occurring wild populations and obviously cultivated or strongly disturbed trees were excluded as far as possible during field investigation, some degree of localized historical human disturbance cannot be completely excluded and may have contributed to part of the observed phenotypic variation among provenances [85].Therefore, these environmental factors are likely to be primary drivers of phenotypic diversity in L. cubeba. These results emphasize the importance of environmental factors in influencing the adaptive strategies and ecological niche of this species.

While this study focused on several key habitat factors, other ecological factors such as rhizosphere microorganisms, light availability, and the presence of other plants’ roots in the rhizosphere may also contribute to variation in the phenotypic traits of L. cubeba [86–88]. Furthermore, genotypic variation is likely the most direct driver of phenotypic differences. Previous research on sorghum (Sorghum bicolor) has shown that genotype plays a significant role in determining phenotypic traits [89]. In further studies, it would be beneficial to investigate how different environmental factors influence the phenotypic traits of trees of the same genotype [90]; and to evaluate the phenotypic variations among genotypically distinct lines cultivated under identical environmental conditions [91]. These factors exert a considerable influence on the seed characteristics of L. cubeba, offering valuable insights for optimizing cultivation conditions and interpreting its pioneering role in ecology. In conclusion, the findings of our research will be useful for devising strategies to protect the genetic diversity, select breeding resources, and increase the supply of this economically significant aromatic tree species [92, 93].

Conclusions

The seed phenotypic characteristics of L. cubeba exhibited significant variation among provenances from different geographical regions. Decanoic acid, myristic acid, and palmitic acid were identified as minor fatty acid components and were detected only in a limited number of provenances. ZJ − LY and SC − KJ showed the highest LCKOC, whereas YN − XC, GZ − ZY, and SC − KJ exhibited the highest SY, highlighting pronounced inter-provenance differentiation in seed characteristics. Multivariate analyses consistently indicated that climatic and soil factors were the principal drivers influencing seed and kernel oil characteristics. Among these, water-related variables, STKC, and longitude emerged as the main environmental correlates. Greater LCKOC was associated with higher AAMinT, STKC, and latitude, whereas a lower UFC/SFC was associated with higher SWC and longitude, while a higher UFC/SFC was associated with higher SBD, SMaxWHC, and SpH. In addition, greater C12:0 accumulation was associated with higher AAMaxT and SWC. Collectively, these findings suggest that ZJ-LY and SC-KJ are promising candidates for breeding programs targeting high kernel oil content, and provide useful information for the selection and utilization of germplasm resources under different environmental conditions. These results also provide a practical basis for the targeted selection of elite provenances, the optimization of cultivation strategies, and the further development of L. cubeba as a valuable woody oil resource for food and industrial applications.
