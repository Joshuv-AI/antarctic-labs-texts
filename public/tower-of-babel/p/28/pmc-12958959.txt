Anti-VEGF Agents and the Inner Retina in nAMD: A Narrative Review of RNFL and Ganglion Cell Changes
Authors: Georgios N Tsiropoulos, Fotis Topouzis, Winfried Amoaku, Georgios D Panos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy is the cornerstone of care for neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), yet its effects on the inner retina are not widely studied. We conducted a narrative review, guided by the SANRA checklist, to map evidence on peripapillary retinal nerve fibre layer (pRNFL), macular RNFL, and ganglion cell–derived metrics (GCL/GCIPL/GCC) during anti-VEGF treatment. Major databases were searched from inception to 10 November 2025 for human studies reporting optical coherence tomography (OCT) measurements of inner-retinal structure during anti-VEGF treatment. Data were charted for study design, OCT device/segmentation, scan protocol, timing relative to injections, treatment regimen, fluid phenotype (intraretinal fluid [IRF], subretinal fluid [SRF], pigment epithelial detachment [PED]), follow-up interval, and magnitude of change (µm). Across cohorts and agents, global pRNFL thickness was largely stable over weeks to years, with short-term peri-injection fluctuations typically within device test–retest limits. In contrast, macular ganglion cell layers more often showed modest thinning over time, most consistently in eyes with baseline IRF; SRF-predominant disease showed less consistent inner-retinal change. Apparent between-agent differences were small and inconsistent; evidence specific to faricimab remains limited but generally reassuring for structural stability. Overall, most observed changes were often within OCT test–retest variability. In practice, clinicians should prioritise GCL/GCIPL monitoring where IRF is present, interpret small absolute changes against device repeatability, and avoid attributing trivial sectoral shifts to drug toxicity without corroboration.Keywords: neovascular age-related macular degeneration, anti-VEGF, faricimab, retinal nerve fibre layer, ganglion cell layer, optical coherence tomography

Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) stands as a leading cause of vision loss in individuals aged 50 years and older, profoundly affecting quality of life and independence.1 AMD comprises two main types: dry (non-exudative) and wet (exudative or neovascular) AMD (nAMD).1 Non-exudative AMD is characterised by the presence of drusen and geographic atrophy, resulting in gradual central vision loss.2 Wet AMD is responsible for the majority of severe vision loss associated with the disease.3 This AMD form is characterised by the growth of abnormal choroidal neovascularisation (CNV), leading to leakage, haemorrhage, and subsequent damage to the retinal architecture.2 The global prevalence of AMD continues to rise due to ageing populations. Recent estimates indicate that AMD affects more than 190 million people worldwide, with wet AMD serving as a significant contributor to advanced disease cases. Understanding the pathophysiology and effective management of wet AMD remains crucial in reducing its visual and socioeconomic burden.4

The advent of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) agents has transformed the treatment landscape for wet AMD. VEGF plays a central role in the pathogenesis of neovascular AMD by promoting angiogenesis and increasing vascular permeability. The first anti-VEGF therapy, pegaptanib, received approval in 2004 and demonstrated modest efficacy in slowing disease progression.5 This was shortly followed by the approval of intravitreal ranibizumab (IVR) (Lucentis) in 2006, which offered more significant improvements in visual outcomes through its ability to bind and inhibit all isoforms of VEGF-A.6,7 Intravitreal bevacizumab (IVB) (Avastin), an off-label alternative originally developed for oncology, gained widespread adoption owing to its comparable efficacy to ranibizumab and lower cost.8,9 Subsequently, intravitreal aflibercept (IVA) (Eylea) was introduced in 2011, offering an extended duration of action by functioning as a decoy receptor for VEGF-A, VEGF-B, and placental growth factor (PlGF).10,11 A further addition to this therapeutic arsenal, intravitreal brolucizumab (IVBr) (Beovu), received approval in 2019, delivering similar efficacy with fewer injections due to its high binding affinity and smaller molecular size.12,13 Intravitreal faricimab (IVF), a novel bispecific antibody targeting both VEGF-A and angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2), has recently gained approval for wet AMD treatment, showing promise in reducing treatment frequency whilst maintaining efficacy.14 Moreover, a higher-dose formulation of aflibercept, known as Eylea 8mg, has received approval, offering an extended dosing interval that could further diminish the treatment burden for patients with wet AMD.15,16 These agents have collectively transformed the prognosis of wet AMD, enabling vision stabilisation and improvement for many patients. Retinal nerve fibre layer (RNFL) thickness, particularly in the peripapillary and macular regions, along with macular retinal ganglion cell layer (RGCL) thickness, represent essential parameters in assessing retinal integrity. Anti-VEGF therapies, whilst efficacious in treating CNV and preserving vision, may influence these structural layers.17–19

This narrative review aims to comprehensively synthesise the current evidence on the structural effects of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy in patients with neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD). Specifically, it focuses on changes in peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness, macular RNFL thickness, and macular retinal ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer (RGCL) thickness. By examining these retinal layers, the review seeks to identify potential structural alterations that may occur following repeated anti-VEGF injections. Understanding these changes is critical for assessing the long-term safety of anti-VEGF therapy and its implications for retinal health, as subtle structural damage could impact visual function over time. This review therefore provides a synthesis of current findings to inform clinicians and researchers about both the efficacy and potential retinal risks associated with anti-VEGF treatment in nAMD.

Methods

Design and Reporting Framework

This work is a narrative review. Reporting quality and methodological transparency were guided by the SANRA checklist for narrative review articles.20 We aimed to map the breadth of evidence on inner-retinal changes during anti-VEGF therapy for neovascular AMD and to summarise patterns rather than generate pooled effect estimates.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

We searched MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library from inception to 10 November 2025. Search strings combined controlled vocabulary and keywords for nAMD, anti-VEGF agents and inner-retinal layers. Reference lists of included studies and key reviews were screened, and forward citation tracking was performed for sentinel papers. In total, 200 records were initially identified. Of the 200 records initially identified, 70 remained after abstract screening and were full-text screened for eligibility; 34 studies were included in the review.

Selection of Evidence

Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and then full texts against eligibility criteria; disagreements were resolved by discussion or a third reviewer. Reasons for full-text exclusion were recorded.

Data Charting

A piloted form captured: study characteristics; treatment regimen (fixed/PRN/treat-and-extend) and number of injections; OCT device, scan protocol and segmentation boundaries; timing of imaging relative to injections; disease activity at baseline/follow-up (IRF, SRF, PED, iRORA/cRORA); and inner-retinal outcomes (mean change in µm with dispersion when available). Where necessary, we noted assumptions and limitations of extraction.

Synthesis

Given heterogeneity in devices, protocols and follow-up, we used narrative synthesis, structuring results by (i) layer/metric, (ii) timeframe, (iii) fluid phenotype and (iv) agent. Where reported, we commented on whether observed changes were within device test–retest limits to aid clinical interpretation. No quantitative pooling or formal risk-of-bias scoring was undertaken, consistent with the scoping–narrative design.

Overview of the Evidence

Across the assembled prospective and retrospective cohorts spanning ranibizumab, aflibercept, bevacizumab, brolucizumab and, to a limited extent, faricimab, a broadly consistent pattern emerges: inner retinal structure is largely preserved during anti-VEGF therapy for neovascular AMD over follow-up intervals ranging from months to years. Where deviations occur, they are typically small in magnitude, restricted to specific sectors (most often temporal), or temporally limited to the peri-injection period. This overarching signal is supported by multiple prospective series reporting stable global peripapillary RNFL (pRNFL) at 6–12 months,21–23 corroborated by longer-term observations in which measurable thinning is comparable between treated and contralateral or control eyes, consistent with ageing and disease rather than treatment toxicity.24,25 At the macula, the picture is more nuanced: macular RNFL tends to remain stable, while the ganglion cell complex (GCL/GCIPL) shows modest thinning in several cohorts—particularly in eyes with baseline intraretinal fluid (IRF)—implicating disease activity as a major driver.26–29 Characteristics of included studies and their principal findings are summarised in Table 1.

Summary of Studies

Peripapillary RNFL

Prospective studies form the backbone of inference for pRNFL. Jo et al found no significant difference in global pRNFL at 6 or 12 months after ranibizumab compared with fellow eyes, with only significant temporal changes.21 El Ashry and Bitirgen et al, in their prospective cohort studies, likewise demonstrated no meaningful changes between treated, fellow, or healthy eyes, even after an average of nearly nine injections.22,23 These findings were echoed by retrospective studies showing stability across different regimens.30–37 Where differences have been noted, such as in Entezari et al, the changes were transient and resolved by subsequent visits, suggesting short-lived biomechanical or perfusion effects rather than true axonal loss.38 Longer-term studies, including Valverde-Megías et al, reported gradual RNFL thinning, but this was similar between treated and fellow eyes, highlighting ageing and disease progression as the dominant drivers rather than anti-VEGF toxicity.24 In a long-term study of eyes receiving chronic anti-VEGF for nAMD, no harmful RNFL thinning was detected; moreover, the temporal pRNFL was relatively thicker in nAMD eyes, highlighting a sectoral baseline peculiarity that can mimic or mask change.39 Collectively, these results and their interpretations suggest that any true treatment effect on global pRNFL is small, sector-limited, and often within OCT test–retest limits. Immediate and Short-Term Effects

The immediate post-injection period is characterised by small fluctuations. Viggiano et al reported an acute reduction in pRNFL minutes after aflibercept, while Zhuang et al observed a temporary increase at one week, both resolving thereafter.40,41 These findings support a mechanistic model whereby transient intraocular pressure changes, choroidal expansion, and segmentation artefacts explain short-lived fluctuations without structural damage.

Macular Inner Layers

Macular RNFL is generally stable, but macular GCL/GCIPL demonstrates more consistent thinning.42,43 However, Aşikgarip et al44 reported both macular RNFL and macular RGCL thinning. Mantopoulos et al reported significant GCL-IPL decrease in the anti-VEGF treated eyes with neovascular AMD compared to their fellow non-exudative AMD eyes who were used as a control group.45 Gunay et al and Inan et al reported parafoveal thinning after one year of aflibercept treatment and ranibizumab treatment, respectively, and Abdolrahimzadeh et al observed progressive GCL loss over two years, greater in treated compared with fellow non-exudative AMD eyes.26,27,46 Zivković et al and Beck et al confirmed this vulnerability, whereas Zucchiati et al and Cao et al reported no significant change.43,47–49 This variability underscores that GCL thinning is more consistently associated with disease activity and baseline fluid (either intraretinal or subretinal) rather than direct treatment toxicity. Disease Activity and Fluid Phenotype as Key Modifiers

Dail et al demonstrated that eyes with baseline IRF exhibited greater GCL thinning, while Wichrowska et al showed thinner GCL in nAMD eyes compared with non-exudative AMD eyes, regardless of injection number.28,29 These findings highlight the modifying role of disease severity and activity, suggesting that much of the observed thinning may reflect chronic nAMD pathology rather than anti-VEGF exposure per se.

Comparisons Between Agents

Comparative studies offer mixed results. Lee et al observed macular RNFL reduction with ranibizumab but not aflibercept, though effect sizes were small.50 Ahn et al found minor decreases with both agents without consistent sectoral patterns, and Lüke et al reported numerically tiny changes of uncertain clinical significance.51,52 Brolucizumab and faricimab data remain limited, though early evidence suggests stability.14,31 Thus, differences between agents, if present, are small and inconsistent.

Injection Burden and Cumulative Exposure

Evidence linking injection number to RNFL or GCL thinning is weak and inconsistent. Sengul et al53 and Demirel et al37 found no correlation, while Parlak et al54 observed thinning only in a subgroup with higher injection numbers.37,53,54 Quadrant-specific associations, such as nasal thinning in Wichrowska et al, are difficult to separate from measurement variability.55 These mixed findings emphasise that disease activity, rather than injection frequency alone, is the likely driver.

Limitations

Substantial methodological heterogeneity complicates cross-study comparisons. Differences in OCT devices and segmentation boundaries, scan protocols and imaging schedules, and the timing of assessments relative to injections all contribute to variable results.35,40,41,50 Control group selection varied, as did treatment protocols (PRN versus fixed dosing), while sample sizes ranged from small single-centre cohorts to larger multicentre datasets, affecting precision and power.21,23,24,29,38,51,56 Many reported absolute changes approached device test–retest variability, complicating causal attribution. Most included evidence is observational, with potential confounding by age, baseline thickness and fluid phenotype (IRF/SRF/PED). Publication and language bias cannot be excluded. Consistent with a SANRA-guided scoping–narrative design, we did not perform formal risk-of-bias scoring or meta-analysis; these constraints should temper the interpretation of structural outcomes.20

Clinical Implications

Despite these complexities, the collective evidence suggests that anti-VEGF therapy is compatible with preservation of inner retinal structure at the population level. The pRNFL is generally stable, while macular GCL/GCIPL shows modest thinning in subsets of patients, particularly those with baseline IRF. From a clinical perspective, small changes should be interpreted cautiously, with attention to device variability, measurement timing, and disease activity. Routine OCT assessment in long-term treated nAMD patients should include evaluation of macular GCL/GCIPL alongside standard macular thickness maps, especially in eyes with persistent fluid or high disease burden.

Future Fields of Research Needed

Future research should prioritise large-scale, longitudinal prospective studies employing harmonised OCT protocols and standardised definitions of retinal layers, as current heterogeneity in methodology limits comparability across studies. Direct head-to-head comparisons between anti-VEGF agents, particularly newer agents such as faricimab and high dose aflibercept, are required to clarify potential differences in their effects on inner retinal integrity. Another important avenue worth investigating is exploring the relationship between structural changes and functional outcomes, incorporating visual field testing, microperimetry, and advanced functional biomarkers to determine the clinical relevance of observed thinning. Stratification by fluid phenotype and baseline disease severity should be incorporated systematically to better distinguish disease-driven degeneration from treatment effects. Furthermore, the influence of cumulative injection burden and extended dosing regimens on RNFL and RGCL requires rigorous evaluation. Finally, future studies should determine whether faricimab’s dual Ang-2/VEGF-A inhibition, which may be more effective than previous anti-VEGF agents in reducing IRF and SRF in exudative AMD patients, alters RNFL and GCL trajectories and, in parallel, evaluate neuroprotective or adjunctive approaches to maintain retinal ganglion cell integrity during sustained anti-VEGF therapy.

Conclusion

While anti-VEGF therapy remains the standard of care for nAMD, its effects on RNFL and RGCL thickness appear complex, and potentially dependent on multiple factors including treatment duration, injection frequency, and individual patient characteristics. Evidence suggests that while most patients maintain stable RNFL thickness, some may experience gradual thinning, particularly in the RGCL. These findings emphasize the importance of regular monitoring of retinal structural parameters during long-term anti-VEGF therapy, while acknowledging that the benefits of treatment in managing nAMD likely outweigh the potential risks of subtle structural changes. A more systematic evaluation if inner retinal changes in nAMD eyes undergoing intravitreal therapies is overdue and should be encouraged.
