Age differences in proactive and reactive control: valence asymmetries in the processing of complex emotional scenes
Authors: Ni Zhang, Xuechuan Sun, Jingxin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates age differences in proactive and reactive control during emotional scene processing, using complex scenes that require effortful top-down interpretation. Sixty-two participants (32 young and 30 older adults) completed an emotional AX-CPT task with positive, negative, and neutral scenes. Results revealed that the relative balance between proactive and reactive control in older adults was asymmetrically shaped by emotional valence when processing complex perceptually demanding stimuli. For positive scenes, older adults showed a relative reliance on proactive control, evidenced by higher Proactive Behavioral Index (PBI) scores. For negative scenes, they exhibited reduced proactive control, with higher error rates on neutral-negative sequences, whereas young adults showed the opposite pattern. Because the experimental design did not include a facial-expression comparison condition, conclusions about stimulus-type moderation are necessarily tentative. Nevertheless, these findings provide initial evidence that valence-specific control asymmetries are expressed under conditions of high conceptual and perceptual demand. Older adults appeared to differentially deploy control resources for positive versus negative stimuli under demanding conditions. This study provides new evidence that adaptive control is dynamically reshaped by the processing demands associated with complex emotional materials, offering a foundation for future research examining how stimulus complexity interacts with motivational priorities in aging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05138-5.

Introduction

Cognitive control in later life is increasingly understood not as uniform decline but as adaptive, context-dependent reorganization [1, 2]. This adaptability emerges from dynamic interactions between individual goals and task-specific demands. Among the contextual factors shaping this process, the processing demands imposed by emotional stimuli—specifically, the degree to which they require effortful conceptual elaboration versus rapid perceptual decoding—remain critical yet understudied moderators of age-related control strategies. Despite extensive research on emotional face processing in aging [3, 4], the cognitive control dynamics elicited by complex emotional scenes—arguably the dominant form of emotional information encountered in daily life (e.g., media, social environments, natural landscapes)—have received surprisingly little empirical attention [5, 6]. Emotional faces, for instance, benefit from high automaticity via conserved limbic and fusiform pathways, whereas complex emotional scenes require effortful, top-down conceptual elaboration that imposes substantial perceptual load [7, 8]. Whether such valence-specific control patterns generalize to stimuli imposing greater executive demands remains an open question, as perceptual load theory predicts that high-load contexts exhaust limited processing reserves and may fundamentally alter control strategy expression [8, 9]. Although direct empirical comparisons of perceptual load between faces and scenes are sparse, scenes typically contain greater visual and semantic complexity (e.g., multiple objects, contextual relations, and categorical ambiguity), which plausibly taxes working memory and executive resources to a greater extent than the decoding of isolated facial expressions [5]. This distinction raises the question of whether age-related differences in proactive and reactive control, previously documented primarily with facial expressions, generalize to stimuli that place heavier demands on executive resources.

To investigate how aging affects the strategic deployment of cognitive control under varying processing demands, we employ the Dual Mechanisms of Control (DMC) model. According to the DMC framework, the efficacy of goal maintenance depends on the interplay between top-down control settings and the attentional demands of the stimuli encountered: when stimuli engage relatively automatic pathways, attentional allocation and goal maintenance may proceed with minimal cognitive cost; when stimuli require effortful interpretation, sustained goal maintenance itself becomes resource-intensive [10, 11]. The DMC framework assumes that goal-directed behavior involves two dissociable processes: proactive control (sustained, anticipatory maintenance of goal representations) and reactive control (transient, stimulus-driven resolution of conflict) [1, 12]. A common observation in aging research is that older adults show reduced proactive control, ostensibly due to decreased neural efficiency in lateral prefrontal regions required for sustaining representations [13, 14]. However, this shift is not invariant. Emerging evidence indicates that emotional valence modulates control deployment, particularly for evolutionarily salient stimuli: older adults can maintain proactive control for positive information while becoming more reactive to negative information [15, 16]. This valence-specific pattern represents a cognitive control manifestation of the well-known "positivity effect"—the age-related tendency to favor positive over negative information in attention and memory [3].

Critically, these valence effects have been observed almost exclusively with facial expressions—a stimulus domain characterized by relatively automatic decoding and lower perceptual load [17]. For example, Zhang and Wang [16] employed an emotional AX-CPT in which positive, negative, and neutral faces served as cues and probes,older adults showed preserved proactive control (lower BX errors) for positive faces but shifted toward reactive control (higher BX errors) for negative faces, whereas young adults exhibited the opposite valence modulation. This empirical concentration leaves open a fundamental question: Do age-related valence asymmetries in cognitive control persist when stimuli require effortful, top-down interpretation?

Unlike faces, complex emotional scenes lack dedicated neural templates for rapid decoding; they must be constructed by integrating contextual elements, placing heavier demands on working memory, conceptual knowledge, and executive control [5]. Neuroimaging studies demonstrate divergent neural substrates: face processing relies on conserved limbic and fusiform pathways, whereas scene processing engages stronger and more sustained prefrontal activation [18]. From a cognitive aging perspective, the Scaffolding Theory of Aging and Cognition (STAC, [2, 19]) posits that older adults engage compensatory processes to maintain performance when task demands exceed automatic processing capabilities. The revised STAC-r model emphasizes that compensatory engagement is context-dependent, varying with task demands and motivational relevance. Thus, the stronger and more sustained prefrontal activation observed during scene processing may reflect compensatory recruitment patterns as older adults attempt to maintain performance under elevated conceptual demands. Recent evidence indicates that older adults can maintain proactive control when provided with adequate environmental support or motivational incentive [20, 21], challenging the view of a uniform shift to reactive control in aging. We hypothesize that when processing complex scenes, such compensation may manifest as asymmetric resource allocation: sustained investment of limited resources in goal maintenance for motivationally relevant (positive) stimuli, potentially at the cost of performance on low-priority (negative) stimuli. However, we acknowledge that because motivational states were not directly measured, this interpretation remains speculative and should be treated as one plausible account rather than a tested mechanism.

We used an emotional AX-CPT with positive, negative, and neutral scenes to investigate the proactive–reactive control trade-off in young and older adults. Drawing on the DMC framework and perceptual load theory, we examine how the processing demands associated with complex scenes shape the expression of valence-specific control strategies in aging. We emphasize that this investigation is exploratory in nature: because the design includes only one stimulus class (scenes), it cannot adjudicate whether observed patterns differ from those obtained with faces or directly test stimulus automaticity as a moderator. Instead, its primary contribution lies in characterizing control deployment in a conceptually demanding stimulus domain that has been largely overlooked in the cognitive control literature.

Based on prior evidence that older adults show a positivity-related proactive control advantage with facial stimuli, we reasoned that if this advantage is robust to increased processing demands, it should also emerge with complex scenes. Conversely, if the conceptual and perceptual demands of scene processing exhausts limited executive reserves, negative stimuli—lacking the protective benefit of elevated motivational relevance—may disproportionately impair proactive goal maintenance. We therefore hypothesized the following asymmetric patterns:

H1: For positive scenes, older adults would show a relative reliance on proactive control (higher PBI) compared to young adults. Materials and methods

Participants

Because the critical effect of interest was the Age Group × Emotional Valence × Sequence Type interaction, and because interaction effect sizes in cognitive aging research are often smaller than medium main effects, a sensitivity power analysis was conducted rather than an a priori sample-size justification for a specific expected effect. Using G*Power 3.1 [22, 23], the minimum detectable effect size for a repeated-measures ANOVA with the final sample (32 young, 30 older), α = 0.05, and target power = 80% was calculated. The analysis indicated adequate power to detect a medium effect (f ≥ 0.25) for the three-way interaction. It is noted that this does not guarantee sensitivity for smaller interaction effects, and null findings are therefore interpreted with appropriate caution (see Data Analysis).

Thirty-two young adults (16 female, Mage = 20.03, SDage = 2.16, age range 18–23) and 30 older adults (16 female, Mage = 68.47, SDage = 2.80, age range 60–75) were recruited.

All participants met the following criteria: right-handedness; normal or corrected-to-normal vision; no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders; no current use of psychoactive medications (e.g., antidepressants, anxiolytics, or antipsychotics) that might affect prefrontal dopaminergic function and cognitive control performance; and a score ≥ 20 on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [24, 25]. Additionally, to control for potential confounds of cognitive reserve, older adults were required to have completed at least 12 years of formal education. Participants provided informed consent and were compensated for their time. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Normal University.

Prior to engaging in the experimental task, each participant completed a battery of standardized questionnaires designed to capture key motivational, emotional, and cognitive states. Specifically, the Future Time Perception Scale (FTP) [26] to assess motivational outlook and perceived time horizons, the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) [27] to measure current trait-like emotional experience, the Depression-Anxiety-Stress Scales (DASS-21) [28] to screen for general psychological distress, and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) as a brief screen of overall cognitive functioning. As summarized in Table 1, group comparisons on these measures yielded several expected results. Consistent with SST, older adults reported a significantly more limited future time perspective (lower FTP scores) compared with young adults. Older adults also reported higher positive affect (PANAS-PA) than young adults, a pattern consistent with the positivity effect and age-related emotional well-being [29]. Both groups fell within the normal range on measures of negative affective and distress (PANAS and DASS-21), and all MoCA scores exceeded the clinical cutoff. Although the group difference in positive affect was statistically significant, both groups' scores fell within normative ranges, suggesting that baseline emotional states were appropriate for experimental testing. Experimental material

Emotion scene stimuli

The core innovation of this study lies in the use of complex emotional scenes, which lack the high degree of automatic processing associated with facial expressions. 105 scenes (35 positive, 35 neutral, 35 negative) from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS) [30], the Chinese Affective Picture System (CAPS) [31], and public sources. Critically, all selected scenes contained no human faces or figures, isolating the emotional content to contextual and conceptual information. The selected scenes were characterized by high visual and semantic complexity: each scene contained multiple interacting objects (M = 5.3 objects per scene, SD = 1.8) embedded in rich contextual backgrounds, requiring integration of spatial relations and categorical knowledge for accurate valence appraisal [32]. The mean visual clutter index, operationalized as the standard deviation of luminance across spatial frequencies, was comparable across valence categories (positive: M = 4.82, SD = 0.71,neutral: M = 4.65, SD = 0.68,negative: M = 4.91, SD = 0.74), confirming that low-level perceptual properties did not confound valence effects. The images were standardized to 433 × 315 pixels with consistent brightness and contrast.

To validate these materials for age groups, an independent sample of twenty-three young adults (8 female, Mage = 22.96, SDage = 3.46) and twenty-one older adults (10 female, Mage = 67.24, SDage = 4.13) rated each picture for valence (1 = very unpleasant, 9 = very pleasant) and arousal (1 = very calm, 9 = very excited). Importantly, neither the young adults nor the older adults involved in this rating process participated in the follow-up experiment.

A 2 (Age Group) × 3 (Valence) ANOVA on valence ratings revealed a significant main effect of Valence, F(2, 84) = 312.32, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.89. As expected, positive scenes (M = 7.50, SD = 1.23) were rated as more pleasant than neutral (M = 5.41, SD = 1.32) and negative scenes (M = 2.83, SD = 1.36) (all ps < 0.001). No significant main effect or interaction involving Age Group was found (ps > 0.05), confirming that both age groups interpreted the scenes’ valence consistently.

Arousal ratings were higher for emotional (positive/negative) than neutral scenes, F(2, 84) = 15.56, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.27, with no difference between positive and negative arousal (p = 0.478). Notably, older adults rated positive scenes as slightly more arousing than young adults did, F(1, 42) = 4.69, p = 0.036, a finding consistent with the age-related positivity effect. The age-related arousal difference for positive scenes was retained in the final stimulus set. This decision precludes a clean dissociation of valence and arousal effects for positive materials; consequently, the enhanced proactive control observed in older adults for positive scenes may partially reflect heightened arousal processing. This confound is addressed in the analyses and discussion.

Emotional AX-CPT task

To examine how cognitive control is strategically deployed during the processing of emotional stimuli that lack automatic processing advantages, the classic AX-Continuous Performance Task (AX-CPT) was adapted to incorporate complex scenes. The core of this adaptation centered on the relationship between an emotionally valenced cue picture and the probe picture that followed. Participants were instructed to make a target response (key “J”) only in one specific case: when an emotional cue (positive or negative) was followed by a probe belonging to the same emotional category. These emotional match trials (e.g., positive cue followed by positive probe) accounted for 70% of all trials, thereby establishing a strong, prepotent tendency to make the target response upon seeing any emotional cue. The remaining 30% of trials were divided equally among three non-target sequence types: emotional-neutral sequences, neutral-emotional sequences, and neutral–neutral sequences. For all these non-target sequences, participants were required to withhold the dominant response and instead press the non-target key (“K”). The mapping of response keys (“J” vs. “K”) to target and non-target responses was counterbalanced across participants.

This task structure creates two critical trial types that tap into distinct cognitive control mechanisms. On emotional-neutral (AY) sequences, the initial emotional cue primes the dominant target response, which must then be overridden when a neutral probe appears. Successful performance here—indexed by low error rates and/or fast correct responses—relies on reactive control: the transient, stimulus-driven suppression of the prepotent tendency after encountering the neutral probe. Conversely, on neutral-emotional (BX) sequences, the neutral cue does not prime a strong response, but the sudden appearance of an emotional probe creates conflict, as it automatically attracts attention and may trigger the dominant response. Resolving this conflict depends on proactive control: the ability to actively maintain the neutral cue representation throughout the delay period, which helps shield the ongoing task goal from interference by the salient emotional probe. Thus, better performance on BX trials (lower errors, faster responses) reflects more efficient proactive control, whereas better performance on AY trials reflects more efficient reactive control. To assess how these dynamics are influenced by emotional content, the task was administered in separate blocks for positive and negative valence, allowing us to compare control deployment across different emotional contexts.

Experimental design

A mixed experimental design with three core factors was adopted: Age Group (between-subjects: young adults vs. older adults), Emotional Valence (within-subjects: positive vs. negative scenes), and Sequence Type (within-subjects: emotional-emotional, emotional-neutral, neutral-emotional, neutral–neutral). This 2 × 2 × 4 design allowed us to examine how age and emotional content interact with different contextual demands during cognitive control. Age Group varied between participants, whereas Emotional Valence and Sequence Type were manipulated within participants across trials. The primary dependent measures were task accuracy (error rate), response speed (reaction time for correct trials), and a derived PBI, which quantifies the relative reliance on proactive versus reactive control strategies.

Procedure

The experiment was programmed in E-Prime 2.0 and presented on a 17-inch LCD monitor in a quiet, dimly lit laboratory room with individual testing booths. Prior to the main task, each participant completed a practice block of 18 trials to learn the task rules and response mappings. To ensure understanding, only participants who achieved at least 80% accuracy in this practice phase advanced to the formal experiment. The formal task was divided into blocks organized by emotional valence. Each participant completed two blocks with positive scenes and two blocks with negative scenes, yielding four blocks in total. Every block consisted of 90 trials. Within each block, trial order was pseudo-randomized with the constraint that no more than three consecutive trials shared the same sequence type, and the same image was never presented more than twice per block (once as cue and once as probe, or vice versa) with a minimum separation of 10 trials between repetitions. This procedure minimized affective habituation while maintaining statistical power for each sequence type. The presentation order of the positive and negative blocks was counterbalanced across participants to control for potential order effects.

Each trial followed the standard temporal sequence of the AX-CPT paradigm (see Fig. 1). A trial began with a central fixation cross displayed for 750 ms. This was followed by the cue picture, presented for 1000 ms. After a blank inter-stimulus interval of 5000 ms, the probe picture appeared for 1000 ms. Participants were required to submit their response within 1750 following probe onset; the probe remained visible for the initial 1000 ms and was followed by a 750 ms response window during which the screen went blank while responses were still accepted. Data analysis

Prior to analysis, a systematic data screening procedure was implemented. Trials with reaction times (RT) shorter than 200 ms (anticipatory responses) or exceeding 3 standard deviations from each participant's mean within each condition (outlier responses) were excluded, accounting for less than 2% of the total data (M = 1.8%, SD = 0.9%, range: 0.3%–4.1% across participants). All 62 participants (32 young adults, 30 older adults) met the practice criterion of 80% accuracy and were included in all analyses.

To address potential concerns about speed-accuracy trade-offs, the correlation between mean RT and error rate across all conditions was calculated for each age group. Neither group showed significant RT-error correlations (young adults: r = 0.12, p = 0.51; older adults: r = −0.08, p = 0.65), suggesting that strategic trade-offs did not substantially influence findings. Additionally, for the two critical sequence types (emotional-neutral and neutral-emotional), Inverse Efficiency Scores (IES; [33]) were computered by dividing mean RT by accuracy proportion (1–error rate) for each participant and condition. The IES patterns mirrored those observed for error rates and RTs separately, suggesting that the primary findings were not driven by differential speed-accuracy strategies. For parsimony, the standard error-rate and RT analyses are reported below, IES results are available upon request.

Given the well-documented multiplicative scaling effect of age-related slowing [34], raw RTs can disproportionately inflate condition differences for slower individuals. To ensure that observed RT patterns reflected age-related differences in cognitive control strategies rather than baseline speed variations, a natural-log transformation (ln(RT)) was applied uniformly across all conditions prior to analysis. All RT results reported below are based on these log-transformed values,for descriptive interpretability, raw ms values are also provided in parentheses.

To verify that findings were robust to stimulus-level variability, supplementary linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) with random intercepts for both participants and individual stimuli were conducted [35]. The pattern of significant effects was consistent with the ANOVA results, confirming that the reported findings did not arise from idiosyncratic item effects.

To address the potential confound of age-related arousal differences for positive scenes, supplementary ANCOVAs with individual mean arousal ratings for the relevant valence entered as a covariate were conducted.

For null findings (e.g., the non-significant three-way interaction), Bayes factors (BF01) were calculated using JASP [36] to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis relative to the alternative.

Statistical analyses were performed using repeated-measures ANOVA in SPSS 25.0, with the dependent variables being error rate and correct-trial reaction time. The primary analysis was a 2 (Age Group) × 2 (Emotional Valence) × 4 (Sequence Type) mixed-design ANOVA for each dependent variable. This omnibus analysis is reported first, followed by separate 2 (Age Group) × 4 (Sequence Type) decompositions for positive and negative scenes to clarify the source of any significant three-way interactions. It is cautioned that patterns of significance in separate models do not mathematically confirm that the patterns differ from one another [37, 38], therefore, conclusions regarding valence asymmetries are based on the omnibus three-way interaction. For all ANOVAs, partial eta squared (η2p) is reported as effect size measures, with 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively [39]. Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons were used for all post-hoc analyses,the corrected alpha level is reported explicitly for each family of comparisons.

In this formulation, “Performance” is operationalized as either error rate or mean reaction time. A positive PBI value suggests a greater dependence on proactive control, while a negative value indicates a stronger reliance on reactive control.

The rationale for this index is grounded in the divergent cognitive demands posed by the two critical sequence types. On emotional-neutral trials, the initial emotional cue primes a prepotent target response. Successful performance therefore hinges on suppressing this primed response upon encountering the subsequent neutral probe—a conflict resolution process closely aligned with reactive control mechanisms. Consequently, superior performance (i.e., lower errors or faster responses) on these trials is interpreted as more effective reactive control. In contrast, on neutral-emotional trials, conflict is introduced by the unexpected appearance of an emotional probe following a neutral cue. Here, effective performance is facilitated by proactively maintaining the representation of the neutral cue throughout the delay period, which helps shield the ongoing task goal from interference by the salient emotional probe. Thus, better performance on this sequence type reflects more efficient proactive control. By directly comparing performance across these two trial conditions, the PBI allows us to quantify an individual’s dominant mode of cognitive control within a given emotional context. It is acknowledged that the PBI is a derived behavioral contrast rather than a direct measure of a latent control mechanism; its interpretation assumes equivalent baseline difficulty across sequence types, an assumption that may be violated by age-related slowing or differential arousal carry-over. To verify that conclusions did not rest solely on the ratio, direct pairwise comparisons of AY and BX performance are also reported in the Results section. Additionally, individual PBI distributions were inspected and no extreme outliers were found (all |z|< 3.0); nonetheless, PBI stability may be limited when the number of critical trials per cell is modest.

Results

Omnibus analysis: error rate and reaction time

A 2 (Age Group) × 2 (Emotional Valence) × 4 (Sequence Type) mixed-design ANOVA on error rates was conducted first. This analysis revealed a significant three-way interaction, F(3, 180) = 4.12, p = 0.007, η2p = 0.06, confirming that age-related differences in performance were qualitatively shaped by emotional valence. The main effect of Age Group was also significant, F(1, 60) = 8.53, p = 0.005, η2p = 0.12, with older adults showing higher overall error rates. The main effect of Sequence Type was significant, F(3, 180) = 56.78, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.49. The Valence × Sequence Type interaction was significant, F(3, 180) = 5.34, p = 0.001, η2p = 0.08, but the Age Group × Valence interaction was not, F(1, 60) = 2.18, p = 0.145, η2p = 0.04. Because the three-way interaction was significant, the effects were decomposed by conducting separate 2 (Age Group) × 4 (Sequence Type) ANOVAs for positive and negative scenes, followed by simple-effects analyses to identify the source of the asymmetry.

The omnibus ANOVA on log-transformed RTs yielded a significant main effect of Age Group, F(1, 60) = 44.90, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.43, with older adults exhibiting longer RTs. The main effect of Sequence Type was significant, F(3, 180) = 104.47, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.64. The Age Group × Sequence Type interaction was significant, F(3, 180) = 3.18, p = 0.026, η2p = 0.05, but the three-way interaction (Age Group × Valence × Sequence Type) did not reach significance, F(3, 180) = 1.81, p = 0.147, η2p = 0.03. Given the non-significant three-way interaction in the RT data, the separate positive- and negative-block analyses are reported below for descriptive completeness, but strong conclusions about valence-specific RT asymmetries are refrained from.

The 2 (Age Group) × 4 (Sequence Type) ANOVA revealed a significant Group × Sequence Type interaction was found, F(3, 180) = 6.46, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.10, representing a medium effect size. Simple effects analyses revealed that this interaction was driven by differential error patterns across groups: older adults showed significantly higher error rates on positive-neutral sequences compared to young adults (see below), whereas young adults showed significantly higher error rates on neutral-positive sequences compared to older adults (F(1, 60) = 8.73, p = 0.004, η2p = 0.13).

Follow-up simple effects analyses with Bonferroni correction (adjusted α = 0.008) indicated that older adults committed significantly more errors on positive-neutral sequences (M = 33.77%, SD = 11.95%) than did young adults (M = 19.28%, SD = 16.12%), F(1, 60) = 15.98, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.21, representing a large effect size [39]. Planned comparisons within each group further clarified this pattern: for older adults, error rates on positive-neutral sequences were significantly higher than on neutral-positive sequences (t(29) = 4.12, p < 0.001, d = 0.75), consistent with proactive control prioritization. In contrast, young adults showed the reverse pattern, with significantly higher errors on neutral-positive than positive-neutral sequences (t(31) = 3.45, p = 0.002, d = 0.61), consistent with reactive control reliance.

Negative scenes

The 2 (Age Group) × 4 (Sequence Type) ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Group, F(1, 60) = 4.09, p = 0.048, η2p = 0.06, with older adults producing higher overall error rates (M = 18.50%, SD = 1.70)%) than young adults (M = 13.70%, SD = 1.70%). The Group × Sequence Type interaction was significant, F(3, 180) = 3.28, p = 0.022, η2p = 0.05. Decomposition of this interaction revealed that older adults committed significantly more errors on neutral-negative sequences (M = 25.93%, SD = 22.67%) than did young adults (M = 10.76%, SD = 19.24%), F(1, 60) = 8.10, p = 0.006, η2p = 0.12, indicating specific impairment in proactive conflict. Conversely, young adults showed peak error rates on negative-neutral sequences (M = 27.78%, SD = 22.40%), F(1, 60) = 9.24, p = 0.003, η2p = 0.13, suggesting pronounced interference from negative cues during reactive control. Both groups maintained high accuracy on the non-conflict neutral–neutral sequences (error rates < 10%), confirming a comparable baseline understanding of the task rules.

To control for the age-related arousal difference observed in positive scenes, a supplementary 2 (Group) × 4 (Sequence Type) ANCOVA on positive-scene error rates with individual mean arousal ratings for positive scenes entered as a covariate was conducted. The Group × Sequence Type interaction remained significant, F(3, 177) = 6.12, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.09, indicating that the observed group differences were not solely attributable to differential arousal responses.

A 2 (Group) × 2 (Valence Block) ANOVA on neutral–neutral error rates revealed no significant main effects or interactions (all ps > 0.15), with both groups showing high accuracy. However, the absence of a significant group difference on neutral–neutral trials does not constitute evidence of equivalent task understanding; equivalence tests [40] confirmed that the observed difference (Cohen's d = 0.37) fell within a conventional equivalence bound of |d|< 0.50, providing modest support for the absence of a meaningful group difference in baseline performance.

Notably, the main effect of Group on the reaction-time PBI was not significant, F(1, 60) = 0.19, p = 0.664, η2p = 0.01, underscoring that valence, rather than age alone, drove the differential patterns of proactive versus reactive control seen in the timing measure.

To account for potential confounding effects of individual differences in cognitive functioning, motivational outlook, and affective state (see Table 1), a series of analyses of covariance (ANCOVA) were conducted. MoCA was selected as a covariate because it provides a global index of cognitive status that might influence control performance; FTP or PANAS were not included as covariates because these variables are conceptually tied to the positivity effect mechanism under investigation, and partialling them out would risk over-controlling for the very processes sought to be examined [41]. Nevertheless, to ensure that the findings were not driven by group differences in these measures, the key analyses were repeated with FTP and PANAS-PA entered as covariates,the pattern of significant effects remained unchanged. For parsimony, the MoCA-adjusted results are reported here.

After adjusting for MoCA, the main effect of group (e.g., error rate in positive tasks: F(1, 59) = 14.75, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.20) and critical interactions (e.g., Valence × Group for PBI error rate: F(1, 59) = 15.88, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.21) remained statistically significant. For the negative picture task, the main effect of group on error rate also remained significant after controlling for MoCA (F(1, 59) = 4.62, p = 0.036, η2p = 0.07). Notably, the effect sizes for these key effects were virtually unchanged after covariate adjustment (e.g., Valence × Group for PBI-error: η2p = 0.22 unadjusted vs. 0.21 adjusted), confirming the robustness of age effects independent of underlying cognitive abilities.

In summary, the error-rate data indicate that older adults showed a control-mode pattern consistent with relatively greater proactive control for positive stimuli but not for negative stimuli, whereas young adults showed the opposite valence modulation. The RT data, while showing pervasive age-related slowing, did not yield a significant three-way interaction, suggesting that the valence-specific control asymmetry is more robustly expressed in accuracy than in response speed.

Exploratory analyses of motivational and affective correlates

To examine whether individual differences in motivational outlook (FTP) and affective state (PANAS-PA, PANAS-NA) were associated with control strategy deployment, exploratory correlational analyses were conducted within each age group. For older adults, FTP scores were negatively correlated with PBI-error for negative scenes (r = − 0.37, p = 0.043, 95% CI [− 0.62, − 0.06]), suggesting that a more limited future time perspective was associated with reduced proactive control for negative stimuli. PANAS-PA was positively correlated with PBI-error for positive scenes in older adults (r = 0.31, p = 0.048, 95% CI [0.01, 0.56]), consistent with the hypothesis that higher positive affect may scaffold proactive control maintenance. No significant correlations emerged in young adults (all |r|s < 0.20, ps > 0.20), and PANAS-NA was unrelated to PBI in either group. These exploratory findings should be interpreted with caution given the modest sample size and multiple comparisons, but they provide preliminary evidence that motivational and affective variables may moderate valence-specific control in aging, and that the group-level PANAS differences reported in Table 1 are not merely descriptive but may carry functional significance for control deployment.

Discussion

Overview and hypothesis evaluation

The present findings demonstrate that age differences in the relative reliance on proactive versus reactive control are qualitatively shaped by emotional valence during the processing of complex scenes. Each hypothesis is evaluated in turn.

H1 predicted that older adults would show greater proactive control (higher PBI) than young adults for positive scenes. This hypothesis was supported: older adults exhibited a significantly higher error-based PBI and lower BX error rates than young adults in the positive block, consistent with a relative reliance on proactive control.

H2 predicted that older adults would show reduced proactive control (lower PBI) than young adults for negative scenes, accompanied by elevated BX errors. This hypothesis was also supported: young adults exhibited a higher PBI and lower BX errors than older adults in the negative block, indicating that young adults, rather than older adults, shifted toward proactive control for negative stimuli.

H3 predicted generalized age-related slowing with disproportionate RT costs on sequences demanding proactive maintenance. While older adults were indeed slower across all conditions, the three-way interaction in RTs was not significant, and the Group × Sequence Type interaction in the positive block did not clearly indicate disproportionate proactive-maintenance costs independent of generalized slowing. Thus, H3 received only partial support: the slowing was pervasive, but the hypothesized qualitative interaction with control demands was not robustly observed in the RT data.

It is critical to note at the outset that the experimental design included only low-automaticity scenes and did not incorporate a high-automaticity comparison condition (e.g., faces). Consequently, although processing demands are discussed as a contextual factor, direct conclusions that stimulus automaticity operates as a boundary condition cannot be drawn; such an inference awaits future research employing a direct manipulation of stimulus type.

Theoretical interpretation

One consistent finding across tasks was that older adults were slower in general than young adults [34]. Other than general speed differences, more subtle, valence-specific control strategies depending on stimulus type were found. These results are consistent with previous work in contingent capture indicating that aging affects voluntary (goal-directed) and involuntary (stimulus-driven) attention shifts, where aging is more affected in the latter case [42]. Here, this distinction is further elaborated by showing that even within goal-directed (proactive) control, aging is associated with a strategic, valence-specific reorganization when processing complex visual materials.

When processing positive scenes, older adults displayed a distinct bias towards proactive control. This was shown by their high PBI, reflecting proactive control prioritization in this stimulus domain. This pattern suggests that the relative preservation of proactive control for positive information may persist under demanding processing conditions, though the present paradigm does not permit us to determine whether this reflects motivated goal maintenance (as postulated by SST; [29], more efficient recruitment of conceptual knowledge [43], heightened arousal responses to positive stimuli in older adults, or a combination of these factors. Mechanistically, this sustained proactive control for positive scenes aligns with the STAC model's prediction of compensatory engagement, and is consistent with neuroimaging evidence that older adults show enhanced medial prefrontal-amygdala connectivity during positive emotion processing [44]. However, because motivational states were not directly measured, SST interpretations remain speculative.

Several alternative mechanisms merit consideration. First, Charles's [45] Strength and Vulnerability Integration (SAVI) model predicts that older adults show emotion regulation strengths but vulnerabilities under intense or prolonged activation,cue- triggered affective carry-over may thus contribute to the pattern via persistence rather than motivational prioritization [46]. Second, Labouvie-Vief's [47] dynamic integration theory frames the results in terms of tension between affective optimization and complexity with complex scenes demanding costly integration that differentially taxes resources by valence. Third, the Arousal-Biased Competition framework [48] notes that older adults’ heightened arousal may amplify attention to positive cues, facilitating or interfering with proactive maintenance. Fourth, Fredrickson's [49] Broaden-and-Build theory offers a complementary account for young adults' reactive control bias in positive blocks: induced positive affect broadens attentional scope and increases cognitive flexibility, suggesting their pattern may reflect adaptive flexibility rather than impaired proactive maintenance. These accounts cannot be adjudicated here without direct arousal manipulations, but should be considered alongside motivational interpretations.

Recent meta-analytic and empirical work refines these dynamics. Reed et al. [4] showed the positivity effect is robust but moderated by task demands. Laulan and Rimmele [46] demonstrated heightened emotional reactivity to complex scenes in aging, suggesting carry-over effects may drive sequential processing differences. Huang et al. [43] showed conceptual knowledge increasingly supports emotion understanding in later life, providing a mechanism for older adults’ top-down resource recruitment for positive materials. Converging neuroimaging evidence indicates older adults recruit prefrontal regulatory regions when adequately motivated [50], supporting SST and STAC-r predictions, [2, 51]. Together, these findings suggest control deployment in aging reflects the interplay of motivational relevance, conceptual demands, and executive resources—not valence alone.

For negative scenes, young adults showed proactive control, whereas older adults exhibited lower performance on “neutral-negative” sequences, indicating proactive goal maintenance difficulty. This is not characterized as a "deficit", since within the DMC framework proactive and reactive control are complementary strategies for different demands [12]. Whether older adults cannot engage proactive control for negative scenes, or strategically disengage due to lower motivational relevance under constraints, remains unclear. Because no facial stimuli were included, whether this pattern reflects low automaticity, high perceptual load, or methodological differences cannot be determined. Complex negative scenes—lacking rapid bottom-up signals—may exceed aging systems’ capacity for resource-intensive active inhibition, supported by electrophysiological evidence that negative regulation depends on early N2-indexed inhibitory processes [52] that such scenes may fail to trigger.

These differential patterns are best understood through processing demand and resource allocation: control deployment in aging depends on the interplay between valence and executive resources for stimulus interpretation. When positive stimuli engage efficiently recruit-able conceptual knowledge [43], proactive control may remain feasible despite high load,whether this represents compensatory scaffolding or selective bias remains open. Conversely, when negative stimuli impose high conceptual demands without motivational protection, resource limitations dominate, leaving insufficient capacity for proactive maintenance. This framework extends existing theories by (a) clarifying how motivational priority interacts with processing constraints, (b) identifying stimulus complexity as altering control mode requirements, and (c) generating testable predictions about automaticity moderation.

Functionally, sustained proactive control for positive scenes may reflect prioritization preventing goal disengagement [10], whereas negative scene difficulties stem from vulnerability to attentional capture when proactive preparation is unavailable. This asymmetry suggests aging cognition differentially recruits anticipatory mechanisms for complex emotional information, consistent with evidence that proactive control can be preserved when motivational support is sufficient [20, 21]. Such deployment may enhance emotional stability: investing limited resources in positive goal representations reduces sensitivity to external negative stimuli.

From an applied perspective, these findings highlight the importance of stimulus ecology in designing interventions that support for older adults’ emotion regulation [53]. Complex positive materials may engage older adults’ conceptual processing strengths, though this remains speculative without training data. This aligns with evidence that “active searching for positive information” training ameliorates emotional bias more effectively than “passive avoidance of negative information” by strengthening top-down control [54], and with longitudinal evidence that sustained cognitive engagement enhances executive function [55]. Future intervention research might explore: (1) conceptually rich positive stimuli for attention and goal maintenance training,(2) progressive difficulty to sustain prefrontal engagement,and (3) combined positive-stimulus engagement with conflict monitoring training for negative content [56].

Limitations and future directions

While this study advances understanding of cognitive aging patterns, several limitations should be noted. First, the experimental design focused exclusively on static emotional scenes with moderate arousal levels and presented positive and negative valence in separate blocks. This block structure precludes a clean dissociation of stimulus valence from induced mood: repeated exposure to positive or negative scenes may have gradually shifted participants' affective states, potentially contributing to the observed block differences independently of trial-level valence processing [30]. Future research should employ intermixed valence presentation or include mood-assessment checks between blocks to address this confound. Additionally, because only 30 images per valence were repeated across 180 trials per block, habituation to repeated scene exposure may have diluted emotional interference over the course of the experiment; although trial order was randomized anew for each participant, habituation effects cannot be ruled out.

Second, the final stimulus set retained an age-related arousal difference for positive scenes (older adults rated them as more arousing), which acts as a potential confound. The enhanced proactive control observed for older adults during positive trials may therefore reflect heightened arousal rather than, or in addition to, emotional valence per se. Future studies should match arousal across age groups or include arousal as a continuous covariate in behavioral models.

Third, the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying the behavioral patterns remain unspecified. Although the findings are interpreted within the STAC framework as reflecting compensatory engagement [2], this interpretation is indirect. Future studies combining behavioral measures with neuroimaging techniques (fMRI or EEG) could directly test whether sustained proactive control for positive scenes involves enhanced medial prefrontal-amygdala connectivity [44], and whether reduced performance on negative scenes reflects failed early inhibitory signaling indexed by the N2 component [52].

Fourth, the interpretive framework relies on specific assumptions regarding the mapping of trial types onto control mechanisms. While neutral-emotional sequences are theorized to tax proactive control and emotional-neutral sequences to tax reactive control [12], these trial types may differ in ancillary demands (e.g., temporal expectation, working memory load) that could contribute to performance patterns. Moreover, the PBI metric assumes equivalent baseline difficulty across sequence types, an assumption that may not hold given pervasive age-related slowing [34]. The comparison of AY and BX trials is further complicated by the fact that these sequences differ not only in control demands but also in the emotional content of the cue and probe: AY trials involve an emotional cue followed by a neutral probe, whereas BX trials involve a neutral cue followed by an emotional probe. Consequently, age differences could reflect differential responses to emotional cues or probes per se, rather than pure control-mode differences. Future research employing paradigms that more directly isolate control mechanisms—such as those manipulating cue validity or using process-dissociation procedures—would strengthen causal inferences regarding strategic control deployment.

Conclusion

In summary, the present findings illustrate that the cognitive control strategies deployed by older adults are not fixed but are dynamically shaped by the processing demands associated with complex emotional stimuli. Within the domain of complex emotional scenes, the relative presence of proactive control for positive scenes suggests that valence-specific control asymmetries can be expressed in domains requiring effortful conceptual integration. In contrast, the reduced proactive control for negative scenes under high load reveals a specific difficulty when executive resources are taxed. These outcomes advance understanding by documenting age-related differences in a conceptually demanding stimulus domain, and by highlighting the need to consider processing demands when theorizing about emotion-cognition interactions in aging. By integrating DMC and perceptual load theory, this study reframes the aging emotion-cognition system as a resource-dependent architecture that may selectively mobilize conceptual processing to support proactive control for positive stimuli, yet exhibit difficulty in sustaining such control for negative stimuli under demanding conditions. Because the experimental design did not include an empirical baseline or comparison condition (such as facial expressions) to represent stimuli with dedicated decoding pathways, claims that these findings "extend beyond" such stimuli are refrained from. Instead, conclusions are anchored strictly to what was observed within the utilized scene paradigm. Future research directly comparing high- and low-automaticity stimuli will be necessary to determine whether these patterns are uniquely determined by processing demands or reflect broader principles of adaptive cognitive aging.
