Dynamic immune profiling predicts response to radiation plus anti-PD-1 therapy in oligometastatic renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Van To, Niko Thio, Rebecca Castle, Sean Macdonald, Tristan Molden-Hauer, James Wells, Ryan McMahon, Mathias Bressel, Ben Tran, Arun A. Azad, Daniel Moon, Sree Appu, Lih-Ming Wong, Declan G. Murphy, Katharine Cuff, David Pryor, Lavinia Spain, Lewis Au, Criselle D’Souza, Paul J. Neeson, Shankar Siva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biomarkers that predict response to combined radiation and anti-PD-1 therapy in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) are poorly defined. RAPPORT trial (NCT02855203) is a prospective phase I/II study of stereotactic ablative body radiotherapy (SABR) and pembrolizumab in 30 patients with oligometastatic ccRCC. The primary endpoint was safety; secondary endpoints included overall survival, time to local progression, distant progression-free survival, objective and disease control rates, duration of response and patient-reported pain. Here, we report the pre-specified exploratory translational analyses. In pre-treatment tumours, responders had greater intra-tumoural cytotoxic T cell infiltration in close proximity to tumour cells, whereas non-responders showed enrichment of immunosuppressive, TGF-β and angiogenic signatures. In peripheral blood post-treatment, responders developed a proliferative burst of activated CD8+ T cells and sustained sharing of pre-existing tumour-enriched TCR clones. These findings identify trafficking and maintenance of pre-existing tumour-enriched T cell clones as determinants of response to SABR plus anti-PD-1 in ccRCC.

Introduction

Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is the most common subtype of renal cell carcinoma, accounting for 70–80% of kidney cancer1,2. ccRCC with distant metastases remains a major therapeutic challenge. Recurrence rates are high after metastasectomy, with studies reporting 83.8% of patients experience recurrence within one year after complete metastasectomy in the first-line setting3. While immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies have emerged in the first line setting for metastatic ccRCC, single-agent ICB demonstrates moderate efficacy, with objective response rates of 36.4%4, highlighting the need for enhanced therapeutic strategies.

Key determinants for ICB response in RCC include high clonal neoantigen load, robust cytotoxic T cell activity, and the presence of tertiary lymphoid structures5,6. Radiation therapy has emerged as a powerful immunomodulatory intervention that induces immunogenic cell death, releases tumour neoantigens, and promotes dendritic cell and T-cell activation7–9, thereby priming systemic anti-tumour immunity. While both ICB and radiation individually remodel the tumour-immune landscape, the radioimmunotherapy approach represents a novel and promising therapeutic strategy, with early evidence of synergy; however, further mechanistic and clinical studies are needed to validate and optimize this strategy.

We previously reported clinical outcomes of the RAPPORT trial (NCT02855203), the first study to evaluate the safety and efficacy of combining stereotactic ablative body radiotherapy (SABR) and pembrolizumab in patients with oligometastatic ccRCC10. SABR delivers precise, high doses of radiation over a short course and is a promising treatment option for metastatic RCC, particularly as an alternative to metastasectomy11–13. This single-arm, phase I/II trial enrolled 30 patients who received SABR to all metastatic sites followed by eight cycles of pembrolizumab, demonstrating efficacy: disease control was achieved in 83% of patients, with a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 15.6 months and a two-year PFS rate of 45% (Supplementary Fig. 1).

To date, no study has reported longitudinal immune monitoring in patients with metastatic ccRCC receiving combined radiation and ICB. Whether the pre-treatment tumour microenvironment (TME) and on-treatment peripheral blood profiling can predict response to SABR plus ICB, or provide insights into the mechanisms driving durable benefit, remains unclear. Here, we leveraged the unique opportunity provided by a fully clinically annotated bio-sample collection from the RAPPORT trial to investigate the immune landscape in tumour-infiltrating and circulating immune cells. Through transcriptomic analyses, immunohistochemistry of pre-treatment tumour biopsies, longitudinal immune profiling and TCR repertoire analysis of blood samples in ccRCC patients (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 2), we aimed to: (1) characterize the TME in ccRCC, (2) explore changes in local and systemic immunity in response to treatment, and (3) correlate these immune changes with treatment outcomes in ccRCC patients.Fig. 1Study design, transcriptomic characteristics and CyTOF peripheral immune profiling overview in the RAPPORT cohort.a Schematic of the treatment protocol, sampling timeline and experimental workflow. b Heatmap showing the differentially expressed genes in pre-treatment tumour samples between responders and non-responders. c FlowSOM tree identified the major cell subsets in populations in the peripheral blood across all patients. Results

Patient characteristics and mass cytometry analysis of all immune populations

All 30 enrolled patients had stage IV disease on the higher end of the spectrum of oligometastatic disease, with a median of 3 metastases per patient. Eligible patients had no more than two prior lines of systemic therapy. The median age of 62 years (range 47–80); 23 men [76.7%] and 7 women [23.3%]; ECOG performance status was 0 in 20 (67%) and 1 in 10 (33%) patients. Clinical response was determined as best overall response based on response evaluation criteria in solid tumours (RECIST) within 12 months of enrolment10. Patients achieving partial or complete response were classified as responders; those with stable or progressive disease were classified as non-responders. Clinical characteristics of patients at baseline

First, we sought to characterize each patient’s TME in pre-treatment specimens in ccRCC patients and its influence on treatment outcomes using NanoString gene expression analysis. Transcriptomic analysis identified genes that are differentially expressed between responders and non-responders (Fig. 1b). To characterize immune cell dynamics, we performed mass cytometry by time-of-flight (CyTOF) on peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) collected at three time points: pre-treatment, on-treatment (cycle 4), and follow-up after treatment (12 months or earlier if relapse occurred). PBMCs were clustered based on the expression of 43 leukocyte antigens (Supplementary Table 6). Cluster analysis identified six major immune cell populations: B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, myeloid cells, NK cells, and other T cells (Fig. 1c).

Pre-existing immune signatures determine response to SABR and anti-PD-1 in ccRCC patients

We first assessed the tumour inflammation signature (TIS) score, an 18-gene signature indicative of the adaptive immune response in tumours, previously shown to predict ICB responses14. In the RAPPORT cohort, expression of TIS genes was enriched in the TME of patients who responded to treatment (Fig. 2a). Responders also showed significantly higher TIS scores than non-responders. Next, we performed differential gene expression analysis between responders and non-responders. The analysis did not yield any significant gene defined by the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR < 0.05 criteria, possibly due to the patient variability within the group. Therefore, we used nominal p-value < 0.05 as a threshold of differentially expressed genes in this analysis for hypothesis generation. Differential gene expression analysis revealed that the responders had elevated expression of genes associated with T cell infiltration (CD8B, CD8A, CD3D, CD3E), pro-inflammatory cytotoxicity (GzmM, GzmA, GzmH, GzmK, IFN-γ, NCR1), adaptive immunity (IL-2RB, IL-4, IRF7, PRAME), T cell co-stimulation and activation (TNFRSF9, TNFRSF14, TNFRSF12A, CD80, FOXP3, KLRK, LCK, SAA1), and inhibitory markers (PDCD1, PD-L1, LAG-3, CD274, TIGIT) (Supplementary Fig. 4a). The entire list of differential genes can be found as Source data. In contrast, non-responders showed elevated genes linked to tumour progression and metastasis, including in RCC (AKT3, DOCK9)15,16. Deconvolution analysis of Nanostring transcriptomic data identified specific immune cell types in the ccRCC TME. Responders had higher abundance of T cells, CD8+ T cells, exhausted CD8+ T cells, cytotoxic cells, and NK cells in pre-treatment tumours compared to non-responders (Fig. 2b). No significant differences were observed for other immune cell types (Supplementary Fig. 4b). There were no significant differences between responders and non-responders in angiogenesis, immunosuppressive or other known signatures from Keynote 426 RCC trials17, Immotion15118 and Javelin19 trials (Supplementary Fig. 4c–e). Functional gene set enrichment analysis further indicated that the responders TME was enriched for pathways related to interferon gamma (IFN-γ) and IL-2 production, T cell infiltration and migration, as well as dendritic cell activation and antigen presentation. In contrast, non-responders upregulated immunosuppressive pathways, including tumour angiogenesis, endothelial cell migration and proliferation, transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) and IL-10 production (Fig. 2c), indicating that these pathways may have an inhibitory role in treatment response to SABR and anti-PD-1 therapy.

Based on these data, we hypothesised the TME of responders is enriched for CD8+ and CD4+ T cells responding to tumour-associated antigens, and this would be reflected in the TME TCR repertoire. To address this, we performed TCRVβ deep sequencing on pre-treatment PBMC and tumour samples. TCR clonality, measured by the Gini index, was higher in the tumour biopsies than in PBMCs (Fig. 2d), reflecting clonal expansion within the TME. We next investigated baseline TCR richness using the Chao1 index, where higher richness correlated with greater numbers of distinct clonotypes in TCR repertoire20. We showed a trend toward greater TCR richness in the peripheral blood of responders (p = 0.05, Fig. 2e). Within the tumour, responders exhibited significantly higher TCR-β Chao1 richness and might provide opportunities for broader tumour neoantigen recognition.

T cell infiltration in pre-treatment ccRCC tumour biopsies and their proximity to tumour cells were associated with favourable treatment outcomes

To validate the TME findings observed in Nanostring data, we performed multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) focused on T cells and using formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) pre-treatment tumour samples. We sought to determine whether baseline intra-tumoural T cells were associated with improved clinical response. Tumour regions were annotated by an independent pathologist (Fig. 3a) and identified using tumour markers CD10, pan-cytokeratin (PanCK), and carbonic anhydrase 9 (CA9). Baseline samples from patients who received a treatment response exhibited a significantly higher proportion of total T cells within the tumour area compared to non-responders (Fig. 3b). Responders also showed a higher proportion of CD8+ T cells. In these patients, CD8+ T cells expressed higher levels of GzmK—a marker of pro-inflammatory and tissue-resident memory T cells21,22—compared to non-responders (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Notably, responders also exhibited increased CD4+ T cells infiltrating the tumour compared to non-responders, with elevated GzmK expression. No significant differences in T cell subset proportions were observed between response groups in the non-tumour region, rather we observed increased tumour-infiltrating effector T cells specifically in the responders (Fig. 3b). As an example, within the cohort, we observed that one responder (PMC004) showed markedly higher T cell infiltration at the metastatic site, subsequently achieving complete response after 6 cycles of pembrolizumab with radiation (Fig. 3c). In contrast, patient PMC009 had the lowest T cell infiltration at baseline and experienced disease progression during radioimmunotherapy. To assess the clinical significance of T cell infiltration, we evaluated the relationship between T cell subset infiltration and PFS. Patients with higher intra-tumoural T cell proportions had better PFS (Fig. 3d). No significant association was found between CD4+ or CD8+ T cell infiltration and PFS (Supplementary Fig. 5c).

We further explored tumour-immune cell interactions by measuring the distance between tumour cells and the nearest T cells using spatial image analysis (SPIAT) (Supplementary Fig. 5d). In responders, tumour cells were in closer proximity to CD8+ T cells compared to non-responders (Fig. 3e). Tumour cells in responders were also closer to tumour-infiltrating PD-1+ CD8+ and PD-1+ CD4+ T cells, and to GzmK+ CD8+ T cells. Furthermore, responders showed closer distances from CD4+ to CD8+ T cells within the tumour region (Supplementary Fig. 5e). In these patients, PD-1+ CD4+ T cells were also positioned closer to PD-1+ CD8+ T cells, and vice versa (Supplementary Fig. 5e), suggesting enhanced cellular interactions between CD4+/CD8+ T cells associated with anti-tumour activity. TME neighbourhood archetype analysis indicated that, within the tumour region, responders exhibited significant enrichment for archetypes with mixed clustering between CD8+ T cells and other T cell subsets (double-negative and double-positive T cells) (Fig. 3f). In contrast, non-responders showed a predominance of CD4+ T cell clustering, though this was not statistically significant. Overall, these mIHC findings highlight that spatial proximity and clustering of T cells—particularly intra-tumour cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, are key determinants of response in ccRCC patients treated with SABR and anti-PD-1 therapy.

Taken together, our data indicate that ccRCC patients who respond to radioimmunotherapy have a higher pre-existing anti-tumour CD8+ and CD4+ T cell response. These T cells, already primed for tumour reactivity, might further invigorate and proliferate upon SABR and anti-PD-1 treatment, driving the therapeutic response. In contrast, non-responders exhibited upregulation of immunosuppressive gene signatures in the TME prior to treatment, which may contribute to the failure of durable tumour regression in RAPPORT trial.

ccRCC patient peripheral blood T cells proliferate and activate in response to SABR and anti-PD-1 therapy

We initially examined global changes in peripheral blood immune subsets—B cells, myeloid cells, NK cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, and other T cells—and observed no significant differences in cell frequencies following the combination treatment (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Since T cells are central mediators of cancer immunotherapy, we next conducted comprehensive longitudinal immune profiling, focusing on T cell proliferation, activation, and exhaustion states. We first examined Ki-67, a robust marker of T cell proliferation and reinvigoration in patients undergoing radiation and ICB23. At the on-treatment timepoint, we observed a trend to increase the frequency of proliferating CD8+ and CD4+ T cells compared to baseline (Fig. 4a, b), indicating treatment-induced T-cell proliferation. To understand treatment-induced changes, we analysed the expression of activation and inhibitory receptors on peripheral blood T cells. Prior studies suggest PD-1-targeted therapy reinvigorates exhausted T cells (Tex) by expanding circulating exhausted phenotypes24,25. The frequency of CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells expressing CTLA-4, ICOS and PD-1 was significantly higher during treatment compared to pre-treatment (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 7a). However, the expression of these proliferative, activated populations subsequently declined in most patients at the follow-up timepoint, suggesting transient changes only during treatment. We further characterized the proliferating T cells and found that Ki-67+ CD8+ T populations co-expressed PD-1, HLA-DR, ICOS, and CTLA-4 in most patients (Fig. 4d). Similarly, Ki-67+ CD4+ T cells predominantly co-expressed PD-1 and HLA-DR. These data indicate proliferating circulating T cell populations were also activated and expressed to immune checkpoints, potentially reflecting reinvigoration of Tex following radiation and anti-PD-1 treatment.

FlowSOM100 meta-clustering identified 27 distinct T cell clusters (T1–T27), reflecting diverse functional states including proliferation, activation, and cytotoxicity (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 7b). Nine clusters (T1–T9) were CD8⁺ T cells, twelve (T10–T21) were CD4⁺ T cells, and six (T22–T27) were unconventional subsets, including CD4−/CD8− double-negative T cells, mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells, and γδ T cells. At cycle 4, we observed a significant reduction in stem-like CD8+ (T4) and CD4+ populations (T20), defined by high PD-1 and TCF1 expression, compared to pre-treatment (Fig. 4f). This reduction likely reflects T cell differentiation following pembrolizumab engagement with PD-1 on stem-like T cells. Eight T cell clusters (1–2, 10–13 and 22) were enriched for proliferating T cells, marked by high Ki-67 expression. Notably, activated CD8⁺ (T2) and CD4⁺ (T13) clusters—identified by PD-1, HLA-DR and CD38 co-expression—showed significant increases post therapy compared to baseline. We also observed a significant increase in proliferating double-negative T cells with high GzmK expression (T22) after treatment. Interestingly, most patients also demonstrated a significant upregulation of Ki-67+ regulatory T cells (T11—Tregs) during treatment compared to pre-treatment.

In the myeloid compartment, non-classical monocytes (M8) expanded by cycle 4 (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). These monocytes exhibited high HLA-DR expression, indicating a potential role in tumour antigen uptake and presentation to T lymphocytes. Moreover, NK cells increased the proportion of mature, proliferating, activated phenotypes (N1, Ki-67+, CD38+, TBET+) upon the treatment (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b).

SABR and anti-PD-1 therapy induce a greater and sustained immune response in responders

We hypothesized that differences in intra-tumoural immune cell abundance within tumour samples could be reflected in broader systemic immune responses. To test this, we analysed pre-treatment systemic immune profiles. Responders had higher frequencies of cluster T16 cytotoxic CD4+ T cells (GzmB, GzmK, TBET, EOMES) and stem-like memory CD4+ T cells (T19—CD62L, PD-1, TCF1) before treatment than non-responders (Fig. 5a). Two previously defined activated T cell subsets—HLA-DR CD8+/CD4+ T cell subsets (T2, T13, Fig. 4e) were also increased pre-treatment in responders, although these differences were not significant. Within myeloid clusters, we found a distinct cluster of myeloid DCs (M3) expressing PD-L1 that was significantly increased in responders (Fig. 5a). SABR and anti-PD-1 treatment led to an increase in activated HLA-DR+ CD8+ T cells (T2) at cycle 4 compared to baseline in both responders and non-responders (Supplementary Fig. 9a). These CD8+ T cells co-expressed proliferation (Ki-67), activation (CD38, ICOS), effector (EOMES), and inhibitory (PD-1) markers. We also observed an increase in frequency of activated HLA-DR+ CD4+ T cell (T13), characterized by expression of Ki-67, ICOS, PD-1 and CTLA-4, from baseline to on-treatment, with a significant increase observed only in responders. A distinct subset of effector double-negative T cells (T22)—characterized by high GzmK, Ki-67, CD38, HLA-DR, CTLA-4 and EOMES—expanded significantly in responders during treatment but remained unchanged in non-responders. Moreover, only responders exhibited a significant increase of Tregs at cycle 4 compared to baseline. Taken together, these findings suggest that SABR combined with anti-PD-1 induces a strong systemic immune shift in responders, marked by an increase in pre-existing proliferating Ki-67+ T cells. These T cells co-expressed cytotoxic, activation, costimulatory, and inhibitory markers, indicating the reinvigoration of Tex into functional effector T cells.

CD4+ T cells are known to support CD8+ T cell anti-tumour activity 27. During treatment (cycle 4), responders had a higher frequency of cytotoxic CD4+ T cells (T16), marked by effector molecules (GzmB, GzmK, TBET, EOMES) and PD-1 (Fig. 5b). Moreover, memory CD4⁺ T cells expressing CD62L were upregulated in responders compared to non-responders. Responders also showed a trend toward higher frequencies of activated and effector T cells (T2, T13, T22), though these differences did not reach statistical significance (Supplementary Fig. 9b). These results suggest that CD4+ T cell involvement is crucial for effective anti-tumour activity in response to radiation and immunotherapy.

The dynamics of tumour-reactive T cells in response to ICB therapy and radiation remains unclear28–30. To quantify expanded T cell clones in ccRCC patients during treatment, we tracked the frequency of the top 1% of peripheral blood T cell receptor (TCR) clones, defined as hyperexpanded TCRs (Fig. 5c). No significant overall clonal hyperexpansion was observed throughout treatment, regardless of the treatment outcome (Supplementary Fig. 10). To assess whether TCR dynamics correlated with clinical benefit, we compared changes in hyperexpanded TCR abundance between responders and non-responders. Notably, rather than increased clonal expansion at the 6-week on-treatment mark (cycle 2), patients who derived clinical benefit showed greater maintenance of hyperexpanded TCRs compared to non-responders (Fig. 5c). Changes in hyperexpanded T cell clone frequency at 12-weeks (cycle 4) did not correlate with treatment response, although responders trended toward greater maintenance of pre-existing hyperexpanded clones (p = 0.076). These findings suggest that SABR and anti-PD-1 treatment maintained the proportion of hyperexpanded T cell clones, and this finding was directly associated with clinical outcomes.

We next hypothesized that a sustained immune response is a hallmark of durable benefit and prevention of tumour relapse. To test this, we compared immune cell proportions between response groups at the end of trial (12 months or earlier in cases of relapse). Responders retained higher frequencies of GzmB⁺ GzmK⁺ T cell populations with cytotoxic (T7, T16) and stem-like memory phenotypes (T19) (Fig. 5d). Two previously identified HLA-DR⁺ activated T cell clusters—CD8⁺ T cells (T2) and CD4⁺ T cells (13)—also remained elevated and proliferative in responders at follow-up. Clinically, patient PMC015, a complete responder, had the highest frequency of proliferating activated CD8⁺ (T2) and CD4⁺ (T13) T cells both pre- and post-treatment, with durable tumour clearance at 2 years (Fig. 5e). In contrast, patient PMC020, a non-responder, showed one of the lowest levels of CD4⁺ effector T cells at relapse, which occurred 6 months into treatment. These data suggest that sustained populations of activated, proliferating, effector, and memory-like T cells are critical mediators of durable clinical benefit in ccRCC patients treated with SABR and anti-PD-1 therapy.

Tumour-infiltrating CD8+ T cell clones found in peripheral blood

Expression of PD-1 has been considered as a hallmark of tumour-reactive T cells in the TME31. The efficacy of ICB has been shown to depend on the presence of progenitor exhausted T cells (Texprog), which serve as a reservoir for replenishing effector T cells32,33. Upon anti-PD-1 therapy, Texprog can expand and differentiate into responsive PD-1+ CD8+ Tex cells. To evaluate whether this occurs in patients with ccRCC, we analysed the proportion of PD-1+ T cells and Texprog (defined as PD-1+ TCF1+) at baseline across two response groups. Our mIHC results indicated that clinical responders had a significantly higher abundance of PD-1+ CD8+ and PD-1+ CD4+ T cells compared to non-responders (Fig. 6a). Additionally, we observed a higher frequency of CD8+Texprog T cells in responders (Fig. 6b), suggesting that increased infiltration of CD8+ Texprog T cells is associated with a more favourable response to radioimmunotherapy. A recent study identified a population of PD-1+ TCF1+ CD4+ T cells with stem-like properties in human RCC34. In line with this study, we detected PD-1+ TCF1+ CD4+ T cells in tumour tissues (Fig. 6b). To assess whether tumour-infiltrating T cell clones could also be detected and expanded in the peripheral blood following SABR and anti-PD-1 treatment, we performed TCRVβ sequencing of tumour tissue and peripheral blood at baseline, cycle 2, and cycle 4 of treatment, as well as post-treatment. We identified the top 1% of TCR clones as tumour-enriched clones (TECs) and tracked their presence in the blood (Fig. 6c). Notably, there was clonal overlap of TECs between tumour and blood. At baseline, responders exhibited a higher frequency of enriched TECs compared to non-responders (Fig. 6d). No significant differences were observed during or after treatment. The frequency of these hyperexpanded ITCs did not increase during treatment, regardless of clinical response. However, longitudinal analysis revealed that responders maintained a greater frequency of hyperexpanded ITCs from pre-treatment to cycle 4 compared to non-responders (Fig. 6e), suggesting effective trafficking of tumour-relevant clones from tumour to blood in ccRCC patients upon treatment.

PD-1+ HLA-DR+ CD38+ Ki-67+ T cell population, known as tumour-specific T cells and their expansion correlates with clinical benefit in cancer patients treated with radiation and ICB, including RCC24,25,35–37. These proliferating T cells in the peripheral blood were re-invigorated after anti-PD-1 therapy and contained TCR clones also present in the tumour 24. We previously observed a proliferative burst of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells during treatment, with effector-like phenotypes (HLA-DR+ CD38+) (Figs. 4f and 5b). Together with the observation of T cell clonal overlap between tumour tissues and blood in ccRCC patients (Fig. 6d), we hypothesized that tumour-reactive T cells in the TME and priming sites are reinvigorated upon treatment and traffic to the peripheral blood to mediate anti-tumour activity. Indeed, higher baseline levels of GzmK+ and tumour-reactive PD-1+ CD8+ T cells in the TME were associated with increased Ki-67+ HLA-DR+ CD8+ T cell population (T2) in peripheral blood at cycle 4 during treatment (Fig. 6f). Furthermore, baseline CD8+ Texprog in the TME correlated with the expansion of this T2 population at on-treatment timepoint (Fig. 6f). This correlation was also detectable before treatment, and became stronger after treatment, suggesting that pre-existing T cell responses in the TME trafficked in the blood and were augmented by SABR and anti-PD-1 therapy. No correlation was observed between baseline TME CD4+ T cell subsets and the expansion of Ki-67+ HLA-DR+ CD38+ CD4+ T cell population (T13) in the blood, either at baseline or during treatment (Supplementary Fig. 11a).

Finally, we investigated whether the proportion of pre-existing tumour-reactive T cells was associated with PFS as a clinical outcome. The level of CD8+ Texprog infiltration emerged as a strong prognostic biomarker in our RAPPORT cohort (Fig. 6g). Infiltration of PD-1+ CD4+/CD8+ T cells and CD4+ Texprog did not correlate with PFS in the present cohort (Supplementary Fig. 11b). Altogether, these findings support the hypothesis that increased pre-existing Texprog correlate with treatment benefit, and that SABR and anti-PD-1 therapy reinvigorate these T cells in the blood, potentially contributing to clinical response in ccRCC patients.

Discussion

In this phase I/II multi-centre RAPPORT study evaluating the combination of SABR and anti-PD-1 in patients with metastatic ccRCC (n = 30), we performed an in-depth analysis of pre-treatment multi-site tumour samples and longitudinal blood samples to uncover immune mechanisms associated with treatment response. We demonstrated several key findings. First, non-responding patients upregulated immunosuppressive pathways in the pre-treatment tumours compared to responders. Second, higher T cell density, particularly CD8+ T cells and their spatial distribution, distinguished clinical outcomes and predicted treatment response. Third, while SABR and anti-PD-1 induced circulating T cells with proliferating, activated, and partially exhausted phenotypes in most patients, only clinical responders demonstrated greater T cell responses, which were sustained at the follow-up timepoint. Finally, we identified maintenance of pre-treatment expanded TCR clones, shared between the tumour and peripheral blood of responders. These findings may reflect reinvigoration of infiltrating T cells in the TME upon treatment (Supplementary Fig. 12).

ccRCC is an immunogenic cancer where T cell infiltration correlates with favourable outcomes38,39. We showed that treatment responders demonstrated greater infiltration of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells that express PD-1 and GzmK compared to non-responders, consistent with published observations that intra-tumoural GzmK+ T cells associate with treatment outcomes in metastatic ccRCC patients receiving anti-PD-1 therapy6,40. Increased intra-tumoural T cells and CD8+ Texprog cells also predicted a favourable prognosis in the RAPPORT cohort. While we acknowledge the importance of larger cohorts to validate our findings, Jansen et al.41 also reported the infiltration of CD8+ Texprog population in kidney cancer, and these cells might give rise to a terminally differentiated effector population. They also showed high frequencies of PD-1+ TCF1+ CD8+ Texprog and TCF1− effector-like cells in brain metastasis following radiation42. Preclinical studies demonstrate that this expansion was dependent on intact lymph node function and adequate IL-15 signalling, as blockade of lymphocyte egress abrogated therapeutic benefit43. The expansion of radiation-induced Texprog improves tumour control both locally and at distant sites43, with depletion of these cells eliminating therapeutic efficacy44. Furthermore, our NanoString analysis showed greater baseline TME dendritic cell activation in responders, which is critical for dendritic cell maturation, successful trafficking to tumour-draining lymph nodes to initiate cross-priming of tumour-reactive T cells45,46. Regarding on-treatment markers, we further showed that immunological changes in the peripheral blood can reflect immune dynamics induced by combination treatment therapy and can serve as minimally invasive biomarkers. We observed NK cell activation following SABR combined with anti-PD-1 in patients with ccRCC. This may be driven by radiation-induced DNA damage, which generates micronuclei formation and activation of the cGAS–STING pathway, resulting in type I interferon production and subsequent NK cell priming47. Following radiation plus ICB, circulating PD-1⁺Ki-67⁺ CD8⁺ T cells also co-expressed HLA-DR and CD38, indicating their activation and antigen-presentation potential48–50. Similar expansions of Ki-67⁺HLA-DR⁺CD38⁺ CD8⁺ T cells were described after ICB therapy in multiple cancers24,25,51. In our study, this activated signature extended to circulating PD-1⁺ CD4⁺ T cells in ccRCC patients. Notably, the frequency of circulating PD-1+Ki-67⁺HLA-DR⁺CD38⁺ CD8⁺ T cells correlate with pre-treatment PD-1⁺ CD8⁺ tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes, with enhanced concordance upon therapy. This finding, together with our observation of TCR overlap between these circulating cells and PD-1⁺ CD8⁺ tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes35, supports the possibility that pre-existing T cell clones are reinvigorated in responders. Our findings have the potential to enable early identification of patients who may best respond to combined radiation and ICB and in order to continue the current treatment strategy, or to identify early non-responders and early treatment resistance.

Longitudinal TCR analysis of blood T cell populations revealed no clonal expansion from baseline through treatment completion. Instead, maintenance of pre-existing expanded TCR clones defined clinical benefit in responders. Other parameters—such as clonal replacement by novel TCRs recognizing new radiation-induced neoantigens—may also determine therapeutic efficacy. Indeed, Carlisle et al. reported that patients with clinical benefit exhibited a burst of new clonotypes following checkpoint blockade in advanced RCC35. Radiation therapy has been shown to induce immunogenic cell death, increasing mutant gene expression and neoantigen release9,52. We hypothesized that these novel neoantigens prime tumour-specific T cells to recognize and kill the tumour. Supporting this mechanism, responding patients in RAPPORT cohort demonstrated greater baseline TCR clonotype numbers, potentially providing broader recognition capacity for distinct neoantigen epitopes. Future investigations should focus on characterizing radiation-induced TCR clones and their neoantigen-specific responses. Such studies will be crucial for understanding how radiotherapy modulates the immune landscape and provide synergistic effects when combined with ICB for better tumour elimination.

While CD8⁺ T cells have traditionally been viewed as the primary effectors for anti-tumour immunity, our data highlight a subset of cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells (GzmK⁺ GzmB⁺) associated with treatment response. These cells were detected both within the TME and in peripheral blood. Although ex vivo functional assays are needed, GzmB⁺ and GzmK⁺ CD4⁺ T cells have been shown in other cancers to mediate direct, MHC class II-dependent tumour cell lysis and correlate with improved prognosis53,54. Notably, MHC class II expression has been documented on RCC tumour cells, particularly in ccRCC subtypes, where IFN-γ further upregulates its expression55–57. We also identified a baseline CD4⁺ Texprog population (PD-1⁺TCF1⁺) associated with clinical benefit. To our knowledge, this represents one of the first reports of CD4⁺ Texprog cells in ccRCC, particularly in a multi-regional metastatic context. Cardenas et al. recently described TCF1⁺ CD4⁺ T cells in kidney cancer that differentiate into type 1T helper cells (Th1) producing IFN-γ, thereby supporting CD8⁺ T cell effector differentiation and tumour control34. As such, future studies should investigate antigen specificity of these tumour-infiltrating CD4⁺ subsets and their interactions with other immune cells within the disease TME.

We observed several immune resistance pathways in non-responders that align with known RCC biology and likely contribute to treatment failure: (i) impaired priming associated with reduced dendritic activation and antigen presentation, (ii) reduced IFN-γ production, potentially reflecting failure to reinforce the IFN-γ-PBRM1 axis58, and (iii) enrichment of TGF-β signalling and tumour angiogenesis pathways, potentially mediated by VEGF signalling. Radiation has been shown to shift TME balance by enhancing antigen release and T cell priming in renal cell carcinoma7–9, but within a TME where TGF-β signalling and tumour angiogenesis can still blunt durable benefit. Tumour angiogenesis, while initially triggered by hypoxic conditions and oncogenic activation59, can be further enhanced by TGF-β1 through VEGF-mediated mechanisms60. In ccRCC patients, upregulated angiogenesis and TGF-β signalling correlate with poor prognosis and resistance to therapy61–63. First-line regimens combining anti-PD-1 therapy with VEGF-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors have improved survival in advanced ccRCC64,65. A therapeutic triad of anti-PD-1 blockade, anti-angiogenic agents, and radiotherapy may overcome these resistance mechanisms, though further work is needed to appropriately select patients for this strategy.

To our knowledge, this is the first translational investigation of the immunological effects of SABR with anti-PD-1 blockade in metastatic ccRCC. We acknowledge several limitations. First, the small sample size restricts the statistical power of our analyses. Validation of our findings in larger ccRCC cohorts is therefore warranted. Second, archived samples were obtained from multiple centres, and despite our best efforts, insufficient tumour material precluded analysis in some patients. In addition, baseline tissue samples (primary and metastatic) varied in sites of disease, which could be a biological confounder. However, we leveraged non-invasive longitudinal blood sampling to track immune signatures associated with treatment response. Third, in this single-arm design, we could not distinguish which immune changes were specifically driven by SABR versus anti-PD-1 therapy and future two-arm, randomised trials are needed to define individual treatment mechanisms. Finally, we were unable to functionally validate tumour-specific T cells in peripheral blood, despite detecting tumour-expanded TCR clones in the peripheral blood. Identifying such cells and RCC neoantigens is experimentally challenging and has only been described in a few RCC studies66–68. While co-culture assays of tumour-infiltrating T cells and autologous tumour cells would confirm tumour specificity, they require fresh tissue, which is often not feasible in clinical trials where metastases are frequently not surgically resectable. To confirm their tumour specificity, future studies could isolate TCR clones from circulating T cells in responders, especially from the PD-1+HLA-DR+CD38+Ki-67+CD8+ T cell subset and trace them back to baseline and on-treatment tumour tissues (if available).

In conclusion, our study highlights key determinants of response and resistance in ccRCC patients receiving SABR plus anti-PD-1 therapy. Baseline high levels of T cell infiltration and their close proximity with tumour cells emerge as predictive biomarkers for treatment response. We showed that peripheral blood profiling reveals dynamic, sustained T cell responses as key drivers of clinical benefits. Finally, our characterization of tumour-immune signatures supports rational combinations of radiation with other targeted agents to overcome resistance and potentially improve survival in patients with advanced ccRCC.

Methods

Patient tumour and blood samples

Patients with stage IV ccRCC received a single 20 Gy SABR dose or, if not feasible, ten fractions of 3 Gy, followed by eight cycles of pembrolizumab (200 mg every 3 weeks). Patient clinical parameters are provided as Supplementary Tables 1–4. Archival tumour tissues were obtained from multiple regions prior to radiotherapy through nephrectomy (primary) or metastasectomy (metastatic sites) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Peripheral bloods were collected at multiple timepoints when feasible, including baseline (pre-radiation), before each pembrolizumab dose, at progression, and at 12-month post-radiation. PBMCs were isolated by Ficoll density gradient and cryopreserved following standard protocols.

NanoString mRNA expression analysis

Archival formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tumour tissues include both primary and metastatic tumours before treatment. A total of 30 samples (23 primary and 7 metastatic biopsies) were extracted for Nanostring analysis. All tumour slides were re-assessed by a certified pathologist for the presence of ccRCC tumour cells based on Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E)-stained slides. Samples with adequate tumour were selected for RNA extraction using DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kits (Qiagen). RNA quantity and integrity was assessed via the 4200 TapeStation RNA system (Agilent). RNA of 150 ng of each sample was hybridized for 16 h at 65 °C using nCounter® PanCancer Immune Profiling Panel on the nCounter system (NanoString Technologies). Each hybridization reaction contains sequence-specific colour barcode representing 770 genes from different immune cell types. After hybridization, samples were transferred to the nCounter Prep Station, where unbound probes were removed, and target-capture probe-reporter probe complexes immobilized and aligned on the nCounter housekeeping genes.

Nanostring count data matrix was normalized using batch control replicates using RUVIII method. TIS gene list is defined by Ayers et al.14, which is scored using z-score method in GSVA package69. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using limma workflow70, and gene-set enrichment analysis was conducted using fgsea71.

List of genes for signature pathways and cell-type deconvolution were listed in Supplementary Table 5.

CyTOF

A total of 79 blood samples (26 pre-treatment, 26 at cycle 4, and 27 post-treatment) were analysed for mass cytometry. CyTOF staining medium was prepared using Ca2+/Mg2+-free PBS (Gibco) supplemented with 2% FBS (Bovogen Biologicals), 2 mM EDTA (Sigma Aldrich), and 0.05% sodium azide (Sigma Aldrich). Each CyTOF run included a healthy donor sample as a batch control. On the day prior to acquisition, PBMCs were thawed at 37 °C, washed with RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 10% FBS (Bovogen Biologicals), followed by overnight culture in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and interleukin-2 (30 U/mL) (PeproTech) for 2 h.

Briefly, cells were washed with PBS, treated with 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (StemCell Technologies) for 15 min at 37 °C, and resuspended in CyTOF staining medium at 2 × 10⁶ cells per sample. Fc receptors were blocked using 100 µL diluted Human TruStain (5 µL stock in 95 µL staining medium) for 10 min at room temperature.

Surface staining was performed with 50 µL of a metal-conjugated antibody cocktail in CyTOF staining medium for 30 min at 4 °C. Cell viability was assessed by staining with 1 µM cisplatin (Sigma-Aldrich) for 3 min at room temperature. After washing, cells were counted (0.5–1 × 10⁶ cells), fixed, and permeabilized using the eBioscience Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Intracellular staining was performed for 30 min at 4 °C with a metal-conjugated antibody cocktail. Cells were then stained with 0.2 mL Iridium intercalator (Fluidigm) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Prior to acquisition, Iridium was removed by dilution, resuspended in CyTOF staining medium, and washed twice with PBS. Finally, cells were resuspended in cell acquisition solution and kept on ice until acquisition on a Helios mass cytometer (Fluidigm) at the University of Melbourne.

Subsequent data processing was conducted utilising CATALYST workflow13 to perform marker intensity normalisation, scaling, clustering and unsupervised meta-clustering (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). Median expression markers of each meta-clusters were visualised using ComplexHeatmap R package72, and visualisation of the clustering in 2D space was performed using UMAP implementation uwot73. Expert annotation was applied on the meta-clusters based on the median marker expression to determine final cell type associated with each cluster. Mass cytometry data was first manually annotated into 26 clusters according to their protein expression profiles (Supplementary Fig. 6a). To achieve higher resolution analysis of immune subsets, we performed meta-clustering of the 26 immune cell clusters into 100 FlowSOM clusters. Myeloid and B cell populations were clustered independently from NK and T cell populations to eliminate potential cross-contamination between lineage-specific markers.

TCR repertoire analysis

Total DNA was extracted from peripheral blood using DNeasy Blood & Tissue kits (Qiagen), including pre-treatment, on-treatment (cycle 2 and cycle 4) and post-treatment timepoints (12-month or relapsed). A total of 27 tumour samples (21 primary and 6 metastatic biopsies) and 101 blood samples (24 pre-treatment, 24 at cycle 2, 28 at cycle 4, and 25 post-treatment) were analysed for TCR repertoire. DNA were also extracted from FFPE blocks and tissue scrolls when samples were available, using AllPrep DNA/RNA FFPE Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, DNA was purified using AMPure XP magnetic Beads and measured using Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity assay kits (Thermo Fischer Scientific). A total of 50 ng DNA was a minimum input amount required for each amplification reaction. The T-cell receptor (TCR) β genes were sequenced using next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology according to the Lymphotrack® TRB Assays on the Illumina® MiSeq platform (Invivoscribe). In brief, TCRβ clonal populations were amplified via quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR), KAPA SYBR® FAST qPCR Master Mix and primers that target the V and J regions of antigen receptor genes. Amplification from individual samples were pooled together to generate the sequencing library. Samples receiving less than 20,000 reads per manufacturer’s recommendation were considered to fail quality control threshold and excluded from the downstream analysis.

Raw sequencing reads were processed using MiXCR software74. Unique TCR clonotypes were identified based on the CDR3 amino acid sequences, and non-productive clonotypes were excluded. The main clonotype analyses, including diversity analysis, tracking and annotation, were performed using Immunarch R package75. Downstream analysis, statistical tests, and visualization were performed in R base package and ggplot2 package76.

To study the shared ITC between blood and tumour tissue, hyperexpanded clones (defined as > 1% of the repertoire) were first identified from tumour tissue of each patient. Then the presence and abundance of these clones in the corresponding patient’s blood samples were tracked throughout the treatment.

mIHC and image analysis

FFPE blocks were sectioned at 3–4 μm and mounted on Trajan 3 slides. The slides were baked for 60 min and stained using the antibodies listed below and opal fluorophores. The following marker panel was used for staining using the Leica Bond RX Fully Automated Research Stainer and Opal Polaris 8 Color Automation IHC Detection Kit protocol (Akoya Biosciences): CD3, CD4, CD8, GzmK K, PD-1, tumour markers (panCK, CD10, CA9) and TCF1. Prior to antibody application, slides underwent a thirty-minute epitope retrieval process for CD4, CD8, GzmK, PD-1, panCK, CD10, CA9 and TCF1 using pH9 BOND epitope retrieval solution 2 (Leica Biosystems, cat: ER2). CD3 used a pH6 BOND epitope retrieval solution 1 (Leica Biosystems, cat: ER1). Following antigen retrieval, tumour tissue sections were stained by OPAL on an autostainer, including serial rounds of epitope labelling with monoclonal antibodies followed by tyramide signal amplification (TSA). A total of 34 samples (25 primary and 9 metastatic biopsies) for the mIHC panel were used for analysis. Antibody clones and dilutions were performed in the following order conjugated to an Opal tyramide signal amplification reagent: CD3 (Rabbit monoclonal, SP7, 1:300) with Opal 690, CD4 (Rabbit monoclonal, EPR6855, 1:215) with Opal 520, CD8 (Mouse monoclonal, 4B11, 1:100) with Opal 570, GzmK (Mouse monoclonal, 1C3A4, 1:1250) with Opal 620, PD-1 (Rabbit monoclonal, EP239, 1:200) with Opal 650, tumour markers (PanCK—Mouse monoclonal, AE1/AE3, 1:300, CD10—Rabbit monoclonal, EPR22867-118, 1:300 and CA9—Mouse monoclonal, EPR4151(2), 1:300) with Opal 480, and TCF1 (Rabbit monoclonal, C63D9, 1:100) with Opal 780. All Opal tyramide signal amplification reagents were diluted at 1:150 dilution, except Opal 480 at 1:300 dilution. All slides were counter-stained with spectral DAPI (Akoya Biosciences) for nuclear detection.

Images of the stained slides were acquired on a Vectra Polaris automated quantitative pathology imaging system (Akoya Biosciences). Representative multispectral images (MSI) from different slides were used while training the algorithms for each marker. The raw images were spectrally unmixed using the Phenoptics inForm software (Akoya Biosciences) and exported as multi-image TIFF files. Scoring of each slide was performed using the inForm software on Vectra Polaris.

Matching haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) image for each mIHC slide was reviewed by a pathologist and areas to annotate on the immunofluorescent images for analysis were identified. Necrotic areas were excluded, and tumour areas were scored.

Merging Vectra mIHC images were performed by HALO software (Indica Labs, v3.0.311.398) to reconstruct unmixed whole tissue image. To capture cells of all different sizes across the entire tissue area, appropriate nuclear segmentation and positive dye threshold intensities were determined. Cells were visualized based on nuclear and cytoplasmic stains, and mean pixel fluorescence intensity in the applicable compartments of each cell were measured (i.e., CD3 in cytoplasm, TCF1 in nucleus). Marker positivity was determined using mean intensity thresholds above background fluorescence, classifying cells as positive or negative for each fluorochrome. The data was then used to define co-localized populations, including PD-1+ CD8+ T cells, PD-1+ CD4+ T cells, TCF1+ CD4+ T cells, TCF1+ CD8+ T cells, GzmK+ CD4+ T cells, GzmK+ CD8+ T cells, CD4+ Texprog, CD8+ Texprog. Proportion of T cells subsets and tumour cells were scored both out of total cells counted per slide. Median scoring value was used for each patient, with statistical comparisons performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.

Downstream analyses were performed using SPIAT R package77 to calculate average minimum distance between cell types, density on tumour regions, and spatial clustering.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in R and GraphPad Prism 10. Wilcoxon rank-sum test or non-paired nonparametric tests (as appropriate) was used to compare responders with non-responders. Mixed‑effects models (glmmTMB R package) were used to perform longitudinal analysis for assessing differences in abundance across time points. Pearson’s correlation coefficient is used to assess the correlation between pre-treatment T cell subset in the TME and proliferating HLA-DR+ T cells in the peripheral blood. Survival curves were plotted by the Kaplan–Meier method and compared by the log-rank test. Patients were categorized into low and high groups with the cohort median as a cutoff value. For linear regression analysis, Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated. P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. Figure visualizations were created with BioRender.com.
