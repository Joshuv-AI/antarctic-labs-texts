Diagnostic accuracy of HPV DNA in oral gargle and rinse samples for Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Arbi Wijaya, Nurtami Soedarsono, Vera Julia, Turmidzi Fath, Bayu Brahma, Alif Rizqy Soeratman, Denni Joko Purwanto, Yutaro Higashi, Tsuyoshi Sugiura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly detection of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) remains challenging because tissue biopsy is invasive and unsuitable for repeated monitoring or large-scale screening. This study systematically evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of human papillomavirus (HPV) DNA detection in oral gargle and rinse samples for HNSCC.MethodsThis meta-analysis followed PRISMA guidelines and included 10 studies with 1315 participants. Pooled sensitivity, specificity, summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC), and diagnostic odds ratios (DOR) were calculated using random-effects models. Comprehensive subgroup analyses were conducted to explore pre-analytical and analytical heterogeneity.ResultsPooled sensitivity and specificity were 0.68 (95% CI: 0.58–0.79) and 0.91 (95% CI: 0.88–0.95), respectively, with an AUC of 0.91. Higher pooled sensitivity was observed in subgroups using smaller collection volumes (≤10 mL), non-SPF10 PCR-based detection methods, and HPV16-specific targets, whereas specificity remained consistently high across subgroups. Fagan’s nomogram showed that the test offers greater clinical value for ruling in than ruling out the disease.ConclusionOral HPV DNA testing via gargle and rinse samples shows high specificity but moderate, highly variable sensitivity. Therefore, it functions best as a non-invasive, supplementary tool for triage or monitoring, rather than as a standalone early detection or rule-out method for HNSCC.Keywords: Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, HNSCC, Oral gargle, Oral rinse, HPV DNA

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), which comprises malignancies originating in the oral cavity, oropharynx, larynx, and hypopharynx, remains a significant global health issue. In 2020, it ranked as the seventh most common cancer worldwide, resulting in about 890,000 new cases and 450,000 deaths annually [1], [2], [3]. Despite advancements in therapeutic modalities, the five-year survival rate for HNSCC remains approximately 50–60%, largely because most patients are diagnosed at advanced stages (Stage III or IV) [4], [5], [6], [7]. Current diagnostic protocols depend heavily on clinical examination followed by invasive tissue biopsy, which remains the gold standard. However, tissue biopsy is invasive, subject to sampling bias, and unsuitable for repeated monitoring or large-scale screening [8], [9], [10], [11], [12].

In recent years, the shift toward liquid biopsy has gained momentum, offering a non-invasive alternative for cancer detection and surveillance. Oral fluids, specifically oral gargle and oral rinse samples, have emerged as promising substrates for liquid biopsy in HNSCC [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]. Unlike plasma-based liquid biopsies, oral gargle samples are in direct or close contact with the primary tumor sites in the upper aerodigestive tract, potentially allowing for a higher concentration of exfoliated tumor cells and cell-free tumor DNA [15], [17]. Different rinsing solutions have been explored in oral sampling protocols; however, their effects on downstream molecular yield remain insufficiently standardized [18]. Furthermore, the application of oral liquid biopsy extends to monitoring preneoplastic disorders, offering a critical window for detecting high-risk lesions before progression to invasive HNSCC [19].

A key molecular driver and biomarker in a substantial subset of HNSCCs, particularly those of oropharyngeal origin, is the human papillomavirus (HPV) [3], [20], [21]. The presence of high-risk HPV DNA (notably HPV16 and HPV18) in oral fluids has been identified as a highly specific marker for HPV-related HNSCC [22]. While several primary studies have investigated the diagnostic performance of HPV DNA in oral gargle samples, reported sensitivities and specificities vary widely across different clinical settings and laboratory protocols [3], [16], [23], [24]. Previous meta-analyses have predominantly focused on salivary biomarkers, leaving a gap in the evaluation of oral gargle samples across the broader HNSCC spectrum. Specifically, no prior study has systematically analyzed how methodological variables, such as collection volume, gargling duration, and PCR detection platforms, affect the diagnostic accuracy of this sampling modality [25], [26].

Therefore, this study aimed to systematically evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of HPV DNA detection in oral gargle and rinse samples for HNSCC across multiple anatomical subsites and HPV target types, and to explore potential sources of heterogeneity through predefined subgroup analyses in order to clarify the clinical utility of this non-invasive approach.

Methods

Search Strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted across five electronic databases: PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, Scopus, and CINAHL to identify studies evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of gargle-derived biomarkers for the early detection of head and neck cancer. The search strategy was developed in consultation with a medical librarian and combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords related to four main concepts: (1) head and neck squamous cell carcinoma and relevant anatomical subsites, (2) HPV-related biomarkers, including high-risk HPV types and viral DNA, (3) sample types, including saliva, oral gargle, oral rinse, mouthwash, and other liquid biopsy specimens, and (4) diagnostic concepts, including diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, screening, detection, and predictive value. The search covered publications from January 2015 to March 2026. In addition, the reference lists of relevant articles and reviews were manually screened to identify additional eligible studies not captured by the initial search. The methods and reporting of this systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The review protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database under CRD420261326767.

Inclusion & exclusion criteria

Studies were considered eligible if they met the predefined PICOS criteria. The population included adults (≥18 years) with histologically confirmed HNSCC at any subsite and healthy controls. The index tests of interest included oral HPV-related DNA-based assays, including HPV DNA detection and HPV DNA methylation-based assays, performed on oral gargle or oral rinse samples. The reference standard was histopathological diagnosis of HNSCC, with or without confirmation of HPV/p16 status using tissue-based methods such as p16 immunohistochemistry, HPV in situ hybridization, or E6/E7 mRNA detection. Eligible studies were required to report sufficient data to construct a 2 × 2 contingency table, including true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN), and false negatives (FN). Eligible study designs included prospective, retrospective, and cross-sectional cohort studies, as well as case-control studies.

Studies were excluded if they did not report original diagnostic accuracy data, such as reviews, editorials, case reports, or conference abstracts without full text; used specimen types other than oral gargle or oral rinse, including blood, tissue, or brush biopsy alone; lacked sufficient data to construct a 2 × 2 contingency table; involved non-human subjects; or represented duplicate publications. In addition, when a single publication reported separate results for different detection methods or HPV type subsets, each distinct dataset was treated as an independent data point in the meta-analysis.

Study selection

All identified records were imported into the Rayyan QCRI platform (https://www.rayyan.ai/, accessed on March 18, 2026) for efficient duplicate removal and blinded screening. In the first phase, two independent reviewers (A.W. and N.S.) screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records to identify potentially eligible studies. Full-text articles were then assessed for final inclusion. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus, or, when necessary, by consultation with a third reviewer (V.J.). Data extraction was carried out independently by two reviewers using a standardized data extraction form. Extracted data included study design, patient demographics, sample collection details, HPV detection platforms, reference standards, and diagnostic accuracy metrics (TP, FP, FN, TN).

Quality assessment

Following Cochrane recommendations, two independent reviewers (A.W. and N.S.) assessed the methodological quality of included studies using the QUADAS-2 tool, resolving discrepancies through consensus or a third reviewer (V.J.). Bias was evaluated across the four standard QUADAS-2 domains (patient selection, index test, reference standard, flow and timing), each rated as low, high, or unclear risk. The resulting risk distribution was synthesized using the RobVis tool (Risk-of-bias VISualization) Shiny web application (University of Bristol, Bristol, UK; https://mcguinnessla.shinyapps.io/robvis/; accessed on March 20, 2026) to generate traffic-light and summary plots for transparent reporting [27].

Statistical analysis

A diagnostic accuracy meta-analysis was performed using a bivariate random-effects model that accounts for both the correlation between sensitivity and specificity and the inherent heterogeneity across studies. Pooled estimates of sensitivity, specificity, diagnostic odds ratio (DOR), and the area under the summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve were calculated with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Between-study heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic, with I2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively.

To systematically explore potential sources of heterogeneity, comprehensive subgroup analyses were conducted across seven a priori defined methodological and clinical variables: sample type (oral gargle vs. oral rinse), collection volume (≤10 mL vs. ≥ 15 mL), gargling/rinsing duration (< 30 s vs. ≥ 30 s), detection method (SPF10-based vs. non-SPF10), HPV type (HPV16-specific vs. pan-HPV/multi-type), cancer target (mixed HNSCC vs. OPSCC), and study design (prospective vs. cross-sectional vs. case-control). For these specific subgroups, pooled metrics were estimated using the DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model following appropriate log and logit transformations. Between-group differences were tested using ANOVA-Q (QB), with p < 0.05 considered significant.

Clinical utility was graphically evaluated using Fagan’s nomogram, which illustrates the shift in disease probability from pre-test to post-test based on the pooled likelihood ratios. Publication bias was assessed using Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test, with p < 0.10 indicative of significant small-study effects. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding studies with a high risk of bias to confirm the robustness of the pooled estimates. All statistical analyses were executed using Stata version 19 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) and R version 4.3.0 (incorporating the “mada” and “meta” packages). Unless otherwise specified, a two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study selection

The systematic search identified a total of 3345 records across databases: PubMed (n = 1124), EMBASE (n = 1089), Web of Science (n = 634), Scopus (n = 431), and CINAHL (n = 67). After removing 856 duplicates, 2489 unique records underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 2421 records were excluded based on title and abstract review, leaving 68 full-text articles for detailed eligibility assessment. After full-text review, 58 articles were excluded for the following reasons: insufficient diagnostic accuracy data (n = 24), wrong sample type (n = 12), review articles or conference abstracts (n = 10), overlapping populations (n = 7), and non-English language (n = 5). Ultimately, 10 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the quantitative meta-analysis [3], [16], [23], [24], [28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33]. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Fig. 1).

The studies employed cross-sectional (n = 5), prospective cohort (n = 3), and case-control (n = 2) designs. Data analysis and data synthesis

Sensitivity and specificity

The pooled sensitivity was 0.68 (95% CI: 0.58–0.79); however, sensitivity estimates ranged widely (0.25–0.99), indicating substantial differences in detection performance across studies. Heterogeneity was extreme (τ²=0.04; I²=94.82%; H²=19.29) and statistically significant (Q(14)= 269.24, p < 0.001), suggesting that the pooled estimate should be interpreted cautiously and is likely influenced by study-level factors (Fig. 2A). Specificity was higher and more stable, with a pooled estimate of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.88–0.95). Most studies reported high specificities (often ≥0.90), although one study reported a notably lower value (0.50). Between-study heterogeneity remained significant (Q(14)= 35.86, p < 0.001) but was moderate (I²=55.19%; H²=2.23; τ²≈0.00), supporting comparatively consistent specificity across studies (Fig. 2B).

Receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve

The summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) analysis showed good overall discrimination, with performance weighted toward specificity (Fig. 3). The SROC curve yielded an AUC of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.88–0.93). The 95% confidence contour around the summary point was relatively narrow, indicating reasonable precision in the pooled estimate. In contrast, the 95% prediction contour was notably wider, reflecting substantial between-study variability in sensitivity and specificity and implying that diagnostic performance may differ meaningfully in future settings depending on study-level factors.

Summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC). Circles represent individual study pairs of sensitivity and specificity; the summary operating point is shown with its 95% confidence region and 95% prediction region. The AUC reflects overall discriminatory performance.

Pooled diagnostic odds ratio (DOR)

Using a random-effects model, the pooled log diagnostic odds ratio (lnDOR) was 2.71 (95% CI: 2.16–3.25), indicating good overall discriminatory performance of HPV DNA detection in oral gargle samples for distinguishing HNSCC cases from non-cases (Fig. 4). This corresponds to a DOR of approximately 2.71, indicating substantially higher odds of a positive test in HNSCC cases. Between-study heterogeneity was moderate (I² = 58.3%, p = 0.002).

Forest plot of log diagnostic odds ratio (lnDOR) (random-effects model). Squares represent lnDOR for each study with 95% confidence intervals; the diamond indicates the pooled lnDOR.

Deeks’ funnel plot publication bias

Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test found no evidence of significant publication bias (small-study effects) in the included diagnostic accuracy studies, with a p-value of 0.93 (Fig. 5). Visually, the study points appear broadly distributed around the regression line without clear asymmetry, and the regression slope is essentially null, further supporting the absence of funnel plot asymmetry. Therefore, based on Deeks’ test, the pooled diagnostic performance estimates are unlikely to be materially influenced by publication bias. However, power is limited given the modest number of included studies.

Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test for assessment of publication bias in diagnostic accuracy studies. The regression line and p-value indicate whether small-study effects are present.

Clinical utility via Fagan’s nomogram analysis

Fagan’s nomogram analysis demonstrated the clinical utility of the index test across four pre-test probability scenarios (0.01%, 0.5%, 10%, and 40%) using a pooled positive likelihood ratio (LR+) of 8.0 and a negative likelihood ratio (LR−) of 0.32 (Fig. 6). At very low pre-test probabilities (0.01% and 0.5%), both positive and negative results produced negligible shifts in post-test probability. In contrast, clinically meaningful changes were observed at higher pre-test probabilities: at 10%, a positive result increased the post-test probability to 46%, while a negative result decreased it to 3%; at 40%, a positive result increased the post-test probability to 84%, whereas a negative result decreased it to 18%. Overall, these findings indicate that the test provides greater value for ruling in disease (with a relatively strong LR+ of 8.0) than for definitively ruling out disease (with a moderate LR− of 0.32), with the greatest impact observed in low-to-intermediate and intermediate-risk clinical settings.

When stratifying these effects, the variations were found to predominantly influence the test's true-positive detection rate (sensitivity). Specifically, studies utilizing non-SPF10 PCR-based detection methods demonstrated a substantially higher pooled sensitivity of 82.1% (95% CI: 70.6–89.7%) compared to SPF10-based methods (60.9%, 95% CI: 51.6–69.5%). Notably, the within-group heterogeneity for the non-SPF10 PCR category was not statistically significant (QW = 5.35, p = 0.374), indicating high consistency among studies employing this technique.

Similarly, assays specifically targeting HPV16 yielded a superior pooled sensitivity of 85.4% (95% CI: 66.7–94.5%) relative to pan-HPV or multi-type detection tests (62.5%, 95% CI: 53.2–70.9%). Furthermore, an oral sample collection volume of ≤ 10 mL resulted in a markedly higher pooled sensitivity (84.6%, 95% CI: 68.7–93.2%) than volumes ≥ 15 mL (63.1%, 95% CI: 52.0–73.0%).

Despite these substantial impacts on the DOR and sensitivity, the test's specificity remained highly stable across all configurations. Pooled specificities remained optimally robust, ranging from 85.6% to 91.8% across detection methods, collection volumes, and HPV target types.

Conversely, no significant between-group differences were identified regarding sample type (oral gargle vs. oral rinse, p = 0.996), gargle duration (p = 0.235), cancer targets (HNSCC vs. OPSCC, p = 0.858), or overall study design (p = 0.767). However, substantial within-group heterogeneity was frequently observed within these subsets (e.g., QW = 18.00, p = 0.012 for oral gargle), suggesting that while broad sampling categorizations do not intrinsically alter pooled diagnostic accuracy across groups, other latent variables at the individual study level continue to introduce variability. Ultimately, the data indicate that the ability to correctly rule out the disease (specificity) is broadly robust, while careful optimization of collection volume and specific PCR targets is critical to maximizing the assay’s diagnostic sensitivity.

Quality assessment result

The methodological quality of the 10 included studies was evaluated using the QUADAS-2 tool, revealing a varied risk-of-bias profile (Fig. 8). The Flow and Timing domain demonstrated the highest quality, with 100% of the studies rated as low risk. In contrast, patient selection presented the most significant concerns; 20% of the studies, specifically Dickey (2022) and Giuliano (2020), were flagged as high risk due to the use of a case-control study design. In diagnostic accuracy research, a case-control design is considered high risk because it selects known cases and healthy controls separately, leading to spectrum bias that artificially inflates sensitivity and fails to reflect the true clinical population. The Index Test and Reference Standard domains showed moderate performance, with 50% of studies achieving a low-risk rating.

Discussion

The clinical shift toward non-invasive liquid biopsy in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) hinges on establishing modalities that are both accessible and diagnostically reliable [34], [35]. In this comprehensive meta-analysis of 10 studies and 1315 participants, we demonstrated that oral HPV DNA testing exhibits a distinct diagnostic profile characterized by a high pooled specificity (0.91; 95% CI: 0.88–0.95) alongside a more moderate pooled sensitivity (0.68; 95% CI: 0.58–0.79). While a summary AUC of 0.91 and an lnDOR of 2.71 indicate strong overall discriminatory power, this performance is asymmetrical. Clinically, this means the assay functions as a reliable confirmation tool, where a positive result strongly warrants immediate oncological suspicion but lacks the sensitivity required to safely rule out malignancy. This interpretation is supported by Fagan’s nomogram, which shows that at a 10% baseline pre-test probability, a positive gargle or rinse test significantly elevates the post-test risk to 46%, whereas a negative result only marginally reduces it to 3%. Consistent likelihood ratio patterns (LR+ < 10; LR− > 0.1) further confirm this limitation. Ultimately, oral gargle-and-rinse HPV DNA testing is best positioned as a supportive or adjunctive triage tool rather than a definitive, standalone diagnostic method.

The diagnostic potential of HPV DNA is particularly evident in HPV-related OPSCC, where persistent high-risk infection plays a central etiologic role [36]. Consistent with this biological rationale, our findings are broadly aligned with prior meta-analytic data. An earlier meta-analysis of eight studies evaluating oral HPV DNA/RNA reported a similar range of pooled specificity (92%), although sensitivity remained lower than that observed with tissue-based specimens, at 72% for HPV-associated HNSCC [37]. In addition, a more recent meta-analysis of fourteen studies demonstrated a significant association between oral HPV DNA, especially HPV16, and both oropharyngeal carcinoma (OPC) and oral cavity carcinoma [38]. However, previous analyses did not clearly define the assay's clinical capacity to rule in or rule out disease. Our study extends the literature by confirming the diagnostic relevance of oral HPV DNA detection while definitively positioning it as an adjunct tool.

Several studies have also supported the feasibility of detecting HPV DNA in oral gargle and rinse samples [3], [23], [29], [30], [31]. Prior studies have consistently supported this feasibility, with oral HPV positivity associated with increased cancer risk (OR = 3.70) independent of tobacco and alcohol [39], and detection rates ranging from 53.2% to 92.9% depending on collection protocols [24], [40], [41]. Collectively, these findings are biologically plausible: tumor-derived DNA enters oral fluids through direct shedding from mucosal tumor surfaces or through filtration from plasma into the salivary glands, allowing these samples to effectively capture molecular material reflecting the tumor microenvironment [42], [43], [44]. As the landscape of oral fluid-based biomarkers continues to evolve, extending beyond HPV DNA to include emerging markers such as circulating tumor DNA, methylation profiles, and salivary miRNAs, the gargle-based sampling platform may serve as a versatile collection medium capable of capturing a broader spectrum of tumor-associated molecular alterations relevant to both HNSCC detection and post-treatment surveillance [25], [26].

However, the utility of oral gargle and rinse sampling specifically for the early detection of HNSCC remains highly debated. While its clinical benefits are substantial, including its non-invasive nature, patient tolerability, and capacity to safely sample a broad mucosal surface without specialized personnel, critical biological disadvantages limit its efficacy as a primary screening tool [15], [22]. First, early-stage (T1-T2) oropharyngeal tumors are often deeply embedded within tonsillar crypts and may shed insufficient DNA to exceed analytical detection thresholds, increasing the risk of false-negative results [15], [22]. s, detecting oral HPV DNA cannot reliably distinguish between a transient viral infection and an active HPV-driven malignancy, which diminishes its positive predictive value in asymptomatic populations [45]. Therefore, its optimal use lies in risk stratification, adjunctive triage, and post-treatment monitoring rather than standalone early detection.

These practical constraints are strongly corroborated by comparative sampling studies. For instance, while oral rinses generally identify the highest absolute number of high-risk HPV infections, they can still miss up to 38% of infections localized to specific oral subsites [15]. Importantly, relying exclusively on oral rinse specimens has been shown to miss 60% of base-of-tongue infections and 75% of tonsillar infections, suggesting a severe risk of underestimation at the most common anatomical sites of oropharyngeal cancer [15]. Furthermore, pre-analytical methodological variables, such as sample preservation and DNA purification techniques, substantially affect the ability to detect genomic DNA by PCR and must be rigorously controlled [46].

The extreme sensitivity heterogeneity (I²=94.82%) was driven by specific methodological factors, revealing a clear dichotomy: while the assay's specificity is inherently robust and universally stable, its sensitivity is highly contingent on specific methodological parameters. Higher pooled sensitivity was observed in studies using smaller collection volumes (≤ 10 mL), HPV16-specific targets, and highly optimized non-SPF10 PCR methods. Mechanistically, smaller volumes likely prevent the excessive dilution of exfoliated tumor DNA, while targeted amplification reduces competitive PCR inhibition. In contrast, sample type and duration did not significantly affect performance, suggesting flexibility in clinical implementation.

Furthermore, the methodological quality of the included studies significantly shapes the interpretation of our findings. As identified in our QUADAS-2 assessment, 20% of the studies utilized case-control designs, which are susceptible to spectrum bias and typically overestimate diagnostic accuracy. This suggests that our pooled sensitivity of 0.68 likely represents an optimistic estimate, and that the test's performance in unselected clinical cohorts may be more conservative. Future research must prioritize prospective cohorts with consecutive enrollment to minimize these biases and confirm the assay’s real-world reliability.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, statistical heterogeneity was high, particularly for sensitivity. Although our subgroup analyses successfully identified sample volume, detection method, and HPV target as significant methodological moderators, the residual variability likely reflects latent differences in DNA extraction protocols and patient clinical staging. Second, the modest number of included studies restricts the precision of publication bias assessments and prevents more complex multivariable meta-regression analyses. Future research should focus on prospective multicenter validation with standardized protocols and explore multi-biomarker approaches to improve sensitivity while maintaining specificity.

Conclusion

Oral HPV DNA testing via gargle and rinse demonstrates high specificity but moderate, highly variable sensitivity for HNSCC detection. Our findings indicate that diagnostic sensitivity is significantly influenced by methodological parameters, with higher pooled sensitivity observed in studies using smaller collection volumes (≤10 mL), HPV16-specific targets, and optimized non-SPF10 PCR methods. Due to inherent biological limitations in early-stage disease and the challenge of differentiating transient infections, this approach cannot currently serve as a standalone early-detection or rule-out method. Instead, it serves best as a non-invasive, adjunctive triage or monitoring tool.
