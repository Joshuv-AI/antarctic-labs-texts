Association between serum vitamins and COVID-19 infection: a cross-sectional study of healthcare workers in a Nigerian tertiary hospital
Authors: Tajudin Adesegun Adetunji, Olusola Akanni Jeje, Temitope Olumuyiwa Ojo, Olufemi Samuel Smith, Olusola Joseph Olarewaju, Baderinwa Opeyemi Akanji, Olusola Fajobi, Taiwo Samson Olumakinde, Waidi Folorunso Sule, Sunday Babatunde Akinde, Rahman Ayodele Bolarinwa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough the global burden of COVID-19 has significantly decreased, understanding the role of micronutrients in modulating immune responses to SARS-CoV-2, especially among vulnerable populations like the healthcare workers, remains very important. This study assessed the association between serum vitamin levels and COVID-19 infection in this high-risk group in a Nigerian tertiary hospital.MethodologyA cross-sectional hospital-based study involving 181 apparently healthy healthcare workers with varying degrees of exposure to COVID-19 patients was carried out over a 6-week period in February to March 2022. Data were collected using an interviewer-administered pretested, semi-structured questionnaire. Socio-demographic data and venous blood of all participants were collected for biochemical assay of both fat- and water-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K, and Vitamins B1, B2, B5, B6, B9, B12 and C, respectively, using high-performance liquid chromatography. Data were analysed using IBM IPSS version 25.ResultsThe prevalence of PCR-confirmed COVID-19 infection was 23.2%. Slightly more than half (99; 54.7%) were below forty years of age. COVID-19 positive individuals had significantly lower mean serum levels of fat-soluble vitamins compared to their COVID-19 negative counterparts, as follows; A (37.31 µg/dL vs. 44.57 µg/dL, p = 0.003), D (42.03 ng/mL vs. 50.59 ng/mL, p = 0.024), and E (0.84 mg/dL vs. 1.01 mg/dL, p = 0.019), and water-soluble vitamins B1 (106.02 nmol/L vs. 136.51 nmol/L, p < 0.001), B2 (52.89 ng/mL vs. 69.56 ng/mL, p = 0.003), B5 (5.54 ng/mL vs. 7.74 ng/mL, p < 0.001), B6 (18.31 ng/mL vs. 25.02 ng/mL, p < 0.001), B9 (11.34 ng/mL vs. 13.92 ng/mL, p = 0.002), and C (0.63 mg/dL vs. 0.78 mg/dL, p = 0.007). Vitamins K and B12 were not significantly different between the two groups.ConclusionsThis study identified an association between lower serum levels of fat-soluble vitamins A, D, and E, and most water-soluble vitamins with COVID-19 infection in healthcare workers. While further research is needed, these findings suggest the potential usefulness of micronutrient intervention to reduce vulnerability to COVID-19 and related infections.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-12959-7.

Background

COVID-19 remains one of the most significant pandemics in recent history, with huge impacts on global health, national economies, and healthcare systems [1, 2]. In addition to the high number of cases and deaths that occurred during its peak, the disease posed many difficulties, particularly in understanding the intricate effects it had on many organ systems. Initially, it was identified as mostly a respiratory condition. The evidence that emerged thereafter clearly positioned it in the systemic group, encompassing the immunological, neurological, renal, cardiovascular, metabolic and gastrointestinal systems, among others [3]. The variation in disease severity among individuals also draws attention to the role of micronutrients such as serum vitamins in the clinical expression of the disease [4, 5].

Micronutrients, particularly vitamins, are vital for immune modulation and modifying immune response to infections [5, 6]. They enhance both innate and adaptive immunity by supporting barrier integrity, regulating inflammatory responses, and enhancing the activity of immune cells such as T lymphocytes, B cells, macrophages, and natural killer cells [7]. Deficiencies in key vitamins have been linked to severe disease outcomes, especially in viral infections like COVID-19. Vitamin D enhances antimicrobial peptides such as cathelicidins and defensins, which help reduce viral replication [8, 9], while vitamin C mitigates oxidative stress and strengthens the immune cell function of phagocytes and lymphocytes [10, 11]. Evidence suggests the potential effect of Vitamin C in reducing the severity and duration of respiratory infections [5, 12, 13]. Vitamin A is important for maintaining mucosal immunity, and vitamin E plays an important role in protecting against oxidative damage [7, 14].

Despite growing evidence supporting the role of vitamins in immune function and infection mitigation, several uncertainties remain regarding their precise impact on COVID-19. Key questions persist, especially about the relative importance of different vitamins and the potential interactions between them. While vitamins C and D have been the most frequently studied in the context of SARS-CoV-2 due to their well-documented immunomodulatory and antioxidant properties, more needs to be known about other essential vitamins, such as B-complex vitamins, as regards their specific roles in COVID-19 infection.

Earlier studies have investigated the relationship between serum vitamin levels and COVID-19 outcomes in different populations and demographic groups, with highly specific emphasis given to hospitalized patients, older adults, and patients with underlying comorbidities [15, 16]. However, despite the role of frontline healthcare workers in combating the pandemic, the extent to which vitamins influence the regulation of their immune responses in the context of COVID-19 remains understudied. Due to their repeated contact with infected patients, healthcare workers are at higher risk of COVID-19 [17], so it becomes relevant to assess how micronutrient status, specifically vitamin status, influences the susceptibility to COVID-19.

This study assessed the serum levels of selected fat and water vitamins among a group of COVID-19-infected health workers and their uninfected counterparts. The study aims to provide valuable information regarding serum vitamin levels and their involvement in disease susceptibility by evaluating the interaction between these vitamins and COVID-19 status. Also, the results of this study can be applied to the development of evidence-based public health interventions and initiatives that lessen the effects of COVID-19 and other similar viral respiratory illnesses on vulnerable groups such as healthcare workers.

Methods

Study design, population and study location

A cross-sectional study was conducted among healthcare workers at the Obafemi Awolowo University Teaching Hospitals Complex (OAUTHC) in Ile-Ife, Nigeria, and a total of 181 respondents were surveyed. The facility is a government-owned and a major referral centre for specialised medical care in the south-west region of the country. It also hosted an isolation facility for the care of COVID-19 patients during the pandemic [18].

Sampling

Health workers engaged in the two categories of clinical and non-clinical duties were recruited sequentially from February to March 2022. The core clinical staff recruited included medical doctors, nurses, pharmacists, laboratory scientists, radiographers among others, while the support staff in the catering, stores and supply, administrative and finance departments constituted the majority of the non-clinical staff. The recruitment period coincided with the COVID-19 vaccination rollout for staff, conducted at the hospital’s vaccination center over a six-week duration. Eligible staff members who presented for vaccinations were invited to participate in the study throughout this period. The inclusion criteria required participants to be consenting hospital staff members who have been employed for at least 6 months and are in apparent good health. Exclusion criteria included individuals who were acutely ill, on immunosuppressants, vitamin supplementation, or currently undergoing treatment for COVID-19, diabetes mellitus and/or cancers. Pregnant and lactating women were also excluded because of increased physiologic demand associated with these conditions.

Sample size and procedure

Sample size: A sample size of 180 was calculated using Fisher’s formula for estimating the sample size of a single proportion. A COVID-19 prevalence rate of 10% was used in the calculation [19], with a 5% error margin and a standard normal deviation of α at a 95% confidence level. Each consenting staff member was provided with detailed information about the study and signed an informed consent form. The staff member was then interviewed in a separate room with visual and auditory privacy, where biomedical samples were collected for testing. First, a nasal swab was obtained from each participant for SARS-CoV-2 testing. Also, blood samples were taken for serum vitamin assays at the same visit.

Study instrument

Data were obtained through an interviewer-administered, semi-structured questionnaire developed specifically for the study. The English translation of the questionnaire can be found in Supplementary File 1. The instrument was designed to gather data for a broader study on COVID-19 among healthcare workers. The instrument was pre-tested at a primary health facility outside of the study location. Insights from the pre-test were used to improve the questionnaire to make it more appropriate for data collection. Data obtained included socio-demographic data, sources of COVID-19 information, perceived risk of infection, history of prior COVID-19, and presence of comorbid conditions. Also, the last portion of the survey instrument was used to collect data applicable to biomedical samples, i.e., COVID-19 PCR test results and vitamin serum levels.

Sample collection, processing and storage

Five millilitres of venous blood was collected into plain sample bottles from each participant from the ante-cubital vein under sterile conditions. The blood was allowed to stay for between 30 min and 1 h to enable clot retraction. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at room temperature and aliquots for analyses of serum vitamins A, B, C, D, E and K were taken and stored frozen at -80 degrees Celsius in 2 ml snap cap Eppendorf tubes until analysed. Furthermore, naso- and oropharyngeal swabs were obtained from participants for SARS-CoV-2 testing. Viral RNA detection was performed using the GeneFinder™ COVID-19 Plus RealAmp Kit (OSANG Healthcare Co., Ltd., South Korea), a commercially available real-time RT-PCR assay targeting multiple viral genes, including RdRp, N, ORF1b, and E. All respiratory samples were processed according to the manufacturer’s recommended protocols and cycling conditions. Results were interpreted using cycle threshold (Ct) values, with Ct values < 40 considered positive and Ct values ≥ 40 considered negative.

Analyses of biochemical parameters

Vitamins A, B, C, D, E and K were analyzed using Waters 616/626 High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (Waters Incorporate, California, USA.) at wavelengths specific for each analyte using a fluorescence detector. Unknown sample concentrations were determined based on charge: mass ratio, i.e. the molecular size relationship among the molecules in the sample. This is interpreted by the built-in software as against that of the known standard concentration using a calibration curve. Analytical accuracy and precision were ensured by simultaneous analyses of commercially available control sera (at levels in low, normal and elevated ranges). Intra and inter-assay coefficients of variation were within allowable limits of acceptance for each analyte. The normal reference ranges for the vitamins are established according to internationally accepted standards [20].

Data analysis

The questionnaires were coded and entered into the Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS) version 25. Data cleaning was performed before analysis. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, while quantitative variables were presented as means and standard deviations. Independent-samples t-tests were employed to compare mean serum vitamin levels between PCR-positive and PCR-negative participants. Tests for normality indicated that most serum vitamin levels were approximately normally distributed. Given this, and the relatively large sample sizes, t-tests were deemed appropriate. Moreover, the t-test is recognized as robust to moderate deviations from normality and variance inequality [21], which makes it suitable for the present analysis. In addition, chi-square statistics was used to assess the association using sociodemographic characteristics, serum vitamin categories and COVID-19 PCR status. Effect sizes were estimated using Cohen’s d for assessing standardized mean differences between PCR-positive and PCR-negative groups. Cohen’s d was calculated as a ratio of the difference in group means to pooled standard deviation.

In keeping with standard recommendations, effect sizes of approximately 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 were interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively, and in-between effect sizes (i.e., 0.4–0.5) were considered small-to-medium in magnitude [22]. 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for mean estimates were estimated based on the t-distribution to provide an index of group mean precision and interval estimates. Effect size estimates and 95% CIs were also presented along with p-values to identify statistical as well as practical significance.For all analyses, a p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Ethical consideration

The ethical approval to conduct the study was obtained from the Ethics and Research Committee of the Obafemi Awolowo University Teaching Hospitals Complex (OAUTHC), Ile-Ife (Approval No-ERC/2022/03/05) as part of the larger study on COVID-19 among health workers in OAUTHC, Ile-Ife, Nigeria. All respondents gave voluntary informed consent after being informed of the study’s risks and benefits. No personal identifying information was recorded, and respondents’ data were carefully uploaded on a password-protected computer reserved for the study while being assured of the confidentiality of the information provided. The study complied with ethical standards according to the Declaration of Helsinki for the conduct of research on human subjects.

Results

A total of 181 asymptomatic individuals participated in this study; 42 (23.2%) participants tested PCR-positive for COVID-19. Slightly more than half (99; 54.7%) were below forty years of age, and 116 (64.1%) were clinical staff. About three-fifths (108; 59.6%) hold at least a diploma or university degree. Majority (133; 73.5%) had received at least one dose of the COVID-19 vaccine. (Table 1).

The range and mean values of serum vitamins of study participants

The majority of participants had serum levels of the fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, and K) within the normal reference ranges. Specifically, 147 participants (81.2%) had vitamin A levels between 30–80 µg/dl, 141 participants (77.9%) had vitamin D levels between 10–65 ng/ml, 155 participants (85.6%) had vitamin E levels ranging from 0.5–1.8 mg/dl, and 167 participants (92.3%) had vitamin K levels between 0.13–1.8 mg/dl

For the water-soluble vitamins, 77 participants (42.5%) had vitamin B1 levels above 140 nmol/l, while 133 participants (73.5%) had vitamin B2 levels ranging from 40 to 240 ng/ml. The majority of participants (155, or 85.6%) had vitamin B5 levels exceeding 2.7 µmol/l, and 140 participants (77.3%) had vitamin B6 levels between 5 and 30 ng/ml. Also, 134 participants (74.0%) had vitamin B9 levels ranging from 1.7 to 17.0 ng/ml, while 171 participants (94.5%) had vitamin B12 levels greater than 201 ng/l. Most participants (159, or 87.8%) had vitamin C levels within the range of 0.4–1.5 mg/dl. (Table 3)

Serum micronutrient status of study participants

The mean serum levels of the fat-soluble vitamins, except Vitamin K, were significantly lower among PCR-positive participants relative to PCR-negative participants and are as follows: Vitamin A (37.31 ug/dl vs. 44.57 ug/dl, p = 0.003), Vitamin D (42.03 ng/ml vs. 50.59 ng/ml, p = 0.024), and Vitamin E (0.84 mg/dl vs. 1.01 mg/dl, p = 0.019). Similarly, apart from Vitamin B12, the mean levels of the water-soluble vitamins were significantly lower in individuals who tested positive for COVID-19 compared to their negative counterparts as follows; vitamin B1 (106.02nmol/l vs. 136.51nmol/l, p < 0.001), vitamin B2 (52.89 ng/ml vs. 69.56 ng/ml, p = 0.003), vitamin B5 (5.54 ng/ml vs. 7.74 ng/ml, p < 0.001), vitamin B6 (18.31 ng/ml vs. 25.02 ng/ml, p < 0.001), vitamin B9 (11.34 ng/ml vs. 13.92 ng/ml, p = 0.002) and vitamin C (0.63 mg/dl vs. 0.78 mg/dl, p = 0.007). Cohen’s d values were calculated for the mean differences in serum vitamin levels between PCR-positive and PCR-negative cases and a large effect was observed for Vitamin B6 (d = 0.78). The Cohen’s effects for Vitamin B1 (d = 0.69), Vitamin B5 (d = 0.61), and Vitamin A (d = 0.58) were moderate-to-large and may suggest significant biochemical differences. There were medium effects observed as regards Vitamins B2 (d = 0.60), B9 (d = 0.56), C (d = 0.52), D (d = 0.42), and E (d = 0.41), which may represent meaningful differences between the PCR-positive and PCR-negatve groups. However, Vitamins B12 (d = 0.08) and K (d = 0.07) showed trivial, non-significant differences with overlapping confidence intervals and comparable means between the groups. (Table 4).

Table 5 shows the association between categorised serum levels of vitamins and COVID-19 PCR status. A higher proportion of the study participants (42.9%) with positive COVID-19 PCR test had vitamin B1 levels of < 90nmol/l, and this was statistically significant (p = 0.007). However, a higher proportion of participants (89.2%) with negative COVID-19 PCR tests had vitamin C levels of 0.4-1.5 mg/dl and this was statistically significant (p = 0.017). Association between serum vitamins and COVID-19 PCR status of participants

Discussion

This study provides insights into the serum vitamin status of healthcare workers as well as the potential associations between micronutrient status and COVID-19 infection. With clinical workers being the majority of participants, the study provides valuable information on the micronutrient status of healthcare workers—a population at increased risk of COVID-19 infection.

The prevalence of COVID-19 among participants was 23.2% as determined by PCR testing. However, this is lower compared to a study by Ogboghodo et al., which had a 34.7% prevalence of COVID-19 among healthcare workers exposed to COVID-19 in a hospital based survey at the peak of the first wave in Nigeria [23]. The higher prevalence in their study may be attributed to the surveillance-based data collection method, as opposed to the cross-sectional approach used in our research. Since we recruited apparently healthy healthcare workers for RT-PCR testing, many of those who tested positive were likely pre-symptomatic or asymptomatic cases, which is consistent with SARS-CoV-2 infection patterns in clinical settings [24, 25]. The lower infection rate among our study participants may be explained by the low prevalence of comorbidities and the high rate of vaccine uptake. It is well-established that unvaccinated individuals and those with underlying conditions, such as diabetes and hypertension, face a higher risk of infection and death due to COVID-19 [26].

On the other hand, the PCR-positivity rate of COVID-19 infection in our study is significantly higher than an earlier reported study by Elimian et al. in the first national study on the sociodemographic and clinical factors associated with COVID-19 infection in Nigeria during the first wave of the pandemic. The differential findings may be attributed to stringent testing criteria employed during the early days of the pandemic in Nigeria which provided the data for the study by Elimian et al. [27] Indeed, Nigeria like many other African countries witnessed a significant challenge in testing capabilities for COVID-19 in its early wave [28], thus limiting testing to symptomatic and reasonably-exposed patients, because of the cost and logistics.

Our study found significantly higher concentrations of vitamins A, D, and E among participants who tested negative for COVID-19 than their COVID-19-positive counterparts. The relationship between fat-soluble vitamins, specifically vitamins A, D, and E, and COVID-19 susceptibility as well as severity has been well established in past studies [29–31]. Participants with deficiencies in these vitamins have an increased rate of infection as well as severe health issues. The role of vitamin A in mucosal immunity and the regulation of inflammatory processes has been extensively documented [29].

Likewise, clinical observations have consistently demonstrated that patients with COVID-19 have reduced serum levels of vitamin D, with evidence indicating vitamin D deficiency is associated with a higher risk of infection, greater disease severity, and elevated mortality rates [32, 33]. Furthermore, vitamin K may have a role in COVID-19 pathogenesis, whereby low levels correlate well with disease severity and poor health outcomes [32, 34]. Nonetheless, our study did not demonstrate any significant association between serum vitamin K levels and COVID-19 status. Also, we found higher serum vitamin E levels in SARS-CoV-2-negative individuals, which supports the findings from past literature on the antioxidant and immune-stimulating properties of vitamin E in viral infections [35, 36].

For the water-soluble vitamins assessed in this study, all of them were found to be at higher levels in COVID-19-negative participants except for vitamin B12. These results are also consistent with earlier studies that have demonstrated lower levels of vitamins B1, B2, B5, B6, B9, and C in COVID-19-infected individuals when compared to non-infected individuals [29, 37]. This is not surprising, as water-soluble vitamins have a pivotal role in the immune response to SARS-CoV-2 infection. These vitamins play an important part in the regulation of the synthesis of pro-inflammatory cytokines, as antioxidants to counteract oxidative stress, and in the mediation of numerous enzymatic functions that are relevant to immune response [6, 37]. For instance, Vitamin C has been shown to enhance phagocytosis, reduce inflammation, and protect against injurious oxidative damage, usually associated with the more severe cases of COVID-19 [12, 38]. To a similar extent, the B-complex vitamins play a role in immune modulation due to their influence on cell metabolism, energy production, and DNA synthesis, all involved in the development of an ideal immune response [7, 37]. However, the exception observed in vitamin B12 levels may warrant further investigation.

This study had some limitations; being a single-centre study, the results may not be generalizable to all health facilities in Nigeria. The cross-sectional design lacks capacity to establish causal or temporal relationships between SARS-CoV-2 infection and serum vitamin levels, including whether observed deficiencies preceded infection or occurred as a result of infection. In addition, the absence of a longitudinal follow-up prevented the assessment of whether asymptomatic or pre-symptomatic PCR positive participants later developed symptomatic COVID-19. Self-reported data about the history of infection and past illness may introduce recall bias. Also, comorbidities and previous COVID-19 infection may potentially influence vitamin status, but given the low proportion of participants with reported comorbidities or a history of COVID-19 infection in this study, it is unlikely that these factors significantly affected the observed serum vitamin levels. However, being one of the few studies on this subject matter, the study provides useful insights about vitamin levels and COVID-19 status among health workers in Nigeria.

Further research using larger sample sizes is needed to assess the role of vitamins in long-term COVID-19 and disease severeity, regarding whether pre-infection or post-infection levels influence recovery pathways and severity of symptoms. Future research should also explore the cause-and-effect relationship between vitamin deficiencies and the clinical progression of respiratory viral infections such as SARS-CoV-2 using a longitudinal study design. Also, the role of diets, nutritional status and vitamin supplementation in reducing disease severity and the recovery process may need to be evaluated using randomized controlled trials in the study context.

Conclusion

This study provides insights about the association between micronutrients, especially fat- and water-soluble vitamins, and COVID-19 infection among healthcare workers, a population at higher risk of exposure. Our key finding that COVID-19-negative subjects had higher serum levels of vitamins A, D, and E, along with most water-soluble vitamins, may lend support to the protective role of these micronutrients. The observation is consistent with the literature that reports the immunomodulatory role of these vitamins and further supports the hypothesis that optimal micronutrient status may probably reduce the risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection. These findings have significant implications for the control of respiratory viral diseases, including COVID-19. Our results suggest that micronutrient status is a potentially modifiable risk factor for COVID-19 among the healthcare workers surveyed and may present an opportunity for targeted nutritional interventions to reduce infection risk.
