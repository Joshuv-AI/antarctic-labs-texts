Age-Related Changes in Myeloid Cells and Their Impact on Subcutaneous Melanoma Growth in Mice
Authors: Kaitlyn M. Landreth, Dylan D. Thomas, Remi Nohoesu, Angisha Basnet, F. Heath Damron, Mary Garland-Kledzik, Emel Sen Kilic, Tracy W. Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Advancing age increases cancer risk due to DNA damage accumulation and a decline in immune function. While aging is known to reduce adaptive immunity, it also leads to an increase in immunosuppressive myeloid cells, which promote tumor progression and are linked to poorer outcomes in melanoma.Aims:This study aims to characterize how aging alters Gr-1+ myeloid cell function and their impact on melanoma growth and immunotherapy response.Methods:Subcutaneous tumor growth using several YUMM melanoma cell lines was evaluated in 2-, 6-, and 12-month-old wild-type mice. Immune profiling of tumor-bearing and age-matched healthy mice was performed via flow cytometry and single-cell RNA sequencing. Gr-1+ myeloid cells were isolated to evaluate CD8+ T cell suppression, reactive oxygen species production, and extracellular trap formation. Altered tumor growth, Gr-1+ myeloid cell function, and immune checkpoint therapy response were evaluated comparing 2-month- and 6-month-old wild-type and syngeneic myeloperoxidase-deficient mice.Results:Aging only accelerated YUMM1.7 tumor growth and was associated with increased myeloid-derived suppressor cells, regulatory T cells, and exhausted T cells. Gr-1+ myeloid cells from aged, tumor-bearing mice showed enhanced CD8+ T cell suppression, reactive oxygen species production, and extracellular trap formation. Myeloperoxidase deficiency abrogated age-dependent tumor growth and improved immunotherapy response in YUMM1.7 tumor-bearing mice.Conclusion:Our findings highlight a context-dependent immune response to melanoma with aging, indicating that age-related variations in melanoma growth and immunotherapy response are not ubiquitous. Age-accelerated tumor growth is associated with increased immunosuppressive cell populations alongside enhanced Gr-1+ myeloid cell-mediated immunosuppression driven in part by myeloperoxidase. Myeloperoxidase deficiency effectively reduced Gr-1+ myeloid cell immunosuppression, decreased reactive oxygen species, and diminished extracellular trap formation, thereby eliminating age-dependent differences in tumor growth and immunotherapy response. This work underscores the impact of aging on Gr-1+ myeloid cells on cancer progression and immunotherapy efficacy.

Introduction

Advancing age is one of the most important risk factors for cancer overall. The incidence of most cancers increases dramatically with age [1]. It has generally been accepted that there is an accumulation of DNA damage that occurs with aging caused by lifelong exposure to environmental factors including ultraviolet radiation, pollution, alcohol, and smoking [1, 2]. Increased DNA damage increases the risk of cell transformation and tumor initiation. Additionally, a progressive decline in immune function is a hallmark of aging, diminishing the ability of older individuals to mount an effective immune response against cancer [2–4]. Reduced functionality of the adaptive immune system contributes to the increased incidence of many cancers, including melanoma, in older individuals [1–4]. Aging has been shown to impair cytotoxic CD8+ T cell activation and effector differentiation with an age-dependent decline in tumor-specific T cells, enabling tumor outgrowth, and may also drive resistance to immunotherapy in the elderly [4]. Conversely, aging has also been associated with a decrease in regulatory T cells, which may enhance responses to immune checkpoint therapy (ICT) in aged melanoma murine models [5]. However, clinical trials assessing ICT efficacy in elderly patients have produced mixed results [5–10]. Several studies have reported similar response rates to immune checkpoints between elderly and middle-aged patients. While adaptive immunity is critical to disease management, the innate immune system also significantly influences cancer progression and treatment response.

An increase in immunosuppressive myeloid cells, including myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), tumor-associated neutrophils, and tumor-associated macrophages, has been implicated in cancer development and therapy resistance [11–17]. MDSCs were initially identified in the context of cancer [12–14] and are a heterogeneous population of cells within the tumor microenvironment with pro-tumor and immune-suppressing functions [18–21]. Though typically present at low levels in healthy individuals [22], MDSCs have been shown to increase with age, both in general aging studies [23–28] and in the context of cancer [26, 29–35]. MDSC share surface markers with neutrophils and monocytes, making them difficult to distinguish phenotypically [36–38]. Nonetheless, increased frequencies of immunosuppressive myeloid cells correlate with poor outcomes in melanoma patients treated with ICT [39–41]. Given that MDSCs in murine models are commonly identified by Gr-1 and CD11b expression, we focused our analysis on Gr-1+ myeloid cells to better understand how aging impacts these innate immune cells and subsequently melanoma development. In this study, we characterize the age-mediated differences in Gr-1+ myeloid cells using subcutaneous preclinical models of melanoma. Our data demonstrate an age-dependent increase in tumor growth using the YUMM1.7 melanoma model driven by immune cell changes, including increased immunosuppressive Gr-1+ myeloid cells and exhausted T cells, as well as heightened suppressive activity of aged Gr-1+ myeloid cells, whereby altering Gr-1+ myeloid cells function results in loss of age-dependent tumor growth and immunotherapy response.

Methods

Reagents

Luminol (sodium salt) and lucigenin (N,N′-dimethyl-9,9′-biacridinium dinitrate) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (MO, USA). L-012 sodium salt was purchased from Wako Chemicals USA Inc. (Richmond, VA, USA). Anti-CTLA-4 (9D9) and anti-αPD-1 (RMP1-14) were obtained from Bio X Cell (NH, USA). Luminol sodium salt was dissolved in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to a final concentration of 50 or 100 mg/mL and stored at −20°C. L-012 powder was dissolved in sterile double-distilled water (ddH20) to a final concentration of 20 mM and stored at −20°C. Lucigenin was dissolved in sterile PBS to a final concentration of 1 mg/mL and made fresh for each imaging session.

Cells

YUMM1.7, YUMM1.G1, and YUMM3.3 cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, VA, USA). Cells were cultured in DMEM F12 supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% nonessential amino acids (NEAA). Cell cultures were grown at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. All cell lines tested negative for mycoplasma.

In Vivo Subcutaneous Tumor Model

The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at West Virginia University approved all animal protocols (2109047227). Note that 1 × 105 YUMM1.7, 1 × 105 YUMM1.G1, or 1 × 105 YUMM3.3 cells were injected subcutaneously on the right flank of male and female 2-month-, 6-month-, or 12-month-old C57BL/6 (The Jackson Laboratory, ME, USA) or 2-month- or 6-month-old age-matched syngeneic C57BL/6 myeloperoxidase (MPO)-deficient MPO−/− (MPO1Lus, The Jackson Laboratory, ME, USA) animals. Prior to tumor cell injection, the fur was removed on the right flank. Tumors were measured by calipers every 3–4 days once palpable. Following institutional animal guidelines, animals were euthanized once at tumor endpoint when tumors reached 1.5 cm in diameter or were ulcerated greater than 0.5 cm. At the endpoint, resected tumors were formalin fixed. Immunohistochemistry was performed using a fully automated workflow (HistoWiz Inc., Brooklyn, NY, USA) following standard operating procedures. Samples were processed, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm. Immunohistochemistry was performed on a Bond Rx autostainer (Leica Biosystems) with enzyme treatment (1:1000) using standard protocols. Antibodies used were E-cadherin (Catalog# CST3195), Ki67 (Catalog# ab15580), Gr-1 (Catalog# ab25377), PD-L1 (Catalog# CST64988), vimentin (Catalog# ab92547), and rabbit anti-rat secondary (Vector, 1:100). Bond Polymer Refine Detection (Leica Biosystems) was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After staining, sections were dehydrated and film coverslipped using a TissueTek-Prisma and Coverslipper (Sakura). Whole slide scanning (40×) was performed on an Aperio AT2 (Leica Biosystems), and slides can be accessed at https://app.histowiz.com/shared_orders/0ba70826-4d88-451e-8dd0-a6507c0095ee/slides/. Image analysis was carried out using Halo v4.0.5107.445 from Indica Labs. In the first part of the analysis, the tumor areas were identified by training a random forest classifier algorithm in an attempt to separate viable tumor tissue from any surrounding stroma, necrosis, and background whitespace/glass areas. Once the tumor areas were identified, the analysis then proceeded to identify the positive and negative cells within the tumor areas based on the following biomarker stains: E-Cadherin, Ki67, Gr-1, PD-L1, and vimentin for each slide. Positive and negative cells were identified using the Halo Multiplex IHC algorithm v3.1.4 by first defining the settings for the hematoxylin counterstain, followed by setting thresholds to detect the E-Cadherin stain positivity of weak, moderate, and strong cells (Halo threshold settings 0.028, 0.12, 0.24), and Ki67 stain positivity of weak, moderate, and strong nuclei (Halo threshold settings 0.14, 0.36, 0.76). Gr-1 stain positivity of weak, moderate, and strong nuclei (Halo threshold settings 0.12, 0.35, 0.55); PD-L1 stain positivity of weak, moderate, and strong cells (Halo threshold settings 0.12, 0.33, 0.55); and vimentin stain positivity of weak, moderate, and strong cells (Halo threshold settings 0.25, 0.45, 0.65). H-scores were then generated following the convention: weak positive (1+), moderate positive (2+), and strong positive (3+). Animals were treated with ICT (200 μg anti-CTLA-4 and 200 μg anti-PD-1 beginning on day 3 post tumor inoculation and subsequently given every 3 days for two doses at 100 μg anti-CTLA-4 and 100 μg anti-PD-1) [16]. Survival curve statistical significance calculation was alpha-adjusted for multiple comparisons. Comparison of survival curves used the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) analysis. Animals were euthanized by carbon dioxide asphyxiation, and single-cell suspensions were harvested from the spleen and tumors of tumor-bearing mice as indicated for ex vivo characterization.

Single-Cell RNA Sequencing

Tumor-bearing and age-matched healthy mice were euthanized using carbon dioxide asphyxiation. Single-cell suspensions from the spleens of tumor-bearing mice were collected at the tumor endpoint. Spleen cells were also harvested from healthy, tumor-free mice. Samples were pooled per group and mixed in equal proportions. RNA was extracted using the Chromium NEXT GEM Single Cell 5’ Kit (10X Genomics, Pleasanton, CA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Read coverage was calculated at 2 billion paired reads per 5’ library sample. For each sample, 10,000 total cells were loaded to each channel, with an average recovery of 7000 cells. Data were processed using the Loupe Browser (8.0.0). Barcodes were filtered based on Log2 thresholds: UMIs between 9 and 14, features (genes) between 8 and 12, and mitochondrial content between 0% and 5%. After quality control filtering, 23,052 cells remained for downstream analysis. t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) plots were utilized to visualize the clustering of cells based on their gene expression profiles using Loupe Browser 8.0.0, and heatmap and dot plot figures were created using R to illustrate differentially expressed genes.

Flow Cytometry

At the tumor endpoint, single-cell suspensions were harvested from the spleen and tumor. Single-cell suspensions were harvested from the spleen of age-matched healthy (tumor-free) mice. Tumors were dissociated using a mouse tumor dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, CA, USA). Ulcerated tumors were excluded whenever possible to avoid confounding effects from necrotic tissue. In cases where all tumors within a group were ulcerated, the ulcerated regions were carefully excluded during tumor dissociation to prevent skewing the immune cell population data. Red blood cells are lysed using 1X red blood cell lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA). Remaining cells were resuspended in cell staining buffer (BioLegend, CA, USA) at a concentration of 2 × 105–1 × 106 cells per 100 μL. Fc receptors are blocked using 10 μg/mL ChromPure of mouse IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch Inc., PA, USA) per 106 cells in a 100 μL volume for 10 min on ice. Cells were washed with cell staining buffer and incubated with an antibody mix to label lymphoid and myeloid immune cells (Tables S1 and S2) for 15–20 min on ice in the dark. Cells were fixed using 200 μL fixation buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA) for 20 min at 4°C in the dark. Cells were washed 2 times with 1X PermWash (BD Bioscience, CA, USA) and permeabilized using permeabilization solution (BD Bioscience, CA, USA). Intracellular antibodies were incubated for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. Stained cells were analyzed using the Cytek Aurora (Cytek, MD, USA) within 2 weeks of staining. Data were analyzed using FCS Express (De Novo Software, CA, USA).

Gr-1+ Myeloid Cells Cell Isolation

At the tumor endpoint, single-cell suspensions were harvested from the spleen and tumors of tumor-bearing mice. Single-cell suspensions were harvested from the spleen of age-matched healthy (tumor-free) mice. Cd11b+Gr-1+ myeloid cells were isolated using an EasySep mouse MDSC isolation kit (STEMCELL Technologies Inc, MA, USA). Cd11b+Gr-1+ myeloid cells were quantified by flow cytometry using CD11b-APC (Miltenyi Biotec, CA, USA) and Gr-1-PE (Miltenyi Biotec, CA, USA), and only Cd11b+Gr-1+ myeloid cells purity of > 80% were used in the subsequent studies quantifying T cell proliferation suppression, reactive oxygen species levels, and extracellular trap formation.

T Cell Proliferation Assay

CD8+ T cells were isolated from the spleen of 8- to 10-week-old, sex-matched healthy C57BL/6 mice using an EasySep mouse CD8+ T cell isolation kit (STEMCELL Technologies Inc, MA, USA). Note that 1 × 106 isolated T cells/mL were stained with a 5 μM concentration of CellTrace CFSE (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA) and incubated at 37°C for 20 min. Cells were incubated for 5 min with culture media to remove unbound dye and subsequently washed two times. CSFE-stained T cells were incubated for 2–3 h at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere at a final concentration of 1 × 105 isolated cells/ 100 μL. The following controls were incubated using a 1:1 bead-to-cell ratio of mouse T cell activation CD3/CD28 dynabeads (5 μL, Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA) and immediately fixed using 0.4% paraformaldehyde overnight after CSFE staining of T cells: 2 × 105 unstained (no CSFE) T cells, 2 × 105 CSFE stained T cells (time zero, t = 0), and 2 × 105 Gr-1+ myeloid cells. Using a 1:1 ratio, 2 × 105 T cells were cultured with 2 × 105 Gr-1+ myeloid cells in 500 μL of RPMI Medium 1640 with 2 mM l-glutamine, 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin, and 5 μL dynabeads in 24-well plates for 72 h at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. After 72 h, the 24-well plate was washed two times with culture media, and the cell pellets were fixed using 0.4% paraformaldehyde overnight in the dark. T cell proliferation was quantified using the BD LSRFortessa Cell Analyzer (BD Biosciences, NJ, USA). Data were analyzed using FCS Express (De Novo Software, CA, USA).

Myeloperoxidase Activity and Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Quantification

Myeloperoxidase activity was assessed by luminol imaging, and ROS were assessed using L-012 and lucigenin imaging of the isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells. Note that 1 × 105 isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells were added to 100 mM luminol, 50 μM L-012, or 100 μM lucigenin in colorless DMEM media in a 96-well black-walled plate. Cells were immediately imaged for 60 min using the Kino imaging system (Spectral Instruments Imaging, AZ, USA) at 37°C under 5% CO2 flow. Typical acquisition parameters were as follows: acquisition time, 5 min; binning, 16; FOV, 13 cm; f/stop, 1.2; filter, open; total number of acquisitions, 12. Bioluminescence photon flux (photons/s) data were analyzed by ROI measurements with background subtraction in Aura (Spectral Instruments Imaging, AZ, USA); these raw data were imported into Excel (Microsoft Corp., WA, USA) and averaged in each individual experiment if done in duplicate or triplicate wells. The area under the curve was calculated from images taken at 5–60 min. The area under the curve for each reporter was taken from n = 3 independent isolation experiments.

Extracellular Traps Quantification

Note that 1 × 105 isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells in 100 μL RPMI 1640 media were incubated for 1–2 h in a 96-well plate. After incubation, the cells were spun at 1000 rpm for 10 min. Note that 50 μL of supernatant was removed and mixed with 50 μL of 1X TE buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA) in a 96-well black-walled, clear-bottom plate. Note that 50 μL of a 1:200 dilution of Pico Green in 1X TE buffer was added to each well, and fluorescence (excitation: 480 nm and emission: 520 nm) was quantified on a Synergy HTX multi-mode plate reader (BioTex, CA, US). A standard curve was made using the lambda DNA standard (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA) in 1X TE buffer (concentration range from 1 μg/mL to 25 pg/mL). The DNA concentration of the sample was determined from the generated DNA standard curve.

Statistical Analyses

Graphs were made and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc, CA, USA). The tumor growth curve fits over time, using an exponential growth fit. Data were expressed as mean ± SD or SEM. For analysis of three or more groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests were performed followed by a Tukey’s or Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test, or false discovery rate test. The standard comparison of survival curves used the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) analysis. Analysis of differences between two normally distributed paired test groups was performed using a Student’s t-test. p values were considered statistically significant if p < 0.05.

Results

Increased Melanoma Tumor Growth With Age

Subcutaneous injection of YUMM1.7 melanoma cells in C57BL/6 wild-type (WT) mice at 2 months, 6 months, and 12 months of age demonstrated a significant increase in tumor growth in 6- and 12-month-old mice when compared to 2-month-old mice (Figure 1A,B). A significant decrease in the survival curve was observed in tumor-bearing 6-month-old aged mice (mean survival time 22.67 ± 1 days) compared to young 2-month-old mice (mean survival time 26 ± 2 days; Figure S1A,B). The survival curve of YUMM1.7 tumor-bearing 6-month-old mice was also significantly decreased compared to 12-month-old mice (mean survival time 26.50 ± 3.33 days; Figure S1A,B). Although tumor volume appeared similar between 6-month-old and 12-month-old mice, the 6-month-old mice reached the tumor endpoint earlier, as all developed ulcers > 5 mm. In contrast, only 30% of tumors in 12-month-old mice ulcerated, contributing to the observed difference in survival. Sex-based differences in tumor growth were only observed in 12-month-old mice where male mice demonstrated a statistically significant increase in tumor growth compared to female mice (Figure 1C). A significant increase in tumor growth with aging in male mice was observed, while in female mice, 6-month-old mice demonstrated the fastest tumor growth with no difference in tumor growth between 2-month- and 12-month-old female mice (Figure 1C and Figure S1C,D). Interestingly, no tumor growth differences were observed for YUMM1.G1 subcutaneous tumors with aging (Figure S2A,B). Similar to YUMM1.7, YUMM1.G1 demonstrated no difference in tumor growth, but 6-month-old mice had a significantly shorter survival curve (mean survival time 34 ± 5 days) due to increased ulcerations compared to 12-month-old mice (mean survival time 43) (Figure S2C). Sex-based differences in tumor growth were only observed in 6-month-old mice where females demonstrated a statistically significant increase in tumor growth compared to male mice (Figure S2D–F). In contrast, YUMM3.3 tumors grew significantly faster in young 2-month-old mice compared to 6-month-old female mice (Figure S3). These data demonstrate that the impact of age on tumor growth appears to be model and context dependent where increased tumor growth with aging was only observed in the YUMM1.7 subcutaneous melanoma mouse model.

Age-Induced Changes in Tumor-Immune Composition

Given the increased tumor growth with aging observed in YUMM1.7 tumors, the tumor-immune composition of YUMM1.7 subcutaneous tumors and systemic immune changes of the spleen from 2-month-, 6-month-, and 12-month-old WT mice were evaluated using flow cytometry at tumor endpoint. A significant increase in CD11b+ myeloid cells was observed in the tumors of 6-month-old WT mice compared to 2-month- and 12-month-old WT mice (Figure S4A). A significant increase in CD3−CD11b− immune cell populations was observed in the spleen of both tumor-bearing 6-month- and 12-month-old WT mice compared to 2-month-old WT mice (Figure S4B). Further characterization of the specific immune cell subsets demonstrated a significant decrease in B cells (CD3−CD11b−CD19+CD45R+) in the tumor of 6-month-old compared to 12-month-old mice, while a significant increase in macrophages (CD11b+F4/80+CD115+) was observed in the tumor of 6-month-old compared to both 2-month- and 12-month-old mice (Figure 2A). A significant decrease in CD4+ T cells was observed in the spleen of tumor-bearing 6-month-old mice compared to 2-month-old mice (Figure 2B). Interestingly, a significant increase in B cells was observed with age in the spleen of tumor-bearing WT mice (Figure 2B). No differences in the tumor-immune composition at any age between male and female mice were observed (Figure S4C–E), except for an increase in CD3−CD11b− immune cells in the spleen in tumor-bearing male compared to female mice, but only at 2 months of age (Figure S2F–H).

In order to elucidate whether these differences in immune composition were due to the presence of a tumor and/or a consequence of aging, the immune composition of the spleen from healthy mice was analyzed using flow cytometry. Similar to tumor-bearing mice, we observe a significant increase in CD3−CD11b− and CD11b+ immune cells with aging but also a significant decrease in CD3+ cells with aging (Figure S5A). Note that 2-month-old healthy mice had significantly more macrophages when compared to 12-month-old healthy mice (Figure S5B). There was a significant increase in CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells in 2-month-old and 6-month-old mice compared to 12-month-old mice (Figure S5B). Note that 6-month-old animals had a significant increase in regulatory T cells when compared to 2-month-old and 12-month-old animals (Figure S5B). Note that 6-month-old mice also had a significant increase in B cells compared to 2-month-old mice (Figure S5B). Note that 12-month-old mice showed a significant increase in CD11b+Ly6G+ cells compared to 2- and 6-month-old animals (Figure S5B). The presence of a YUMM1.7 tumor significantly increased B cells at every age (Figure S5C–E). A decrease in CD4+ T cells was observed at 6 months of age when a tumor was present (Figure S5D), while a significant increase in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells was observed at 12 months of age when a tumor was present (Figure S5E). These flow cytometry data suggest that aging alone leads to a decrease in CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and macrophages and an increase in CD11b+Ly6G+ myeloid cells and B cells within the spleen. Additionally, the presence of YUMM1.7 tumors further significantly elevated B-cell populations.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) evaluated the transcriptomic changes in the spleen of young 2-month-old and aged 12-month-old tumor-bearing and healthy mice (Figure 3A). Immune cell populations were annotated using known markers, allowing for the identification of key subtypes such as T cells (CD3d, CD3e, CD3g), B cells (CD19, Ms4a1, CD79a, CD79b), NK cells (Ncr1, Ncam1), neutrophils (S100a8, S100a9), monocytes (Nr4a1, Ms4a4a), macrophages (Mertk, CD163, CD5l), dendritic cells (Siglech, Adam23, Baft3), and basophils (Mcpt8) (Figure 3B). Granulocytic (G-MDSCs) and monocytic (M-MDSCs) MDSCs were identified based on a combined Log2-transformed expression (sum expression > 5) of specific marker genes: Retnlg, Ngp, S100a8, and S100a9 for G-MDSCs, and Itgam, CD14, Ccr2, and Chil3 for M-MDSCs [42–47] (Figure S6). The percentage of each immune cell subtype was determined, demonstrating the relative proportions of immune cells in tumor-bearing and healthy spleens at different ages (Figure 3C). Additionally, the absolute number of cells for each immune cell subtype was calculated, providing a representation of the total immune cell counts across all experimental groups (Figure 3D). A heatmap of the neutrophils, G-MDSCs, M-MDSCs, macrophages, dendritic cells, and monocytes shows the top 10 expressed genes within each subset (Figure S6). A similar number of barcoded cells was quantified regardless of whether a tumor was present in each age group. B cells were the predominant population found in the spleen (Figure 3D). B cells constituted 49% and 50.7% of immune cells in 2-month-old tumor-bearing and healthy mice, and 54% and 63% in 12-month-old tumor-bearing and healthy mice, respectively (Figure 3E–H). The proportion of B cells increased with age in tumor-bearing mice from 50.7% at 2 months to 63% at 12 months of age. In tumor-bearing mice, the proportion of G-MDSCs increased approximately fivefold with age when comparing 2-month-old to 12-month-old animals; in contrast, M-MDSCs showed only a modest increase over the same period (Figure 3C,E–H). Notably, the proportions of macrophages and neutrophils declined in tumor-bearing older mice (Figure 3C,E–H).

T cells represented the second most abundant immune cell population identified (Figure 3D). A reduction in T cell proportion was observed in 12-month-old tumor-bearing mice (27%) compared to age-matched healthy controls (35%) (Figure 3F,H). In contrast, T cell proportions were similar between 2-month-old healthy (38%) and tumor-bearing mice (35%) (Figure 3E,G). We further investigated the T-cell population (Figure 4A,B). Transcriptional profiles of individual T cells allowed grouping of similar cells into clusters, which were named based on expression levels of well-known genes (Figure S7). We observed an increased abundance of exhausted T helper cells, exhausted cytotoxic T cells, and regulatory T cells in 12-month-old tumor-bearing mice compared to an age-matched healthy control group (Figure 4C). Tox2, Pdcd1, Lag3, Tigit, and Tnfrsf9, which are associated with exhaustion [48–51], were highly expressed in T cells from 12-month-old tumor-bearing mice relative to other groups (Figure 4D–G). In addition, the regulatory T cells in tumor-bearing mice also expressed several exhaustion-associated markers (Figure 4C, Figure S7). An increase in T regulatory cells, exhausted T helper cells, and exhausted cytotoxic T cells, and a decrease in T helper and naïve cytotoxic T cells in 12-month-old compared to 2-month-old tumor-bearing mice was also observed (Figure 4C). Together, these data highlight age- and tumor-associated shifts in immune cell populations, marked by increases in MDSCs and exhausted cytotoxic and helper T cells, suggesting age-related shifts driving decreased protection against cancer.

Age-Related Increase in Gr-1+ Myeloid Cell T Cell Suppression, ROS Production, and Extracellular Trap Formation

We isolated Cd11b+Gr-1+ myeloid cells from the tumor and spleen of tumor-bearing WT mice. No significant difference in isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cell numbers was observed with aging in tumor-bearing mice (Figure 5A and Figure S8A). No significant difference was observed between isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cell numbers from the tumor or spleen in male and female mice (Figure S9A–C). Isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells from YUMM1.7 tumors and spleens of tumor-bearing WT mice demonstrated significantly increased suppression of CD8+ T cell proliferation with age (Figure 5B and Figure S8B). Gr-1+ myeloid cells isolated from the tumor and spleen of 12-month-old mice demonstrated the greatest T cell suppression. No difference in suppression of T cells was observed between Gr-1+ myeloid cells isolated from male and female mice at any age (Figure S9D–F). Both tumor- and spleen-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells demonstrated a statistically significant increase in ROS levels with age using L-012 bioluminescence quantification where 12-month-old mice demonstrated the highest levels of ROS (Figure 5C and Figure S8C). To further investigate specific ROS species, we employed luminol, which quantifies MPO activity and primarily produces hypochlorous acid, and lucigenin, which measures superoxide anions. MPO is a myeloid lineage-restricted enzyme that primarily produces hypochlorous acid [52, 53]. MPO activity was quantified using luminol bioluminescence [54–56] and was significantly increased in Gr-1+ myeloid cells isolated from the tumor of 6-month-old mice compared to 2-month- and 12-month-old mice (Figure 5D). While spleen-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells also demonstrated a significant increase in luminol bioluminescence with age where 12-month-old mice had the highest levels of MPO activity (Figure S8D). The levels of superoxide anion, another specific ROS species, were quantified using lucigenin bioluminescence [57] where no difference was observed in tumor-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells (Figure 5E). Spleen-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells from 2-month-old mice compared to 6-month- and 12-month-old mice had a significant increase in lucigenin bioluminescence (Figure S8E). Variations were observed in ROS levels quantified using L-012, luminol, and lucigenin bioluminescence between male and female mice and at the various ages (Figure S9G–L). The most significant differences in ROS levels were observed in tumor-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells at 6 months of age (Figure S9I). A significant increase in extracellular trap formation, as measured by the release of cell-free DNA in cultured Gr-1+ myeloid cells supernatant, was observed with aging but only from Gr-1+ myeloid cells isolated from the tumor (Figure 5F), while no differences were observed from spleen-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells (Figure S8F). No differences in extracellular trap formation were observed between male and female mice at 2 and 12 months of age (Figure S9M,O). A significant increase in extracellular trap formation was observed in tumor-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells from male compared to female mice at 6 months of age (Figure S9N). In YUMM1.7 tumor-bearing mice, aging is generally associated with a significant increase in T cell suppression (as indicated by decreased T cell proliferation), enhanced ROS production, elevated MPO activity, and greater extracellular trap formation by Gr-1+ myeloid cells (Figure 5G and Figure S8G).

In order to elucidate whether these differences in Gr-1+ myeloid cells were due to the presence of a tumor and/or a consequence of aging, Gr-1+ myeloid cells were isolated and characterized from the spleen of healthy mice at 2, 6, and 12 months of age. No significant differences were observed in the number of spleen-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells with aging (Figure S10A) or between male and female mice at 2- and 6-month-old mice (Figure S10B,C). A significant increase in Gr-1+ myeloid cell number was observed in male compared to female mice at 12 months of age (Figure S10D). A significant increase in the number of spleen-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells was observed in tumor-bearing mice compared to healthy (tumor-free) mice but only at 2 months of age (Figure S10E), and no differences in Gr-1+ myeloid cell number were observed at 6 or 12 months of age, regardless if a tumor was present (Figure S10F,G). Gr-1+ myeloid cells isolated from healthy mice did not suppress T cell proliferation, as proliferation values remained at or above 1 (Figure S10H). No differences were observed in T cell proliferation between females and males at any age (Figure S10I–K). However, these healthy isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells had minimal suppressive activity in comparison to spleen-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells from tumor-bearing mice; a significant increase in Gr-1+ myeloid cells suppression of T cell proliferation was observed at all ages in the presence of a tumor (Figure S10L–N). An increase in ROS production levels was seen in Gr-1+ myeloid cells isolated from 6-month-old healthy WT mice compared to 2- and 12-month-old mice by L-012, luminol, and lucigenin imaging (Figure S11A–C). Gr-1+ myeloid cells isolated from 2-month-old female mice had a significant increase in L-012 and luminol bioluminescence compared to age-matched male mice (Figure S11D,E) but no difference in lucigenin bioluminescence (Figure S11F). No differences in L-012 or luminol bioluminescence were observed between females and males in Gr-1+ myeloid cells isolated from 6-month-old healthy mice (Figure S11G,H), while an increase in lucigenin bioluminescence was observed in 6-month-old female Gr-1+ myeloid cells compared to males (Figure S11I). Gr-1+ myeloid cells isolated from 12-month-old healthy females had significantly increased L-012 ROS production compared to age-matched male Gr-1+ myeloid cells (Figure S11J), while no differences were observed in luminol or lucigenin bioluminescence between female and male Gr-1+ myeloid cells isolated from 12-month-old healthy mice (Figure S11K,L). Interestingly, at 2 months of age, the presence of a tumor resulted in significantly decreased MPO activity but increased superoxide anion levels in spleen-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells, comparing YUMM1.7 tumor-bearing and healthy mice (Figure S11M). While at 6 months of age, spleen-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells from tumor-bearing mice had significantly decreased ROS levels measured by L-012, luminol, and lucigenin (Figure S11N). In contrast, at 12 months of age, the presence of a tumor resulted in significantly increased L-012 and luminol bioluminescence in spleen-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells from tumor-bearing compared to healthy mice (Figure S11O). No differences in extracellular trap formation were observed with aging in Gr-1+ myeloid cells isolated from healthy mice (Figure S12A). A significant increase in extracellular trap formation was observed in Gr-1+ myeloid cells isolated from males compared to females at 2 months of age (Figure S12B), while no differences were observed between males and females at 6 and 12 months of age (Figure S12C,D). The presence of cancer significantly increased extracellular trap formation at all ages in Gr-1+ myeloid cells isolated from tumor-bearing mice compared to healthy mice (Figure S12E). These data suggest that aging enhanced T cell suppression, ROS production, and extracellular trap formation in Gr-1+ myeloid cells, but only in the context of cancer.

Myeloperoxidase Alters Gr-1+ Myeloid Cell Function

Given the increase in ROS levels and MPO activity with age in YUMM1.7 tumor-bearing mice, we evaluated how MPO altered Gr-1+ myeloid cell function. Since a significant increase in tumor volume was observed between 2-month- and 6-month-old WT mice, we evaluated how MPO deficiency affected tumor growth and Gr-1+ myeloid cells function at these two ages. The tumor growth differences observed in WT mice (Figure 1A,B) were lost when MPO was deficient (Figure 6A and Figure S13A). No significant YUMM1.7 tumor growth differences were observed in 2-month- versus 6-month-old C57BL/6 syngeneic MPO-deficient (MPO−/−) mice or when compared to age-matched WT mice (Figure 6A and Figure S13A). However, a significant increase in survival time was observed when MPO was deficient in 6-month-old mice (Figure S13B). Similar to the aging studies, although tumor volume appeared similar between WT and MPO−/− mice, only 40% of tumors in MPO−/− mice ulcerated, contributing to the observed difference in survival. Immunohistological analysis of endpoint tumors demonstrated a significant increase in PD-L1 expression in 6-month-old WT tumors compared to 2-month-old and MPO-deficient tumors (Figure S14A). A significant increase in Ki67 expression and E-cadherin was observed in 6-month-old MPO-deficient tumors compared to 2-month-old MPO−/− tumors (Figure S14B,C), suggesting that, in older MPO-null mice, tumor cells are proliferating more rapidly, and the tumor may be shifting toward a more epithelial phenotype. No differences in Gr-1 or vimentin expression were observed (Figure S14C,D). Given the lack of age-dependent differences in YUMM1.G1 subcutaneous tumor growth, as expected, no difference in tumor growth or survival was observed when MPO was deficient (Figure S15A,B). These findings suggest that MPO contributes to age-associated tumor progression specifically in YUMM1.7 melanoma tumors.

Flow cytometry analysis of endpoint YUMM1.7 tumors demonstrated no differences in specific immune cell subsets found within the tumor microenvironment when MPO was deficient (Figure 6B). However, a significant increase in CD11b+ myeloid cells in 2-month-old mice was observed in the tumor in MPO−/− mice compared to age-matched WT mice (Figure S13C). Similarly, flow cytometry analysis of the spleen demonstrated a significant increase in CD11b+ myeloid cells in tumor-bearing MPO−/− mice that was further increased in 6-month-old MPO−/− mice compared to age-matched WT mice (Figure S13D). In contrast, CD3−CD11b+ cells were significantly decreased in the spleen of tumor-bearing MPO−/− mice compared to tumor-bearing WT mice at both 2 and 6 months of age (Figure S13D). A significant increase in CD11b+ myeloid cells was observed in the tumors of male compared to female MPO−/− mice at 2 and 6 months of age (Figure S13E,F). A significant decrease in CD4+ T cells and a significant increase in B cells were observed in the spleen of 2-month-old MPO−/− compared to WT mice (Figure 6C). However, a significant increase in CD11b+Ly6G+ myeloid cells and a significant decrease in B cells were observed in 6-month-old MPO−/− compared to WT mice (Figure 6C). A significant decrease in B cells was observed in 6-month-old compared to 2-month-old MPO−/− (Figure 6C). No sex-based differences in immune cell subsets in the spleen were observed (Figure S13G,H). These data suggest that MPO alters systemic and tumor-associated immune cell populations in a context-dependent manner.

Similar to the flow cytometry studies, a significant increase in tumor-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells from 2-month-old MPO−/− mice compared to WT mice was observed (Figure S16A). However, a significant increase in spleen-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells was observed from tumor-bearing 6-month-old MPO−/− mice compared to WT mice (Figure S16B). No differences were observed in the number of Gr-1+ myeloid cells isolated between male and female MPO−/− mice (Figure S16C,D). A significant decrease in MPO activity measured by luminol in tumor- and spleen-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells from MPO−/− mice compared to WT age-matched mice only at 6 months of age was observed. (Figure 6D and Figure S16E). A significant decrease in ROS levels measured by L-012 was observed in tumor-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells from 2-month-old MPO−/− mice compared to WT mice, while a significant increase in L-012 bioluminescence was observed from 6-month-old MPO−/− mice compared to WT mice (Figure 6E). No differences in ROS levels were observed in spleen-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells between MPO−/− and WT mice (Figure S16F). Only tumor-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells from 6-month-old MPO−/− mice demonstrated a significant decrease in lucigenin compared to age-matched WT Gr-1+ myeloid cells (Figure 6F and Figure S16G). A significant increase in L-012 bioluminescence in tumor-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells from 2-month-old MPO−/− males compared to female mice was observed (Figure S16H,I), while spleen-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells from 6-month-old MPO−/− males demonstrated a significant increase in L-012 bioluminescence compared to females (Figure S16J,K). Gr-1+ myeloid cells’ ability to suppress CD8+ T cell proliferation was significantly reduced when MPO was deficient (Figure 6G and Figure S16L). No differences in Gr-1+ myeloid cells ability to suppress T cells were observed between male and female MPO−/− mice (Figure S16M,N). A significant decrease in extracellular trap formation by tumor-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells was observed when MPO was deficient in 6-month-old mice (Figure 6H). No differences were observed in extracellular trap formation in spleen-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells between WT and MPO−/− mice (Figure S16O). A significant decrease in extracellular trap formation was observed in tumor-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells from 2-month-old male MPO−/− mice compared to females (Figure S16P). However, a significant decrease in extracellular traps was observed in spleen-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells from 6-month-old male MPO−/− mice compared to female mice (Figure S16Q). These data demonstrate that loss of MPO decreased Gr-1+ myeloid cells immunosuppression and extracellular trap formation, which was more pronounced with age.

Age and MPO Affected ICT Response

ICT has become a standard of care in the treatment of melanoma. YUMM1.7 tumor-bearing WT mice were treated with dual anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 ICT, and a significant increase in survival was observed in 2-month-old compared to 6-month-old WT mice (Figure 7A). Given the significant decrease in MPO activity, T cell suppression, and extracellular trap formation when MPO was deficient in Gr-1+ myeloid cells, particularly in aged mice, we evaluated how MPO deficiency affected ICT response. A statistically significant increase in survival time was observed in 6-month-old mice with MPO deficiency compared to age-matched ICT-treated WT mice (Figure 7A). However, given the survival differences observed in untreated WT and MPO−/− mice (Figure S13B), the loss of MPO may be the primary factor influencing these survival differences. In contrast, while the increase in survival time in 2-month-old mice approached significance, it did not reach statistical significance. It should be noted that 2 of the 12 ICT-treated 2-month-old MPO−/− mice (16.7%) had long-term survival of 100 days with no palpable tumors (Figure 7A), while 2-month-old WT mice were 100% ICT resistant. When MPO was deficient, age-dependent responses to ICT were lost. Calculating the exponential tumor growth curves demonstrated statistically significantly slower growth rates from ICT treatment when MPO was deficient at both 2 months and 6 months of age compared to ICT-treated WT mice (Figure 7B,C). The tumor growth doubling time of ICT-treated 2-month-old MPO−/− mice was 11.34 ± 9.75 days, while that of ICT-treated 2-month-old WT mice was 4.23 ± 1.22 days. However, for 6-month-old ICT-treated MPO−/− and WT mice, the tumor growth doubling time is 7.06 ± 4.67 and 3.87 ± 1.40 days. No differences in tumor growth rates were observed between ICT-treated male and female mice at 2 months of age (Figure S17A,B). However, ICT-treated 6-month-old female WT mice have faster tumor growth rates than 6-month-old male WT mice (Figure S17C), while no difference was observed in MPO−/− mice between females and males (Figure S17D). We also evaluated the impact of MPO deficiency and age on ICT response in YUMM1.G1 tumor-bearing mice, and similar to the untreated cohort, no significant differences in tumor growth or survival were observed in ICT-treated WT versus MPO−/− mice at 2 or 6 months of age (Figure S17E,F). However, it should be noted that there was one long-term survivor with no palpable tumor at Day 100 in the 6-month-old MPO−/− cohort (Figure S17F). We previously reported a significant increase in ICT efficacy in 6-month-old YUMM3.3 tumor-bearing mice when MPO was deficient or inhibited [16]. Interestingly, 2-month-old YUMM3.3 tumor-bearing WT mice demonstrated 100% long-term survival with no palpable tumors at Day 100 when treated with ICT (Figure S17G). Since YUMM3.3 subcutaneous tumor-bearing mice were completely responsive to ICT, evaluating improved response to MPO deficiency was not possible. However, YUMM3.3 tumors demonstrated improved ICT response in young compared to aged mice. These data demonstrate that MPO deficiency enhanced ICT response specifically in age-accelerated YUMM1.7 subcutaneous tumors and suggest MPO may overcome age-associated limitations in immunotherapy responsiveness.

Discussion

Aging and immunity do not follow a linear trajectory; both undergo complex, dynamic changes that influence cancer development. It is important to evaluate how aging and the immune system impact tumor progression, considering the asynchrony in aging-related immune cell alterations. Our data demonstrate a nonlinear, context-dependent immune response to subcutaneous melanoma tumors with aging, indicating that age-related differences in melanoma growth and immunotherapy response are not ubiquitous. The results presented here suggest that the accelerated tumor growth observed in aged YUMM1.7 models is associated with an increase in immunosuppressive MDSCs, exhausted T cells, and regulatory T cells, alongside enhanced Gr-1+ myeloid cell-mediated immunosuppression driven in part by MPO, which collectively contribute to reduced ICT efficacy.

Like many cancers, melanoma incidence increases with age. The average age of melanoma diagnosis is 65 years [58, 59]. However, melanoma is unique among cancers, as it is also one of the most common malignancies in young adults aged 20–35 [58, 59]. Our study examined distinct phases of aging using 2-, 6-, and 12-month-old mice, representing key developmental and immunological stages: 2 months corresponds to sexual maturity, 6 months reflects immunological maturity, and 12 months captures age-related immune changes [60–62]. Life phase equivalency defines a 3- to 6-month-old mouse as an adult, comparable to a 20- to 30-year-old human, while a 10- to 14-month-old mouse corresponds to 38- to 47-year-old humans [61, 62]. Thus, a 2-month-old mouse would be equivalent to a human under 20 years of age. Our studies demonstrate that YUMM1.7 subcutaneous tumors exhibited accelerated growth in aged mice (Figure 1A,B), whereas YUMM1.G1 tumors showed no age-related differences (Figure S2), and YUMM3.3 tumors grew slower in aged mice compared to young controls (Figure S3). These age-related differences in tumor growth may be attributed to differences in the expressed genetic drivers among the YUMM cell lines: YUMM1.7 (BrafV600E/wt Cdkn2−/− Pten−/−), YUMM1.G1 (BrafV600E/wt Cdkn2a−/− Pten−/− mcr1(e/e)), and YUMM3.3 (BrafV600E/wt Cdkn2a−/−) [63]. Previous studies using the YUMM1.7 melanoma cell line stably expressing an mCherry or GFP fluorescent reporter demonstrated reduced primary tumor growth in 12-month-old mice compared to 2-month-old mice [33, 64]. While this remains speculative, the observed age-related differences in YUMM1.7 tumor growth may be influenced by the inclusion of the fluorescent reporter, which could enhance the immunogenicity of the tumor cell line. Given that melanoma is a highly immunogenic cancer, it could be argued that the YUMM1.7 mCherry or GFP cell lines more closely model clinical disease by increasing tumor immunogenicity. Another study reported no difference in YUMM1.7 subcutaneous tumor growth between 12-month-old and 4-month-old mice [65]. Variations in YUMM1.7 tumor growth outcomes across studies may stem from several factors, including differences in the number of melanoma cells injected, which can influence tumor kinetics, as well as disparities in microbiota composition arising from distinct animal vendors or housing facilities. Although beyond the scope of this work, we observed sex-based differences, wherein in male mice, YUMM1.7 tumor growth was significantly increased with aging, while in females, tumor growth appears to be bimodal with aging (Figure 1C). YUMM1.7 melanoma cells, which originate from the skin of male mice [63], show sex-based differences in tumor growth only in 12-month-old mice. The reduced tumor growth in 12-month-old female mice may result from an immune response targeting the Y chromosome present in the YUMM1.7 cell line. In contrast, YUMM1.G1 melanoma cells only show sex-based differences at 6 months of age, with female mice showing a significant increase in tumor growth compared to male mice (Figure S2D–F). Since YUMM1.G1 cells originate from female mice [63], this effect is unlikely to be driven by sex chromosome incompatibility. If an immune response against sex chromosomal differences were a primary factor, we would expect consistent effects across ages. Additionally, it is unclear why sex-based differences in tumor growth were observed only in aged mice. The restriction of sex-based differences to specific age groups suggests a more complex interplay. These findings may reflect a combination of enhanced antitumor immune responses in females and age-related alterations in immune function that differ between sexes [66, 67].

Our studies characterized how aging altered the immune cell populations, comparing tumor-bearing and healthy mice. As expected, healthy aged mice exhibited a decline in CD8+ and CD4+ T cells alongside an increase in CD11b+ Ly6G+ myeloid cells in the spleen (Figure S5B). Surprisingly, quantification of Gr-1+ myeloid cells from endpoint flow cytometry and isolation studies did not demonstrate an age-associated increase in Gr-1+ myeloid cells within the YUMM1.7 tumors. These analyses were conducted at the tumor endpoint. Perhaps Gr-1+ myeloid cells recruitment is an early event in melanoma development, and increased frequency of myeloid cells would be observed if evaluated at earlier timepoints. In contrast, scRNAseq analysis of the spleen demonstrated a five-fold increase in granulocytic MDSCs in 12-month-old tumor-bearing mice compared to 2-month-old tumor-bearing mice and age-matched healthy control mice, albeit they constituted a relatively small proportion of immune cells (Figure 3E–H). Importantly, even low numbers of MDSCs are capable of exerting profound immunosuppressive effects [68]. Furthermore, an increase in exhausted T helper cells, exhausted cytotoxic T cells, and regulatory T cells alongside a decrease in T helper and naïve cytotoxic T cells was observed in 12-month-old compared to 2-month-old tumor-bearing and age-matched healthy control mice (Figure 4C). These data suggest that aging promotes an immunosuppressive environment characterized by increased MDSCs, exhausted T cells, and regulatory T cells, which may underlie the increased YUMM1.7 tumor progression observed with age. Interestingly, B cells emerged as a predominant population that increased with age and the presence of a tumor as observed by both the flow cytometry and scRNAseq data (Figures 2 and 3). B cells are known to have both tumor-protecting and promoting properties in cancer [69–73]. The observed age-related increase in B cells may reflect a compensatory shift in immune cell composition due to thymic involution and contribute to a less effective antitumor immune response in older individuals [74, 75]. It remains unclear from our data whether B cells contribute to tumor growth or their antitumor functions are being inhibited, particularly in an aged microenvironment. Given the concurrent increase in YUMM1.7 tumor growth and B cells with aging, further studies are required to understand whether this observation is correlative or reflects a causative role in age-associated tumor progression. Future work will evaluate how B cells mediate tumor growth during aging and what specific subsets of B cells are altered by age and the presence of melanoma.

Our work also evaluated how the function of Gr-1+ myeloid cells changed with age. Isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells demonstrated a significant increased immunosuppression with age, but only in the presence of a tumor (Figure 5B and Figure S8B). Our studies focused solely on the impact of Gr-1+ myeloid cells suppression on CD8+ T cell proliferation. It remains unclear whether this age- and cancer-dependent effect is also applicable to the suppression of other immune cells, such as dendritic cells and NK cells, which are also known to be inhibited by Gr-1+ myeloid cells [15, 76–78]. Additionally, Gr-1+ myeloid cells immunosuppressive function has been linked to increased production of ROS [13–15]. Our data show that ROS levels, measured by L-012, increased with aging in YUMM1.7 tumor-bearing mice (Figure 5C and Figure S8C), correlating with the observed suppression of T cells. In the YUMM1.7 melanoma model, the ROS species generated by Gr-1+ myeloid cells seem to be influenced by both age and tumor presence. Increased ROS levels [13–15] and MPO [15–17] in Gr-1+ myeloid cells have been previously reported in the context of cancer, and our findings are consistent with these observations. Variations in ROS levels between male and female isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells were observed across all ages and may stem from a combination of sex-specific immune regulation, hormonal influences, and age-related changes. Additionally, Gr-1+ myeloid cells exhibited increased formation of extracellular traps, a key immunosuppressive function, especially with aging and tumor presence (Figure 5F). However, it should be noted that while the PicoGreen assay for detecting extracellular DNA is a well-established method for identifying NETs, there is a potential concern that it could also detect artifacts from increased cell death in isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells. The supernatant was measured approximately 1 h post-isolation, a timeframe during which minimal cell death is anticipated. The cells are pelleted from the supernatant and removed from the assay, so any further cell death should not impact the PicoGreen results. The tumor environment also notably enhances extracellular trap formation by Gr-1+ myeloid cells compared to healthy controls (Figure S12E–G). Overall, these results suggest that in the presence of a tumor, aging amplifies the immunosuppressive functions of Gr-1+ myeloid cells, including ROS production and extracellular trap formation. It is important to note that the Gr-1+ myeloid cells isolation protocol isolates both polymorphonuclear (Ly6G+) and mononuclear (Ly6C+) MDSCs, neutrophils, and monocytes. Emerging evidence indicates that, beyond MDSCs, certain populations of neutrophils and monocytes can also acquire T cell suppressive phenotypes within the tumor microenvironment. In our study, the observed increase in T cell suppression by aged tumor-associated Gr-1+ myeloid cells may reflect the cumulative activity of multiple suppressive myeloid subsets. Further studies are required to determine whether there are age-dependent differences in the function of the different subsets of Gr-1+ myeloid cells.

Given that Gr-1+ myeloid cell immunosuppression is dependent upon ROS production and MPO, a major ROS [52] producer implicated in immunosuppression [16, 17, 79, 80], was elevated in YUMM1.7 tumor-bearing aged mice, we investigated how MPO influences Gr-1+ myeloid cells function and subsequent melanoma growth. Notably, MPO activity was significantly higher in 6-month-old mice compared to 2-month-old mice, prompting further evaluation of these two age groups. The significant increase in YUMM1.7 tumor volume that was observed in 6-month-old compared to 2-month-old WT mice was lost when MPO was deficient (Figure 6A). Loss of MPO did not impact YUMM1.G1 tumor growth, which lacked age-dependent differences in WT mice (Figure S17E,F). No significant differences in YUMM1.7 tumor immune composition were observed between age-matched MPO−/− and WT mice or between young and aged MPO−/− mice (Figure 6B). Loss of MPO altered CD4+ T cells, B cells, and CD11b+Ly6G+ myeloid cells within the spleen compared to WT mice. How MPO impacts B and T cell infiltration requires further studies. Using this YUMM1.7 melanoma model, a significant decrease in MPO activity measured by luminol was only observed in 6-month-old MPO−/− mice compared to WT mice, while overall ROS levels measured by L-012 were only significantly decreased in 2-month-old MPO−/− mice compared to WT mice (Figure 6D,E). A decrease in overall ROS levels when MPO was deficient was previously observed in other preclinical melanoma models, albeit at older ages [16] and in pancreatic cancer models at young ages [17]. In this YUMM1.7 melanoma model, it is possible that these MPO-deficient Gr-1+ myeloid cells have developed a compensatory method to continue to produce ROS. MPO has also been shown to be a critical step in extracellular trap formation [81], and a significant decrease in extracellular trap formation was observed but only in tumor-isolated Gr-1+ myeloid cells from 6-month-old MPO-deficient mice (Figure 6H). MPO-deficient Gr-1+ myeloid cells lost the ability to suppress T cell proliferation regardless of age (Figure 6G and Figure S16L). Overall, these findings highlight the pivotal role of MPO in modulating Gr-1+ myeloid cells functions, including immune cell composition, ROS production, and extracellular trap formation, resulting in the loss of age-dependent differences in YUMM1.7 tumor growth.

Our data suggest that age-related differences in response to ICT are also not universal, mirroring what is observed clinically. Interestingly, the age-related differences seen in untreated sub-cutaneous tumor growth were also reflected in ICT response: YUMM1.7 tumors exhibited reduced ICT responsiveness in 6-month-old compared to 2-month-old WT mice, YUMM1.G1 tumors showed no age-dependent difference in ICT response, and YUMM3.3 demonstrated enhanced ICT responsiveness in young 2-month-old WT mice. In the YUMM1.7 melanoma model, the increase in immunosuppressive T cells and MDSCs alongside an increase in Gr-1+ myeloid cells immunosuppression in aged mice likely contributes to enhanced tumor growth and decreased ICT response. Previous reports demonstrated a decrease in T regulatory cells in aged mice, contributing to the increased anti-PD-1 response using a YUMM1.7 melanoma model [5]. Our studies demonstrate an increase in T regulatory cells systemically (but not in the tumor microenvironment) and decreased anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 responses. This differential response may be due to differences in the ages studied, 6- versus 10- to 12-month-old mice, use of combination anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1, or, as discussed above, differences in the melanoma cell number used and/or expression of fluorescent reporters. Host MPO deficiency eliminated the age-dependent differences in ICT response (Figure 7A). In both young and aged mice, the loss of MPO improved ICT outcomes compared to age-matched WT mice, suggesting that MPO in Gr-1+ myeloid cells contributes to immunotherapy response in the YUMM1.7 melanoma model. The variability in the function of MPO-deficient Gr-1+ myeloid cells in the YUMM1.7 melanoma model, particularly in ROS production, may explain why MPO deficiency did not lead to long-term survival from ICT, as observed in other preclinical aged melanoma models [16]. However, in young YUMM1.7 tumor-bearing mice, MPO deficiency resulted in over 15% long-term survival, suggesting that in certain contexts, limiting MPO at a young age may enhance the effectiveness of ICT.

While our data have provided valuable insights, it is essential to acknowledge that there are several limitations in our studies. We recognize that characterizing the spleen may not be entirely representative of the systemic immune system; however, in order to compare how Gr-1+ myeloid cells were altered in the presence of cancer, the spleen was the most practical organ to isolate Gr-1+ myeloid cells from healthy, tumor-free mice. Additionally, our studies used a subcutaneous tumor model, which does not fully recapitulate the complex melanoma microenvironment during advanced-stage metastatic disease, which is primarily when ICT is used in patients. While we observed various immune infiltrates in our subcutaneous tumors, the immune cells found in subcutaneous tumors may not be the ideal representation of the immune composition of melanoma. Ongoing studies are under evaluation to understand the contribution of Gr-1+ myeloid cells in preclinical metastatic melanoma models. Another limitation of our study is that immune composition and Gr-1+ myeloid cell function were assessed only at the tumor endpoint, capturing a single timepoint in tumor development. However, immune cells are dynamic, and their accumulation and function within the tumor microenvironment may vary at earlier stages of melanoma progression. Future studies will aim to characterize Gr-1+ myeloid cells across different stages of disease development. It is also important to recognize that the MPO−/− mice are global genetic knockouts. However, since MPO expression is restricted to myeloid-lineage cells within the host, it is expected, as previously reported, that other host immune cells will function normally [82].

Overall, our findings highlight that immune responses to subcutaneous melanoma are shaped by age in a complex and context-specific manner, suggesting that age-related variations in tumor progression and immunotherapy efficacy are not consistent across all settings. The age-enhanced tumor growth and decrease in ICT response in the YUMM1.7 melanoma model are driven in part by enhanced immunosuppression mediated by Gr-1+ myeloid cells. Aging and the presence of YUMM1.7 melanoma not only enhanced Gr-1+ myeloid cell-mediated immunosuppression, ROS production, and extracellular trap formation but also altered the balance of immune cells within the tumor microenvironment, including a notable increase in B cells, exhausted T cells, and regulatory T cells. The elimination of age-dependent differences in tumor growth and ICT response by MPO deficiency underscores the role of MPO in shaping the tumor microenvironment and immunotherapy outcomes. While MPO deficiency did not uniformly improve long-term survival, its impact on ICT efficacy suggests that targeting MPO may be a viable strategy to optimize immunotherapy across different age groups, particularly in those that exhibit high MPO activity in Gr-1+ myeloid cells. Further research is needed to develop age-stratified treatment strategies that effectively leverage age-related immunological changes.
