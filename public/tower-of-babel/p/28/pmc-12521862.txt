Updated safety evaluation of the food enzyme phospholipase A2 from the genetically modified Trichoderma reesei strain RF8793
Authors: EFSA Panel on Food Enzymes (FEZ), Holger Zorn, José Manuel Barat Baviera, Claudia Bolognesi, Francesco Catania, Gabriele Gadermaier, Ralf Greiner, Baltasar Mayo, Alicja Mortensen, Yrjö Henrik Roos, Marize Solano, Henk Van Loveren, Laurence Vernis, Ana Criado, Yi Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe food enzyme phospholipase A2 (phosphatidylcholine 2‐acylhydrolase; EC 3.1.1.4) is produced with the genetically modified Trichoderma reesei strain RF8793 by AB Enzymes GmbH. In a previous opinion, the total organic solids (TOS) values could not be calculated, which led to the inability to establish the representativeness of the batch used for toxicological examination and to calculate the dietary exposure. In this assessment, EFSA completes the safety evaluation of this food enzyme by taking into account the chemical compositional data from new food enzyme batches. The food enzyme is intended to be used in three food manufacturing processes. Dietary exposure to the food enzyme–TOS was estimated to be up to 0.008 mg TOS/kg body weight (bw) per day in European populations. Genotoxicity tests did not indicate a safety concern. The systemic toxicity was assessed by means of a repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study in rats. The Panel identified a no observed adverse effect level of 1000 mg TOS/kg bw per day, the highest dose tested, which, when compared with the estimated dietary exposure, results in a margin of exposure of at least 125,000. Based on the new data and the previous evaluation, the Panel concluded that this food enzyme does not give rise to safety concerns under the intended conditions of use.Keywords: EC 3.1.1.4, EFSA‐Q‐2014‐00411, EFSA‐Q‐2023‐00574, food enzyme, genetically modified microorganism, phosphatidylcholine 2‐acylhydrolase, phospholipase A2

INTRODUCTION

Article 3 of the Regulation (EC) No 1332/2008 1 provides definition for ‘food enzyme’ and ‘food enzyme preparation’.

‘Food enzyme’ means a product obtained from plants, animals or microorganisms or products thereof, including a product obtained by a fermentation process using microorganisms: (i) containing one or more enzymes capable of catalysing a specific biochemical reaction; and (ii) added to food for a technological purpose at any stage of the manufacturing, processing, preparation, treatment, packaging, transport or storage of foods.

‘Food enzyme preparation’ means a formulation consisting of one or more food enzymes in which substances such as food additives and/or other food ingredients are incorporated to facilitate their storage, sale, standardisation, dilution or dissolution.

Before January 2009, food enzymes other than those used as food additives were not regulated or were regulated as processing aids under the legislation of the Member States. On 20 January 2009, Regulation (EC) No 1332/2008 on food enzymes came into force. This Regulation applies to enzymes that are added to food to perform a technological function in the manufacture, processing, preparation, treatment, packaging, transport or storage of such food, including enzymes used as processing aids. Regulation (EC) No 1331/2008 2 established the European Union (EU) procedures for the safety assessment and the authorisation procedure of food additives, food enzymes and food flavourings. The use of a food enzyme shall be authorised only if it is demonstrated that:

it does not pose a safety concern to the health of the consumer at the level of use proposed;

it does not pose a safety concern to the health of the consumer at the level of use proposed;

there is a reasonable technological need;

there is a reasonable technological need;

its use does not mislead the consumer.

its use does not mislead the consumer.

All food enzymes currently on the European Union market and intended to remain on that market, as well as all new food enzymes, shall be subjected to a safety evaluation by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and approval via an EU Community list.

Background and Terms of Reference as provided by the requestor

Background as provided by the European Commission

Only food enzymes included in the Union list may be placed on the market as such and used in foods, in accordance with the specifications and conditions of use provided for in Article 7(2) of Regulation (EC) No 1332/2008 on food enzymes.

An application has been introduced by the applicant ‘AB Enzymes GmbH’ for the authorisation of the food enzyme Phospholipase A2 from a genetically modified strain of Trichoderma reesei (strain RF8793).

The food enzyme has been assessed by EFSA with an inconclusive opinion on 27 October 2020. The new application contains supplementary information to address the data gaps identified in the scientific opinion (EFSA‐Q‐2014‐00411).

Following the requirements of Article 12.1 of Regulation (EC) No 234/2011 3 implementing Regulation (EC) No 1331/2008, the Commission has verified that the application falls within the scope of the food enzyme Regulation and contains all the elements required under Chapter II of that Regulation.

Terms of Reference

The Commission requests the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) to verify the requirements of Article 32b of Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, the suitability of the data for risk assessment following the requirements of Article 12(2) of Regulation (EU) No 234/2011, and to carry out the safety assessment and the assessment of possible confidentiality requests on the new data only for the following food enzyme: Phospholipase A2 from a genetically modified strain of Trichoderma reesei (strain RF8793) in accordance with Regulation (EC) No 1331/2008 establishing a common authorisation procedure for food additives, food enzymes, and food flavourings. 4

Interpretation of the Terms of Reference

The applicant submitted previously an application for phospholipase A2 from the genetically modified Trichoderma reesei strain RF8793, for which EFSA could not conclude on the safety, due to data gap (EFSA CEP Panel, 2020).

Chemical parameters

The current evaluation supersedes Section 3.3.2 of the previous evaluation (EFSA CEP Panel, 2020).

Data on the chemical parameters of the food enzyme were provided for three batches used for commercialisation 11 and two batches produced for the toxicological tests 12 (Table 1). The mean total organic solids (TOS) of the three batches for commercialisation was 20% and the mean enzyme activity/TOS ratio was 1182 PLU/mg TOS.

Purity

The lead content in the three commercial batches and in the batches used for toxicological studies was equal to or below 0.09 mg/kg 13 , 14 , 15 which complies with the specification for lead as laid down in the general specifications for enzymes used in food processing (FAO/WHO, 2006).

The food enzyme complies with the microbiological criteria for total coliforms, Escherichia coli, and Salmonella, as laid down in the general specifications for enzymes used in food processing (FAO/WHO, 2006). 16 , 17 No antimicrobial activity was detected in any of the tested batches. 18 , 19

Strains of Trichoderma species, in common with most filamentous fungi, have the capacity to produce a range of secondary metabolites (Frisvad et al. 2018). The presence of mycotoxins (HT‐2 and T2 toxin) was examined in all food enzyme batches and was below the limits of quantification (LoQ) of the applied analytical methods. 20 , 21 , 22 Adverse effects caused by the possible presence of other secondary metabolites are addressed by the toxicological examination of the food enzyme.

The absence of viable cells of the production strain was demonstrated ■■■■■ no colonies were produced. A positive control was included. 23 These results confirmed the previous evaluation (EFSA CEP Panel, 2020).

Toxicological data

The representativeness of the test item could not be established in the previous evaluation; consequently, the Panel evaluated the three toxicological tests provided only partially: a bacterial gene mutation assay (Ames test), an in vitro mammalian chromosomal aberration test and a repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study in rats (EFSA CEP Panel, 2020).

The batch used in those toxicological tests was the food enzyme phospholipase A2 produced with the T. reesei strain RF8793 (batch 4 in Table 1). In the context of the current application, EFSA requested an in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test. The batch used in this toxicological test was batch 5. As batches 4 and 5 have a similar activity/TOS ratio as the three commercial batches (Table 1), the Panel considered them representative of the food enzyme batches intended for commercialisation, therefore, suitable test items.

Following the establishment of the suitability of the test item, the Panel re‐evaluated the repeated 90‐day oral toxicity study, in particular the relationship between the test‐item and the mortality observed.

Genotoxicity

Bacterial reverse mutation test

In the previous evaluation, the Panel concluded that the food enzyme did not induce gene mutations under the test conditions employed in this study (EFSA CEP Panel, 2020).

In vitro mammalian chromosomal aberration test

In the previous evaluation, the Panel concluded that the food enzyme did not induce chromosomal aberrations under the test conditions employed in this study (EFSA CEP Panel, 2020).

In vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test

The in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test was carried out according to OECD Test Guideline 487 (OECD, 2016) and following good laboratory practice (GLP). 24 A dose‐range finding test and two main experiments were performed with duplicate cultures of human peripheral whole blood lymphocytes. The cell cultures were treated with the food enzyme with or without metabolic activation (S9‐mix).

Based on the results from the range‐finding test, cells were exposed in the first experiment to the food enzyme and scored for the frequency of binucleated cells with micronuclei (MNBN) at concentrations of 1000, 2500 and 5000 μg TOS/mL in a short‐term treatment (4‐h exposure and 40‐h recovery period) either with or without S9‐mix.

In the second experiment, cells were exposed to the food enzyme and scored for MNBN at concentrations of 500, 1000 and 1750 μg TOS/mL in a long‐term treatment (44‐h exposure and without recovery period) without S9‐mix.

No cytotoxicity was seen in the short‐term treatment with and/or without S9‐mix. In the long‐term treatment, cytotoxicity of 57% (based on cytokinesis block proliferation index) was observed at a concentration of 1750 μg TOS/mL. The frequency of MNBN was not statistically significantly different from the negative controls at all concentrations tested.

The study was considered reliable without restrictions, and the results of high relevance.

The Panel concluded that the food enzyme phospholipase A2 did not induce an increase in the frequency of MNBNs under the test conditions applied in this study.

Conclusions on genotoxicity

Based on the negative results obtained with the Ames test, with the in vitro chromosomal aberration test and with the in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test in human peripheral lymphocytes, the Panel concluded that there is no concern of genotoxicity for the food enzyme phospholipase A2.

Repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study in rodents

The current evaluation supersedes Section 3.4.2 of the previous evaluation (EFSA CEP Panel, 2020).

The repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study was performed in accordance with OECD Test Guideline 408 (OECD, 1998) and following GLP. 25

Groups of 10 male and 10 female RccHan™:WIST rats received the food enzyme by gavage at dose levels of 100, 300 and 1000 mg TOS/kg body weight (bw) per day. Controls received the vehicle (bi‐distilled water).

Three mid‐dose males and two high‐dose males were found dead between days 29 and 69 of administration, without precedent clinical signs. At microscopic examination, congestion, alveolar and perivascular oedema and haemorrhage were observed in the lungs, variably accounting for the macroscopic observations of incompletely/not collapsed and/or dark red discoloured lungs with or without reddish foci. These changes were compatible with a gavage accident of the test item into the lungs. Despite the apparent dose relationship, the Panel considered the premature deaths as not related to the test item administration, based on (i) pulmonary macro and microscopic findings only in decedent rats, (ii) absence of any toxicologically relevant finding in these rats or any other rat in the study and (iii) no preceding respiratory clinical signs.

The feed consumption was statistically significantly decreased between days 1–15, 22–29, 36–43 and 43–50 of administration in mid‐dose males (−2% to –16%). The Panel considered the change as not toxicologically relevant, as it was only recorded sporadically, was only observed in one sex, there was no dose–response relationship and there was no statistically significant change in the final feed consumption, the body weight and the body weight gain.

In the functional observations, a statistically significant decrease in the hind limb grip strength was observed in mid‐dose females (−9%) and in total locomotor activity in mid‐dose males (−8%); a statistically significant increase in locomotor activity was observed in low‐dose females in the intervals 0–10 and 10–20 min (+43% and +53%). The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant, as they were only recorded sporadically (locomotor activity in females), were only observed in one sex (all), and there was no dose–response relationship (all).

Haematological investigations revealed a statistically significant decrease in haemoglobin distribution width (HDW) and low fluorescence reticulocytes (L‐Ret) and an increase in high fluorescence reticulocytes (H‐Ret) in high‐dose males (−11%, −0.3%, +0.2%), an increase in reticulocytes (Ret) in high‐dose females (+38%) and in prothrombin time (PT) in mid‐dose females (+7%); an increase in white blood cell count (WBC), differential basophil (Baso) and large unstained cell counts (LUC) in high‐dose males (+16%, +150%, +60%) and a decrease in absolute lymphocyte count (Lymph) in mid‐dose females (−29%). The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant, as they were only observed in one sex (all), there was no dose–response relationship (PT, Lymph), the changes were small (HDW, L‐Ret, H‐Ret, WBC, Bas, Baso, LUC), there were no changes in other relevant parameters (red blood cell parameters for HDW and Ret, other white blood cell parameters for WBC, Baso and LUC in males and for lymph in females), there were no histopathological changes in lymphohaematopoietic organs (HDW, Ret, WBC, Baso, LUC and lymph) and the changes were within the historical control values (all except L‐Ret).

Clinical chemistry investigations revealed a statistically significant decrease in lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) in mid‐ and high‐dose females (−42%, −49%) and in creatine kinase (CK) in all treated females (−37%, −40%, −49%), and an increase in globulin in high‐dose females (+11%), an increase in sodium (Na) and chloride (Cl) concentrations in mid‐dose males (+2%, +3%) and in calcium (Ca) and phosphorus (P) concentrations in high‐dose females (+3%, +15%). The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant as they were only observed in one sex (all parameters), there was no dose–response relationship (Na, Cl), the changes were small (LDH, CK, globulin), there were no changes in other relevant parameters (other liver enzymes, albumin, other electrolytes), there were no histopathological changes in liver, muscle or kidney, and all changes were within the historical control values.

Statistically significant changes detected in organ weights were an increase in absolute brain weight in low‐dose males (+5%) and an increase in relative adrenals to body weight ratio in mid‐dose females (+14%). The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant as they were only observed in one sex (both), there was no dose–response relationship (both), the change was small (brain weight) and there were no histopathological changes in brain or adrenals.

No other statistically significant or biologically relevant differences from controls were reported.

Based on the re‐evaluation of the data, the Panel identified a no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) of 1000 mg TOS/kg bw per day, the highest dose tested.

The intended uses reported in the current evaluation are identical to those in the previous evaluation, except that they have been reclassified according to the current guidance document (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023).

The Panel noted changes in the use levels when compared to the previous levels. The applicant ascribes these changes to the revised calculation of TOS in the enzyme batches. 27

In the processing of egg and egg products, the food enzyme is added to raw egg yolk prior to pasteurisation. 28 The hydrolysis of phospholipids catalysed by the phospholipase A2 leads to improved emulsifying properties. 29 The food enzyme–TOS remain in the modified egg yolk.

In the production of refined edible fats and oils by degumming, the food enzyme is added to crude oil prior to degumming. 30 This phospholipase A2 is used to hydrolyse phospholipids naturally present in crude oils. This process results in higher oil yields. 31 The food enzyme–TOS are removed by repeated washing steps applied after degumming (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023).

In the production of modified lecithins, the food enzyme is added to egg lecithin 32 or to plant lecithin 33 prior to pasteurisation to improve the emulsifying properties. 34 The food enzyme‐TOS remain in the modified lecithin.

The food enzyme has a temperature optimum around 50°C (pH 3.4) and a pH optimum around pH 3–5 (40°C). After pre‐incubation of the food enzyme at 80°C and 85°C for different periods, the phospholipase A2 activity decreased by 90% after 10 min at 80°C, showing no activity after 30 min. At 85°C, activity decreased by 100% after 10 min (EFSA CEP Panel, 2020). Based on these data and the downstream processing steps applied in the respective food manufacturing processes, the Panel concluded that the food enzyme may remain in its active form in the food manufacturing processes in which the food enzyme‐TOS remain.

Dietary exposure estimation

In accordance with the guidance document (EFSA CEP Panel, 2021), dietary exposure was calculated for the two food manufacturing processes where the food enzyme‐TOS remain in the final foods.

Chronic exposure to the food enzyme‐TOS was calculated using the FEIM webtool 35 by combining the maximum recommended use level with individual consumption data (EFSA CEP Panel, 2021). The estimation involved selection of relevant food categories and application of technical conversion factors (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023) together with the information provided in Appendix C.

Enzymatically hydrolysed lecithin is a type of lecithin (E322) that is an authorised food additive in the EU according to Annex II and Annex III of Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008 on food additives. 36 The EFSA Panel on Food Additives and Nutrient Sources added to Food (ANS Panel) re‐evaluated lecithins used as food additives (EFSA ANS Panel, 2017). To enable a single calculation of the food enzyme‐TOS from all dietary sources, in this assessment, the FoodEx categories considered by the ANS Panel were disaggregated further to the more detailed hierarchy level 7 to account for the dietary exposure to the food enzyme‐TOS via modified lecithins. Technical factors were also adjusted to account for the possible presence of enzymatically modified lecithins in foods (see Appendix C).

Table 3 provides an overview of the derived exposure estimates across all surveys. Detailed mean and 95th percentile exposure to the food enzyme‐TOS per age class, country and survey, as well as contribution from each FoodEx category to the total dietary exposure are reported in Appendix A – Tables 1 and 2. For the present assessment, food consumption data were available from 48 dietary surveys (covering infants, toddlers, children, adolescents, adults and the elderly), carried out in 26 European countries (Appendix B). The highest dietary exposure was estimated to be 0.008 mg TOS/kg bw per day in toddlers at the 95th percentile.

The conservative approach applied to estimate the dietary exposure to the food enzyme‐TOS, in particular assumptions made on the occurrence and use levels of this specific food enzyme, is likely to have led to an overestimation of the exposure.

The exclusion of one food manufacturing process from the exposure estimation was based on > 99% of TOS removal. This is not expected to impact on the overall estimate derived.

Margin of exposure

A comparison of the NOAEL (1000 mg TOS/kg bw per day) identified from the 90‐day rat study with the derived exposure estimates of 0–0.003 mg TOS/kg bw per day at the mean and from 0.001 to 0.008 mg TOS/kg bw per day at the 95th percentile resulted in a margin of exposure of at least 125,000.

CONCLUSIONS

Based on the new data provided and the previous evaluation, the Panel concludes that the food enzyme phospholipase A2 produced with the genetically modified Trichoderma reesei strain RF8793 does not give rise to safety concerns under the intended conditions of use.
