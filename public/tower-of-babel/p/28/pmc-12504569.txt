The influence of school environment and demographics on teacher wellbeing
Authors: Joy C Nwoko, Oyelola A Adegboye, Aduli E O Malau-Aduli, Emma Anderson, Bunmi S Malau-Aduli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong-term exposure to occupational stress can significantly impact teachers’ wellbeing. This study evaluated both teacher and school-related factors influencing the core dimensions of teacher occupational wellbeing using an adapted Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development survey tool, validated in an Australian context. A total of 247 Australian primary school teachers participated. A multivariate analysis examined wellbeing across four dimensions: Cognitive, Subjective, Physical-Mental, and Social. Inter-correlation analysis showed significant relationships between these dimensions, and a confirmatory factor analysis model demonstrated an acceptable fit to the data, supported by significant chi-square test results. Findings from the exploratory factor analysis identified a 4-factor solution, revealing the underlying structure of the measurement items. The reliability of the survey tool was confirmed and the core dimensions of primary school teachers’ occupational wellbeing were assessed. Male teachers reported fewer psychosomatic symptoms than female teachers, while older teachers had higher self-efficacy and reported lower psychosomatic symptoms. Most teachers indicated good self-efficacy and moderate job satisfaction. Social support from school principals significantly contributed to positive occupational wellbeing. Introduction

The teaching profession is widely acknowledged for its invaluable contributions to society and its profound impact on the future of individuals and communities. However, despite its significance, teachers often encounter a multitude of stressors that can significantly affect their wellbeing and job satisfaction1,2. There are growing concerns over teacher occupational wellbeing that are mainly driven by the increasingly high rates of attrition and work-related stress among teachers worldwide3,4. Furthermore, there is mounting global apprehension about the declining appeal of teaching as a profession5–7. Educators are constantly exposed to highly demanding and challenging aspects of teaching, resulting in stress and high attrition rates 4. This, in turn, contributes to occupational burnout and diminishes teacher performance, ultimately impacting student learning outcomes8–10. Long-term exposure to occupational stress can have detrimental effects on both teachers and their institutions5, making it imperative to address these stressors with utmost urgency. Two critical elements central to understanding teacher wellbeing are teacher characteristics and school conditions11.

Teacher characteristics encompass various individual attributes such as age, gender, experience, and educational attainment, all of which can influence their wellbeing in the profession11,12. Younger teachers may face challenges related to job insecurity and work-life balance, while experienced teachers may struggle with maintaining enthusiasm and adapting to modern teaching methods13. Gender differences are also apparent in stress perception, emotional exhaustion, and depersonalisation among teachers, with female teachers experiencing higher levels of occupational stress compared to their male counterparts14–16, probably due to higher levels of overall workload17. Years of work experience also play a vital role in teacher wellbeing, as more experienced teachers tend to have better-developed professional wellbeing than their less experienced counterparts18. Early career teachers often face heightened stress levels and attrition rates1,19,20. The first five years of service are critical for early career teachers, who may lack the fully developed ability to appraise their workplace, unlike their more experienced colleagues13,21. Research findings on the influence of age on teacher wellbeing suggest that younger teachers are more likely to leave the profession compared to middle-aged teachers22. Professional development and qualifications also influence teacher wellbeing. Teachers with more exposure to professional development have shown higher job satisfaction, particularly for female teachers with high self-efficacy 23. Highly qualified female teachers are less likely to leave the teaching profession, whereas male teachers’ attrition is not influenced by qualification24.

In the context of school conditions and characteristics, the school environment significantly impacts teacher experiences and wellbeing. Positive school climates, supportive leadership, and collaborative teacher relationships enhance job satisfaction and overall wellbeing25. Conversely, negative school environments marked by inadequate resources, excessive workload, and limited professional growth opportunities, can lead to burnout and decreased job satisfaction26. Schools that offer administrative support, mentoring programs for early-career teachers, and opportunities for networking and cooperation tend to have lower attrition rates25. Additionally, class size and composition can impact teachers’ job satisfaction11. Some studies have shown that public schools with high enrolments of economically disadvantaged students tend to have higher teacher turnover, while others have contested this claim27,28.

Addressing the factors influencing teacher wellbeing is crucial for minimising stress, burnout, and attrition among educators. Understanding how teachers’ demographic factors interact with the school environment is vital for developing targeted interventions to improve teacher wellbeing across different demographics. Additionally, as the school environment is multifaceted, analysing its individual components and their collective impact on teacher wellbeing requires a holistic approach11. Furthermore, evidence from past research suggests that primary school teachers seem to experience greater levels of stress and burnout compared to secondary school teachers as a result of the hours spent in meeting classroom demands which is higher compared to other teacher types1,29,30. A holistic approach to gaining a deeper understanding of how the school environment and demographic factors synergistically impact primary school teacher wellbeing, does not only shed more light, but also aids researchers to uncover nuanced insights and formulate targeted interventions designed to support the mental, emotional, and professional welfare of this group of teachers. This is the key objective of the current study within an Australian context.

The Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) recently developed a comprehensive framework focusing on four core (cognitive, subjective, physical-mental, and social) dimensions of teachers’ occupational wellbeing11. Understanding the intricate interactions between teacher characteristics and school conditions in relation to these core wellbeing dimensions is vital for developing targeted interventions that support the wellbeing of teachers across different demographics. Addressing the complex interplay of teacher characteristics and school conditions is crucial for enhancing teacher wellbeing, and by extension, improving student learning outcomes and the overall quality of education. Research in this area would not only benefit teachers but could also contribute to the sustainable growth and success of educational institutions worldwide31. By creating a positive and supportive work environment for teachers, schools can foster higher job satisfaction, greater teacher retention, and ultimately, improved student outcomes. Therefore, this study aims to (1) validate the survey tool for teacher occupational wellbeing used in this study and (2) evaluate the school and teacher conditions/characteristics that influence and shape the core dimensions of mainstream primary school teachers’ occupational wellbeing.

Methods

The conceptual framework

The OECD teacher occupational wellbeing framework developed by Viac and Fraser11 based on the 2018 Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) and 2021 Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) was utilised as the theoretical model for this study. In this framework, teachers’ occupational wellbeing was defined around four key dimensions, including cognitive, subjective, physical-mental, and social wellbeing. As shown in Fig. 1, these dimensions are influenced by school and teacher characteristics. Each of these dimensions has several indicators that aid the measurement of teachers’ occupational wellbeing, and relevant indicators from the OECD questionnaire were adapted in developing the survey instrument for this study. The OECD teacher occupational wellbeing framework is a good fit for this study because it considers how teacher characteristics and working conditions within the school environment can shape and impact teachers’ wellbeing. Permission was sought and obtained from the authors before using the framework and questionnaire. The domains and characteristics depicted in Fig. 1 are described below.

Theoretical model for Teacher Occupational wellbeing 11.

Cognitive Wellbeing: Teachers’ cognitive wellbeing refers to the skills and abilities that a teacher needs to work effectively32. It relates to self-efficacy and the teachers’ ability to solve problems, make decisions and coordinate processes like formation of knowledge, attention, judgement and evaluation33.

Subjective Wellbeing: The subjective dimension of teachers’ wellbeing relates to their job satisfaction. It refers to teachers’ emotions and perceptions about how satisfied or fulfilled they are with their job7. Job satisfaction is a sense of fulfilment that teachers derive from working34, and it is positively related to teacher wellbeing and commitment to teaching, self-efficacy and motivation7,35.

Physical-Mental Wellbeing: This dimension of teachers’ wellbeing relates to teachers’ health11. The stress that teachers experience in their job may result in psychosomatic symptoms36. It has been argued that long working hours can lead to psychosomatic symptoms which can affect the teacher’s physical-mental wellbeing32.

Social Wellbeing: The social dimension of teachers’ wellbeing in this context relates to the quality of social interactions that transpires between the teacher and all stakeholders11.

Teacher Characteristics: The teacher’s individual attributes that may influence their wellbeing as considered in this study, include their level of experience, highest qualification, age, and gender11.

School Characteristics: The variables within the school environment that can influence teacher wellbeing include school type (mainstream public or private), perceived organisational support, class size, current class taught and the dynamics of the classroom11.

Study design

A quantitative cross-sectional research design was utilised due to its suitability for the study’s aim of examining relationships between dimensions of wellbeing and primary school teachers’ occupational wellbeing, as it involves collecting and analysing numerical data to identify associations between variables37. The application of results from quantitative data can also be generalised to wider populations38. Teachers’ responses to the survey were used to capture their perceptions of the school and teacher characteristics that influence the core dimensions of occupational wellbeing. The collated data aided the process of validating the survey tool within the Australian context.

Participants

The participants for this study were Australian mainstream primary school teachers employed on full-time, part-time or fixed-term contract basis. A national approach was used in recruiting participants for the study. The survey was administered through Qualtrics, an online survey platform39, and data was collected between May and July 2022. The study was also advertised on online teacher networks including Facebook, WhatsApp, X (Twitter), and other social media platforms. Incentives were offered to participants in the form of a $10 coffee voucher, to increase the response rate.

The survey instrument

This research study adapted a questionnaire from the OECD teacher wellbeing framework developed by Viac and Fraser11. The adapted questionnaire had 46 questions that focused on the four core dimensions of teacher occupational wellbeing. There were 13 additional questions on demographic variables such as age, experience, and gender as shown in Table S1 (Supplementary Tables).

Cognitive dimension: teacher self-efficacy

Twelve questions measured teacher self-efficacy in relation to three distinct competencies that teachers require while teaching in the classroom: efficacy in classroom management (n = 4), efficacy in instruction (n = 4), and efficacy in student engagement (n = 4). Teachers rated all items on a four-point scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 4 (a lot).

Subjective dimension: job satisfaction

Job satisfaction was assessed using nine questions in relation to how fulfilled or satisfied teachers were with their jobs. The questions measured two aspects of teachers’ job satisfaction: Job satisfaction with the profession (n = 5), and the current working environment (n = 4). Teachers rated all items on a four-point scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree).

Physical-mental dimension: psychosomatic symptoms

Physical and Mental wellbeing were combined together in this study in line with the OECD framework to assess teachers’ psychosomatic symptoms. The psychosomatic symptoms were assessed using ten questions that measured ten (n = 10) health related complaints that can affect teachers’ occupational wellbeing. Teachers rated all items on a five-point scale ranging from 1 (Never or almost never) to 5 (Every day or almost every day).

Social dimension: social support

Fifteen questions measured social relationship/support in three distinct social interactions that transpire between the teacher and other stakeholders in the school environment: social support from colleagues (n = 5), social support from principal (n = 5) and teacher-student relations in terms of support to students (n = 5). Teachers rated all items on a four-point scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree).

Statistical analyses

Data analyses was performed using R software version 4.3.140, following a systematic approach. The suitability of the dataset for factor analysis was assessed by evaluating the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy prior to conducting Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity. An exploratory data analysis (EDA) was carried out to further interrogate the data and summarise the main characteristics, including inter-correlations, internal consistencies and reliabilities of the four core dimensions of occupational wellbeing. This was followed by an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to examine the interrelationships among the variables and provide insight into the underlying structure of the measurement items41. Subsequently, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to test the theories about the latent variables of the four core dimensions. The CFA model demonstrated an acceptable fit to the observed data with various statistical indices such as non-significant chi-square test results, high Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) values (> 0.90), low Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) (< 0.06) and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) values (< 0.08). Finally, a multivariate regression analysis was applied to investigate the participants’ (primary school teachers) wellbeing across the four main dimensions with regard to personal and school characteristics.

Exploratory factor analysis: four-factor pattern

The KMO measure of sampling adequacy of the data set shows an overall value of 0.87, indicating excellent factorability (Fig. 2). This suggests that the sum of partial correlations among variables is relatively low, and factor analysis is likely to yield reliable results, reinforcing the robustness of our factor analysis. However, individual Measure of Sampling Adequacy (MSA) values revealed variability, with some items like “Cog1: Get students to follow classroom rules” having a low MSA of 0.41, indicating poor suitability for factor analysis. Hence it was removed. Most other items scored well above 0.6, which is adequate, and several were exceptionally high, nearing or exceeding 0.9, indicating excellent suitability for factor analysis. Similarly, Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity results suggested that the variables are interrelated and suitable for factor analysis (chi-square = 5567.26, df = 1035, p < 0.001).

KMO measure of sampling adequacy for each item across the four core dimensions. The red dotted line represents the overall value of 0.87.

Factor loading from exploratory factor analysis

Figure 3 and Table S3 (Supplementary Tables) present the factor loadings obtained from the EFA for the four core dimensions. Factor loadings represent the strength and direction of the relationships between each item and its respective dimension. Items with factor loadings of at least 0.5 were considered well-loaded into their specific dimension, indicating a substantial association with that particular construct. Within the Physical-Mental dimension, items Phy1, Phy2, Phy3, Phy4, Phy5, Phy6, Phy7, Phy9 and Phy10 demonstrated factor loadings above 0.5, signifying their strong connections with Physical-Mental constructs. These items were considered well-loaded into the Physical-Mental dimension, implying their better representation of the Physical-Mental aspects under investigation. In the Social dimension, items Soc6, Soc7, Soc8, and Soc9 exhibited factor loadings exceeding 0.5, indicating their substantial associations with social-related constructs. These items were well loaded into the Social dimension, suggesting their relevance in capturing the social aspects. In the Cognitive dimension, items cog8, cog9, cog10, cog11, and cog12 had factor loadings greater than 0.5, indicating their strong alignment with cognitive aspects. These items exhibited significant relationships with the Cognitive dimension, suggesting that they represented cognitive-related constructs in the study. Lastly, in the Subjective dimension, items Soc5, Soc11, Soc13, Soc14, and Soc15 had factor loadings of at least 0.5, indicating their substantial associations with subjective experiences. These items are well loaded into the Subjective dimension, suggesting their relevance in capturing subjective aspects within the study.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

Figure 4 and Table S4 (Supplementary Tables) present the results of the CFA that explored the latent variables of Cognitive, Subjective, Physical-Mental, and Social dimensions, along with their corresponding measurement items. The Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) were found to be 0.929 and 0.920, respectively. Both indices were close to 1, indicating an acceptable fit between the CFA model and the data. Also, the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) was 0.061, while the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) was 0.061, which is within an acceptable range, indicating a reasonable fit between the CFA model and the observed data.

Standardised parameter estimates from CFA for the four components models.

For the Cognitive latent variable, five items (Cog8, Cog9, Cog10, Cog11, Cog12) showed significant positive factor loadings, ranging from 0.41 to 0.865. These factor loadings indicate the strength of association between the latent variable (Cognitive) and each respective item. The Subjective latent variable included five items (Soc5, Soc11, Soc13, Soc4, Soc15), all demonstrating significant positive factor loadings, that ranged from 0.5 to 0.859. Regarding the Physical-Mental latent variable, all nine items (Phy1, Phy2, Phy3, Phy4, Phy5, Phy6, Phy7, Phy9, Phy10) exhibited significant positive factor loadings, ranging from 0.598 to 1.177. For the Social latent variable, all five items (Soc6, Soc7, Soc8, Soc9, Soc10) showed significant positive factor loadings, ranging from 0.71 to 0.849. The four latent variables demonstrated high reliability, with standard factor loadings (Std.lv) and standard factor loadings (Std.all) exceeding 0.5 for all items.

Association between school and teacher characteristics and occupational wellbeing

In Table 5, the results of the relationship between each of the four core dimensions of teachers’ wellbeing and various personal and professional characteristics are presented. Among the personal characteristics, being male negatively affected the four core dimensions of wellbeing. Specifically, the effect of being male was significantly associated with a slight decrease in Physical-Mental wellbeing (−0.50, p = 0.005). Compared to teachers aged 18–29 years, older teachers showed a significant positive effect on Cognitive wellbeing, suggesting that teachers in this age group reported higher Cognitive wellbeing than their younger counterparts. On the other hand, the older the teachers, the lower their Physical-Mental wellbeing. Age was not significantly associated with Subjective and Social wellbeing. School type, Class size and Experience in teaching were not significantly associated with any of the four dimensions of wellbeing.

Discussion

This study aimed to validate the adapted OECD teacher wellbeing survey tool and evaluate the school and teacher conditions/characteristics that influence and shape the core dimensions of mainstream primary school teachers’ occupational wellbeing. Teacher wellbeing is pivotal for a conducive learning environment and has continued to receive much attention4. The study provides validated evidence for the adapted teacher wellbeing tool based on its internal structure. The factor loadings for each item underscored the robustness of the measurement instrument, with items aligning well with their respective dimensions. The internal consistency assessments using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients demonstrated good reliability for all wellbeing dimensions—cognitive, subjective, physical-mental and social dimensions. The positive correlations between the cognitive dimension and the subjective and social dimensions suggest that teachers with higher cognitive wellbeing also tended to experience higher levels of subjective and social wellbeing. Conversely, the negative correlation between the cognitive and physical-mental dimensions implies a potential trade-off between cognitive wellbeing and physical-mental wellbeing. The identified factors (physical-mental, social, cognitive, and subjective) align with theoretical frameworks in wellbeing research. Previous research indicates that individual characteristics and contextual factors are closely interwoven in terms of teacher wellbeing, especially because both background and organisational factors are related to wellbeing parameters like burnout42. Further analysis of the newly formed four-factor dimensions revealed interesting patterns of correlations. The strong positive correlations between the new cognitive dimension and the new subjective and social dimensions highlight the interconnectedness of cognitive wellbeing with subjective experiences and social interactions. The negative correlation between the new cognitive dimension and the new physical-mental dimension suggests a potential tension between cognitive demands and the physical and mental aspects of wellbeing. The inter-correlations among the wellbeing dimensions provide insights into the complex relationships between cognitive, subjective, physical-mental, and social aspects of teachers’ wellbeing6,11.

The results indicate that Australian primary school teachers generally exhibit good self-efficacy, positively linked to social support from colleagues and principals, leading to job satisfaction and lower psychosomatic symptoms. This study found that teachers demonstrated high self-efficacy in instruction, student engagement, and classroom management. Moreover, social support networks further enhanced teacher wellbeing. The teachers also derived job satisfaction from social support from colleagues and principals. Previous studies have reported that high teacher self-efficacy is associated with improved job satisfaction and performance, lower stress levels, and greater persistence43. Teachers experiencing psychosomatic symptoms reported low or lack of social support from colleagues and principals. The findings align with previous studies highlighting the psychosomatic challenges faced by teachers, emphasising the correlation with burnout syndrome, a leading cause of premature retirement44. Previous studies argued that teachers suffered more psychosomatic disorders than other occupational groups45. The study also underscores the importance of social support, especially from school leadership and colleagues, as a significant factor in teacher wellbeing. However, non-classroom teachers showed a significant negative decrease in social wellbeing compared to teachers from Prep classes. Positive social support correlates with higher self-efficacy and job satisfaction, while low social support is associated with poor mental health in teachers46–48. Additionally, the findings of this study corroborate the international data from OECD regarding female gender dominating the profession, limited resources, need for professional training, classroom dynamics and challenges, positive impact of teacher collaboration and the need for principal’s support to enhance teacher wellbeing.

In terms of practical implications, the study findings indicated that gender and age significantly influenced teachers’ wellbeing across the four core dimensions. Previous studies had opined that demographic variables such as gender, may have an impact on the levels of occupational stress experienced by teachers12,49,50. It is no surprise then that gender has often been investigated as a biographical variable in studies on occupational stress51. In this study, most of the participants (80%) were females, reflecting the broader gender distribution within the teaching profession. Male participants had lower self-efficacy compared to female participants but experienced lower psychosomatic symptoms when compared to female teachers. This finding is consistent with the results of a previous study where female teachers displayed higher levels of self-efficacy when compared to male teachers52. Self-efficacy has also been shown to influence teaching behaviour, students’ achievement, and motivation53,54. The observed differences in wellbeing dimensions between male and female teachers emphasise the importance of considering gender-specific factors in designing wellbeing interventions and support systems. This reinforces previous findings that teacher self-efficacy is linked with job satisfaction55. Additionally, teachers who had children with behavioural, social, emotional, and specific learning needs in their classroom reported low self-efficacy. This finding might be due to the peculiarity of the population that participated in this study. Notably, a substantial portion of teachers reported having less than one year experience, indicating the presence of mostly early-career educators in the sampled population. This demographic characteristic could have implications for understanding wellbeing dynamics, considering the potential challenges faced by novice teachers as they navigate the demands of the profession1,19. A study by Klassen and Chiu55 suggested that teacher self-efficacy levels are low for inexperienced teachers, mainly because new classroom teachers have not fully developed their ability to sufficiently appraise their work environment21. Some scholars have argued that teachers’ sense of competence and wellbeing may be directly and indirectly associated with the classroom dynamics56. Therefore, working conditions are very important in schools, not only for teacher effectiveness, motivation, and job satisfaction, but also for student opportunities to learn26.

Although the findings of this study suggest that teachers without exposure to professional training also had job satisfaction. The high percentage of teachers (88%) who agreed to have received professional development training is indicative of a proactive approach towards continuous learning and skill enhancement within the teaching community. This commitment to professional development may influence wellbeing outcomes and is a noteworthy aspect to consider in the broader context of teacher support and career growth. Teachers with more exposure to professional development have demonstrated higher job satisfaction, particularly for female teachers with high self-efficacy23. The non-significant associations between school type, class size and teaching experience with wellbeing dimensions suggest that these factors may not play a significant role in shaping teachers’ wellbeing in this particular context. However, these findings challenge assumptions about the direct impact of school type class size and experience on wellbeing and invite further exploration of the factors influencing teacher occupational wellbeing. Overall, the nuanced examination of personal and professional characteristics revealed valuable insights. The negative effect of being male on cognitive wellbeing, along with the slight decrease in physical-mental wellbeing, suggests potential areas for targeted interventions to address gender-specific wellbeing challenges. The positive association between older age and cognitive wellbeing aligns with existing research findings highlighting the potential benefits of experience and maturity in coping with cognitive demands. However, the inverse relationship between age and physical-mental wellbeing warrants attention, signaling a potential need for targeted support for older teachers in managing the physical-mental aspects of their occupational wellbeing. Furthermore, fostering teacher occupational wellbeing requires a multi-level approach, including gender- and experience-sensitive support, professional learning focused on inclusive practice, and systemic improvements to school environments. This study highlights the importance of gender-responsive wellbeing initiatives that account for differing experiences and coping mechanisms among teachers. It is also essential for school administrators to implement structured induction programs and mentoring opportunities that explicitly support the development of self-efficacy and resilience in early-career teachers. Additionally, given the increasing diversity of contemporary classrooms, this underscores the need for ongoing professional development and resource allocation to better equip teachers for inclusive education. Ensuring access to targeted training in classroom management and student support strategies can foster teacher confidence and wellbeing. Moreover, the role of working conditions—including class size, workload, and access to resources—was reaffirmed as a critical determinant of teacher effectiveness, motivation, and job satisfaction. Policymakers should therefore prioritise policies that improve structural working conditions to not only enhance teacher wellbeing but also positively impact student learning outcomes.

Ultimately, this study underscores the need for multi-level, evidence-informed interventions that reflect the lived experiences of educators and supports the case for revisiting both the design of global teacher surveys and the formulation of national education strategies. The findings from this study offer valuable insights that could inform both international data collection efforts, such as the OECD’s TALIS survey, and national or state-based education policies in Australia. Given the observed gender and career-stage differences in teacher wellbeing, future iterations of the TALIS survey could benefit from including more nuanced items that capture gender-specific experiences and the distinct challenges faced by early-career teachers. At the state policy level, the findings reinforce the importance of differentiated support. Policymakers should consider developing gender-responsive wellbeing programs and ensure structured induction pathways for early-career teachers, including mentoring and workload management. Furthermore, investment in inclusive education training and classroom support is critical, particularly for teachers working in high-demand environments. Improving these structural conditions is essential not only for sustaining teacher wellbeing but also for enhancing student learning outcomes and reducing workforce attrition.

Finally, building on the comprehensive findings discussed in this paper, several potential avenues for future research emerge. A better understanding of the nuanced experiences and stressors associated with gender can inform tailored support mechanisms and professional development opportunities. Investigation of the role of school leadership in fostering a positive wellbeing culture is also warranted. Such research could provide insights into the systemic factors contributing to a healthy teaching environment.

In summary, this study recommends the following: collaboration and support from school leadership, in-service professional training opportunities, particularly for early career teachers, adequate support for inclusive education, resource availability, reassessing workload policies, and tailored training programs to improve teachers’ coping mechanisms. By implementing these targeted strategies, educational stakeholders and policy makers can strengthen teacher retention, enhance teacher occupational well-being, and the overall quality of education

Limitations

While the study provides valuable insights into teacher wellbeing, there are certain limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. The study focuses specifically on Australian mainstream primary school teachers, and therefore, the findings may not be easily generalised to non-mainstream teachers and other educators in different educational levels or cultural contexts. The study sample included more female participants than males which may further limit the study’s broader applicability. Additionally, the reliance on self-report measures introduces the possibility of response bias. Participants may have provided socially desirable responses. Furthermore, the study has adopted a cross-sectional research design, capturing data at a single point in time. This design restricts the ability to fully establish causal relationships between variables. In-depth qualitative studies and/or longitudinal studies would offer a more comprehensive understanding of the dynamic nature of teacher wellbeing over time. Finally, this study identified aspects contributing to cognitive, subjective, physical-mental and social dimensions of teacher wellbeing. However, it does not extensively explore other potential sources within each dimension.

Conclusion

The present study validated an adapted teacher occupational wellbeing tool and provided a robust understanding of the demographic characteristics, inter-correlations, factor structure, and associations with personal and professional factors in the context of Australian primary school teachers’ wellbeing. The findings contribute valuable insights for educational policymakers, school administrators, and teacher support programs aiming to enhance the overall wellbeing of primary school teachers. Additionally, the nuanced exploration of gender-specific and age-related factors opens avenues for targeted interventions to address the diverse occupational wellbeing needs within the teaching profession.
