Long QT syndrome and hypoglycemia in a postbariatric surgery patient with a likely pathogenic variant in KCNE1
Authors: Einer Carlos Eduardo Arevalo-Rios, Roselyn Mateo-Collado, Hyunki Kim, Elvira Isganaitis, Rohit N Kulkarni, Mary-Elizabeth Patti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 41-year-old woman with history of hypoglycemic symptoms underwent sleeve gastrectomy for weight loss. Her postoperative course was complicated by cardiac arrest due to polymorphic ventricular tachycardia arising from QT interval prolongation. Weeks after surgery, hypoglycemic symptoms worsened, with postprandial capillary glucose as low as 33 mg/dL (SI: 1.2 mmol/L) (reference, 70-140 mg/dL [SI: 3.9-7.8 mmol/L]). An inpatient fast indicated appropriate suppression of insulin. The combination of worsening hypoglycemia early postoperatively and prolonged QT interval prompted genetic testing, which revealed a likely pathogenic variant in KCNE1, encoding a subunit of the voltage-gated potassium channel Kv7.1, expressed in cardiomyocytes and pancreatic β cells. The patient was treated with nutrition therapy and diazoxide for hypoglycemia and β-blocker for long QT syndrome (LQTS). Patients with KCNE1 pathogenic variants are known to have LQTS and have been reported to have postprandial hypoglycemia. This case highlights the importance of considering genetic etiologies when encountering concomitant hypoglycemia and LQTS, and that preexisting LQTS may confer risk for hypoglycemia after upper gastrointestinal surgery.Keywords: hypoglycemia, long QT syndrome, bariatric surgery, genetics

Introduction

Hypoglycemia is increasingly recognized as a complication of upper gastrointestinal surgery; up to 30% of postbariatric individuals develop hypoglycemic symptoms, typically 1 to 3 years postoperatively [1]. In this setting, rapid intestinal nutrient delivery results in high postprandial glucose and increased incretin and insulin secretion, contributing to subsequent hypoglycemia [1]. The dominant risk factor for postbariatric hypoglycemia in the Longitudinal Assessment of Bariatric Surgery study was preoperative symptoms of hypoglycemia, suggesting that preexisting or genetic factors could contribute to postoperative metabolic changes conferring increased risk [2].

Long QT syndrome (LQTS) is characterized by prolonged QT interval due to delayed ventricular repolarization, predisposing to ventricular arrhythmias. Congenital (genetic) LQTS is related to pathogenic variants in KCNQ1, KCNH2, KCNE1, and other genes [3]. Acquired LQTS can be caused by drugs, electrolyte imbalances, hypoglycemia, and cardiac and neurological abnormalities [3, 4].

Coexistence of LQTS and hypoglycemia raises the possibility of shared pathophysiology. Voltage-gated potassium channels are fundamental both for cardiomyocyte excitability and insulin secretion, with potassium channelopathies potentially contributing to LQTS and hypoglycemia. Here, we present a patient with LQTS and worsening hypoglycemia after sleeve gastrectomy who was found to have a likely pathogenic variant in KCNE1.

Case presentation

A 41-year-old woman with a body mass index (BMI) of 44 kg/m2 and a previous history of fasting and postprandial hypoglycemic symptoms underwent sleeve gastrectomy for weight loss. Her immediate postoperative course was complicated by cardiac arrest due to polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, with a QT interval as long as 576 milliseconds (reference, <460 milliseconds). Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved with chest compressions. On review, QT prolongation was present in previous electrocardiograms. Weeks postoperatively, she reported worsening postprandial hypoglycemic symptoms, including anxiety, palpitations, dizziness, and sweating. Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) revealed sensor values between 40 and 50 mg/dL (SI: 2.2-2.8 mmol/L) (reference, 70-180 mg/dL [SI: 3.9-10 mmol/L]), with 86% of readings at less than 80 mg/dL (SI: <4.4 mmol/L). Postprandial capillary glucose was as low as 33 mg/dL (SI: 1.8 mmol/L) (reference, 70-140 mg/dL [SI: 3.9-7.8 mmol/L]).

In retrospect, onset of hypoglycemic symptoms occurred at age 20, with dizziness, confusion, and sweating after prolonged fasting. Weight gain occurred despite dietary and lifestyle modifications. At age 31, she had episodic near-syncope associated with capillary glucose 40 mg/dL (SI: 2.2 mmol/L); these episodes were controlled with nutritional therapy. Preoperative CGM revealed a peak sensor glucose of 225 mg/dL (SI: 12.5 mmol/L) postmeal, minimum less than 50 mg/dL (<2.8 mmol/L), and 17% of values less than 80 mg/dL (SI: <4.4 mmol/L). Episodes occurred between 8 Pm and 4 Am, with associated sweating, particularly after carbohydrate consumption. Capillary glucose measurements did not reveal hypoglycemic events.

Past medical history included hypertension managed with chlorthalidone and migraines treated with topiramate (both discontinued after cardiac event). Family history was notable for coronary artery disease, type 2 diabetes, and cardiac arrest (uncle). Her daughter was born at 38 weeks, large for gestational age (3.77 kg, [reference 38-week girls, 2.64-3.71 kg]), and had neonatal hypoglycemia attributed to poor feeding, which resolved with formula supplementation. The patient’s brother had obesity, managed with sleeve gastrectomy without hypoglycemia.

Diagnostic assessment

At the time of the cardiac event, the patient’s potassium was mildly low at 3.3 mg/dL (SI: 3.3 mmol/L) (reference, 3.5-5 mg/dL [SI: 3.5-5 mmol/L]) with no recurrent hypokalemia after repletion. An inpatient fast, performed to determine insulin suppressibility, resulted in progressive decrease in blood glucose, with a nadir of 59 mg/dL (SI: 3.3 mmol/L) at 46 hours of fasting with associated headache, sweating, and shakiness. Concurrent testing included insulin 1.9 μIU/mL (SI: 13.2 pmol/L) (reference during hypoglycemia, <3 μIU/mL [SI: <20.8 pmol/L]), C-peptide 0.54 ng/mL (SI: 0.18 nmol/L) (reference, <0.6 ng/mL [SI: 0.2 nmol/L]), proinsulin less than 65 pg/mL (SI: <7.5 pmol/L) (reference, <44 pg/mL [SI: <5 pmol/L]), β-hydroxybutyrate 26 mg/dL (SI: 2.5 mmol/L) (reference during hypoglycemia: >28 mg/dL [SI: >2.7 mmol/L]), and glucagon 73 pg/mL (SI: 73 ng/L) (reference, 50-100 pg/mL [SI: 50-100 ng/L]). Glucose rose to 67 mg/dL (SI: 3.7 mmol/L) after glucagon administration (1 mg). These results were consistent with a lack of autonomous insulin secretion with fasting. Morning cortisol (6.9 μg/dL [SI: 190 nmol/L]) (reference, 5-25 μg/dL [SI: 138-690 nmol/L]), postcosyntropin cortisol (22.7 μg/dL [SI: 626 nmol/L] at 60 minutes) (reference, >18 μg/dL [SI: >500 nmol/L]), and plasma metanephrines were normal. Clinical and laboratory evaluation did not suggest non–insulin-mediated causes of hypoglycemia (malnutrition, kidney, adrenal, liver disease). Genetic testing (36-gene LQTS panel, Ambry Genetics) revealed a likely pathogenic heterozygous variant in exon 3 of KCNE1, c.226G>A (p.Asp76Asn).

Evaluation of the daughter at age 6 revealed the same genetic variant. Seven-day CGM revealed 0% of readings at less than 55 mg/dL (SI: <3.1 mmol/L), 7% at 56 to 70 mg/dL (SI: 3.1-3.9 mmol/L), 93% at 71 to 160 mg/dL (SI: 3.9-8.9 mmol/L), and 1% greater than 160 mg/dL (SI: >8.9 mmol/L). Minimum capillary glucose was 49 mg/dL (SI: 2.7 mmol/L). Hypoglycemia occurred between midnight and 8 Am, with somnolence after awakening. Exercise testing revealed normal baseline QT interval (440 milliseconds) and upper-normal values throughout recovery (440-480 milliseconds); glucose was not measured during testing.

Treatment

Therapy included frequent small meals with low-glycemic-index foods to reduce postprandial insulin, CGM to alert for low sensor glucose, cornstarch, diazoxide 50 mg daily at bedtime, and glucagon for emergency use. Nadolol 20 mg daily was prescribed for LQTS, and QT-prolonging medications were avoided. The daughter was treated with nutritional therapy, cornstarch at bedtime, and avoidance of QT-prolonging medications.

Outcome and follow-up

On therapy, there have been no episodes of severe hypoglycemia or arrest. The patient’s BMI reduced to 35 kg/m2 at 1 year postoperatively, but increased to 45 thereafter. Her daughter had no severe hypoglycemia or cardiac events, and therapy was discontinued.

Discussion

This case illustrates the coexistence of 2 phenotypes—LQTS and hypoglycemia—linked to a common underlying mechanism. Channelopathies of the voltage-gated potassium channel have been associated with concomitant LQTS and hypoglycemia. KCNQ1 encodes the pore-forming protein of the Kv7.1 channel, while KCNE1 encodes a modulatory subunit [5]. KCNH2 encodes Kv11.1, a distinct potassium channel.

Previous studies demonstrated that KCNQ1 pathogenic variants modulate postprandial glucose and insulin secretion. In 14 individuals with KCNQ1 loss-of-function variants [5], oral glucose tolerance testing (OGTT) revealed lower plasma glucose at 210 minutes in carriers vs controls (79.2 ± 5.4 mg/dL [SI: 4.4 ± 0.3 mmol/L] vs 97.2 ± 9 mg/dL [SI: 5.4 ± 0.5 mmol/L]) and nearly 2-fold higher plasma insulin in carriers (area under curve 45.6 ± 6.3 vs. 26.0 ± 2.8 minutes × nmol/L).

Similarly, KCNH2 pathogenic variants have been associated with altered glucose, insulin, and incretin meal responses. During OGTT, individuals with KCNH2 loss-of-function variants had lower glucose vs controls [6] (mean 6-hour glucose 97 ± 4 mg/dL [SI: 5.4 ± 0.2 mmol/L] vs 106 ± 2 mg/dL [SI: 5.9 ± 0.1 mmol/L]). Two patients with LQTS, but no controls, developed plasma glucose less than 50 mg/dL (SI: <2.8 mmol/L). Additionally, there was a 56% to 78% increase in insulin, C-peptide, glucagon-like peptide-1, and gastric inhibitory polypeptide vs controls [6]. In mice, KCNH2 deficiency led to increased incretin response to oral glucose, with prolonged action potential in enteroendocrine cells [7].

A prior report linked a KCNE1 variant with hypoglycemia since infancy [8]. At age 11 months, a patient was incidentally found to have plasma glucose of 20 mg/dL (SI: 1.1 mmol/L). During hypoglycemia, plasma insulin was 4.4 μU/mL (SI: 26.4 pmol/L). After glucagon administration, glucose rose from 37 mg/dL (SI: 2.1 mmol/L) to 166 mg/dL (SI: 9.2 mmol/L). Diazoxide (9.8 mg/kg/day) was started at age 11 months for suspected congenital hyperinsulinism. Provocative testing revealed hypoglycemia in response to protein (nadir 65 mg/dL [SI: 3.6 mmol/L]), glucose (nadir 67 mg/dL [SI: 3.7 mmol/L]), and exercise (nadir 50 mg/dL [SI: 2.8 mmol/L]). Cardiac evaluation revealed borderline long QT (487 milliseconds). Genetic testing revealed a KCNE1 insertion-deletion (c.172_177delACCCTGinsCCCCCT), also found in her father, who had been diagnosed with congenital hyperinsulinism as a child.

Mechanisms responsible for alterations in glucose metabolism and increased insulin and incretin secretion with LQTS-associated variants remain unclear. KCNQ1, KCNH2, and KCNE1 are expressed in cardiomyocytes, where pathogenic variants contribute to LQTS [3, 9]. However, expression is not exclusive to cardiomyocytes, and expression in pancreatic islets and enteroendocrine cells may contribute. For example, KCNQ1 expression is relatively enriched in ductal, acinar, and endothelial cells [10, 11] in PanKbase, a large human islet single-cell RNA sequencing database [12-23]. KCNH2 is also expressed in α and β cells [11]. For KCNE1, expression in the pancreas is variable [11]. Expression of KCNE1 is limited in PanKbase, but bulk messenger RNA sequencing of sorted α and β cells reveals heterogeneous expression [10]. In enteroendocrine cells, expression of KCNQ1, KCNH2, and KCNE1 is limited [24]. These observations provide an opportunity to explore KCNE1 in β-cell regeneration [25].

In our patient, given strong clinical suspicion and desire to avoid potentially unsafe hypoglycemia-related QTc prolongation, meal testing was not performed. Moreover, provocative testing with measurement of hormonal responses is largely performed in a research setting as it is not required for clinical diagnosis in most patients and can result in dumping symptoms and hypoglycemia.

It is likely that rapid gastric emptying resulting from bariatric surgery was an aggravating factor for increased hypoglycemia frequency and severity. Rapid intestinal delivery of food can induce nausea, palpitations, lightheadedness, flushing, sweating, diarrhea, and abdominal cramping (“early” dumping, usually within 30 minutes postprandially). This rapid nutrient delivery also increases insulin and incretin secretion, contributing to subsequent hypoglycemia and related symptoms (“late dumping,” usually 1-3 hours postprandially). Additionally, it is important to consider whether medications could contribute to hypoglycemia. In this patient, migraine therapy included topiramate, previously associated with hypoglycemia [26]. Despite discontinuation, documented hypoglycemia persisted, reflecting the major contribution of the postsurgical state.

The postsurgical metabolic milieu may also contribute to further prolongation of the QT interval in a patient with genetic risk. In the acute postoperative state, electrolyte shifts and medications could contribute, while chronically, excessive postprandial insulin secretion could activate the Na/K ATPase pump, resulting in transient hypokalemia and QT prolongation [27]. For patients with LQTS and hypoglycemia, management should address QT prolongation, hyperinsulinemia, attendant hypokalemia, and subsequent hypoglycemia. β-Blockers require risk-benefit assessment. While β-blockers decrease cardiac events in LQTS [29], they can reduce hypoglycemic symptoms and counterregulatory responses. CGM with alarms may help to improve hypoglycemia awareness. Hypoglycemia-focused therapy is aimed at reduction in prandial insulin secretion, using controlled quantities of low-glycemic-index carbohydrates to limit glucose spikes, and inhibition of insulin secretion using diazoxide.

In summary, this case highlights the importance of considering genetic etiologies when encountering concomitant hypoglycemia and LQTS. Patients with LQTS planning to undergo bariatric or upper gastrointestinal surgery affecting gastric emptying should be counseled on potential risks of surgery-induced metabolic and hormonal changes that may further increase QT interval, incretin and insulin secretion, and postprandial hypoglycemia.
