Inhibitory effect of Eimeria maxima IFN-γ inhibitory molecules on the immune function of T cell subsets in chickens
Authors: Xianglin Pu, Yangdong Pan, Quanjia Xiang, Mingmin Lu, Lixin Xu, Ruofeng Yan, Xiangrui Li, Xiaokai Song
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt has been reported that infection of chicken coccidian could inhibit the production of Th1 cytokine IFN-γ, thereby evading clearance by the host immune system. The present study aimed to have a further investigation into the effects of Eimeria maxima IFN-γ inhibitory molecules (EmHPSP-2 and EmHPSP-3) on the immune function of chicken peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) and various T cell subsets. First, separated PBMC or sorted T cell subsets were used for incubation with recombinant proteins of EmHPSP-2 (rEmHPSP-2) and EmHPSP-3 (rEmHPSP-3). Subsequently, the effects of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 on proliferative capacity, nitric oxide (NO) release and mRNA levels of cytokines of the above cells were detected. The sorting purity of CD8+, CD4+ CD25−, CD4+, and CD4+ CD25+ T cells was 93.01, 88.88, 87.04, and 81.26%, respectively. The NO release of PBMC was significantly inhibited by rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3. The proliferation of PBMC and CD4+ T cells was significantly inhibited by rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3, whereas CD8+, CD4+ CD25−, and CD4+ CD25+ T cells was significantly promoted by the 2 proteins. The 2 proteins significantly downregulated interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) mRNA level, upregulated the transcriptional levels of interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-beta1 (TGF-β1) in PBMC. IFN-γ and IL-2 transcriptional levels were markedly inhibited in CD8+ T cells. IFN-γ transcriptional level was significantly inhibited, but IL-4 was promoted by rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 in CD4+ CD25− T cells. Meanwhile, the inhibitory effects of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 on the transcriptional levels of IFN-γ and IL-2 were more obvious in CD4+ T cells containing CD25+ cells compared with the CD25+ cells depletion group. It was found that IL-10, TGF-β1, and cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen-4 (CTLA-4) mRNA levels were significantly upregulated upon stimulation of chicken CD4+ CD25+ T cells by proteins. This study is not only of great significance to clarify the immune evasion mechanism of chicken coccidia, but also provides candidate antigen molecules for development of a novel vaccine against chicken coccidiosis.Key words: Eimeria maxima, IFN-γ inhibitory molecule, immune evasion, T cell subset

INTRODUCTION

Avian coccidiosis is an intestinal disease caused by one or multiple Eimeria species that can lead to death, weight loss, reduced feed conversion, bloody feces, and significant economic losses in the poultry industry (Rose et al., 1979; Blake et al., 2014; Blake, 2015; Fatoba et al., 2018; El-Shall et al., 2022). The estimated annual loss of production as well as preventive and therapeutic costs reach £ 10.36 billion (Blake et al., 2020). Currently, the controlling of avian coccidiosis is mainly based on the usage of anticoccidial drugs (Chapman et al., 2014). However, with the continuous use of anticoccidial drugs, problems such as drug resistance, drug residues, and high cost of prevention and control have emerged (Allen et al., 2002; Clarke et al., 2014; Pop et al., 2019), coupled with various countries around the world successively proposed “withdrawal” program of antibiotics and growth promoters even banned the use of antibiotic and hormonal growth promoters in livestock nutrition (Maria Cardinal et al., 2019; Krysiak et al., 2021). Therefore, vaccination is considered an approach used to take place antibiotics for prevention of avian coccidiosis (Kadykalo et al., 2018).

However, a serious challenge faced by vaccination is the immune evasion problem of avian coccidia, which contributes greatly to the unsatisfactory protective efficacy of anticoccidial vaccines. During the infection process of chicken coccidia, it could suppress the host's immune response through various pathways. A crucial strategy for achieving immune evasion is the inhibition of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) secretion, which plays a significant role in antiavian coccidia infection (Dalloul et al., 2006; Kim et al., 2019; Bremner et al., 2021). It was reported that expression of IFN-γ was inhibited in the corresponding parasitic intestines after infection with E. maxima, E. tenella, and E. acervulina (Cornelissen et al., 2009). There were also some reports of Eimeria infection causing upregulation of interleukin-10 (IL-10). Haritova and colleagues observed a significant increase of IL-10 mRNA level in liver and cecum following infection with E. tenella (Haritova et al., 2012). Wu and his colleagues reported that IL-10 protein in serum also significantly increased in chicken infected with E. tenella (Wu et al., 2016). Another infection test also demonstrated an increasing trend in the IL-10, transforming growth factor-beta1 ( TGF-β1), and cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen-4 (CTLA-4) mRNA levels in the cecum tissues, with the levels peaking on d 6 after infection (Yu et al., 2021). These results indicated that Eimeria infection could upregulate regulatory T cells (Tregs) cytokine of IL-10, and downregulate Th1 cytokine of IFN-γ, which was further supported by a study of Walston. In the study of Walston and his colleagues, they found IFN-γ mRNA content reduced to half of that in the uninfected group in the spleen, meanwhile, IL-10 mRNA level significantly upregulated in the cecal tonsils on d 11 after coinfection with E. maxima, E. tenella, and E. acervulina (Walston et al., 2016). In summary, although IFN-γ is crucial for pathogen clearance and is normally thought to be upregulated by infection, there are some immune inhibitors in the parasites that could inhibit IFN-γ expression, helping to evade attack by the host immune system. However, the antigen components of chicken coccidia are complex, and there have been many reports of immunoprotective antigens, but the reports about immunosuppressive molecules of avian coccidia are quite limited. Hence, searching for Th1 cytokine inhibitory molecules and revealing their inhibitory effects would be of some reference values for elucidating the immune evasion mechanism of avian coccidia.

The cell-mediated immunity driven by T cells and their secreted cytokines plays a dominant role in the immunity against chicken coccidia (Rose et al., 1979; Trout et al., 1996). CD8+ T cells play an important role in the fighting against intracellular protozoa infection through the secretion of cytokines and the exertion of cytotoxic effects, as they are the main effector cells that achieve specific cell killing effects (Lillehoj et al., 2004; Jordan et al., 2010; Novais et al., 2021). In addition, various studies have shown that molecules such as CD8α, CD8β, Perforin, Granzyme A and Granzyme K were significantly upregulated in the host on d 10 after primary infection with E. tenella (Wattrang et al., 2016a; Sandholt et al., 2021). Another study by Wattrang suggested that the mobilization of surface molecule CD107a of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells was induced with E. tenella infection, which is a marker of degranulation (Wattrang et al., 2016b). The above studies provide evidence in support of the activation and recruitment of CD8+ T cells in chicken following infection with coccidia. CD4+ T cells, a significant component of the chicken immune system, consist mostly of helper T cells, which also participate in the host immune response against coccidia infection (Bessay et al., 1996). There are at least 4 different T cell subpopulations, namely Th1, Th2, Th17, and Treg, have been reported in the process of chicken coccidia infection (Min et al., 2013; Morris et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2019). And each T cell subpopulation could help the body cope with the invasion of pathogens by secreting different cytokines (Zhu et al., 2008). The Th1 cell is one of the most crucial types of cells involved in the immune response against coccidia infection, which plays a major role in mediating Th1 immune response (Kim et al., 2019). It is possible that chicken coccidia may rely on inhibiting the immune functions of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells to achieve immune evasion, given the important role in anticoccidia infection.

Previously, we identified 4 IFN-γ inhibitory molecules, including EmHPSP-2 (GenBank: XM_013477735.1) and EmHPSP-3 (GenBank: XM_013392836.1), from an Eimeria maxima (E. maxima) sporozoite cDNA expression library by detecting the expressions of differentiation specific transcription factor (T-bet) and hallmark cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-2) of Th1 immune response through cDNA library immunization (unpublished data). The present study aims to have a further investigation into the effects of the above 2 IFN-γ inhibitory molecules on the immune functions of chicken T cell subsets, including effector T cells (CD4+ CD25− and CD8+ T cells) and Treg cells (CD4+ CD25+ T cells). This study is not only of great significance to clarify the immune evasion mechanism of chicken coccidia, but also provides candidate antigen molecules for developing a novel vaccine against chicken coccidiosis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Strains, Plasmids, and Animals

The strains and recombinant plasmids of E. maxima IFN-γ inhibitory molecules (EmHPSP-2 and EmHPSP-3) were stored in our laboratory. In a strictly sterilized house free of coccidian oocysts, 1-day-old Hy-Line chickens were fed. The chickens were provided unrestricted access to feed and water throughout the duration of the experiment, with both the feed and water being devoid of anticoccidial drugs.

Expression and Purification of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3

Recombinant protein EmHPSP-2 (rEmHPSP-2) or recombinant protein EmHPSP-3 (rEmHPSP-3) was expressed using E. coli BL21 (DE3) containing the expression plasmid of EmHPSP-2 or EmHPSP-3 and purified using His Trap FF protein purification Kit (GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ, USA). The purity of protein samples was assessed by the performance of a sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) analysis. Next, endotoxin was removed from the protein using the ToxinEraser Endotoxin Removal Kit (GenScript, Nanjing, China) to eliminate possible interference. To ensure sterility, the protein was lastly filtered through a 0.22 μm filter.

Identification of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 by Western Blot

The serum was prepared by following a previously utilized method for chicken anti-E. maxima (Tian et al., 2017). rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 were separated by the performance of an SDS-PAGE, following which they were transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Merck Millipore, Tullagreen, Carrigtwohill, Ireland). Next, 5% skimmed milk was used to block the membrane, made using Tris-buffered saline solution containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST). Subsequently, the primary antibody used in the Western blot assay was either the chicken anti-E. maxima serum (1:100) or the His-tag Mouse Monoclonal antibody (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) (1:8,000), while the secondary antibody was either the horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-chicken IgG (Abbkine, Redlands, CA) (1:5,000) or the HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (Affinity Biosciences, Cincinnati, OH, USA) (1:10,000), respectively. The incubation was performed overnight at 4°C and then 2 h at room temperature. Finally, the application of Chemistar High-sig ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Tanon, Shanghai, China) for chemical exposure of the membrane and detection of bound antibodies.

Separation of Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells in Chicken

The density gradient cell centrifugation method was used to separate mononuclear cells from the peripheral blood of chicken. In brief, sterilized blood collection tube (containing 4% sodium citrate) was used to take blood from the heart of healthy chicks. Subsequently, the anticoagulant blood and sterilized phosphate buffer saline (PBS) were gently mixed in a 1:1 ratio. Slowly added 5 mL of diluted chicken blood along the tube wall into a 15 mL centrifuge tube containing 5 mL of lymphocyte separating solution (TBD, Tianjin, China). The samples were centrifuged horizontally at 500 × g for 20 min at room temperature. Finally, the second layer of milky white lymphocyte was sucked out to another 15 mL sterile centrifuge tube, and washed it twice with sterile PBS.

Magnetic Bead Sorting of Chicken CD8+ and CD4+ T Cells

The cell density of chicken peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) was adjusted to 1 × 108 cells per mL using 4°C precooled PBS. The primary phycoerythrin (PE)-conjugated mouse anti-chicken CD8α or CD4 antibody (Southernbiotech, Birmingham, AL) was added according to 1 × 108 cells. The cells were mixed well and incubated in the dark at 4°C for 30 min, following by washing the cells with PBS to remove the unbound antibodies. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with Anti-PE MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) in the dark at 4°C for 15 min. The incubated cells were resuspended in PBS to achieve a cell density of 2 × 108 cells per mL. Magnetic bead sorting of chicken CD8+ or CD4+ T cells was performed using the magnetic bead sorting system (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). It was chicken CD8+ or CD4+ T cells that attached to the sorting column. Finally, flushed out the sorting column with 5 mL of PBS to obtain magnetically labeled positive cells.

Magnetic Bead Sorting of Chicken CD4+ CD25− and CD4+ CD25+ T Cells

Similarly, chicken PBMC were sequentially incubated with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated human anti-chicken CD25 antibody (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) and Anti-FITC MultiSort MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Performance of magnetic bead sorting of chicken CD25+ cells by the magnetic bead sorting system instruction. The cells passed through the sorting column are chicken CD25− cells, and the cells attached to the sorting column are chicken CD25+ cells. Subsequently, chicken CD25− cells or CD25+ cells were incubated sequentially with PE-conjugated mouse anti-chicken CD4 antibody and Anti-PE MicroBeads, and finally chicken CD4+ CD25− or CD4+ CD25+ T cells were obtained by the performance of magnetic bead sorting. The specific steps refer to magnetic bead sorting of chicken CD8+ and CD4+ T cells.

Detection of Cell Sorting Purity by Flow Cytometry

The positive cells underwent a 1-time wash using RPMI 1640 complete medium and were subjected to flow cytometry analysis to determine the purity of magnetic bead sorting.

Release of Nitric Oxide

Freshly separated chicken PBMC was plated into a 48-well cell culture plate at a density of 1 × 106 cells per mL with 250 μL per well. The cells were stimulated at 10, 20, 40, and 80 μg/mL by rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 for 24 h at 37°C. Additionally, 3 control groups were established: PBS negative group, pET-32a tag protein group, and 3 μg/mL lipopolysaccharides (LPS) group. The cell supernatant was used to detect the total content of nitrate and nitrite using the total nitric oxide assay kit (Beyotime, Nanjing, China), and further determined the content of released nitric oxide by calculating the value of OD540 in a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Cell Proliferation Assay

Separated fresh chicken PBMC and T cell subsets were distributed in 96-well cell culture plates at 100 μL per well. Each well was supplemented with different concentrations of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3(10, 20, 40, and 80 μg/mL). In addition, PBS negative group, pET-32a tag protein group, and 3 μg/mL concanamycin A (ConA) or 2 μg/mL LPS group were established. The plates were plated into a cell incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37°C for 24 h. Then the Cell Counting Kit-8 (Beyotime, Nanjing, China) solution was added to each well with 10 μL, and continued to culturing for 2 h in the dark. At the same time, RPMI 1640 complete medium supplemented with 10 μL CCK-8 solution was used for calibration purposes. We calculated the proliferative index using the OD450 value of the negative control group as the baseline of 100%.

Detection of the Effects of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 on the mRNA Levels of Cytokines in Chicken PBMC

Chicken PBMC was plated into 12-well cell culture plates with 2 mL per well. rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 (10, 20, 40, and 80 μg/mL) were incubated with PBMC for 6 h at 37°C. The RNA of collected cells was extracted using a reagent specifically designed for total RNA extraction (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was prepared using HiScript III RT SuperMix from Vazyme (Nanjing, China), which was used for real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR (qPCR) assay. Cytokine primers (Table 1) were designed by NCBI, and all specific primers were verified to be available for this test. The reaction system was prepared in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions of PerfectStart Green qPCR Super Mix (+DyeII) (Transgen, Nanjing, China). The assay was conducted using the QuantStudio 6 and 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster city, CA, USA) under the following reaction program: predenaturation at 94°C for 30 s; 40 cycle reaction at 94°C 5 s, 60°C 30 s; and the melt curve stage at 95°C 15 s, 60°C 60 s, 95°C 15 s. The 2−ΔΔCT method, with β-actin serving as the internal reference gene, was utilized to determine the fold change in cytokine transcriptional levels.

Detection of the mRNA Levels of Cytokines in Chicken T Cell Subsets

The optimal concentration of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 was selected to costimulate chicken T cell subsets (CD8+, CD4+ CD25−, CD4+, or CD4+ CD25+ T cells) with 3 μg/mL ConA for 6 h at 37°C. Total cellular RNA was extracted using the RNA extraction reagent and then reverse transcribed to cDNA for the qPCR assay. And the assay results were used to analyze the effects of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 on cytokine mRNA levels in different chicken T cell subsets. Meanwhile, CD4+ T cells were used as a control to further observe the differences in the effects of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 on cytokines before and after CD25+ cells depletion.

Statistical Analysis of Data

The SPSS 23.0 system software was used for statistics and analysis, with the 1-way ANOVA Duncan method used to analyze the differences between groups, and the independent sample t test used to analyze the differences between CD4+ T cells before and after CD25+ cells depletion. The same letters indicate no significant difference (P > 0.05), while the different letters indicate significant differences (P < 0.05).

RESULTS

Purification of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3

The purification of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 was carried out using protein purification Kit of His Trap FF. The result of protein purification was determined by performing an SDS-PAGE. As can been seen from Figure 1, a single band of approximately 44 kDa (Figure 1A, Line 2) and 74 kDa (Figure 1B, Line 2) was appeared for both rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3, respectively, the size of which was consistent with the predicted protein molecule, which suggested that rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 were well purified.

Identification of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 by Western Blot

The identification of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 was performed using chicken anti-E. maxima serum and His-tag monoclonal antibody derived from mouse, respectively, via Western blot assay. As shown in Figure 1, 2 single bands with molecular weights of approximately 44 kDa (Figure 1A, Line 3) and 74 kDa (Figure 1B, Line 3) were identified respectively by chicken anti-E. maxima serum, which were consistent with the size of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3. Meanwhile, the 2 single bands were also identified by His-tag monoclonal antibody derived from mouse (Figure 1A and B, Line 5). However, both rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 were not identified by negative serum in chicken (Figure 1A and B, Line 4).

Detection of the Sorting Purity of T Cell Subsets in Chicken

The magnetic bead sorting of positive cells was performed by indirect labeling, and the cell sorting purity was determined by running a flow cytometry analysis. The proportions of CD8+, CD4+, CD4+ CD25−, and CD4+ CD25+ T cells to total PBMC increased to 93.01% (Figure 2, A3), 87.04% (Figure 2, B3), 88.88% (Figure 2, C3), and 81.26% (Figure 2, D5) after magnetic bead sorting, respectively, which was basically in accordance with subsequent cell incubation assays.

Release of Nitric Oxide in Chicken PBMC

The effects of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 on release of nitric oxide from chicken PBMC were detected using nitrate reductase method. Figure 3A shows that rEmHPSP-2 (10, 20, and 40 μg/mL) and rEmHPSP-3 (20, 40, and 80 μg/mL) significantly inhibited the release of NO from chicken PBMC (P < 0.05), indicating that both proteins effectively inhibited the release of NO in chicken PBMC.

The differences between groups were analyzed using Duncan method of 1-way ANOVA, where the same letter indicates no significant difference (P > 0.05) and different letter indicates significant difference (P < 0.05).

Detection of Cell Proliferation

rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 were incubated with chicken PBMC and T cell subsets at various concentrations. The effects of the 2 proteins on proliferation level of chicken PBMC and T cell subsets were observed using the CCK-8 solution. As shown in Figure 3B and C, the proliferation of chicken PBMC and CD4+ T cells was considerably restrained with increasing concentrations of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 proteins (P < 0.05). However, the proliferation level of chicken CD8+ T cells was significantly promoted with increasing protein concentrations (P < 0.05). The proliferation of CD4+ CD25− T cells was significantly promoted by rEmHPSP-2 at 10, 20, and 40 μg/mL (P < 0.05), as did rEmHPSP-3 at 10 and 20 μg/mL (P < 0.05). Additionally, the proliferation of Treg cells was significantly promoted by the 2 proteins (P < 0.05).

Effects of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 on the Transcription of Cytokines in Chicken PBMC

Study on the mechanisms of T cell-mediated immunity in avian coccidiosis has primarily focus on immune responses of Th1, Th2, and Treg. Therefore, we managed to detect the effects of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 on the transcriptional levels of cytokines associated with above immune responses by qPCR assay. The transcriptional level of IFN-γ in chicken PBMC was significantly inhibited by rEmHPSP-2 (Figure 4A) (P < 0.05), and simultaneously significantly enhanced the transcriptional levels of IL-2, IL-4, IL-10, and TGF-β1 (Figure 4A) (P < 0.05). And the transcriptional levels of IFN-γ, IL-2, and IL-4 were significantly inhibited (Figure 4B) (P < 0.05), whereas IL-10 and TGF-β1 transcriptional levels were promoted by rEmHPSP-3 (Figure 4B) (P < 0.05).

Effects of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 on the Transcription of Cytokines in Chicken CD4+ CD25− and CD4+ T Cells

The study found that the IFN-γ mRNA level was significantly inhibited, whereas IL-2 and IL-4 were significantly promoted by both rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 in chicken CD4+ CD25− T cells, with on significant effects on IL-10 and TGF-β1 transcriptional levels (Figure 6A and B) (P < 0.05). The result showed that chicken CD4+ T cells, without removing CD25+ cells, had a more obvious inhibitory effect on IFN-γ and IL-2 mRNA levels after being stimulated by rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3, and significantly upregulated IL-10 mRNA level (Figure 6C) (P < 0.05).

Summary of the Results

A summary of the results is shown in Table 2. It was found that EmHPSP-2 and EmHPSP-3 not only significantly downregulated the NO release and proliferation of chicken PBMC, but also downregulated the IFN-γ mRNA level of chicken PBMC, CD8+, CD4+, and CD4+ CD25− T cells and IL-2 mRNA level of chicken CD8+ and CD4+ T cells. In addition, the 2 proteins could significantly upregulate the levels of IL-10 and TGF-β1 mRNA in chicken PBMC and CD4+ CD25+ T cells and the level of IL-10 mRNA in chicken CD4+ T cells.

DISCUSSION

Avian coccidiosis is one of the intestinal parasitic diseases that hinder the development of poultry industry, seriously threatening the productivity and welfare of domestic chickens as well as causing huge economic losses (Fatoba et al., 2018; Gilbert et al., 2020). During avian coccidia infection, the cell-mediated immune response plays a crucial role (Kim et al., 2019), which is mainly mediated by T lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells. In the host immune response against avian coccidia infection, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells play an important role (Walston et al., 2016; Davou et al., 2018). Studies have shown that monoclonal antibody of anti-chicken CD4 or CD8 molecule was used to treat chicks, and then infected them with avian coccidia, which can lead to more severe lesions and increased oocysts (Trout et al., 1996; Lillehoj et al., 2007). Therefore, the cell proliferation or proportion of CD4+ or CD8+ T cells can be used to preliminarily evaluate the immune protective effect of antigen molecules of avian coccidia. This study found a significant inhibition in the proliferation of chicken PBMC and CD4+ T cells, while chicken CD8+, CD4+ CD25−, and Treg cells was significantly promoted, possibly due to CD25+ cells depletion.

NO is secreted by dendritic cells (DCs), Mast cell, NK cells, and macrophages, which is a response to various cytokines and plays a significant role in immune protection against various pathogens (including parasitic protozoa) in vitro and animal models (Wang et al., 2021). During the infection of E. tenella, the level of NO production is relatively high, playing a role as an effector against avian coccidia (Allen et al., 1998). After stimulating cells with exogenous NO, the sporozoite of E. tenella will be rapidly expelled from the primary nephrocytes of chicken (Yan et al., 2021). IFN-γ can increase the activity of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and further produce adequacy NO in protozoa infection, which can directly kill the parasite or hinder the growth of the parasite in the host, so as to achieve immune protection (Hunter et al., 2012; Yarovinsky, 2014; Behnke et al., 2016). The study showed that the release of NO in chicken PBMC was significantly inhibited by stimulation with rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3, which is consistent with the downregulation of IFN-γ mRNA level, confirming the inhibitory effect of rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 on IFN-γ.

IFN-γ are mainly secreted by activated CD4+ Th1 cells and CD8+ T cells (Lee et al., 2022). During the phase of coccidia infection, DCs and macrophages secrete the cytokine IL-12, inducing the differentiation of Th1 cells, which further secrete Th1 cytokines such as IFN-γ to mediate cellular immunity against coccidia infection (Lillehoj et al., 1996; Lillehoj, 1998; Dalloul et al., 2006; Truong et al., 2017). However, multiple parasitic protozoa could achieve immune evasion by inhibiting Th1 cytokines such as IFN-γ. For example, Leishmania (Mandal et al., 2017; da Fonseca-Martins et al., 2021), Toxoplasma gondii (Rosowski et al., 2014; Rommereim et al., 2019), and Plasmodium falciparum (Belachew, 2018) could inhibit the secretion of Th1 cytokines through different forms to achieve immune evasion. During infection with avian coccidia, the expression of IFN-γ is inhibited (Cornelissen et al., 2009; Walston et al., 2016), and further inhibits IFN-γ-mediated cellular immunity, which is conducive to its survival in the host. This is also one of the important reasons for poor immune prevention and immune failure of avian coccidiosis. This study found that rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3 can significantly downregulated IFN-γ or IL-2 mRNA level in chicken PBMC, CD8+, CD4+, and CD4+ CD25− T cells, indicating that they can indeed inhibit Th1 cytokine expression.

Although Th1 cytokines, including IFN-γ, and their mediated Th1 immune response are crucial in providing the protection against avian coccidia, other cytokines like IL-4, IL-10, and TGF-β may dampen this immune response. As a Th2 cytokine, IL-4 can initiate Th2 immune response, mediate humoral immunity, and plays an important role in resisting helminth infection (Laidlaw et al., 2016). IL-4 affects the differentiation of Th cell, activates STAT6 and GATA3, promotes Th2 cell differentiation through IL-4/STAT6/GATA3 positive feedback, and inhibits Th1 cell differentiation (Stark et al., 2019). During the process of coccidia infection, IL-10 is considered an important cytokine, and its expression is conducive for parasites to evade the host's immune attacks. In the animal model infected with Leishmania, the parasite stimulates the host body to secrete IL-10, and downregulates IFN-γ and IL-12 production to inhibit the proliferation of specific T cells and the production of type 1 cytokines, making it difficult for macrophages to be activated by IFN-γ, thus avoiding intracellular killing (Samant et al., 2021). Studies have shown that the transcriptional levels of IL-4, IL-10, and TGF-β all show an upward trend after infection with avian coccidia (Hong et al., 2006; Cornelissen et al., 2009; Walston et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2021). These reports are consistent with our research findings. Our study showed that rEmHPSP-2 significantly upregulated IL-4, IL-10, and TGF-β1 mRNA levels in chicken PBMC, rEmHPSP-3 significantly upregulated IL-10 and TGF-β1 mRNA levels. The transcriptional levels of IL-4 and IL-10 were significantly upregulated in CD4+ T cells, the transcriptional level of IL-4 was upregulated in CD4+ CD25− T cells by rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3. The transcriptional levels of IL-10 and TGF-β1 were upregulated in Treg cells by rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3.

The gene homologous with the mammalian forkhead/winged-helix transcription factor 3 (FoxP3) has not yet been identified in chickens, but chicken CD4+ CD25+ T cells lacking the putative FoxP3 gene have similar functions to mammalian CD4+ CD25+ FoxP3+ T cells and are considered as regulatory T cells in chickens (Shack et al., 2008; Selvaraj, 2013). Treg cells have the ability to protect the host from excessive immune responses, but overactive Treg cells may interfere with the function of T, B and other immune cells (Li et al., 2008; Workman et al., 2009). Interestingly, some parasitic protozoa have evolved to stimulate Treg cells differentiation to evade host's immune attacks. The explanation may be that Treg cells highly express IL-10, which further inhibit T cell responses by IFN-γ and NO secretion by macrophages, thereby inhibiting an effective antiprotozoa defense in the host (McDowell et al., 1999; Sacks et al., 2002; Mariano et al., 2008; Wei et al., 2008). Studies have confirmed that the proportion of Treg cells is upregulated by infection with avian coccidia, indicating that coccidia induces the emergence of a subgroup of Treg cells in chicken (Selvaraj, 2013). In other protozoan infections, the related function of Treg cells are also promoted (Hall et al., 2012; Asad et al., 2019). These all suggest the importance of Treg cells in evading host immune responses in parasitic protozoa, including avian coccidia. Eliminating the inhibitory properties of Treg cells could be a potential approach to treating diseases caused by parasitic protozoa, since Treg cells are involved in helping protozoa evade the host's immune defenses (Tiwananthagorn et al., 2012; Onyilagha et al., 2014). Therefore, in order to exclude the possible inhibitory effect of Treg cells, we deleted CD25+ cells from CD4+ T cells to obtain CD4+ CD25− T cells and conducted the study of the functional effects of proteins on CD4+ CD25− T cells. However, the control experiment without depleting CD25+ cells was also performed to further observe the differences before and after CD25+ cells depletion. Our study found that chicken CD4+ T cells containing CD25+ cells had a more obvious inhibitory effect on IFN-γ and IL-2 mRNA levels after being stimulated by rEmHPSP-2 and rEmHPSP-3, and significantly upregulated IL-10 mRNA level, indicating that CD25+ cells play a certain role.

In conclusion, this study determined the effects of E. maxima IFN-γ inhibitory molecules (EmHPSP-2 and EmHPSP-3) on immune functions such as proliferative capacity, NO release and cytokine secretion of chicken PBMC and T cell subsets through both Th1 immune response and inhibitory immune response of Treg cells, providing a reference for elucidating the immune evasion mechanisms of avian coccidia and theoretical basis for improving the immune efficacy of avian coccidiosis vaccines.
