Integrated environmental and genomic analysis reveals the drivers and genetic mechanisms of agro-climatic adaptation in geese
Authors: Xiaoli Zhou, Junliang Xia, Weiding Chen, Jiajia Zou, Jiahui Chen, Xiquan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdaptation is one of the key processes of animal domestication, environmental pressures will leave footprints in the genome. Geese are widely distributed across multiple geographical conditions with distinct adaptations. However, few reports have focused on the environmental adaptability of geese. Moreover, the key environmental drivers that trigger local adaptation and its genetic mechanisms are still unknown. To this end, 35 agro-climatic variables of 257 geese from 14 Chinese breeds were obtained, the key environmental drivers and its genetic mechanism were elucidated by combining the genome data.ResultsFive key environmental drivers, elevation, water scarcity (BIO14), two variables as proxies of food availability (Grass and Landuse), and one variable as proxy of disease resistance (Forest) were identified after dissecting 35 agro-climatic variables by using ecological niche modeling (ENM). Integrating five key environmental drivers with single-nucleotide polymorphism variations, we conduct genome-wide selection signature analyses and genome-environment analysis. This analysis identified 1984 key genes likely integral to the environmental adaptation and mainly enriched in MAPK signaling pathways and metabolic pathways. Allele frequency distribution and selective sweep analysis revealed that some genes play crucial roles in environmental adaptation, including SATB1, NTRK2 and CTIF involved in adapting to the high-altitude hypoxic environments; ARMH3 and BDH2 participated in adapting to water deficiency; COL5L1, ULK4 and PI4KA associated with disease resistance, and other genes involved in metabolic adaptations including IQSEC1, TBC1D14, GRK5, FGGY and LRP1B, etc. Notably, we noticed that feather colour differentiation in geese might be driven by human agricultural practices, with LRP1B involved in this process.ConclusionsOverall, our study elucidated the five key environmental drivers and their genetic mechanisms. The candidate genes related to environmental adaptability offer new insights into genetic resource development and breeding strategies of Chinese indigenous geese.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12991-6.Keywords: Local environmental adaptation, Whole genome sequencing, Genotype-environment association, Selection signature, Redundancy analysis, Chinese indigenous goose

Background

Adaptation is one of the key processes of animal domestication. Environmental and artificial selection pressures will leave footprints in the genome, causing organisms to produce mutations in a direction conducive to survival [1]. Environmental stress is a key driving force shaping the phenotypic and genetic diversity of organisms. Compared to the exotic breeds, indigenous livestock populations exhibit greater adaptation to their local environmental conditions [2]. The exploration of environmental local adaptation is crucial for comprehending species evolution, preserving livestock genetic resources, and promoting breeding.

Goose is one of the oldest domesticated poultry in the world, which was domesticated approximately 7,000 years [3, 4]. As an important economic poultry, it globally supplies food (such as meat, eggs, Foie Gras), clothing and various other resources all around the world. China is the largest goose farming and consuming country in the world. According to the national list of livestock genetic resources in China, there are 31 local goose breeds [5]. These breeds are widely distributed, ranging from 19°N to 47°N, spanning multiple geographical conditions and agro-climates. Goose inhabiting different environments have developed distinct agro-climate adaptation mechanisms. In addition, compared with other poultry, the relatively low intensity of artificial selection in geese makes them a good model for studying local environmental adaptability.

Nowadays, many studies have focused on the mechanisms underlying species adaptation. A comprehensive study analysing 348 genome-wide datasets from Tibetan pigs has identified a robust set of genetic signatures linked to enhanced heart function adaptive to high altitudes [6]. Comparing whole genome data between the Chilika buffalo (from a saline region) and the Murrah buffalo (from a normal region), several biological pathways, including the MAPK signalling pathway, renin secretion, and endocytosis, were revealed to contribute to the Chilika buffalo’s adaptability to a saline environment [7]. These studies have revealed the genetic mechanisms underlying adaptation to various environments, offering invaluable insights for the selection of livestock breeds. However, these studies employed simple and direct methods for grouping and comparing samples, like high versus low altitudes, cold versus warm regions, and saline versus normal regions, without considering the environmental drivers that shape the local agroecology.

Ecological niche model (ENMs), also known as species distribution model (SDMs), is a popular tool in quantitative ecology to predict the distribution of a species across geographic space and time [8]. ENMs are used to predict the possible geographical distribution of species across the entire landscape, and estimate how environmental variables influence the formation of species’ habitats. This method has been a powerful tool in the conservation efforts of wild species [9]. In recent years, livestock scientists have expanded the conceptual framework for SDMs, using them to identify environmental drivers associated with local adaptation, and subsequently integrated with genomic data to identify candidate loci potentially involved in environmental adaptation [10–12].

Although there are many studies focused on local environmental adaptation, the research on geese has just begun. A study has pinpointed several candidate genes, such as PIP5K1B and NMNAT3, linked to cold adaptation through an analysis of whole genome data from Huoyan geese (cold regions) and local geese from Southern China (warm regions) [13]. But the major environmental factors that trigger adaptive responses in the geese ecosystem, and the genetic mechanisms underlying these adaptations are still largely unknown. In this study, the whole genomes of 257 geese from 14 Chinese breeds were obtained. Then, we dissected the key environmental drivers that trigger local adaptation from 35 agro-climatic variables by using ENM. Finally, by combining with genomic analyses (selection signature analysis, SSA) and landscape genomics analysis, the genetic mechanisms underlying goose adaptation were revealed. Our study will offer a valuable insight into agro-climatic driven adaptation, which will be useful for breed protection, genetic resource development and breeding strategies in geese.

Methods

Ethical statement

All animal experiments were ethically approved and conducted under the requirements of the Animal Protection Committee of South China Agricultural University (SCAU: 2020C030).

Sample collection and data downloads

In this study, a total of 257 WGS datasets were used. Within this dataset, 137 WGS datasets were newly generated, including 7 Chinese indigenous goose breeds [20 Wugang geese (WG), 19 Shitou geese (ST), 20 Magang geese (MG), 20 Wuzong geese (WZ), 20 Yangjiang geese (YJ), 19 Wanxi white geese (WX) and 19 Sanhua geese (SH) (Table S1, Fig. 1A)]. We downloaded an additional 120 WGS datasets from a publicly accessible database, including 7 Chinese indigenous goose breeds [20 ZI geese (ZI), 20 Huoyan geese (HY), 20 Sichuan white geese (SC), 15 Guangfeng white geese (GF), 20 Linxian white geese (LX), 15 Xupu geese (XP) and 10 Yili geese (YL)]. In order to better understand the genetic structure of geese, we also downloaded two European indigenous geese WGS datasets [20 Landes geese (LD) and 15 White Roman geese (RM)]. Totally, this study included 14 Chinese indigenous goose breeds and 2 European domestic goose breeds. The accession number of sequence data was shown in Table S1.

137 blood samples were collected from the medial pterygoid vein of geese. DNA was extracted from the whole blood by using a DNA extraction kit (Omega, Georgia, CA). The DNA concentration, purity and integrity were evaluated by a spectrophotometer and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The WGS libraries were prepared in accordance with the standard Illumina library preparation kit protocol (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). After library preparation, WGS was conducted at Novogene Bio-tech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Sequence quality control and SNP calling

All of the raw data (raw reads) of the fastq format were filtered for low-quality paired reads (base-calling duplicates and adapter contamination) by using SOAPnuke (v 2.1.8) to generate clean fastq [14]. Clean reads were mapped to the goose (Anser cygnoides domesticus) genome version 1.0, using BWA-men (v 0.7.17) [15]. The alignments were sorted by using Samtools (v 1.19.1) [16]. SNP calling was conducted on the GVCF file using “HaplotypeCaller” tool and underwent quality filtering using “VariantFiltration” in GATK (v 4.4.0.0) with the recommended parameters. Finally, the filtered SNPs dataset was used to remove sites with missing rate > 90% and allele frequency < 5% to generate the final SNPs dataset in VCFtools (v 0.1.16) [17]. After filtering, we obtained 13,142,333 high-quality SNPs from the initial 23,654,319 raw SNPs in 292 individuals. This SNP dataset was used in the subsequent analyses.

Population genetics analysis

The principal component analysis (PCA) was used to depict the population relationships in Plink (v 1.9). The identical by state (IBS) genetic distance matrix was established using “dnadist” and “neighbor” tools in Phylip (v 3.697) to construct an individual phylogenetic tree. The ITOL (v 4) online tool was used to visualize the phylogenetic tree [18]. ADMIXTURE (v 1.3.0) (default parameters) was utilized to analyze the population structure with K = 2 [19].

Genetic diversity

We performed a genetic diversity analysis on 16 goose breeds. The software Vcftools (v 0.1.16) was utilized to calculate the nucleotide diversity (π or “Pi”) and pairwise population-differentiation (Fst). The average genome Pi and Fst were estimated in 40 kb windows with 20 kb sliding steps. The observed heterozygosity (Ho), expected heterozygosity (He) and the runs of homozygosity (ROH) were calculated in Plink (v 1.9). A minimum length set of ROH was 100 kb. Redundant loci were removed to generate a set of SNPs for linkage disequilibrium analysis (LD) in Plink (v 1.9) (using the parameter “--indep-pairwise 50 10 0.5). PopLDdecay was used to calculate the LD decay of the 16 goose populations [20].

Environmental data collection

The distribution data of the 14 Chinese indigenous goose breeds were collected from the catalogue of national livestock and Poultry Genetic resources in China [5] (total 74 distributions, Table S2, Fig.S1). Geographic coordinates of distributions (latitude and longitude) were recorded, which were the “entry points” for the collection of environmental data for ENM. A total of 35 environmental data points were obtained from public databases at a spatial resolution of 30 arc-seconds (~ 1km2), details were shown in Table S3. The environmental data from each distribution site were extracted by ArcGIS (v 10.8) software, and considered as “occurrence points” for ENM.

Ecological niche modeling

In this study, ENM was used to analyze the environmental contribution to detect the key environmental driver with the geographic and environmental data. Maxent software (v 3.4.4) was used to perform ENM [21]. The R package “MaxentVariableSelection” was used to shortlist the environmental variables (Fig.S3). The details of choosing the ENM parameter were shown in the Supplementary material. The optimized model parameters used for ENM included three Feature Classes, viz. Linear, Hinge and Quadratic, and a Regularization Multiplier value of 3.5 (Fig.S4). The Area Under ROC Curve (AUC) values were used to assess the predictive power of the models (Fig.S5A). A jackknife assay was used to assess the importance of the environmental variables (Fig.S5B). The habitat suitability maps for the 14 Chinese indigenous goose breeds were generated using Maxent’s cumulative output.

Redundancy analysis

The Vegan (v 2.6-8) R package was used to perform RDA [22]. First, the LD-filtered genotype data were transferred into a non-reference allele (0,1,2) format. Subsequently, RDA was carried out with genotypes as the response variable and environmental data as the explanatory variables, conditioned on ancestry-coefficients for K = 2 from ADMIXTURE analysis. The significance of the model was assessed using the anova.cca function in the Vegan package with 999 permutations. SNPs that exhibited RDA loadings greater than 3.5 standard deviations were used to identify outliers (two-tailed p-value = 0.0005) for each significant axis.

Selection signature analysis

According to the population genetics analysis, in Chinese indigenous goose breeds, YL originated from graylag geese (anser anser) which was different from other Chinese goose breeds. In order to avoid any potential bias from population structure, we sorted the value of the 5 selected environmental drivers of each goose breed, then combined the bottom 2 and top 2 breeds to become the low and high groups for selection signature analysis (SSA) (Fig.S6-7). The details of selecting the low and high groups were shown in the Table 1 and Supplementary Materials.

Selection signature analyses were performed for each environmental variable. Population differentiation (Fst) and nucleotide diversity ratio (Pi ratio; high group/low group) were calculated using VCFtools with a 40 kb sliding window and a 20 kb step size. The XP-CLR was calculated for sites in the 40 kb window with a 20 kb step size by using xpclr software [23]. Genotype data were phased using Beagle (v5.1), and cross-population extended haplotype homozygosity (XP-EHH) was computed for individual SNPs using selscan (v1.3.0), followed by averaging within 40 kb windows [24]. Compared with allele frequency–based statistics such as Fst and Pi ratio, haplotype-based methods (e.g., XP-EHH) and linkage disequilibrium (LD)–aware multi-locus approaches (e.g., XP-CLR) generally have greater power to detect positive selection [25, 26]. Therefore, following previous studies, more stringent thresholds were applied to XP-EHH and XP-CLR [27, 28]. Windows exceeding the top 5% of Fst and Pi ratio values, and the top 1% of XP-CLR and |XP-EHH| scores, were defined as candidate regions. These SSA regions were used to screen candidate genes according to the annotation file gff3, and the genes overlapped at least two methods were considered as the final candidate genes.

Allele frequencies and correlation analysis

Allele frequencies of SNPs were calculated for each goose breed using PLINK (option “--freq”). Correlation analyses between allele frequencies and environmental variables were performed in R using the “cor” function. The geographic distribution of allele frequencies for SNPs located within candidate genes was visualized across breeds locations on a map.

Results

Population structure and genomic diversity

In this study, we downloaded 155 and generated 137 WGS datasets of 14 Chinese and 2 European geese breeds, with an average 13.7 × sequencing depths. Analysis of the WGS data detected 22.1 M SNPs, after filtering, we obtained 13 M high-quality SNPs.

Based on the SNP data, we performed the PCA, NJ-tree and Admixture to reveal the population structure of 16 goose breeds (Fig. 1). The PCA and NJ-tree based on the genetic distance of 292 geese revealed that 16 goose breeds were divided into two groups, the first was located in the Greylag branch, including YL, RM and LD. The second was located in the Swan branch, including YJ, MG, WZ, XP, WG, ST, GF, WX, HY, ZI, SC, LX and SC (Fig. 1C, E). These two branches were also separated by Admixture analysis at K = 2 (Fig. 1F). The PCA result (PC2 and PC3) also showed a weak sub-structure based on geographic closeness among 14 Chinese indigenous geese breeds (Fig. 1C, D).

The results of genetic diversity showed that the Greylag geese (LD and RM) had higher LD level and average ROH, and lower Ho, He and Pi levels than Swan geese. Among Chinese indigenous geese, YL had the highest LD decay distance (r2= 0.19) and lowest average ROH levels (1.16 kb). We found that WZ had the lowest genomic diversity in all Chinese indigenous geese (Swan geese), as reflected in having the lowest Ho (0.15), He (0.20) and Pi (2.1 × 10− 3) (Fig. 1G-J, Table S4).

Habitat suitability and the important environmental drivers

In this study, the agro-climatic variables included 24 climatic variables, 8 soil variables and 3 vegetation variables from public databases (Table S3). For performing ENM, we first removed those 35 variables that were correlated (rs > 0.7, removed one from each correlated group) and /or with the contribution of less than 4% (Fig.S2-S3). It retained only five variables: elevation (Ele), precipitation of driest month (BIO14), grass cover (Grass), forest cover (Forest) and proportion of cultivated land (Landuse).

The final model with five selected variables performed an estimate of the relative contribution of the variables (Fig. 2A). Ele showed the largest contribution (47.7%), followed by Bio14 (17.5%), Landuse (14.5%), and Grass (12.5%). Forest showed the smallest contribution (7.8%). PCA based on five selected variables spread the Chinese indigenous geese in the environmental space, showing the heterogeneity in Chinese agro-climatic conditions (Fig. 2B). The habitat suitability maps produced by ENM also suggested that the 14 Chinese indigenous geese have different niches (Fig. 2C).

Genomic analyses identify candidate genes for environmental adaptation

After identifying five key environmental drivers of selection, two types of analyses (selection signature analysis and genotype-environment association analysis) were performed to determine the genetic basis of adaptation to these factors.

Redundancy analysis (RDA) was used to perform a genotype-environment association analysis (GEA). In RDA, a genome-wide LD-filtered SNP dataset was the response variable, and the five environmental drivers were as explanatory variables. The overall RDA model was highly significant (permutation analysis P-value < 0.001) with five axes and 6.59% explanation of the total genetic variance (Fig. 2D, E). The SNP loadings on each RDA axis showed a normal distribution (Fig.S8). SNPs from the two extreme ends of the loading distribution (SD > 3.5) at each axis were taken as outliers (n = 7,620 in total), which were associated with the niche adaptation (Table S5). The correlation values were generally low to moderate, ranging between 0.05 and 0.64 (median = 0.33) (Fig. 2F, G). Since we performed RDA using the SNP dataset filtered by linkage disequilibrium, some actual causal variants may have been excluded. Therefore, we then added some variants that were in complete linkage disequilibrium with the outliers (r2 = 1). This added another 244 SNPs to the candidate list, taking the total to 7,864. From these SNPs, 4516 protein-coding genes were annotated. The top 10 KEGG enrichment results showed the importance of the metabolic pathways and the MAPK signaling pathway in environmental adaptation of geese (Fig. 2H, Table S6). Subsequently, we identified SNPs and genes associated with five key environmental drivers. The number of SNPs and genes of five environmental drivers were 634 SNPs in Ele with 451 genes, 1840 SNPs in Bio14 with 893 genes, 2,392 SNPs in Forest with 1,238 genes, 1,657 SNPs in Grass with 784 genes and 1,340 SNPs in Landuse with 790 genes (Table S5).

In SSA, Fst, Piratio, XP-CLR and XPEHH were performed by comparing extreme groups of population (Low group vs. High group) for each environmental factor (Table 1). Fst, Piratio, XP-CLR calculation was performed with a sliding window size of 40 kb and a step size of 20 kb. For Fst and Piratio, the top 5% windows were considered as candidate regions. In Ele, 1132, 653, 1051 and 503 genes, were identified by Fst, XP-EHH, Pitatio and XP-CLR respectively (Table S7). In BIO14, 1130, 668, 1264 and 292 genes, were identified by Fst, XP-EHH, Pitatio and XP-CLR respectively (Table S7). In Forest, 1203, 650, 1686 and 320 genes, were identified by Fst, XP-EHH, Pitatio and XP-CLR respectively (Table S7). In Grass, 1262, 714, 1229 and 360 genes, were identified by Fst, XP-EHH, Pitatio and XP-CLR respectively (Table S7). In Landuse, 1453, 690, 1699 and 357 genes, were identified by Fst, XP-EHH, Pitatio and XP-CLR respectively (Table S7). The candidate genes were identified at least two SSA methods. The final SSA candidate genes in Ele, BIO14, Forest, Grass and Landuse were 815, 457, 477, 671 and 576, respectively (Table S8). In SSA, a total of 1984 genes were associated with five key environmental drivers, the KEGG enrichment results showed that these genes were mainly enriched in the metabolic pathway, MAPK signaling pathway and regulation of actin cytoskeleton (Fig. 2I, Table S9). By conducting a combined analysis of the KEGG pathways enriched in both RDA and SSA, the results revealed that there were 134 signaling path ways commonly enriched in both RDA and SSA, and the results also highlighted the importance of the metabolic pathway and MAPK signaling pathway in environmental adaptation (Fig. 2J, K, Table S10).

Adaptation to high altitude

The high-altitude areas have extreme environments consisting of hypoxia, low temperature and strong ultraviolet radiation. Livestock inhabiting high-altitude regions undergo huge selection pressure, potentially evolving adaptations to hypoxic environments, modifying energy metabolism, and resisting ultraviolet radiation [29–31]. By combining the results of SSA and RDA, we found 20 overlapping candidate genes in Ele (Fig. 3A, B). By performing the correlation analysis within the 20 genes across different goose populations, we observed that the allele frequencies of candidate adaptive SNPs of 9 genes showed a significant correlation and similar geographic distribution with altitude (P < 0.05) (Table 2, Table S11). For example, Fig. 3G-H showed the allele frequencies distribution of candidate adaptive SNP located in the intron region of CTIF (NW_025927781.1:2823686), with significant correlation with altitude (rs = 0.628, P = 0.018) (Fig. 3G-H) (Table S11). The G allele was mainly distributed in the high-altitude region, whereas the A allele was almost fixed in low altitude region (Fig. 3H). We then performed the selective sweep analysis of the CTIF gene. Compared to the low group (MG and SH), the selective sweep within CTIF region exhibited lower Pi and Tajima’D values in the high group (YL and SC) (Fig. 3I, J). These results suggested the potential adaptive role of candidate genes under high-altitude pressure.

Genes detected by both SSA and RDA in relation to environmental adaptations

Short-term drought adaptation

The precipitation of driest month (BIO14) showed a strong positive correlation with the precipitation of driest quarter (BIO17, rs=0.97) and the precipitation of the coldest quarter (BIO19, rs=0.98) (Fig.S9). This suggested that there are differences in the abundance of water between the low group (ZI and YL) and the high group (GF and LX). Combining the results of SSA and RDA, we found 53 overlapping candidate genes in BIO14 (Fig. 4A, B). By performing the correlation analysis within the 53 genes across different goose populations, we observed that the allele frequencies of candidate adaptive SNPs of 25 genes showed a significant correlation and similar geographic distribution with BIO14 (P < 0.05) (Table 2, Table S11). For example, Fig. 4G-H showed the allele frequencies distribution of candidate adaptive SNP located in the intron region of ARMH3 (NW_025927678.1:1912579), with significant correlation with BIO14 (Table S11). In the candidate SNP of ARMH3, the C allele was mainly distributed in the high-water source region, whereas the T allele was mainly distributed in the low water source region (Fig. 4H). The selective sweep analysis showed that the ARMH3 region exhibited lower Pi and Tajima’D values in the low group than the high group (Fig. 4I, J). These results suggested the potential adaptive role of candidate genes under short-term drought pressure.

Disease resistance - adaptation to the forest

Forest exhibits remarkable specific diversity, which also coexists with a diverse array of pathogens and disease vectors [72, 73]. Combining the results of SSA and RDA, we found 53 overlapping candidate genes in Forest (Fig.S10A, B). By performing the correlation analysis within the 53 genes across different goose populations, we observed that the allele frequencies of candidate adaptive SNPs of 9 genes showed a significant correlation and similar geographic distribution with Forest (P < 0.05) (Table S11). Fig.S10 G-H showed the allele frequencies distribution of candidate adaptive SNP located in the intron region of the PI4KA gene (NW_025927689.1:4202221), with significant correlation with Forest (rs = 0.651, P = 0.014) (Table S11). The C allele was mainly distributed in the high forest cover region, whereas the T allele was mainly distributed in the low region (Fig.S10H). The selective sweep analysis within the PI4KA region exhibited lower Pi and Tajima’D values in the high group than the low group (Fig.S10I, J). These results suggested the potential adaptive role of candidate genes under high forest cover pressure.

Grass and Landuse –are important determinants of food abundance

As herbivorous waterfowl, geese rely on grass and by-products from the harvesting and processing of cultivated grains or crops as important food sources for geese. These mean that the population living in the areas with low landuse cover or low grass cover is likely to have limited access to supplementation from crop residues and grass grains. This may enable geese to develop different digestive adaptations, metabolic patterns and food-seeking adaptabilities.

In the environmental predictor of Grass, combining the results of SSA and RDA, we found 64 overlapping candidate genes in Grass (Fig.S11A, B). We observed that the allele frequencies of candidate adaptive SNPs of 31 genes showed a significant correlation with Grass (P < 0.05) (Table S11). Fig.S11G-H showed the allele frequencies of candidate adaptive SNPs located in the intron region of IQSEC1 (NW_025927815.1:1384693), with significant correlation with the geographical distribution of Grass (rs = 0.680, P = 0.010) (Table S11). The C allele was mainly distributed in the high grass cover region, whereas the T allele was mainly distributed in the low region (Fig.S11G, Table S11). Compared to the high group, the selective sweep within the IQSEC1 region exhibited lower Pi and Tajima’D values in the low group (Fig.S11I, J). These results suggested the potential adaptive role of candidate genes under grass scarcity pressure.

In the environmental predictor of Landuse, a total of 43 overlapping genes were found by combining the results of SSA and RDA (Fig. 5A, B). These genes are involved in a variety of biological processes (Table 2). The allele frequencies of candidate adaptive SNPs of 22 genes showed a significant correlation with Landuse (P < 0.05) (Table S11). For example, Fig. 5G-H displayed the geographical distribution of allele frequencies of the SNP located in the intron region of the LRP1B gene (NW_025927686.1:3121831) (rs = 0.732, P = 0.004) (Table S11). The C allele was mainly distributed in the high Landuse cover region, whereas the A allele was mainly distributed in the low region (Fig. 5H, Table S11). Compared to the low group (YL and ST), the selective sweep within LRP1B region exhibited lower Pi and Tajima’D values in high group (ZI and GF) (Fig. 5I, J).

It is interesting that the feather color of Chinese indigenous geese (except YL) has a similar distribution pattern to Landuse cover. Chinese indigenous geese mainly have gray or white feathers (YL goose have white, gray and pied feather color), and some breeds have both gray and white feather colors (XP and WG) (Table S1). Our result showed that there was highly correlation between feather color and landuse cover (Spearman correlation = 0.711, P = 0.006) (Fig. 5K), with white color mainly distributed in high-landuse cover region, whereas gray color located in low-landuse cover region (Fig. 5L). We further carried out SSA on different feather colors (gray group vs. white group). With SSA for feather color, 141 candidate genes were identified. Of these candidate genes, the region of the LRP1B gene also showed a strong selection for feather color (Fig. 5N-P).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the population structure, genetic diversity and environmental adaptation mechanisms of 14 Chinese indigenous goose breeds. Our results were similar to previous studies that European domestic geese and YL goose were located in the Greylag geese, whereas other Chinese indigenous geese were located in the Swan geese [74, 75]. We observed that Chinese geese exhibit higher genetic diversity than European geese. LD and RM are utilized for foie gras, meat production and feather yields under strong artificial selection. The high genetic diversity in Chinese geese reflected the relatively low artificial selection process. Among Chinese indigenous geese, we found that WZ had the lowest genetic diversity. A national resource census indicated that the rearing of WZ has been gradually decreasing [5]. The low genetic diversity of WZ may be due to the small population size of this breed or inbreeding. This also indicated that an urgent need for additional conservation measures is required to safeguard this important genetic resource.

Compared with exotic breeds, indigenous livestock usually show superior adaptation to their local environments and have developed distinct adaptive characteristics. However, agro-climatic environments pose complex selective pressures, which make it extremely difficult to analyze the genetic basis of environmental adaptation. In this study, we performed ENM to identify the key agro-climatic environmental drivers in the primary production regions of Chinese indigenous geese. We ultimately identified five environmental drivers with the highest contribution levels (Elevation, BIO14, Forest cover, Grass cover and Landuse cover), and the genetic basis of environmental adaptation was identified by combining with SSA and RDA. We found that these adaptive genes were mainly enriched in the MAPK pathway and metabolic pathways. MAPK pathway has been proven to it can regulate the protein-protein interactions, cellular function and cell cycle to adapt the abiotic stress responses [76, 77]. Previous study revealed that sheep adapted the desertification or semi-desertification environments by metabolic pathways [78]. Our results further revealed the important role of these pathways in the process of environmental adaptation. In addition, we noticed that most of the SNPs related to these environmental drivers are located in the non-coding regions of candidate genes, including downstream and upstream of the candidate gene (12.5%), intronic region (80.8%) and 3’ UTRs and 5’ UTRs (2.5%). In the relevant studies on environmental adaptability, economic traits in other species and humans, non-coding variations in most candidate genes were also found [79–82]. This suggested that regulatory evolution, rather than amino acid substitutions, may be the primary driver of local adaptation under environmental selection pressures.

Extreme environments pose a huge challenge to animals, which will undergo strong selective pressure. The mechanisms of livestock adapted to the extremely high-altitude environment have always been a research hotspot. In the current study, we found 9 key candidate genes identified with both SSA and RDA, and displayed significant correlation with altitude. These genes were involved in the high-altitude environmental adaptation (Table 2). For example, SATB1 involved in inflammation and immune responses and related process, which was located in the strong selective region in Tibetans and Tibetan Chickens [83, 84]. The expression of NTRK2 in lung was highly increased and showed a significant correlation with diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide during the high-altitude hypoxia conditions (~ 9000 m.a.s.l) [85]. ESR1 mediate activation of the eNOS-NO-cGMP pathway to use the higher levels of circulatory nitric oxide that facilitates high-altitude acclimatization [86, 87]. Our study found that CTIF gene is also a key candidate gene for high-altitude adaptation, which was not detected in previous high-altitude adaptation studies. CTIF mediates the non-canonical translation initiation pathway and the nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD) process by combining cap-binding complex (CBC; a heterodimer of CBP80 and 20) – dependent translation (CT) [88, 89]. Under long-term hypoxia conditions, 4E-BP and 4E-T will be activated to block the classical translation initiation pathway. CT is insensitive to 4E-BP activity and can support the translation of mRNAs under stress conditions (hypoxia and serum-starved conditions) when the classical translation initiation pathway is impaired [89–91]. This indicated that CTIF can mediate a non-classical translation pathway to adapt to the high-altitude with low-oxygen environment. And CTIF can serve as a new candidate gene for high altitude adaptation. These results suggest that the diffusing capacity of the lung and translation pathway regulation play potential roles in long-term adaptation to high-altitude hypoxia.

In addition to high altitudes, another extreme environment, drought, also exerts strong environmental selection pressure. We found 25 key candidate genes identified in both SSA and RDA. These genes displayed significant correlation with short-term drought adaptation (Table 2). For example, ARMH3 plays an important role in maintaining the integrity of the Golgi apparatus structure as well as in material transport and secretion [92–94]. During the water shortage, the Golgi can transport and release antidiuretic hormone to maintain the internal environmental balance [95–97]. This indicated that ARMH3 could potentially influence the release of ADH via the Golgi to adapt to the short-term drought environment. The increase in osmotic pressure and blood viscosity caused by water deficiency will increase the risk of thrombosis and damage to vascular endothelial cells. RASA3 is involved in endothelial cell adhesion and vascular lumen integrity by Rap1-dependent mechanism [98], and maintains the osmotic pressure stability by regulating calcium channel activity [Uniprot]. Drought will cause the oxidative stress of livestock [99], whereas BDH2 can protect cells against free radical-induced oxidative stress [Uniprot]. These results suggested that geese adapt to the short-term drought via regulating osmotic pressure and against oxidative stress.

In the environmental driver Forest, we found that most of the candidate genes overlapping by SSA and RDA were involved in the immune response. For example,

COL5A1 can promote tumor cell-macrophage crosstalk [41]. ULK4 inhibited autophagy and inflammatory responses [Uniprot]. PI4KA participates in the migration of immune cells and the initiation of immune responses [42–44]. High forest cover regions exhibit greater species richness, but they also serve as a breeding ground for pathogens and microorganisms. Previous studies have shown that forest cover is positively correlated with the spatial variation in malaria prevalence among chimpanzees [100]. SNPs inferred to be under positive selection in forests were enriched for pathogen-related genes [73]. A lower forest coverage increases the predation risk, and animals may develop more acute responses in order to improve their survival rate [101]. These indicated that geese inhabiting different forest coverage may exhibit disease resistance and neural sensitivities. In the environmental drivers of Forest, these immune-related genes could potentially explain variations in stress resistance among goose breeds, which is of great significance for the development of excellent stress-resistant breeds in the future.

In addition to the extreme environments, the abundance of food in the environment exerts selective pressure on animals [102]. Among the five environmental drivers screened in this study, two are related to the food intake of geese (Grass and Landuse). As herbivorous poultry, geese are mainly fed on grass and cereals. Different grass and landuse coverage mean differences in supplementation with crop residues, grain and grass, which may lead to differences in the digestive system and metabolism. TBC1D14 plays a role in the regulation of starvation-induced autophagosome formation [54]. GRK5 is required for adipocyte differentiation [103]. IQSEC1 controls a guanine nucleotide exchange factor for the ARF subfamily and ARF6 [49, 50]. ARF1 maintains intestinal homeostasis [104, 105], and ATF6 triggers microbial dysbiosis [106]. This suggested that IQSEC1 has a potential role in the adaptability of grass coverage by regulating intestinal or microbial homeostasis for adaptation through ARF1 and ARF6. Some candidate genes of Landuse were also found that involved in the metabolism process. FGGY influence dietary obesity by modulating lipid metabolism [65]. LRP1B gene is involved in the regulation of lipid metabolism [107, 108]. These results indicated that the adaptation of different food types may be regulated by the efficiency of lipid metabolism.

Interestingly, we noticed that the feather color of Chinese indigenous geese (except YL) also has a similar distribution pattern to Landuse. And the overlapping gene between Landuse and feather color was LRP1B. Previous studies have shown that human impacts are altering the evolution of animals. Deforestation has underpinned repeated selection at the ebony locus controlling color polymorphism in insects [109]. Human light pollution prolonged birds’ vocal activity by 50 min [110]. The feather color variability encouraged and maintained by humans in domestic plants and animals may be due to the result of selection pressure [111]. The coloring process of bird feathers requires the participation of lipids. For example, carotenoids related to feather color formation are fat-soluble pigments and must ultimately be derived from the diet. Their absorption, transport and deposition depend on lipoprotein complexes. A study in the red-backed fairywren showed that a gene module was correlated with back feather color, which was enriched in lipid metabolic functions, and some lipid processing genes for carotenoid coloration were detected [112]. In the study of crested ibis, the lipids contribute to an oil base, which enhances the black substance on the feather surfaces [113]. In addition, LRP1B has been proven to be related to the formation of melanoma [63, 64]. These findings implied that the process of feather color differentiation in Chinese indigenous geese might be associated with human agricultural practices, and LRP1B may play a role in regulating the development of various feather colors, but the precise regulatory mechanisms remain to be explored.

Regrettably, several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, although the allele frequency-environment correlation analysis adds some support, all conclusions about environmental adaptation are based solely on genomic inferences. We did not have any experimental support (e.g., qRT‑PCR, RNA‑seq) for the candidate genes identified. By integrating SSA and RNA-seq, previous study had shown that Tibetan sheep adapt to the plateau environment by enhancing cardiopulmonary function, regulating body fluid balance via renal reabsorption, and modifying nutrient digestion and absorption pathways [114]. Second, our study did not include phenotypic data, which would have enabled us to identify the specific economic phenotypes associated with the genetic basis of environmental adaptation. A study of African indigenous chickens showed that the solar radiation above 18,000 kJ/m2/d has a negative effect on female body weight by combining phenotypic distribution models (PDMs) and ENM [115]. Another study identified outlier SNPs associated with environmental and phenotypic variation in Ethiopian indigenous chickens via RDA [116]. Integrated genome and other omics, and /or linking genomic data with environmental and phenotypic variation can provide a complete picture of local adaptive variation. This will be more conducive to understanding the geese’s responses to environmental variables and their effects on economic traits, and developing better breeding strategies.

In summary, our study analyzed the population genetic structure and genetic diversity of 14 Chinese goose breeds. ENM revealed that these goose breeds exhibited different niches, and five main environmental drivers were identified, including two extreme environments (altitude and short-term drought), two variables as proxies of food availability (Grass and Landuse), and one variable as a proxy of disease resistance (Forest). By integrating WGS data, the candidate genes related to environmental adaptability were identified. These genes may underlie phenotypic variation and adaptation, and have important implications for the conservation, genetic resource development and breeding strategies for local goose breeds.
