Pulmonary mucormycosis and Achromobacter xylosoxidans co-infection in an immunocompromised host: a case report
Authors: Balkrishna Subedi, Naveen Gautam, Anuja Upadhyay, Manish Sharma, Daniel Bitetto
Published: 2025

Introduction

Invasive fungal infections, particularly mucormycosis caused by Mucorales, represent a severe threat to immunocompromised individuals, characterized by aggressive angioinvasion and high mortality[1]. While various clinical forms exist, pulmonary mucormycosis (PM) poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, often exacerbated by underlying conditions like poorly controlled diabetes mellitus which impairs host defenses and promotes fungal growth[2–4]. The clinical presentation frequently mimics common pulmonary diseases, demanding a high index of suspicion for timely intervention[2].

The complexity of managing PM escalates dramatically when complicated by co-infections. While concurrent infections with common pathogens like Aspergillus are known, especially during viral pandemics[5], the co-occurrence of PM with rarer opportunistic bacteria adds significant diagnostic and therapeutic hurdles. Achromobacter xylosoxidans, an emerging pathogen in compromised hosts[6,7], represents one such challenging co-infecting agent, potentially masking or worsening the underlying fungal process, particularly as bacterial co-infections may occur in over half of mucormycosis cases[8].

This report details a fatal case of PM co-infected with A. xylosoxidans in a patient with severe type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), whose hospital course was further complicated by a subsequent SARS-CoV-2 superinfection. We aim to elucidate the clinical presentation, diagnostic difficulties, and challenging management of this multifaceted infectious process. This case highlights the critical need for clinical vigilance, rapid diagnostics, and aggressive multidisciplinary care in managing invasive fungal infections and their complex interplay with bacterial co-pathogens and viral illnesses in vulnerable patient populations. This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the SCARE 2025 guidelines[9] to ensure accuracy, transparency, and usefulness.

Case presentation

A 55-year-old female with a significant past medical history including T2DM on insulin therapy, a history of both asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), with pulmonary function tests from 2016 suggesting severe obstructive changes consistent with a predominant COPD phenotype (Asthma-COPD Overlap), heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (<30%), non-ischemic cardiomyopathy status post implantable cardioverter-defibrillator, hypertension, and stage 4 chronic kidney disease presented to the emergency department (ED) complaining of dyspnea. The patient had recently visited the ED for similar symptoms, showing temporary improvement with corticosteroids and inhaled albuterol, which led to a diagnosis of mild asthma exacerbation. Subsequently, her primary care physician increased her prednisone dosage due to persistent symptoms. However, approximately 5 days later, the patient returned to the ED with worsening symptoms. Upon arrival, she reported persistent dyspnea for 3 weeks, fatigue, a productive cough with yellow sputum, and associated chest pain. She specifically denied nausea, vomiting, light-headedness, or diarrhea. The remaining review of systems was negative.

Her medication regimen at presentation included aspirin, atorvastatin, carvedilol, digoxin, hydralazine, sacubitril/valsartan, budesonide/formoterol, ipratropium/albuterol, and insulin.

On physical examination, vital signs were notable for a temperature of 36.2°C, heart rate of 102 beats per minute, blood pressure of 166/77 mmHg, respiratory rate of 24 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 95% on room air. The patient was alert, oriented to person, place, and time, and in no acute distress. Chest auscultation revealed mild wheezing and bilateral rales, more pronounced on the right side. The cardiac examination was notable for a regular rate and rhythm with normal S1 and S2 heart sounds. No jugular venous distention, ankle edema, or skin rashes were observed.

Initial laboratory investigations revealed several significant abnormalities, most notably severe hyperglycemia (glucose 927 mg/dL), hyponatremia (115 mmol/L, corrected for hyperglycemia: 128 mmol/L), and elevated creatinine at 2.8 mg/dL (baseline 1.5 mg/dL). The patient’s hospital course involved several transfers dictated by her clinical needs. She was initially admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for management of hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state. On day 3, she was transferred to another facility for an urgent bronchoscopy. Upon her return, she was admitted to the step-down unit but deteriorated, experiencing a pulseless electrical activity (PEA) cardiac arrest on day 4, which necessitated re-admission to the medical ICU (MICU).

Given the patient’s profound immunocompromised state from uncontrolled diabetes, the differential diagnosis for her cavitary pneumonia was broad, and her clinical condition fluctuated significantly throughout her admission (Table 2). The initial empirical antibiotic regimen of ceftriaxone and doxycycline was initiated to cover typical and atypical community-acquired pneumonia, with the plan to tailor therapy based on definitive microbiological data. To rule out other etiologies, a comprehensive diagnostic workup was initiated. An echocardiogram was performed to investigate for endocarditis, which revealed no valvular vegetations, and multiple blood cultures remained negative. The suspicion of endocarditis was therefore low. For the pulmonary infection, a bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was chosen over less-invasive sputum or serum tests to obtain a definitive sample. Two acid-fast bacilli stains from the BAL were negative, and a culture was sent for weekly monitoring, lowering the suspicion of tuberculosis. Staining for Pneumocystis jirovecii was also negative. The bronchoscopy revealed a large cavitary lesion with inflammatory and atypical cells in the posterior segment of the RUL. BAL and bronchial brush cultures grew >100 000 CFU/mL Achromobacter xylosoxidans/denitrificans. Pathology confirmed acute necrotizing pneumonia with fungal elements favoring Zygomycetes (Mucor); no malignant cells were identified. Based on these findings, the antibiotic regimen was adjusted. Ceftriaxone and doxycycline were discontinued, and therapy was escalated to cefepime, guided by sensitivity testing which showed the Achromobacter isolate was susceptible. Initial antifungal treatment included isavuconazole and micafungin.

A CT scan of the neck and soft tissues revealed mucormycosis in the nasopharynx, extending to the inferior ethmoid air cells and oropharynx down to the larynx level, causing near occlusion of the upper aerodigestive tract without tracheal extension (Fig. 3). One week later, a repeat CT thorax demonstrated an interval increase in the size of the RUL cavitary lesion, now occupying almost the entire lobe. Increased wall thickness of the lesion, worsening surrounding consolidation, and ground-glass opacification were noted. Additionally, new bilateral GGOs and consolidations indicated progressing multifocal pneumonia.

The patient’s hospital course was complicated. On the fourth day of admission, she experienced a PEA cardiac arrest attributed to hypoxia secondary to hemoptysis and aspiration. She became critically ill, requiring multiple vasopressors and intubation in the MICU. Although she eventually improved, was extubated, and transferred to a general medical floor, she subsequently developed worsening cough and pulmonary congestion, tested positive for COVID-19, representing a hospital-acquired superinfection, and experienced an episode of massive hemoptysis. At this point, she was designated do-not-resuscitate/do-not-intubate and ultimately succumbed.

Discussion

This report details a complex and ultimately fatal case of PM co-infected with A. xylosoxidans in a 55-year-old female with poorly controlled T2DM and multiple other comorbidities. The case highlights the significant diagnostic challenges, therapeutic complexities, and grim prognosis associated with opportunistic infections in severely immunocompromised hosts, further complicated by a subsequent SARS-CoV-2 superinfection. Mucormycosis, an aggressive angioinvasive fungal infection caused by Mucorales, carries persistently high mortality rates despite therapeutic advances[1]. While rhino-orbito-cerebral mucormycosis (ROCM) is the most common form, particularly in diabetic populations[3], PM presents a distinct and formidable clinical challenge, recognized by the WHO as a high-priority fungal pathogen threat[4].

The patient’s profound hyperglycemia highlights uncontrolled diabetes mellitus as a critical predisposing factor. Diabetes is the most frequently cited underlying condition for mucormycosis globally[2,3,5], creating a permissive environment through impaired neutrophil and macrophage function (chemotaxis and phagocytosis), increased free iron availability favored by acidosis, and the ability of certain Mucorales like Rhizopus oryzae to thrive in high-glucose, acidic conditions via ketoreductase systems[2,3]. The initial picture of asthma exacerbation, treated with corticosteroids, likely worsened glycemic control and further suppressed immune function. This is a critical point, as corticosteroid use is strongly associated with adverse outcomes in invasive fungal diseases. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis found that corticosteroid therapy was associated with a significantly higher risk of death in patients with mucormycosis (OR: 4.19), demonstrating the profound negative impact of these agents in the setting of an aggressive fungal infection[10].

Definitive diagnosis of PM remains challenging. Clinical features are non-specific, often overlapping with other pulmonary infections[2,8]. The cavitary lesion seen in our patient is a hallmark of several infectious and non-infectious pathologies, including bacterial lung abscesses, tuberculosis, and other fungal infections like aspergillosis, making radiological differentiation difficult without a high index of suspicion[11]. This diagnostic mimicry can lead to potentially fatal delays[2]. Ultimately, diagnosis relies on the histopathological identification of characteristic broad, aseptate hyphae in tissue, as culture sensitivity is limited and can yield false negatives[3,8]. The necessity of invasive procedures like bronchoscopy with BAL and biopsy is therefore paramount for timely and accurate diagnosis in critically ill patients[12].

A crucial and devastating aspect of this case was the patient’s PEA cardiac arrest secondary to hemoptysis, a direct consequence of the angioinvasive nature of Mucorales. These fungi have a high propensity to invade pulmonary blood vessels, leading to thrombosis, tissue necrosis, and the erosion of vascular walls, which can precipitate sudden, massive, and often fatal hemoptysis[13]. The development of a thick-walled cavity, as seen in this patient, should raise alarm for this complication, as it signifies progressive necrotizing pneumonia. Ineffective or delayed antifungal therapy is a key factor that can lead to such severe complications, underscoring the poor prognosis for patients who develop massive hemoptysis[13].

A significant complicating factor was the co-infection with A. xylosoxidans. This bacterium is an opportunistic pathogen known to cause infections in patients with comorbidities like diabetes and COPD[6,7]. Its intrinsic resistance to multiple antibiotic classes presents a substantial therapeutic challenge[7]. The presence of bacterial co-infections in mucormycosis is common, occurring in over half of cases in some reviews, and may delay the diagnosis of the underlying fungal process or worsen outcomes[8]. Our therapeutic strategy, which involved escalating to targeted therapy with cefepime based on sensitivity results, reflects an appropriate, evidence-based approach to this complex polymicrobial scenario.

Effective management of mucormycosis hinges on a rapid, multi-modal strategy: prompt diagnosis, reversal of underlying risk factors (e.g., aggressive glycemic control), immediate initiation of systemic antifungal therapy, and urgent, often aggressive, surgical debridement of all necrotic tissue[1–3]. Lipid formulations of amphotericin B (L-AmB) are the cornerstone of medical therapy, preferred over deoxycholate AmB due to improved tolerability and the ability to use higher doses[1,3]. Unfortunately, our patient’s rapid deterioration precluded any surgical intervention.

Delayed initiation of AmB (>6 days) is associated with significantly increased mortality[3]. Isavuconazole and posaconazole are approved alternatives, used as first-line therapy in cases of renal impairment or AmB intolerance, or as step-down/salvage therapy[1,2]. Combination antifungal therapy (e.g., AmB plus an echinocandin or azole) has shown potential benefits in some studies, but data remain limited and sometimes contradictory[1]. Surgical resection is critical, as angioinvasion leads to thrombosis and necrosis, limiting drug penetration[1,2]. Combined medical and surgical therapy consistently demonstrates superior survival rates compared to medical therapy alone[2,3,5,10]. Early and aggressive debridement, potentially including exploration of adjacent spaces like the pterygopalatine fossa in ROCM, is vital[2]. Our patient received appropriate antifungal therapy but deteriorated too rapidly for surgical intervention.

The patient’s acquisition of SARS-CoV-2 during hospitalization added a further layer of complexity. The COVID-19 pandemic saw an unprecedented surge in mucormycosis (CAM), particularly in regions like India with high diabetes prevalence[5]. Critically ill ICU patients with COVID-19 are known to be highly susceptible to secondary bacterial and fungal infections, which are significant contributors to mortality[14]. Although PM preceded the viral illness in our patient, the subsequent superinfection undoubtedly acted as a “second hit,” worsening her already critical state and contributing to her death.

This case report has several limitations. First, its retrospective nature relies on the completeness and accuracy of the medical record. Second, due to the patient’s rapid clinical deterioration and critical illness, a detailed patient perspective on her experience could not be obtained. Finally, while we have detailed the complex interplay of infections, the precise contribution of each pathogen to the final outcome is difficult to quantify. Despite these limitations, the report provides valuable insight into the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of managing multiple, sequential infections in a highly vulnerable patient.

Conclusion

This case of PM with A. xylosoxidans and a subsequent SARS-CoV-2 superinfection in a diabetic patient illustrates how sequential opportunistic infections overwhelm compromised hosts, emphasizing the need for high clinical suspicion, prompt invasive diagnostics for fungal disease despite clinical mimicry, and attention to bacterial co-pathogens that complicate management. Despite aggressive multimodal therapy, the patient’s death reflects mucormycosis’s high mortality, a prognosis that was likely worsened by the compounding effects of critical illness and a viral superinfection. This case underscores the ongoing need for enhanced vigilance, rapid diagnostics, aggressive surgical intervention, and research into polymicrobial interactions.
