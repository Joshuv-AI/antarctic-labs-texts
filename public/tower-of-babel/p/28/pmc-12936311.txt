Heterologous prime-boost immunization of AAV-based neoantigen cancer vaccine induces anti-tumor immunity to inhibit tumor growth and relapse
Authors: Chi-Hsien Huang, Jhen-Yu Chen, Hsin-Yu Chang, Wei-Ze Hong, Yi-Wen Jiang, Chien-Yueh Lee, Chia-Hsin Wu, Yu-Sen Lin, Yuan-Yao Tsai, Wei-Ling Kao, KS Clifford Chao, Kevin Chih-Yang Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTherapeutic cancer vaccines, which induce anti-tumor immunity by targeting specific antigens, constitute a promising approach to cancer therapy. Our previous work developed a novel engineered adenovirus-associated virus 2 (AAV2)-based tumor-specific (neoantigen) cancer vaccine to boost antitumor immunity in combination with radiotherapy, resulting in tumor regression and less distant metastasis. However, the therapeutic efficacy of constitutive vector-based vaccination may be limited by poor immunogenicity, pre-existing vector-specific immunity, and vaccine-induced vector-specific immunity. Here, we examined the combinational therapeutic efficacy of AAV-based cancer vaccine, local radiotherapy, and immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) in different poorly immunogenic cancer animal models. We found that administration with AAV-based neoantigen vaccine significantly increased the response to radiotherapy and ICB, and decreased the risk of distant metastasis. Furthermore, we evaluated a heterologous prime-boost immunization strategy using two optimized AAV serotype vaccines to amplify tumor-specific immunity to neoantigens. These optimized AAV2/AAV6 neoantigen vaccine displayed strong immunogenicity with potent induction of CD8+ T cells. As combined with local radiotherapy, the prime-boost vaccine-induced superior tumor clearance and survival compared with other groups. Remarkably, optimized AAV2/AAV6 vaccination promoted CD8+ T-cell infiltration in the tumors and elicited the enrichment of T-cell clones. Furthermore, exhausted T-cell marker expression was significantly decreased in the tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells. Taken together, these results highlight the synergistic potency of engineered AAV2/AAV6 vaccines combined with local radiotherapy for poorly immunogenic cancers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-026-04336-9.Keywords: Adenovirus-associated virus, Cancer vaccine, Tumor microenvironment, Immunotherapy, Radiotherapy

Introduction

Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has transformed cancer treatment strategies, highlighting the potential of immunotherapy for cancer management. However, the success of ICB therapy is highly associated with the tumor-mutational burden (TMB) and the recruitment of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes [1, 2], making it largely ineffective for poorly immunogenic tumors such as microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer (MSS-CRC), triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), and pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PDAC). Indeed, clinical results indicated that ICB therapy is only effective for approximately 12.5% of patients [3]. Thus, there is an urgent need to develop novel effective immunotherapeutic strategies for poorly immunogenic tumors, such as therapeutic cancer vaccine.

Therapeutic cancer vaccines represent a powerful immunotherapeutic approach by inducing polyclonal tumor-specific CD8+ T cells to eradicate cancer cells [4] and sensitizing poorly immunogenic tumors to ICB therapy [5–8], such as PROVENGE [9] and mRNA-based therapeutic cancer vaccines [10]. Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors, traditionally utilized in gene therapy for stable transgene expression in inherited diseases [11], have recently gained prominence in vaccine delivery and immunotherapy due to the discovery of new serotypes and advancements in vector bioengineering [12–14], such as COVID-19 and cancer vaccines [15–17]. Attractively, AAV6 exhibits the ability to directly target antigen-presenting cells (APCs), thereby enhancing antigen presentation and eliciting robust cytolytic antitumor activity [18, 19]. Furthermore, capsid modifications of AAV6 have been shown to amplify these immune responses by enhancing antigen presentation, facilitating the eradication of tumor cells and demonstrating the potential of AAV-based platforms for cancer immunotherapy [14, 17–19]. However, the clinical efficacy of AAV-based therapeutic vaccines may be hindered by pre-existing anti-vector immunity and the induction of neutralizing antibodies following repeated immunizations [5, 20, 21].

Neoantigen (neoAg)-based cancer vaccines have demonstrated significant potential in eliciting effective T-cell responses [22, 23] and synergizing with ICB therapy across various preclinical and clinical settings [24–26]. Such induction of neoAg-specific immunity has been successfully observed even in challenging malignancies like glioblastoma and pancreatic cancer [27, 28]. Our previous studies developed and optimized a novel AAV2 vector co-expressing a PD-1 blocker and a viral clearance inhibitor, which enhances neoAg expression and activates robust CD8+ T-cell responses [29–31]. This AAV2-based platform showed therapeutic efficacy in eradicating cancer cells and providing protective immunity against recurrence and metastasis when combined with radiotherapy [29, 31].

In the present study, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of engineered AAV2-based neoAg vaccine in combination with ICBs as well as radiotherapy in poorly immunogenic models, including MSS-CRC and TNBC. Our findings indicate that this vaccine significantly delays tumor growth and reduces metastatic risk. Furthermore, we demonstrated its immunogenicity and antitumor efficacy in a mouse lung cancer model, where the vaccine boosted CD8+ TIL infiltration and sensitized tumors to ICB therapy. Additionally, we optimized a novel AAV6 vector for an AAV2/AAV6 heterologous prime-boost vaccination strategy to overcome the limitations of vector-specific immunity. This approach not only overcomes the barriers of neutralizing antibodies but also enhances long-term memory responses and eradicates distant abscopal tumors. Taken together, our results suggest that this optimized multi-modal strategy provides a superior therapeutic efficacy to overcome the limitation of AAV2-specific neutralizing antibody to achieve durable tumor control and prevent tumor relapse.

Results

AAV2-based cancer vaccines combined with radiotherapy and ICBs to suppress tumor growth in poorly immunogenic CRC and TNBC

Our previous studies developed an engineered AAV-based neoantigen cancer vaccine (AAVax), which co-expresses multiple neoantigens and a PD-1/PD-L1 blockade module, enhancing the efficacy of local radiotherapy (RT) in two poorly immunogenic models CT26 (MSS-CRC) and 4T1 (TNBC) [29]. In this study, we further evaluated whether AAVax could sensitize these poorly immunogenic tumors to RT and ICBs. We employed a moderate-dose RT regimen (8 Gy for three consecutive days), a protocol previously shown to elicit superior antitumor immunity in poorly immunogenic settings [32, 33]. Following tumor inoculation in the right back legs of BALB/c mice, a combination therapy was administered consisting of six doses of AAV2-based neoAg vaccines (1 × 108 vg/dose, i.m. injection), local RT (8 Gy × 3), and anti-mouse PD-1 antibodies (100 μg/mouse, i.p. injection, Fig. 1A). In the MSS-CRC model, we found that repeated AAVax slightly delayed tumor volume, suggesting that AAVax vaccination alone was insufficient to decrease tumor volume. But the tumor volumes were significantly reduced in both the AAVax/RT and AAVax/RT/anti-PD1 groups compared to the AAV-Vec control (p < 0.05, Fig. 1B, C). Notably, the complete response (CR) rate reached 33.33% in the AAVax/RT group and 66.67% in the AAVax/RT/anti-PD1 group, with tumor-free survival maintained for at least 100 days. Consistent with our previous studies [29], these results demonstrate that AAVax and local RT lead to substantial tumor regression and markedly enhance the therapeutic response to ICBs (Fig. 1B). Analysis of the resected tumors revealed a significant increase in the density of tumor-infiltrating CD3+, GzmB+ T cells, and GzmB+CD8+ T cells (Fig. 1C, D). Similar outcomes were observed in the TNBC model, where AAVax and RT increased both the ICB response (Fig. 1E) and the infiltration of CD3+, GzmB+ T cells, and GzmB+CD8+ T cells (Fig. 1F, G). Furthermore, the occurrence of lung metastases (Fig. 2A–C) and the risk of liver metastases (Fig. 2D, E) were significantly lower in the AAVax/RT/anti-PD1 group compared to all other groups, indicating potent inhibition of spontaneous metastasis [34]. Taken together, these results suggest that the engineered AAVax platform synergizes with RT and ICBs to suppress tumor growth and distant metastasis by remodeling the tumor microenvironment (TME) and eliciting systemic antitumor immunity.

Neoantigen vaccine combined with radiotherapy synergistically enhances ICB response in lung cancer

Combinations of cancer vaccines and ICB represent a potent strategy for treating ALK-driven non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [35, 36]. We then investigated whether AAVax combined with RT could enhance the response to ICBs in a syngeneic mouse model using ASB-XIV lung cancer cells, which have previously been reported as initial responsive to anti-PD1 antibodies [35, 37]. To identify candidate mutation-associated neoantigens, we first performed whole-exome sequencing (WES) and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to analyze the genomic profiles of resected ASB-XIV tumors (Fig. 3A). WES analysis identified 1,453 missense mutations. By integrating RNA-seq data, we determined which of these mutated genes were expressed in ASB-XIV cells, identifying 407 missense mutations with an FPKM > 1. These genes were translated in silico, and 8-, 9-, and 10-mer peptides containing the mutated amino acids were generated. We then used NetMHCpan to predict their binding affinities to H-2Kd or Dd molecules. We selected ten neoantigens that met two criteria: an estimated MHC binding affinity (IC50) < 500 nM and a binding affinity higher than that of their corresponding wild-type peptides. Subsequently, we developed an AAVax vaccine carrying nine of these neoantigens (AAVax-ASB, Table 1) and tested its therapeutic efficacy in BALB/c mice (Fig. 3B).

Potential neoantigens in mouse lung carcinoma ASB-XIV cell line

The antitumor efficacy of the AAVax-ASB vaccine was evaluated using a therapeutic experimental design. BALB/c mice were subcutaneously injected with 5 × 105 ASB-XIV cells in the right leg; four days later, they received AAVax-ASB, local RT, and anti-PD1 antibodies according to the indicated schedule (Fig. 3B). As shown in Fig. 3C, AAVax-ASB vaccination significantly delayed tumor growth (p < 0.01). Furthermore, the antitumor activity of the vaccine was significantly enhanced by RT, with 16.67% of mice (1/6) achieving complete response. Notably, this combination therapy remarkably sensitized the tumors to ICB, resulting in significantly delayed tumor growth (p < 0.001, Fig. 3C) and a substantially higher CR rate of approximately 66.67% (~ 66.67%, Fig. 3D). These results demonstrate that AAV-based neoantigen vaccination enhances the response to ICBs in ASB-XIV cancers (Fig. 3C and D).

We also characterized the immune cell profiles within resected tumors using flow cytometry (gating strategies shown in Fig. S1). The frequency of tumor-infiltrating CD4+ T lymphocytes (TILs) was significantly increased in the AAVax-ASB/RT/anti-PD1 group (Fig. 3E and Fig. S1A). Similarly, the density of CD8+ TILs was remarkably higher following AAVax-ASB administration (Fig. 3F and Fig. S1A), and the triple combination (AAVax-ASB/RT/anti-PD1) further increased this population (Fig. 3F). Additionally, we observed a significant expansion of CD4+ and CD8+ effector/memory T-cell responses (Fig. 3G, H and Fig. S1A). Cytotoxic IFNγ+CD8+ T cells were also significantly induced in the triple-therapy group (AAVax-ASB/RT/anti-PD1, Fig. 4A and Fig. S1B). Consistent with these flow cytometric findings, the frequencies of CD3+, GzmB+ and GzmB+CD8+ T cells were significantly elevated in the AAVax-ASB/RT/anti-PD1 group (Fig. 4B–E), suggesting that AAVax-ASB effectively triggers activated T-cell responses and enhances the efficacy of ICB.

Because ASB-XIV tumors eventually unresponsive to PD1 blockade despite a high mutational load [37], we hypothesized that the expression of exhaustion markers on CD8+ TILs might limit the expansion and cytotoxicity of neoantigen-specific T cells, thereby hampering antitumor activity [37]. Therefore, we evaluated the exhaustion markers PD1 and TIM3 on CD8+ TILs. We found that PD1 levels on CD8+ TILs were remarkably decreased in the AAVax-ASB/RT/anti-PD1 group (Fig. 4F and Fig. S1C), although no significant difference was observed for TIM3 levels (Fig. 4G). Collectively, these results indicate that engineered AAV-based neoantigen vaccination elicits potent cytotoxic T-cell responses and significantly enhances the therapeutic response to ICBs in lung cancer.

A heterologous AAV2/oAAV6 prime-boost strategy circumvents vector immunity to promote potent antitumor neoantigen-specific T-cell responses

A major limitation of viral-vector vaccines is the induction of vector-specific immunity, which can interfere with the immune response against the target transgene. Specifically, neutralizing antibodies (NAbs) against the common AAV2 serotype are present in over 70% of healthy donors, significantly blunting the efficacy of AAV-based therapies [38]. Since a primary objective of therapeutic cancer vaccines is to elicit a high frequency of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells, we sought to optimize the delivery vehicle. Previous reports have demonstrated that the AAV6 vector is particularly potent for targeting dendritic cells (DCs) to generate cytotoxic effector and memory T cells [17, 18]. Furthermore, optimization of the AAV6 capsid has been shown to dramatically improve DC transduction efficiency and stimulate protective immune responses [18, 19]. The optimized AAV6-S663V/T492V vector increases DC transduction efficiency approximately fivefold compared to wild-type AAV [19]. Additionally, incorporating an Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) peptide into the AAV6 capsid at residue I585 improves transduction efficiency roughly three-fold in cancer cells overexpressing integrins [39]. Given that α3β1 integrin is highly expressed in primary lung cancer cells but weakly detected in normal alveolar basement membranes [40], we generated several modified AAV6 capsids: S663V, T492V/S663V, and T492V/S663V/I585-RGD to deliver the ASB-XIV neoantigens (Fig. 5A). In vitro evaluation revealed that the triple-modified AAV6 capsid (optimized AAV6: T492V/S663V/I585-RGD) exhibited superior transduction efficacy (Fig. 5B) and significantly upregulated the maturation markers CD80 and CD86 on DCs (Fig. 5C). We then hypothesized that a heterologous prime-boost approach would elicit a more robust antitumor response than homologous vaccination; we compared homologous (AAV2/AAV2) and heterologous (AAV2/oAAV6) regimens in our mouse lung cancer model (Fig. 5D). While tumor regression was observed in the AAV2/AAV6 group, the effect was significantly more pronounced in mice receiving the AAV2/oAAV6 (optimized AAV6) vaccination (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, AAV2/oAAV6 vaccination markedly increased the infiltration of CD11c+ DCs and CD3+ T cells (Fig. 5E and F).

To further investigate the immune cell profiles within resected tumors, we isolated tumor-infiltrating immune cells for flow cytometry (gating strategies shown in Figs. S2A and S3). Flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cells confirmed that AAV2/oAAV6 heterologous vaccination resulted in higher levels of the DC maturation marker CD86 compared to other groups (Fig. 5G), though CD80 levels remained similar across groups (Fig. S2B). Notably, the frequency of CD8+ TILs was significantly increased following AAV2/oAAV6 vaccination (Fig. 6A). Within this population in CD8+ TILs, effector memory CD8+ T cells (TEM, CD44+CD62L−CD8+CD3+CD45+ live T cells) were significantly expanded (Fig. 6B and Fig. S3A), whereas CD4+ TEM (TEM, CD44+CD62L−CD4+CD3+CD45+ live T cells) frequencies remained largely unchanged (Fig. S3B). Additionally, the levels of cytotoxic markers GzmB and IFNγ in CD8+ TILs (CD8+CD3+CD45+ live T cells) were significantly upregulated by AAV2/oAAV6 vaccination (Fig. 6C, D and Fig. S3C, D). Taken together, these results indicated that combination with optimized AAV6 for heterologous AAV prime-boost vaccination remarkably increased the therapeutic efficacy and enhanced immune cell infiltration.

To further characterize the T-cell response, we analyzed T-cell receptor (TCR) diversity within the tumors. TCR sequencing yielded a substantial number of unique CDR3 sequences for both TCRα (395,399 ± 187,399, mean ± SD) and TCRβ (593,294 ± 186,696). These sequences included 15,944 ± 10,980 unique TCRα and 31,213 ± 13,751 unique TCRβ CDR3 sequences. Using the diversity index (DI) to represent clonal expansion (where lower DI indicates higher expansion), we found that the TRBV region diversity was significantly decreased after AAV2/oAAV6 vaccination (Fig. 6E). Several dominant T-cell clones were significantly enriched, suggesting a focused response against neoantigens. This was confirmed by the increased magnitude of reactive T-cell responses against the specific neopeptides mPhf3 and mGlud1 (Fig. 6F and G). Finally, heterologous AAV2/oAAV6 vaccination resulted in reduced levels of anti-AAV2 neutralizing antibodies compared to homologous vaccination (Fig. 6H–J). The transduction inhibition of AAV2-GFP was significantly decreased in the serum from heterologous AAV2/oAAV6-vaccined mice, compared to the homologous AAV2/AAV2-vaccined mice. These findings suggest that while pre-existing AAV2 immunity limits the efficacy of repeated AAV2 administration, an AAV2-prime/oAAV6-boost strategy can amplify neoantigen-specific T-cell responses and circumvent the limitations of vector-specific neutralizing antibodies.

Administration with heterologous AAV2/oAAV6 neoAg vaccination elicited systemic antitumor immunity to eradicate distant tumors in combination with radiotherapy

Finally, we evaluated whether this heterologous prime-boost vaccination strategy could enhance the efficacy of RT by eliciting systemic antitumor immunity. To test this, BALB/c mice were bilaterally implanted with subcutaneous ASB-XIV tumors. Following AAV2 priming, local RT was administered to the ipsilateral (right) tumors three times, followed by three booster vaccinations with either AAV6 or oAAV6 (Fig. 7A). In the primary irradiated tumors, we observed a CR rate of approximately 66.7% in the AAV2/oAAV6 + RT group, which was significantly higher than the 33.3% CR rate observed in the AAV2/AAV6 + RT group (Fig. 7B). Notably, the volume of non-irradiated abscopal tumors also significantly decreased following the combined AAV2/oAAV6 and RT treatment (Fig. 7B). These results suggest that heterologous AAV2/oAAV6 neoantigen vaccination, when combined with radiotherapy, significantly increases the magnitude of the T-cell response, enabling the eradication of distant, untreated tumor cells.

J The density of tumor-infiltrating IFNγ+CD8+ T cells (IFNγ+CD8+CD3+CD45+ T cells) was analyzed by flow cytometry (n = 3–4). *p < 0.05. One-way ANOVA t-test. Mechanistically, the combination of AAV2/oAAV6 and RT significantly increased the infiltration of CD11c+ DCs and CD3+ T cells (Fig. 7C–E). Transcriptome-based immune cell quantification further confirmed that NK cells and T cells were significantly enriched in the AAV2/oAAV6 + RT subgroup (Fig. 7F). Flow cytometric analysis corroborated these findings, showing a significant increase in the density of mature CD86Hi DCs (CD86HiMHC-II+CD11c+ live DCs) and CD80Hi DCs (CD80HiMHC-II+CD11c+ live DCs, Fig. 7G, H, Fig. S4A, B). Furthermore, the frequency of CD8+ TILs was markedly elevated (Fig. 7I and Fig. S4C), with a significant upregulation of cytotoxic markers, including IFNγ+CD8+ TILs (IFNγ+CD8+CD3+CD45+ live T cells) and GzmB+CD8+ TILs (GzmB+CD8+CD3+CD45+ live T cells) in the AAV2/oAAV6 + RT subgroup (Fig. 7J, K, Fig. S4D, E). Finally, splenic T cells isolated from the AAV2/oAAV6 + RT group exhibited superior killing ability against ASB-XIV cells ex vivo (Fig. 7L), suggesting that systemic antitumor T-cell response was elicited and quickly eliminate residual cancer cells. Collectively, these results demonstrate that heterologous AAV2 and optimized AAV6 neoantigen vaccination significantly reshape the tumor microenvironment (TME) and enhance systemic T-cell responses, providing a potent synergistic effect in combination with radiotherapy.

Discussion

Cancer vaccines represent a powerful therapeutic approach, promising to inhibit tumor progression by inducing robust, neoantigen-specific T-cell immune responses [41]. We previously developed an engineered AAV2 vector carrying a PD-1 blocker and PD-L1-targeting miRNAs, which successfully enhanced RT efficacy, reduced recurrence risk, and improved survival in poorly immunogenic mouse models [29]. In the current study, we demonstrate that this AAV2-neoAg platform not only sensitizes tumors to ICB but also fundamentally reshapes the TME when integrated with RT across multiple poorly immunogenic tumor models. Furthermore, the novel heterologous AAV2 and optimized AAV6 prime-boost strategy triggered superior antitumor immunity, inhibited the tumor growth, and overcome the pre-existing and neutralizing immunity after repeated vaccination. The effectiveness of the novel heterologous AAV2/AAV6 prime-boost strategy can eradicate distant tumor cells in combination with radiotherapy and ICBs.

The efficacy of modern immunotherapeutics, such as ICBs and adoptive T-cell therapies, relies heavily on the enhancement of endogenous tumor-reactive T cells. However, clinical responses to ICBs are strictly dependent on TMB and the density of tumor-infiltrating immune cells [2]. Consequently, ICBs remain largely ineffective against TMB-low and immunologically "cold" tumors, including MSS-CRC, TNBC, and pancreatic cancer [1]. In this context, our engineered AAV2-based neoantigen vaccine has reemerged as an attractive strategy to prime tumor-reactive T cells. We found that this AAV-based cancer vaccine quickly elicits antigen-specific T-cell response and delays tumor growth by repeated administration. But administration of AAVax is not sufficient to provide long-term protection. Consistent with our previous studies, AAVax combined with RT significantly delayed tumor growth, suggesting that RT may promote immunogenic cell death (ICD) to enhance dendritic cell maturation and induce antigen-specific T-cell response. Therefore, these results demonstrated that AAVax platform effectively overcomes ICB resistance in these poorly immunogenic tumor models, eliciting a systemic T-cell response that synergizes with RT and ICB. Recently, the advancement of AAV-based vaccines through the engineering of transgene cassettes and capsids has gained significant traction [14, 42]. Previous studies have shown that optimized AAV6 vectors (e.g., AAV6-S663V or AAV6-T492V/S663V) can dramatically improve DC transduction efficiency and antigen presentation [17–19]. Furthermore, incorporating an RGD peptide at the I588 (or I585) residue of the AAV6 capsid enhances viral entry into integrin-overexpressing malignant cells, such as lung cancer [39, 40]. Our data corroborate these findings; the triple-modified oAAV6 (T492V/S663V/I585-RGD) showed markedly superior transduction efficacy and maturation induction in DCs, providing better antitumor immunity and therapeutic efficacy in lung cancer model.

Despite these technological advances, viral vaccination still faces the hurdle of vector-specific immunity. Repeated administration of the same serotype is often neutralized by pre-existing or treatment-induced neutralizing antibodies (NAbs) [43]. The therapeutic efficacy of adenovirus 5(Ad5)-based vaccination had been attenuated by individuals carrying Ad5-specific NAbs which are induced by prior natural infection with Ad5 or previous vaccination with Ad5-based vectors [44, 45]. Given the high prevalence of AAV2 NAbs in the human population (~ 30 to 60%) [46], which can attenuate therapeutic efficacy and induce adverse effects [38], we hypothesized that a heterologous prime-boost regimen would be superior. By using AAV2 to prime the immune response and the optimized oAAV6 to boost it, we circumvented the limitations of homologous vaccination.

Our results demonstrate that the AAV2/oAAV6 heterologous strategy elicits superior antitumor immunity, increasing the infiltration of CD11c+ DCs and CD3+ T cells compared to AAV2 homologous protocols. The recent successes in other heterologous platforms supported our findings. Recent study by Flickinger et al. showed that heterologous prime-boost immunization regimen utilizing the chimeric adenovirus Ad5.F35 and bacterium Listeria monocytogenes (Lm) [47] elicited superior antitumor immunity against colorectal tumor-associated antigen GUCY2C without toxicity. More recently, Ramirez-Valdez et al. demonstrated the therapeutic efficacy of a novel heterologous prime-boost vaccination strategies by self-assembling peptide nanoparticle TLR-7/8 agonist vaccine (prime) and chimp adenovirus (ChAdOx1) vaccine (boost) that elicits potent CD8+ T cells and tumor regression. They found intravenous heterologous prime-boos vaccination highly enhanced the CD8+ T cells and remodulated the TME by type I interferons (IFNs) [48]. Additionally, Xiao also showed that heterologous prime-boost vaccination by Lm and influenza A virus elicited potent tumor-specific response for better efficient tumor control, and potentiated the therapeutic efficacy of ICB against poorly immunological cancers [49]. These results indicated that heterologous prime-boost vaccination strategy can elicit superior antitumor immunity. Despite neoantigen-specific T cells are necessary, they are often insufficient for complete tumor eradication as a monotherapy. Our study highlights that the combination of AAV2/oAAV6 vaccination and RT triggers a formidable systemic response, significantly increasing the frequency of cytotoxic IFNγ+CD8+ and GzmB+CD8+ T cells in combination with RT. This synergy is essential for overcoming the immunosuppressive TME and eradicating distant, non-irradiated tumor cells.

In conclusion, this novel combination therapy significantly enhances antitumor efficacy without detectable toxicity. This heterologous neoantigen-targeted regimen represents an ideal strategy to prevent recurrence and achieve systemic control in cancer patients when combined with radiotherapy and immune checkpoint blockade.

Materials and methods

Mice

Six-week-old female BALB/c were purchased from Biolasco. Taiwan Co., Lt.

All day-to-day care was provided by trained mouse-house staff members. All procedures were approved by the local animal care committee (CMUIACUC-2022-472). All procedures involving the use and care of animals were performed according to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Research Council (NIH Publication No. 85-23, revised 1996).

Cell culture

CT26 (BALB/c mouse undifferentiated colon carcinoma, CRL-2638, ATCC), 4T1 (BALB/c mouse mammary carcinoma, CRL-2539, ATCC), ASB-XIV (BALB/c mouse lung carcinoma, CVCL_5686, RRID), and 293 T (human embryonic kidney, CRL-3216, ATCC) were included in this study. The cell lines were cultured in complete RPMI-1640 (Gibco#31800022, Thermo Fisher Scientific), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco# 16000044, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco#25030081, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco#10378016, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2. They were not further authenticated but were cultured for a limited number of passages (< 10 passages). The cell lines were tested for mycoplasma contamination using PCR [50].

RAW264.7 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 2 mM L-glutamine at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The cell then stimulated with LPS (1 μg/ml) for 48 h to differentiate into immature dendritic cell-like phenotype, characterized with CD11c and MHC II expression.

AAV vectors construction, generation, and recombinant AAV virus purification

Codon-optimized CT26 and 4T1 neoantigen cassette sequences fused with RERK linkers were synthesized and cloned into a CMV-driven AAV-CMV expression vector as previous described [29]. The eight neoantigen sequences in 4T1 cells are listed in Table S2.

The viruses were all produced by the triple transfection method: AAV-Vector (AAV-Vec), AAVax-CT26 (ten neoantigens from CT26), AAVax-4T1 (eight neoantigens from 4T1), AAVax-ASB (nine neoantigens from ASB-XIV), and the helper plasmids pRC2-miR342 and pHelper using 293 T cells. Seventy-two hours after transfection, the cells were collected by centrifugation and recombinant AAV2 vectors were produced and purified using an AAVpro purification kit (6232, Takara, Japan). AAV2 titration was performed by quantitative PCR (qPCR) on the vector genomes [51].

qRT–PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cell lines using TRIzol (Invitrogen, CA, USA), quantified by measuring the absorbance at 260 nm, and reverse-transcribed into cDNAs using iScript™ Reverse Transcription Supermix (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [52]. Primers were designed using the Primer Design Tool (NCBI, USA), according to sequence information from the NCBI database. qRT–PCR was performed in a final reaction volume of 20 µL with iQ™ SYBR® Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using a StepOne Plus Real-Time PCR (Applied Biosystems) [53]. All reactions were performed in triplicate for each sample, and GAPDH was used as the reference gene for normalization. The relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Gene expression levels were compared using the t-test.

In vitro differentiation of DCs from bone marrow (BM)

BM-derived DCs were generated from primary cultures of the femoral marrow obtained from 6 to 8-week-old male BALB/c mice. Briefly, after 2-h culture, the adhered BM cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco#31800022, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 1% penicillin–streptomycin, 1% non-essential amino acids, 1% sodium pyruvate, 1% L-glutamine, recombinant murine granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (mGM-CSF; 20 ng/ml; R&D Systems, Inc., Minneapolis, MA, USA), and recombinant murine interleukin 4 (mIL-4; 10 ng/ml; R&D Systems). The medium was changed every two days, and fresh complete RPMI medium containing mGM-CSF and mIL-4 was applied each time [54].

The adherent DCs were divided into two subgroups. For AAV infection, immature DCs were infected with AAV for 48 h and treated with mGM-CSF and mIL-4 for six days. For peptide stimulation, immature DCs were incubated with mGM-CSF and mIL-4 for six days and then treated with neopeptides for two days. The percentages of differentiated DCs were determined by fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis of the expression of CD11c (#117,311, Biolegend) and CD86 (#105014, Biolegend).

In vivo tumor growth

BALB/c mice (female, 4 weeks old) were maintained in accordance with institutional guidelines approved by the China Medical University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee [protocol no. CMUIACUC-2022-472]. Briefly, 100 μL Matrigel (Corning, CA, USA) containing fresh cancer cells was inoculated subcutaneously into the right leg of each mouse. Briefly, when the tumor volume reached 70–100 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to different groups as previous described [29]. For radiotherapy, the mice were anesthetized with 300 μL of PBS with ketamine (140 mg/kg) and xylazine (3 mg/kg) by intraperitoneal injection before irradiation. Before radiotherapy, the dosimetry data on the irradiation square (8.5 cm × 8.5 cm, depth 5 cm) were collected to validate the dosage of irradiation (293.2 ± 4.4 cGy /300MU). Local tumors were then treated with radiotherapy (6MV X-ray at 400MU/min, TrueBeam, Varian) according to different protocols. Following complete anesthesia, the right leg was placed in a square irradiation field (6 × 6 cm), and the body of the mouse was kept away from the leg. Radiation was delivered to the irradiation field at the center height of the tumor using an X-ray beam collimator [55]. A half-beam block was used to protect vital organs, and a 1.5-cm transparent tissue-equivalent bolus was used to cover the irradiated site for an even distribution of irradiation throughout the tumor. The dose was calibrated by using a radical ion chamber (Monrovia, CA, USA). The tumor volume was measured every 3 days throughout the study. The longest and shortest diameters (L and W, respectively) of the tumors were measured using Vernier calipers (Sata, Shanghai, China) every three days, and tumor volume (V) was calculated using the formula: V = (L × W2)/2. The mice were euthanized at the end of the experiments, and tumor tissues were collected for lysis, subjected to immunoblotting analysis, and stained by immunohistochemistry. AAV cancer vaccines were administered via intramuscular injections in the quadriceps at a volume of 50 µL per side at 1 × 108 vg on the indicated days. For survival analysis, mice were sacrificed when the tumor volume reached 2000 mm3.

WES and RNA sequencing data analysis

Total genomic DNA (gDNA) and total RNA from ASB-XIV resected tumors were were extracted using the gDNA purification kit (Qiagen) and the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen), respectively, according to the manufacturers’ instructions. As a control, we used gDNA isolated from the lung tissue of BALB/c mice. For whole-exome sequencing (WES), DNA libraries were prepared from isolated gDNA using the Kapa Hyper DNA library preparation kit (Kapa Biosystems). Briefly, DNA samples were fragmented to an average size range of 200 bp using a Covaris E220 shearing instrument. Adapter-ligated libraries were generated and enriched by PCR. Then the pooled libraries were hybridized with whole-exome SeqCap EZ probe pool. Finally, samples were run on a HiSeq4000 sequencer (Illumina) with paired-end reads of 2 × 100 cycles. The sequencing reads were cleaned by trimming of adapter sequences and low-quality bases using cutadapt v1.9.1 [56] and were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38) using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) v0.7.8. The aligned BAM file was further refined by duplicate removal with Picard toolkit v1.110 (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard). Indel realignment and base quality recalibration were performed with the Genome Analysis Toolkit v2.8-1 [57]. Somatic variants were detected using VarScan2 v2.3.7 [58]. For RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq), libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Sample Library Preparation kit (Illumina), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For RNA-seq data, quality was examined by analyzing per base sequence quality plots using FastQC. The trimming of sequence reads was performed by Trimmomatic (v0.39). RNA-seq reads were aligned to the mouse genome (mm10) using HISAT2 (v2.1.0) software. Reads that overlapped with exon coordinates were counted using featureCounts (v2.0.3). The read count matrix was normalized by converting the counts to counts per million (CPM). Subsequently, a statistical analysis was performed using edgeR (v4.2.2) to identify genes that were significantly expressed.

For WES analysis, the normalized cDNA libraries were pooled and sequenced on a HiSeq4000 sequencer (Illumina) with paired-end reads of 75 cycles. The sequencing reads were cleaned as described for the WES and were aligned to the mouse reference genome using STAR v2.5.2b [59]. Raw read counts per gene were extracted using HTSeq-count v0.6.1 [60]. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 v1.6.3 [61]. Before prediction of neoantigens, variant call format files containing somatic mutations were subjected to standard variant effect predictor, downstream, and wild-type annotations, and filtered for frameshift and missense mutations. Somatic variants were identified as described above and filtered before neoantigen prediction for normal tissue VAF (< 2%), tumor DNA and RNA VAF (> 10%), and tumor RNA variant coverage (> 5 reads). Neoantigen prediction was performed on variants that passed filtering of both in vitro and in vivo data sets. The NetMHC algorithm (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/NetMHC/) was used to predict MHC-I binding for all possible 8- to 11-amino acid-long variants and corresponding WT peptides spanning the mutation using the pVAC-Seq pipeline [62], with a cutoff of less than 500 nM for the predicted affinity.

Evaluation of the immune cell infiltration in vivo

Tumors were isolated and weighed and then placed in a petri dish containing blank RPMI media at room temperature to prevent dehydration. The tumor was minced into small pieces (1–2 mm) using a beaver blade, filtered through a 70-μm strainer, spun down, and resuspended in blank RPMI media. Thereafter, the cell suspensions were layered over Ficoll–Paque medium (#17,144,002, Cytiva) and centrifuged at 1025 × g for 20 min. The mononuclear cell layer was transferred into a conical tube, 20 ml of complete RPMI medium was added, and then gently mixed and centrifuged at 650 × g for 10 min. Finally, the supernatant was removed and the tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) were resuspended in complete RPMI medium [63].

TILs were then resuspended in 500 μL of staining buffer (2% BSA and 0.1% NaN3 in PBS). The cells were stained with a surface marker and intracellular markers panel containing (1) TEM panel: CD3-APC/Fire750, CD4-FITC, CD8a-PE/Cy7, CD44-PE, CD62L-APC; (2) IFNγ + CD8 + panel: CD3-FITC, CD45-APC/Fire750, CD8a-APC, IFNγ-PE; (3) Exhausted T-cell panel: CD45-APC/Fire750, CD3-FITC, CD8a-APC, Tim3-PE/Cy7, PD1-PE; (4) DC panel: CD19-BV605; CD11c-APC, MHC-II-Cy7, CD80-BV570, CD86-BV421 (Biolegend, CA, USA), and their isotypes. TILs were restimulated with PMA (20 ng/mL), ionomycin (1 µg/mL), and BFA (1:1000) at 37 °C for 3 h prior to intracellular staining. Cells were then resuspended in 500 µL staining buffer, blocked with Fc block, and stained with surface antibody mix (30 min, 4 °C), followed by fixation/permeabilization and intracellular marker staining according to the manufacturer's protocol. Samples were analyzed using a Cytek Aurora flow cytometry (Cytek Bioscience, CA, USA) [64].

Serum preparation and in vitro neutralization analysis

Recombinant AAV2-GFP vectors were produced according to prior triple-plasmid transfection protocol and titered by quantitative PCR (qPCR) on the vector genomes/mL. The blood was collected from mice immunized with different AAV subgroups, and serum samples were heat-inactivated at 56 °C for 30 min to eliminate non-specific neutralizing activity from the complement system. AAV2-GFP vectors (MOI = 1000) were mixed with an equal volume of each serum dilution (1:10, 1:100, 1:1000) and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to allow antibody-capsid binding. Then, HEK293T cells (5 × 104 cells/well) were inoculated with the serum-virus mixtures for 48 h. Cells incubated with AAV2-GFP and serum media alone served as the positive transduction control (100% signal), while uninfected cells served as the negative control. After 48-h incubation, the GFP+ cells were evaluated and calculated by fluorescent microscopy to analyze the neutralizing ability. Neutralizing Ab inhibition% = (1-% of GFP + cells in test sample/positive control) × 100.

Immunofluorescent analysis

For immunofluorescence analysis of mouse tissues, 3-μm-thick histological tissue sections were analyzed with the following antibodies: anti-mouse CD11c (ab219799, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-mouse CD3 (ab16669, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-granzyme B (ab4059, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and anti-mouse CD8a (ab217344, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) [65]. The tissue sections were then stained with Alexa 546- and Alexa 488-conjugated secondary antibodies and mounted with DAPI. Immune cell staining was positive when detected in tumor-infiltrating dendritic cells and was evaluated using fluorescence microscopy. The area with the highest density of immune cells among the malignant cells was counted at 400 × magnification (no. for DCs and T cells/high-power fields). The average number of tumor-infiltrating dendritic cells and T cells in five high-power fields was included in the evaluation [66].

In vitro T-cell cytotoxicity assay

Splenic T cells were harvested from vaccinated or control mice at Day 30. Briefly, spleens were dissociated into single-cell suspensions using a 70-μm cell strainer. After lysis of red blood cells using ACK lysis buffer, T cells were isolated using Ficoll-Paque medium. To evaluate the cytotoxic activity of the vaccine-induced T cells, 1 × 104 ASB-XIV tumor cells (target cells) were seeded in 96-well plates overnight and incubated with different Effector-to-Target (E:T) ratios (1:1, 2.5:1, 5:1 and 10:1) for 24 h. The percentage of tumor cell lysis was determined by LDH release assay [67, 68].

Statistical analysis

Between-group comparisons were performed using an unpaired t-test and ordinary one-way ANOVA (including Dunnett’s and Tukey’s multiple comparison tests). A two-sided p-value was reported for all tests using GraphPad Prism 7 statistical software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 and ***p < 0.001 were the significant levels in this study.
