Dynamic evolution of higher alcohols from CO2 on Fe3O4-Fe5C2-Cu catalytic interfaces with amorphous Ti layout
Authors: Teng Li, Lijun Zhang, Zhenkun Liu, Jiaming Liang, Hao Chen, Linlin Zhao, Shunnosuke Fujii, Peng Qin, Qiang Liu, Tao Li, Tao Xing, Jiancai Sui, Yongqiang Gu, Minghui Tan, Mingbo Wu, Zhiliang Jin, Noritatsu Tsubaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Selective synthesis of alcohols from carbon-based feedstocks remains a fundamental challenge due to complex reaction networks and competing pathways. Here we report a titanium iron ore material FeTiOx synthesized via a high-temperature calcination strategy that introduces amorphous Ti species into the catalyst framework. The FeTiOx system exhibits an intrinsic preference for higher alcohols formation, and tuning the dispersion of Cu species further enhances higher alcohols selectivity to 30.2%, while maintaining a CO2 conversion of 42.5%. Operando X-ray diffraction and diffuse reflectance Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy are employed to monitor the evolution of the catalyst phase structure (Fe2O3 → Fe3O4 → Fe5C2) and chemical intermediates (CO*, CHx*, CH3CH2O*) in real time, thereby elucidating the dynamic evolutions of the catalyst phases and surface intermediates under reaction conditions. These results uncover the structural adaptability of the catalyst and its role in regulating key reaction intermediates. This work highlights the importance of phase-structure engineering in Fe-based catalytic systems and provides a strategy for designing efficient catalysts for selective higher alcohols synthesis.

Introduction

Mounting environmental pressures, driven by escalating CO2 emissions and the depletion of conventional fossil resources1–5, demand transformative strategies for sustainable fuel and chemical production. As carbon neutrality becomes an international mandate, catalytic processes capable of valorizing CO2 into energy-dense molecules have emerged as compelling alternatives6–10. In this context, CO2-modified Fischer–Tropsch synthesis has gained significant attention, as it couples syngas chemistry with carbon-utilization pathways to reshape traditional hydrocarbon formation11,12. By integrating CO2 activation with tailored catalyst design, this approach offers a promising route toward carbon-circular manufacturing while retaining the versatility of the Fischer–Tropsch platform13–15.

The accelerating accumulation of atmospheric CO2 has intensified the urgency of developing catalytic routes capable of transforming this inert molecule into value-added oxygenates. Among these pathways, CO2-modified Fischer–Tropsch synthesis offers a particularly compelling platform for constructing carbon-efficient processes, especially for the selective formation of higher alcohols16,17. Recent advances highlight the promise of Cu-doped iron catalysts, whose distinctive electronic and structural synergies enhance both CO2 activation and subsequent C-C coupling18,19. The incorporation of Cu modifies the reducibility and phase evolution of iron species, stabilizes key carbide and oxide intermediates, and promotes hydrogenation-carbonylation equilibria that governs alcohol-chain growth16,18,20,21. These cooperative effects lead to more controlled propagation of oxygenated intermediates and improved selectivity toward C2+ alcohols. As mechanistic insights into metal-metal and metal-oxide interfaces continue to deepen, Cu-modified iron catalysts are emerging as a central platform for steering CO2 conversion toward higher alcohols with greater efficiency and precision22,23.

Building on these considerations, we successfully synthesized a titanium iron ore material FeTiOx catalyst through a high-temperature calcination strategy, which enables the formation of amorphous Ti species within the lattice. Catalytic evaluation reveals that this inorganic perovskite exhibits a pronounced selectivity toward alcohol formation during the reaction. By further regulating the dispersion of Cu species on the catalyst surface, the selectivity toward alcohols in products can be significantly enhanced, reaching up to 30.2%. To gain mechanistic insight into the catalytic process, operando XRD and operando DRIFT spectra were employed to real-time monitor the dynamic evolution of the catalyst phase structure and surface intermediates under reaction conditions. These operando measurements provide direct evidence for the formation of Fe5C2 and Fe3O4 as the active phases, and for the CO-insertion pathway via CHx* and intermediates, offering a reliable basis for the rational design of selective alcohol synthesis catalysts.

Results

Catalytic activity

All catalysts were synthesized using a high-temperature calcination strategy (for detailed information, refer to the Supporting Information). Prior to activity testing, each catalyst underwent activation in pure hydrogen gas at 400 °C under atmospheric pressure for 8 h. The detailed optimization procedures and corresponding rationale for the amorphous Ti catalytic system, reaction pressure, Na loading, and feed gas composition are presented in Supplementary Figs. 1–4 and Supplementary Tables 1, 2. Subsequently, the activated catalysts were evaluated for the performance in the hydrogenation of CO2 to produce higher alcohols under conditions of 5.0 MPa, feed gas (Ar 5 vol%, CO2 27 vol%, and H2 68 vol%), and a gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) of 4800 mL gcat−1 h−1 (unless otherwise specified). As illustrated in Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1, the 1%Na-FeTiOx catalyst exhibits a selectivity of 16.4% (16.1%) for alcohols (higher alcohols) during the CO2 hydrogenation process, indicating that the presence of amorphous Ti imparts specificity in the directed synthesis of alcohols and higher alcohols from Fe-based catalysts. The results of CO2-TPD analysis indicate that the introduction of amorphous Ti effectively enhances CO2 adsorption, which is crucial for the efficient catalytic conversion of CO2 (Supplementary Fig. 5). The CO2-TPD results are consistent with the conversion of CO2 (Supplementary Table 1). Importantly, the CO-TPD results for spent catalysts indicate that the introduction of Ti effectively enhances the degree of CO desorption, with amorphous Ti being more effective than crystalline Ti in promoting CO desorption (Supplementary Fig. 6). This suggests that the strategic introduction of amorphous Ti significantly enhances the non-dissociative activation capacity, which is consistent with the results regarding the selectivity of higher alcohols. Furthermore, the introduction of metallic Cu further reduces CO2 adsorption, which is consistent with the results regarding the catalyst’s catalytic activity. The introduction of an appropriate amount of Cu further enhances the selectivity of higher alcohols. Figure 1a, Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 demonstrate that the introduction of an optimal amount of Cu significantly increases the higher alcohols selectivity of the 1%Na-FeTiOx from 16.4% (16.1%) to 28.0% (26.9%). Notably, the introduction of Cu into the iron-based catalytic system inevitably reduces the CO2 activation capability while promoting H2 activation (Supplementary Fig. 7), thereby facilitating the hydrogenation of CO2 to produce higher alcohols. However, excessive Cu content can diminish the concentration of surface carbonaceous intermediates, thereby favor hydrogenation termination processes and suppress C-C coupling reactions. This ultimately leads to an increase in CO selectivity and a decrease in C5+ selectivity16. By precisely controlling the Cu content and distribution, an optimal catalyst (1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx) was achieved, demonstrating selectivity of 28.0% (26.9%) for alcohols (higher alcohols). The space velocity of 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx was further adjusted to enhance the STY of higher alcohols. As shown in Fig. 1d, the catalytic performance of 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx for CO2 decreases with increasing space velocity, which is attributed to the reduced residence time of CO2 on the catalyst surface. Concurrently, the selectivity for CO increases, indicating that higher space velocities facilitate the desorption of CO. Interestingly, the 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx catalyst maintains a high selectivity for higher alcohols up to a space velocity of 8000 mL gcat−1 h−1 (Fig. 1e). However, further increases in space velocity result in a decline in the selectivity for higher alcohols. This reduction is attributed to the suppression of coupling between alkyl species and C(H)O* intermediates at elevated space velocities, leading to an increased formation of C1-C5 hydrocarbon products22. These findings are in complete agreement with the results presented in Fig. 1d and Supplementary Table 3.

The effect of temperature on the thermodynamic equilibrium of CO2 hydrogenation to higher alcohols was investigated by systematically adjusting the reaction temperature. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8, an increase in temperature (below 340 °C) leads to a corresponding enhancement in the selectivity towards alcohols, with a notable increase in the selectivity for higher alcohols as well (Supplementary Fig. 9). This behavior can be attributed to the reaction is endothermic reaction, where elevated temperatures increase the equilibrium constant, thereby favoring the formation of higher alcohols. However, excessively high temperatures may lead to increase side reactions, such as methane formation, thereby reducing the selectivity of alcohols and higher alcohols (Supplementary Table 4). These results indicate that the thermodynamic equilibrium constant for CO2 hydrogenation to higher alcohols using a 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx catalyst reaches an optimal value at 340 °C. Importantly, the thermodynamic equilibrium of the CO2 hydrogenation reaction is also reflected in these findings. According to the principles of chemical equilibrium, increasing the temperature favors the forward reaction of the reverse water-gas shift (RWGS) reaction, thus facilitating the conversion of CO2 to CO. Consequently, with rising temperature, the CO2 conversion increases dramatically (from 18.4% at 300 °C to 48.0% at 360 °C). In summary, the enhancement of CO2 activation and the improvement of higher alcohols selectivity significantly elevate the STY of higher alcohols (from 50.9 g kgcat−1 h−1 at 300 °C to 245.4 g kgcat−1 h−1 at 340 °C) (Supplementary Fig. 10). To further evaluate the stability and practical applicability of 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx, its stability was tested over a period of 180 h (Supplementary Fig. 11). The stability test results indicate that 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx exhibits stable CO2 conversion and higher alcohols selectivity. As summarized in Supplementary Table 5, comparison with previously reported catalysts for higher alcohols synthesis via CO2-FTS demonstrates that 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx exhibits outstanding performance, particularly in terms of higher alcohols selectivity and STY.

Active phase

The phase structure, morphological features, elemental distribution, specific surface area, and valence-state profiles of the corresponding fresh catalysts are thoroughly characterized and description as detailed in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Figs. 12–18 and Supplementary Table 6). Investigating the active phase of the catalyst is essential for elucidating the mechanism during the reaction process. The phase composition of the spent catalyst was analyzed using XRD. As shown in Fig. 2a, the spent 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx catalyst is composed of Fe3O4, Fe5C2, and Cu. Notably, no metallic titanium or titanium oxide phases were detected in the spent catalyst, indicating that the form of titanium remains unaffected during the CO2 hydrogenation catalytic process. To further elucidate the microstructure of the spent 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx catalyst, the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) analyses were conducted. Figure 2b reveals that the spent 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx exhibits particles of different sizes. HRTEM results (Fig. 2c) demonstrate the presence of the (311) crystal planes of Fe3O4, the (510) and (020) crystal planes of Fe5C2, and the (111) crystal plane of Cu, along with an amorphous region. Importantly, the HRTEM findings not only correlate with the phase structure observed in the XRD analysis (Fig. 2a) but also suggest that the amorphous region may be attributed to the presence of metallic titanium or titanium oxides in an amorphous state. Furthermore, the valence states and the elemental composition of the spent 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx catalyst were investigated using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As depicted in Fig. 2d, the most significant difference between the spent and fresh 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx catalysts is observed in the characteristic peak located around 707.77 eV, which is attributed to the presence of Fe-C chemical bonds in the spent catalyst. This formation of Fe-C bonds is a result of the carburization of elemental iron during the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis process24–26. The XPS fine spectrum of Ti is shown in Fig. 2e. The characteristic peaks with binding energies of 457.81 and 463.72 eV are caused by Ti 2p3/2 and Ti 2p1/2 in the 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx catalyst, respectively27,28. This also fully demonstrates the presence of the Ti element. Simultaneously, a notable alteration in the valence state of copper was observed (Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21). Compared to the fresh 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx catalyst, the spent catalyst exhibited a significant decrease in the content of Cu (II) and a corresponding increase in the content of Cu (0) and Cu (I)4,29, attributed to the reduction processes occurring during the catalytic reaction and the CO2-Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. Subsequently, the distribution of copper valence states was further examined using Cu LMM Auger electron spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 2f, Cu (0), Cu (I), and Cu (II) coexist in the spent catalyst, indicating a complex interplay of copper species post-reaction.

Operando dynamic evolution of phase structure and chemical intermediates

The operando dynamic evolution of the catalyst phase structure is crucial for identifying the active phases governing catalytic performance30. During the reduction and reaction processes of 1% Na-FeTiOx (Fig. 3a, b), the material initially present as Fe2O3 under pure H2 (consistent with Supplementary Fig. 12b) first transforms into Fe3O4 and is ultimately reduced to metallic Fe. Upon exposure to reaction conditions, metallic Fe progressively evolves into Fe5C2 and Fe3O4, in agreement with the analysis shown in Supplementary Fig. 22. These observations indicate that Fe5C2 and Fe3O4 serve as the catalytically active phases during CO2 hydrogenation to higher alcohols. As shown in Fig. 3c, d, the phase evolution of 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx proceeds through distinct transformation steps. The fresh catalyst initially consists of Fe2O3 and CuO phases. Upon exposure to pure H2 with increasing temperature, these phases gradually transform into Fe3O4, metallic Fe and Cu, which remain stable at the end of the reduction process. This indicates that the introduction of Cu species could significantly suppress the excessive reduction of Fe species. Upon subsequent introduction of the CO2/H2 reaction mixture, the Fe phase gradually disappeared with increasing reaction time, while the characteristic features of Fe5C2 progressively emerged. However, the diffraction intensity associated with Fe5C2 remained relatively weak, which may be attributed to the comparatively weak diffraction signals of Fe5C2 being masked by the much stronger XRD reflections corresponding to Fe3O4 and metallic Cu phases. As evidenced by the results in Fig. 2a, after the reduction and catalytic reaction processes under practical conditions, the catalyst composition consists of Fe3O4, metallic Cu and Fe5C2. Operando monitoring of surface intermediates provides critical insight into the mechanistic pathways of CO2 hydrogenation toward higher alcohols. More importantly, correlating the evolution of these intermediates with catalytic performance enables elucidation of the structure-activity relationship governing higher alcohols synthesis. To closely approximate practical reaction conditions, the catalyst was first reduced at 400 °C under pure H2, followed by pressurization to 5.0 MPa using He. Subsequently, the reactant gas mixture was introduced into the reactor, and the evolution of surface intermediates was operando monitored. Taking 1% Na-FeTiOx as an example (Fig. 4a, c), an intense band in the 2400–2200 cm−1 region was observed, which is attributed to strongly adsorbed CO2 species on the catalyst surface. Two carbonate species were subsequently identified: monodentate carbonate (m-CO3*: 1512, 1457, 1315, and 1289 cm−1) and bidentate carbonate (b-CO3*: 1651 and 1343 cm−1)31–33, indicating that CO2 initially adsorbs on the catalyst surface in the form of carbonate species. These carbonates undergo stepwise hydrogenation to form bicarbonate (HCO3*: 1419 and 1396 cm−1)33, which is further hydrogenated to generate formate (HCOO*: 1362 cm−1) and CO (2183 and 2106 cm−1)31,34,35. The introduction of Cu species significantly enhanced the intensity of linearly adsorbed CO* species (2076 cm−1) 36, indicating increased surface coverage and stabilization of reactive CO intermediates. Importantly, the increased CO* intensity positively correlates with the enhanced selectivity toward higher alcohols observed over 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx, suggesting that Cu-promoted CO adsorption facilitates the CO insertion pathway during higher alcohols synthesis. The CO species generated subsequently hydrogenated during the FTS process to produce CHx* intermediates (2934 and 3083 cm−1)36, while transiently formed CH4 (3012 cm−1) desorbs from the catalyst surface. Importantly, CO undergoes insertion into CH3* species to form CH3CO*, which is further hydrogenated to CH3CHO* (1749 and 1544 cm−1)18,31. Subsequent hydrogenation yields CH3CH2O* (2964 and 2878 cm−1)37, and continued hydrogenation ultimately produces CnH2n + 1OH and desorbs from the catalyst surface. A similar reaction pathway is observed for 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx during CO2 hydrogenation to higher alcohols (Fig. 4b, d). Notably, the operando spectra of 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx exhibit significantly stronger characteristic bands corresponding to CO*, CHx*, and CH3CH2O* intermediates, which directly correlates with its substantially enhanced higher alcohols productivity. In particular, the increased intensity of CH3CH2O* species suggests facilitated hydrogenation of intermediates and accelerated alcohol-chain propagation on the Cu-modified catalyst surface. Combined with operando phase evolution analysis, the superior higher alcohols selectivity of 1% Na-Cu0.10FeTiOx can be attributed to the synergistic interplay between Fe3O4 and Fe5C2 phases. Specifically, Fe3O4 preferentially promotes CO2 activation and oxygenate intermediate formation, while Fe5C2 facilitates C-C coupling and chain growth. The amorphous Ti and Cu-induced enhancement of surface CO* coverage further accelerates CO insertion into CHx* species, thereby promoting higher alcohols formation.

Dynamic evolution mechanism of higher alcohols

Based on the operando identification of active phases, surface intermediates, and phase evolution behavior, we propose that the enhanced higher alcohol synthesis performance originates from the synergistic interplay at the Fe3O4-Fe5C2-Cu interfacial structure11,38–40. The operando-observed transformation from Fe2O3 to Fe3O4 and Fe5C2, together with the sequential evolution of carbonate → CO* → CHx* → CH3CH2O* intermediates, provides mechanistic evidence for a CO insertion pathway during CO2 hydrogenation to higher alcohols. The hydrogenation of CO2 to higher alcohols generally proceeds through three interconnected processes: RWGS, FTS, and CO insertion reactions (Fig. 4e). Within the proposed interfacial structure, Fe3O4 primarily facilitates CO2 activation and oxygenate intermediate formation, while Fe5C2 promotes C-C coupling and chain propagation. Meanwhile, Cu species enhance H2 activation and stabilize linearly adsorbed CO* intermediates. The intimate interfacial contact among these phases enables efficient intermediate transfer and promotes the subsequent CO insertion pathway toward higher alcohol formation. Taking ethanol synthesis as an example, CO2 is first converted into carbonate species on Fe3O4, followed by sequential hydrogenation to generate HCO3*, HCOO*, and CO intermediates. The CO species generated are subsequently hydrogenated on Fe5C2 to form CHx* intermediates. At the Fe3O4-Fe5C2-Cu interfacial sites, linearly adsorbed CO* species stabilized on Cu sites migrate toward adjacent Fe5C2 sites, where CO insertion into CHx* intermediates forms CH3CO* species. Subsequent hydrogenation produces CH3CHO* and finally CH3CH2OH, which desorbs from the catalyst surface. Notably, the enhanced formation of linearly adsorbed CO* and CH3CH2O* intermediates on the Cu-modified catalyst strongly correlates with the improved higher alcohol selectivity, further supporting the critical role of the Fe3O4-Fe5C2-Cu interfacial structure in promoting CO insertion and oxygenate hydrogenation during higher alcohol synthesis.

Discussion

In summary, we report a titanium iron ore material, FeTiOx, containing amorphous Ti species synthesized via a high-temperature calcination strategy for selective higher alcohols synthesis. Under the regulation of the electronic aid Na, the FeTiOx framework exhibits an intrinsic preference for alcohol formation, while adjusting the dispersion of Cu species further enhances higher alcohols selectivity to 30.2%, while maintaining a CO2 conversion of 42.5%, highlighting the critical role of surface Cu sites in regulating catalytic behavior. The operando X-ray diffraction (XRD) and operando DRIFTS results reveal the dynamic evolution of catalyst phases (formation of a Fe3O4-Fe5C2-Cu interface) and surface intermediates (carbonate → CO* → CHx* → CH3CH2O*) during the catalytic reaction. These observations provide direct insight into the structure-reactivity relationship governing alcohol formation. This study offers mechanistic understanding of Fe-based perovskite catalysts and establishes a viable strategy for designing efficient catalytic systems for the selective production of higher alcohols from carbon-based feedstocks.

Methods

Synthesis of FeTiOx and TiO2-Fe2O3

Titanium iron ore material FeTiOx was synthesized via high-temperature calcination. The specific procedure was as follows: 20 mmol of iron acetate was dissolved in 120 mL of ethylene glycol, followed by the dropwise addition of 6.8 mL of tetrabutyl titanate under stirring conditions, with continuous stirring for 5 h. The resulting product was centrifuged and washed, then dried overnight in an oven at 120 °C. It was subsequently transferred to a muffle furnace and heated at a rate of 2 °C/min to 700 °C, where it was held for 4 h. After cooling to room temperature, FeTiOx was obtained. The metal ratio in the TiO2-Fe2O3 preparation process remains the same. The difference is that the Ti source uses commercial TiO2, and it is mixed with the Fe source via an impregnation method. After impregnation, TiO2-Fe2O3 is obtained through the same high-temperature calcination process.

Synthesis of Cuy-FeTiOx (y = 0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.25, 0.40) and Cu0.10-TiO2-Fe2O3

The Cu-doped titanium iron ore material is prepared by the same method as described above. After adding iron acetate, an appropriate amount of copper acetate is added. Where y represents the molar ratio of Cu to Fe. Cu0.10-TiO2-Fe2O3 was synthesized by introducing an appropriate amount of Cu precursor during the preparation process of TiO2-Fe2O3, while all remaining synthesis procedures were identical to those used for TiO2-Fe2O3.

Synthesis of alkali metal (Na)-supported catalysts

Taking 1% Na-FeTiOx as an example, weigh 1 g of FeTiOx into a crucible, then weigh out 0.037 g of NaNO3 (where the mass of sodium relative to FeTiOx is 1%) and dissolve it in an appropriate amount of water. Next, add the NaNO3 solution dropwise to the crucible and ultrasonicate to ensure the NaNO3 is thoroughly dispersed onto the FeTiOx. After ultrasonication, transfer the catalyst to a muffle furnace, heat to 400 °C, hold for 2 h, then cool to room temperature to obtain 1% Na-FeTiOx.

Characterization methods

The crystalline structures of the catalysts were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Rigaku RINT 2400 diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (40 kV, 40 mA), and data were collected over a 2θ range of 10–80°. Surface chemical states and elemental compositions were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) on a Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi instrument. All spectra were calibrated using the C 1s peak at 280.80 eV as the reference.

Surface morphology and elemental distribution were investigated by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, JEOL JSM-6360LV) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) detector (JEOL JED2300, 100 mm2 silicon drift detector). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM, JEOL JEM-2100F) operated at 200 kV was employed to examine lattice fringes and nanoscale structural features.

Textural properties, including specific surface area, pore size, and pore volume, were determined from N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms measured on a BELSORP MAX II-334 analyzer. Prior to analysis, the samples were degassed under vacuum at 200 °C for 6 h.

CO2 temperature-programmed desorption (CO2-TPD) measurements were carried out on a BELCAT-B-TT instrument equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD). Prior to analysis, the catalyst was reduced under flowing H2 at 400 °C for 2 h and subsequently purged with He (20 mL min−1) to remove residual gases. After cooling to 50 °C, CO2 was introduced until saturation adsorption was achieved. Weakly and physically adsorbed CO2 species were then removed by He purging at the same temperature. The desorption profile was subsequently recorded by heating the sample from 50 to 400 °C at a ramping rate of 10 °C min−1 under a continuous He flow.

H2 temperature-programmed desorption (H2-TPD) measurements were carried out on a BELCAT-B-TT instrument equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD). Prior to analysis, the catalyst was reduced under flowing H2 at 400 °C for 2 h and subsequently purged with He (20 mL min−1) to remove residual gases. After cooling to 50 °C, H2 was introduced until saturation adsorption was achieved. Weakly and physically adsorbed H2 species were then removed by He purging at the same temperature. The desorption profile was subsequently recorded by heating the sample from 50 to 700 °C at a ramping rate of 10 °C min−1 under a continuous He flow.

CO temperature-programmed desorption (CO-TPD) measurements were conducted using a BELCAT-B-TT apparatus equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD). Prior to CO adsorption, the spent catalyst was pretreated under flowing He (20 mL min−1) at 200 °C for 2 h to remove residual surface species. The sample was then cooled to 50 °C and exposed to CO until adsorption saturation was achieved. Subsequently, weakly bound and physically adsorbed CO species were removed by He purging at the same temperature. The desorption behavior was monitored by heating the sample from 50 to 800 °C at a rate of 10 °C min−1 under a continuous He flow.

The evolution of crystalline phases during reduction and reaction was tracked in real time using the operando X-ray diffractometer (SmartLab-TD). The sample was treated under a flow of H2 (70 mL min−1) while heating to 400 °C at a rate of 7.5 °C min−1, followed by an isothermal hold for 3 h. The system was then cooled to 320 °C, after which a CO2/H2 gas mixture (Ar 5 vol%, CO2 27 vol%, and H2 68 vol%) was introduced at atmospheric pressure to initiate the reaction.

Operando diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) measurements were performed on a NICOLET iS50 FTIR spectrometer. The catalysts were first pretreated in flowing H2 at 400 °C for 2 h and subsequently cooled to 320 °C. The pressure then increased to 3.0 MPa using He, followed by switching to a CO2/H2 mixture (Ar 5 vol%, CO2 27 vol%, and H2 68 vol%). Spectra were collected under these reaction conditions.

Catalytic activity evaluation

Catalytic performance was evaluated in a fixed-bed reactor. Typically, 0.25 g of catalyst was homogeneously mixed with 0.75 g of quartz sand and loaded into the reactor. Prior to reaction, the catalyst was reduced under a flow of pure H2 (60 mL min−1) at atmospheric pressure. The temperature increased from room temperature to 400 °C at a rate of 5 °C min−1 and maintained for 8 h. After reduction, the reactor was cooled to 200 °C. The system was then pressurized to the desired value using a feed gas mixture (Ar 5 vol%, CO2 27 vol%, and H2 68 vol%), while the temperature was simultaneously raised to the target reaction temperature.

The effluent gases were analyzed online using two gas chromatographs (GC-2014, Shimadzu). One GC, equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and an activated carbon column, was used for the quantification of Ar, CO, CH4, and CO2. The second GC, fitted with a flame ionization detector (FID) and an HP-PLOT/Q column, was employed for C1-C6 hydrocarbons analysis. Quantification of C1-C6 products was performed online with CH4 as an internal standard. Liquid products were collected in an ice-cooled trap containing n-dodecane as the solvent and further analyzed via two offline gas chromatographs (GC-2014) equipped with FID. The DB-624 column and InerCap 5 column were used to analyze oxygenated compounds and liquid products, respectively.
