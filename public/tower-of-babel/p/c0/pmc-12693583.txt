Introduction

Unilateral absence of a pulmonary artery (UAPA) is a rare congenital abnormality with few reports in the literature demonstrating resultant myocardial ischaemia. Our case illustrates a rare cause of acute coronary syndrome (ACS) secondary to steal phenomena, from a left-sided coronary-pulmonary fistula. Herein, we exhibit stress echocardiogram-proven resolution of coronary steal phenomenon following successful coil embolization of a left circumflex-to-pulmonary fistula, thus offering novel insight into the assessment and management of this uncommon pathology.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old female was admitted to the hospital with a non-ST elevation myocardial infarction after experiencing persistent chest pain while unloading groceries. Her medical history included right pulmonary artery atresia, exploratory cardiac surgery as a child due to a chest deformity, dyslipidaemia, and severe emphysema (demonstrated on CT chest), ex-smoker (previously one pack-per-day, quit 12 years prior), and obstructive sleep apnoea. On physical exam in the emergency department, she had normal oxygen saturation with coarse breath sounds over the right hemithorax and clear breath sounds over the left (her baseline); her cardiac exam was normal. Initial investigations demonstrated a peak Troponin I of 758 ng/L (normal range: 0–9.0 ng/L) and an electrocardiogram (ECG) unchanged from baseline. Echocardiogram showed normal left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) with no other significant valvular or structural abnormalities. There was insufficient tricuspid regurgitation to assess right ventricular systolic pressure; a right heart catheterization was not performed. Coronary angiography revealed a codominant system without apparent coronary artery disease. There was, however, a prominent collateral from the left circumflex (LCx) artery supplying the right lower lung lobe (Figure 1A), likely via a bronchial artery.

(B) Selective coronary angiogram of the left coronary system post-intervention demonstrating successful embolization of the collateral artery. (C). Post-intervention CT coronary artery reconstruction, demonstrating successful embolization of the LCx collateral artery (asterisk).

Following discharge, stress echocardiography testing was performed. She was only able to complete 1 min and 34 s (3.9 metabolic equivalents of task, METS) of a Bruce protocol, limited by dyspnoea; there were no ischaemic ECG changes. Stress echocardiography showed exercise-induced LV dilatation and akinesis of the lateral wall, associated with a decrease in LVEF from 53% to 34% (see Supplementary material online, Inline Video S1 and Supplementary Material S1). Cardiac computed tomography (CT) imaging confirmed right pulmonary artery atresia with collateral supply from the circumflex artery, as well as the right internal mammary artery and celiac axis.

Given the patient’s history of dyspnoea when walking on an incline, ACS event and evidence of exercise-induced decrease in LVEF due to steal phenomena, we looked to exclude the coronary-pulmonary collateral. We first confirmed safety by performing ventilation/perfusion imaging. The right lung did not significantly participate in pulmonary perfusion (8%, vs. left lung 92% of perfusion).

Given the above, we proceeded with coil embolization of the LCx–lung collateral. This was performed via a right radial approach with a 6 French sheath. A 6 French XB 3.5 guide catheter was used to engage the left main artery. A 5.5 French guide liner was used for additional support before advancing a Sion blue wire with a Caravel microcatheter into the collateral vessel (see Supplementary material online, Inline Video S2). Two penumbra LP coils (3X15, 3X10) were deployed, resulting in complete exclusion of the collateral (Figure 1B; Supplementary material online, Inline Video S3 and Supplementary Material S2). The procedure was well tolerated, there were no complications, and the patient did not demonstrate any symptoms suggestive of pulmonary infarct. The patient was discharged home following the procedure with 3 months of dual anti-platelet therapy (aspirin 81 mg p.o. daily and Plavix 75 mg p.o. daily) and then low-dose aspirin indefinitely. Follow-up CT coronary angiogram confirmed complete exclusion of the LCx–lung fistula (Figure 1C), and subsequent stress echocardiography 2 months following the procedure showed elimination of the steal phenomena (see Supplementary material online, Supplementary Material S3 and S4).

Discussion

This case highlights a distinctive presentation and proof of treatment for an ACS caused by a rare LCx-to-lung fistula, which resulted in coronary steal and myocardial ischaemia. The latter is presumed secondary to diminished blood supply to the myocardium during a stressor, such as physical exertion. Broadly, UAPA is a rare congenital abnormality, which was first reported by Frantzel in 1868.1 Left-sided UAPA often manifests with other cardiac abnormalities such as Tetralogy of Fallot, or septal defects.2 Conversely, isolated UAPA has an estimated prevalence of 1 in 200 0003 and involves the right PA in ∼60% of cases.2,4 There are a number of reports of UAPA with collateralization from coronary arteries reported in the literature,2,5–11 but few reports demonstrating associated symptoms or findings of myocardial ischaemia.8,12 de Dominicis et al. reported embolization of a right coronary-to-bronchial artery collateral with aneurysm, in the context of coronary steal.12 Our case also illustrates a rare cause of ACS secondary to steal phenomena, but from a left-sided coronary-pulmonary fistula. To our knowledge, this case uniquely demonstrates stress echocardiogram-proven resolution of coronary steal phenomenon following successful coil embolization of a LCx-to-lung fistula, thus offering novel insight into the assessment and management of this uncommon pathology.
