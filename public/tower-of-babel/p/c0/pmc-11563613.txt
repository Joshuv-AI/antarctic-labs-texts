Hematological profile of free-range Sunda porcupine (Hystrix javanica)
Authors: Yuda Heru Fibrianto, Arinda Devi Larasati, Teguh Budipitojo, Woro Danur Wendo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:The Sunda porcupine, or the Javan porcupine (Hystrix javanica), is a rodent native to Indonesia. Although information about its conservation status is available, the hematological profile is limited. The normal hematological profile of an animal is essential as a basic health indicator to determine the initial state of a pathological process of a disease. Since the hematological profile can provide significant evidence regarding the health status of the species, the Sunda Porcupine’s hematological profile can benefit conservation initiatives. Therefore, hematological evaluation of these porcupines can play a role in conservation initiatives, breeding strategies, and the prevention of zoonotic diseases.Aim:This research revealed the routine blood evaluation and white blood cell (WBC) morphological features of the free-ranging Sunda porcupine (H. javanica).Methods:Blood samples from four free-range individuals captured Sunda porcupine (H. javanica) were obtained intracardially for routine hematological evaluation and WBC staining for morphological identification. The profiles were then analyzed descriptively.Results:Hematology profile averages were generally 4.04 × 106/Ul for RBC; Hemoglobin was in 12.83 g/dl; Hematocrit by 37.8%; MCV 107.1 fl; MCH 31.80 pg; MCHC 31.23 g/dl; while WBC and platelet (PLT) were at 9.67 × 103/μl and 503.00 × 103/μl, respectively.Conclusion:The morphology of red blood cells, WBCs, and the hematological profile of the Javan hedgehog is not much different from that of other mammals such as crested porcupines, ferrets, and guinea pigs. Until now, there has been limited research on the hematology of Sunda porcupines, which has led to a limited understanding of their health status. We anticipate that the findings of this study will serve as a diagnostic instrument for evaluating the health condition of Sunda porcupine and serve as a benchmark.

Introduction

Indonesia is a country that has many resources in animal diversity, including porcupines. Sunda porcupine (Hystrix javanica), also known as Javan porcupine, is endemic to Java, Madura, Bali, Lombok, Sumbawa, and Flores islands in Indonesia, which is classified as “Least Concern” (LC) on the International Union for Conservation of Nature red list of threatened species (Heinrich et al., 2020). This classification indicates that the species will not face a high risk of extinction shortly. However, despite its conservation status, illegal hunting and exploitation of the Sunda porcupine continue to increase, threatening the species sustainability (Farida et al., 2022). According to recent reports, porcupines are among the most commonly seen animals in Asian markets that have been surveyed (Cantlay et al., 2017). People poached and hunted the porcupine for food, ethnomedicinal application, and commercial purposes. Porcupine meat has important nutritional and supplementary values as a source of protein and medicinal substance, including aphrodisiac potency (Anita et al., 2015). Their visceral organs and gastrointestinal products, called bezoars, are used medicinally, while their hair and quills are employed as ornaments (Duffin, 2013).

In conservation efforts regarding the Sunda porcupine, research has been done on the carbohydrate residue distribution in the testes for spermatid maturation, which can serve as foundational information in the breeding strategy (Budipitojo et al., 2020a). The placenta anatomy of Sunda porcupine has been identified which has a discoid shape and is classified as the hemochorial type (Budipitojo et al, 2020b). Body measurements correlate with the body volume and body weight of the Sunda porcupine fetus, with a significant positive correlation found in horizontal body diameter, vertical body diameter, and head diameter (Adi et al, 2021). Ultrasonography measurements were performed to analyze the macroanatomy of the female reproductive in the Sunda porcupine without damaging the specimen (Wahid et al., 2020). Evaluation of the results of radiographs of the front legs of the sunda porcupine in the lateral view and dorso-palmar view does not show any abnormalities (Suparmin et al, 2018). Nevertheless, the data for determining the health status of H. javanica is rarely known due to the lack of research conducted. The most basic information to know about the health status of this animal is to have specific parameters regarding the hematological profile. Until now, there is no exact information on the normal hematological levels of the Sunda porcupine. However, hematological changes in the skin wound healing of Javan porcupine have been studied (Agungpriyono et al., 2020).

Knowledge of the hematological profile of blood can be a diagnostic and prognostic tool for porcupine’s health status. Hematological parameters and blood chemistry can be used to diagnose diseases, establish disease processes, and track disease development. Through hematological parameters, potential diseases can be identified to develop appropriate treatment strategies. Apart from that, hematological parameters also help monitor the prevalence of a disease so that disease spread can be prevented and effective disease management can be carried out (Proverbio et al., 2021). These insights are crucial for implementing timely interventions and maintaining the health of porcupine populations. This is because the well-being of the wild H. javanica is starting to be threatened. Porcupines can occasionally be discovered dead or ill in their natural habitat without documentation of the incident. Moreover, zoonotic pathogen transmission also jeopardizes human health since porcupines are denoted as the new host of leptospira (Cantlay et al., 2017). Studies on the hematological profiles of other porcupine species, such as the crested porcupine (Hystrix cristata), can reveal important details about their current health and possible illnesses. As an illustration, elevated urea and creatinine levels in crested porcupines may signify renal impairment resulting from leptospira infection (Coppola et al., 2020).

The present study aimed to investigate the hematology in relation to the blood cell morphology of the Sunda porcupine (H. javanica). The current findings will serve as reference data for health assessment studies, epidemiology, and the conservation of this remarkable species in Indonesia. Additionally, these findings will enhance our understanding of the population processes, ecological relationships, geographical variations, and physiological conditions of the Sunda porcupine. Assessing the health status of the Sunda porcupine can improve conservation initiatives, breeding strategies, and the prevention of zoonotic diseases. Therefore, preliminary data from routine blood evaluations and white blood cell (WBC) morphological features of free-range Sunda porcupines may provide insight into their health status compared to other species. These data are expected to serve as a valuable parameter for health assessment and pathogenesis prediction in species protection management and a one health approach.

Materials and Methods

This study used four Sunda porcupine blood samples (H. javanica) obtained from porcupine hunters in Tawangmangu subdistrict, Karanganyar Regency, Central Java. Blood collection can be done by taking blood from the neck, hands, feet, or from the heart. A blood sample of 3–5 ml is taken from the intracardiac with a 10 ml syringe after the porcupine is slaughtered and then placed in an EDTA tube (purple cap) in a vacutainer for the plasma sample, which will be used for the hematology examination process. This method refers to research from Coppola et al. (2020) Routine hematology examinations are carried out using the 5-Diff Vet Hematology Analyzer method. The hematological values analyzed included red blood cells (RBC), hemoglobin (Hgb), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular Hgb (MCH), MCH concentration (MCHC), RBC distribution width (RDW), platelets (PLT), mean platelet volume (MPV), platelet distribution width (PDW), platelet crit (PCT), WBCs, lymphocytes, monocytes, granulocytes, and eosinophils. Blood smears were fixed using methanol, and they were stained with Giemsa.

Ethical approval

For this research, ethical clearance (Number: 0129/EC-FKH/Int./2019) was obtained through the Research Ethics Commission of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Universitas Gadjah Mada, Yogyakarta.

(2004) range of normal lymphocytes (some adjacent to segmented neutrophilic granulocytes). Some larger lymphocytes with granules may occur in average persons. Occasionally, and without known triggers, the cytoplasm may expand. Lymphocytes are irregularly shaped, with different shapes from one monocyte to another. The cytoplasm is widened and narrowed to resemble the shape of the nucleus. The morphology of lymphocytes in other mammals (Theml et al., 2004) does not show a significant difference.

Discussion

There is a lack of information regarding the Sunda porcupine’s hematological profile. To confirm the hematological results of the Sunda porcupine, the comparison parameters in this study used the results from research using the crested porcupine by Coppola et al. (2020) using ten samples from the crested porcupine. The hematology results of the Sunda porcupine that we studied showed an average level of RBC (4.04 × 106/ul). Coppola et al. (2020) reported a higher value of 5.7 × 106/ul than this result. Sunda porcupine RBC is also lower on average compared to ferret RBC levels stated by Tilley et al. (2016) (6.60–12.18 × 106/ul), lower compared to Guinea pig (5.3–6.0 × 106/ul) (Coleman et al., 2019) and African hedgehog (5.01 × 106/ul) (Okorie-Kanu et al., 2014). Hgb levels in our Sunda porcupine showed an average level of 12.83 g/dl, not much different from Crested porcupines (13.6 g/dl), Guinea pigs (13–14.4 g/dl), and African hedgehogs (10.71–14.86 g/dl), and still within the normal range of Ferret (12.5–18.2 g/dL). The average hematocrit level of the Sunda porcupine (41.3%) was compared with that of the crested porcupine (34.8%) by Coppola et al. (2020). The hematocrit results of the Sunda porcupine were higher than those of the crested porcupine, compared to ferrets (37.5%–59%) by Tilley et al. (2016) and guinea pigs (42–46 g/dl). Sunda porcupine hematocrit levels are almost identical. MCV and MCH levels in Sunda porcupines are much higher compared to crested porcupines (MCV: 77.3 fl; MCH: 23.8 pg), ferrets (MCV: 43.6–61.2 fl; MCH: 14.5–20.5 pg) (Coppola et al., 2020; Tilley et al., 2016), and guinea pigs (MCV: 75–79 fl; MCH: 24–24.5 pg) also African hedgehog (MCV: 76.34–9.78 fl; MCH: 22.47–31.44 pg) (Okorie-Kanu et al, 2014). Sunda porcupine MCHC levels (31.2 g/dl) are in the same range as crested porcupine (30.1 g/dl), ferret (29.0–37.0 g/dl), guinea pig (30.9–31.4 g/dl), and African hedgehog (27.69–35.19 g/dl). From the parameters of the WBC component, the most different results from the Sunda porcupine are RBCs, which are lower than the others in the Sunda porcupine. Hence, the MCV and MCH of the Sunda porcupine are higher than the other animals.

The average WBC and platelet (PLT) levels in Sunda porcupine were 9.67 × 103/ul and 503.00 × 103/ul, respectively. These results are still in the range of values that are almost the same as the values for crested porcupines (WBC: 2.50–15.80 × 103/ul; PLT: 238–910 × 103/ul), ferrets (WBC: 8.1–22.5 × 103/ul; PLT: 86–1479 × 103/ul) (Tilley et al., 2016; Coppola et al. 2020), Guinea pigs (WBC: 6.3–6.4 × 103/ul; PLT: 260–278 × 103/ul) (Coleman et al., 2019), and African hedgehog (WBC:11.50–21.65 × 103/ul) (Okorie-Kanu et al., 2014). Neutrophils in Sunda porcupines (62.68%) are still in the range of ferrets (16.5%–81.30%) and African hedgehog (52%–76%), but the minimum levels in Sunda porcupines are like guinea pigs (43%–45%). The average basophil value of the Sunda porcupine (0.80%) is like the basophil level in ferrets (0.0%–1.8%), African hedgehog (0.0%–1.00%), and guinea pigs (0.2%). The average eosinophil value in Sunda porcupines (2.90%) is similar to that of ferrets (0.0%–6.7%), guinea pigs (1%–2.2%), and African hedgehog (0.0%–2.0%). The average Sunda porcupine lymphocytes (25.08%) are still in the range of ferret lymphocytes (15.3%–72.8%), crested porcupines (12.9%–44.6%), and African hedgehog (22%–47%), but when compared with the average guinea pig lymphocyte levels (49%–50%) they are low. Sunda porcupine monocyte levels (8.55%) when compared with ferret monocyte levels (0.0%–8.8%) are still within the range, whereas when compared with crested porcupine monocyte levels (2.7%–3.9%), African hedgehog (0.0%–4.0%), and in guinea pigs (3.5%–4.5%), Sunda porcupine monocyte levels are much higher.

The morphology of Sunda porcupine RBCs was not significantly different from the morphology of RBCs in other mammals. The morphology of WBCs in Sunda porcupine blood smear preparations shows that neutrophils have a ribbon-like nucleus and are segmented. Lymphocytes are irregularly shaped, with different shapes from one monocyte to another. A cytoplasm is widened, and a cytoplasm is narrowed to resemble the shape of the nucleus. The monocytes in the blood smear are seen to have gray lobes, nuclei, and cytoplasm with granulations. The Sunda porcupine eosinophil is seen to have lobes with granulations clustered around the nucleus. The morphology of Sunda porcupine WBCs, when compared with the morphology of WBCs in other mammals, does not show a significant difference. The Sunda porcupine is an endangered breed; thus, understanding the normal range of hematological and biochemical markers is critical for determining if the animal is healthy or suffering from a pathological condition. This knowledge is vital in plans for protecting endangered breeds, such as the Sunda porcupine, and the overall landscape of biodiversity. More research with a larger sunda porcupine population is required to establish a credible breed-based reference range.

Conclusion

The results of Sunda porcupine blood measurements were 4.04 × 106/ul for RBC; Hgb 12.83 g/dl; Hematocrit 37.8%; MCV 107.1 fl; MCH 31.80 pg; MCHC 31.23 g/dl; WBC 9.67 × 103/ul and platelet (PLT) value 503.00 × 103/ul. The morphology of red and WBCs from the Sunda porcupine does not differ significantly from the morphology of other mammals’ red and WBCs. Until now, there has been limited research on the hematology of Sunda porcupines, which has led to a limited understanding of their health status. We anticipate that the findings of this study will serve as a diagnostic instrument for evaluating the health condition of Sunda porcupine and serve as a benchmark.
