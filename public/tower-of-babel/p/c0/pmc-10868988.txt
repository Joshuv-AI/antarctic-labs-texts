ERK/MEK Pathway Regulates Th17 Cell Differentiation in Patients with Pemphigus Vulgaris
Authors: Kai Han, Song-Shan Li, Wen Pan, Mei-Nian Xu, Mei-Zhen Zhong, Wen-Jing Zhang, Xiao-Wen Huang, Kang Zeng
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:T helper (Th) cells are involved in the pathogenesis of pemphigus vulgaris (PV). However, the mechanism still needs more exploration.Aims:This study aimed to evaluate the molecular mechanism of the dysregulation of Th17 cells in the peripheral blood of patients with PV.Materials and Methods:Serum levels of IL-17 and anti-Dsg3 titres in patients with PV were analysed using ELISA. The mRNA expression of retinoic acid orphan receptor γt (RORγt) in CD4+ T cells was detected using reverse transcription–quantitative PCR (qPCR). The number of Th17 cells was examined using flow cytometry. Western blot analysis and immunofluorescent staining were also performed to investigate the expression levels of ERK/MAPK signalling proteins and Th17 lineage-associated proteins.Results:The proportion of Th17 cells and Th17 spectrum-associated proteins (p-STAT3, RORγt and IL-17) were upregulated in CD4+ cells in PV patients. The increased transcriptional levels of RORγt and IL-17 correlated positively with the severity of PV. Elevated phosphorylation of the ERK signalling factors was found in the collected CD4+ T cells in PV patients. The inhibition of the ERK signalling pathway significantly reduced the differentiation of Th17 cells in PV patients in vitro.Conclusion:Th17 cells are essential in the dysregulation of PV, and ERK signalling is involved in Th17-type immunity and promotes the development of PV. The study here provides us with a potential therapeutic target for PV.

Introduction

Pemphigus vulgaris (PV) is a chronic autoimmune disease with the clinical significance of blisters related to the key target antigens of desmogleins (Dsg1 and Dsg3).[1] The levels of Dsg1 and Dsg3 antibodies are associated with the appearance of PV; for example, patients with the most severe cutaneous and mucous lesions have both higher levels of Dsg1 and Dsg3 antibodies, while patients with a high level of Dsg1 antibody and a low level of Dsg3 antibody tend to present cutaneous lesions.[2] The diagnosis of PV mainly relies on the detection of tissue-bound and circulating autoantibodies, together with the clinical presentation.[1] PV starts to present as painful mucosal ulceration in the mouth, followed by lesions on the skin without any complaint of itches usually. For the treatments, corticosteroids and steroid-sparing drugs (azathioprine and mycophenolate mofetil) are widely used in the clinic. Immunotherapies are also approved, such as a humanised chimeric anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody (rituximab).[3] The immunotherapies could minimise the adverse effects of systemic corticosteroids, which makes them a promising therapeutic and critical research region for PV.

The key pathologies of pemphigus diseases include antibody generation and immune imbalance. The generation of desmoglein antibodies directly causes immune responses against adhesion molecules in keratinocytes, followed by breaking down membrane integrity under the formation of immune complexes, together with the appearance of blister and immune cell recruitment.[4] Beyond autoimmune antibodies, CD4+ T cells and inflammatory cytokines participate in pemphigus diseases. CD4+ T cells regularly help B cells with proliferation, differentiation and maturation (antibody production), as well as cause tissue damage through cytokine secretion. The immune imbalance of Th1/Th2 and Th17/Treg is considered to play a role in the pathogenesis of PV. Numerous studies have shown that IL-17+ cells in the skin biopsy samples[5] and IL-17 levels in the serum,[67] together with Th17 cells in the circulation of PV patients, significantly increased.[689] The transcription factor of RORγt expresses in Th17 cells plays a role in Th17 differentiation. It was reported that the relative expression of RORγt increased in PV patients.[6]

The p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK) signalling pathway plays a role in PV.[10] The activation of p38 MAPK might produce inflammatory mediators causing inflammation and tissue damage. p38 is phosphorylated and activated in human keratinocytes challenged with PV autoantibodies, PV patients’ skin and PV autoantibody-stimulated mice. Inhibition of p38 eliminates the blistering symptom in PV mice models.[1112] However, silencing of p38 MAPK in mice failed to block the formation of blisters after being challenged with PV autoantibodies.[13] Other signalling pathways might participate in the pathogenesis of PV.

ERK signalling pathway regulates Th17 cell functions and contributes to the pathogenesis of some autoimmune diseases.[1415] Therefore, we hypothesise that, as Th17 and IL-17 play a role in PV, ERK signalling pathway might weigh a bit in PV.[16] Thus, in this study, we focused on the isolated CD4+ T cells and Th17 cells from PV patients, determined the expression of the ERK pathway in peripheral blood CD4+ T cells and analysed the effect of ERK signalling on the differentiation of Th17 cells. It might provide a potential therapeutic strategy for treating PV and a basis for unravelling the pathogenesis.

Materials and Methods

Patients

The study was conducted at Nanfang Hospital (Guangzhou, China) from March 2019 to January 2020. Peripheral blood samples were obtained from fourteen patients with PV and ten sex- and age-matched healthy controls. All patients were diagnosed based on Japanese PV diagnostic criteria,[17] and the Autoimmune Bullous Skin Disorder Intensity Score (ABSIS) was used to assess the severity index of PV. The exclusion criteria contained other autoimmune diseases, cancers, metabolic diseases, severe infections, stress status and pregnant or breastfeeding women. Clinical information for the fourteen patients with PV is shown in Table 1. The Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University, approved the study (NFEC-2019-199). All subjects provided written informed consent, and the study adhered to the guidelines in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Isolation of peripheral CD4+ T cells

A total of 45–60 mL of peripheral venous blood was extracted from each PV patient and healthy control. PBMCs were isolated using the Ficoll–Hypaque density centrifugation (TBD Science, Tianjin, China). Positive selection was utilised to isolate CD4+ T cells with the EasySep Human CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from CD4+ T cells using the RNA Quick Purification Kit (Yishan Biotech, Shanghai, China). After reverse transcription, the synthesised cDNA was used for quantitative real-time PCR on the LightCycler 480 System (Roche, Switzerland). The relative expression of the target gene was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. The oligo sequences for RORγt (5’- GGCTCCCTGGATGAATAGAATG-3’ and 5’- AGGCAGAGGCAGAAAATGTAAAG-3’) and GAPDH (5’-AGAAGGCTGGGGCTCATTTG-3’ and 5’- AGGGGCCATCCACAGTCTTC-3’) were utilised in qPCR.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Sera were separated from the venous peripheral blood samples. The culture supernatant of CD4+ T cell was collected from the culture plate. Sera and culture supernatants were subjected to ELISA for measuring IL-17A levels using an ELISA kit for IL-17A (Shanghai ExCell Biology, Shanghai, China). The titres of autoantibody against Dsg3 were detected by the MESACUP Desmoglein TEST “Dsg3” kit (Medical and Biological Laboratories, Aichi, Japan). The optical density values were determined at 450 nm.

Western blot analysis

Isolated CD4+ T cells were lysed in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors on ice. The soluble partition was separated on an 8% or 12% SDS–PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane, which was blocked for 1 h at 25°C, and subsequently incubated with the indicated primary antibody [Supplementary Table 1] overnight at 4°C. HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or mouse antibodies were used as secondary antibodies. Proteins were visualised using enhanced chemiluminescence. Images were analysed by densitometry using ImageJ (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland). The calculation formula is as follows: relative expression of protein = grey value (target protein)/grey value (GAPDH).

Immunofluorescent staining

Isolated CD4+ T cells were spread on the slide-proof section, mounted and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Cells were permeabilised with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and the slides were blocked with goat serum. The indicated primary antibodies were diluted in PBS and applied overnight at 4°C. Secondary antibodies, Alexa Fluor 594 donkey anti-rabbit (H + L) highly cross-adsorbed and Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-chicken IgY (H + L), were subsequently utilised for 1 h at 25°C. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Digital images were captured with an Olympus BX53 upright microscope at 400 × magnification.

Th17 cell differentiation in vitro

The cell culture plate was coated with 2 μg/mL anti-human CD3 antibody (Catalogue No. 05121-25-500, BioGems, Rocky Hill, CT, USA) overnight at 4°C. After washing with PBS, isolated CD4+ T cells were suspended at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL and seeded with culture medium of X-VIVO 15 serum-free medium (Catalogue No. 04-418Q, Lonza, Alpharetta, GA, USA) containing 1 μg/mL anti-human CD28 antibody (Catalogue No. 10311-25-100, BioGems, Rocky Hill, CT, USA) and Th17 cell differentiation-related cocktail: IL-6 (30 ng/mL), TGF-β (2.25 ng/mL), IL-23 (40 ng/mL), IL-1β (30 ng/mL), anti-IFN-γ (5 μg/mL) and anti-IL-4 (5 μg/mL) (Catalogue Nos. 96-200-06-5, 96-200-23-2, 96-100-21C-2, 96-200-01B-2, 80812-25-100, and 81121-25-500; PeproTech, Rocky Hill, CT, USA).

The inhibition of MEK was performed by adding 10 μM of PD98059, which blocks the phosphorylation and activation of ERK1/2 with no effect on CD4+ T-cell proliferation.[18] DMSO was used as a reagent control for PD98059. The control group was not added to a cytokine cocktail. Cells were cultured for 4 days and subsequently analysed by flow cytometry, qPCR and ELISA.

Flow cytometry

Isolated PBMCs and cultured CD4+ T cells in vitro were stimulated with PMA or lonomycin mixture in the existence of BFA or monensin inhibitors (Catalogue Nod. CS1001 and CS1002; Multisciences (Lianke) Biotech, Hangzhou, China) at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 for 4 h. After being stained with PE-labelled anti-human CD3 antibody and FITC-labelled anti-human CD8a antibody (Catalogue Nos. 300307 and 301005; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), cells were fixed, permeabilised and blocked. The staining of APC-labelled anti-human IL-17A antibody (Catalogue No. 512333, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) was performed at last for 15 min. Finally, the levels of CD3+-, CD8- and IL-17+-expressing Th cells were determined on BD’s LSRFortessa Multidimensional HD Flow Cytometer and the data were analysed by Tree Star’s FlowJo software.

Statistical analysis

Following the data analysis for normal distribution, the percentage of Th17 cells and the relative levels of proteins between the two groups were compared using an independent-samples t-test. The mean value of the mRNA expression of RORγt and IL-17 between the patients with PV and healthy controls was analysed by the Mann–Whitney U-test. Spearman’s correlation test was used to evaluate the association between the variables. An independent-samples t-test was applied to analyse the differences among the groups used for the in vitro experiments. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS v20.0 software and GraphPad Prism 7.0. The level of significance was set at P < 0.05, and the data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Results

Increased cell proportion and dysfunction of Th17 cells in PV patients

To ascertain the role of Th17 cells in the pathogenesis of PV, flow cytometry was utilised to quantify peripheral Th17 cells. The blood was collected from seven patients with PV and seven healthy controls on the same day. The percentage of Th17 cells in PV patients’ peripheral CD4+ T cells was significantly increased (P = 0.0006) [Figure 1a] compared with that in healthy controls. Next, sera were isolated from fourteen PV patients and ten healthy controls. As shown in Figure 1b, IL-17 in serum was significantly upregulated (P = 0.0024) in PV patients compared with healthy controls. The enhanced expression of RORγt was confirmed in the RNA of CD4+ T cells from PV patients versus 10 healthy people (P = 0.0271) [Figure 1c]. Meanwhile, as shown in Figure 1d, the protein levels of p-STAT3, RORγt and IL-17 were significantly increased in PV patients. The increased expression level of p-STAT3 in PV patients was also confirmed by cellular immunofluorescence staining [Figure 1e]. These results suggested that Th17 cells and related factors were overactivated in patients with PV.

The positive correlation between RORγt mRNA level, IL-17 serum level and clinical severity of PV

The clinical presentations of patients are summarised in Table 1. PV patients had higher titres of autoantibody against the Dsg3 and clinical index of ABSIS, which are utilised to diagnose PV. As displayed in Figure 2a and b, the revealed RORγt expression levels in PV patients were positively correlated with the titres of autoantibody against Dsg3 (r = 0.561; P < 0.05) and ABSIS (r = 0.642; P < 0.05). Furthermore, there were also significant positive correlations between IL-17 serum levels and the titres of autoantibody against Dsg3 (r = 0.824; P < 0.001) [Figure 2c], as well as the ABSIS (r = 0.814; P < 0.001) [Figure 2d].

Increased expressions of MEK and ERK signalling proteins in CD4+ T cells from PV patients

We continued to explore the MAPK-dependent mechanism involved in Th17 cells in patients with PV. p-MEK and p-ERK were significantly increased compared with healthy controls [Figure 3a]. These were further confirmed by immunofluorescence staining, which revealed an increased accumulation of p-MEK1/2 and p-ERK1/2 proteins in CD4+ T cells from PV patients [Figure 3b and c]. These results indicated the ERK signalling pathway was activated in PV subjects.

Inhibition of MEK-ERK cascade-suspended Th17 cell polarisation from PV patients

Following the identification of increased Th17 cell-associated factors and the activation of the ERK signalling pathway in PV patients, the role of the ERK signalling pathway during Th17 differentiation in PV was investigated in vitro using human primary cells. PD98059 is a highly selective in vitro inhibitor of MEK activation and the MAP kinase (ERK) cascade. CD4+ T cells were isolated from PV patients’ peripheral blood (control: healthy people) and treated with PD98059 (solvent control: DMSO) under Th17 cell polarisation conditions in vitro. There were four groups: DMSO-treated healthy control, PD98059-treated healthy control, DMSO-treated PV patient and PD98059-treated PV patient. As demonstrated in Figure 4a, PD98059 inhibited the proportion of Th17 cells under the condition of Th17 cell polarisation.

Inhibition of the ERK signalling prevented Th17 cell differentiation in PV. The inhibition was performed in vitro by the addition of PD98059. DMSO was used as a reagent control. (c) The IL-17 levels in cell culture supernatant were detected by ELISA. *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01

We next examined the mRNA level of RORγt under the Th17 cell polarisation condition with or without PD98059. After inhibition with PD98059, the elevated expression of RORγt was abolished [Figure 4b]. The polarisation of Th17 cells secreted more cytokine of IL-17. Furthermore, the blockade of ERK activation by PD98059 promoted the downregulation of secreted IL-17 from in vitro cells [Figure 4c]. These data demonstrated the importance of the MEK/ERK signalling pathway in PV-associated Th17 cell development and differentiation. The MEK/ERK pathway inhibition might be a novel, potential treatment for PV patients.

Discussion

Damaged immune tolerance and abnormal T-cell response are contributing factors in the development of PV. The imbalance of Th17 could lead to the abnormal activation of reactive T cells and promote B-cell proliferation and antibody production.[19] IL-17 from Th17 cells promotes B-cell differentiation along with B-cell-activating factors, thereby enhancing the antibody response.[20] Some studies revealed a higher accumulation of IL-17 in the serum and in the lesions of PV patients, which were consistent with our results here.[5] Multiple types of cells could secrete IL-17, and we focus on the expression of IL-17 produced by Th17 cells. The increased IL-17 partly contributed to the infiltration of superficial dermal neutrophils and eosinophils in the pathology of PV. It has been reported that IL-17 inhibitors were effective in multiple mucocutaneous disorders.[21] Although there are no sufficient clinical data for the use of IL-17 inhibitors in PV currently, the study here provides us with a potential therapeutic target for PV. According to our previous research,[22] we confirmed that key transcriptional factors (RORγt and p-STAT3) were activated abnormally in Th17 cells from PV patients. Based on this, we further assessed these key factors and found that they were positively correlated with the clinical severity of PV. It confirmed that Th17 cells were involved in the development of pemphigus inflammation.

The activated signalling pathways and molecules are involved in the keratinocytes of PV, including the p38-MAPK signalling pathway, epidermal growth factor receptor, Rho GTPase, MYC proto-oncogene and caspase pathways. Recently, the role of the ERK signalling pathway in skin lesions of PV was reported by showing that a MEK/ERK inhibitor (U0126) effectively ameliorated blister formation and ultrastructure alterations of desmosome.[16] Furthermore, suppressing the MEK/ERK signalling pathway by PD98059 and U0126 significantly sensitised the corticosteroid-induced transactivation of anti-inflammatory genes in human epidermal keratinocytes, which was helpful in treating PV.[23]

Increasing evidence indicates that the ERK/MAPK signalling pathway is critical in regulating the differentiation of Th17 cells; however, in different diseases the mechanism remains controversial. The research suggested that the MEK/ERK signalling pathway positively regulated Th17 cell development by inducing RORγt and suppressing Foxp3, contributing to colitis pathogenesis.[14] Recent studies indicated that administering CL097 could promote the autoreactive Th17 cell response and its lineage-associated genes, mediated by ERK activation in experimental autoimmune uveitis mice.[24] miR-181a decreases Th17 cell activation by enhancing the ERK signalling cascade.[25] Furthermore, ERK inhibitors suppress dendritic cells to induce antigen-specific Th17 cells. By enhancing the signalling cascade from the T-cell receptor, it reduces the threshold of Th17 cell activation. In contrast, this studydemonstrated that another ERK inhibitor did not inhibit Th17 cell proliferation.[26] Using U0126, Tan and Lam suggested that the inhibition of the ERK/MAPK signalling pathway promotes Th17 differentiation in vivo.[27] In the present study, we confirmed the positive role of the ERK/MAPK signalling pathway for regulating Th17 in PV. Suppressing the ERK/MAPK signalling pathway attenuated Th17 cell differentiation by decreasing RORγt expression and secretion of IL-17. The ERK/MAPK pathway influences the severity of PV by affecting the differentiation of Th17 cells. We provide some new aspects in understanding the pathogenesis of PV, but we also recognise the unavoidable limitations. Although the relationship between Th17 cells and ERK/MAPK signalling pathways in the pathogenesis of PV has been demonstrated here, the mechanism underlying the regulation of Th17 differentiation by ERK/MAPK signalling still needs further research, focusing on the ERK/MAPK signalling pathway’s specific molecular mechanism regulating Th17 cell differentiation.

Conclusions

In summary, the present study highlights the critical role of Th17 cells in the pathogenesis of PV. Aberrantly increased ERK/MAPK signalling may lead to a disruption of pathogen-targeting Th17-type immunity and exacerbate PV severity. Suppression of this signalling pathway relieves the inflammation reaction and reduces the recruitment of various inflammatory cells in PV. Further investigation into this mechanism may offer a novel strategy to understand the pathogenesis of PV and provide a potential molecular target for treating this disease.
