Impact of Analytical Decisions on Physical Activity Outcomes in a Cardiovascular Mobile Health Study
Authors: Tianyi Wang, Evan Luff, Tanima Basu, Michael P Thompson, Mark W Newman, Lesli E Skolarus, Michael P Dorsch, Brahmajee K Nallamothu, Jessica R Golbus
Published: 2026

Mobile health (mHealth) technologies have automated collection of behavioral and physiologic data, allowing these devices to support lifestyle modification and outcomes assessments in clinical trials. Despite their growing use, few standards exist around aggregating and analyzing mHealth data. Consequently, analytic decisions for even seemingly simple questions like how to define “missing” data based on wear time can vary substantially, and the impact of these decisions on interpretation of results is poorly understood. The purpose of our study is to understand, as proof of principle, the impact of analytic decisions related to missing wear time on mHealth trial outcomes within the framework of a previously conducted cardiovascular clinical trial.

The data supporting this study's findings are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The study was approved by the University of Michigan Health institutional review board (HUM00205845). The myBPmyLife trial was a randomized controlled trial of an mHealth intervention among patients with hypertension. 1 All participants were provided with a Fitbit Versa 2 and followed for 6 months. Minute‐by‐minute and daily aggregate data related to heart rate (HR) and step count were extracted from each participant's device from study days 1 to 180 (N=102 060 data points). We defined adherent days by 4 different methods based on approaches used in previous clinical trials: wear time ≥8 hours by the Fitbit device's built‐in sensor, wear time ≥8 hours by calculating aggregated HR minutes (ie, total distinct minutes in which a HR was recorded by the smartwatch each day), ≥100 daily steps, and ≥500 daily steps. 2 , 3 , 4 Data were considered available on days that participants met thresholds for adherence, with data from remaining days categorized as missing. The percentage of missing days was calculated as days in which data was absent or participants were considered nonadherent divided by the total number of study days.

Daily step counts by each method for adherent days and daily wear time were compared with all available data (reference standard). Median differences and corresponding 95% CIs were estimated using bootstrap resampling with 2000 iterations; 95% CIs were defined by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution of median differences. Analyses were performed using SAS version 14.2 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

A total of 602 participants enrolled in the myBPmyLife study between December 2021 and July 2023. Mean age was 59.6 years (SD 13.6), 74.4% self‐identified as White, and 48% as women. Compared with the reference standard (all data), wear time assessments based on HR and step count resulted in significant reductions in available data (range 8.1%–13.4%; Table1). Approaches filtering on HR counts and step count resulted in fewer adherent days though substantially higher daily step counts as compared with the reference standard and proprietary Fitbit data, which had similar step counts and adherent days. The minute‐level HR approach had the fewest adherent days and the highest estimated daily step counts (705 [95% CI, 683–730] step difference compared with reference; Table1). Although estimated step counts were numerically higher for the minute‐level HR approach, the results were similar to simpler approaches that filtered on a minimum daily step count.

In this secondary analysis of a previously conducted cardiovascular clinical trial, we found that the analytic approach used to account for “missing” data resulted in substantial differences in estimated daily step count. Approaches using aggregated HR minutes or step count resulted in less usable data, but higher and potentially more accurate representations of daily physical activity compared with more inclusive approaches that used proprietary Fitbit data or all available device data. Importantly, the 705‐step difference between the HR method and the reference standard approaches the minimum clinically significant difference of 1000 steps cited for many cardiovascular conditions, highlighting the importance of standardizing these decisions in mHealth trials to enhance generalizability and cross‐study comparisons. For example, a meta‐analysis found that a 1000‐step increase was associated with a 15% decreased risk of all‐cause mortality. 5

Our results should be interpreted within the context of their limitations. First, this was an analysis of a single cardiovascular mHealth trial performed using a single device. Thus, results may not be generalizable to other populations or devices. Second, participants did not maintain self‐reported logs of device wear time. Third, we evaluated the impact of these approaches on aggregate measures of watch wear over 6‐months. Finally, we focused on a limited number of approaches to missing data and daily step count though future studies are needed to evaluate additional analytic approaches and health outcomes (eg, sleep).

In conclusion, variations in approach to determining missing wear time data can lead to substantially different results for mHealth physical activity metrics. These differences may challenge result interpretation and limit comparisons across trials. These findings demonstrate, as proof‐of‐concept, the importance of standardizing approaches for data aggregation and analysis of physical activity outcomes within mHealth trials.
