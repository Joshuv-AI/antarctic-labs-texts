ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRegional anesthesia techniques are increasingly used to improve analgesia and reduce opioid consumption after breast surgery. Several approaches have been proposed, including paravertebral block and different fascial plane blocks, but their comparative effectiveness remains uncertain. This network meta-analysis (NMA) aimed to compare the efficacy and safety of regional anesthesia techniques in patients undergoing mastectomy with or without axillary clearance. A systematic review and NMA of randomized controlled trials were conducted according to established methodological guidelines. Studies evaluating regional anesthesia techniques in patients undergoing mastectomy were included. Outcomes assessed were intraoperative opioid consumption, 24-hour postoperative morphine milligram equivalents (MMEs), pain at rest and with movement at 12 and 24 hours, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), use of rescue analgesics, and the incidence of complications such as pneumothorax and local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST). Regional anesthesia techniques were associated with improved analgesic outcomes compared with LA infiltration or no regional block. Paravertebral block showed the most consistent benefit across outcomes, particularly for opioid consumption and postoperative pain. Fascial plane blocks demonstrated comparable performance to paravertebral blocks for most outcomes, although paravertebral blocks showed superior results for pain on movement. LA infiltration alone or the absence of regional anesthesia performed worse across most outcomes. Serious complications were rarely reported. Paravertebral block appears to provide the most consistent analgesic benefit for mastectomy when performed by experienced practitioners. Fascial plane blocks represent a reasonable alternative when a paravertebral block is not feasible. Simple surgical infiltration alone appears insufficient for optimal analgesia in major breast surgery.

Introduction

Major breast surgery, including mastectomy with or without sentinel lymph node biopsy or axillary lymph node dissection, remains associated with a substantial burden of perioperative pain despite the widespread adoption of multimodal analgesic strategies.[12]

Although frequently considered routine procedures, these operations may result in clinically significant postoperative pain, delayed functional recovery, prolonged hospital stay, and reduced patient satisfaction.[3]

Moreover, inadequate control of acute postoperative pain after breast surgery has been recognized as a relevant contributor to the development of chronic postsurgical pain, with important consequences for long-term quality of life.[3] These unresolved clinical issues, together with the marked heterogeneity in perioperative analgesic practice, provided the rationale for the development of the Italian expert consensus on regional anesthesia in breast surgery that has been previously published.[4]

Current guidelines recommend the use of opioid-sparing perioperative strategies as part of multimodal analgesia.[5] However, the growing number of available regional anesthesia techniques, combined with the limited number of direct head-to-head randomized trials, makes it difficult to determine their comparative effectiveness. Network meta-analysis (NMA) provides a valuable methodological approach in this context, as it allows the simultaneous comparison of multiple interventions by integrating both direct and indirect evidence within a single analytical framework. The aim of this NMA is to provide readers and panelists with updated evidence regarding regional anesthesia interventions for patients undergoing mastectomy with or without axillary clearance.

Methods

Methods have been published in extenso in a previous publication.[4]

Briefly, we followed best-practice guidelines for conducting and reporting systematic reviews.[67]

First, a systematic search of the medical literature was conducted to identify relevant studies in the following databases: PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Scopus; the full search strategy is provided in Supplementary Material (SM) 1.

No language restrictions were applied to the search, and the databases were queried from their inception to the date of the search (last updated June 12, 2025). We aimed to include randomized controlled studies evaluating adult patients undergoing major breast surgery (P), comparing any regional anesthesia intervention (I) with another intervention, placebo, sham block, or no intervention (C). The list of regional anesthesia blocks was selected according to the latest European Society of Regional Anaesthesia and Pain Therapy/American Society of Regional Anesthesia (ESRA/ASRA) consensus on nomenclature standardization for chest wall blocks.[8]

The outcomes (O) investigated in this NMA were intraoperative opioid consumption (converted to fentanyl equivalents), 24-hour postoperative morphine milligram equivalents (MMEs), pain at rest and with movement at 12 and 24 hours, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), use of rescue analgesics, and the incidence of complications such as pneumothorax and local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST).

Two investigators independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records to identify potentially eligible studies. Full texts of selected articles were assessed independently by two reviewers for eligibility. Data extraction was performed independently by two authors using a predefined data collection form. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion, and if consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted. For outcomes related to opioid consumption, doses were converted to MMEs using the GlobalRPh morphine equivalent calculator, applying a 0% cross-tolerance modifier (http://www.globalrph.com/narcotic).

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool.[9] Disagreements were resolved through discussion or by consultation with a third reviewer when necessary.

The NMA was conducted using R software (version 4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the “netmeta” package. Treatment effects for continuous outcomes were expressed as mean differences (MD) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Dichotomous outcomes were reported as odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% CI.

Interventions were ranked according to the frequentist analog of the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA).[10]

When studies reported continuous data as medians with interquartile ranges, these were converted to estimated means and standard deviations (SDs) using the method described by Hozo et al.[11]

No continuity correction was applied for zero-event studies.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the Chi-squared test and the I² statistic, interpreted as low (<25%), moderate (25–50%), or high (>50%) heterogeneity.[12] Within-design heterogeneity and between-design inconsistency were evaluated using the Cochrane Q statistic. A random-effects model was applied irrespective of the degree of heterogeneity or inconsistency. Publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots and by using the Egger test whenever appropriate.

A large language model (ChatGPT 5.2; OpenAI) was used solely to improve grammar and fluency; all content was critically reviewed and verified by the authors, who retained full responsibility for the manuscript.[13]

Intraoperative opioids

A total of 44 studies reported outcomes for intraoperative opioids. The network suggested that most regional techniques reduced opioid requirements versus control, although the certainty of relative rankings should be interpreted cautiously because heterogeneity was very high (τ² =727.75; I² =97.6%). Against control, the greatest reduction was observed with IP + PS plane block (MD: −44.56, 95% CI: −59.34 to − 29.78; P < 0.001), followed closely by PVB (MD: −41.50, 95% CI: −57.92 to − 25.08; P < 0.001). Accordingly, the SUCRA ranking placed the IP + PS plane block first (0.794), followed by PVB (0.722), whereas control ranked last (0.084).

Postoperative opioid consumption

Seventy-two studies reported MME as an outcome. All blocks, with the exception of the PS plane block—for which the sample size was extremely limited, as noted above—demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in MME compared with no intervention. The effect sizes ranged from − 8.16 for the deep SAP block to − 6.27 for the superficial SAP block, suggesting a broadly similar and clinically meaningful reduction in opioid consumption across these interventions; again, the heterogeneity was very high (τ² =11.65; I² =98.7%).

Pain at 12 hours

For pain at 12 hours at rest, the network favored several block techniques over control, but heterogeneity remained substantial for pain at rest (τ² =0.83; I² =97.0%) and on movement (τ² =1.18; I² =97.4%).

PVB showed the greatest and most credible reduction versus control on movement (MD: −1.91, 95% CI: −2.69 to − 1.14; P < 0.001), followed by ESP (MD: −1.54, 95% CI: −2.24 to − 0.84; P < 0.001), IP + PS plane block (MD: −1.55, 95% CI: −2.30 to − 0.80; P < 0.001), superficial SAP block (MD: −0.97, 95% CI: −2.16 to 0.23; P = 0.112), and deep SAP block (MD: −0.64, 95% CI: −1.51 to 0.22; P = 0.145).

At rest, PVB remained one of the strongest reduction in pain (MD: −1.12, 95% CI: −1.50 to − 0.74; P < 0.001) but without any clinically significant difference with other fascial plane blocks, IP + PS plane block (MD: −1.27, 95% CI: −1.65 to − 0.89; P < 0.001), superficial SAP block (MD: −1.12, 95% CI: −1.65 to − 0.59; P < 0.001), ESP (MD: −1.05, 95% CI: −1.41 to − 0.70; P < 0.001), and deep SAP block (MD: −1.04, 95% CI: −1.57 to − 0.50; P < 0.001).

Pain at 24 hours

PVB emerged as the most favorable intervention for pain at rest (MD: −1.24, 95% CI: −1.49 to − 0.99; P < 0.001) followed by superficial SAP block (MD: −1.01, 95% CI: −1.34 to − 0.67; P < 0.001), ESP (MD: −0.98, 95% CI: −1.22 to − 0.75; P < 0.001), deep SAP (MD: −0.90, 95% CI: −1.25 to − 0.55; P < 0.001), and IP + PS plane block (MD: −0.94, 95% CI: −1.19 to − 0.69; P < 0.001).

For pain at 24 hours on movement, PVB again ranked highest and showed the largest, most robust effect among the well-represented interventions. Compared with control, PVB reduced pain by MD: −1.94 (95% CI: −2.39 to − 1.49; P < 0.001), ESP block (MD: −1.29, 95% CI: −1.69 to − 0.88; P < 0.001), and superficial SAP block (MD: −0.72, 95% CI: −1.35 to − 0.10; P = 0.024). Deep SAP block did not clearly differ from control (MD: −0.57, 95% CI: −1.12 to − 0.02; P = 0.041 in the summary, a marginal effect), whereas intertransverse process block and LA infiltration showed no significant benefit.

Postoperative nausea and vomiting

PONV was the most statistically coherent outcome in the network, with minimal heterogeneity (τ² =0.028; I² =5.6%), making these estimates more internally consistent than those for pain and opioid consumption. Compared with control, IP + PS plane block (OR: 0.36, 95% CI: 0.27–0.50; P < 0.001), PVB (OR: 0.39, 95% CI: 0.30–0.51; P < 0.001), ESP (OR: 0.36, 95% CI: 0.27–0.47; P < 0.001), deep SAP (OR: 0.37, 95% CI: 0.23 to 0.60; P < 0.001), superficial SAP block (OR: 0.58, 95% CI: 0.36 to 0.94; P = 0.028), and retrolaminar block (OR: 0.24, 95% CI: 0.10 to 0.57; P = 0.001) all reduced PONV. LA infiltration was the only intervention associated with significantly worse PONV than control (OR: 2.91, 95% CI: 1.37–6.15; P = 0.005).

Rescue analgesics

For rescue analgesic requirements, the direction of effect strongly favored most regional techniques over control, although heterogeneity was moderate (τ² =0.956; I² =69.4%). The most reliable estimates again came from PVB, ESP block, IP + PS plane block, and control, with deep SAP block contributing moderate representation; intertransverse process and retrolaminar blocks remained limited by small numbers. Relative to control, superficial SAP block had the lowest odds of requiring rescue analgesia (OR: 0.09, 95% CI: 0.03 to 0.29; P < 0.001), followed by retrolaminar block (OR: 0.05, 95% CI: 0.01 to 0.51; P = 0.011), IP + PS plane block (OR: 0.12, 95% CI: 0.05 to 0.27; P < 0.001), PVB (OR: 0.13, 95% CI: 0.06 to 0.27; P < 0.001), ESP block (OR: 0.15, 95% CI: 0.08–0.28; P < 0.001), and deep SAP block (OR: 0.16, 95% CI: 0.06 to 0.43; P < 0.001).

Pneumothorax and LAST

Complications were reported very rarely in the retrieved studies. Pneumothorax was investigated in 70 studies, and only two reported its occurrence in patients undergoing ESP block (four patients in study[45] and one patient in study[22]), corresponding to an overall incidence of 0.3%. No cases of pneumothorax were reported in the remaining studies.

Signs of LAST were explicitly investigated in 64 studies. However, no cases of LA toxicity were reported in any of the included studies, highlighting the favorable safety profile of the commonly used anesthetic doses.

Risk of bias assessment

The overall risk of bias across the included studies was generally adequate [Figure 3], with some studies having a low risk of bias (n = 24), with a substantial proportion of trials demonstrating adequate randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, and complete outcome reporting. However, several studies (n = 43) were judged as having “some concerns,” mainly due to insufficient reporting of allocation concealment, incomplete blinding of personnel, or lack of a clearly pre-specified analysis plan. The remaining studies (n = 34) were rated as high risk of bias, most commonly due to absence of blinding, incomplete outcome data, or unclear randomization methods.

Discussion

Overall, the network supports a consistent benefit of PVB, ESP block, and IP + PS plane block, with deep SAP block also performing well for several outcomes. Retrolaminar and, to a lesser extent, superficial SAP block often ranked highly, but their apparent performance should be interpreted more cautiously because they were much less representative than the major nodes. LA infiltration was generally the weakest active strategy and was the only intervention associated with worse PONV.

Our results suggest that PVB should be used for major breast surgery whenever practitioners have adequate experience and confidence with this technique. However, fascial plane blocks represent a valid alternative, as their performance was comparable to PVB for most outcomes, with the exception of pain on movement. In contrast, simple LA infiltration or no regional block performed worse across all outcomes, suggesting that surgical field infiltration alone is insufficient for major breast surgery.

Several limitations of this NMA should be acknowledged. First, substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed for most pain and opioid-related outcomes, likely reflecting clinical variability across studies in surgical procedures, anesthetic techniques, block timing, LA type and dose, and perioperative analgesic protocols. Second, the network relied largely on indirect comparisons, as relatively few head-to-head randomized trials directly compared different regional techniques, which may reduce the certainty of relative rankings. Third, some interventions, particularly retrolaminar block, intertransverse process block, and PS block alone, were represented by a small number of studies and limited sample sizes, making their estimates less robust and potentially unstable. Fourth, outcome reporting was not fully standardized across trials, and several studies reported data as medians or used different pain scales, requiring statistical conversions that may introduce imprecision. Finally, although complications were rarely reported, the included studies were not primarily designed to detect uncommon adverse events, and therefore, the safety estimates should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusion

In patients undergoing major breast surgery, regional anesthesia techniques consistently reduce postoperative pain, opioid consumption, and PONV compared with no block or simple LA infiltration. When a paravertebral block is not feasible, fascial plane blocks provide effective alternatives and should be incorporated into a multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesic strategy.

Overall, this NMA provides the quantitative evidence base underpinning the consensus process. The final stage of the project will be presented in a subsequent dedicated publication integrating these findings with the Delphi process and reporting the consensus statements developed in response to the 11 pre-specified PICO questions.
