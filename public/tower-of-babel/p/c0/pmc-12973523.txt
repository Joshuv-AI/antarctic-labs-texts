Extreme acidosis in hospitalised patients: A 10-year single-centre, retrospective analysis
Authors: Zoe Y Guo, Humphrey GM Walker, Alastair J Brown, Pamisha Purewal, David Reid, John Santamaria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAcidaemia is a commonly occurring abnormality of acid-base balance. The literature assessing extreme acidosis (pH ≤ 7) is limited and is largely focused on those admitted to intensive care. Therefore, we sought to review the demographics, aetiologies, and outcomes of all hospitalised patients with extreme acidosis.Design and SettingThis was a retrospective cohort study conducted at a single centre, tertiary hospital in Melbourne, Australia Participants: Patients with acidosis were identified using the hospital pathology database. Exclusion criteria included hospital readmissions or samples taken in a non-inpatient setting. Main Outcome Measures: The primary outcome was 30-day mortality. Secondary outcomes included 90-day mortality and the association of mortality with type and aetiology of acidosis. Data were analysed using a Cox proportional hazards model.ResultsBetween June 2015 and June 2025, 162,697 patients had 451,788 multi-day admissions. Five hundred eighty-six admissions were identified, in which a patient had a measured pH ≤ 7.0. The final cohort consisted of 493 first admission episodes. Three hundred twenty-four (68.5 %) were male with a median age of 59.3 (interquartile range: 41.2–71.7). By day 30, 226 (45.8 %) of patients had died. Mixed acidosis (321, 65.1 %) and cardiorespiratory arrest (160, 32.5 %) were the most frequently occurring type and aetiology of acidosis. Relative to metabolic acidosis, both mixed acidosis (adjusted hazard ratio (aHR): 2.25, 95 % CI: 1.55–3.27) and respiratory acidosis (aHR: 2.08, 95 % CI: 1.01–4.27) had a higher hazard of 30-day mortality. Causes associated with the highest risk of 30-day mortality were vascular (aHR: 31.2, 95 % CI: 9.60–101) and cardiorespiratory arrest (aHR: 27.0, 95 % CI: 9.17–79.4). One hundred nine (22.1 %) patients did not require intensive care unit admission; however, 37 (33.9 %) of these patients still had died by day 30.ConclusionsExtreme acidosis is extremely rare in hospitalised inpatients. Mortality varies according to type and aetiology of the cause of acidosis and therefore careful consideration as to the implications of extreme acidosis when considering cessation of treatment is crucial.Keywords: Intensive care, Acidaemia, Acidosis, pH

Introduction

Normal blood pH is between 7.35 and 7.45, and acidaemia is present if pH is < 7.35.1 There are a number of pathophysiological processes that alter the body’s ability to maintain acid-base homeostasis.2 Acidosis is a potentially life-threatening abnormality and can both be a symptom of, or cause of severe illness. Severe acidosis can have profound effects on a number of physiological processes including cardiovascular and pulmonary functions.3,4

Mochizuki et al. reported that over half of intensive care unit (ICU) admissions were acidotic within the first 24 h, and this group of patients had a 10 % in-hospital mortality rate.5 Moderate or severe acidosis (pH ≤ 7.20); however, is rarer and associated with poorer outcomes. An observational study conducted in 18 ICUs in Japan, Taiwan, and Australia found moderate or severe acidosis affected approximately 15 % of admissions.6 In randomised trials (BICARICU 1 and 2) assessing ICU patients with metabolic acidosis, and a pH of ≤7.2 overall mortality rates were between 60 and 70 %.7,8 However, most patients in these trials had a pH at randomisation of >7.10. Several observational studies have assessed patients admitted to ICU with extreme acidosis (pH ≤ 7.0).[9], [10], [11] However, these studies were small (a total of 336 patients), only included those admitted to ICU and focused on shorter-term survival metrics. There is limited literature that assesses all hospitalised patients with extreme acidosis. Thus, our study sought to describe the 30-day mortality of extreme acidosis in the general inpatient population, regardless of whether the patient had been admitted to ICU.

Methods

Study population

We conducted a single-centre retrospective cohort study in a tertiary Australian public hospital between June 2015 and June 2025. St Vincent’s Hospital, Melbourne, is a 450-bed tertiary university-affiliated hospital. The hospital undertakes cardiac surgery and neurosurgery and is supported by a single intensive care unit of 19 beds. The study was approved by St Vincent’s Hospital, Melbourne, as a quality assurance project (2023/SVH00228 and QA 25037).

Patients with extreme acidosis were identified via blood gas (arterial or venous) results obtained from a database of hospital pathology results. Any admission in which a blood gas had a recorded pH ≤ 7.0 was potentially eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria included subsequent readmissions to hospital within the 10-year period, a clinical history that suggested that the pH was incongruent with other laboratory values, and blood gases taken outside the inpatient setting (rehabilitation and mental health). Data relating to comorbidities, frailty, diagnosis, ICU utilisation, and outcomes were taken from hospital administrative data. Subsequent admissions or attendances to hospital alongside hospital mortality and administrative data were used to identify a patient’s vital status. Frailty risk was defined as a Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS) ≥ 512 and comorbidities were defined by the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index.13

Blood gas type

Both arterial and venous blood gases (VBGs) were included in an analysis. Blood gas analyses were performed on ABL90 FLEX® blood gas analysers (Radiometer Medical ApS, Brønshøj, Denmark). Blood gases were classified as metabolic (PCO2 <40 mm Hg and HCO3- <24 mmol/L), respiratory (PCO2 >40 mm Hg and HCO3- ≥24 mmol/L), or mixed acidosis (PCO2 ≥40 mmHg and HCO3-<24 mmol/L).9

Aetiology

Patient’s charts were reviewed by a single author to establish the diagnosis (ZG, PP). The clinical notes were used to ascertain the most temporally proximate diagnosis leading to the blood gas abnormality. Any disagreements were resolved by consensus with a senior member of the study team (JS, HGW). International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) diagnoses codes were available from administrative data. These were used to crosscheck aetiological diagnosis, and if a patient had multiple potential aetiologies leading to acidosis, then the relevant principal discharge diagnosis based on the ICD-10 discharge diagnosis was used. Reviewers were unblinded to outcomes. These diagnostic codes are shown in Table S1.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the rate of 30-day mortality for all patients. Secondary outcomes included in-hospital mortality, 90-day mortality, association of 30-day mortality with both type and aetiology of acidosis, requirement for ICU admission, and the trajectory of pH recovery. Two post-hoc analyses were conducted. First, the association of initial pH severity and 30-day mortality was assessed. Second, a sensitivity analysis whereby thresholds delineating acidosis type were altered to account for potential differences in arterial and venous CO2 tensions was assessed.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics, outcomes, causes and types of acidosis were summarised using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were presented using count and percentages and compared using the chi-square test. Continuous variables were presented using the median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test as appropriate. The 30-day mortality was analysed, and comparisons were made between different types of acidosis using the Kaplan–Meier estimator and the log-rank test for equality of survivor functions. Two separate models were fitted using the Cox proportional hazards test to assess the relationship between (1) type of acidosis and (2) aetiology of acidosis with 30-day mortality. For adjusted analyses, the covariates for inclusion were decided a priori and were the same for each model. These were chosen based on clinical reasoning. These included age and lactate at the time the blood gas was taken. Collinearity between continuous variables was assessed. ICU admission was included as a stratification variable. A random effect for each patient was included. Adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) are presented with 95 % confidence intervals (95 % CIs). The proportionality assumption was checked using Schoenfeld residuals. The reference levels were metabolic acidosis and seizures for blood gas type and aetiology, respectively. To model the trajectory of pH recovery, all pH measurements after the first blood gas showing a pH ≤ 7.0 were included. The time of the first blood gas with a pH ≤ 7.0 was T0. Patients who only had a single blood gas measurement or died prior to 6 h were excluded from this analysis. Locally estimated scatterplot smoothing curves of the mean pH and 95 % CI were plotted.

To account for the 10-year timeframe, a sensitivity analysis was conducted where year was added as a covariate to the analyses. A second post-hoc sensitivity analysis altered the CO2 thresholds in venous gases only. The venous CO2 threshold for classifying the type of acidosis was changed to 50 mmHg based on the difference in CO2 tensions demonstrated. An additional post-hoc analysis was also conducted, in which a linear model was used to assess pH severity and the association of 30-day mortality.

No missing data were imputed. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R (R Core Team, Vienna, 2024).

Ethics approval

The study was approved as low risk with a waiver of individual patient consent by St Vincent’s Hospital Melbourne Ethics Committee (2023/SVH00228).

Results

Between June 2015 and June 2025, 162,697 patients had 451,788 multi-day admissions (i.e. at least overnight). Of these, 493 admission episodes (493 patients) were included for analysis (0.003 % of all patients). Reasons for exclusions are shown in Fig. 1.

Patient characteristics

Of the 493 included, 324 (68.5 %) were male, with a median age of 59.3 years (IQR 41.2–71.7). Amongst the cohort, 176 (35.7 %) had a HFRS ≥5 and 343 (69.6 %) had two or more Elixhauser comorbidities. The minimum pH per visit was 6.9 (6.8–7.0) and the median lactate was 10.3 mmol/L (IQR: 5.5–15.0). The median hospital length of stay in survivors was 6.8 (IQR: 2.7–14.1) days. This is shown in Table 1. A total of 32.4 % (n = 160) of the initial gases were arterial, with 67.1 % (n = 331) venous. For two blood gas samples, the type was unknown. Table S2 shows the characteristics by blood gas type. Overall, 24.1 % (n = 119) never had an arterial sample taken. The median number of blood gases following a pH ≤ 7.0 was 15 (IQR: 3–33). In inpatients, the time from admission to the first blood gas with a pH ≤ 7.0 was 26.3 h (IQR: 4.3–119.1).

The most common type of acidosis was mixed acidosis with 321 cases (65.1 %), followed by pure metabolic acidosis with 143 cases (29.0 %). Pure respiratory acidosis was the least common with 29 cases (5.9 %). Some differences between the types of acidosis were noted; this includes gender, having 2 or more Elixhauser co-morbidities, and lactate. Metabolic acidosis had the highest proportion of patients with two or more Elixhauser comorbidities (121, 84.6 %). This is shown in Table S3.

Cardiorespiratory arrest was the most common aetiology of those with a pH ≤ 7.0, comprising 160 (32.5 %) of the cohort. This was followed by diabetic-related aetiology (n = 66, 13.4 %) and seizure-related aetiology (n = 55, 11.2 %). The highest lactate was seen in the seizure group (17, IQR: 15–21 mmol/L). Seizures had the shortest length of hospital stay in survivors at 2.6 (IQR: 1.4–6.6) days. This is shown in Table S4.

Primary outcome: 30-day mortality

By day 30, 226 (45.8 %) of patients had died. This is shown in Fig. 2A. The median time to death in non-survivors was 0.8 (0.2–4.0) days. Risk of 30-day mortality was greater in those whose initial sample was arterial (log rank p < 0.001, Fig. S1). Different types of metabolic acidosis had different risks of 30-day mortality (log rank <0.001, Fig. 2B). Relative to metabolic acidosis, both mixed acidosis (aHR: 2.26, 95 % CI: 1.55–3.29) and respiratory acidosis (aHR: 2.08, 95 % CI: 1.01–4.27) had a higher hazard of 30-day mortality. Diabetic causes had the lowest rate of 30-day mortality (2/66, 3.0 %) followed by seizures (4/55, 7.3 %). Relative to seizures, diabetic (aHR: 0.92, 95 % CI: 0.16–5.27) and toxicology causes (aHR: 4.07, 95%CI: 0.70–23.6) did not have an increased hazard of 30-day mortality. The aetiologies associated with the greatest hazard of 30-day death were vascular pathology (aHR: 31.2, 95 % CI: 9.60–101), cardiorespiratory arrest (aHR: 27.0, 95 % CI: 9.17–79.4). This is shown in Table 2. Full adjusted models are shown in Table S5. Age and lactate levels were associated with 30-day mortality on univariate and multivariate regression. The post-hoc analysis assessing severity of the acidosis at the time of initial blood gas with a pH ≤ 7.0 was not associated with 30-day mortality. This is shown in Table S6.

Secondary outcomes

Day 90 mortality was similar, with 236 (47.9 %) who had died (Fig. S2). A majority of deaths (94.1 %) were in-hospital deaths (222, 45.0 %). Associations between acidosis type and aetiology were similar to the day 30 analysis.

ICU admission characteristics and supports

Three hundred eighty-four patients (77.9 %) were admitted to ICU, with a length of stay in those admitted of 61 (IQR: 30–145) hours. Two hundred eighty-two patients (57.2 %) required invasive mechanical ventilation, with a duration of invasive ventilation being 55 (IQR: 20–122) hours. One hundred six patients (21.5 %) required continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). Those with respiratory acidosis were most likely to be admitted to the ICU (29, 100 %) and had the highest proportion requiring invasive ventilation (n = 26, 89.7 %). Whilst not statistically significant, rates of CRRT were the highest (n = 34, 24.5 %) in the metabolic acidosis compared to the mixed (n = 70, 22.6 %) and respiratory (1, 3.9 %) groups. ICU length of stay and duration of mechanical ventilation was similar across all groups. This is shown in Table S3, S4, and S7.

One hundred nine (22.1 %) patients did not require ICU admission. Patients who were not admitted to ICU had fewer comorbidities, with 64 (58.7 %) compared to 279 (72.7 %) having two or more Elixhauser comorbidities. Age, HFRS, and minimum pH were similar between groups. Aetiological causes differed between those needing ICU and not. Rates of seizures were higher in the group who were not admitted to ICU (29 (26.6 %) compared to 26 (6.8 %)). Of those not admitted, 37 (33.9 %) died (Fig. S3). Median length of hospital stay in survivors was shorter in those not admitted (2.5 (IQR: 1.1–6.7) vs 8.9 (IQR: 3.9–16.1) days). This is shown in Table S7.

Trajectory of recovery

In those who survived 6 h, the mean pH at 12 h was 7.26 (95 % CI: 7.25–7.26). By 48 h, the mean pH was 7.37 (95%CI: 7.37–7.38). Trajectory of recovery was slower in those who died. These trajectories are shown in Fig. 3 and Fig. S4. Mixed acidosis had the lowest pH at 48 h (pH: 7.29, 95 % CI: 7.27–7.30) compared to metabolic (pH: 7.40, 95 % CI: 7.39–7.41) and respiratory (pH: 7.38, 95%CI: 7.37–7.38). This is shown in Fig. S5.

Sensitivity analysis

With the addition of admission year as a covariate, the associations with 30-day mortality were largely unchanged, except that the increased hazard for respiratory acidosis was no longer demonstrated (aHR: 2.01, 95 % CI: 0.97–4.13). This is shown in Table S8. Second, when different CO2 thresholds were used for arterial and venous samples, respiratory acidosis no longer had a statistically significant association (HR: 2.00, 95 % CI: 1.00–4.02, p = 0.051) with day 30 mortality when compared to metabolic acidosis. Discussion

Key results

Extreme acidosis is rare and over half of all patients survived to 30 days. Longer-term survival (90 days) was similar to 30-day mortality. It appears that once a patient’s pH is less than 7.0, the underlying diagnosis and not severity of the acidaemia is most important. Therefore, the sole use of pH without consideration of the underlying aetiology should not be used as justification for stopping treatment. Additionally, whilst 20 % of these patients did not require ICU, approximately 40 % of these had died by 30 days highlighting the importance of adequate follow-up if a patient is not admitted to ICU and is still for active treatment, especially as it appears to take approximately 48 h for a patient’s pH to return to normal levels.

Relationship to previous studies

Our rates of mortality are similar to Henrique et al.10 This single-centre retrospective review included 222 patients. Similar to our study, the overall mortality rate was 42 %. However, no association between the aetiology of the acidosis and mortality was demonstrated. Our cohort may have been less unwell as the duration of time on mechanical ventilation, ICU, and hospital length of stay were markedly shorter in our study.

Henrique et al. hypothesised that at very low pH levels, the effects of acidosis may have greater influence on outcomes as opposed to the primary disorder. In our study, there was a clear association between mortality and aetiology of the acidosis, and it appears that there is no association between survival and the severity of the acidosis. Regardless of the presence of acidosis, differing underlying pathologies have very different rates of survival.14,15 Moreover, in other settings, admission diagnosis has been highlighted as important.16 The reasons for this are not entirely clear. Duration of the pathophysiological process leading to acidosis may be relevant. In healthy populations (elite athletes), a transient pH < 6.8 have been demonstrated with no “ill” effect.17 In our cohort, seizures had the highest median lactate level and had one of the greatest proportion of patients with a pH < 6.8. Seizures, however, are often transient with a quick resolution of lactate levels.18 Other pathologies, such as cardiac arrest and prolonged ischaemia reperfusion response may lead to progressive acidosis and worsening of pathology (for example, worsening cardiac function in organ systems already affected by the primary insult such as post cardiac arrest myocardial dysfunction).19 Various aetiologies will also have differing pathophysiological processes driving the acidosis. Severe acidosis resulting from metabolic aetiologies has been associated with lower rates of mortality.16 In our study diabetic-related aetiologies had low rates of mortality. In diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), ketones are produced in response to low levels of insulin and high levels of counterregulatory hormones such as glucagon, cortisol and catecholamines20 and have shown to have a number of physiological beneficial effects.21

Other studies assessing extreme acidosis have shown higher mortality rates than both our data, and that by Henrique et al. Both Allyn et al.9 and Paz11 reported a mortality rate of just under 70 %, similar to the overall mortality reported in randomised studies in critically ill patients with metabolic acidosis.7,8 The lower mortality rates in our study may represent a diluted sample with the inclusion of patients who were too well to be admitted to the ICU. Allyn also reports that the cause of the acidosis is important, cardiac arrest in particular.

To our knowledge, no studies assessing extreme acidosis have assessed mortality at longer time points or the trajectory of recovery. Our results show that longer term mortality rates are similar to those in hospital or at day 30. This has also been demonstrated in BICARICU-2.8 With respect to the trajectory of pH, this is similar to a study assessing those with an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest.22 The authors in this study also demonstrate a difference in trajectory between those with favourable and unfavourable neurological outcomes, which is comparable to our results that showed a difference between survivors and non-survivors.

Implications of our study findings

This study is important in further understanding the outcomes of patients with extreme acidosis. This study further highlights the importance of considering both the type and the aetiology of extreme acidaemia when considering prognostic implications. This may also help clinicians decide which groups might benefit most from interventions targeted at improving acid-base balance. It also highlights that once extreme acidosis is present, the absolute pH value may not be a prognostic marker. This differs from studies assessing a wider range of pH values.5,23 In extreme acidaemia, we have shown other characteristics such as age or lactate, may be more prognostically useful. Finally, it appears that it takes approximately 48 h for acid-base balance to normalise and this may be longer in mixed pathologies.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has a number of strengths. Firstly, to our knowledge, it is the largest sample size to date for a study assessing extreme acidosis. Secondly, it also includes all those admitted to hospital not just those admitted to ICU. This is important because of the inherent selection bias in the decision to admit a patient to ICU. Moreover, the inclusion of all hospitalised patients also increases the generalisability. Third, we have been able to assess longer-term outcomes such as 90-day mortality. Fourth, we have been able to plot the trajectory of recovery which is important to help inform clinician’s around expected recovery. Finally, we had no missing data.

We acknowledge that our study has several limitations. First, due to the observational retrospective design, we cannot confirm the causality of the association we observed, and whilst we have tried to adjust for some factors, some bias may remain. Particularly, we cannot fully address the survival and selection bias as we do not have information on withdrawal and withholding active treatment. Importantly, differing pathologies will likely have systemically different practices around withdrawal or withholding of active treatment (for example, cardiac arrest compared to DKA) and this might introduce confounding into the associations drawn around acidosis aetiology. Second, the single-centre nature may reduce external generalisability. Third, our study included both venous and arterial blood gas samples as we noted an increased tendency to perform and utilise VBG for assessment of acidosis in some clinical settings. This difference could be significant for subtyping of the patient cohort. However, a number of studies have shown that there is good correlation between arterial and venous measurements of pH, with a pooled mean difference of 0.035 pH units,11,24 hence we believe that the data generated from VBGs were adequate to be used for patient selection and thus were included in the analysis. Additionally, the point estimates in the sensitivity analysis with altered CO2 thresholds were largely unchanged. Fourth, some bias maybe introduced into the aetiological determination as reviewers were unblinded to outcomes and there was an unavoidable subjective nature around determining aetiological diagnosis. However the use of discharge diagnosis coded for hospital administrative data helps mitigate this. Finally, some diagnostic subgroups were small with very wide CIs so further work to validate this in larger, multi-centre cohorts is needed.

Conclusions

The 30-day mortality for all hospitalised patients with extreme acidaemia is under 50 %. Mortality differed considerably depending on the aetiology and type of extreme acidosis; and the severity of the acidosis did not appear to be associated with mortality.
