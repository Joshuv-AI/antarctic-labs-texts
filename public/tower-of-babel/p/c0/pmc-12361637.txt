Toward Home‐Based Telerehabilitation for Cerebral Palsy Patients: A Qualitative Study on Feasibility, Barriers and Facilitators
Authors: Faridokht Salahshoori, Majid Jangi, Ebrahim Sadeghi‐demneh, Farhad Fatehi, Alireza Rahimi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimTelerehabilitation has emerged as a promising solution to address accessibility, cost‐effectiveness, and continuity of care for patients requiring long‐term rehabilitation, like cerebral palsy (CP) patients. This study aimed to qualitatively explore the perceptions of clinical specialists, and CP patients regarding the feasibility, barriers, and facilitators of home‐based telerehabilitation.MethodsThis qualitative study conducted a thematic analysis approach. Participants include two groups: 17 medical informatics and rehabilitation professionals and 13 CP patients and/or their caregivers. Participants were selected via expert and snowball sampling. Interviews were semi‐structured, transcribed, and analyzed using the Braun‐Clarke thematic analysis technique and MAXQDA software.ResultsThematic analysis revealed four dominant themes including feasibility, barriers, facilitators, and advantages. Feasibility was affected by technological infrastructure (internet connectivity, data security), human resources (availability of multidisciplinary specialists), legal aspects (patient data privacy), and financial sustainability. Key barriers included low digital literacy, limited access to specialized rehabilitation technologies, cultural resistance, legal regulations, and financial constraints. The findings also highlighted several advantages of home‐based telerehabilitation, including cost savings, improved accessibility to rehabilitation, and enhanced patient engagement in therapy.ConclusionThe current study suggested that home‐based telerehabilitation can be a feasible alternative for CP patients in Iran, but a variety of technological, financial, legal, and cultural barriers must first be addressed for its successful development. Overcoming such barriers requires targeted investments, supportive policies, and cultural education for successful implementation.

Introduction

Technological advancements have profoundly reshaped health care delivery by offering innovative solutions in persistent challenges in patient management [1]. Telerehabilitation, a specialized domain of telehealth, utilize digital communication technologies to facilitate the remote provision of rehabilitation services, thereby enhancing accessibility, improving cost‐efficiency, and supporting continuity of care [2, 3].

Cerebral palsy (CP), is a nonprogressive neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by persistent motor and postural impairment stemming from early brain development issues [4]. With an estimated global prevalence, of approximately 2 per 1000 live births, it represent one of the leading causes of lifelong physical disability in children [5]. CP requires long‐term, multidisciplinary rehabilitation programs [6] to improve quality of life, functional independence, and social participation [7]. Traditional rehabilitation programs often involve travel to specialized centers, posing challenges, especially, in rural and low‐resource settings [8]. These burdens are more severe in developing countries where access to specialized therapies are limited, and health care resources are constrained [9, 10, 11].

Home‐based telerehabilitation systems a promising strategy for enhancing therapeutic accessibility and patient engagement by decentralizing service delivery to the home environment [12]. However, its effective implementation is contingent upon a range of contextual determinants, including socio‐culture norms, economic capacity, political and legal framework, and technological advancements, all of which differ across regions [13]. In the Iranian context, the feasibility of such systems remains insufficiently investigated. Existing barriers such as technological incompatibility, financial limitation, user resistance, and regulatory problems pose significant challenges. Addressing these dimensions is critical to fostering the adoption, integration, and long‐term sustainability of telerehabilitation services [14].

Prior qualitative studies have identified the main barriers to implementation of telerehabilitation, including technological constraints, readiness of both providers and patients, stakeholder engagement, and contextual challenges within healthcare systems [14, 15, 16]. Despite the potential of home‐based telerehabilitation, there are notable gaps in the literature, regarding its feasibility, challenges, and implementation in developing contexts. This study addresses this gap by adopting a qualitatively approach within the Iranian context to explore the feasibility of home‐based telerehabilitation. As the first study of its kind in this context, it offers valuable insights for policymakers, healthcare practitioners, and and clinical informatioists technology developers for designing more effective and patient‐centered services [17, 18, 19]. The findings may inform the design and implementation of culturally appropriate and effective telerehabilitation systems, with the potential to enhance quality of life, reduce disparities in care, and offer a model for similarly resourced settings.

Objective

This study aimed to qualitatively investigate the perceptions of medical informatics experts, rehabilitation experts, and users of telerehabilitation systems (CP patients and their families or caregivers) regarding the feasibility, barriers, and facilitators of the application of home‐based telerehabilitation systems among CP patients in Iran.

Methodology

Study Design

This qualitative research employed the thematic analysis technique to explore the experiences and opinions of specialists, individuals with CP, and their caregivers as the system's end users. Qualitative methods allow researchers to develop deep insights into real‐world problems important for the creation of new information in the support of hypotheses, theories, research, and clinical care and make such insights into applied health service research available in ways not possible for the other approaches [20, 21]. Thematic analysis is an extremely flexible, nuanced tool that allows researchers to generate high‐quality, qualitative findings through the identification, analysis, and reporting of patterns in data, better known as themes [22].

An overview of the study's phases.

Participants

The study consisted of two participant groups: The Expert Group, which consisted of 17 individuals. This group included medical informatics experts, rehabilitation specialists (occupational therapy, speech therapy, and physiotherapy), health information management professionals, psychologists, and experienced physicians who had developed and/or implemented telerehabilitation systems. In this regard, expert sampling was done to capture a wide variation in expertise, to draw on a comprehensive representation of relevant viewpoints. Expert sampling, which is a purposive sampling method, was used. This sampling technique is used when gathering information from individuals with particular areas of expertise. This may also include valuable expertise during the exploratory phase of qualitative research in identifying new areas of interest or opening up to other participants [23]. Target End‐User Group (n = 13): The participants in this group consisted of CP patients or their caregivers. Snowball sampling was utilized by starting with initial contacts identified through collaborations with the director of the physical movement school, the occupational therapy clinic of the Faculty of Rehabilitation, and the private rehabilitation clinic.

Data Collection

The study employed semi‐structured interviews for data collection, which comprised open‐ended questions regarding the feasibility, obstacles, facilitators, and user requirements of home‐based telerehabilitation systems for patients with CP in Iran. The interview questions were extracted from a previous review and studies related to the feasibility of telerehabilitation services and the opinions of the research team.

Before the interview, an interview guide for the expert group was sent via email. After agreeing to conduct the interview, the time and place were coordinated. In the end‐user group, visits were made to the physical‐motor school, the occupational therapy clinic of the Rehabilitation Faculty, and a private rehabilitation clinic in Isfahan province. Using snowball sampling, interviews were conducted with parents or caregivers of patients with CP. Again, before conducting the interview, an interview guide was provided to both groups, and the purpose of the interview, its voluntary nature, confidentiality, and duration were explained. All interviewees signed a written consent form before the interview and were aware that the interview would be recorded.

In‐depth face‐to‐face interviews were conducted, totaling 24, in Isfahan province, which typically lasted between 30 and 45 min. The video calls, a total of four in number, were conducted via Google Meet, Zoomit, and Skype, and each lasted roughly between 30 and 45 min. The interviews were carried out over 2 telephones and took around 30 min to complete. The number of interviews conducted in this qualitative study was decided based on data saturation, where it became evident that no new information was being provided by the participants [24].

The interviews began with open‐ended, guiding questions intended to facilitate in‐depth, narrative responses from participants. These questions included: “What do you know about telerehabilitation or using technology for therapy at home?”, “What are your main needs or concerns regarding home‐based rehabilitation services?”, “Would you be willing to use digital tools for therapy at home? Why or why not?”, “What difficulties do you face when trying to access rehabilitation services?”, “What kind of support (technical, emotional, or instructional) would help you use telerehabilitation effectively?”

And the questions which asked from healthcare providers and maternal health policymakers include: “From your professional perspective, what are the main barriers to implementing home‐based telerehabilitation for patients with CP?”, “What technological, organizational, or policy‐related factors affect the feasibility of such services?”, “How do you perceive the readiness of patients and families for engaging in telerehabilitation?”, “What resources or support systems would be necessary for successful implementation in Iran?”, “What cultural or ethical considerations should be taken into account when designing telerehabilitation interventions for this population?”

Interview questions were adapted to align with the participants' cultural context, using culturally relevant language and examples. The interview process was conducted in accordance with local customs and norms to ensure participant comfort and respect. Active listening was employed to capture responses within their cultural framework, ensuring the authenticity and relevance of the data.

Data Saturation

Data saturation was achieved in both the expert and patient groups. Saturation was determined when no new themes or significant insights emerged from the interviews, and subsequent data became repetitive across participants. Specifically, saturation was reached after conducting 14 interviews with the expert group and 10 interviews with the patient group. This indicates that the sample size was sufficient to capture the breadth and depth of perspectives required for thematic completeness, consistent with established qualitative research practices.

Data Analysis

A thematic analysis approach was employed to extract, examine, and report themes and trends within the data, thereby enabling the authors to concentrate on identifying and describing the explicit and implicit concepts embedded in the data [22]. Data analysis was conducted concurrently with data collection using the Braun–Clarke approach which includes a six‐phase process. The analysis is recursive and iterative and requires the researcher to move back and forth through the phases as necessary. Phase one: familiarization with the data; Phase two: generating initial codes; Phase three: generating themes; Phase four: reviewing potential themes; Phase five: defining and naming themes; phase six: producing the report [25]. The recorded interviews were transcribed and analyzed using MaxQDA software (2022) to identify main themes and sub‐themes.

Trustworthiness of Qualitative Data

The study's accuracy and quality were evaluated using the Guba and Lincoln evaluation method, which considers criteria like credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability [16]. To confirm the validity of the results, the extracted data were shared with the interviewees to gauge the similarity between their views and the researchers' findings. In addition, the research team reviewed the study to ensure its reliability. The study's dependability was established through ongoing data analysis and impartial verification by a neutral third‐party assessor. Confirmability, aimed at reducing researcher influence, indicates the newly extracted themes and conceptual codes indicate that the findings of the study are not based on the researcher's assumptions and biases. Confirmability criteria were ensured by reviewing the coding process by an external panel of experienced qualitative researchers. Furthermore, the transferability of results was enabled by offering comprehensive contextual information, featuring participant profiles and constraints, to facilitate the implementation of the findings in similar environments. These measures collectively enhance the rigor and reliability of the study's findings.

Member checking was conducted by sharing preliminary findings with a subset of participants from both the expert and patient groups. Participants were asked to review the results for accuracy and provide feedback. Any discrepancies or suggestions were incorporated into the final analysis. The feedback affirmed the validity of the themes, enhancing the credibility and rigor of the study.

To ensure methodological rigor and enhance the credibility of the findings, the research team adopted a reflexive stance throughout the study. Researchers engaged in continuous critical self‐evaluation to identify and mitigate the influence of personal assumptions, values, and prior experiences on data interpretation. Reflexive journaling was employed to document decision‐making processes and reflections during data collection and analysis. Additionally, peer debriefing was conducted at key stages of the study to promote analytical transparency and reduce the risk of interpretive bias. These strategies contributed to the trustworthiness and authenticity of the qualitative inquiry.

To enhance the methodological rigor and credibility of the qualitative analysis, intercoder reliability procedures were systematically implemented. Two trained qualitative researchers independently coded the full data set using MAXQDA software. Initial coding frameworks were developed deductively based on the interview guide and inductively refined through iterative engagement with the data. To ensure coding consistency, intercoder agreement was assessed through a consensus‐building process whereby all discrepancies were examined and resolved through reflective discussion and negotiated agreement. This approach aligns with established standards in qualitative research for ensuring analytic trustworthiness and interpretive validity, particularly in thematic content analysis.

Ethical Considerations

In this study, all relevant ethical principles such as: obtaining informed consent from participants before the interview for interview and recording them, considering the right of participants to leave at any stage of the study and keeping information confidential were fully observed. This article is extracted from the PhD thesis and has an ethics code approved by the ethics committee of the Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUI.NUREMA.REC.1401.166).

Results

Demographic Characteristics of Participants

The study consisted of two distinct groups of participants: rehabilitation and medical informatics specialists, as well as patient caregivers. The expert group included a variety of academic specialists, including assistant professors and associate professors, who specialized in various fields such as health information management, computer science, medical informatics, speech therapy, occupational therapy, and physiotherapy. The professionals had relevant work experience in the area of telerehabilitation systems and with CP patients. The gender split among participants was roughly even, consisting of 10 male and 8 female individuals. The second group comprised caregivers, particularly parents (mainly mothers), of patients with CP, whose ages varied from 2 to 36 years. Most patients' conditions were congenital, with diagnoses given either at birth or within the first year of life, underscoring the need for long‐term care within this demographic. Feasibility Domain

Several key factors were identified by participants that contribute to the feasibility of implementing a home‐based TR system for patients with CP in Iran. 4 themes and 12 subthemes were identified as feasibility domains that are visualized in conceptual maps in Figure 2.

Themes and subthemes emerged from interviews for the feasibility domain.

Technological Feasibility

Technical feasibility is the foundation for implementing a telerehabilitation system. It ensures the reliability, security, and efficiency of the platform, which are vital for the continuity of patient care. The system must accommodate the needs of patients with varying levels of physical and cognitive ability while remaining scalable and accessible for caregivers and therapists. Without a robust technological infrastructure, the system cannot provide consistent, high‐quality remote care, making this theme critical for success.

Technological Infrastructure

The point that was strongly emphasized by experts was that “The availability of a strong internet connection, minimum bandwidth requirements (512 Kbps for audio, 4 Mbps for video) [P7, P1, P13, P16], network stability (internet connectivity issues) [P6, P10], and strong server and database (RAM with storage capacity) [P7, P12, P16] was identified as a critical requirement.”Interviewee P7: “Usually, you need a spoke center that can be at home or in a central, rural area and a hub center that can be assumed to be a rehabilitation center in a big city or the capital of a province, and then these spokes and branches are connected to it.”

Interviewee P7: “Usually, you need a spoke center that can be at home or in a central, rural area and a hub center that can be assumed to be a rehabilitation center in a big city or the capital of a province, and then these spokes and branches are connected to it.”

These technologies also improve monitoring and feedback, allowing for personalized treatment plans.

Several participants emphasized that technologies such as virtual reality or augmented reality (patient education through 3D video design), game therapy along with Kinect (Microsoft's motion tracking device for patients), and video conferencing can be used for telerehabilitation systems. Human Resource Feasibility

Participants noted that the implementation of a telerehabilitation system demands multidisciplinary expertise, and the effective operation of telerehabilitation depends on the integration of both technical and clinical expertise. Trained professionals are responsible for developing, maintaining, and using the system to its maximum capabilities.

Technical Experts

Most specialists emphasized that “technical professionals such as medical informatics specialists, database specialists, health IT specialists, and bioinformatics specialists (familiar with both clinical and technical domains) [P12, P2, P1, P15, P16, P17], technical specialists in software, hardware, network, and database engineering for database updates and development [P12, P6, P10, P3, P16], and a system administrator or operator [P3] are required for developing and maintaining a robust telerehabilitation platform.”

Interviewees P1, P2, P3, P7, P9, P10, and P16: “When implementing a telerehabilitation system, a support team is needed to respond to system users.”

Clinical and Paramedical Experts

The majority of the interviewees emphasized the subject of employing clinical experts in the process of implementing the telerehabilitation system. For example, they said: “Physiotherapists, speech therapists, and occupational therapists (trained in a telehealth or remote rehabilitation) [P7, P12, P10, P3, P8, P1, P5, P4, P13, P15, P17], nutrition and diet therapy consultants and gastroenterologists [P7, P12, P5, P4, P17], orthopedic specialists [P12, P1, P5, P13, P17], physicians and nurses [P1, P5, P4], pediatric specialists [P4], neurologists or neuroscientists [P7, P3, P8, P1, P5, P4], and physical medicine specialists [P1, P12] are essential for guiding patients and addressing specific needs remotely.”.

A few of the interviewees stated that paramedical specialties can also help during the implementation of the telerehabilitation system, for example:Interviewees P1, P5: “Medical librarians can perform tasks such as providing clinical information, managing information and managing resources.” Interviewees P1, P5, P4, and P15: “Healthcare social workers provide valuable insights and ideas about social factors affecting health outcomes and, by supporting patients' needs, ultimately promote patient well‐being.”

Legal Feasibility

Legal compliance ensures the telerehabilitation system operates within ethical and regulatory frameworks. This is especially crucial for protecting patient data, defining service boundaries, and preventing misuse of the system. Without addressing legal feasibility, issues such as unauthorized data access and lack of accountability could undermine the system's credibility and trustworthiness.

Information Security (Legal)

experts indicated that specific regulations for remote rehabilitation are currently absent in Iran, creating ambiguity around data ownership and privacy. Addressing these gaps is vital to secure patient trust and system sustainability.

The interviewees insisted that for the legal security of information, these issues should be considered in the launch and implementation of the telerehabilitation system: Authorization and access control (defining passwords to enhance security) [P6, P1, P4]. Authentication and professional qualification verification of remote service providers [P1, P10], the definition of data ownership, data theft and confidentiality [P1, P6, P7, P10, P13, P15] and follow‐up on patient data copyright and privacy issues [P12, P13, P15].

Financial Feasibility

Financial feasibility assesses whether the system is affordable and sustainable for all stakeholders, including patients, service providers, and government agencies. High costs could limit access to healthcare for rural and low‐income families.

Patient Costs

The interviewees stated that patients and their families are required to incur various costs to access telerehabilitation services, for example, the cost of internet and purchasing electronic devices (e.g., smartphones, tablets, laptops), especially in rural and low‐income areas [P7, P6, P12, P1, P5]; the cost of purchasing surface EMG electrodes [P8]; and the cost of renting hardware installation (two sensors and a small computing device) at the patient's home [P9].Interviewee P8: “The high price of devices like the Xbox with Kinect limits accessibility for many families, especially in rural areas.”

Interviewee P8: “The high price of devices like the Xbox with Kinect limits accessibility for many families, especially in rural areas.”

Challenges and Barriers Domain

Implementing a telerehabilitation system for individuals with CP presents complex challenges that greatly affect its feasibility and effectiveness. 5 themes and 12 subthemes were identified as challenges and barriers domain that are visualized in the conceptual maps in Figure 3.

Themes and subthemes emerged from interviews for challenges domain.

Technological Challenges

Technical barriers represent foundational obstacles to implementing telerehabilitation systems.

Infrastructure Challenges

Interviewees highlighted the lack of adequate infrastructure, particularly in rural areas, as a significant barrier to the implementation of telerehabilitation systems.

Participants emphasized that “the absence of reliable internet connectivity, particularly in rural regions, hindered the effective use of telerehabilitation systems.” [P7, P10, P12, P5, P13, P15, P17].Interviewees P12, P8, P15: “The robust hardware and software infrastructure required for such systems are either unavailable or outdated in Iran.”

Interviewees P12, P8, P15: “The robust hardware and software infrastructure required for such systems are either unavailable or outdated in Iran.”

Lack of Specialized Rehabilitation Technology

interviewees stated that “we don't have specialized software platforms designed for telerehabilitation and disabled individuals [P10, P8].”

Several interviewees highlighted the technical performance challenges of the telerehabilitation systems, for example: “Difficulty in performing physical and motor rehabilitation through the system (e.g., range of motion, strength, endurance, speed, etc.), Difficulty in addressing cognitive issues and examining patients through the telerehabilitation system and Challenges in installing hardware at the patient's home (cost, lack of space, and difficulties in technical staff commuting).” [P7, P9, P10].

Human and Organizational Challenges

The factors of human and organizational challenges and the engagement of caregivers and healthcare professionals influence the success of the implementation of a tele‐rehabilitation system.

Digital Literacy of System Users

Participants highlighted that “many patients and caregivers, especially older adults, lacked the necessary digital skills to use the system effectively [P7, P1, P9, P15].”

One participant said: “Patients and their families prefer face‐to‐face interactions and were often unwilling to share personal information without a therapist present, and the lack of direct contact with therapists reduces their motivation to use the system.” [P17]

Challenges Related to Patients’ Families

some of the interviewees pointed out challenges related to families and caregivers of patients, for example: “Lack of time, motivation, and hope among parents or caregivers to care for these patients [C2, C7, C12]; need for a companion or caregiver for patients unable to use the system [P1, P3]; and need for high intelligence of the mother or caregiver to understand the service provider's instructions [P5].”

Organizational Challenges

Interviewees emphasized, “organizational factors are the primary reasons behind the failure of information systems. Without addressing these issues, no system can function effectively, regardless of its technical capabilities.” [P1, P9]

Cultural Challenges

Cultural attitudes play a significant role in shaping the adoption of new technologies, and these factors directly affect participation and engagement.

Resistance to System Adoption

Participants noted that “some patients and their caregivers, especially women, were uncomfortable with recording and sharing videos to therapists due to cultural and religious reasons [P8, P17].”

Several participants reported that “many physicians are reluctant to adopt telerehabilitation and prefer to provide traditional rehabilitation services [P6, P12, P3, P2, P1, P9, P13].”

General Culture of Society

Some interviewees highlighted that “Our society is not ready for such services due to cultural norms and the lack of trust in technology. Iran's cultural, social, and physical environment for remote rehabilitation services is not suitable. [P7, P8, P2, P1, P9, P15].”

Legal Challenges

Legal difficulties can lead to a lack of trust and insecurity among both patients and providers of telerehabilitation services.

Patient Rights

Participants emphasized that “There are no clear legal guidelines to address medical errors or malpractice in telerehabilitation, which makes both patients and providers vulnerable.” [P1, P4, P9, P13] Interviewees P13, P5: “Patients are often unaware of their rights, and there is no legal framework to protect them in case of negligence or malpractice.”

Interviewees P13, P5: “Patients are often unaware of their rights, and there is no legal framework to protect them in case of negligence or malpractice.”

Lack of Electronic Medical Guidelines and Laws

The majority of participants emphasized, “The lack of standardized guidelines and electronic medical laws specific to remote rehabilitation services was identified as a major barrier. (P7, P12, P10, P1, P9, P13, P16, P17).” Interviewees P13, P5: “The absence of data protection laws, similar to HIPAA in the US, raises concerns about patient privacy and data security.”

Discussion and Conclusion

The findings of this study provide a comprehensive understanding of the feasibility, barriers, and facilitators associated with implementing home‐based telerehabilitation systems for CP patients in Iran. The results highlight the potential of telerehabilitation to address some of the longstanding challenges in delivering rehabilitation services, particularly in resource‐constrained settings. However, the study also underscores the complexity of implementing such systems, as they are influenced by a range of technological, financial, legal, and cultural factors.

Feasibility

Our results showed that the feasibility of implementing telerehabilitation systems requires several key factors, such as the technological readiness of such systems, which depends on the availability of reliable internet connectivity, secure data management systems, and advanced technologies, such as virtual reality, wearable sensors, and the Internet of Things (IoT). In addition, multidisciplinary teams of technical experts and clinical specialists must design, maintain, and deliver high‐quality telerehabilitation systems. Furthermore, clear legal regulations addressing data privacy, ownership, and ethical practices are crucial for safeguarding patient trust and ensuring accountability. Financial factors such as device costs, infrastructure, and system development must be addressed to ensure accessibility and sustainability, particularly for rural and low‐income populations. These findings are in agreement with previous studies in the literature. Cunha et al. (2023) emphasized that a reliable technological infrastructure, including stable internet access, secure servers, and advanced tools like artificial intelligence, virtual reality, and wearable sensors, is crucial for optimizing the efficacy of home‐based rehabilitation systems for shoulder [26]. Virtual reality provides immersive environments that can motivate patients and improve their rehabilitation experience [27]. Maintaining trust and system reliability requires adherence to global data security standards and compliance regulations. Rybarczyk et al. (2019) stressed the significance of creating a secure and efficient telerehabilitation platform, underscoring the necessity for specific guidelines to guarantee patient safety and accurate exercise execution [28]. Malakhov et al. (2024) highlighted that successful telerehabilitation systems require human resources from different fields such as medicine, engineering, and behavioral sciences, to facilitate innovative solutions tailored to patient needs [29]. Studies by Benassi & Balboni (2023) and Alhabter et al. (2023) highlight the need for well‐defined ethical guidelines and legal frameworks in telerehabilitation systems, specifically regarding data protection, ownership rights, and ethical [30, 31].

Challenges

Results identified that home‐based telerehabilitation encounters significant challenges, including poor infrastructure, lack of specialized software, and technical challenges that prevent effective implementation, especially in rural areas. Many patients and caregivers don't have digital literacy skills, prefer in‐person care, and lack motivation to use the system. Cultural resistance, such as privacy concerns and distrust of technology, further limits adoption. Also, legal barriers to patient privacy and data security create uncertainty, while high costs, lack of insurance support, and insufficient government funding make it financially inaccessible. Previous studies align with our studies for example, one study identified significant electrical and technological barriers in rural America that hinder telemedicine implementation, including inadequate infrastructure and unreliable internet connectivity, which affect health outcomes and patient satisfaction [32]. Rabanifar & Abdi. (2021) Leochico, Carl Froilan D., et al. (2020) noted that insufficient infrastructure, including lack of specialized rehabilitation equipment, high‐speed internet, and difficulties in installing necessary hardware in patients' homes, significantly inhibits telerehabilitation in developing countries [33, 34]. Galavi, et al. (2022) in a systematic review study highlighted that knowledge and skills are critical barriers [35]. Patients often express a preference for traditional face‐to‐face rehabilitation, because they think it's more effective and personal [36]. Mustafa, Aqsa, et al (2024) conducted a systematic review study and identified that 75% of studies reported concerns regarding patient privacy, highlighting the prevalence of these issues in telemedicine [37]. Puri (2024) provided recommendations that include improving data protection laws and establishing a clear liability framework to address legal challenges [38]. The development and maintenance of telerehabilitation technologies can be expensive, making it difficult for patients to afford ongoing services [39]. Also, many insurance companies do not cover the costs of telerehabilitation, resulting in patients having to bear the full financial burden [40].

Advantages

Our results showed that home‐based tele‐rehabilitation telerehabilitation offers significant economic and accessibility benefits, including reducing costs, eliminating travel expenses, easing financial burdens, and enhancing care quality. Participants highlighted its affordability compared with in‐person therapy and noted potential income opportunities, such as advertising and employing capable patients. The system also improves access to care, particularly for those with mobility difficulties. Patients benefit from greater convenience and better therapy outcomes. Several studies agree with the results. Summers et al. (2024) reported that a home‐based clinician‐controlled telerehabilitation system (HCTS) resulted in remarkable cost savings [41]. One study indicated that telerehabilitation is cost‐effective, often more effective, and less expensive than traditional methods [42]. Wersényi et al. (2024) reported telerehabilitation system can generate income through targeted advertising, promoting health‐related products and services to users [43]. Patients who achieve proficiency in their rehabilitation may be employed as peer mentors or trainers, creating a supportive community while providing income opportunities [44]. Embree et al. (2018) discuss telerehabilitation's benefits in enhancing access and improving employment outcomes for consumers [45].

Facilitators

This study found that facilitators like technological, cultural, financial, human resource, and family support factors contributed to the adoption of telerehabilitation systems. The widespread availability of mobile devices and internet access makes the system both practical and accessible. Government, charitable, and stakeholder funding decreases expenses, thereby lowering the system's overall cost. A highly‐skilled highly skilled workforce comprising motivated professionals and capable individuals affects the efficiency of telerehabilitation systems. The trust and experience within families, combined with their familiarity with telerehabilitation during the COVID‐19 pandemic, also contributed to the adoption and long‐term viability of the treatment.

A comparative analysis of the present study's findings with existing literature on telerehabilitation, both within Iran and internationally, reveals important convergences and divergences. Consistent with prior Iranian studies, the current research identifies key barriers such as limited technological infrastructure, insufficient digital literacy, and prevailing cultural resistance as significant impediments to the adoption of home‐based telerehabilitation. These findings underscore persistent systemic challenges in low‐ and middle‐income setting [46, 47, 48].

Conversely, studies conducted in high‐income countries report more favorable conditions for telerehabilitation implementation, attributed to well‐developed infrastructure, clearly established legal and ethical frameworks, and higher levels of user acceptance [49, 50, 51]. These discrepancies may be explained by contextual differences in economic resources, healthcare policy, and the historical integration of telehealth technologies into clinical practice [51]. Nevertheless, across both contexts, there is a shared recognition of the critical role played by multidisciplinary collaboration, regulatory clarity, and financial sustainability. Such parallels reinforce the generalizability of certain facilitators while highlighting the need for tailored, context‐specific approaches to address localized barriers in countries like Iran.

The findings confirm that in addition to significant barriers to developing telerehabilitation in Iran, even strong facilitators can be used to overcome obstacles. Iran's social and cultural environment is one in which obstacles have long, deep origins in terms of limited resources and traditional care structures. There is a growing awareness of the benefits of telerehabilitation in terms of reduced expenses and increased access to care. A noteworthy observation was the interdependence between facilitators and barriers. For example, financial constraints are a barrier to telerehabilitation, but the economic benefits of telerehabilitation can help overcome these costs. Similarly, cultural resistance is a barrier, but increased acceptance of technology by younger generations can bridge such a barrier.

The context of Iran, with its respective socioeconomic and cultural background, plays a critical role in the feasibility of telerehabilitation systems. Iran's financial constraints, including high inflation and budget‐constrained care for medical expenses, amplify financial barriers to telerehabilitation implementation. The cultural background is also a critical consideration in the adoption of telerehabilitation. In Iran, traditional medical practices are prevalent, and direct contact with medical professionals is preferred in most cases. With increased familiarity with technology and its simplicity, such values change for years, with considerable impact on youth caregivers.

The findings of this study agree with several theoretical frameworks of rehabilitation and telemedicine. The most relevant is the technology acceptance model (TAM), in that it explains “Perceived Ease of Use” and “Perceived Usefulness” in the adoption of new technology acceptance [52]. In this study, “perceived ease of use” in terms of overcoming geographical distance and incurring reduced cost aligns with the TAM, while the “Perceived Usefulness” in using telerehabilitation devices reflects the challenges related to “Ease of Use.” Additionally, the Theory of Diffusion of Innovation can provide a useful lens through which to understand cultural resistance to telerehabilitation. According to this theory, the acceptance of new technologies is related to compatibility with existing values, relative advantage over traditional methods, and complexity of use [53]. The research indicates that although telerehabilitation provides distinct benefits in terms of expense and accessibility, its compatibility with current social standards and the intricacy of utilizing the technology persist as major obstacles.

Our findings have important implications for policymakers and clinicians. It's necessary for policymakers and clinicians to prioritize the development of robust technological infrastructure in underserved communities, to make telerehabilitation systems accessible and effective for use. Financial incentives, such as government subsidies or payment through medical insurance, will have to be in place to make such systems accessible for low‐income communities. Clear legal frameworks must also be developed to cover such concerns regarding security of information, patient confidentiality, and ethics to engender trust and make such a system sustainable in the long run. Training programs must then be designed to enable clinicians to effectively deliver rehabilitation remotely, and educational programs must be initiated to enhance computer and information literacy and overcome cultural reluctance in both patients and caregivers.

This study highlights key implications for stakeholders involved in advancing home‐based telerehabilitation for individuals with cerebral palsy in Iran. Policymakers should prioritize national strategies that expand digital infrastructure, establish legal frameworks for data privacy, and incorporate telerehabilitation into health insurance coverage. Practitioners require specialized training in remote care delivery, digital tools, and culturally responsive engagement. Technology developers must design accessible, low‐bandwidth‐compatible platforms tailored to CP patients' needs, integrating features like adaptive interfaces and caregiver support. Public awareness campaigns are also needed to promote digital literacy and reduce cultural resistance. These coordinated efforts are essential for the effective and sustainable implementation of telerehabilitation in Iran.

Limitations

Despite the valuable insight obtained, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was conducted solely in Isfahan province, which may limit the generalizability of the results to another region of Iran. Different provinces may have varying levels of healthcare access, socioeconomic status, cultural attitudes toward technology, and digital infrastructure. Therefore, caution should be exercised when applying these results to broader populations across the country.

Second, the use of rich information in qualitative interviews can introduce bias and subjectivity. Mixed‐methods studies with both qualitative and quantitative information could best serve future studies by offering a balanced picture of the feasibility, barriers, and facilitators of telerehabilitation. Besides, no evaluation of the effectiveness of telerehabilitation systems in improving patient outcomes, including social participation, functional independence, and quality of life, was performed in such a study. Long‐term studies over years will have to evaluate the long‐term impact of telerehabilitation in patients with CP and their families.

Future Research Directions

While this study offers valuable insights into the feasibility, barriers, and facilitators of home‐based telerehabilitation for children with cerebral palsy, further research is essential to evaluate its clinical effectiveness. Specifically, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) should be conducted to determine the impact of home‐based telerehabilitation on key outcomes such as functional independence, social participation, and quality of life. These studies would provide robust, empirical evidence regarding the efficacy of telerehabilitation interventions and could identify the most effective components and delivery methods. Such evidence would be critical for informing clinical practice and the development of evidence‐based guidelines for the widespread implementation of home‐based telerehabilitation.

Conclusion

This study highlights the potential of home‐based telerehabilitation to improve access to rehabilitation services for CP patients in Iran. It also identifies significant barriers that must be addressed to ensure its successful implementation. The findings underscore the importance of technological infrastructure, financial support, legal frameworks, and human resources in the development and sustainability of telerehabilitation systems. The involvement of caregivers and families is crucial for patient engagement and adherence to therapy, while efforts to address cultural resistance and improve digital literacy are essential for increasing acceptance of telerehabilitation as a viable alternative to traditional in‐person rehabilitation.

The study revealed that a home‐based tele‐rehabilitation system for CP patients in Iran is feasible but faces significant barriers. Addressing these challenges through targeted infrastructural investments, policy support, and cultural education is essential. The findings of this study contribute to the growing body of literature on telerehabilitation and provide valuable insights for policymakers and healthcare providers seeking to implement telerehabilitation in resource‐limited settings.
