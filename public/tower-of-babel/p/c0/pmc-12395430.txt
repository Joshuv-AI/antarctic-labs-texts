Introduction

Primary mucinous carcinoma of the skin is a very rare malignant tumor entity, which develops from the sweat glands. This entity was first described by Lennox et al. [1] in 1952. To date, just over 200 cases have been described in the literature. It is a low-grade tumor with a low metastatic potential but high recurrence rate. The preferred location for this tumor is the head, particularly in the periorbital area in 30 % of cases [2]. This condition can occur at any age but is most prevalent after 60 years. The diagnosis is histological and immunohistochemical and it is necessary to eliminate a secondary nature before considering a primary disease.

We report a case of primary cutaneous mucinous carcinoma located at the intergluteal cleft mimicking an infected pilonidal cyst in a 72-years-old patient.

This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [3].

Presentation of case

We present a 72-year-old man with no medical or surgical history. He was presented to the emergency department of our hospital with coccygeal pain evolving for 1 month without other associated signs. Perineal examination showed a swelling in the coccygeal region with multiple fistulous orifices at the level of the intergluteal cleft discharging a gelatinous liquid with two other fistulized anal margin abscesses. Diagnosis of infected pilonidal cyst has been retained, and the patient was taken to the operating room. Peroperatively, the coccygeal fistulas contained gelatinous fluid and extended to the presacral space and coccyx. He had a complete excision of the coccygeal fistulas and part of the eroded coccyx and incision of the anal margin abscesses (Fig. 1). The postoperative course was uneventful.

Intraoperative view demonstrating a significant tissue defect in the sacrococcygeal region following complete excision.

Histological examination showed a fibrous adipose tissue extensively infiltrated by a well-differentiated carcinoma with numerous irregular tubular glands and structures arranged within a fibroinflammatory stroma There was abundant intra- and extra glandular mucoid material (Fig. 2). Moreover, there were no vascular emboli. The coccygeal fragment analyzed was clear of any tumor cells.

Histological examination showing irregular glandular structures (blue arrows) with abundant extracellular mucus (red arrows) (Haemathein-Eosin ×400).

Immunohistochemical examination revealed positivity for Cytokeratin-7 and S100 protein.

At this stage, we needed to identify a primary site. For this reason, a thorough evaluation of the disease was performed, including oesogastroduodenal endoscopy, colonoscopy and thoracic-abdominal-pelvic Computed Tomography (CT) scan. These additional examinations did not show any primary or secondary lesion. Diagnosis of primary cutaneous mucinous carcinoma of the intergluteal cleft was therefore confirmed. The patient has been made aware of the diagnosis and the need for close follow-up due to the high risk of locoregional recurrence.

Discussion

Primary cutaneous mucinous carcinoma is a very rare adnexal tumor of the sweat glands. It presents typically as a slowly growing nodule with a low metastatic potential but high recurrence rate. It usually occurs in the sixth decade of life, and it affects predominantly male patients [4]. It preferentially affects the cervicofacial region in 80 % of cases, with a predilection for the scalp and the eyelid (40 %). other locations such as the abdomen, thorax or vulva have also been reported in the literature [5] [6], but none in the intergluteal cleft. Approximatively 200 cases of primary cutaneous mucinous carcinoma have been described in the literature [4] making it a rare disease. The apocrine or eccrine origin of this tumor is still debated. For some authors, the differentiation depends on the site. Primary mucinous carcinomas of the axilla are apocrine and those of the face are eccrine [7]. A primary location of the skin is rare. In fact, most common sites of mucinous carcinomas are the breast, parotid and lachrymal glands, ovary or gastrointestinal tract. Therefore, most cases of mucinous carcinoma of the skin are secondary. Clinical diagnosis is difficult because of its non-specific clinical presentation, which is a small, solitary, flesh-colored skin lesion that evolves slowly. This can be confusing and lead to a wrong diagnosis as in our case where the patient was operated with the diagnosis of a pilonidal coccygeal cyst.

Diagnosis is usually made by histological and immunohistochemical examination of the surgical specimen. Primary cutaneous mucinous carcinoma expresses low molecular weight cytokeratins, EMA (Epithelial Membrane Antigen), CEA (carcinoembryonic antigen), GCDFP-15 (gross cystic disease fluid protein) and rarely S100 protein, whereas cytokeratin 20 is always negative, thus eliminating a digestive origin.

It is also essential to differentiate between a primary or secondary cutaneous lesion, as this will determine the prognosis of the disease. A cutaneous metastasis of a mucinous carcinoma of the breast has a poor prognosis, whereas the prognosis of a primary cutaneous mucinous carcinoma is excellent after surgical excision. For this reason, a thorough evaluation of the disease should be carried out, including oesogastroduodenal endoscopy, colonoscopy and thoracic-abdominal-pelvic Computed Tomography (CT) scan. Recent advances in artificial intelligence offer promising avenues to address these diagnostic challenges. AI-driven image analysis models can detect subtle pathological features from clinical and histological images, enabling early differentiation of primary cutaneous mucinous carcinoma from benign lesions and reducing diagnostic delays [8].

Currently there are no evidence-based guidelines for the treatment of this tumor, however surgical excision with clean margins or surgery using the Mohs technique [4] should be sufficient. Local recurrence rate is nearly 50 % and distant metastases occur in 2.7 % [9].

In recent years, immunotherapy has shown promise in multiple solid tumors, including mucinous carcinomas of the breast and gastrointestinal tract, particularly through the use of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors [10]. Although primary cutaneous mucinous carcinoma has not been systematically investigated as a target for immunotherapy, its low metastatic potential coupled with a high recurrence rate may make it a candidate for adjuvant or salvage immunotherapeutic strategies. Future research should focus on characterizing the immune microenvironment of primary cutaneous mucinous carcinoma, evaluating biomarkers such as PD-L1 expression and tumor mutational burden, and assessing the clinical utility of checkpoint inhibitors in high-risk or recurrent cases.

Our patient presents an unusual form of primary mucinous carcinoma located in the intergluteal cleft, successfully treated by an extended excision.

Patients should be counseled about the importance of frequent follow-up to assess for local tumor recurrence or development of regional lymphadenopathy.

Conclusion

Primary cutaneous mucinous carcinoma located in the intergluteal cleft is exceptional and poses difficulties in diagnosis due to its location. Diagnosis was made by histological and immunohistochemical examination of the surgical specimen. The prognosis of a primary cutaneous mucinous carcinoma is excellent after complete surgical excision, but frequent and regular follow-up remains necessary for an early detection of an eventual locoregional recurrence.
