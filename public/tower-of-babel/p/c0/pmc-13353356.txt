Multifunctional electrochemical memory stabilized by phase coexistence
Authors: Sangheon Oh, Adam L. Gross, Adam S. Christensen, Jacklyn Zhu, Patrick S. Finnegan, Joshua D. Sugar, Sean R. Bishop, Simeon J. Gilbert, R. Stanley Williams, Perla B. Balbuena, Kyung Seok Woo, Timothy D. Brown, Elliot J. Fuller, Suhas Kumar, A. Alec Talin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Our growing computing needs, especially in applications that heavily rely on artificial intelligence (AI), motivate a search for new components that could substantially augment the performance of general-purpose digital computers. Beyond ON/OFF switching, new components with linear multistate analog resistive tuning, nonlinear volatile switching, spiking, oscillatory, stochastic and other complex functionalities could enable highly efficient neuromorphic computing schemes for AI information processing. Compared to the extreme multifunctionality of biological neurons, realizing all the above characteristics in a single, scalable analog component remains a grand challenge. Here we investigate electrochemical gating combined with localized thermal activation to program and switch a single, vertically integrated and dimensionally scaled electrothermal chemical random access memory (ETCRAM) with a channel and reservoir composed of phase-separated vanadium oxide. Closely related to electrochemical RAM (ECRAM), ETCRAM uses an integrated gate-heater electrode to overcome kinetic barriers that help retain states at ambient temperatures. In addition to synapse-like stable and programmable analog resistance states arising from redox-tunable phase coexistence, a single component exhibits neuron-like nonlinear conductance switching with a tunable threshold and self-driven dynamics owing to the thermally driven metal-insulator phase transition in vanadium dioxide. More broadly, we demonstrate that electrochemically stabilized phase coexistence could unlock analog electronics with novel functionality, stability, reconfigurability, and scalability.

INTRODUCTION

Digital information storage presently relies on electronically encoded binary states separated by at least 12 kBT or ~0.3 eV (kBT is the thermal noise floor at temperature T, and kB is the Boltzmann constant) of energy to process information, and typically requires voltages >10 V to program information in a nonvolatile manner in most commercial storage solutions (e.g., flash memory) (1). In contrast, analog information storage relies on quasi-continuous states, defined by nonequilibrium thermodynamic and kinetic processes, accessed by motion of electrons, ions, and domains in various heterogeneously integrated materials (2). Each of these processes [such as Fowler-Nordheim tunneling in flash memories (3), crystal structure annealing in phase-change memories (4), or frozen nonequilibrium kinetic states in vacancy-migration-oxide memories (5)] pose one or more fundamental issues that adversely affect performance, especially in terms of stability, scalability, and limited functionality (6, 7). A combination of these properties, especially broadened functionality and stable functional reconfigurability, will help make analog hardware competitive with entrenched digital processors, the strength of which is their general-purpose nature (i.e., arbitrary functional reconfigurability).

Motivated by these limitations, there have been many recent efforts to realize information storage and processing by using working mechanisms that include structural and/or chemical transformations, like conducting filament formation in resistive random access memory (RRAM) and transformation between crystalline and amorphous phases in phase change memory (PCM) (2, 8, 9). These mechanisms can enable multistate programmability, long-term state retention, and dimensional scalability that are needed for in-memory computing applications. However, the extensive need for Si-based complementary metal-oxide semiconductor (CMOS) circuitry as selectors and input/output functions substantially limits the device density and efficiency gains in these “CMOS+X” schemes (10). Furthermore, RRAM and PCM typically lack the multifunctionality and reconfigurability needed to realize more advanced neuromorphic systems that can exhibit sparsity, edge of chaos, and lifelong learning characteristics.

Recently, we reported on mixed-phase vanadium oxide (VOx) electrochemical random access memory (ECRAM) devices that could be voltage-programmed to perform synaptic, neuronal, and reconfigurable logic gate functionalities and that exhibited exceptional state retention at elevated temperature under short-circuit condition (~1 year at 453 K) (11). Consisting of coplanar reservoir and channel electrodes, each containing a mixture of V2O5 and VO2 phases, these devices were programmed by reversible insertion (extraction) of oxygen vacancies (VO··) to reduce (oxidize) the channel. Transport of VO·· was enabled by a monocrystalline yttria stabilized zirconia (YSZ) electrolyte (500 μm thick), which also served as the substrate. A collocated microheater was used to accelerate the programming of the VOx ECRAM devices. The enhanced retention times contrasted with the results of Lu et al. (12) who reported rapid self-discharge under short circuit for approximately centimeter square area V2O5/VO2 electrodes defined on similar thick monocrystalline YSZ substrates but with metallic platinum (Pt) used as the gate electrode instead of the V2O5/VO2 reservoir that we used. Very short retention times under short circuit (~15 min at 453 K) were also reported by our group for similar devices based on TiOx (13), consistent with other reports on ECRAM based on Li-ion (14) or proton mobile species (15). Note that outstanding >10-hour retention under short-circuit at 600°C was recently reported by Li et al. (16) for phase-separated TaOx ECRAM. However, unlike the single-function (programmable resistor) TaOx ECRAM, the VOx ECRAM we demonstrated is multifunctional, with tunable resistance, tunable threshold activation function and tunable oscillator characteristics.

Our previous results (summarized above) demonstrated (i) that multifunctional characteristics can be programmed dynamically in a phase-separated system such asV2O5-VO2-x where one of the phases (e.g., VO2-x) exhibits an insulator-to-metal transition (IMT); (ii) that phase-separation in both the channel and the reservoir, combined with sluggish VO·· diffusion at ambient temperatures, contribute thermodynamic and kinetic stabilization, respectively, to improve retention in ECRAM. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the integration of a microheater to elevate the device temperature during programming can substantially accelerate the programming times, as was recently demonstrated for Pr0.7Ca0.3MnO3-x ECRAM (17). However, the scalability of using electrochemical phase conversion for programming analog states and the device functionality with a material system that exhibits an IMT, has yet to be demonstrated.

An approach to improve retention in ECRAM by increasing the resistance of the solid electrolyte to ion movement has previously been investigated (13, 15, 18). Overcoming the high ionic resistance by field-accelerating the ions could be an alternative strategy to heat-assisted programming, but the required high switching voltage is likely to degrade the solid electrolyte and limit endurance, as observed in nonvolatile flash-like memories (19). The gate-integrated heaters, in addition to overcoming the kinetic barriers to phase conversion also enable the use of a high-resistance electrolyte (YSZ) without needing to rely on large electric fields for programming (thereby minimizing damage).

Here, we demonstrate VOx multifunctional, analog neuromorphic computing elements with vertically integrated thermal-gate electrodes with lateral dimension of a few micrometers and with active layer thicknesses scaled to ~20 nm. In contrast to our previous work, here we use thermal-gate electrodes that simultaneously provide an electric bias and local heating to drive ion transport and phase conversion reactions, as was recently demonstrated by Gross et al. (20) for TaOx electrothermal ECRAM (ETCRAM). Using electrochemical methods, scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM), and micro-Raman spectroscopy, we corroborate our proposed device mechanisms as follows: (i) The composition of VOx is tuned via electrochemical conversion of phases, VO2-x and V2O5; (ii) the redox state of the mixed-phase VOx defines the analog states for synaptic and neuronal functions; and (iii) the retention of these states depends on the kinetic and thermodynamic stabilization mechanism that persist even when active layer thickness is ~20 nm. The VOx ETCRAM can be dynamically programmed to function as an analog nonvolatile resistor, a nonlinear switch with a tunable threshold voltage, and an oscillator with a tunable frequency. Furthermore, the enhanced retention we previously reported for the VOx compared to TiOx remains valid for scaled devices with a vertical geometry.

RESULTS

Synaptic programming

The ETCRAM devices in this work belong to a broader class of electrochemical memories that use reversible redox reactions to tune the conductance state of a functional material and are commonly referred to as ECRAM (21). Many of the early ECRAM device types, including the Li-ion synaptic transistor that we demonstrated almost a decade ago used ion intercalation for programming analog states (22). Like Li-ion batteries, these early ECRAM devices had a redox state-dependent offset voltage resulting from the chemical potential difference (Gibbs free energy) between the channel and the reservoir (Fig. 1A). For example, in a LixWO3 ECRAM, the potential increases up to 4 V due to ion insertion, like LixCoO2-based devices (14, 22). When short-circuited, these devices rapidly lose state due to self-discharge. Reducing the thermodynamic driving force by making the reservoir and channel symmetric in composition and replacing the fast-diffusing Li-ions with VO·· improved retention at ambient temperature, but not sufficiently for long term inference applications (13). Truly nonvolatile ECRAM devices have only recently been demonstrated by Li et al. (16), who reported stable switching and state retention even at 873 K in ECRAM devices with phase-separated TaOx channel and reservoir electrodes, separated by YSZ electrolyte. Programming these TaOx ECRAM however, required external heating to ~573 K, making their integration into functional system challenging. The ETCRAM, shown schematically in Fig. 1B, overcomes the integration challenge of thermal activation of by using the gate electrode as the heater, in a manner similar to PCM (23). However, in contrast to PCM, which only relies on heat for encoding states, ETCRAM combines heating with applied electrochemical potential to improve analog programming and retention (20).

The VOx ETCRAM devices described in this work consist of a vertically stacked VOx channel with Au source and drain contacts, a thin film YSZ electrolyte, a VOx reservoir, and Pt heater-gate electrode (Fig. 1, E and F), all deposited by sputter deposition and patterned using optical lithography. Placement of the channel on top (Fig. 1E) or bottom (Fig. 1F) of the device stack did not significantly affect the device performance. The channel-on-top geometry is particularly useful for operando micro-Raman spectroscopy. Channel conductance was tuned by biasing the heater-gate while grounding the channel (Fig. 1, A and B). Under typical programming conditions of 1.5 V, we measured a gate current of ~80 mA, resulting in heating of the device to 473 K (fig. S1). The increased temperature lowered the ionic resistance of the YSZ electrolyte by ~100× (fig. S3C). The direction of the electrochemical transport of VO·· is determined by the polarity of the electrical bias on the gate with respect to the channel. With positive (negative) bias applied to the gate, the channel was reduced (oxidized), while the reservoir was oxidized (reduced). The temporal dynamics during electrochemical programming are displayed in fig. S4. Comparison of pristine (Fig. 1, E and F) and cycled (Fig. 2E) devices did not reveal indications of structural degradation such as delamination that may be caused by cyclic heating and cooling during programming. This is consistent with the fact that the materials we used to fabricate our VOX ETCRAM have comparable thermal coefficients: Pt = 9 μm/(m⋅K) (24), YSZ = ~10 μm/(m⋅K) (25), and VOx = 6 to 17 μm/(m⋅K) (26). The programming power can be substantially reduced by scaling device dimensions and optimizing the gate-heater resistance. For example, reducing the device dimensions to 180 nm by 180 nm will reduce power required for a ΔT = 100 K to ~60 μW, as we recently demonstrated with TaOx ETCRAM (20).

We were able to reproducibly program resistance states across conductances separated by a ratio of over 17 (Fig. 1G), without observed degradation for >104 pulses at 353 K (also see fig. S2, G and H). Furthermore, we observed similar conductance switching using write pulses of 1.6 to 1.9 V and pulse widths of 10 ms to 100 μs (see fig. S2, A to D). The programmed states, under short-circuit conditions, exhibited a loss of ~10% or less in conductance over a period of 1 hour at 353 K (Fig. 1H). Similarly fabricated ECRAM with anatase TiOx electrodes and a thin-film YSZ electrolyte that was approximately twice as thick, had retention of ~15 min under short circuit at 353 K, indicating that the phase conversion reactions in VOx present a higher activation barrier compared to TiOx (13). However, Lu et al. (12) who studied phase conversion in VO2/V2O5 cells with Pt counter electrodes using x-ray absorption spectroscopy, reported rapid loss of state under short circuit, which indicates the importance of symmetric ECRAM device design (e.g., ΔGrxn ~ 0). To evaluate the device-to-device variability, we measured the channel (Gch) and heater (Gheater) conductances for 13 as-fabricated VOx ETCRAM devices with 4-μm channel lengths resulting in Gch = 0.33 ± 0.19 μS, Gheater = 12.1 ± 0.1 Ω, where the uncertainty corresponds to one standard deviation. The device-to-device variability in the initial conductance can be minimized at the array level using initialization procedure (20).

The enhanced retention of VOx ETCRAM compared to ECRAM based on Li+ (14) or H+ (15) mobile ions results from higher energy barrier for VO·· diffusion in YSZ, combined with additional barrier due to phase conversion in the channel and reservoir. For our thin-film nanocrystalline YSZ, we determined an activation energy of 0.42 eV for VO·· diffusion (see fig. S3C), which is substantially lower than the typically reported value of ~1 eV observed for large-grain polycrystalline and single-crystal YSZ, and consistent with previous reports of enhanced oxygen diffusivity in nanocrystalline YSZ (27). The energy barrier for phase conversion reactions for vanadium oxides based on the reported VO·· diffusion activation energies is ~0.8 eV [i.e., 0.65 eV for r-VO2 at 370 K (28), 0.93 eV for M-VO2 at 333 K (28), and 0.79 eV for α-V2O5 at 470 K (11)]. However, just as in the case of YSZ, we expect that in nanocrystalline vanadium oxides, the activation energy for diffusion and phase conversion could be significantly reduced compared to values obtained for larger grain microstructures.

In addition to phase conversion, stabilization is also provided by the symmetric design of the ETCRAM. So long as the programming redox reaction only changes the relative fractions of the phases present in the channel and the reservoir electrodes, there is no net Gibbs free energy change (ΔGrxn ~ 0). For example, in a Li+/WO3 ECRAM, the electrochemical potential increases up to 4 V for a channel conductance modulation by ~300 μS (14). In contrast, the VOx ETCRAM exhibits <40 mV potential difference between the channel and reservoir electrodes, with little dependence on the conductance state (Fig. 2B). Despite a near-zero open-circuit voltage, we observed a loss of ~10% in conductance state in 1 hour at 353 K under short circuit (Fig. 1H). We suspect that parasitic reactions with the ambient and/or interfaces could lead to retention loss, a process that becomes increasingly important as the channel thickness is decreased and will require additional development to realize long term retention. Passivation coatings that present an effective barrier to oxygen and water diffusion, such as HfOx, can dramatically improve retention in the ambient at elevated temperature and short circuit (20, 29). Retention loss could also result because of formation of metastable phases and/or point defects distributions, particularly at interphase and grain boundaries. Further scaling and optimization of the programming schemes can minimize these effects.

The conductance switching characteristics we observe for VOx ETCRAM is consistent with synaptic functionality; however, they fall short of the linearity, symmetry, range, and speed requirements for emerging non-volatile memories to be competitive with digital hardware solutions for artificial intelligence applications (30). Further scaling of the device lateral dimensions and the solid electrolyte thickness is likely to improve the overall switching efficiency and speed (i.e., from 100 μs to 17 ns as YSZ is scaled down from 320 to 5 nm, see fig. S10 and note S1 for details), as we recently demonstrated for TaOx ETCRAM that could be programmed with ~2 V pulses, <15 ns (20).

Scaling the device dimensions and increasing the device areal density could lead to undesired thermal cross-talk. Previously, VO2 based devices fabricated on single-crystal sapphire substrates (thermal conductivity ~35 W/m⋅K), exhibited thermal disturbance with heat source separated by 25 μm or less (31). We estimate this distance to be ~714 nm for 1-μm-thick SiO2/Si given the lower thermal conductivity (~1.1 Wm⋅K). Moreover, as VOx ETCRAM shows a stable retention at an elevated temperature (i.e., 353 K), VOx ETCRAM will tolerate thermal cross-talk even during programming (i.e. ΔT = ~80 K), which we anticipate will enable further scaling of an array of VOx ETCRAMs.

Demonstrating phase conversion and coexistence

As we recently demonstrated for planar VOx ECRAM devices, operando micro-Raman spectroscopy is a powerful tool for capturing phase transformations occurring during electrochemical programming. Using ETCRAM devices with the heater-on-the-bottom geometry, we mapped the channel composition using micro-Raman in reduced (Rch = 553.3 kΩ) and oxidized states (Rch = 7.6 MΩ) (Fig. 2A). Both states show peaks consistent with VO2 (192 cm−1) and V2O5 (145 cm−1) but with different ratios (Fig. 2C). As expected, the micro-Raman maps reveal that the high-resistance state is dominated by fully oxidized V2O5 phase, VO2 dominates the low-resistance state (Fig. 2D). Additional micro-Raman data are shown in fig. S7.

Although optical spectroscopy is convenient, its resolution is limited to ~1 μm. To investigate phase transformations in our devices at smaller dimensions, we used scanning transmission microscopy with electron energy-loss spectroscopy (STEM/EELS), shown in Fig. 2E and fig. S6. Because of the highly reduced channel thickness and the nanocrystalline nature of the VOx layers, we were not able to use selected area diffraction to identify phases, relying instead on EELS analysis, as we have already done previously (11). The EELS analysis is largely consistent with the micro-Raman results, showing majority presence of VO2 in the pristine channel, while the oxidized channel contained a layered mixture of an oxygen-rich V2O5 phase close to the YSZ interface (cyan in color). Not only is there clear phase coexistence, but there is also appreciably uniform layering of the two phases consistent with transport of O vacancies. Such layering independently confirms the bulk nature of the phase coexistence (that was also inferred from Raman mapping). In the reduced state, we observed an oxygen-deficient phase close to V2O3 in composition (likely driven by O vacancies being transported into the channel during reduction). We did not observe this phase in our micro-Raman analysis likely because of the higher electronic conductivity that results in Raman signal loss the weakness of Raman modes for this phase (32). Nonetheless, independent verification of the main phases using both EELS and Raman spectroscopy provides a high degree of confidence in phase identification. To study stability and reliability of these results, we performed these EELS measurements after the devices were stored in an ambient environment for about 1500 hours (2 months). The observation of phase coexistence in a 20-nm-sized system is notable given the dominant interfacial and surface energies that plague energy storage systems based on similar principles and is consistent with the notable chemical versatility of vanadium oxides able to coexist in a variety of structures and oxidation states (33).

Programmable neuronal characteristics

We have previously shown that planar VOx ECRAM can exhibit highly nonlinear switching when a voltage pulse exceeding the threshold value, Vth, is applied to the channel to cause an IMT driven by joule heating, (11). We also demonstrate that Vth along with the IMT temperature, could be reversibly tuned, similarly to the low bias synaptic conductance. Threshold switching is a natural consequence of the VO2 in the channel undergoing a sufficient increase in local temperatures (to reach the IMT temperature) at a threshold electrical power level, leading to an abrupt increase in conductance (34, 35). We observe similar characteristics in VOx-ETCRAM, where the heater-gate electrode can be effectively used to tune the Vth by changing the channel temperature (Fig. 3, C and D, and fig. S1) or its redox state (Fig. 3, E and F, and fig. S10). We used a nanogap structure (i.e., lateral channel lengths in the order of 140 nm) on the channel (Fig. 3, A and B) to have reasonable Vth (i.e., <7 V) by confining Joule heating in a small area. As expected from phase-coexistence and tuning of phase fractions, electrochemical reduction or elevated temperature result in a lower Vth (Fig. 3, E and F).

Our mechanistic understanding of synaptic programming is based on the electrochemical tuning of the relative fractions of VO2 and V2O5 via conversion reactions. To examine how this mechanism can explain the tuning of threshold switching (activation function), we adapted an experimentally calibrated effective medium approximation (EMA) model to VOx-ETCRAM by creating percolation pathways in a mixture of insulating and metallic phases (Fig. 3G), which evolve with temperature or electrochemical tuning (36). We then applied this model to reproduce the resistance-temperature curves we measured for ETCRAM programmed to two different states (Fig. 3H). By combining a compact thermal model (fig. S9 and table S1) with the EMA model, the reconfigurable neuronal switching behavior also could be reproduced (Fig. 3, I and J) with reasonable quantitative accuracy (Fig. 2, E and F). This result bolsters the notion that the synaptic and the neuronal behaviors follow phase coexistence.

Threshold switching often accompanies instabilities that manifest as oscillations [e.g., when biased close to the switching threshold, explained elsewhere (37)]. Our VOx ETCRAM also exhibited self-oscillating behavior upon biasing near the threshold with a load resistor connected in series (Fig. 4, A and B). Similarly to the threshold switching, these self-oscillations were tunable (Fig. 4C). In some biasing conditions, the oscillations were periodic (Fig. 4C and fig. S11, A to E), while in other biasing conditions, they were highly stochastic (Fig. 4D). Transitioning from highly periodic to stochastic oscillations is consistent with systems capable of exhibiting edge of chaos characteristics, as previously reported for VO2 based devices (38) and is one of the key properties of biological neurons responsible for the high efficiency of animal brains (39).

DISCUSSION

We demonstrated a scaled electrochemical memory with an integrated heater gate electrode and mixed-phase VOx channel and reservoir electrodes (VOx ETCRAM) that can be voltage programmed to exhibit synaptic (analog tunable conductance) and neuronal (threshold switching with tunable threshold and oscillatory) functionalities. We showed that programming of the VOx ETCRAM proceeds by phase conversion of two thermodynamically stable phases, VO2 and V2O5, and persists as channel thickness is reduced to ~20 nm, which indicates promising size scaling for potential applications in densely packed electronic chips. The integrated heater-gate electrode in VOx ETCRAM helps overcome the kinetic barriers due to oxygen vacancy transport and conversion reactions in VOx ETCRAM, thus enabling faster switching and enhanced retention. We emphasize that our demonstrations are aimed at proving a scientifically fundamental process as a plausible primitive for processing and storing of information, while performance optimization is a subject for following studies. Such a primitive that offers a pathway to stabilized phase coexistence may be useful in various other applications as well.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

VOx ETCRAM fabrication

Two types of vertically stacked VOx ETCRAM device with a microheater were fabricated. For the heater on the top devices, the fabrication was started with 20.8-nm-thick VOx channel layer sputtered and patterned on a Si wafer with 1-μm-thick thermal SiO2, followed by 319.6-nm-thick YSZ, 66.4-nm-thick VOx reservoir layer, and 128.7-nm Pt gate and annealing at 673 K for 1600 s in air. Eighty-nanometer-thick SiO2 layer is used as a passivation layer for the devices (Fig. 1E). For the channel on the top devices, 90-nm-thick Pt is sputtered and pattern on a Si wafer with 1-μm-thick thermal SiO2 followed by 74.3-nm-thick VOx reservoir layer, 234.8-nm-thick YSZ, and 70.5-nm VOx channel layer and annealing at 673 K for 1600 s in air (Fig. 1F). For the nanogap devices used for neuronal behavior, nanogaps (Au/Cr) are formed on the channel on the top devices with e-beam lithography and lift-off. Eighty-nanometer-thick passivation layer is deposited and patterned on the devices’ active area (Fig. 3B). Pt was particularly chosen (as against Au) because Pt is both chemically inert as well as nondiffusive, whereas Au is known to diffuse into semiconducting materials.

Transmission electron microscopy

TEM lamellae were prepared on an FEI Helios Nanolab 660 Dualbeam FIB/SEM. STEM-EELS measurements were performed at 300 kV on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Titan Themis Z equipped with a SuperX EDS detector and a Gatan Quantum 969 GIF. STEM EELS was performed with a monochromated electron beam (FWHM@ZLP = 0.16 eV) and on the CMOS-based K2 detector in counted mode. Typical pixel times were 5 to 10 ms to limit damage to the V-O phase. The compositions of VO2 and V2O5 identified using EELS were also independently identified using Raman spectroscopy, meaning that there were no uncertainties in these identifications. Since V2O3 was not independently verified by Raman spectroscopy, we estimate the error in estimating x (within VOx) for V2O3 to be <0.5.

Raman spectroscopy

The Raman map of the VOx ETCRAM’s channel at its various resistance states were measured with Renishaw inVia micro Raman spectroscopy system with a 532-nm laser as a light source. The map was acquired with 1 μm of resolution on a 10 μm–by–10 μm area of the 8-μm VOx ETCRAM’s channel. The laser power was minimized (to 0.5% of 50-mW laser) to prevent the heating of the channel during the mapping (in a duration of 5 s of acquisition time for each pixel). The resistance state of the VOx ETCRAM device was programmed with a 4200A-SCS and its pulse measure unit by applying a pulse train with pulse amplitudes ±2.4 V and widths of 100 ms until achieving target resistance states. The inter pixel values on the Raman map (Fig. 2C) were linearly interpolated between the data points (i.e., 1 μm of resolution) for better visualization. The phase fraction of VO2 and V2O5 on the channel was estimated from the Raman map data (fig. S6) by calculating the relative peak area at 145 cm−1 (V2O5) and 192 nm−1 (VO2) at each pixel.

Thermal imaging with an IR camera

The thermal imaging of the integrated heater of a VOx ETCRAM during its Joule heating was captured with a Telops FAST infrared (IR) camera (FAST M2k) with a 4× microscope lens (G4X). The Joule heating of the heater was captured with 34 μs of exposure time and 64 by 4 window. The temperature of the heater during its Joule heating was calibrated with the IR camera data with an external heater

(fig. S12). The temperature from the IR camera data was used only for visualization of heating and long-term heater endurance test as it cannot measure the exact peak temperature of a heater due to its limited resolution. The exact peak temperature of the heater was estimated by using the integrated Pt heater as a resistive temperature detector (the inset of fig. S3C).

Electrical measurement

The synaptic behavior of VOx ETCRAM were measured with a Keithley 4200A-SCS semiconductor parameter analyzer. The measurements that require a pulse shorter than 5 ms, such as neuronal behavior, were performed with PMUs attached to the Keithley 4200A-SCS. The gradual cycling (i.e., Fig. 1G figs. S7 and S9) was measured with our custom setup with a NI DAQ and transimpedance amplifier circuits for faster data acquisition over long period of time. For measuring oscillations, the input bias was provided by 4200A-SCS, while the output voltage oscillation was monitored using a Keysight MSOX2000A oscilloscope. The cyclic voltammetry measurements were performed with a BioLogic SP-300 potentiostat. All the measurements were performed in an ambient condition. For the measurements that require the measurement temperature as a variable, the temperature was controlled with a Nextron microprobe system with a heating stage.
