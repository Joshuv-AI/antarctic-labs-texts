Experimental models in immuno-oncology and their translational impact
Authors: Shayan Boozarjomehri-Amnieh, Shaghayegh Mohammadioun, Sina Mahmoudian, Kiarash Saleki, Nima Rezaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExperimental models play a fundamental role in bridging basic scientific discoveries with clinical applications in humans. Integrating in vivo studies into oncology research approaches has the potential to improve therapeutic innovation and support translational strategies. The remarkable similarities between animals and human immune systems have enabled researchers to better clarify immune pathways involved in cancer development and therapy. Moreover, ongoing preclinical investigations are still required to engineer immunomodulatory drugs and other emerging therapeutic approaches. Despite the considerable contributions of in vivo models, particularly murine systems, their future use in experimental theragnostics has become a subject of debate. Ethical concerns and questions regarding the extent to which these models accurately replicate human disease have prompted growing interest in alternative platforms, including organoids, organ-on-chip technologies, and advanced two- and three-dimensional cell culture systems. In the present review, we summarize current experimental evidence on immuno-oncology experimental models used across various cancer types and examine the evolving role of these models. We also discuss whether animal models are gradually becoming less satisfactory due to emerging technologies or biological limitations, or whether their continued application remains justified in certain circumstances.Keywords: Experimental models, Immuno-oncology, Immunotherapy, Translational Medicine

Introduction

Cancer is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide and continues to signify a major global health burden in spite of significant advances in diagnosis and treatment [1]. Increasing evidence indicates that tumor development and progression are not solely determined by the intrinsic properties of malignant cells but are also influenced by complicated interplays with the surrounding tumor microenvironment, particularly the immune system [2]. Such interactions regulate critical processes, including tumor initiation, immune evasion, metastatic dissemination, and therapeutic responsiveness. Consequently, the study of tumor–immune system interactions has become a major topic in modern oncology and has led to the rapid expansion of immuno-oncology as a major field of cancer research [3].

Experimental animal models have long been indispensable tools for investigating the biological mechanisms underlying cancer development and immune regulation. These models provide controlled in vivo platforms that allow researchers to examine tumor growth, immune responses, and therapeutic interventions under defined experimental conditions [4]. A wide range of animal models has been developed to reproduce different aspects of human malignancies, including xenograft models, syngeneic tumor models, genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs), patient-derived xenografts (PDXs), and humanized mouse systems [5, 6]. Each of these experimental systems offers special benefits for studying tumor–immune interactions and evaluating novel immunotherapeutic strategies.

Recently, animal models have contributed to the preclinical development of multiple immunotherapeutic approaches that have revolutionized cancer treatment. These include immune checkpoint inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies, cancer vaccines, and adoptive cell therapies such as chimeric antigen receptor T cells (CAR T) and CAR-NK cells [7–9]. Additionally, emerging technologies such as nanomedicine and gene-editing systems including CRISPR/Cas, and microbiome-based treatments are increasingly being investigated using animal models to explore innovative diagnostic and therapeutic approaches in oncology [10].

Despite their substantial role in medical research, animal models present important translational limitations. Differences in immune system architecture, tumor heterogeneity, metabolism, and microenvironmental factors between animals and humans may have an effect on the predictive value of preclinical findings [11]. As a consequence, therapies that demonstrate promising antitumor effects in animal studies may fail to achieve similar efficacy in clinical trials. These challenges have stimulated growing interest in complementary experimental platforms designed to better mimic human physiology, including three-dimensional organoid cultures, organ-on-chip technologies, and advanced in vitro tumor models [12–14].

Given these considerations, a detailed assessment of the role of animal models in immuno-oncology research remains essential.

This review provides a structured, translationally oriented analysis of animal models in immuno-oncology. Specifically, it examines how different model systems help the development of cancer immunotherapies and critically evaluates their translational relevance and limitations. The manuscript first describes the principal animal models used to investigate tumor–immune interactions, then analyzes their roles in advancing immunotherapeutic strategies. Finally, key translational challenges are discussed along with the limitations of current models and emerging approaches that may improve the predictive value of preclinical research.

History of animal models in immuno-oncology

The hypothesis that the immune system can prevent or control cancer dates back to Paul Ehrlich, who first demonstrated tumor immunogenicity using rodent transplantation models. His work showed that hosts could be immunized against specific tumors and that cellular mechanisms mediate tumor rejection. With the development of inbred rodent strains, researchers identified tumor-specific antigens and confirmed that immunized animals rejected subsequent tumor challenges but not normal cells. Later studies, such as those by Old and Zbar with BCG, further validated immune-mediated antitumor responses [15].

Research in both rodents and humans also revealed that tumors employ immune-evasion mechanisms, culminating in to the concept of immunoediting, where immune surveillance shapes tumor progression by eliminating some cell populations while allowing others to persist. Over time, animal models have become essential for studying these interactions and have expanded into evaluating diagnostic tools, molecular technologies, and imaging methods, reflecting continuous progress in experimental immuno-oncology. These historical developments not only established the foundation of tumor immunology but also highlighted early discrepancies between experimental findings and clinical outcomes, underscoring the importance of model selection in translational research.

Experimental models in immuno-oncology

In preclinical immunotherapy research, several key approaches rely on specialized animal models, including xenograft systems, GEMMs, and humanized rodent models. These platforms utilize living organisms to explore the complex biological mechanisms underlying cancer and to assess the efficacy and mechanisms of emerging immunotherapeutic strategies [16].

Xenograft

Xenograft models involve the transplantation of human or animal tumor cells into immunodeficient rodents, allowing partial reconstruction of the tumor microenvironment (TME), including vascular components. These models enable researchers to study tumor growth dynamics and evaluate therapeutic responses in a relatively individualized setting. However, despite being more complex than conventional in vitro cell line systems, xenograft models are limited in their ability to investigate interactions between tumor cells and immune cells, as the host rodents lack a fully functional immune system [17]. However, due to the absence of a functional immune system, xenograft models have limited predictive value for immunotherapy studies, particularly in evaluating immune checkpoint inhibitors and other immune-mediated therapies.

Genetically-modified mouse

GEMMs are rodents modified to develop specific cancer subtypes, allowing tumor initiation and progression to occur in ways that closely mimic human oncogenesis. As a result, they can reproduce key features of the TME, including complex immune interactions. These models are highly valuable for studying cancer biology and evaluating therapeutic interventions. However, GEMMs typically require long periods to develop and often display limited heterogeneity, as the engineered alterations usually target only a select set of genes [18]. Despite major advances in in vitro tumor modeling, no system can fully reproduce the complexity of the TME, cellular interactions, or the anatomical architecture of tumors. To investigate cancer biology in preclinical settings, tumor cells can be implanted into genetically engineered rodents either as cell suspensions or as tumor tissues obtained from patients. Typically, immunodeficient rodents are required to avoid immune-mediated graft rejection [19].

Despite their ability to model tumor–immune interactions, GEMMs may not fully capture the genetic and immunological heterogeneity of human cancers, which can limit their translational applicability.

Patient-derived xenograft

PDX models preserve the donor’s genetic characteristics and histopathological features across multiple passages, making them valuable tools for studying tumor growth, tumor–microenvironment interactions, metastatic behavior, and responses to anticancer therapies. These models are frequently analyzed using techniques such as fluorescent labeling of tumor cells and bioluminescence imaging to monitor tumor progression in vivo [19]. However, the use of immunodeficient hosts limits their application in immuno-oncology, as they cannot fully replicate human immune responses.

Germ-free

Germ-free models provide a unique platform to investigate the role of the microbiome in modulating anti-tumor immunity and influencing responses to immunotherapy. The interaction between the gut microbiota and the innate immune system has been clarified through studies using germ-free (GF) preclinical models. The gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in shaping the architecture and maintaining the physiological function of the gut-associated lymphoid tissues (GALTs). Furthermore, the identification of the pattern recognition receptor (PRR) superfamily has highlighted their key function in detecting exogenous microorganisms through innate immune cells and intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) [20].

Commercial probiotic strains are characterized by multiple structural layers composed of polysaccharides (PS), peptidoglycan, teichoic acids, and lipoproteins. These components carry microbe-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) that are specifically recognized by PRRs, thereby contributing to host immune modulation and intestinal homeostasis [20].

Gram-positive bacteria possess a peptidoglycan (PG) cell wall, an essential polymer that maintains bacterial cell shape and structural integrity. In certain Lactobacillus species (LAB), peptidoglycan has been associated with the stimulation of T helper type 1 (Th1) immune responses. The interaction between lactobacilli and the host is influenced by the specificity of receptor molecules expressed on host cells, which recognize distinct molecular structures present on bacterial surfaces [21].

In addition to their cell wall components, these microorganisms secrete various bioactive substances that contribute to the intestinal microenvironment and interact with the host intestinal mucosa. Many probiotic bacteria also produce exopolysaccharides (EPS), which can modulate the adhesion of both probiotics and enteropathogens to intestinal mucus. The beneficial effects of EPS include the formation of protective biofilms that inhibit pathogen colonization, thereby contributing to host defense against infectious agents. Furthermore, EPS exhibit antioxidant properties by scavenging free radicals that promote oxidative stress and DNA damage, suggesting potential implications in cancer prevention. For example, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum has been reported to enhance antioxidant activity and protect epithelial cells from oxidative injury [21].

The symbiotic relationship between the host and its microbiota is maintained by mucosal surface barriers that act as critical interfaces between the body and the external environment. Because these barriers are continuously exposed to environmental stressors, efficient and rapid repair mechanisms are necessary to preserve tissue homeostasis. When barrier integrity is compromised, microbial components can modulate immune responses in ways that promote either pro-inflammatory or immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments. Consequently, alterations in gut microbiota may contribute to cancer progression, such as breast cancer, through persistent inflammation, which has been closely associated with tumor initiation and development.

Overall, experimental animal models remain indispensable tools for investigating tumor biology and tumor–immune interactions under controlled conditions. Each model offers distinct advantages; however, no single model fully reproduces the complexity of human cancers. Species-specific differences in immune regulation, tumor heterogeneity, stromal composition, and metabolic pathways may limit the translational accuracy of preclinical findings. Consequently, the most reliable experimental strategies often rely on combining complementary models to capture different biological dimensions of cancer and to improve the predictive value of preclinical research.

Collectively, these models differ in their ability to recapitulate human antitumor immunity, which directly influences their predictive power in immunotherapy development. Therefore, understanding the strengths and limitations of each model is essential for selecting appropriate systems to evaluate immune-based therapies such as checkpoint inhibitors, cancer vaccines, and adoptive cell therapies.

Translational contributions of animal models to cancer immunotherapy

Experimental animal models have served as a critical bridge between fundamental immunological discoveries and the clinical development of cancer immunotherapies. Beyond enabling the identification of key immune mechanisms, these models have provided essential platforms for testing therapeutic strategies and understanding their biological effects in vivo. In this section, we organize the evidence along a translational continuum, highlighting how mechanistic insights derived from animal models have informed therapeutic development, while also emphasizing the limitations that influence their clinical applicability.

Mechanistic insights into tumor–immune interactions from animal models

Animal models have been instrumental in uncovering the cellular and molecular mechanisms that govern tumor–immune system interactions. These mechanistic insights form the foundation for the development of immunotherapeutic strategies. However, the extent to which these findings can be translated into clinical benefit depends on how accurately these models reflect human immune biology.

Regulatory immune cells and immune suppression

Due to the close functional relationship between Th17 and Treg cells, a degree of cellular plasticity exists between these subsets in both mice and humans. Several studies have demonstrated that Treg cells can produce IL-17, a process associated with decreased expression of Foxp3 and increased expression of RORγt (Rorc in humans). This shift in transcriptional regulation promotes the conversion of Treg cells toward a Th17-like phenotype, thereby favoring the expansion of Th17 cell populations [31].

The presence of T cells with regulatory and immunosuppressive functions was first recognized through early experimental observations in mice following thymectomy, which highlighted the critical role of thymus-derived cells in controlling immune responses [32]. These cells have significant implications for studying therapeutics in experimental models [33].

An expanded population of regulatory B cells has been associated with suppression of NK cell–mediated, antigen-dependent cytotoxicity. In syngeneic rodent models of tumor development, genetically engineered B-cell-deficient animals exhibited reduced tumor progression accompanied by enhanced NK cell infiltration. Conversely, restoration of B cells diminished NK cell infiltration and promoted tumor growth [34].

In another study, the development of Kras-driven orthotopic pancreatic tumors was markedly inhibited in B-cell-deficient rodents compared with wild-type controls. However, reconstitution with the CD1d^high CD5⁺ B-cell subset in B-cell-deficient mice, performed 2 days prior to orthotopic implantation of Kras-expressing pancreatic ductal epithelial cells, restored tumor growth [35].

IL-17 has been shown to promote tumor cell proliferation while inhibiting tumor cell apoptosis. Nam et al. demonstrated that knockdown of the IL-17 receptor in 4T1 mouse mammary tumor cells significantly reduced tumor growth and increased apoptotic activity [36]. In addition, another experiment showed similar results in IL-17R-/- mice in comparison to WT rodents [37].

Despite numerous reports describing the pro-tumorigenic effects of IL-17, several studies have also demonstrated its suppressive role in tumor growth and invasion. Moreover, IL-17 has been shown to enhance antitumor immune responses in immunocompetent mice [38, 39].

In two syngeneic rodent tumor models, RenCa renal cell carcinoma and B16 melanoma, the therapeutic efficacy of subcutaneous and intratumoral administration of IL-21 protein was evaluated. The results demonstrated that intratumoral delivery of IL-21 was more effective than SC administration in reducing tumor growth and improving survival outcomes [40].

Another study demonstrated that mice treated with IL-21 exhibited enhanced elimination of tumor cells, with a greater proportion remaining tumor-free compared with untreated control groups. Furthermore, tumor rejection in certain cancer types occurs through an IL-21 mediated mechanism involving the NKG2D-dependent pathway [41].

The antitumor effects of IL-21 have been investigated in rodent models of B16 melanoma and MCA205 fibrosarcoma. Treatment with IL-21 plasmid DNA significantly inhibited tumor growth in a dose-dependent manner compared with animals receiving control plasmid DNA. This therapeutic approach enhanced NK cell–mediated antitumor activity, reduced tumor progression, and improved survival without causing significant toxicity [42].

IL21 pretreatment has been shown to inhibit the lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-regulated maturation of dendritic cells (DCs) and reduce cytokine release in DCs, similar to the effects observed in rodent DCs [43].

Preclinical studies have reported varying degrees of therapeutic success in models of non-small cell lung cancer, Hodgkin’s disease, renal cell carcinoma, head and neck tumors, hepatocellular carcinoma, and several other malignancies [44].

Effector immune responses and cytotoxic mechanisms

In parallel with regulatory mechanisms, animal models have also provided key insights into effector immune responses responsible for tumor cell elimination.

NKT lymphocyte subsets have been identified based on their TCR repertoire. In the early 1990s, a distinct population of T cells was described in rodents, later termed type I NKT cells, characterized by the expression of the invariant Vα14Jα18 TCR α-chain [45]. Early findings indicate that CAR-NKT cells can be applied safely and effectively for the treatment of patients with cancer [46].

Engineering type I NKT cells from hematopoietic stem cells has been proposed as a strategy to provide a long-term source of therapeutic NKT lymphocytes. In a humanized rodent model reconstituted with human hematopoietic stem cells and modified with a human NKT TCR gene, sustained and robust production of NKT-like cells was observed. These generated cells closely resembled endogenous human NKT lymphocytes, demonstrated the ability to target tumor cells, and significantly inhibited tumor progression across several human tumor xenograft rodent models [47].

Further investigations have explored whether the in vivo persistence and therapeutic efficacy of CAR-NKT cells could be improved by incorporating the NKT homeostatic cytokine IL-15 into the CAR construct. CAR-NKT cells engineered to target GD2 and express IL-15 demonstrated greater in vivo persistence, enhanced trafficking to tumor sites, and improved tumor control in rodent models of neuroblastoma. Histopathological analyses additionally indicated that these engineered cells did not produce significant toxicity [48].

The inherent ability of NK cells to exert cytotoxic effects against tumor cells initially generated considerable interest in their potential use in immunotherapy. Early preclinical studies demonstrated that NK cells contribute to the control of metastatic spread. Subsequent research further showed that stimulation of NK cells with activating agents, such as Poly I:C, a TLR-3 agonist, significantly enhanced resistance to metastatic disease in rodent models [49].

Cytokine signaling and immune modulation

Several subtypes of type I interferons are present in certain species but are absent in humans, with some being identified specifically in rodents [50].

Activation of the JAK2 pathway can inhibit AKT signaling, which in turn reduces the release of reactive oxygen species (ROS) while maintaining STAT-mediated signaling mechanisms. ROS play a dual role in cancer biology, as they may exert both protective and harmful effects. Through their involvement in immune surveillance and intracellular signaling pathways, ROS can contribute to improved control of cancer and infectious diseases. However, excessive or poorly regulated ROS production may promote tumor development and is also associated with inflammation and tissue damage. Experimental evidence from transgenic rodent models with elevated ROS levels has further demonstrated that excessive ROS can lead to the development of intestinal lesions [51, 52].

Although IFN-λ–based therapies show considerable potential as a treatment strategy for cancer, their mechanisms must be carefully understood to minimize possible protumor genic effects. In some studies, on bladder cancer, IFN-λ treatment was associated with the induction of matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), which contributed to enhanced tumor growth and invasiveness. Preclinical investigations have also reported that IFN-λ therapy can promote tubule formation and increase tumor cell migration. In experimental studies using malignant canine mammary gland cells, IFN-λ production was shown to stimulate myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), leading to increased metastasis, enhanced vasculogenesis and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), greater tumor cell migration through STAT3 signaling, and elevated invasive capacity. Additionally, higher levels of IFN-λ have been detected in rodents bearing invasive tumors [53, 54].

In patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the combination of immunotherapy with surgical intervention has been associated with complete tumor remission. One notable advantage of IFN-based combination strategies is that experimental studies in rodents have suggested that localized administration at low doses may be more effective than systemic high-dose exposure. This dose reduction can markedly decrease adverse effects and may also lower the risk of treatment-related conditions that could otherwise promote tumor progression [55].

Although limited evidence is available regarding the functional state of immune cell populations within the prostate cancer TME, findings from both animal models and human studies suggest that type I interferon (IFN-I) signaling in the TME can exert antitumor effects in cancers lacking the tumor suppressor gene phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN). In addition, several molecular regulators have been identified to play a significant role in tumor-immune connections in the TME, such as Zymogen granule protein 16B (ZG16B), which has developed as a specific mediator of tumor progression and immune modulation [56]. Experimental models deficient in both prostate-specific STAT3 and PTEN demonstrated accelerated tumor growth, increased metastatic potential, and the formation of significantly larger lesions compared with PTEN-deficient models alone. These effects appear to be mediated through the ARF–MDM2–p53 regulatory axis, which PTEN-deficient tumors fail to effectively activate, ultimately facilitating tumor dissemination. Nevertheless, preclinical studies in prostate cancer models indicate that inhibition of STAT3 can reduce tumor cell proliferation and limit metastatic spread [57].

Assessment of gene expression and protein production was performed to measure inflammatory mediators in rodent models of colon cancer. The findings showed that TNF-α and IFN-γ acted synergistically to intensify inflammation and promote tumor progression through activation of the NF-κB/STAT3 signaling pathway [58].

Multiple experimental studies in melanoma and other tumor models have confirmed the antitumor activity of interferons. Evidence from melanoma and colon adenocarcinoma models demonstrated that interferon-based gene therapy can exert significant anticancer effects. In tumor cells transfected with IFN-α2 cDNA, increased caspase activity was observed together with induction of p21 and dephosphorylation of the retinoblastoma protein, leading to cell-cycle arrest and apoptotic cell death. In hepatoma models, both IFN-α and IFN-λ were found to exhibit comparable antitumor efficacy, although their effects appeared to be mediated through distinct molecular pathways. Notably, combined administration of IFN-α and IFN-λ markedly improved therapeutic outcomes in rodent hepatoma models. Unlike treatment with IFN-α alone, the combined IFN-α/IFN-λ approach resulted in complete suppression of tumor growth, and approximately half of the treated animals achieved complete responses when either gene-therapy–based delivery or direct administration of these interferons was used [59].

Tumor progression has also been associated with the activation of inflammasome components and elevated levels of IL-1β at both primary and metastatic tumor sites in experimental animal and cellular cancer models. In contrast, rodents deficient in inflammasome components exhibited markedly reduced pulmonary tumor growth and metastatic spread. Evidence further indicates that the NLRP3 inflammasome, particularly within tumor-infiltrating macrophages, contributes to lymph angiogenesis, lymph node invasion, and metastasis through the production of IL-1β [60].

Pro-tumorigenic functions of γδ T cells have been associated with the production of specific cytokines, particularly interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and interleukin-17A (IL-17A), which are secreted by two distinct γδ T-cell subsets [61]. These subsets can be distinguished by the expression of surface markers such as CD27, CD122, and CD45RB. In rodents, the classification of these γδ T-cell populations is largely based on phenotypic marker expression, whereas in humans, their identification is primarily defined by their cytokine secretion profiles [62].

Recent studies have suggested tumor-associated lymphatic endothelial cells can activate immunosuppression and influence effector T-cell activity in the tumor microenvironment. Meanwhile, mouse models offer useful mechanistic visions, but species differences and reliance on a single model confine clinical translation. Further studies should confirm these results for various tumor types and investigate lymphatic-mediated immune evasion and the Ch25h/25-HC pathway as potential therapeutic targets [63].

Immunotherapies developed and validated in animal models

Building on mechanistic insights, animal models have played a pivotal role in the development and preclinical validation of diverse immunotherapeutic strategies. These systems have enabled the evaluation of efficacy, toxicity, and mechanisms of action prior to clinical translation.

Immune checkpoint modulation and monoclonal antibodies

Cancer immunotherapies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, cytokine therapies, vaccines, and adaptive cell approaches like CAR-T cells, have been used to explore potential predictive and therapeutic effects in animal studies [64].

Incidentally, some studies have gained notable results, such as the combination therapy of anti-PD-1 or CTLA-4 blockade with some like HSP90 inhibitor ganetespin, which has shown potentially changed survival rate and improved anti-tumor immunity in tumor-bearing mice. Additionally, the triple regimen used, like the PD-1 and CTLA-4 inhibitors alongside an ALDH⁺ cancer stem cell-based dendritic cell vaccine, represented substantial tumor regression, which boosted T-cells, lowered TGF-β, and strengthened CD8⁺ T-cell activity targeting cancer stem cells specifically [65, 66].

HER2-targeted CAR T cells suggested significant antitumor affinity against treatment-resistant in melanoma, eradicating both uveal and cutaneous melanoma cells in laboratory and preclinical studies. In immunodeficient NOG mice, tumor relapse was observed, highlighting their usefulness for evaluating CAR T cell safety and efficacy. Furthermore, IL-12 transgenic NOG mice have been identified as an advanced and more physiologically relevant platform to assess CAR-T therapies and contain cytokine modulation for enhanced therapeutic prediction [67].

Monoclonal antibodies of murine origin, containing rodent-specific sequences, exhibit relatively high immunogenicity. This limitation led to the development of chimeric antibody constructs, hybrid molecules composed of a rodent-derived variable region and a human constant region, designed to reduce the immunogenic potential observed in earlier monoclonal antibodies. Monoclonal antibody technology was enabled by Köhler and Milstein’s hybridoma method. Subsequent advances in recombinant DNA engineering led to chimeric antibodies, consisting of mouse variable regions and human constant regions, and later humanized antibodies, in which most murine sequences were substituted with human sequences to reduce immunogenicity [68, 69].

The involvement of BTLA in tumor progression has been examined in rodent models through the administration of plasmids encoding the extracellular domain of murine BTLA (psBTLA). Treatment with psBTLA resulted in a noticeable suppression of tumor growth in mice. This intervention was also associated with increased levels of IL-2 and IFN-γ, accompanied by decreased concentrations of IL-10 and TGF-β. In addition, a higher infiltration of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) was detected in psBTLA-treated animals. Collectively, these findings suggest that the BTLA–HVEM coinhibitory pathway plays a significant role in the regulation and progression of tumor growth [70].

In rodent models, deletion of the CTLA-4 gene resulted in early mortality caused by severe systemic immune dysregulation, characterized by extensive lymphocytic infiltration affecting multiple organs. Therefore, inhibition of CTLA-4 must be approached with caution, as excessive blockade can lead to severe autoimmune toxicity [71, 72].

Adoptive cell therapies

HER2-specific CAR-T cells have demonstrated the ability to induce regression of osteosarcoma xenografts in metastatic rodent models [73].

Encouraging evidence for the therapeutic potential of CAR-T lymphocytes has been demonstrated in preclinical studies targeting glypican-3 (GPC3) in HCC. CAR-T cells engineered to recognize GPC3 selectively eliminated GPC3-positive HCC cells while showing no cytotoxic activity against GPC3-negative tumor cells. Furthermore, in rodent models, these CAR-T lymphocytes effectively suppressed the growth of HCC xenografts expressing low levels of GPC3 and completely eradicated tumors with high GPC3 expression [74].

Experimental studies have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of human CAR-T cells targeting viral surface antigens in hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection. Beneficial effects were observed in an immune-competent mouse model, and the efficacy of human hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg)-specific CAR-T cells was further confirmed in humanized mouse models [75, 76].

However, rodent-derived CAR-T cells targeting the HBV envelope exhibited limited persistence and underwent cell death following an initial phase of expansion. These observations suggest potential limitations in the application of CAR-T cell therapy for the treatment of advanced HCC [77].

Several CAR-T cell constructs directed against the GD2 antigen have shown the capacity to recognize and lyse GD2-positive sarcoma and osteosarcoma cell lines in vitro. However, these constructs did not demonstrate significant therapeutic efficacy in xenograft osteosarcoma mouse models [78].

In addition to CAR-T cell therapy, T cell receptor (TCR) engineered T cells represent another promising immunotherapeutic approach. Although this strategy depends on antigen presentation through major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, it remains an attractive area for further investigation, particularly because it enables targeting of antigens that are not necessarily expressed on the cell surface. Recent studies have shown that TCR-transduced T cells directed against the histone H3 variant H3.3K27M, a frequently occurring antigen in diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma (DIPG), were able to suppress the progression of glioma xenografts in mouse models [79].

Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain these observations, suggesting that the tumor microenvironment may hinder CAR T cell activity by promoting early T cell exhaustion before effective tumor eradication occurs, or that heterogeneous expression of the targeted antigen on tumor cell surfaces may limit therapeutic efficacy. Nevertheless, recent research has demonstrated that CAR-T cells are capable of eliminating uveal melanoma (UM) and cutaneous melanoma cells both in vitro and in vivo in humanized mouse models [67].

GD2-targeted CAR-T cells combined with IL-15 demonstrated potent cytotoxic activity against tumor cell lines in vitro. Additionally, the use of chitosan–polyethylene glycol (PEG) thermoresponsive hydrogels as an encapsulation system for CAR-T cells served as an effective delivery platform that prolonged lymphocyte survival and enhanced their persistence. This approach resulted in efficient tumor cell elimination without detectable toxicity or adverse immune reactions and was associated with improved visual function in treated mice. Overall, the integration of IL-15 to enhance T-cell longevity, together with encapsulation in chitosan-PEG injectable hydrogels, improved the biodistribution and persistence of GD2 CAR-T cells, enabling effective eradication of retinoblastoma in vivo [80].

A phase II clinical trial (NCT02944162) has been conducted to further evaluate the efficacy of these CAR-NK cells on a larger scale. In addition, CAR-NK cells directed against CD4, an antigen expressed on acute myeloid leukemia (AML) blasts, have also been investigated. In one study, CD4-CD28-4-1BB-CD3ζ CAR-NK92 cells were evaluated both ex vivo and in mouse xenograft models and were found to efficiently eliminate CD4-positive AML cells [81].

Cytokine-based and combinational immunotherapies

Following observations that infection with Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) increased resistance in mice against transplanted syngeneic tumors [82], in 1976, BCG was introduced as a therapeutic approach for bladder cancer in humans and demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes [83]. These observations reinforce the concept that concurrent infections or immunogenic, non-infectious preparations can enhance antitumor immunity, thereby reduce the likelihood of recurrence or slow the progression of osteosarcoma.

In an experimental setting using TUBO cells, HER2-overexpressing cells derived from mammary carcinoma in Balb-NeuT transgenic mice, researchers evaluated immune activation both in vitro and in female Balb/C mice bearing TUBO tumors. While the in-vitro assays showed strong induction of immune responses, the in-vivo experiments did not reveal meaningful suppression of tumor growth. This discrepancy was attributed to the activity of regulatory T cells (CD4 + CD25 + Foxp3), which can dampen cytotoxic T-cell function and thereby diminish the antitumor immune activity generated in vivo [84, 85].

Another investigation evaluated a vaccination strategy for melanoma in mice using a CD40 agonist combined with poly I:C and tumor-derived antigens. This approach increased the number of T cells infiltrating the tumor; however, many of the pre-existing intratumoral T cells displayed reduced functional activity. Overall, the intervention led to a measurable reduction in tumor growth [86].

An in vivo investigation showed that combining immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) with vaccination strategies enhances antitumor activity and leads to prolonged survival in mice bearing B16-F10 melanoma. Autologous tumor vaccines have therefore been proposed as a promising approach for melanoma immunotherapy aimed at limiting disease progression; however, confirmation through advanced clinical studies is still required [87].

Collectively, studies using animal models have significantly contributed to the development and validation of modern cancer immunotherapies. These models have enabled mechanistic insights into immune checkpoint regulation, antigen presentation, and adoptive cellular therapies. Nevertheless, variability in immune system architecture between species can influence treatment responses and toxicity profiles. As a result, therapies that demonstrate promising efficacy in preclinical models do not always replicate similar outcomes in clinical trials. Addressing these translational gaps requires careful model selection, integration of humanized systems, and the use of complementary experimental platforms to better approximate human immune responses.

Integration of conventional and emerging therapies in animal models

In addition to enabling the development of standalone immunotherapies, animal models have been extensively used to investigate combinational strategies and emerging therapeutic technologies. These approaches aim to enhance treatment efficacy by modulating the tumor microenvironment and improving immune system engagement.

Chemotherapy–immunotherapy interactions

One of the most commonly used and traditional approaches against cancer is chemotherapy, which kills rapidly dividing cancer cells. Meanwhile, evidence from animal studies suggests it can also influence anti-tumor immune responses and affect the success of immunotherapy. Moreover, immunogenic cell death (ICD) is one of the chemotherapeutic approaches that activates the release of tumor antigens and danger signals that activate dendritic cells and get powered T-cell responses. Studies have shown that certain chemotherapy drugs also reshape the tumor microenvironment by reducing immunosuppressive cells, such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells and regulatory T-cells, by improving the effectiveness of immune checkpoint therapies.

Moreover, mice lacking CCR2 exhibited an improved response to immune checkpoint blockade therapy. Another chemokine receptor implicated in resistance to chemotherapy is CXCR2, which has been reported across several cancer models. For instance, deletion of CXCR2 was associated with enhanced sensitivity to paclitaxel treatment in breast cancer models [88].

In the setting of doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity, findings from animal studies suggest that angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors may exert antioxidant effects and help preserve mitochondrial respiratory function [89]. In a large randomized controlled trial, patients who developed elevated troponin I levels following chemotherapy were randomly allocated to receive either enalapril or a placebo. Regarding doxorubicin-associated cardiotoxicity, an animal study demonstrated that spironolactone could attenuate doxorubicin-induced cardiac fibrosis and apoptosis [90].

In experimental models of doxorubicin exposure, treatment with angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors was found to suppress mitochondrial fission and decrease apoptosis in mice [91].

In experimental models, trastuzumab was shown to enhance oxidative stress in myocardial cells exposed to the β-adrenergic agonist isoproterenol, whereas ranolazine mitigated these oxidative effects [92]. In an in vivo experimental study, administration of ranolazine to mice previously treated with trastuzumab emtansine resulted in improved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and attenuation of cardiac remodeling in the post-treatment setting [93].

One of the approaches toward triggering systemic inflammatory responses beyond their direct tumor-killing effects is chemotherapy drugs. In mouse studies, the changes in blood–brain barrier integrity were caused the activation of the astrocytes, and upregulation levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [94].

In addition, animal models of chemotherapy-induced cognitive damage suggest increased inflammatory cytokines in brain regions involved in neurogenesis after treatment with drugs such as doxorubicin, 5-fluorouracil, cisplatin, paclitaxel, and trastuzumab, as well as combination therapies like cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, 5-fluorouracil, docetaxel, doxorubicin, and cyclophosphamide [95–101].

In this respect, these findings signify that chemotherapy not only caused direct cytotoxicity but also changed the TME. Therefore, it is influencing the overall response to immunotherapy.

Nanomedicine and theragnostic approaches

Doxorubicin (DOX) release from the nanoparticles occurred preferentially under hypoxic conditions. In an in vivo SCC7 tumor-bearing rodent model, animals received intravenous injections of saline, free DOX, or DOX-conjugated nanoparticles at a dose of 5 mg/kg. Treatment with the nanoparticle formulation resulted in approximately a twofold reduction in tumor weight and volume compared with free DOX therapy, with the nanoparticles showing preferential accumulation at the tumor site [102].

In rodent lymphoma models, administration of edelfosine in solution reduced the rate of metastasis but failed to completely prevent its occurrence. In contrast, delivery of edelfosine through lipid nanoparticles (LNs) resulted in complete suppression of metastatic spread. Beyond LNs, squalenoyl-based nanoparticles have also been explored as carriers for anticancer agents such as edelfosine and gemcitabine. Multidrug nanoparticles composed of squalenoyl-gemcitabine and edelfosine have demonstrated strong inhibitory effects against osteosarcoma cells. Notably, squalenoyl nanoparticles are capable of loading higher drug quantities than LNs and are lipid-free carriers [103, 104].

Amino-bisphosphonates (N-BPs) can be applied as anticancer agents either alone or in combination with γδ T-cell–based immunotherapy. One strategy to improve their therapeutic performance involves encapsulating N-BPs within liposomal carriers. In a mouse model of lung cancer metastasis, liposomal alendronate demonstrated superior antitumor efficacy and lower toxicity compared with zoledronate in vivo. In this approach, liposomal alendronate was directed toward tumor cells through the αvβ6 integrin receptor and combined with γδ T-cell immunotherapy. The treatment increased tumor cell sensitivity to γδ T cells and enhanced cytotoxic activity in vitro. However, despite these findings, the targeted formulation did not provide additional therapeutic benefit in the overall treatment outcome [105]. Treatment with methotrexate-loaded solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) has been reported to significantly prolong survival in mice [106].

Graphene-based platforms combining chemotherapy with photodynamic and photothermal therapies have demonstrated remarkably high therapeutic efficacy, leading to complete tumor eradication in mouse models. Similarly, fullerene-based systems that integrate diagnostic photoimaging with phototherapeutic modalities within a synergistic photo-theragnostic framework have shown considerable potential for precision-guided cancer treatment [107].

Over recent decades, graphene has emerged as a highly promising nanomaterial for photothermal applications due to its strong absorption in the near-infrared (NIR) region. Early in vivo investigations demonstrated the feasibility of this approach using polyethylene glycol (PEG)–modified nanographene sheets to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of photothermal therapy [108]. PEGylated nanographene sheets (NGS) have exhibited notable in vivo properties, including effective passive tumor targeting and relatively low accumulation in the reticuloendothelial system. Upon irradiation with an 808 nm laser at 2 W/cm2, tumor volumes steadily decreased until complete elimination was achieved. Importantly, preliminary toxicity assessments in mice showed no detectable adverse effects, as confirmed by histological analyses, blood chemistry evaluations, and comprehensive hematological profiling [108].

Multifunctional nanocarriers were developed by modifying magnetic graphene oxide (MGO) with folic acid (FA) and triformyl cholic acid (TCA), resulting in MGO-TCA-FA nanostructures that enable enhanced targeted recognition of HCC cells via FA. DOX was loaded onto these nanocarriers through hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking interactions. Control groups, NIR laser irradiation alone, or MGO-TCA-FA without laser application showed continuous tumor growth. However, the combination of MGO-TCA-FA with NIR laser produced a significant inhibition of tumor development, attributed to a synergistic photothermal effect. Furthermore, treatment with DOX-loaded MGO-TCA-FA plus NIR laser resulted in the most pronounced tumor suppression and near-complete eradication, demonstrating superior efficacy compared to individual therapies. These findings underscore the improved therapeutic potential by integrating chemotherapy with novel technologies [109]. Chemotherapy is a widely used treatment approach in oncology; however, its clinical effectiveness is often compromised by poor water solubility, limited specificity in targeting tumor cells, and considerable systemic toxicity, which adversely affect patient health. Nanomaterials have emerged as highly effective drug delivery vehicles, offering improved targeting precision that can enhance therapeutic outcomes while minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissues [109].

A stealth liposomal formulation of docetaxel (DTX) modified with TZM demonstrated enhanced antiproliferative effects in various breast cancer models, including 2D cultures, 3D spheroids, and orthotopic xenograft mice, compared to standard treatments. In vivo distribution data indicated that attaching TZM to the liposomes primarily improves the internalization of the drug by cancer cells rather than increasing its accumulation within the tumor tissue [110].

Modification of PEG-PLA nanoparticles encapsulating arsenic trioxide with an anti-human CD44v6 single-chain variable fragment (scFv) led to increased apoptosis in PANC-1 cancer cells and enhanced accumulation of the nanoparticles in PANC-1 tumor-bearing mice [111].

Although targeting PSMA is a widely utilized strategy with considerable therapeutic promise, antibody-nanoparticle conjugates can also be engineered to target prostate stem cell antigen (PSCA). In nude mice implanted with PC3M xenografts, docetaxel-loaded core–shell nanoparticles conjugated with single-chain PSCA antibodies demonstrated sustained tumor suppression over the long term without causing significant systemic toxicity [111].

Nanoparticles based on fullerene were engineered to enable photoacoustic (PA) imaging-guided combined tumor photothermal therapy (PTT) and photodynamic therapy (PDT). These nanoparticles incorporate NIR absorbers formed by conjugating NIR-absorbing antenna molecules to fullerene. After intravenous injection, these nanoparticles accumulated near tumor sites in nude mice over 6 h, primarily via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, and produced strong photoacoustic signals. Compared to both fullerene and antenna-only nanoparticles, the fullerene conjugates exhibited superior generation of reactive oxygen species and heat. Subsequent in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that these nanoparticles effectively suppressed tumor growth through synergistic PDT and PTT mechanisms. These results provide new insights into the development of photosensitizers for enhanced cancer photo-theragnostic applications [112].

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) containing high atomic number (Z) elements, such as hafnium-based MOFs (Hf-MOF), enhance radiotherapy (RT) effectiveness by increasing X-ray attenuation. Incorporating high-Z elements into inorganic nanoparticles within MOFs further improves tumor treatment through radio-sensitization. The high porosity and optimal pore size of MOFs contribute to improved radio-sensitizing efficiency. A zirconium-based MOF nanocomposite has been developed for dual radiotherapy sensitization, demonstrating efficacy in treating A549 tumor-bearing immunodeficient mice [113]. PDT is a therapeutic approach that employs photosensitizing agents activated by light in the presence of oxygen to induce apoptosis and cause targeted tissue damage [114].

Porous DBP-UiO nanoparticles, synthesized via a solvothermal method using 5,15-di(p-benzoate) porphyrin (H2DBP) ligands coordinated with Hf4⁺ ions, measure on average 76.3 nm in diameter and act as efficient photosensitizers. Their high singlet oxygen generation efficiency is attributed to the spatial isolation of H2DBP ligands within the porous framework. Following PDT, DBP-UiO demonstrated significant tumor reduction with minimal toxicity in SQ20B subcutaneous xenograft mouse models, indicating promise for overcoming therapeutic resistance. Additionally, a chlorin-based MOF incorporating Hf4⁺ ions and 5,15-di(p-benzoate)chlorin (H2DBC) ligands, referred to as DBC-UiO, exhibited notable PDT efficacy in colorectal adenocarcinoma models (CT26 and HT29) [115].

Following nano-crystallization via cyanine-assisted exfoliation and loading with a DOX prodrug, TP-Por formed a composite material capable of inhibiting 4T1 tumor cells through combined chemotherapy and PTT under 808 nm light exposure. Additionally, ultrasonic exfoliation of TP-Por yielded two-dimensional covalent organic nanosheets (CONs), which were administered intravenously into HeLa tumor-bearing mice and demonstrated antitumor effects through PDT and under 635 nm irradiation [116].

A theragnostic nanoplatform based on covalent organic frameworks (COFs) was developed to induce immunogenic phototherapy. The system, designated as ICG@COF-1@PDA, combines near-infrared photothermal agents, ultrasonic exfoliation, polydopamine (PDA), and polyethylene glycol (PEG) coating. Upon irradiation with an 808 nm laser, this platform generated reactive oxygen species (ROS) and localized hyperthermia, enabling the synergistic effects of PDT and PTT. This approach induced immunogenic cell death, effectively inhibiting the growth of distant tumors in colorectal cancer models. Additionally, ICG@COF-1@PDA demonstrated efficacy in suppressing pulmonary metastases in breast cancer mouse models [117].

Liposomes were engineered to co-deliver indoximod (IND) and an anti-PD-1 antibody, targeting the IDO-1 and DOX pathways. In a murine breast cancer model using 4T1 cells, this liposomal formulation improved the pharmacokinetic profile of the encapsulated agents, resulting in enhanced antitumor efficacy and significant tumor size reduction. Toxicity assessments confirmed the safety of the treatment with no observed adverse effects on renal, cardiac, or hepatic functions [118].

The synthesized nanoparticles were dispersed within a hydrogel matrix and tested against both in vitro and in vivo tumor models derived from 4T1 breast carcinoma cells. These cells are characterized by low immunogenicity, high tumorigenic potential, invasive behavior, and spontaneous metastasis, and they can be readily transplanted into animal models [119].

Nanotechnology-based strategies have emerged as promising tools for improving cancer diagnosis and therapy through targeted drug delivery, controlled release systems, and combined theragnostic applications. Animal studies have demonstrated the potential of nanoparticle-based platforms to enhance drug accumulation in tumors and to modulate immune responses within the tumor microenvironment. However, challenges related to nanoparticle biodistribution, toxicity, long-term safety, and interspecies pharmacokinetic differences remain important limitations. Therefore, translating nanomedicine from experimental models to clinical practice requires further optimization of nanoparticle design, standardized evaluation protocols, and more predictive preclinical systems.

CRISPR-based immunotherapy

CRISPR/Cas9 therapies, especially those utilizing homology-directed repair (HDR) to correct gene mutations, hold great promise for revolutionizing the treatment of genetic disorders. However, HDR-based approaches face challenges due to the requirement for simultaneous in vivo delivery of Cas9 protein, guide RNA, and donor DNA. A novel strategy employed gold nanoparticles conjugated with DNA and combined with cationic polymers that disrupt endosomes to deliver Cas9 ribonucleoprotein complexes and donor DNA efficiently across diverse cell types. This approach successfully corrected the causative DNA mutation of Duchenne muscular dystrophy in mouse models through localized injection, while minimizing off-target genomic damage [120].

CRISPR technology has also been applied to the treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). A single-guide RNA (sgRNA) was designed to specifically target the dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) gene, aiming to regulate glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) activity. Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) nanocomplexes were administered via direct injection in a diabetic db/db mouse model. This intervention significantly downregulated DPP-4 gene expression, leading to a reduction in DPP-4 enzymatic activity. Consequential improvements included normalization of blood glucose levels, enhanced insulin sensitivity, and decreased hepatic and renal damage. These therapeutic effects were comparable to those achieved with sitagliptin, the widely used pharmacologic DPP-4 inhibitor that necessitates repeated dosing [121]. The A10-liposome-CRISPR/Cas9 chimera, when intravenously administered to mice bearing PSMA-positive prostate cancer, significantly reduced tumor size. Targeting PSMA with a specific RNA aptamer inhibited its enzymatic activity, resulting in decreased migration and invasion of prostate cancer cell [122]. Gene-editing technologies, particularly CRISPR-Cas systems, have revolutionized experimental oncology by enabling precise genetic manipulation in both tumor cells and immune components. In animal models, these tools have facilitated the generation of sophisticated cancer models as well as the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting oncogenic pathways or enhancing antitumor immunity. Despite these advances, concerns remain regarding potential off-target effects, delivery efficiency, and long-term genomic stability. Consequently, while CRISPR-based approaches hold considerable promise, rigorous validation in multiple preclinical systems and careful evaluation of safety parameters are essential before widespread clinical translation.

Microbiome–immune interactions in therapy response

Several studies have examined the efficacy of specific nutritional interventions, including prebiotics, probiotics, postbiotics, and synbiotics, in enhancing immunotherapy outcomes. For example, the infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells into colon carcinoma tumors in mice was significantly increased only when arginine supplementation was combined with cyclophosphamide treatment [123].

Dietary exclusion of tryptophan has been found to markedly inhibit tumor growth in animal models. In microbiome research and beyond, studies frequently focus on colorectal and breast cancers when exploring preventive or therapeutic strategies in many preclinical pathway analysis and drug-targeting studies [124–127]. While the relationship between the gut microbiome and response to CAR-T cell therapy remains unclear, it is essential to pursue additional mechanistic and interventional studies to clarify how the microbiome may enhance therapeutic outcomes in lymphoma treatment [128].

Studies in mice have indicated that supplementation with prebiotics like galacto-oligosaccharides and the probiotic Bifidobacterium longum effectively upregulates CD44 gene expression and protects against trans-epidermal water loss as well as ultraviolet-induced erythema [129].

The investigation aimed to assess the effects of ketogenic diets on malignant gliomas in animal models. Research using balanced dietary formulations demonstrated enhanced outcomes, and the therapeutic benefit of high-fat ketogenic diets is linked to the Warburg effect, which leverages cancer cells’ reliance on carbohydrates [130].

Research has shown that diets high in fat and predominantly composed of animal-based products can promote the proliferation of Bilophila wadsworthia, a bacterium that produces hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), a compound implicated in inducing inflammation within intestinal tissues [131, 132].

Recent evidence highlights the critical role of the microbiome in modulating immune responses and influencing cancer progression and therapeutic outcomes. Animal models have been particularly valuable in elucidating host–microbiota interactions and their impact on antitumor immunity. Studies using germ-free animals and microbiota manipulation strategies have demonstrated that microbial composition can alter immune activation, inflammation, and responsiveness to immunotherapy. Nevertheless, substantial differences between animal and human microbiomes complicate direct translation of these findings. Future research integrating microbiome analysis with immunological and metabolic profiling may help clarify these complex interactions and guide the development of microbiome-targeted therapeutic strategies.

Animal models have shed light on translating basic immunology to the modern cancer immunotherapies, which have helped to recognize immune regulatory pathways and maintain the preclinical testing of checkpoint inhibitors, adoptive cell therapies, and combination treatments. Meanwhile, their clinical predictive value is limited by differences between species in immune system structure, tumor diversity, and microenvironment complexity. To conquer these challenges, more harmonious y and human-relevant models are needed to improve the precision of preclinical immuno-oncology studies.

Diagnostics in experimental cancer models

Deletion of focal adhesion kinase (FAK), an essential intracellular mediator of extracellular matrix (ECM) signaling, in fibroblast-specific protein-1 (FSP-1)–positive cells has been shown to elevate chemokine production, which consequently promotes the progression of breast and pancreatic tumors in murine models [133].

Animal studies have provided valuable insights that support informed decision-making and the development of appropriate experimental approaches. Consequently, animal models have been widely utilized to investigate various biological and pathological processes. For instance, a study proposed that traumatic oral ulcers may act as promoting factors for oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) in the presence of other carcinogenic agents, thereby increasing the risk of malignant transformation [134]. A meta-analysis reported that dentures may be associated with an increased risk of cancer development, particularly when chronic irritation and mucosal trauma are present. In related experimental investigations, the transfer of knockout anaerobic melanoma cells into immunocompetent mice resulted in the development of highly aggressive tumors characterized by reduced immunogenicity, including decreased expression of MHC class I molecules and lower levels of PD-L1 expression. These findings suggest that alterations in tumor immunological markers may contribute to enhanced tumor aggressiveness and immune evasion [135]. Scientists have also shown, using animal models, that therapeutic responses are strongly influenced by differences in histopathological profiles, the extent and composition of lymphocytic infiltration, and patterns of cytokine secretion. Notably, treatment with anti-PD-1 monotherapy or combination regimens was associated with distinct immune signatures, including variations in CD8⁺CD103⁺ T-cell infiltration and IL-6 levels, highlighting key determinants of treatment responsiveness.

Furthermore, in an experimental animal model, monoclonal antibody blockade of both CTLA-4 and the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway was shown to induce diabetes, while CTLA-4 blockade alone triggered diabetes only when administered early in life. These findings illustrate the differential immune-related adverse effects associated with various immune checkpoint inhibition strategies [136].

Activation of CB2 receptors exhibits notable antifibrotic effects, demonstrated in murine models of scleroderma as well as rat and mouse models of lung fibrosis. Findings suggest that CB2 receptor signaling may play a protective role by attenuating fibrotic progression across multiple tissues.

A separate study demonstrated that elevated levels of hippocampal TNF-α and IL-6 were strongly correlated with dendritic spine loss and with the severity of cognitive impairment observed in treated mice. These results highlight the contribution of neuroinflammatory cytokines to structural synaptic deterioration and cognitive dysfunction [137].

Biomarkers have been proposed as potential diagnostic tools, as they represent protein markers of pancreatic cancer–initiating cells (PCICs), including CD44v6, Tspan8, EpCAM, MET, and CD104, and show promising potential for the early detection of pancreatic cancer [138]. ADAM10 is overexpressed in colon cancer cells and is responsible for the cleavage of L1-CAM, a neuronal cell adhesion molecule that is frequently detected in various cancers. Experimental studies have shown that HCT116 cells transfected with ADAM10, when injected into animal models, exhibit enhanced metastatic potential and readily metastasize to the liver [139].

Mouse embryonic stem cells, human embryonic stem cells, and mouse embryonic fibroblasts have been shown to express midkine (MK), a growth factor that promotes cellular proliferation and supports self-renewal capacity [140].

MMP-9 has been identified as a key contributor to breast cancer progression and metastasis. In experimental animal models, mice treated with a non-targeted control virus developed pulmonary metastases, whereas mice in which MMP-9 expression was silenced showed no evidence of metastatic spread [141].

In mice, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) are commonly identified by the expression of cell-surface markers recognized by Gr-1 and CD11b antibodies. These cells include subsets characterized by macrophage and neutrophil-associated markers such as Ly6C and Ly6G. Functionally, MDSCs suppress both innate and adaptive immune responses, thereby facilitating tumor immune evasion. They have been shown to inhibit IL-2 production by anti-CD3-activated intratumoral T cells and have been detected in a wide variety of cancers [133, 142].

The molecular mechanisms underlying cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (CKIs) and their signaling pathways remain incompletely understood. Studies using knockout mouse models have demonstrated that proteins of the INK4 family exert distinct and context-dependent roles in regulating the cell cycle. However, genetic alterations such as deletions, mutations, rearrangements, and amplifications in these genes have not been reported as major events in the initiation of most cancers [143]. The development of CKI-deficient mouse models has significantly improved the understanding of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (CKIs) as tumor suppressors. The INK4a/ARF locus on chromosome 9p21 is frequently associated with various human malignancies. Mouse models with deletion of INK4a/ARF, or ARF alone, have demonstrated the critical role of this locus in suppressing carcinogenesis. In particular, single-gene deletion of ARF in mice has been associated with the development of angiosarcomas [144].

Knockdown of the p18Ink4c gene in mice has been shown to induce several malignancies, including thymic lymphoma, renal cell carcinoma, pheochromocytoma, B-cell lymphoma, and angiosarcoma. Deletion of ARF alone highlights the role of p19ARF as a critical regulator of cellular proliferation, as ARF-deficient mice develop a similar spectrum of tumors. Likewise, INK4a/ARF-null mice exhibit overlapping tumor phenotypes. In experimental models, p27Kip1-deficient mice develop tumors in multiple organs accompanied by developmental abnormalities, while p57Kip2 knockout mice show the development of prostatic adenocarcinomas. Notably, combined knockout models tend to develop tumors more rapidly than single-gene knockout animals.

Furthermore, the gut microbiota has been shown to influence host homeostasis, including the regulation of amino acid metabolism. Comparative studies between germ-free mice and conventionalized mice have demonstrated differences in amino acid profiles, with altered distribution of amino acids observed in the gastrointestinal tract of conventionalized animals [145].

Commensal microbiota has been shown to influence the physiological functions of multiple organs beyond the gastrointestinal tract. Evidence suggests that bone marrow hematopoiesis is closely associated with the presence of microbiota. Studies investigating hematopoiesis under steady-state conditions have demonstrated that germ-free (GF) mice or mice treated with antibiotics to deplete gut microbiota exhibit significant alterations in hematopoietic processes. These conditions lead to reduced myelopoiesis and consequently lower numbers of myeloid lineage cells in the bone marrow, peripheral blood, and spleen. However, the influence of microbiota on lymphopoiesis remains controversial. Current evidence suggests that microbiota may regulate hematopoiesis primarily by modulating early stages of hematopoietic development, highlighting the importance of host–microbiota interactions in maintaining effective hematopoietic function [146].

Bregs and Tregs have been suggested as potential therapeutic targets in cancer and have been associated with improved short-term survival in non-metastatic breast cancer. In experimental mouse models deficient in T and B cells, administration of Bregs alone was shown to reduce metastatic development through a transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) dependent pathway. This effect was mediated by the induction and expansion of CD4⁺ T cells, resulting in mice remaining free of metastasis via a TGF-β–dependent mechanism [147].

Challenges and future perspective

The structural and functional similarities between certain human and murine T-cell populations have enabled researchers to investigate key mechanisms involved in T-cell–mediated immune pathways using animal models. Insights obtained from these studies have contributed significantly to understanding the role of T cells in immune regulation and have supported the development of T-cell–targeted immunomodulatory therapies. Nevertheless, further investigation of human T-cell–based immunity remains essential to optimize immunotherapeutic drug design and clinical applications.

Among these populations, non-conventional T cells represent a unique group capable of recognizing a broad spectrum of antigens independently of MHC molecules. These cells play an important role in facilitating immune responses by functioning at the interface between innate and adaptive immunity. Both in humans and mice, non-conventional T cells are subdivided into several distinct subsets that are distributed across different tissues according to their specific immunological roles.

Experimental studies using PD-1–deficient mice have also provided important insights into immune checkpoint regulation. These models demonstrated that loss of PD-1 signaling may lead to immune dysregulation and autoimmunity, highlighting the critical role of PD-1 in maintaining immune tolerance while suppressing T-cell–mediated lymphoma development in peripheral tissues. Similarly, animal models investigating immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) induced hypophysis’s have contributed valuable information regarding the mechanisms underlying immune-mediated endocrine toxicity.

Despite their value, animal models cannot fully reproduce the complexity of human clinical conditions, particularly the extensive interindividual variability observed among patients. In contrast, many animal experiments rely on genetically homogeneous inbred strains, which may limit the translational relevance of the findings. Furthermore, lifestyle and metabolic factors such as diet composition may be related to tumor progression in experimental models. For instance, mice maintained on low-fat diets exhibit longer survival compared with those receiving high-fat diets, which often develop a higher tumor burden. Similarly, studies have reported a relationship between dietary fat intake and the progression of prostate tumors, with high-fat diets accelerating disease progression compared with low-fat dietary regimens. Additionally, biological sex differences may influence metabolic pathways, protein expression profiles, and therapeutic responses, further complicating the interpretation of experimental results.

Animal studies have also contributed to the investigation of novel pharmacological targets, including the role of the endocannabinoid system. Experimental findings suggest that cannabinoid agonists and anandamide can act on presynaptic CB1 receptors in the area postrema, suppressing serotonin release and thereby influencing nausea pathways. In addition, these compounds may function as allosteric inhibitors of 5-HT3 serotonergic receptors, indicating a potential therapeutic strategy for managing treatment-related symptoms. Moreover, evidence suggests that opioids may modulate immune responses in cancer patients, although the precise mechanisms and clinical implications remain under investigation.

Although animal experimentation has played a fundamental role in biomedical discovery and drug development, several ethical, methodological, and practical challenges remain. Some experimental endpoints in animal research, particularly those involving severe pathological progression, raise ethical concerns and may increasingly be replaced by refined laboratory-based biomarkers and non-invasive testing strategies. Additionally, maintaining animal welfare, including factors related to quality of life, housing conditions, environmental parameters such as temperature, light exposure, cage size, breeding practices, and diet, is essential for ensuring reliable and ethically responsible research outcomes. Prolonged exposure to animal experimentation may also affect researchers' mental health, an often-overlooked aspect of laboratory work. Also, new technology incorporating organoids, nanocarriers, exosomes and cell-free treatments that modulate immune responses as well as rigorous computational approaches could be investigated to develop a more robust framework for the treatment of various cancers [148–151].

Due to these limitations and ethical considerations, increasing efforts are being directed toward the development of alternative experimental platforms that may reduce or partially replace animal use in biomedical research. Emerging technologies such as three-dimensional spheroid cultures, organoids, organ-on-a-chip systems, 3D bioprinting, cytokine-based therapies, and adoptive cell transfer approaches offer promising avenues for studying tumor biology and immune responses in more physiologically relevant environments.

In the field of cancer immunotherapy, animal experiments have generated valuable insights into the immunomodulatory effects of several pharmacological agents, including cannabinoids and opioids. However, while these findings provide important mechanistic information, clinical studies in human patients remain essential to validate these observations and determine their relevance for therapeutic practice.

Conclusion

In immuno-oncology studies, preclinical studies have played a role in a wide range of variety of animal models, such as chemically induced tumors and genetically engineered mouse models. These models are designed to resemble key structures of human cancer. In addition, improvements in genome-editing technologies have further enabled the development of more refined and controllable systems, thereby influencing progression, immune communications, and tumor initiation. Despite this progress, translating and applying these findings to animal models has faced challenges, raising persistent concerns about their predictive accuracy. The majority of limitations and gaps of this approach are the presence of fundamental interspecies biological differences, specifically, in immune system organization and function, and efficacy. In this regard, differentiations in the substantially T-cell receptor collection and diversity, antigen recognition, and the magnitude of antitumor immune responses in mice and humans are undeniable. In the myeloid compartment study, these disparities have been reported, including macrophages, dendritic cells, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, as well as in cytokine signaling pathways and immune checkpoint regulation. Furthermore, the limited capacity in many experimental models makes it challenging to reproduce the extensive immune heterogeneity reported in human tumors. Human malignancies are featured in highly diverse tumor microenvironments shaped by variable immune infiltration, stromal interactions, metabolic states, and microbiome influences. The importance of gut-brain axis is extended to other disorders beyond cancer which may deserve attention as cancers influence a wide range of body systems [152]. However, conventional preclinical systems such as syngeneic and xenograft models often generate simplified or artificial immune contexts that fail to account for this diversity and complexity. Due to these biological inconsistencies, immunotherapeutic agents frequently demonstrate greater efficacy in animal studies than in clinical trials. In preclinical models, treatments that stimulate robust antitumor responses often fail to gain comparable benefits in patients, as a result of alterations in immune cell trafficking, checkpoint signaling, and tumor antigen presentation across species. Alterations of traditional animal models, the animal-free experimental platforms gained more attention. Therefore, such approaches, such as tumor organoids and organ-on-chip technologies, particularly integrated with genome-editing strategies, offer promising opportunities to recap key structural and functional sides of human tissues and tumor–immune connections, which provide complementary, ethically promising tools toward investigating tumor biology, immune dynamics, and therapeutic responses. However, animal models remain a vital component of the oncology drug development pipeline, especially for studying systemic interactions among tumors, immune responses, and host physiology that cannot yet be fully recreated in vitro. Therefore, till alternative platforms can fully recognize the dynamic complexity of human cancers, strictly designed and ethically shown animal studies will pave the way in translational immuno-oncology. In this regard, the concentration of future studies should be on systematically defining the assets and limitations of existing models by integrating next-generation experimental systems to enhance the predictive value of preclinical cancer research.
