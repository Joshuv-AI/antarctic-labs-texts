Is the development of personality beliefs associated with levels of childhood traumas and emotional intelligence? A prospective cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Özgür Maden
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPersonality disorders (PD) are associated with personality beliefs (PBs). Childhood traumas (CTs) and emotional intelligence (EI) are effective in the development of PDs. However, the relationships between CTs and EI and PBs are not well known. This study examined the effects of CTs and EI on PBs.MethodThree hundred and eighty-seven university students who applied to the psychiatry clinic and did not receive any psychiatric diagnosis according to Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) diagnostic criteria were recruited for the study. We applied the Childhood Trauma Scale, BarOn Emotional Intelligence Scale, and Personality Beliefs Questionnaire-Short Form to the volunteers. According to scores from the childhood trauma subscale scores, the participants divided into two groups: those with childhood trauma (CT (+), n = 180) and those without childhood trauma (CT (-), n = 207). The results will be evaluated with the SPSS ver. 22.0 program.ResultsEI and PB levels were higher in CT (+) than in CT (-). We found positive and significant relationships between the participants’ CT, EI, and PB levels. In all participants and also in CT (+), total levels of CTs and EI predicted paranoid, schizoid, antisocial, borderline, narcissistic, histrionic, dependent, avoidant, and passive-aggressive PBs other than obsessive-compulsive PBs. The subtypes of childhood trauma did not predict PBs in CT (+), while the EI-stress management subscale was linked to borderline, histrionic, avoidant, and passive-aggressive PBs, and the adaptability subscale was linked to dependent PBs.ConclusionThis research provides important contributions regarding how CTs and EI affect PBs. Given the significant impact of CT on PBs, early intervention programs should focus on promoting personality belief relationships in children who have experienced trauma. Mental health professionals and psychotherapists should integrate EI-based and emotion-focused interventions when working with individuals with trauma histories. Therapy approaches such as dialectical behavior therapy and trauma-informed cognitive-behavioral therapy may be useful in both improving EI and relational functioning in trauma survivors.Trial registrationThis study is registered on Clinical Trials.gov (ID: 06/05/2023- NCT05847335).Keywords: Childhood traumas, Emotional intelligence, Personality beliefs

Introduction

Personality can be described as the dynamic organization of psychobiological systems that are found in every person and provide adaptation to the inner and outer world specific to the person. The concept of dynamics emphasizes that personality has a structure that constantly changes and adapts to environmental factors [1]. Within the scope of cognitive theory, personality is conceptualized as a stable organization of modes. Modes are patterns of thinking, feeling, and acting that work together in certain situations, helping people meet their basic needs for survival and reproduction by fulfilling their desires and urges. The schemas that constitute the cognitive component of modes consist of beliefs, attitudes, and expectations that determine the strategies to be used by the individual [2]. Otherwise, a personality disorder (PD) is indicated by a stable, unchanging personality organization that differs greatly from the norms of the culture in which the individual lives.

Because of the strong positive or negative personality beliefs (PBs) that shape the schemas linked to PDs and lead to certain emotions and actions, normal modes are triggered, which then create unhealthy personality modes that result in PDs. When a person exhibits over- or underdeveloped behaviors that confirm their pathological beliefs, they meet the cognitive and behavioral criteria for PDs.

The cognitive model of PDs highlights the influence of childhood experiences on the formation of pathological PBs. Individuals who are hypersensitive to rejection, abandonment, and frustration develop negative self-beliefs in adulthood when they experience events that confirm their vulnerabilities in childhood [2]. Since childhood experiences that cause pathological PBs tend to be repeated, the basic pathological beliefs of individuals with PDs are overgeneralized, rigid, and resistant to change [3].

The link between childhood traumas (CTs) and PBs has been remarkably supported by recent research. An adult research study found that the level of CT significantly predicted passive-aggressive, schizoid, paranoid, and borderline PBs [4]. Another study found that CTs were associated with all PBs except obsessive-compulsive PBs [5]. In a study with 393 participants aged 18 to 65, it was found that emotional abuse and controlling behavior from parents during childhood increased the likelihood of avoidant personality behaviors [6].

The ability to make accurate judgments based on emotions and to use emotions and emotional knowledge to develop thinking are two characteristics of emotional intelligence (EI) [7]. Based on the BarOn model, EI is a collection of interconnected social and emotional competencies and talents that affect our capacity to identify and express who we are, comprehend and relate to others, and handle life’s obstacles [8]. Individuals with high EI discover and describe their own and others’ emotions, develop empathy for others, express and control their emotions accurately, can better adapt to challenging situations, cope with environmental factors using feelings, and have the ability to establish positive relationships. Research demonstrates that individuals with high EI exhibit greater resilience to negative childhood experiences in adulthood [9].

Personality is the characteristics that allow a person to become an “individual” by distinguishing them from others. EI is the person’s awareness of their own and others’ emotions during this process and their ability to use their emotions effectively in the interactions that occur [10]. There is an interdependent relationship between personality traits and EI. Studies show that personality traits have an impact on the development of an individual’s EI, and EI impacts the implementation and development of an individual’s personality [11–13]. Peter et al. (2018) identified a significant inverse correlation between borderline PD and EI scores in their research [14]. A recent meta-analysis showed that EI ability explains 28% of the variance shared with personality traits, while EI explains 41% of the variance shared with personality traits [15].

In light of this information, according to the cognitive model, PBs are a reflection of personality traits [2]. Previous studies have suggested that CTs have an impact on personality development [16, 17]. Studies examining the effects of CT on PDs [18–20] and PBs have yielded different results [4, 6]. Investigations looking at the connection between EI levels and PDs [7, 13, 21] and personality traits [22, 23] have also yielded various results. However, the relationship between EI levels and PBs, as well as the interactive effects of CTs and EI levels on PBs, remains unclear. When we evaluate all these factors together, it’s possible that CTs and EI levels influence PBs. This study aimed to examine the relationships among CTs, EI levels, and PBs and to investigate the interactions between these variables in individuals with childhood trauma. In this context, the hypotheses of our study are as follows:

H1: EI levels and PBs differ according to CTs.

H1: EI levels and PBs differ according to CTs.

H2: CTs and EI levels affect PBs.

H2: CTs and EI levels affect PBs.

H3: In those with CT, CTs and EI total levels have an effect on PBs.

H3: In those with CT, CTs and EI total levels have an effect on PBs.

H4: In those with CT, the CT and EI subscales have an effect on PBs.

H4: In those with CT, the CT and EI subscales have an effect on PBs.

Method

This current research was designed as a cross-sectional and correlational study. The study involved 495 university students who applied in succession between July 2017 and June 2018 to the Mental Health and Diseases outpatient clinic of a Training and Research Hospital in Istanbul. G-Power 3.1.9.7 software was used to conduct power analysis to ascertain the bare minimum of participants needed for the analysis of the study data [24]. This software (multiple regression option) determined that at least 295 participants were necessary for the analysis, taking into account seven factors (gender, age, income, department, class, region of residence, and place of residence), with a 5% chance of error, 80% power, and enough statistical strength to look at the relationships between these factors. The study sample was composed of university students who consented to participate and did not have chronic physical and mental illnesses, mental disorders, or alcohol and substance use disorders using the convenience sampling method. Those who did not participate in the study voluntarily, pregnant and breastfeeding women, and those with chronic physical and mental illnesses, alcohol and substance use disorders, and mental disorders were excluded from the study.

Participants’ adherence to the inclusion and exclusion criteria was assessed post-interview by a psychiatrist in accordance with the diagnostic standards of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5). The sociodemographic data form, Childhood Trauma Scale (CTS), BarOn Emotional Intelligence Scale (EIS), and Personality Beliefs Questionnaire-Short Form (PBQ-SF) were administered to the participants who consented to the study. Forty-nine participants who responded “I strongly disagree” to item 88 of the EI scale and 59 participants who filled out the forms incompletely were excluded from the study. Thus, the study included 387 participants. Figure 1 presents the research model.

Research Model

Ethics

All participants were enlightened about the procedures and objectives of the study, and written informed consent was obtained from each. Ethical approval was procured from the İstanbul Haydarpaşa Education and Research Hospital Clinical Research Ethics Committee before the study (Ethical Approval No: 12.06.2017/487). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration. Ethical guidelines determined by institutional and national research organizations were followed during the study.

Data collection tools

Sociodemographic data form

This is a form created by the researcher based on the features of the study. It is used to record the sociodemographic and clinical traits of individuals, such as gender, age, income, department, class, region, and place of residence.

Childhood Trauma Scale (CTS)

This scale, developed by Bernstein et al. in 1994, is a five-point Likert-type scale consisting of 28 questions [25]. It measures five types of CTs: physical abuse (PA), emotional abuse (EA), sexual abuse (SA), emotional neglect (EN), and physical neglect (PN). After reading each question, participants choose a number from 1 (never) to 5 (very often) that best reflects their experience. The scale can help assess individuals with CTs and identify instances of trauma. The validity and reliability study of the scale in Turkish was conducted by Şar et al. (2012) [26]. A cut-off point of 5 points has been suggested for SA and PA, 7 points for PN and EA, and 12 points for EN [26], and these cut-off points were used in this study. In the original study, Cronbach’s alpha values ranged from 0.79 to 0.94. The current study revealed that the sub-dimensions had Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficients ranging from 0.64 to 0.87.

Personality beliefs Questionnaire-Short form (PBQ-SF)

The scale, created by Beck and others in 1991 for diagnosing certain PDs, includes statements designed to find out what a person believes about themselves, other people, and the world, based on psychological theory and clinical observations [27]. Every item on the scale pertains to attitudes and beliefs associated with the paranoid, schizoid, antisocial, borderline, narcissistic, histrionic, dependent, obsessive-compulsive, avoidant, and passive-aggressive PDs as delineated in the DSM. The scale consists of 65 items and is a five-point Likert-type scale. The borderline PD subscale, which was not included in previous studies, was added to the scale by creating common items derived from other subscales [3]. The abbreviated version of the scale was created by Butler et al. (2007) [28]. Participants evaluate each statement by indicating its relevance to them from zero (I do not believe at all) to four (I completely believe). The scale is applicable in both the evaluation and therapy of individuals with PDs. Taymur et al. (2011) conducted the Turkish validity and reliability study for the short Turkish form of the scale, which was created by selecting questions from the Turkish long form, as in the original short scale [29]. The test assessment omitted items 64 and 65 and lacked a cut-off threshold. The test is scored by adding the weighted score of the participant’s responses to each subtest. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the short form of the scale with the borderline PD subscale was conducted by Bilge and Bilge (2019) [30]. While the total Cronbach reliability coefficient of the scale was 0.92, the reliability coefficients of the subscales ranged from 0.61 to 0.85. The test-retest study revealed correlation coefficients ranging from 0.66 to 0.87.

Emotional Intelligence Scale (EIS)

The scale developed by Bar-On (1997) was adapted to Turkish by Acar (2001) [31, 32]. This scale is a five-point Likert-type scale that includes 87 items. The ratings are as follows: 1: I completely agree, 2: I agree, 3: I am undecided, 4: I disagree, and 5: I completely disagree. The questionnaires of those who answered “I strongly disagree” to the statement “I answered the above statements sincerely and accurately” in the 88th item of the scale are not evaluated. The scale assesses five sub-dimensions of EI: personal skills, interpersonal skills, adaptability, stress management, and general mood. Personal skills refer to the individual’s ability to understand and be aware of their feelings and thoughts; interpersonal skills refer to the ability to understand and be mindful of others’ feelings and emotions; adaptation refers to the ability to be flexible and change their emotions according to changing conditions; stress management refers to the individual’s ability to cope with stress and control their emotions; and general mood refers to the ability to express, feel, and be optimistic about positive emotions. Elevated scores derived from the overall scale and its subdimensions indicate an elevated level of EI. The total Cronbach reliability coefficient of the scale was 0.92.

Determination of childhood trauma survivors

Participants were evaluated according to their CTS subscale scores. It is suggested that giving 5 points to PA and SA, 7 points to PN and EA, and 12 points to EN can be considered as positive reports of CT [26]. Accordingly, those with at least one subscale score above the specified threshold values ​​were determined as those who experienced childhood trauma (CT (+)), and those with each subscale score below the specified values ​​were determined as those who did not experience childhood trauma (CT (-)). According to these evaluations, participants were divided into two groups: those who experienced childhood trauma (CT (+), n = 180) and those who did not experience childhood trauma (CT (-), n = 207).

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS (ver. 22.0, Chicago, IL, USA) software. The obtained data were moved to the computer environment. Number, percentage, mean value, and standard deviation were used to describe the data. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to examine whether the data showed normal distribution, and with the help of this, it was chosen to analyze the scale scores using parametric tests due to their normal distribution. The independent sample t-test was used to compare the scale scores of the sample group, while the associations between the scale scores were analyzed using the Pearson correlation coefficient. Multiple regression analysis was performed to determine the predictive power of the relationships among the total and sub-dimensions of childhood traumas (CTs), emotional intelligence (EI), and personality behaviors (PBs). In statistical interpretations, p < 0.05 values ​​were accepted as significant at the 95% confidence interval.

Discussion

This study examined the effects of CTs and EI levels on PBs. EI levels and PBs were compared according to CT status. At the same time, it evaluated the effects of CTs and EI levels on each PB. According to our literature knowledge, this study was the first study to evaluate the effects of CTs and EI levels on PBs.

One of the results of this study was that, with the exception of obsessive-compulsive personality beliefs, levels of paranoid, schizoid, antisocial, borderline, narcissistic, histrionic, dependent, avoidant, and passive-aggressive personality beliefs were higher in CT (+) than in CT (-). Recent studies indicate that CTs are linked to paranoid, schizoid, borderline, avoidant, and passive-aggressive PBs [4–6]. CTs negatively affect personality development [15, 16] and cause the development of PDs in adulthood [18, 20]. According to cognitive theory, the more a pathological belief cluster is adopted, the more likely it is to be seen in that PD and related behaviors [33]. These findings emphasize that CTs may be effective in the development of PBs.

Another result of this study is that EI levels are higher in CT (+) than in CT (-). A study involving university students revealed that individuals without unpleasant childhood experiences exhibited elevated levels of emotional intelligence (EI) [34]. In contrast to these results, a study conducted in India found that those who experienced severe CT had higher levels of EI and empathy than those who experienced low or moderate levels of CT [11]. These results of the study explain that childhood traumatic experiences have a positive effect on the development of EI.

Negative significant relationships were found between the ages of the participants and schizoid, antisocial, narcissistic, histrionic, and passive-aggressive PBs. In a study conducted on a group of employees and their families in a public institution, significant differences were found between the age groups with avoidant, obsessive-compulsive, histrionic, schizoid, and paranoid PBs [35]. PDs are more common in young adults compared to older adults, whereas medical problems, cognitive decline, and changes in behavioral patterns over time in older adults affect the diagnostic accuracy of PDs [36, 37]. These results are an indication that there may be changes in PBs with increasing age.

Childhood traumas and EI levels were found to have positive significant relationships with PBs. In a study conducted with 409 participants, it was reported that SA was linked to paranoid, schizoid, borderline, and avoidant PDs; PA was associated with antisocial PD; EA was associated with paranoid, schizotypal, borderline, and cluster C PDs; and EN was associated with histrionic and borderline PDs [18]. It can be stated that CTs and EI levels have a positive effect on the development of PBs related to PDs.

In CT (+), significant relationships were observed between CTs and dependent PBs. Recent research suggests that childhood maltreatment may significantly contribute to the formation of narcissistic personality structures [19] and that childhood SA is the strongest predictor of histrionic personality pathology in adulthood [20]. Similarly, CTs can cause psychological effects such as decreased self-esteem in individuals during adulthood, developing a dependent personality, negative self-evaluation and feeling worthless, disorder in personal relationships, introversion, incompatibility, anxiety, fatigue, and insecurity [38–40]. These traumatic experiences can cause individuals to believe that they will be unsuccessful in adulthood, will not be able to adapt in interpersonal relationships, and therefore will be unhappy.

A substantial and meaningful correlation has been identified between CTs and EI levels. In a study conducted on adults aged 20–30, a negative relationship was found between CTs and EI levels [41]. Süslü (2020) examined the effects of CTs on the EI, Life satisfaction, and interpersonal communication style of individuals aged 20–45 [42]. According to research findings, it is stated that as individuals’ reports of CT increase, their EI scores decrease. Another study conducted with a sample of 227 young female participants from Croatia and Bosnia-Herzegovina showed that individuals who had traumatic experiences in their early lives were more likely to develop borderline PD symptoms and had lower levels of emotion regulation and empathy [43]. These study ’findings contradicted the findings in the literature. These results indicate that the fact that the participants were university students and their coping skills with stressors improved may have been effective in developing positive PBs by overcoming the negative effects of CTs. However, these results need to be confirmed in future studies.

According to the multiple regression results, it was determined that the participants’ global CTs did not predict their PBs. The reasons why CTs do not predict PBs may be that the possible post-traumatic growth that develops with CTs [44, 45] and individual factors may act as a protective factor against personality pathologies and dysfunctional PBs. The trigger required for the development of post-traumatic growth is the perceived threat related to the trauma. In the face of this threat, individuals often change their personal belief systems and the meaning of the trauma to fit their new or restructured personal views and assumptions about life and the world [46]. According to the post-traumatic growth model of McElherance et al. (2012), it has been stated that children exposed to SA may exhibit post-traumatic growth [47]. In addition, it has been stated that girls exposed to SA may be more prone to post-traumatic growth. In line with these findings, these protective factors may prevent the emergence of PBs by reducing the development of PDs.

Another finding of the study is that total EI levels predict PBs. A study conducted with university students indicated that as EI levels increase, the personality traits of extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, and openness to experience increase, while neuroticism levels decrease [48]. Another study conducted with individuals with psychopathic personality traits indicated that high psychopathic levels are associated with low EI levels [49]. Individuals with personality problems tend to have low EI levels, which may lead to the development of negative personality traits. On the contrary, it is explained that PBs develop positively in individuals with high EI levels. Moreover, individuals who are aware of their emotions can control their behaviors and have positive thoughts and beliefs.

In CT (+), EI levels predicted PBs other than schizoid and obsessive-compulsive personality traits. However, CTs did not predict PBs. The reason for this is that there is no relationship between CTs and PBs in CT (+). Schizoid individuals have a limited ability to feel, distinguish, and show their emotions. They experience feelings of loneliness due to loss of emotion; obsessive-compulsive individuals are unemotional, critical, and judgmental in their interpersonal relationships [50]. It is obvious that EI characteristics are not sufficiently developed in individuals with schizoid and obsessive-compulsive personality traits and that they are not aware of their own or others’ emotions. Individuals with paranoid, antisocial, borderline, histrionic, dependent, avoidant, and passive-aggressive personality traits may differ from individuals with schizoid and obsessive-compulsive personality traits in terms of their EI levels, as they are aware of both their own and others’ emotions, whether positive or negative. Having experienced childhood trauma may cause these emotions to be revealed more, whether positive or negative, and the development of these PBs. However, there is a need for relational studies on this subject.

Another result of our study is that in CT (+), the EI subscales of stress management predict borderline, histrionic, avoidant, and passive-aggressive PBs, while adaptability predicts dependent PBs. The two main characteristics of borderline PD are instability/inconsistency and impulsivity. Sudden mood swings or violent outbursts can be seen in these individuals with histrionic PD, which is characterized by excessive emotionality, attention-seeking, seductive or provocative behaviors, sudden and shallow emotions, and using appearance to attract attention [51]. They have variable emotions, tend to get angry easily, and have difficulty concentrating [52]. Individuals with avoidant PD have negative beliefs about themselves, others, and unwanted emotional experiences. These individuals also see their dysphoric emotions as unbearable and avoid situations and environments that make them uncomfortable, as well as thinking about things that make them uncomfortable [2]. Individuals with passive-aggressive PBs hold external factors and other people responsible for the events that happen to them due to their aggressive feelings [53].

Emotional intelligence consists of stress management, stress tolerance, and impulse control skills. In people who have experienced childhood trauma, high levels of stress management can positively affect borderline and histrionic personality beliefs through impulsivity control and avoidant and passive-aggressive personality beliefs through stress tolerance.

Emotional intelligence adaptability encompasses the capacity to acknowledge one’s emotions, acclimate to one’s feelings, ideas, and behaviors in varying circumstances, and recognize issues while devising effective remedies. Individuals with dependent PBs need others intensely for their lives. These individuals have overdeveloped help-seeking, trusting others, and people-pleasing behaviors, while self-expression, self-confidence, and healthy separation behaviors are underdeveloped [2]. A high level of EI adaptability may affect the beliefs of individuals with dependent personality traits about having their feelings validated by others, exhibiting people-pleasing behaviors, trusting others rather than themselves, and being accepted by them.

This research possesses considerable limitations. First, this research evaluates the CTs of university students. It is not known whether the participants had other traumatic experiences such as natural disasters, serious illnesses, accidents, etc. In addition, researchers do not know when the participants’ childhood traumas first occurred or whether the traumatic events are still ongoing, and the time factor may also affect the study’s results. Future research can include studies on childhood traumatic experiences and time factors of other traumatic experiences to generalize the findings. Secondly, we designed this study using a cross-sectional design. It does not compare the dynamic change trajectories of CT, EI levels, and PBs of university students. Third, only one assessment method (i.e., PBQ-SF) was used to assess PBs. This scale was designed to address dysfunctional beliefs associated with a range of PDs. This method does not assess dysfunctional beliefs related to schizotypal PD. We should encourage future researchers to employ multifaceted approaches to PBs, such as self-report, semistructured diagnostic interviews, and clinical interviews. In addition, methods to assess schizotypal PBs should be developed, and studies should be conducted within these methods. Fourth, nonclinical participants were university-based, and the majority were male students. The results may not be representative of the larger population. Future research should assess whether these findings can be replicated in a clinical setting and ensure a balanced distribution of participants in terms of gender. Finally, there may be a bias in the compilation of the questionnaire used to assess early trauma. Given these limitations, we need to conduct future studies using a longitudinal, multi-method design to examine the effects of CT exposure on both EI and PBs. Additionally, it would be important to analyze different groups to see if there are variations in the types of CT experienced and how acceptable they are, as well as to investigate the impact of delayed trauma and the development of EI in future studies.

This research possesses multiple strengths. To our knowledge, this research was the first to evaluate the effects of CTs and EI levels on PBs. This enriched the studies in the field of psychological trauma. It showed that EI levels have a positive effect on PBs in CT (+). This study provided several clinical implications for clinical psychological intervention. For those working on trauma, changing the individual cognitions of clients and developing EI levels to help individuals evaluate the effects of their traumatic experiences may be beneficial in psychotherapeutic interventions targeting dysfunctional PBs. The fact that this study was not conducted on a clinical sample can also be considered a strength of the study. The evaluation of different personality pathologies in a non-clinical sample and the dimensional nature of personality disorders increase the generalizability of the study results [54].

Conclusion

The findings obtained in this study show that there is a significant association between CTs and EI levels in individuals. CTs and EI affect the PBs of university students. At the same time, the EI of CT (+) affects their PBs. Contrary to expectations, CTs are positively associated with EI and PBs. In other words, CTs increase EI levels and enhance the development of positive PBs.

The results of this study hold significant significance for mental health practitioners, educators, and policymakers. Given the significant impact of CT on PBs, early intervention programs should focus on promoting personality belief relationships in children who have experienced trauma. Schools and community organizations can combine EI training programs to help young adults develop better emotional regulation skills, which can reduce the long-term effects of CTs. Additionally, mental health professionals should integrate EI-based and emotion-focused interventions when working with individuals with trauma histories. Therapy approaches such as dialectical behavior therapy and trauma-informed cognitive-behavioral therapy may be useful in both improving EI and relational functioning in trauma survivors.

The research results are valid for students who apply to the clinic within the scope of the research. Different results may be obtained in other samples and clinical studies to be selected. However, it was evaluated that this study is important because it statistically explains the relationships between CTs, EI, and PBs within a model and that it may offer certain perspectives to future researchers working in this field.

Although this study did not examine gender differences, future research could investigate how CTs affect EI and PBs differently between men and women. Longitudinal studies could provide more information about the long-term effects of CTs on emotional and relational outcomes at different life stages.

In conclusion, this study provides valuable contributions to the understanding of how childhood trauma and emotional intelligence affect personality beliefs. The findings emphasize that although childhood trauma significantly affects emotional intelligence, the effects of childhood trauma types are only minimal on dependent personality beliefs. This suggests that emotional intelligence plays a vital role in emotional well-being and that personality beliefs can be improved through targeted interventions.
