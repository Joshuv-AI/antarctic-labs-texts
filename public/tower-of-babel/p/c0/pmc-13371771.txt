Clostridioides difficile Infection–Associated Functional Bowel Disorder: A Three-Year Cohort Study
Authors: Garrison P Wright, Nina Gu, Natalie V S Pham, Marieke K Jones, Deiziane V S Costa, Jae H Shin, Brian W Behm, Cirle A Warren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecurrent diarrhea after an episode of Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is common although not always secondary to recurrent disease. Bacterial and viral gastroenteritis has been associated with postinfectious functional bowel disorders (FBDs). We aimed to compare characteristics of patients diagnosed with CDI-associated FBD with those of patients diagnosed with initial or recurrent CDI alone.MethodsThis was a retrospective review of the electronic medical records of patients referred to the Complicated C. difficile Clinic at University of Virginia Health from March 2020 to January 2023. The characteristics of the patients we studied included demographics, risk factors, and outcomes.ResultsOf 159 patients seen at the clinic, 44 (27.7%) referred for recurrent CDI had symptoms of FBD; 90 (56.6%) had symptoms of CDI alone. Compared with patients with CDI only, patients with CDI-associated FBD were younger (P = .001), more likely to be female (P = .029), had fewer comorbidities (P < .001), and more frequently had a history of anxiety (P < .001) and depression (P = .003). In a multiple regression model, FBD remained associated with fewer comorbidities (P = .032). At 3 months, patients with FBD were less frequently well (P = .039) and more frequently continued to have gastrointestinal symptoms (P < .001) than patients with CDI alone.ConclusionsCDI-associated FBD is prevalent but may be misdiagnosed as solely CDI recurrence, contributing to incomplete resolution of symptoms despite anti–C. difficile treatments.

Clostridioides difficile is the most common infectious cause of antibiotic-associated diarrhea, as well as the most common cause of health care–associated infections in the United States [1–3]. While appropriate antibiotic treatment may lead to resolution of C. difficile infection (CDI), it may also cause further disruption of the gut microbiota, leading to increased susceptibility to CDI recurrence. Symptomatic recurrence after resolution of the initial CDI occurs in ∼20%–30% of patients, and higher rates are noted in those who have had at least 1 previous CDI [4, 5].

Postinfectious irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a known sequela of acute gastroenteritis associated with certain pathogens including E. coli, C. jejuni, and C. difficile [6]. Diagnosis can be made once underlying organic disease has been ruled out. Like CDI, IBS imposes significant burdens on the health care system. The prevalence of IBS in the United States is between 7% and 16%, with annual direct costs estimated at >$1 billion [7], while the prevalence of postinfectious IBS has an incidence of ∼14.5% in patients with infectious enteritis [8]. IBS symptoms include chronic abdominal pain and altered bowel habits, and subcategories are diarrhea (IBS-D), constipation (IBS-C), mixed diarrhea and constipation, and unclassified [9–11]. IBS is the most prevalent subcategory of functional bowel disorders (FBDs), which also includes functional constipation and functional diarrhea [9–11]. FBD, in turn, is a subgroup of disorders of the gut–brain interaction (DGBI) within the Rome IV classification system [9–11].

Standard treatment for recurrent CDI may consist of either additional C. difficile–active antibiotics or fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) [1]; however, administration of antibiotics to a patient with CDI-associated FBD may worsen their gut dysbiosis and gastrointestinal symptoms [12, 13]. Appropriate management of FBD includes lifestyle and dietary modifications, antispasmodic, antidiarrheal agents for FBD with predominant diarrhea, and laxatives for FBD with predominant constipation symptoms [7]. In this study, we determined the frequency of CDI-associated FBD in patients seen at our Complicated C. difficile Clinic and compared the demographics, risk factors, and outcomes in these patients with those of patients diagnosed with recurrent CDI alone.

METHODS

Study Description

This retrospective study examined the electronic medical record of consecutive unique patients referred to the Complicated C. difficile Clinic (CCDC) at the University of Virginia from March 2020 to January 2023. The CCDC was staffed by 2 infectious disease physicians who ensured optimal medical management and a gastroenterologist who performed FMT, as indicated, in referred patients.

Ethics Approval

The research protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Virginia (IRB-HSR#23045, Fecal Microbiota Transplantation). This study met the criteria for an exempt determination per 45CFR46.104(d)4(iii). Secondary research for which consent is not required: secondary research uses of identifiable private information or identifiable biospecimens, and (iii) the research involves only information collection and analysis involving the investigator's use of identifiable health information when that use is regulated under 45CFR parts 160 and 164, subparts A and E, for the purposes of “health care operations” or “research” as those terms are defined in 45CFR164.501 or for “public health activities and purposes” as described under45CFR164.512(b). Consent was not required as no identifiable HPI is being published. An appropriate Study-Data Security Plan is in place. The study adheres to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data Collection

The EPIC electronic medical record system (a software application that allows health care providers to store, update, and view patients’ medical records) was accessed to pull out the list of patients seen at the CCDC. For each patient identified, a chart review in EPIC was performed to collect patient demographics, histories, clinical presentation, physical exam findings, laboratory findings, imaging findings, medical diagnoses, and treatment outcomes. All data extracted were coded in RedCap for storage and statistical analyses.

Case Definition

Patients diagnosed at CCDC with initial or recurrent CDI have active symptoms including ≥3 liquid stools per day, positive tests for C. difficile toxin gene (NAAT) and/or antigen (enzyme immunoassay), and improvement of symptoms with use of anti–C. difficile antibiotics (metronidazole, vancomycin, or fidaxomicin). Patients referred from other institutions were diagnosed using their respective facility microbiologic assay and had documented treatment for CDI, with treatment response ascertained at CCDC. Suspicion of CDI-associated FBD was based on the presence of 1 or more of the following: (1) persistent gastrointestinal symptoms including bowel movement irregularities (diarrhea, constipation, or mixed), abdominal pain or discomfort (increased sensation or perception of intestinal motility), abdominal bloating or distention; (2) symptoms occurring upon exposure to specific triggers, including particular foods or stressors; (3) postprandial diarrhea, bloating, gaseousness, or abdominal discomfort; (4) abdominal pain or relief of pain with defecation; (5) temporal pattern of loose bowel movement (mostly occurring in the morning or afternoon). In addition, there was no other evidence for acute infection including fever, leukocytosis, colitis by abdominal imaging; and no other causes of inflammatory intestinal conditions including enteric infections or inflammatory bowel disease. A retrospective review of the symptoms was based on Rome IV diagnostic criteria for IBS, defined by recurrent abdominal pain, on average at least 1 day per week in the last 3 months, associated with 2 or more of the following: (1) related to defecation, (2) associated with a change in frequency of stool, (3) associated with a change in form (appearance) of stool [9]. Persistent diarrhea not fulfilling Rome IV criteria for IBS was described as “functional diarrhea.”

Statistical Analysis

The statistical methods of this study were performed by Marieke Jones, PhD, staff biostatistician at the University of Virginia School of Medicine. Data were analyzed using R (version 4.3.3). Comparisons were drawn between 2 patient groups: CDI only (those with first episode or recurrent CDI with or without other diagnoses, excluding IBS and functional diarrhea) and any FBD (inclusive of IBS with diarrhea, constipation, or mixed type; or functional diarrhea, with or without other diagnoses, including recent or past history of CDI). Statistical analyses included the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, Pearson chi-square test, and Fisher exact test. A multiple logistic regression model was run including variables with P < .1 in univariate statistics. P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Of the patients with FBD, 17 (39%) presented to the clinic with preexisting diagnoses of FBD, while 27 (61%) were diagnosed with it for the first time at CCDC. The remaining 26 patients (16.7%) did not fit neatly into either diagnosis category (CDI or FBD) and were excluded from further analysis. Of the 26 excluded patients, 13 (50%) were diagnosed with resolved CDI, 7 (27%) with asymptomatic C. difficile colonization (documentation of stool positive for C. difficile but without diarrhea or any symptoms consistent with infection), 4 with concern for underlying IBD, 2 (8%) with diarrhea secondary to lactose intolerance, and 6 (23%) with other diagnoses.

Patients with CDI-associated FBD were younger than patients with CDI only, with a median age (interquartile range [IQR]) of 58 (49–80) years, compared with 73 (60–81) in the CDI group (P = .001) (Table 3). A higher proportion of patients with CDI-associated FBD were female (82% vs 63%; P = .029) and had a history of anxiety (43% vs 13%; P < .001) and depression (43% vs 19%; P = .003) compared with patients with CDI only. Additionally, patients with CDI-associated FBD had fewer medical comorbidities (with a median [IQR] Charlson Comorbidity Index [CCI] of 2 [1.75–4] vs 5 [3–7]; P < .001) and fewer prior recorded episodes of CDI (median episodes [IQR], 3.00 [1.00–3.25] vs 3.00 [2.00–4.00]; P = .048) compared with patients diagnosed with CDI only.

At the first clinic evaluation, FMT was recommended for 21 patients, workup for IBD or other gastrointestinal (GI) conditions for 20, vancomycin tapering regimen for 51, fidaxomicin for 5, and stop all anti-CDI agents for 17 patients. The following medications were recommended, as needed: hyoscyamine, loperamide, and dicyclomine. The rest of the patients received education and a plan of action in case of recurrent diarrhea or another episode of CDI.

Outcomes at Three-Month Follow-up

One hundred eight of 159 patients came back for a 3-month follow-up, including 64 of 90 with CDI and 26 of 44 patients with FBD. Patients diagnosed with FBD were less likely to experience symptom resolution than patients diagnosed with CDI only (8 [31%] vs 35 [55%]; P = .039) and more likely to have continued symptoms of FBD (12 [46%] vs 2 [3%]; P < .001). More patients in the CDI only group (n = 18; 28%) compared with the FBD group (n = 4; 15%) experienced recurrent CDI; however, this difference was not statistically significant (P = .2). Similarly, no differences were found between the any FBD and CDI only groups regarding the proportion of patients who received FMT (3 [12%] vs 9 [14%]; P = .9). Of note, 3 patients with FBD received FMT for developing recurrent CDI within the follow-up period.

DISCUSSION

In this single-center study, we report a high prevalence of CDI-associated FBD. While many of the patients were referred for consideration of FMT for recurrent CDI, we found that more than a quarter (27.7%) of the study population exhibited symptoms more consistent with FBD. The proportion of patients affected by CDI-associated FBD in our clinic appears slightly higher than what was previously reported in the literature (4%–25%) [14–17] for post-CDI IBS and PI-IBS in general (14.5%) [8]. This observation may be secondary to selection of patients with persistent symptoms being referred to the CCDC and inclusion of patients with persistent diarrhea but not fulfilling Rome IV criteria. Given the nature of the specialty clinic, most patients had multiple episodes of recurrent diarrhea, either secondary to CDI or not. Many patients had been treated empirically with antibiotics due to reported diarrhea in the setting of previous diagnoses of CDI, and increased exposure to antibiotics in this cohort may be a contributing factor in the development of FBD [12, 13].

Compared with patients with CDI only, patients with CDI-associated FBD were generally younger in age with fewer medical comorbidities. Additionally, history of anxiety was significantly more common. Patients with CDI-associated FBD were less likely to develop recurrent CDI at 3-month follow-up. These patients also tended to have persistent gastrointestinal symptoms.

Our findings emphasize the importance of differentiating between CDI alone and the presence of FBD. Use of antibiotic therapies appropriate for treatment of CDI (such as vancomycin or fidaxomicin) may precipitate or exacerbate functional diarrhea or predispose a patient to true recurrent CDI due to further alterations of the gut microbiota. Interestingly, although both IBS and CDI are associated with dysbiosis, FMT has unclear benefits in IBS [18]. Concerns persist regarding the safety of FMT, which has been associated with rare gram-negative bacteremia and death [19, 20]. Thus, patients under consideration for FMT must be screened adequately to avoid unnecessary risk [21]. Patients with FBD appear more likely to benefit from supportive treatments such as lifestyle and dietary modifications, antispasmodic, antidiarrheal agents for IBS-D, and laxatives for IBS-C [7].

Limitations of our study include the small sample size and its being a single-center study in a subspecialty clinic, limiting the generalizability of the findings in other settings. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, previous history of IBS and undiagnosed FBD are difficult to ascertain, and thus, whether CDI precipitated the development of or exacerbated an underlying FBD is unclear. FBD may likewise be underdiagnosed as patients may be erroneously categorized as CDI in cases of diarrhea with a positive C. difficile assay secondary to colonization. Documentation of diagnostic testing of patients coming from other facilities before being assessed at CCDC is difficult to ascertain based on referral notes and limited availability of actual laboratory results. Additionally, our study period overlapped with a hold on colonoscopic FMT at medical centers using OpenBiome; therefore, patients with recurrent CDI who might otherwise have been able to benefit from FMT may not have received it. Moreover, 97.7% of the patients referred to the clinic identified as Caucasian, which is not representative of the racial makeup of the overall patient population in the geographic region serviced by the CCDC. This may be indicative of racial disparities in health care access in our area.

In conclusion, CDI-associated FBD appears to be very common (27.7%) in patients referred to our specialty clinic for suspicion of CDI recurrence. CDI-associated FBD was associated with younger age, history of anxiety and depression, fewer medical comorbidities, and lower risk of recurrences than patients with CDI. It is important to ascertain diagnosis in patients with recurrent diarrhea and history of CDI, as CDI-associated FBD may be misdiagnosed as solely CDI recurrence. This misdiagnosis leads to unnecessary additional antibiotic treatment or interventions that may result in incomplete resolution of symptoms or further exacerbation of symptoms while exposing patients to unwarranted risks. Further studies are needed to investigate distinguishing factors for the diagnosis of CDI-associated FBD.
