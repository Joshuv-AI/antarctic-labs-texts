A 35-year-old man with immune thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura presenting with dark urine
Authors: Gurjeet Bhangu, Renee Arreglo, Hayley Merkeley
Published: 2026

A previously healthy 35-year-old man presented with 1 week of fatigue, abdominal discomfort, petechiae, and 1 day of dark urine (Figure 1A). Laboratory investigations revealed severe thrombocytopenia (platelet count 2 [normal 150 to 400] × 109/L), mild anemia (hemoglobin 120 [normal 135 to 170] g/L) with a normal reticulocyte count (71 [normal 25 to 90] × 109/L), normal neutrophils, elevated creatinine (160 [normal 55 to 109] μmol/L), indirect hyperbilirubinemia (total bilirubin 51 μmol/L, indirect 45 [normal 0 to 20] μmol/L), low haptoglobin (0.10 [normal 0.40 to 2.80] g/L), and markedly elevated lactate dehydrogenase (2349 [normal 123 to 255] U/L). A peripheral smear showed 4% to 5% schistocytes, and urinalysis revealed hemoglobinuria without erythrocytes. A urine sample was black, suggesting intravascular hemolysis.

(A) Black urine from a 35-year-old man with immune thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura. (B) Dark brown plasma observed during the initial plasma exchange treatment.

We calculated a PLASMIC (platelets, lysis, active cancer, stem cell or solid organ transplant, mean corpuscular volume, international normalized ratio, creatinine) score of 7, indicating high risk for thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP).1 Thus, we initiated intravenous methylprednisolone (1 g daily for 3 days) and therapeutic plasma exchange within 3 hours of presentation. The discarded plasma appeared dark brown (Figure 1B). After 5 daily exchanges, the patient’s platelet count and urine colour returned to normal. His ADAMTS13 enzyme activity was less than 0.01 (normal > 0.40) IU/mL, and an autoantibody against ADAMTS13 was detected at a concentration of 29.8 (normal < 12.0) U/mL, confirming acquired immune TTP rather than congenital TTP. We discharged him on day 10 with a prednisone taper.

Dark urine may be a presenting symptom of TTP, representing hemoglobinuria from intravascular hemolysis. Hemoglobinuria can also be caused by paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, severe malaria, drug-induced or mechanical hemolysis, and other thrombotic microangiopathies, including Shiga toxin–associated and atypical hemolytic uremic syndromes.1,2 Recognizing hemoglobinuria and its differential diagnosis allows clinicians to rapidly narrow causes of hemolysis and initiate life-saving therapy. Clinicians should suspect TTP in patients with anemia, thrombocytopenia, schistocytes, and hemolytic markers. Clinicians can calculate a PLASMIC score, which is a validated tool that predicts ADAMTS13 deficiency and the need for plasma exchange while awaiting confirmatory ADAMTS13 testing.3 This enzyme is a plasma metalloprotease that cleaves ultra-large von Willebrand factor (VWF) multimers; severe deficiency (< 10% activity) defines TTP.1 Accumulation of uncleaved VWF promotes microvascular thrombosis and end-organ damage, and untreated disease has a mortality of more than 90%.1
