Transcriptomic analysis of three annelid species: looking for markers of positional information
Authors: Sofia E. Platova, Liudmila O. Poliushkevich, Zinaida I. Starunova, Viktor V. Starunov, Elena L. Novikova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPositional information, classically defined through morphogen gradients, underlies the establishment and maintenance of spatial coordinates in developing organisms and remains essential throughout post-embryonic growth and regeneration. Many conserved developmental regulators, including components of the Wnt, FGF, retinoid, caudal, Hox, and ParaHox pathways, continue to function after embryogenesis, supporting posterior growth, segmentation, stem cell activity, and regenerative responses. Studies across metazoans demonstrate that gradients of these factors are maintained in adult tissues and are reorganized after injury, thereby guiding correct pattern restoration. Annelids provide a powerful model for investigating post-embryonic patterning and the maintenance of positional information. They grow by adding segments from a posterior growth zone, exhibit dynamic shifts in segment position throughout life, and show diverse regenerative capacities, offering an opportunity to identify molecular correlates of these differences.ResultsWe generated spatially resolved transcriptomes for three annelid species with distinct regenerative potentials – Pygospio elegans, Platynereis dumerilii, and Arenicola marina. By analyzing differential gene expression across body fragments, we reconstructed expression patterns along the anterior-posterior axis and examined genes involved in organogenesis, growth zone function, and potential positional information.ConclusionThis comparative transcriptomic study shows that spatial gene expression in three annelid species broadly corresponds to major anatomical territories but also reflects deeper developmental origins. The clear transcriptional differences between larval and postlarval segments in P. elegans and P. dumerilii suggest that segment ontogeny, not anatomy alone, contributes to regional molecular identity. The posterior growth zones of these two species exhibit strong activation of Wnt, cad, Hox, and GMP genes, consistent with an active stem-cell–based growth program. In contrast, the posterior region of A. marina lacks these signatures, supporting previous evidence for an inactive growth zone and the absence of posterior regeneration. Gradient-like expression of Hox, ParaHox, and Wnt genes in P. elegans and P. dumerilii indicates that these factors may encode positional information along the body axis and facilitate regeneration. Their truncated expression in A. marina likely reflects functional specialization of the adult body. Overall, our findings highlight conserved molecular systems involved in posterior growth and positional identity, while revealing species-specific modifications associated with reduced regenerative capacity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12671-5.

Background

Positional information governs the formation and maintenance of a coordinate system within a developing organism, directing cell differentiation according to their spatial localization. Classical theory holds that this information is embodied in gradients of secreted signaling molecules, or morphogens, which emanate from defined sources within the organism and trigger concentration-dependent responses in surrounding tissues [1, 2]. In embryos, positional information is defined by evolutionary conserved factors that constitute an integral part of the genetic developmental toolkit [3–5]. One of the key steps of development is the establishment and patterning of the anterior-posterior (AP) body axis, in which a leading role is played by factors such as members of the Wnt signaling pathway [6–9], as well as by caudal [10, 11], retinoids and fibroblast growth factor (FGF) signaling [4, 12].

The majority, if not all, of the factors involved in defining positional information during early embryogenesis continue to function at later embryonic stages and throughout the postembryonic period. They support, among other processes, posterior growth, segmentation, stem cell proliferation, neural specification and activity, and adipogenesis [13–17]. Furthermore, several factors implicated in the establishment of positional information during embryogenesis have been shown to retain their gradient expression patterns during the post-embryonic period. Such a maintenance of positional values along the body axes appears to be a critical mechanism ensuring the restoration of appropriate structures at correct locations during regeneration following injury, i.e. reparative regeneration. Studies in Hydra and planarians have highlighted the importance of Wnt signaling, in which Wnt gradients persist in adult organisms, reorganize in response to tissue damage, and coordinate regenerative responses [2, 6, 18]. Disruption of Wnt gradients impairs regeneration, whereas their restoration using RNA interference (RNAi) successfully reinstates regenerative capabilities [19, 20]. Similarly, molecular gradients play essential roles in the regeneration of anamniotes. For instance, gene expression profiling of uninjured pectoral fins in Danio rerio revealed region-specific patterns that mark individual fin rays throughout adulthood, thereby preserving their positional identity [21]. In salamanders, the salamander-specific gene Prod1 establishes proximo-distal gradients within the limb, ensuring accurate regeneration following ablation or natural damage [22]. During embryonic development, Hox genes do not function as morphogens in the classical sense of the term, but they play a key role in the patterning of the anterior–posterior body axis [23–25]. The maintenance of Hox gradients in post-embryonic bodies and their reorganization following injury was demonstrated across various taxa, including planarians, annelids, and newts [26–28].

These observations highlight that positional information is an essential component of post-embryonic growth and regeneration. Identifying potential factors that define positional values along the AP axis in juvenile and adult animals will shed light on how developmental patterning mechanisms are co-opted and maintained during regeneration. Annelids represent a particularly promising and intriguing model for studying post-embryonic patterning programs, including the maintenance and re-establishment of positional information. Most annelid species typically consist of numerous body segments and exhibit continuous growth for the whole life or until sexual maturation, adding new segments from the posterior growth zone [29–32]. Consequently, the relative positions of segments along the AP axis shift throughout the worm’s life.

Many annelids are capable of reparative regeneration [33], that is, the restoration of body parts following natural loss or traumatic injury [34, 35]. Regeneration of both anterior and posterior structures is considered an ancestral feature of annelids [36]. While posterior regeneration is widespread among various annelid families, the ability to regenerate anterior structures has been lost multiple times during annelid evolution [33, 37]. Reparative regeneration in annelids proceeds primarily through epimorphosis, which involves active cell proliferation and the formation of a blastema that serves as the source of cells for rebuilding lost structures [38–40]. However, morphallactic events, involving reorganization of existing tissues through cell movements [41], also occur at different stages of regeneration [42–45]. At the earliest stages, it is crucial to determine the precise injury location to initiate the correct regenerative program, i.e. to form either ‘head’ or ‘tail’ structures. Because overall body length and proportions are altered, the gut and neural system must also be reorganized to fit the new body dimensions [44, 46–48]. During anterior regeneration, annelids often form a prostomium and a limited number of anterior segments, while the segments adjacent to the wound site undergo morphallactic remodelling to reconstruct the missing body parts [42, 43, 49]. Collectively, these observations imply that the annelid body retains positional markers that help to define the relative positions of segments along the AP axis throughout continuous growth. These positional markers must reorganize their expression when body size changes, for instance, after injury or during asexual reproduction, to guide subsequent morphallactic events. To understand how annelids govern the morphallactic steps of regeneration, it is essential to identify the factors that contribute to the molecular profile of their AP axis and to determine which of them support the functioning of the juvenile and adult organisms at the cellular, organ and systemic levels.

In this study, we generated and analyzed spatially resolved transcriptomes from three annelid species exhibiting distinct regenerative competencies. Pygospio elegans, a sedentary annelid from the Spionidae family, is capable of regenerating both anterior and posterior segments following amputation or natural ablation [50, 51]. Platynereis dumerilii, an errant annelid from the Nereididae family, can regenerate posterior segments [52–54]. In contrast, Arenicola marina (the blow lugworm), a sedentary annelid of the Arenicolidae family, can only repair wounds and restore lost body parts through compensatory hypertrophic growth, without the addition of new segments [55, 56].

For each species, the body was divided into twelve fragments based on the external and internal segments’ morphology, and RNA sequencing was performed for each fragment. We subsequently identified differentially expressed genes, reconstructed their expression profiles along the anterior-posterior axis, and analyzed the dynamics of genes associated with the morphogenesis of key organs and systems. Particular attention was given to factors potentially involved in establishing positional information within the annelid body, as we hypothesize that these may contribute to species-specific regenerative capacities.

Considering the evolutionary conservation of patterning factors active during post-embryonic development of bilaterians, we focused on key developmental regulators, including members of the Hox, Wnt and ParaHox gene families and described their variable distribution along the bodies of studied annelids. We specifically examined the characteristics of molecular signatures of the growth zone in the studied worms, the distribution of expression of morphological markers and the molecular features of the larval and postlarval body regions, and paid attention to the distribution patterns of species-specific transcripts.

Overall, this study advances our understanding of post-embryonic patterning mechanisms and proposes potential molecular candidates for positional markers that may underlie reparative processes in annelids. Our findings provide a solid foundation for future investigations into annelid regeneration and the mechanisms by which positional information contributes to this process.

Materials and methods

Culture methods of P. dumerilii and sample collection of P. elegans and A. marina

P. dumerilii is cultivated in the laboratory of experimental embryology (Saint-Petersburg State University) in artificial seawater (Red Sea Coral Pro) with salinity 32–34‰. The breed culture was kept under artificial food and light conditions at 18 °C. P. elegans and A. marina were collected at the intertidal zone near the marine biological station Dalnie Zelentsi (Barents Sea, 69°07′ N, 36°05′ E). The animals were kept at 18 °C in plastic containers filled with sand and artificial seawater (Red Sea Coral Pro Salt) at a salinity of approximately 32‰. P. dumerilii were fed twice a week with pureed spinach and mashed quail egg yolks. P. elegans were fed twice a week with pureed spinach. A. marina were used in experiments shortly after collection from the natural habitats and were not fed in the laboratory.

mRNA extraction, library preparation and sequencing

The worms were divided with a razor into 12 fragments (for details, see Results). Ten P. elegans, ten P. dumerilii with 40 segments and one A. marina of 3–4 cm length were taken for the experiment. The experiment was repeated twice for each species. Total RNA was isolated from P. elegans and P. dumerilii with a Direct-zol RNA Miniprep kit (Zymo Research); RNA isolation from A. marina was performed with QuickRNA Miniprep (Zymo Research) as it showed better results. The fragments were thoroughly dispensed in Trizol reagent. All further steps were accomplished according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primary RNA quality check was performed using gel electrophoresis.

Paired-end cDNA-libraries were generated from 72 total RNA samples using NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs) following the manufacturer’s instructions, and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. Library quality was assessed on the QIAxcel Advanced System (Qiagen). The work was performed at the research center ‘Biobank’ of St. Petersburg State University. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 Platform at the biotechnological company Evrogen (Russia, Moscow). Before sequencing, the quality of the libraries was additionally checked on the Agilent 2200 TapeStation System. The final paired reads comprised 24 pairs of FASTQ files for each annelid.

Read processing and de novo transcriptome assembly

The processing of raw reads and transcriptome assembling for A. marina and P. elegans were previously performed by our research group and described in [57]. For P. dumerilii, we also employed a de novo transcriptome assembly strategy to ensure absolutely identical methodological processing across all species and to minimize potential artifacts and biases during comparison. Read processing and assembly of the P. dumerilii transcriptome were carried out strictly according to the same protocol. For paired-end reads, initial quality control was conducted using FastQC v0.11.5 [58]. Adapter sequences and low-quality reads were removed with Trimmomatic v0.36 using the following command: ILLUMINACLIP: TruSeq3-PE.fa:2:30:10:2:True, SLIDINGWINDOW:5:20, LEADING:25, TRAILING:25, HEADCROP:15, MINLEN:25 [59]. Possible sequencing errors were corrected with Karect [60], and potential researcher biomaterial contamination was eliminated using Bowtie2 v2.3.4.1 (with parameter --un-conc) [61] and the human reference genome GRCh38 (hg38). A second round of quality control was performed for all processed reads with FastQC v0.11.5.

Paired-end reads from P. dumerilii were assembled using Trinity v2.14.0 (with parameters --normalize_reads, --kmer_size 25, --min_contig_length 200) [62]. The rnaQUAST v2.2.2 [63] was employed to evaluate the quality of the transcriptome assembly. Artificial redundancy from the assembly was removed with CD-HIT v4.7 [64], which allows the clustering of similar sequences (set similarity threshold – 95%). The Barrnap v0.8 [65] was utilized to identify ribosomal sequences for transcriptome composition analysis. Transcriptome decontamination was performed using the MCSC method [66] with the Diamond v0.9.14 tool [67] and UniRef90 database. The completeness of the final transcriptomes of P. dumerilii, P. elegans, and A. marina was assessed using BUSCO v5.8.2 in the “transcriptome” mode with the metazoa_odb12 lineage dataset [68, 69].

Quantification of transcript abundance

Transcript abundance was quantified using Salmon v0.7.2 (with parameter –validateMappings) [70]. Processed paired-end reads were mapped to the transcriptome separately for each body fragment. For P. elegans and A. marina, previously assembled transcriptomes were used [57]. Transcript expression values were summarized to the gene level using the tximport v3.16 R package [71] with gene information from Trinity.

To examine overall expression patterns across body regions, soft k-means (fuzzy) clustering was performed on log₂(TPM + 1)-transformed expression values using the scikit-fuzzy library (v0.5.0), with the number of clusters set to five (n_clusters = 5). Heatmaps displaying Z-score–normalized expression levels were generated separately for annotated and unannotated genes.

Identification of protein-coding genes, functional annotation and orthology analysis

Putative protein-coding regions within transcripts were identified using TransDecoder v5.5.0 [72]. The longest predicted protein was selected as the representative sequence for each gene. For downstream differential gene expression analysis, we retained genes encoding proteins longer than 100 amino acids that showed significant expression (TPM > 1) in at least one body fragment. The completeness of the filtered protein-coding subsets for P. dumerilii, P. elegans, and A. marina was assessed using BUSCO v5.8.2 in “proteins” mode with the metazoa_odb12 lineage dataset [68, 69].

The predicted protein sequences were annotated using the eggNOG-mapper v2 web server with specified parameters (Min. hit e-value = 0.001, Min. hit bit-score = 50, Min. % of identity = 30%, Min. % of query cov. = 10%, Min. % of subject cov. = 10%) [73]. Additionally, the sequences were queried against the UniProt/Swiss-Prot and NCBI protein (Spiralia) databases using DIAMOND. Putative orthologous sequences among annelids were identified using OrthoFinder v3.1.0 [74] with MMseqs2 [75] to calculate sequence similarity scores, and an inflation value of 2.

Differential gene expression analysis, clustering and visualization

Differential gene expression analysis was performed pairwise between different body fragments of annelids using Trinity toolkit scripts run_DE_analysis.pl (parameter --method DESeq2) and analyze_diff_expr.pl (parameters -P 1e-3 and -C 2). All differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were clustered by expression profile using TMM (Trimmed Mean of M-values) normalized values with the k-means method implemented in the define_clusters_by_cutting_tree.pl script from the Trinity protocol [76]. After testing multiple values, the number of clusters (k) was set to 25. For each cluster, Gene Ontology (GO) terms enrichment analysis was performed using the topGO v2.44.0 R package [77], with GO terms obtained from eggNOG-mapper.

For the analysis presented in Fig. 7, orthogroups shared across all three species were annotated with GO terms. When multiple GO terms were assigned to an orthogroup, the most specific term was selected based on its hierarchical depth (number of ancestor terms), calculated using the GO.db package (v3.18) in R. When multiple terms shared the same maximum depth, the first term was retained. For genes lacking GO annotations, eggNOG functional descriptions were used instead.

GO terms describing biological processes were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test, and terms containing at least ten significantly expressed genes were outlined.

All results – including sequence identification numbers, functional annotations from eggNOG-mapper, UniProt/Swiss-Prot, and NCBI (Spiralia) protein databases, orthogroup numbers, expression levels (log₂[TPM + 1]) averaged across two replicates, and expression cluster assignments – were consolidated into comprehensive tables (see Additional file 1: Table S1; Additional file 2: Table S2; Additional file 3: Table S3).

Selection and phylogenetic analysis of genes involved in body patterning

The selection of genes of particular relevance to positional information – specifically, genes traditionally associated with body patterning and AP axis determination – was based on a combination of functional annotation, BLAST searches against nucleotide transcriptome databases, and confirmation through OrthoFinder analysis. Candidate genes were further validated by phylogenetic analysis. Amino acid sequences were aligned using the MAFFT algorithm [78] in Geneious Prime 2025.2.1. The optimal amino acid substitution model was estimated with ProtTest [79] and selected according to the AIC criterion. Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using MrBayes v3.2.3 [80]. Final trees were visualized in FigTree v1.4.4 [81] and are presented in Fig. S12 (“Phylogenetic genes”). A list of the selected genes of interest is provided in supplementary Additional file 19: Tables S7.

Results

Defining the boundaries of annelid body fragments for prospective transcriptome sequencing

For prospective transcriptome sequencing, we used intact juvenile worms of A. marina with 30 segments, as well as P. dumerilii and P. elegans with approximately 40 segments, and sectioned their bodies into 12 fragments (Fig. 1). Our animal sectioning strategy was guided by larval and postlarval boundaries and by distinct anatomical landmarks. The major landmarks included the cephalic structures (prostomium) together with the peristomium, which correspond to fragment P1 in all three species, and the posterior growth zone with the pygidium, which consistently correspond to fragment P12. In P. elegans and P. dumerilii, since it is technically difficult to separate the growth zone and pygidium into distinct fragments, P12 also contained two to three of the youngest, newly formed body segments. The corresponding larval and postlarval body regions were further subdivided into fragments based on external anatomical features (such as branchiae in P. elegans and A. marina) and internal structures (including nephridia, stomach, and midgut, particularly in A. marina). Because P. dumerilii lacks pronounced external anatomical landmarks and its body is less distinctly tagmatized than that of P. elegans and A. marina, the postlarval body of P. dumerilii was sectioned into fragments containing an equal number of segments.

For P. elegans, fragments P2-P4 encompass larval segments, the number of which in this population of worms is twelve (the first, peristomial segment, remained in the fragment P1) [51] (Fig. 1A). The abdominal segments with branchiae were divided into P5-P9 fragments, while fragments P10-P11 include “tail” segments without branchiae. P. elegans has no gizzard-like structure [82], so the digestive system possessed only pharynx (P1-P2) and midgut (P2-P11). The nephridia started from the chaetiger 4 (P2), however starting from the chaetiger 17 (P6) they were modified to perform the reproductive function as gonoducts [83].

For P. dumerilii, fragment P2 consists of larval segments, three of which are formed by the nectochaete during larval development, with the first larval segment subsequently incorporated into the peristomium [52] (Fig. 1B). The remaining part of the body included segments formed from the growth zone and was evenly cut into fragments P3-P11. The pharynx was located in fragments P1 and P2, the salivary glands and the short esophagus were restricted by P3, and the midgut was situated in the P3-P11. The nephridia started from P3 and the germ cells from P4.

In A. marina, the body is divided into several regions (Fig. 1C). The first two segments possessing no appendages were isolated as fragment P2. The next six anterior segments with parapodia but lacking branchiae were divided into fragments P3-P4. The following 13 segments with pronounced parapodia and branchiae were encompassed in fragments P5-P8. The tail part of the body, with segments lacking appendages, was evenly divided into fragments P9-P11. According to the internal anatomy of the worm, the fragments P1-P2 contained pharynx, P3-P4 possessed esophagus and three muscular diaphragms, P5-P6 encompassed the stomach, and P7-P11 contained the midgut. The salivary glands were found in fragments P4-P5. Nephridia and gonads were restricted by fragments P4-P5. Additionally, the blood plexus around the gut was situated in fragments P5-P11, and the main propulsatory vessels (“hearts”) were located between P4 and P5 (Fig. 1C) [84].

Sequencing and de novo transcriptome assembly

For A. marina and P. elegans, sequencing and transcriptome assembly were previously performed by our research group and are described in Chen et al. [57]. As part of the present study, we conducted de novo transcriptome assembly for P. dumerilli. High-throughput P. dumerilii transcriptome sequencing yielded 32,083,076 to 87,889,022 raw paired-end Illumina reads per library. After raw read processing and quality filtering, the number of clean reads ranged from 29,641,204 to 80,671,978 (Additional file 4: Fig. S1). All cleaned reads were assembled into 555,760 transcripts using the Trinity program [62]. To evaluate the quality of the generated transcriptome, we computed classical reconstruction metrics using rnaQUAST (Additional file 5: Fig. S2). The number of transcripts longer than 500 bp (base pairs) was 228,977, and longer than 1000 bp was 115,379. The average length of assembled transcripts was 810.17 bp, the total length was 450,258,782, and the N50 was 1,427 bp. The rnaQUAST report demonstrates high-performance metrics of the de novo transcriptome assembly, and the assembly results of P. dumerilii reads are comparable to those of P. elegans and A. marina (Table 1) [57].

Trinity software is known to produce artificially inflated transcript numbers [85]. To address this redundancy, we used the CD-HIT program, which clusters highly similar sequences, resulting in a refined dataset comprising 408,071 transcripts. Subsequent analysis of the transcriptome with Barrnap revealed contamination by non-target organisms. Similar contamination issues were previously reported in transcriptome assemblies for P. elegans and A. marina [57]. Given the non-model organism status of these annelids, the Model-based Categorical Sequence Clustering (MCSC) method was employed to effectively identify and remove contaminant sequences [57, 66]. To ensure methodological consistency across all annelid assemblies, we also applied the MCSC approach to the P. dumerilii transcriptome, retaining only sequences matching the specified target group (“Annelida”). Following this decontamination step, the total number of transcripts for P. dumerilii was reduced to 340, 507.

We evaluated the BUSCO completeness of the assembled P. dumerilii transcriptome, as well as previously assembled transcriptomes for A. marina and P. elegans [57], using the metazoan lineage dataset. The results are consistent with previous studies on P. elegans [86] and P. dumerilii [87]. Specifically, the transcriptomes showed 86.6% completeness in P. elegans (59.7% single-copy), 95.5% completeness in P. dumerilii (66.4% single-copy), and 90.3% completeness in A. marina (53.4% single-copy) (Fig. 2B). Overall, these results indicate that all three transcriptomes are of high completeness and suitable for downstream comparative analyses.

Read mapping and evaluation of gene expression levels

Processed annelid reads from each of the twelve body fragments (two biological replicates per fragment) were independently mapped to their respective transcriptomes using Salmon software (Additional file 6: Fig. S3). Previously assembled transcriptomes for P. elegans and A. marina were used for this analysis [57]. Notably, mapping efficiency differed among the three annelids, ranging from 79.25% to 86.12% for P. elegans, 87.12% to 94.51% for P. dumerilii, and 49.36% to 71.86% for A. marina (Additional file 6: Fig. S3)). Transcript-level expression values were subsequently summarized to gene-level expression using the R package tximport. This yielded gene expression data in the form of raw read counts and abundance values (TPM, transcripts per million) across all twelve body fragments, identifying a total of 362,355 genes in P. elegans, 250,940 genes in P. dumerilii, and 151,119 genes in A. marina. Using TransDecoder, we identified genes encoding protein sequences longer than 100 amino acids, genes exhibiting significant expression levels (TPM > 1) in at least one fragment, and weakly expressed transcripts (Fig. 3A). For downstream differential expression analysis, we selected genes encoding proteins longer than 100 amino acids that showed significant expression (TPM > 1) in at least one body fragment. This filtering resulted in a final set of 48,621 genes for P. elegans, 34,046 genes for P. dumerilii and 21,910 genes for A. marina (see Additional files 1–3: Tables S1–S3).

We evaluated the completeness of the final gene sets for the three annelids using BUSCO analysis against the metazoa database in “proteins” mode. The results showed 75.7% completeness in P. elegans, 80.7% in P. dumerilii, and 67.9% in A. marina (Fig. 3B). These results demonstrate overall high completeness and reliability of the predicted protein-coding gene sets for downstream analyses.

Functional annotation of genes

Gene annotation was performed using eggNOG-mapper. Out of the total genes, successfully annotated 25,846 genes (53.16%) for P. elegans, 20,330 genes (59.71%) for P. dumerilii, and 14,446 genes (65.93%) for A. marina. Additionally, sequences were compared against the UniProt/Swiss-Prot and NCBI protein (Spiralia) databases using DIAMOND, revealing a moderate degree of overlap among annotation sources (43–60%) (Fig. 3C). Orthogroups and orthologs were subsequently identified using OrthoFinder (Fig. 3D) [74]. In total, 15,993 orthogroups were identified in P. elegans, 12,916 in P. dumerilii, and 11,258 in A. marina, with 6,722 orthogroups shared among all three species. Most genes were successfully assigned to orthogroups, with assignment rates of 74.3% for P. elegans, 81.2% for P. dumerilii, and 80.0% for A. marina. Notably, P. elegans exhibits the highest proportion of species-specific genes, with 43.0% of its genes (20,904 genes) belonging to 5,084 species-specific orthogroups, suggesting extensive lineage-specific gene family expansion. Additionally, the highest number of unassigned genes in P. elegans (25.7%) may reflect either rapidly evolving or highly divergent sequences, incomplete annotation, or the presence of taxonomically restricted genes unique to this lineage. Nevertheless, one-to-one orthologs correctly reflect the phylogenetic relationship of the three species, with A. marina and P. elegans sharing the most single-copy conserved genes (6,410). Orthology analysis revealed that 7,740-9,192 genes per species have orthologs present in all three species. OrthoFinder statistics for each species are provided in Additional file 7: Table S4.

Differential gene expression along the anterior-posterior axis in annelid bodies

We evaluated reproducibility between biological replicates using Principal Component Analysis (PCA), which revealed low variability between replicate pairs (Additional file 8: Fig. S4). PCA also demonstrated clear separation among samples from different body fragments, highlighting the influence of fragment position along the AP axis on gene expression profiles. Gene expression distributions across the twelve body fragments, visualized using histograms, further confirmed consistency between biological replicates (Additional file 9: Figure S5). The distribution that best fits RNA sequencing data, considering some variability between biological replicates and commonly utilized in differential expression analysis tools, is the negative binomial distribution [88, 89]. The histograms visually correspond to this distribution and illustrate some common features of RNA quantification data - most genes exhibit low expression, with only a small fraction being highly expressed. Additionally, the plots display a long right tail due to the absence of an upper limit of expression. It is worth noting that some variations in gene expression profiles between different fragments of the annelid body are evident in the graphs, reflecting the influence of fragment position along the anterior-posterior axis of the body on the pool of active and inactive genes.

To examine overall expression patterns, we applied soft k-means clustering to all annotated genes across body fragments (Fig. 4). In both P. elegans and P. dumerilii, a substantial number of genes showed elevated expression in fragments P1 and P12, corresponding to the prostomium and the terminal region (comprising the pygidium, growth zone, and several newly formed segments), respectively (Fig. 1). The P1 fragment of A. marina similarly displayed prominent expression of numerous genes; however, in contrast to the other two species, most genes in P12 were downregulated (Fig. 4C).

Since clusters enriched with GO terms associated with patterning and anterior–posterior axis determination contained patterning-related genes and genes involved in regeneration regulation, we conducted a targeted analysis of these evolutionarily conserved genes in the three annelid species. Based on previous studies and the results presented in Fig. 9, we selected a set of candidate genes: 11 Hox genes, 12 Wnt genes and 3 ParaHox genes [93–96]. Discussion

Quality of transcriptome assembly, read mapping, and functional analysis

At the time of transcriptome assembly and analysis, reference transcriptomes existed only for P. dumerilii and P. elegans, while none were available for A. marina. Therefore, de novo transcriptome assemblies were performed by Chen et al. for P. elegans and A. marina [57]. To ensure methodological consistency in our study, we also conducted a de novo assembly of the P. dumerilii transcriptome, strictly following the workflow described in [57]. Performing analyses differently for each species would introduce artificial variation, potentially reflecting differences in data types, sequencing quality, or reconstruction methods, rather than genuine biological differences.

Compared to the previously published de novo assembly of the reference P. dumerilii transcriptome [97], our assembly yielded a slightly higher number of transcripts (340,507 vs. 331,646). However, other key assembly metrics were lower in our dataset: total assembly size (450 Mb vs. 627 Mb), mean transcript length (810 bp vs. 1,890 bp), and N50 value (1,427 bp vs. 2,933 bp). These differences are expected, given that our transcript assembly derives exclusively from Illumina short reads, whereas the published assembly incorporates both Illumina short-read and Oxford Nanopore Technologies long-read data [97]. Despite these methodological constraints, the large number of assembled transcripts allowed us to carry out comprehensive downstream analyses.

We observed substantial variation in read mapping efficiency among the three annelid species, with the lowest efficiency (49.36%) recorded for A. marina and the highest (94.51%) for P. dumerilii. Such a broad range of mapping efficiency could be associated with differences in final assembly sizes (A. marina: 292 Mb vs. P. dumerilii: 450 Mb), as well as varying degrees of contamination in the original sequencing data. Specifically, for this study, P. dumerilii worms were obtained from a laboratory culture, whereas P. elegans and A. marina were collected from their natural habitats. Both P. elegans and A. marina are sedentary annelids inhabiting marine sandy sediments within small tubes or burrows [51, 55], environments likely rich in symbiotic organisms and parasites, which can serve as sources of contamination. Indeed, contamination by the archigregarine Selenidium pygospionis, a known parasite of P. elegans, was previously reported in [57].

For downstream differential gene expression analysis, we selected transcripts encoding proteins longer than 100 amino acids that showed significant expression levels in at least one body fragment. The resulting filtered protein-coding gene sets demonstrated overall high completeness according to BUSCO “proteins” metrics, ranging from 67.9% in P. elegans to 80.7% in P. dumerilii. Despite fairly comprehensive functional annotation of the selected genes, some sequences do not show similarities with known proteins (Fig. 3). These sequences, most abundant in P. elegans, may represent lineage-specific orphan genes, as previously reported in other regenerating annelids [98]. Such unique genes may also participate in orchestrating regeneration processes. For instance, studies on axolotl limb regeneration revealed numerous novel genes activated during blastema formation, suggesting they contribute significantly to regenerative success [99].

It is important to note that sequences excluded from our analysis – namely, genes encoding short proteins and non-coding sequences – may still hold biological significance. Although our data do not provide direct evidence for the potential involvement of these sequences in maintaining positional information in the studied worms, they may nonetheless represent valuable targets for further investigation. Multiple studies have indicated their possible contribution to the organism’s regenerative capacity. For instance, abundant non-coding RNAs identified during regeneration in the earthworm Eisenia fetida are thought to play critical roles in regulating regenerative processes, and differences in regenerative potential among annelid species could be related to the activity of such regulatory RNAs [98].

Differential gene expression analysis: defining the boundaries of body regions and anatomical structures

Differential gene expression analysis reveals a consistent distribution of coding transcripts within defined boundaries along the anterior-posterior axis of annelid bodies. Notably, these expression patterns correspond with certain morphological and anatomical features of annelids (Fig. 1). In the prostomium of all three annelids we observe ongoing neural, signaling and sensory processes [100] (Additional files 15–17; Fig. S10-12), which is in line with the location of the head ganglion and sensory organs [52, 83, 84]. The activity of stem cells in P. elegans prostomium can be explained by the presence of the palps with the feeding grooves. The cells at the basis of the palps constantly proliferate to renew the cilia-bearing cells in the feeding groove (personal observations [51], . Proteolytic processes and the activity of genes coding for digestive enzymes are observed in the A. marina stomach (P5 and P6 fragments) and in fragments containing the midgut of annelids, such as subtilisin-2, a marker of the midgut [101]. In P6-P11 fragments of P. elegans nephridia with gonoduct function and germ cells are located (Fig. 1), which is in line with the mitotic and meiotic activity in this region. P2 fragment of A. marina, in which a great number of the processes are down-regulated (Fig. 4), contains most of the papilla-covered pharynx [102]. We presume that the active biosynthesis in this area can be explained by the secreting function of the pharynx cells, although the down-regulation of so many other processes is quite unclear. The P9-P11 fragments of A. marina, which incorporate the posterior part of the midgut, are also marked by variable biosynthetic processes, probably involved in the secretion of digestive enzymes.

The distribution of the morphological markers largely corresponds to the location of the respective tissues. Muscle complexes of various types are distributed throughout the worm body, comprising the body-wall musculature, parapodial musculature, and the musculature supporting internal organs [51, 103, 104]. The markers we analyzed - calponin, troponin T, gata456, among others - accord with the known distribution of muscle types along the body axis.

Previously, we described the distribution of FMRFamide-positive neural elements in P. elegans [105]. The present study corroborates this pattern, showing the highest concentration of FMRFamide-positive cell bodies in the anterior region of the worm.

In P. elegans and P. dumerilii, alpha-amylase marks the midgut, localized in fragments P5-P11 and P3-P10, respectively. In A. marina, alpha-amylase transcripts occur in P1-P11, whereas the midgut itself is confined to P7-P11. This suggests that alpha-amylase may have a broader functional role in A. marina, extending its expression beyond the strict boundaries of the midgut. Similar to observations in Pristina leidyi [106], the globin marker occupies mid-body territories.

GMP genes are markers of stem cells, germ cells, and proliferating cells [107–109]. In P. dumerilii, germ cells are located beginning from segments 4–5 (fragment P3), consistent with the expression pattern of vasa [110]. In P. elegans and A. marina, germ cells mature within the coelomic cavities of the abdominal segments [83, 111]. In all three species examined, most GMP transcripts are distributed along the entire body axis, and their localization generally coincides with that of pcna, a marker of proliferating cells [108]. This co-distribution may reflect ongoing cell-renewal processes throughout the body in which these genes are likely involved.

We find the differential expression of genes between the fragments P1–P2 vs. P3–P11 in P. dumerilii and P1–P4 vs. P5–P11 in P. elegans particularly intriguing (Figs. 4, 7 and 8). The boundary between these groups of fragments corresponds to the transition between larval and postlarval segments in these annelids (Fig. 1). In P. dumerilii, this boundary also coincides with the division between the pharynx and the midgut, which may account for the differences in cellular processes revealed by our cluster analysis. However, based on the anatomical structure of P. elegans, there are no clear morphological boundaries between the major organ systems between fragments P4 and P5 (Fig. 1). The germ cells, which in annelids are localized in postlarval segments, are located starting from P6 in P. elegans and from P4 in P. dumerilii, respectively. Thus, the differences in metabolic processes that are differentially active in larval and postlarval segments of annelids cannot be explained solely by the anatomical distinctions between these regions. We suggest that the ontogenetic origin of a segment may also contribute to shaping its specific transcriptional profile [112]. Interestingly, in the lugworm, whose life cycle also includes a larval stage [92, 113], we do not observe a distinct boundary between larval and postlarval segments in total gene expression analysis or cluster analysis. Nevertheless, a targeted analysis of the genes orthologous to those that show differential expression between the larval and postlarval body regions in P. elegans and P. dumerilii revealed a similar expression pattern in A. marina as well (Fig. 7). It is likely that we do not detect these genes in the general analysis because, due to the high degree of body specialization in the lugworm, the observed expression boundaries for the majority of genes correspond to the borders between organ systems.

Transcription signatures of the posterior growth zone of three annelids

The visualization of the total expression pattern of all annotated genes, along with the cluster analysis, revealed that a substantial number of genes were downregulated in the posterior body fragment of all three annelid species (Figs. 4 and 8). The analysis of enriched GO terms within the clusters discussed in the previous section indicated that these genes are predominantly associated with intracellular processes, which are expected to be downregulated in the actively dividing cells of the posterior growth zones of P. elegans and P. dumerilii. In contrast, clusters enriched for GO terms related to morphogenesis, patterning, stem cell maintenance, and axis specification showed upregulation in the posterior fragment of P. elegans and P. dumerilii, but not in A. marina (Fig. 9). This distinctive molecular profile of the lugworm’s posterior body fragment, which differs from that observed in the other annelids examined, may indicate an inactive state of the growth zone – an interpretation consistent with previous morphological observations [56].

Posterior growth in bilaterians, including postlarval growth in annelids, has been hypothesized to be governed by a Wnt > cad > Hox regulatory expression cascade [114, 115]. Posterior Wnt signaling regulates the expression of the transcription factor cad, which is required for axis elongation, segmentation, and the establishment of Hox-mediated anterior–posterior patterning [11, 115]. Our results are consistent with this hypothesis. Targeted gene expression analyses revealed robust activity of caudal (cad/Cdx), Hox genes, and several Wnt genes as well as GMP genes in the posterior growth zones of P. elegans and P. dumerilii (Figs. 5 and 11). These findings align with previously reported in situ hybridization data: in P. dumerilii, six Wnt genes (wnt1, wnt5, wnt7, wnt10, wnt11, wnt16) are expressed in the pygidium at the posterior end of the body [95], and cad as well as hox2 and hox3 are expressed in the growth zone located anterior to the pygidium [114, 116]. It has been proposed that Wnt signaling in P. dumerilii emanates from a posterior signaling center within the pygidium, thereby regulating the activity of cad-positive cells [95]. Consistent with our observations, the expression of GMP genes (piwi1, piwi2, vasa, pl10, and nanos) has previously been demonstrated in the growth zone of P. dumerilii [117]. GMP genes play a crucial role in maintaining the undifferentiated state of germline and multipotent stem cells [110, 118]. In annelids, strong GMP expression in the posterior growth zone has been linked to segment formation [118, 119]. Accordingly, GMP genes and cad are expressed in the growth zones of both P. dumerilii and P. elegans, supporting actively dividing stem cell populations (Figs. 5 and 11). In P. dumerilii, de novo activation of Pdum-hox3, as well as cad and piwi genes, was demonstrated at the posterior end at 12 hpa (hours post amputation) by single-cell RNA sequencing. This reflected early re-patterning of gene expression and specification of a new posterior end of the worm’s body prior to blastema formation [90].

In contrast, the terminal body fragment of A. marina shows a marked reduction in cad, GMP, and Wnt expression (Figs. 5 and 11), indicating that the posterior growth program in this species is either strongly diminished or completely inactive. This interpretation is consistent with previous findings demonstrating that reduced cad activity results in the cessation of segmentation and anterior–posterior axis elongation ([11]). Substantial molecular similarities have been observed between posterior growth and posterior regeneration in annelids [120]. Therefore, it is plausible that the absence of posterior regeneration in A. marina is a consequence of the cessation of posterior growth at a specific developmental stage [56] and the failure to reactivate a functional growth zone at the appropriate location – probably due to the inability to redistribute gene expression and activate the required genes at the site of injury.

Potential candidates for providing positional information

According to our results, the expression of genes involved in defining the anterior-posterior body axis, morphogenetic processes, and regeneration is spread along the body axis of P. elegans and P. dumerilii and exhibit variable gradients that do not strictly correspond to the boundaries between body regions and anatomical structures (Figs. 1 and 9). These genes largely represent various conserved factors implicated in body patterning and thus constitute strong candidates for providing positional information within annelids’ bodies. In contrast, these genes display distinct expression patterns in the body trunk of A. marina with slight up- and downregulation of transcription in particular fragments (Fig. 9).

Our targeted analysis of genes revealed that the expression of at least several Hox genes of P. elegans and P. dumerilii, i.e. hox4, hox7, lox4, lox2 and post2, is spread in a gradient manner along the worms’ bodies (Fig. 11A, B). This expression pattern is in line with the previously described for the close relative of P. dumerilii, the nereidid Alitta virens [121]. For A. virens, it has been shown that as the worm grows and the position of its segments shifts along the anteroposterior body axis, corresponding changes occur in the expression profiles of Hox genes [121]. Thus, this gradient system is thought not primarily to drive morphological differentiation, but to encode and maintain positional information in each segment. In continuously growing worms’ bodies, such a system assigns a unique Hox code to every segment, reflecting its position along the anterior-posterior body axis and enabling the initiation of the appropriate regeneration program upon injury [28, 121]. This assumption is supported by the fact that Hox gradients undergo reorganization in response to caudal amputation in A. virens [28].

This observation is not fully supported by the results obtained on another annelid, Capitella teleta [122]. In juvenile C. teleta, most Hox genes exhibit their main expression domains within the thoracic segments, whereas three genes – lox4, lox2, and post2 – are expressed in the abdominal region of the body [122]. Reorganization of expression patterns has been observed only for those genes expressed in the abdomen of C. teleta. A possible explanation is that different annelid species employ distinct sets of Hox genes to establish the positional code in the growing body. The genes active in the thoracic region of C. teleta appear to preserve the expression pattern established during the larval stage, when they display a similar arrangement with stable anterior and posterior boundaries [122]. Collinear expression of Hox genes involved in larval formation has been described in several annelid species [31, 123]. We hypothesize that the thoracic segments, characterized by their “larval” Hox code primarily dedicated to larval patterning, maintain a stable expression pattern that cannot be reorganized in response to changes in body proportions. In contrast, Hox genes expressed in the continuously growing posterior region acquire roles in defining segment’s positional identity and can therefore respond to injury through the reorganization of their expression domains.

By analogy with Hox genes, spatial distributions of ParaHox genes may help maintain positional identity of segments along the anterior–posterior axis and facilitate re-activation of the developmental program during anterior regeneration. Targeted in situ studies are required to test this hypothesis.

In the lugworm A. marina, a truncated, segment-restricted Hox expression pattern appears to persist in the worm’s body. We cannot associate this expression with specific anatomical structures, but we assume that in the specialized postlarval body of the lugworm, Hox genes have acquired cell- or tissue-specific functions. Another option is that segment-restricted Hox pattern may permit proportional rescaling of remaining segments after injury (e.g., restoring the tail-to-trunk balance), while the inactive posterior growth program prevents the addition of new segments [56].

Gradient expression of Hox genes has been demonstrated along the anterior–posterior body axis in several other species, including the planarian Schmidtea mediterranea and the acoel Hofstenia miamia [124], within the nervous system of the newt [27], and in the pectoral fin of Danio rerio [21]. In all of these models, early reorganization of Hox gene expression patterns following amputation has been observed, indicating the positional role of Hox genes during regeneration [21, 27, 124]. Four posterior Hox genes in planarians (post-2c, post-2d, hox4b, and lox5a) and one posterior Hox gene in the acoel worm were shown to be affected by disruption of canonical Wnt signaling [125]. Wnt signaling is known to play a key role in establishing the anterior–posterior polarity in planarians: the loss of the Wnt gradient prevents normal anterior regeneration, whereas restoration of the gradient rescues this process [19, 20].

In P. elegans and P. dumerilii, the expression of nearly all genes, encoding Wnt ligands, is distributed along the AP axis, although transcription levels vary among individual ligands (Fig. 11A, B). Previous studies have shown that in P. dumerilii, Wnt genes display a segment-by-segment expression pattern, at least in newly formed segments [95]. It remains unclear whether such segment-specific expression has positional significance comparable to the gradient-like expression observed in other animals. Nevertheless, given that almost all Wnt transcripts exhibit high expression levels in the posterior-most body fragment, it is plausible that at least some of them contribute to defining the posterior identity of the body in P. dumerilii and P. elegans. To clarify their potential roles, it will be essential to investigate the dynamics of Wnt gene expression during regeneration.

In A. marina, only Amar-wnt10 shows pronounced expression across all body fragments, while Amar-wnt8 is highly expressed in P9 and P10, and Amar-wnt1 displays elevated transcription levels in P1, P7, and P12 (Fig. 11C). At present, it is difficult to speculate on the specific functions these genes may perform in the body of A. marina.

The entirely open question is why P. dumerilii appears unable to activate the program required for the anterior body reconstruction. Our previous morphological and cellular analyses revealed that wound closure at the anterior amputation site in P. dumerilii deviates from those observed during anterior regeneration in other annelids [126]. Additionally, no cellular proliferation is initiated at this site, and apoptotic dynamics differ from those typical for successful regeneration [126]. Consequently, early failure in anterior regeneration in P. dumerilii may be partially attributed to incorrect interpretation of positional information, which is supposed to be one of the earliest steps in the reparation process. Nevertheless, additional experiments are strictly needed to clarify this question.

Conclusion

Our comparative transcriptomic analysis of three annelid species with distinct regenerative capacities, Pygospio elegans, Platynereis dumerilii, and Arenicola marina, reveals that spatial gene expression along the anterior–posterior axis closely corresponds to major anatomical territories and tissue specializations. Despite this correspondence, the transcriptional profiles of larval versus postlarval segments in P. elegans and P. dumerilii cannot be fully explained by anatomy alone, suggesting that the ontogenetic origin of segments contributes to the establishment of region-specific molecular identities.

Distinct transcriptional signatures of the posterior growth zone in P. elegans and P. dumerilii - including elevated expression of Wnt, cad, Hox, and GMP genes - support the presence of an active posterior growth program. In contrast, the posterior region of A. marina lacks this molecular activity, consistent with the cessation of segment addition and absence of posterior regeneration in this species.

Patterns of Hox, ParaHox, and Wnt gene expression in P. elegans and P. dumerilii further suggest that these conserved developmental regulators may provide positional information along the body axis, potentially enabling the respecification of segment identity during regeneration. By contrast, segment-restricted and attenuated expression of these patterning genes in A. marina likely reflects the high degree of specialization of the adult trunk and may constrain both posterior growth and regenerative competence.

Together, this study provides a comparative framework for understanding how conserved patterning systems are deployed in annelid growth and regeneration, and highlights molecular candidates likely to underlie the maintenance and reactivation of positional information in segmented animals [127].
