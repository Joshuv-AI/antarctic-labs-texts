Introduction

The United States is a major global beef supplier, operating a highly diverse production system that spans extensive rangelands and intensive feedyards. Figure 1 provides a conceptual overview of the U.S. beef supply chain, illustrating the major production stages and their connections from cow-calf through feedyard and processing, consistent with established descriptions of the supply chain in the literature (Thoma et al., 2017; Asem-Hiablie et al., 2019; Rotz et al., 2019). While a complex supply chain, representing over 700,000 individual producers and countless supply chain partners, decades of innovation in genetics, nutrition, reproduction, and land management have enabled the industry to improve efficiency and reduce environmental impacts per unit of beef (Capper, 2011; Herrero et al., 2013; Rotz et al., 2019; Klopatek and Oltjen, 2022; Putman et al., 2023; USDA NASS, 2024). Despite this progress, a key challenge remains: how to generate clear, consistent, and actionable evidence of sustainability performance and identify scalable solutions that can be implemented across highly heterogeneous production systems. This is a challenge driven by increasing expectations across stakeholder groups (i.e., producers, packers and processors, retail and food service companies, consumer packaged goods companies, other supply chain partners, and policymakers) for transparent metrics, continuous improvement, and coordinated action.

To guide action, stakeholders across the U.S. beef value chain have pursued greater alignment on where research can most effectively advance environmental outcomes. In 2022, the U.S. Roundtable for Sustainable Beef (USRSB) established industry-wide sustainability goals and sector-specific targets across six high-priority indicators of sustainability, providing a unifying framework for consistent communication, coordination, and meaningful action (USRSB, 2022). These goals are the result of multistakeholder collaboration and were set to provide a north star for industry action, while sector targets were set to: 1) demonstrate where each sector can take ownership of its actions to support advancement toward the overarching goals and 2) how other sectors can support one another, based on these prioritized areas of focus.

Given the pressure placed on the beef supply chain to reduce emissions, the USRSB goal areas of Air & Greenhouse Gas Emissions, Land Resources, and Water Resources are the areas where clarity and alignment on action are needed most urgently (Lai et al., 2026). These areas combine high potential for measurable improvement with substantial remaining scientific uncertainty and growing importance to downstream supply-chain partners working to meet their own sustainability commitments. Furthermore, the live-animal stages of production exert the greatest influence on environmental outcomes and are where the most significant knowledge gaps persist and prevent meaningful progress. In order to support continued alignment and coordinated action, there is a pressing need for a coordinated, cross-sector research agenda. The work herein aims to bring such an agenda to life through the creation of a research roadmap that is anchored in industry-defined priorities, supports consistent measurement, and directs investment to the highest-impact opportunities.

Organized within the USRSB environmental goals framework, this roadmap aims to clarify where improvement potential is greatest and where stronger evidence is most urgently needed across the U.S. beef supply chain. It offers a clear, investable research agenda spanning measurement, technology, modeling, genetics, and producer practicality and is designed to support continuous improvement, strengthen data credibility, and accelerate progress. In short, this work moves the conversation from why alignment is needed to what to do next, prioritizing research that producers can adopt, supply chains can verify, and the industry can scale.

Roadmap Methodology

While Lai et al. (2026) make the case for a coordinated, cross-sector research agenda, highlighting fragmentation, inconsistent methodologies, and limited alignment that currently constrain progress, the work herein advances the next step: a practical, science-based roadmap that identifies the critical research needs required for sustained, credible improvement across the U.S. beef value chain.

The research priorities presented herein were developed through a structured, multistep process that combined literature synthesis, subject matter expert (SME) leadership, and broad, multistakeholder engagement to build consensus. This process was designed to ensure that the resulting roadmap reflects both the current state of scientific knowledge and the practical considerations necessary for implementation across diverse beef production systems.

USRSB partnered with the Foundation for Food & Agriculture Research (FFAR) to convene academic and industry experts through a series of virtual focus group sessions followed by an in-person workshop. These engagements were designed to identify key knowledge gaps, evaluate emerging areas of research, and refine priorities through facilitated discussion and iterative feedback.

Eight subject matter experts (SME) were selected to guide information gathering and support development of the research roadmap. SMEs were chosen based on their demonstrated expertise in beef production systems and environmental sustainability and were responsible for synthesizing available scientific literature, identifying key knowledge gaps, and facilitating technical discussions. Focus group sessions

USRSB, FFAR, and a third-party facilitation team hosted three publicly accessible virtual focus group sessions corresponding to the three environmental indicator areas: Air & Greenhouse Gas Emissions, Land Resources, and Water Resources. Each session was led by SMEs and designed to assess the current state of the science, identify promising research directions, and highlight key gaps requiring further investigation.

Each session included participation from a broad cross-section of stakeholders across the beef value chain, including producers, packers and processors, retailers and food service companies, nongovernmental organizations, researchers, and other industry partners, with more than 80 participants engaged per session. These discussions provided critical input to ensure that the emerging research priorities reflected diverse perspectives and real-world considerations. Feedback gathered during these sessions was used to refine and expand draft research topics and identify additional areas of importance for further evaluation.

In-person workshop

Following the virtual engagement process, USRSB and FFAR convened a 2-d, facilitated, in-person workshop to further refine and prioritize the research roadmap. Eighty participants representing more than 50 organizations across the beef value chain participated, including again producers, packers and processors, retailers and food service companies, nongovernmental organizations, researchers, and other industry partners.

The workshop built upon draft research priorities developed through SME analyses and focus group input, including synthesis of current scientific knowledge presented in companion topic areas addressing air, land, and water systems. The workshop began with expert panel presentations summarizing the current state of the science and initial identification of research gaps, followed by a plenary discussion to clarify key issues and gather additional input from participants.

The majority of the workshop was dedicated to structured breakout sessions, where participants reviewed and provided feedback on draft research priorities and worked collaboratively to refine, modify, and prioritize research topics. This iterative process enabled integration of scientific expertise with stakeholder perspectives, resulting in a consensus-driven set of research priorities that reflect both scientific rigor and practical applicability.

Research Agenda

Accurate, scalable, and cost-effective measurement methods are essential to meet the research needs across the environmental indicator areas of Air & Greenhouse Gas Emissions, Land Resources, and Water Resources. There was a consensus from the extensive stakeholder input gathered to develop this roadmap that to fully address the industry’s needs for sustainable advances, long-term, coordinated, and interdisciplinary research efforts are essential. Greater quality and quantity of data are clearly needed to increase understanding of the key metrics that should be used across research efforts to support more accurate assessments and effective information sharing. Major barriers that exist around data and data sharing include ownership, control, and privacy. Inherent in efforts to support addressing research needs will be a need to recognize and work to resolve these sorts of barriers through clear data governance and an enabling and trusting environment when working alongside producers.

Importantly, decisions intended to improve one sustainability outcome may introduce trade-offs or unintended consequences across other environmental, economic, or social dimensions. For example, practices aimed at reducing greenhouse gas emissions may influence water use, land productivity, input costs, or management complexity, with variable implications depending on region and production system. Consequently, no single practice or technology should be assumed to universally optimize outcomes across air, land, and water. This research roadmap, therefore, emphasizes systems‑level evaluation and producer practicality, recognizing that progress toward sustainability requires careful consideration of synergies, trade-offs, and context‑specific impacts rather than one‑dimensional solutions.

Supporting the research needs outlined in this roadmap will ultimately drive adoption of best management practices across the cow-calf and feedyard stages of the U.S. beef value chain, providing opportunities for producers, scientists, and supply chains to optimize U.S. cattle production for environmental outcomes. Critical research area: grazing management plans

Grazing cattle present a key opportunity to make meaningful progress toward the environmental indicator areas of Air & Greenhouse Gas Emissions, Land Resources, and Water Resources. For the cow-calf sector, implementation of a grazing management plan (GMP) is the primary metric to assess progress toward USRSB’s sustainability targets. Grazing management plans present a mechanism to enhance management capabilities across the cow-calf sector, bringing value to producers across the United States, while anecdotally providing the foundation for making progress toward industry environmental goals. To this end, the USRSB target aims to have 385 million acres operating under written GMPs by 2050. Details on how this ambitious target was set are available in (USRSB, 2022).

Having a GMP does not necessarily guarantee improved environmental outcomes; however, the act of writing a plan down may support producers to perform a more critical assessment of their operation and develop a clearer understanding of actions that can be taken to achieve operational goals (Likins et al., 2024). The intent and focus of GMP uptake is to provide a tool that any land manager can implement. Meanwhile, the intent for addressing research needs tied to grazing management planning herein is to concretely demonstrate the value of written GMPs to support producer decision-making and operational goals, as well as determine the key elements and actions that can be used to demonstrate progress toward achieving industry environmental goals around air, land, and water. If successfully addressed, then further mechanisms for GMP uptake and means of measuring progress can be further defined.

Air and greenhouse gas emissions

Grazing cattle, or the cow-calf sector, represent just over 50% of the U.S. beef carbon footprint (in carbon dioxide equivalents, CO2e), with enteric methane (CH4) representing nearly 76% of the sector’s emissions (Rotz et al., 2019; Putman et al., 2023). Beyond enteric CH4 emissions from grazing animals, nitrogenous emissions, such as nitrous oxide (N2O) and ammonia (NH3), present other important sources of emissions from grazing animals (Rotz et al., 2019; Thompson and Rowntree, 2020). In addition to emissions from grazing animals, grazing lands play a key role in soil carbon storage and accrual, yet the magnitude remains uncertain due to a lack of robust research (Stanley et al., 2024; Watts et al., 2025). Soil carbon accrual presents an important opportunity to offset emissions from grazing cattle. With this in mind, two critical research areas for grazing cattle are outlined below.

Land resources

Well-managed grazing lands provide both ecological and economic benefits by improving soil health, increasing productivity, and enhancing resilience to climate change (Sollenberger et al., 2020; Rouquette et al., 2023). Adaptive grazing can aid in restoring functionality and productivity of degraded grazing lands, strengthen water retention, and support biodiversity while maintaining or even increasing productivity (Barnes and Howell, 2013; Grissom and Steffens, 2013; Likins et al., 2024). While substantial research has been conducted around adaptive grazing, important gaps remain in scaling findings across diverse production systems and translating results into operationally feasible practices. Furthermore, many grazing lands are unsuitable for cropping and can more easily enhance soil carbon storage and accrual through strategic grazing, but the extent of carbon sequestration potential across different regions and grazing systems is uncertain (Stanley et al., 2024). Grazing management plans help producers optimize resources and plan more strategically and adaptively, yet their impact on ecosystem services remains unclear (Díaz-Pereira et al., 2020). Understanding the relationship between these factors is essential for guiding conservation and production strategies that maximize environmental and economic benefits over time. With this in mind, one critical research area for grazing cattle is outlined below.

Water resources

Water use and water quality in grazing cattle are complex topics, and the use of GMPs to support improvements in both water use and quality is not well studied. Grazing cattle account for around 59% of blue water use (i.e., water sourced from surface and groundwater reservoirs) across the live animal stages of beef production (i.e., cow-calf through feedyard), and the primary source of blue water use is irrigation for feed production (Rotz et al., 2019; Klopatek and Oltjen, 2022). In regard to water quality, grazing cattle contribute to around 63% of nitrogen, primarily in the form of nitrate (NO3−) emissions leaching to groundwater across the live animal stages of beef production (Rotz et al., 2019). Both water use and quality, however, are highly variable by region of production. Some studies have demonstrated the ability of well-managed grazing to support better water retention and holding capacity in soils and filtering of excess nutrients in water run-off (Wagner et al., 2012; Abdalla et al.,2018; Kim et al., 2023). While some data provide evidence that well-managed grazing strategies support improvements in use and quality, further research is necessary to best understand how GMPs support these improvements. Critical research area: feedyard optimization

Air and greenhouse gas emissions

The uniqueness of feedlot settings creates a variety of emissions in the U.S. beef system. However, many of these emissions are within the control of feedyard management through a variety of strategies (Hristov et al., 2013; Waldrip et al., 2015; Beauchemin et al., 2020). There is a linkage between what an animal eats and how that interacts with enteric methane production while in confinement. While substantial research has been conducted in the area of mitigating emissions from feeding cattle, important gaps remain in understanding the co-supplementation impacts of various amendments, as well as scaling findings across diverse production systems and translating results into operationally feasible practices. A greater understanding of how feedyard diets impact enteric methane emissions provides an opportunity for more effective decision-making at the feedyard level. In addition to GHG emissions, volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and reactive nitrogen (N) are important emissions to consider at the feedyard level (Waldrip et al., 2015). Water resources

Water use, availability, accessibility, and quality are among the most crucial factors in cattle production. Regardless of operation size, ensuring access to high-quality drinking water for animals is essential, along with implementing management practices to minimize waste. Wastewater from lagoons provides an opportunity to rethink recycling water for cattle consumption as a means of water conservation. Along with minimizing waste, water use in forage and feedstuff crops is a key consideration, as crops purchased for feed are one of the key contributors to feedyard blue water use. Alternative forages and grains, such as sorghum, millet, and triticale, can contribute to water conservation in cattle production by reducing overall water consumption, improving soil moisture retention, and enhancing drought resilience; however, lagging prices and research gaps lead to a preference for traditional grains fed to cattle. Water quality is another important consideration in relation to the feedyard sector and waste management, and treatment is an important consideration for both environmental and public health. Specifically, more information is needed to fully understand contaminants of emerging concern (CEC) and the ways to minimize impacts to water quality from lagoons and wastewater. With all of this in mind, the following are key research areas for water resources in the feedyard sector.

Water resources

Satellite imagery and remote sensing are powerful tools for tracking water use, availability, and efficiency in livestock operations. By using various remote sensing technologies, producers and researchers can assess water conditions over large areas, reducing reliance on manual monitoring and improving resource management. In addition to opportunities with remote sensing, virtual fencing is a relatively new technology that shows promise for enhanced water management in extensive systems. This tool allows producers to implement virtual fences and manage grazing, including moving cattle to and from areas or restricting animals from certain areas, without a physical fence in place. Ongoing research efforts around the potential benefits to water quality and riparian management using virtual fencing are currently being studied at scale; however, more work is needed to fully understand the potential abilities of this technology. Critical research area: modeling to evaluate impact

Across air, land, and water, modeling is an essential tool to support benchmarking critical metrics across beef production and evaluating progress made across the beef industry over time. Several lifecycle assessment models have been performed to better understand environmental impacts across the beef industry, and these models support identifying hot spots where the industry has opportunity for advancement (Battagliese et al., 2013, 2015; Thoma et al., 2017; Tichenor et al., 2017; Asem-Hiablie et al., 2019; Rotz et al., 2019; Putman et al., 2023). Further work is necessary across air, land, and water models to help refine models and support effective decision-making at the operational level. Across all three indicator areas, opportunities exist for greater collaboration across research institutions and industry partnerships to promote more streamlined data collection that supports better-informed models and, by extension, more informed decision-making opportunities for producers and supply chain partners.

Air and greenhouse gas emissions

Comprehensive GHG emission measurements and updating current models to improve accuracy will allow the U.S. cattle industry to better characterize its baseline emissions and accurately quantify emissions reductions (Tedeschi et al., 2022). A target for emissions reductions cannot be set without first fully quantifying the baseline for GHG emission production. Updated models must be affordable, accurate, and utilize precise measurement technologies that scale from individual animal trials to whole operation assessments for monitoring, reporting, and verification (MRV) applications. A more mechanistic approach to modeling emissions, particularly enteric emissions, must be taken. Biological systems such as the rumen do not function linearly, and mechanistic models to better represent this system are needed. Furthermore, there is a pressing need for a framework that supports accurate assessment of emissions, accounting for the complexities of working in a biological system. To accomplish this, the scientific community will need to work cohesively in developing and testing new measurement technologies, proxy evaluations, frameworks, and models (Tricarico et al., 2022). Building national and/or regional consortia to share data and results and discuss experimental challenges may enable more timely advances in addressing modeling needs. Critical research area: genetic improvements

Genetic improvement is a long-term, permanent, and cumulative approach to mitigating air, land, and water impacts, particularly in grazing animals (Pickering et al., 2015). Genetic improvement is the product of using selection tools, such as expected progeny differences (EPDs) and selection indices, to identify animals with high genetic merit and deploy those genetics across the industry to drive improvements. Numerous examples of genetic selection driving improvements to production efficiency exist across the beef industry (Kuehn and Thallman, 2016; Rowan, 2022). At the heart of genetics and beef sustainability, one critical area of research is understanding maternal and terminal animal efficiency across diverse environments (Snelling et al., 2019). While genetic selection tools cover many profit and sustainability drivers, gaps exist, particularly in measuring efficiency traits in grazing contexts. Investments in phenotyping can support fine-tuning current selection tools for improved maternal and terminal efficiency as well as the development of novel genetic predictions (e.g., for methane emissions). Continued implementation of best practices like crossbreeding and using selection indexes will be essential for continued sustainability improvements. Critical Areas of Innovation and Action

Advancing sustainability in the cattle industry requires a multifaceted approach that addresses the social, economic, and environmental outcomes of beef production. Critical areas of innovation and action must be centered around supporting producers on the ground (Figure 4).

Critical areas of innovation and action for progress in the U.S. beef value chain.

Producer incentives

In some global contexts, sustainability outcomes are driven in part by compliance-based or punitive policy mechanisms. While such approaches may accelerate adoption in certain systems, their applicability within the diverse and highly heterogeneous U.S. beef sector remains uncertain. Incentives that encourage producers to adopt sustainable practices without compromising, and ideally enhancing, their profitability are a key area for innovation and action. These incentives can range from financial support to market access, helping bridge the gap between traditional practices and other innovative solutions. Equally important is strong advocacy for implementing these solutions, which fosters a supportive environment for change and highlights the benefits of sustainability for both producers and consumers. Ideally, the coupling of these two should be considered when encouraging the adoption of practices. As a result, producer support is a key target area for downstream supply chain partners to engage in more impactful ways (USRSB, 2022). Combined with collaborative efforts across the supply chain to support research needs identified herein, the opportunities for innovation and action can be amplified.

Technical support

Ensuring producer access to accurate information, resources, and ongoing support has the opportunity to empower producers to make informed decisions about adopting sustainable practices and management strategies. Producers are more likely to embrace change when they can clearly see the value proposition, both in terms of profitability and environmental stewardship. Access to technical assistance further ensures that producers can successfully implement new practices and technologies, especially when they determine that these changes offer tangible benefits to their operations. This research roadmap and the goals put forward by USRSB have the potential solutions to help close the gap in technical assistance and scientific discovery to help propel producers forward. The pressing need for technical support highlights a critical area for industry-wide collaboration to fund people and programs.

Trusted advisor networks

For decades, U.S. Extension agents and educators at land-grant universities have worked alongside producers and industry to help share and translate relevant university research. Strengthening the network of trusted technical advisors ensures that producers receive reliable, science-based guidance tailored to their specific needs. Expanding this advisor network is critical to meeting the growing demand for expertise in sustainable agriculture and fostering meaningful connections between research institutions and the farming and ranching communities. The research projects defined by this group of experts can help make this work possible.

Conclusion

The U.S. beef supply chain has prioritized sustainable production for decades and must remain steadfast in actively supporting future sustainability efforts. From processors through retailers and researchers to other partners linked to the beef supply chain, every stakeholder has a role in promoting responsible practices, creating market incentives, and ensuring that the entire value chain is aligned with supporting the critical research areas and key topics addressed with this roadmap. While this roadmap is grounded in science and producer practicality, its implementation will occur within evolving policy and market contexts. Regulatory frameworks, environmental reporting requirements, and incentive structures, whether voluntary or mandatory, may influence which research pathways are prioritized and how outcomes are operationalized. As such, this roadmap should be viewed as adaptive, with periodic reassessment needed to ensure alignment with policy developments and regional realities. Importantly, realizing the full value of this research roadmap will require parallel attention to data governance, trade-offs, policy context, and evolving incentive structures alongside scientific advancement.

By working collaboratively across sectors and providing producers with the tools, knowledge, and support they need, the U.S. cattle industry can make significant strides toward a continued and sustainable future. This roadmap serves as a testament to the power that collaboration has and how it can advance science for generations to come. This roadmap should be looked at with high regard by individuals with the ability and interest in supporting science-based advances in sustainable beef production.
