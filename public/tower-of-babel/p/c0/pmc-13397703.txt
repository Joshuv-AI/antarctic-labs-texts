Transcriptomic analysis of genes in bladder cancer patients with resistance to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy
Authors: Chunxiao Chen, Xincheng Liu, Abai Xu, Ning Jiang, Peng Wang, Yazhen Liu, Jianzhou Cheng, Wei Wang, Chunxiao Liu, Peng Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe tumor downstaging and pathological complete response of bladder cancer were found to be improved with the application of neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy. However, about 34.2% of patients with muscle-invasive bladder cancer were insensitive or unresponsive to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. Despite the diversity of biomarkers for therapeutic effect of neoadjuvant immunotherapy or chemotherapy, biomarkers to accurately evaluate the therapeutic effect and toxicity of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in bladder cancer are currently lacking. To identify potential genomic biomarkers for the responsiveness of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, we analyzed the genomic dynamic changes of patients with muscle-invasive bladder cancer.ResultsFive patients were sensitive to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy and were in the response group, while the other 5 patients were insensitive to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy and were in the resistance group. Before neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, the number of sequenced genes was higher in the response group than in the resistance group (P = 0.002), and this difference persisted at the end of chemoimmunotherapy (P = 0.014). After neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, 256 mRNAs were up-regulated and 394 mRNAs were down-regulated in the resistance group, while 773 mRNAs were up-regulated and 296 mRNAs were down-regulated in the response group. In addition, there were significant differences in mRNA expression between the response group and the resistance group before and after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. By comprehensively considering the dynamic changes of mRNA expression before and after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in the response group and the resistance group, 7 genes, including PTHLH and S100A2, which may be related to the resistance of chemoimmunotherapy in bladder cancer were finally screened. The results of GO and KEGG enrichment analysis showed that upregulated mRNAs in the resistance group were mainly related to sialyltransferase activity, regulation of ROS metabolic process and HIF-1 signaling pathway, while downregulated mRNAs were mainly involved in the regulation of NADH dehydrogenase activity and oxidative phosphorylation.ConclusionOur study is the first to identify molecules and pathways closely related to treatment resistance based on dynamic transcriptome changes before and after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in bladder cancer, and to find that genes such as PTHLH and S100A2 may be involved in the resistance of chemoimmunotherapy in bladder cancer.Keywords: Bladder cancer, Neoadjuvant therapy, Immunotherapy, Chemotherapy, Biomarker

Background

Bladder cancer, with approximately 210,000 deaths and 570,000 new cases each year, is the 10th most common malignancy in the world [1]. Muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) accounts for approximately 20% of newly diagnosed cases of bladder cancer [2]. Radical cystectomy with pelvic lymph node dissection remains the mainstay of treatment for MIBC. However, nearly 50% of patients ultimately develop metastatic bladder cancer after surgery [3]. Researches showed that downstaging of MIBC with neoadjuvant chemotherapy is conducive to improved survival [4]. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with immune checkpoint blockers for MIBC, with a pathological complete response ranging from 33% to 50%, was reported to result in enhanced antitumor efficacy [5]. However, approximately 34.2% of the patients with MIBC did not respond pathologically to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy [6]. Thus, it is of great significance to identify the molecules that affect the responsiveness of patients with MIBC to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy and their mechanism of action for improving the therapeutic effect of bladder cancer.

In order to distinguish patients with MIBC sensitive to neoadjuvant immunotherapy or neoadjuvant chemotherapy from those resistant to neoadjuvant therapy, a variety of biomarkers have been explored. For example, patients with DNA damage response (DDR) genomic alterations, including ERCC2, FANCA, BRCA1, BRCA2, and ATM, were more likely to achieve pathological complete response (pCR) from cisplatin-based neoadjuvant therapy [7–9]. Strong expression of ERCC1 and FGFR3 mutations were associated with pathologic response subsequent to neoadjuvant chemotherapy of bladder cancer [10]. As for predictive biomarkers of the response to neoadjuvant immunotherapy, neuronal subtype cancers with low expression of transforming growth factor-beta (TGFβ) and high mutation/neoantigen burden were more sensitive to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy [11]. In contrast, high levels of TGFβ are associated with immunotherapy unresponsiveness [12, 13]. However, despite the diversity of biomarkers for therapeutic effect of neoadjuvant immunotherapy or chemotherapy, validated biomarkers that can accurately evaluate the responsiveness of patients with MIBC to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy and spare non-responders from unnecessary toxicities are currently lacking.

In this study, transcriptomics was used to analyze the genomic dynamic changes before and after treatment in patients with MIBC who were sensitive or resistant to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, and to identify potential genomic biomarkers for the responsiveness of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy and molecules closely related to treatment resistance. The results can serve as a resource for further research to explore the therapeutic resistance mechanism of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, and also provide a new target for improving the efficacy of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in bladder cancer.

Methods

Study design and sample collection

The aim of this cross-sectional study was to identify potential genomic biomarkers for the responsiveness of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy and molecules closely related to treatment resistance. Pre-treatment and post-treatment cancerous samples were collected from MIBC patients admitted to Zhujiang Hospital in China between June 2021 and March 2022 who received three or four cycles of Toripalimab+Gemcitabine/Cisplatin and for whom adequate pre-treatment and post-treatment tissues were available. A 21-day cycle of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy consisting of Toripalimab, cisplatin and gemcitabine was administered to all patients in the study. Ten male patients with imaging and pathological diagnosis of MIBC were included in the cohort, in which 5 patients displayed a pathologic response (complete response: ypT0N0, n = 2; partial response: ypTa, ypT1 or ypTcis, n = 3) and were in the response group (group R), while the other 5 patients failed to respond (≥ ypT2) and were in the resistance group (group NR). Following the Medicine Institutional Review Board of Zhujiang Hospital of Southern Medical University approval (ethical code: 2024-KY-040-01), all participants gave written informed consent for their information and tissue collection with analysis for research purposes.

RNA extraction and sequencing

Total RNA from the tumor samples was extracted according to the manufacturer’s protocol using Trizol reagent kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). After RNA quality assessment and check, rRNAs were removed to retain mRNAs and ncRNAs. Then, the enriched ncRNAs and mRNAs were fragmented into short fragments and reversely transcribed into cDNA with random primers. Following the synthesis of the second-strand cDNA, the cDNA fragments were purified using QiaQuick PCR extraction kit (Qiagen, Venlo, The Netherlands), end repaired, poly(A) added, and ligated to Illumina sequencing adapters. Then the second-strand cDNA was digested with Uracil-N-Glycosylase. After size selection by agarose gel electrophoresis and PCR amplification, the digested products were sequenced using Illumina HiSeq™ 4000 by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. (Guangzhou, China).

Bioinformatics analysis

In order to obtain high-quality clean reads, the raw data from the sequencing machines were filtered by fastp [14] (version 0.18.0) to eliminate reads containing adapters, reads containing more than 10% of unknown nucleotides and reads containing more than 50% of low quality (Q value ≤ 20) bases. The clean reads were mapped to rRNA database with short reads alignment tool Bowtie2 [15] (version 2.2.8) for removing rRNA. After establishment of an index of the reference genome, HISAT2 [16] (version 2.1.0) was used for mapping paired-end clean reads to the reference genome with “-rna-strandness RF” and other parameters set as a default. To identify new genes and new splice variants of known ones, the transcripts were reconstructed with Stringtie [17, 18] (version 1.3.4) and HISAT2 [16] (version 2.1.0). Then, the reconstructed transcripts were aligned to reference genome and divided into twelve categories using Cuffcompare for identification of the new transcripts. Only the transcript longer than 200 bp with more than two exons was identified as reliable novel gene. To obtain protein functional annotation, novel transcripts were aligned to the Nr, KEGG, and GO database. Two softwares CNCI [19] (version 2) and CPC [20] (version 0.9-r2) (http://cpc.cbi.pku.edu.cn/) were used to evaluate the protein-coding potential of novel transcripts by default parameters. For each transcription region, a FPKM (fragment per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) value was calculated to quantify its expression abundance and variations using StringTie software. The DESeq2 [21] software was used to analyse the differential expression of coding RNAs between two different groups with the parameter of false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.05 and absolute fold change ≥ 2. Differentially expressed coding RNAs were then subjected to enrichment analysis of GO (Gene ontology) functions and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathways.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Shapiro-Wilk test was used to evaluate the normal distributions of measurement data, and Levene test for homoscedasticity. Data were represented by mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (upper and lower quartiles) for continuous variables, and n (%) for categorical variables. The Student’s t test was used for the comparison of measurement data obeying both normal distribution and homoscedasticity, while the differences between groups were tested using Mann-Whitney U test for measurement data that did not conform to normal distribution. Meanwhile, Fisher’s Exact Test was used for the statistical analysis of counting data. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sequencing data

A total of 1,703,298,994 clean reads were obtained from 20 samples after the removal of adapters, reads containing more than 10% of unknown nucleotides and reads containing more than 50% of low quality bases. Among them, 19,067 genes were detected. Before neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, the number of sequenced genes was higher in the R group than in the NR group (P = 0.002) and this difference persisted at the end of chemoimmunotherapy (P = 0.014) (Fig. 2A-B). However, there was no significant difference in the number of sequenced genes before and after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in either group R or group NR (Fig. 2C–D). The mRNA expression profile of all samples was displayed by violin plot (Fig. 2E).

Gene expression in group R and group NR before and after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy

Although the number of genes did not change significantly after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (Fig. 2C-D), some differentially expressed mRNAs appeared in both the response group and the resistance group after chemoimmunotherapy. After therapy, 256 mRNAs were up-regulated and 394 mRNAs were down-regulated in the resistance group, while 773 mRNAs were up-regulated and 296 mRNAs were down-regulated in the response group (Fig. 3A-B). In addition, there were significant differences in mRNA expression between the response group and the resistance group before neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (Fig. 3C), and significant differences in mRNA expression were also found between the two groups after chemoimmunotherapy (Fig. 3D).

Genes associated with resistance to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in bladder cancer

Although numerous differentially expressed genes were identified between the response and resistance groups before and after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (Fig. 4A), 53 genes were consistently upregulated and 91 genes consistently downregulated in the resistance group (Fig. 4B-C). When comparing the differentially expressed genes before and after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in the response and resistance groups, we found that the alteration of 21 genes was consistent between the two groups (8 genes down-regulated and 13 genes up-regulated, Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). Considering that the genes with consistent changes in the response group and the resistance group were less likely to affect the efficacy of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, these genes were eliminated from the 53 genes upregulated in the resistance group, and eventually, 7 genes that may be associated with chemoimmunotherapy resistance were screened out with 0.05 of FDR threshold (Table 2).

Gene ontology

The functions of differentially expressed genes between the response and resistance groups before neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, including biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions, were predicted by GO enrichment analysis. Compared with the response group, the up-regulated and down-regulated genes in resistance group enriched 40 and 39 functional items, respectively. The upregulated mRNAs in resistance group were mainly involved in biological processes (e.g., “regulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolic process” and “negative regulation of protein phosphorylation”), participated in the composition of cellular components (e.g., “plasma membrane” and “intrinsic component of plasma membrane”) and related to some molecular functions (e.g.,“sialyltransferase activity” and “calcium ion binding”) (Fig. 5A-B, Supplementary Fig. 2A), while the most significantly enriched GO terms of down-regulated mRNA in the biological process, cellular component, and molecular function categories were mitochondrial ATP synthesis coupled electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation, nucleosome, NADH dehydrogenase (ubiquinone and quinone) activity, respectively (Fig. 5C-D, Supplementary Fig. 2B).

KEGG pathway analysis

KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of differentially expressed genes between the response and resistance groups were shown in bubble charts. Before neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, compared to the response group, upregulated mRNAs in the resistance group were mainly associated with the HIF-1 signaling pathway, p53 signaling pathway, etc. (Fig. 6A), while downregulated mRNAs in the resistance group were mainly related to oxidative phosphorylation and immune-related diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (Fig. 6B). After neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, the upregulated mRNAs in the resistance group were mainly involved in oxidative phosphorylation, whereas focal adhesion and PI3K-Akt signaling pathway were the main enrichment pathways of downregulated mRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 3A-B).

Discussion

In this study, we have characterized the gene expression profile in patients with bladder cancer resistant to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy and the results showed that the number of sequenced genes was significantly higher in the response group. This suggests that the responders may exhibit higher gene expression. In addition to mutation/neoantigen burden serving as an indicator for evaluating the sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy [11], the number of sequenced genes may also be used as a preliminary indicator for assessing the sensitivity to chemoimmunotherapy. Meanwhile, despite insignificant change in the number of genes after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, some differentially expressed mRNAs appeared in both the response group and the resistance group after chemoimmunotherapy, and the expression of genes in the two groups was also significantly different before and after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. It is conceivable that the status of gene expression before treatment may affect the responsiveness of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, the alteration of gene expression during treatment may also be related to the efficacy of chemoimmunotherapy, and the drug resistance of tumors may also develop during this period.

At the mRNA level, genes PTHLH and S100A2 showed significantly higher expression in the resistance group than in the response group, implying that these two genes may be involved in the insensitivity or resistance to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. In a study by the Junkang Wang group, researchers found higher PTHLH levels in immunotherapy non‑responders and patients with stable or progressive disease, and PTHLH may function by inducing M2 macrophage polarization [22]. In contrast, Chenglin Yang and colleagues reported that upregulation of the CEP55 3’-UTR could lead to disinhibition of PTHLH and reduce cisplatin sensitivity in bladder cancer cell lines and xenograft tumors [23]. As for S100A2, it is a member of the S100 protein family, all of which share the EF‑hand motif present in calcium‑binding proteins. Chen Y et al. found that S100A2 occupied the most critical position in a risk model for survival prediction in pancreatic cancer, and was positively correlated with a lower proportion of CD8+ T cells and PDL1 expression in the tumor microenvironment, suggesting that S100A2 may contribute to the maintenance of an immunosuppressive state in pancreatic cancer [24]. However, in their study, patients with high S100A2 expression were more likely to benefit from immunotherapy, which was not fully consistent with our findings. This discrepancy may be due to the addition of chemotherapy affecting S100A2 function, or the opposing roles of S100A2 in different cancer types. In cervical cancer, S100A2 has been identified as an oncogene that promotes tumor proliferation and migration, and also acts as an immunosuppressive factor involved in regulating PDL1 expression [25]. Together with our results, these studies support the notion that PTHLH and S100A2 may be associated with responses to immunotherapy or chemotherapy and could serve as genomic signature biomarkers for predicting therapeutic responsiveness. However, the combination of immunotherapy and chemotherapy, as well as differences in cancer types, may lead these genes to exert opposite effects.

The GO and KEGG analyses provided evidence that the upregulated mRNAs in the resistance group were mainly related to sialyltransferase activity, regulation of ROS metabolic process, HIF‑1 signaling pathway, and so on. In vivo, Hong Y et al. found that ST3 β‑galactoside α‑2,3‑sialyltransferase 1 was a negative regulator of cancer‑specific migration of chimeric antigen receptor T cells, which induced spontaneous nonspecific tissue sequestration of T cells via glycosylation modification, thus conferring resistance to T cell immunotherapy in malignancies [26]. After confirming the immunosuppressive effect of intracellular sialyltransferase, researchers designed a core‑shell membrane‑fusogenic liposome to reprogram sialic acid‑induced immunosuppression on the tumor surface, thereby improving the immunotherapeutic effect and tumor elimination [27]. Combining these studies with ours, we speculated that sialyltransferase may be involved in resistance to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in bladder cancer and may also serve as a promising therapeutic target. Regulation of ROS metabolism was another process identified in our study that may influence the efficacy of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. In cisplatin‑resistant bladder cancer cells, lower ROS levels were found, and CAB39 played a critical role in reducing ROS levels. Meanwhile, the researchers knocked out CAB39 and found that cisplatin‑resistant bladder cancer cells became more sensitive to cisplatin [28]. Zhu Z et al. found that photodynamic therapy with porphyrin‑based PLZ4 nanoparticles generated ROS and induced protein carbonylation and dendritic cell maturation, thus synergizing with immunotherapy in the treatment of locally advanced and metastatic bladder cancer [29]. The HIF signaling pathway plays a crucial role in the etiology of several malignancies, and overexpression of HIFs has been proven to be an important cause of poor therapeutic responses in a wide range of cancers, including bladder cancer [30, 31]. Moreover, the addition of HIF inhibitors can reverse resistance, and a synergistic effect has been observed in combination with chemotherapy or immunotherapy in preclinical studies [30]. Consistent with these studies, our results also suggested that targeting the ROS metabolic process and HIF signaling pathways could provide a new rationale for overcoming tumor therapeutic resistance.

Both GO analysis and KEGG analysis showed that downregulated mRNAs in the resistance group were mainly involved in NADH dehydrogenase activity, mitochondrial ATP synthesis coupled electron transport, and oxidative phosphorylation. It is well known that NADH dehydrogenase activity and mitochondrial ATP synthesis coupled electron transport are closely related to oxidative phosphorylation. In pancreatic cancer, the conversion from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis inhibited antitumor immunity in various ways [32], whereas inhibition of PDK1 shifted metabolism from glycolysis to glucose oxidation, resulting in increased immune activation against cancer cells [33]. Salaroglio IC et al. reported that intermittent hypoxia impaired the electron transport chain and reduced oxidative phosphorylation, increasing the HIF‑1α/LAP complex in non‑small cell lung cancer and conferring chemo‑immunotherapy resistance [34]. In addition, aerobic glycolysis and suppressed oxidative phosphorylation were also metabolic characteristics of osteosarcoma with high LIN28B expression and crucial factors contributing to its chemoresistance. However, depletion of LIN28B not only restored oxidative phosphorylation function but also attenuated tumorigenesis and chemoresistance in osteosarcoma [35]. In our study, oxidative phosphorylation was mainly enriched by downregulated mRNAs in the resistance group, suggesting that suppressed oxidative phosphorylation may play an important role in the reduced sensitivity to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in bladder cancer.

Our study does have some limitations. Firstly, although this study identified potential RNA molecules and suspected pathways that may be associated with resistance to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in bladder cancer, further in vitro and in vivo studies are required to verify whether differences exist at the protein level and to confirm their exact roles. Secondly, the use of genomic alterations after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy may be more conducive to screening out the molecules that do participate in therapeutic resistance; however, the sample size of this study is small, and subsequent studies with a larger sample size may provide more stable results.

Conclusions

For the first time, based on dynamic transcriptome changes before and after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in bladder cancer, our study identified molecules and pathways that were closely related to treatment resistance. Genes like PTHLH and S100A2 were found to be potentially involved in chemoimmunotherapy resistance of bladder cancer. Further research on the exact roles of these two genes may offer new prospects for enhancing the efficacy of chemoimmunotherapy in bladder cancer.
