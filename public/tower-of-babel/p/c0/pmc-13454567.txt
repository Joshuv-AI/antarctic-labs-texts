Decoding the crosstalk between ubiquitination and other post-translational modifications in cancer immunity: from mechanisms to clinical prospects
Authors: Kai-Qiang Li, Si-Qi Ding, Yun Lei, Xiao Bai, Hao-Zhu Liu, Zhe-Ming Zhao, Dong-Qiu Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The tumor microenvironment is dynamically regulated by complex post-translational modifications (PTMs), which play pivotal roles in cancer immunity. Ubiquitination, along with other PTMs such as phosphorylation, glycosylation, and acetylation, orchestrates immune checkpoint activity and immune cell function, shaping antitumor responses. In this review, we discuss the intricate crosstalk and regulatory mechanisms between ubiquitination and other PTMs in cancer immunity. Studies have revealed that the stability and function of immune checkpoint proteins, such as PD-L1, are dynamically regulated by synergistic or competitive modifications, which directly shape the tumor microenvironment’s immunological characteristics. We highlight how PTMs regulate immune cell function (e.g., T cells, NK cells, and macrophages) and key signaling pathways (e.g., STAT, type I IFN, and NF-κB) in the TME. Furthermore, we summarize potential therapeutic strategies targeting these PTMs, including small-molecule inhibitors and novel technologies (e.g., PROTACs and cell-penetrating peptides), which offer insights into overcoming immunotherapy resistance and optimizing combination therapies. Future research should explore non-classical PTMs and leverage multi-omics approaches to refine precision immunotherapy strategies.

Introduction

Tumor immunotherapy is a therapeutic approach that elicitstumor-specific immune responses through active or passive mechanisms to inhibit and eliminate tumor cells1. Recent research advances indicate that immunotherapy is an effective treatment alongside conventional treatments, such as surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy. This emerging field encompasses several modalities, including adoptive cell therapies, immune checkpoint blockade, tumor vaccination strategies, and dual-targeting antibody constructs2. Different immunotherapy methods have different mechanisms of action. Immune checkpoint inhibitors such as programmed cell death 1 (PD-1)/programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors and cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4) inhibitors reactivate T cell-mediated antitumor immunity by blocking their signaling pathway3,4. Adoptive cell therapy, such as chimeric antigen receptor T-cell immunotherapy and T-cell receptor-engineered T-cell therapy, uses the patient’s immune cells by collecting, altering, and expanding them in vitro to recognize and kill tumor cells5. Tumor vaccines stimulate a specific immune response through the inoculation of tumor antigens to prevent or treat tumors6,7. In addition, cytokine therapies such as IL-2 and interferon-alpha (IFN-α) can enhance antitumor immunity by activating immune cells8,9. Among immunotherapy strategies, immune checkpoint inhibitors are the most widely used due to their significant clinical benefits. In 2011, after the positive results of phase Ⅲ clinical trials, the CTLA-4 antibody ipilimumab became the first immune checkpoint inhibitor approved by the US FDA for the treatment of advanced melanoma10. Since then, additional immunomodulatory targets such as PD-1/PD-L1, Natural Killer Group 2, Lymphocyte-activation gene 3, T cell immune receptor with Ig and ITIM domains, and other targets have been discovered and confirmed to have important therapeutic value11. The ultimate efficacy of immunotherapy is affected by multiple factors, including immunosuppressive properties that impact the tumor microenvironment (TME), abnormal T-cell function, defects in tumor antigenicity and antigen presentation, and host-related factors12,13.

Post-translational modifications (PTMs) consist of the chemical modification of translated amino acid residues through the addition or removal of specific groups, which regulate protein activity, localization, and interactions between proteins and other biological macromolecules, and ensure the dynamic balance of physiological processes14,15. Approximately 400 types of PTMs have been described, including classical modifications such as ubiquitination, phosphorylation, glycosylation, methylation, acetylation, and lipidation14,16. During tumorigenesis and development, these modifications are widely involved in the regulation of key cellular processes, such as DNA repair mechanisms17, cellular signaling18, metabolic alterations19,20, blood vessel formation, cell cycle progression, epithelial–mesenchymal transition-mediated invasion, autophagic processes, and programmed cell death16,21,22.

Crosstalk between PTMs is related to competition or co-regulation at the same site or between different sites of the same protein, as well as the co-regulation of PTMs between different proteins. In a single substrate, different PTMs can have different effects when acting simultaneously or sequentially23–26. PTM interactions can be categorized as positive or negative. Positive crosstalk occurs when one PTM actively induces or removes another modification or serves as a binding platform for protein recognition. Negative crosstalk involves either direct competition for modification sites or indirect interference, in which one PTM blocks access to the target site of another PTM25,27.

PTMs can affect the efficacy of tumor immunotherapy through multiple mechanisms. First, PTMs can affect the secretion of cytokines/chemokines by regulating related signaling pathways, thereby changing the activation state of immune cells (including T cells, B cells, natural killer cells (NK), and macrophages) and their functions in the TME28–30. Second, PTMs regulate the expression and function of immune checkpoint (such as PD-1 and CTLA-4), thereby modulating the anti-tumor immune response of T cells31,32. Third, PTMs can mediate immune escape by masking tumor-associated antigens, thereby affecting the immune system’s ability to recognize and eliminate tumor cells33. The crosstalk between PTMs also affects tumor immune regulation. For example, phosphorylation and acetylation crosstalk can regulate the dynamic expression of PD-L1 and alter the infiltration of immune cells in the TME34,35. Therefore, understanding the role of PTM crosstalk in tumor immune escape can provide new ideas for immunotherapy strategies targeting multiple PTMs.

Overview of ubiquitination

Ubiquitination is a classic PTM that regulates the stability and activity of proteins. It frequently functions in a synergistic network with other PTMs such as phosphorylation, acetylation, and glycosylation to participate in various biological functions in cancer36,37. Ubiquitination is a cascade reaction involving an E1 ubiquitin-activating enzyme, an E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme, and an E3 ubiquitin ligase, in which ubiquitin is covalently attached to target proteins. ATP is required for ubiquitin activation by the E1 enzyme. It regulates the stability, localization, activity, or interaction of target proteins and then affects a variety of cellular functions38,39. The ubiquitin protein contains seven lysine residues (K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, and K63)38, and different lysine sites are involved in different effects of ubiquitination. For example, K48-linked chains target substrates for proteasomal degradation40,41, whereas K63-linked chains mediate non-degradative functions42,43. Some other connection structures (K6, K11, K27, K29, K33) also play specific roles in cells, such as chemotherapy resistance in cancer, cell cycle regulation, and innate immune signal transduction, etc44–47. Ubiquitination critically influences tumor immunity by modulating immune cell function, antigen presentation, the stability of immune checkpoint molecules, and inflammatory signaling pathways within the TME39,48.

Based on the above research background, this paper systematically reviews the dual mechanism of dynamic crosstalk between ubiquitination and other PTMs in tumor immune regulation. To further elucidate this mechanism, this paper takes PD-L1 as the core example and focuses on how ubiquitination and multiple PTMs synergically regulate its expression and function, thus driving cancer immune escape. At the same time, it will also be extended to other key immune checkpoint proteins, such as Cluster of Differentiation 47 (CD47) and PD-1, to reveal the universal regulatory pattern of PTM crosstalk. This regulatory network not only acts on immune checkpoint proteins but also widely influences the functions of immune cells and key signaling pathways to regulate tumor immune responses. At the mechanism level, the interaction between classical PTMs, such as phosphorylation and acetylation, and ubiquitination has established a relatively clear regulatory framework, forming a solid foundation for targeted therapy. In contrast, crosstalk between emerging PTMs like lactylation and succinylation and ubiquitination presents novel areas for exploration, with their functions and mechanisms still undergoing continuous elucidation. Through in-depth analysis of these molecular interaction networks, we aim to provide a theoretical basis and potential intervention targets for the development of novel combined immunotherapy strategies based on the regulation of PTMs.

PTMs mediate cancer immune evasion through multiple regulatory mechanisms, and modulating the stability of checkpoint molecules is a core one49–51. Ubiquitination, which regulates protein degradation and is a key mechanism for protein quality control, forms a complex interaction network with various PTMs, including phosphorylation, acetylation, and glycosylation. Phosphorylation: bidirectional regulation of ubiquitin ligase recognition of immune checkpoints

Phosphorylation is the transfer of the phosphate group in ATP to specific amino acid residues of a target protein through kinases. Phosphorylation is catalyzed by kinases, which recognize specific substrate proteins and phosphorylation sites, the most common of which are serine (S), threonine (T), or tyrosine (Y) residues. Phosphorylation is classified as O-phosphorylation, N-phosphorylation, and acyl-phosphorylation according to the position of the phosphate group attached to the amino acid52,53. Protein kinases can regulate the recognition of immune checkpoint proteins by E3 ubiquitin ligases through phosphorylation, showing a bidirectional regulatory effect on the ubiquitination and degradation of immune checkpoints, thus affecting tumor immune escape.

Protein kinases directly phosphorylate key sites of immune checkpoints, blocking their recognition and degradation by ubiquitin ligases. This maintains the stability of immune checkpoints, inhibits immune signaling, and weakens T cell activation and cytotoxic functions, ultimately promoting tumor immune escape. For example, Never-in-mitosis A-related kinase 2‌‌ and leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2) phosphorylate the Thr194/Thr210 site of PD-L1, thereby preventing its ubiquitin-proteasome pathway-mediated degradation and stabilizing the PD-L1 protein, which promotes immune escape54,55. Sun et al. found that IFN-γ secreted by CD8+ T cells upregulates LRRK2 expression in tumor cells, forming a positive feedback loop that increases PD-L1-mediated immunosuppression54. In another study, casein kinase 2 (CK2) phosphorylates PD-L1 at Thr285/Thr290 to disrupt the binding of PD-L1 to the aptamer SPOP in the E3 ubiquitin ligase, thereby inhibiting the CUL3-SPOP complex-mediated PD-L1 ubiquitination degradation56. Activated epidermal growth factor receptor recruits c-Src kinase to phosphorylate the Tyr288 site of immune checkpoint CD47, thereby blocking the ubiquitination of CD47 at Lys99/Lys102 mediated by the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM21 and its degradation, stabilizing CD47 expression. The high expression of CD47 inhibits phagocytosis and promotes immune escape by interacting with the macrophage surface protein SIRPα57.

In addition to directly phosphorylating immune checkpoint proteins, protein kinases can also phosphorylate other proteins, thereby crosstalk with the ubiquitination system to indirectly maintain immune checkpoint stability. Rho-associated protein kinase phosphorylates the Thr558 residue of moesin, which induces a conformational change that enhances PD-L1 binding. This binding competitively blocks the recognition of PD-L1 by the E3 ubiquitin ligase SPOP, thus inhibiting the ubiquitination and degradation of PD-L1 and stabilizing its protein expression58. Elevated PD-L1 suppresses T cell function and facilitates tumor immune evasion through interaction with T cell surface PD-158. Liu et al. found that CK2 phosphorylates Inhibitor of Growth 4‌‌ at Ser150, resulting in its recognition by the E3 ubiquitin ligase JFK and its degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Since inhibitor of growth family member 4 itself degrades PD-L1 by activating the autophagy pathway, its degradation leads to a decrease in PD-L1 clearance, which ultimately maintains PD-L1 expression and promotes immune escape59.

Phosphorylation can also exert the opposite effect by promoting ubiquitination and degradation of PD-1/PD-L1, thereby enhancing antitumor immune responses rather than facilitating immune escape. Cyclin Dependent Kinase 1 (CDK1) phosphorylates PD-1 at Ser261 to promote its nuclear translocation and binding to FBW7. FBW7 mediates the K48-linked polyubiquitination of PD-1 at Lys233, leading to its degradation60. Wang et al. also found that GSK3α directly phosphorylated the Ser279 and Ser283 sites of the intracellular segment of PD-L1, and recruited the E3 ligase ARIH1 to label PD-L1 for ubiquitin degradation by K48-linked polyubiquitin chain61. Glycosylation: masking ubiquitination sites to stabilize immune checkpoint molecules

Glycosylation is an important PTM consisting of the covalent attachment of sugars (carbohydrates) to protein or lipid molecules. Glycosylation is classified into different types according to the type of attachment of the sugar chain to the target molecule: N-glycosylation, O-glycosylation, C-glycosylation, and glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchoring (GPI anchoring)63–65. In tumors, aberrant glycosylation alters the structure of cell surface glycan chains, which affects signaling, cell adhesion, immune recognition, and microenvironment regulation63–65. Glycosylation regulates the stability of immune checkpoint molecules primarily by masking their ubiquitination sites. This modification inhibits ubiquitin-mediated degradation, thereby stabilizing immune checkpoint expression and promoting tumor immune escape.

STT3 oligosaccharyl transferase complex catalytic subunit A (STT3A) acts as the catalytic subunit of the oligosaccharide transferase complex, catalyzing N-glycosylation of immune checkpoint molecules65,66. Transmembrane and ubiquitin-like domain-containing protein 1 recruits STT3A to promote PD-L1 N-glycosylation, thereby inhibiting the E3 ubiquitin ligase HUWE1 from catalyzing the ubiquitination degradation of PD-L1 at the K281 site67. Similarly, Polypeptide N-acetylgalactosaminyl transferase 6 promotes N-glycosylation of PD-L1, thereby inhibiting its degradation by the proteasome and facilitating immune escape68. The RNA-binding motif single-stranded interacting protein 1 enhances PD-L1 glycosylation and blocks its ubiquitin-mediated degradation by up-regulating glycosyltransferase β-1, 4-galactosyltransferase 169. In addition, N192/N200/N219 glycosylation of PD-L1 masks the binding site of glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β) phosphorylation and avoids recruitment of E3 ubiquitin ligase β-TrCP-mediated ubiquitin degradation70,71. Notably, Wang et al. found that atypical cadherin 4 promotes the formation of the degradation transcription factor β-catenin by promoting the degradation complex (AXIN1/APC/GSK3β/CKI). Moreover, both PD-L1 and STT3A expression levels were downregulated, forming a negative feedback regulation71.

Beyond PD-L1, glycosylation of additional immune checkpoints, including B7-H4 and PD-1/PD-L2 significantly contributes to tumor immune evasion72–74. As a member of the B7 family, N-glycosylation of B7-H4 catalyzed by the oligosaccharide transferase complex and UDP-glucose glycoprotein glucosyltransferase 1 blocks the K138/K146 ubiquitination site and inhibits Autocrine Motility Factor Receptor-mediated degradation, thereby stabilizing protein expression and promoting immune escape75. B7-H4 also suppresses immunogenic cell death signals, such as CALR and HSP70/90, by inhibiting the PRKR-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase/elF2α/CALR axis, weakening the antitumor function of DCs and T cells75. Similarly, PD-1 glycosylation can decrease the immunological response by preventing MDM2-mediated ubiquitination and degradation at K78. However, glycosidase N-Glycanase 1 deglycosylation activity reverses this process and mediates a dynamic regulation process76. The glycosylation of PD-L2 at N64/N157/N163/N189 by α-1, 6-fucosyltransferase (FUT8) inhibits its ubiquitination degradation and enhances its binding to PD-1 to inhibit T cell activity synergistically. Furthermore, the interaction between glycosylated PD-L2 and Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR) activates the EGF/EGFR signaling pathway, leading to cetuximab resistance. Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3) can further improve this glycosylation alteration by up-regulating FUT877. Meanwhile, FUT8-glycosylated CD47 inhibits the degradation of E3 ubiquitin ligase SMURF1, thereby promoting immune evasion. Conversely, inhibiting CD47 glycosylation enhances the infiltration of DCs and recruitment of NK, ultimately suppressing tumor growth78.

Acetylation/deacetylation: epigenetic regulation and post-translational crosstalk with ubiquitination

Acetylation is a PTM process of proteins, catalyzed by Acetyltransferases, through the transfer of an acetyl group (–COCH₃) to the target proteins (e.g., the ε-amino group of lysine)79,80. According to the type of substrate, it can be divided into two groups: histone acetylation mainly occurs on lysine residues of histone proteins and regulates gene expression by changing chromatin structure and non-histone acetylation modulates the activity of transcription factors (such as FOXO3a), signaling molecules and metabolic enzymes, contributing to the regulation of cell function79. Deacetylation occurs when deacetylases (HDACs/SIRTs) catalyze the removal of acetyl groups from lysine residues in proteins80.

Crosstalk between acetylation and ubiquitination mainly regulates the transcription level of immune checkpoint molecules by modulating the stability of transcription factors, thereby affecting the expression of immune checkpoints and promoting tumor immune escape. Qiu et al. found that in drug-resistant tumor cells, downregulation of the E3 ubiquitin ligase COP1 reduced ubiquitination and degradation of the transcription factor c-Jun, whereas JNK phosphorylation activates c-Jun. The accumulated c-Jun in the nucleus inhibits the expression of the deacetylase HDAC3, leading to an increase in histone H3 acetylation, which in turn activates PD-L1 transcription81. Elevated PD-L1 levels suppress CD3⁺ T cell activity, thereby promoting immune evasion81. FOXO3a acts as a transcriptional repressor of PD-L1, and its degradation reverses the inhibition of PD-L1 expression82. Studies have reported that the deacetylase SIRT1 deacetylates FOXO3a, promoting its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. Reduced FOXO3a levels increase PD-L1 expression and suppress cytotoxic T cell function83. Estradiol acts through estrogen receptor β to upregulate SIRT1, and inhibition of either molecule reverses PD-L1 upregulation83. Collectively, the interplay between acetylation and ubiquitination is a promising area for understanding immune checkpoint regulation. However, current evidence is limited. Future studies should validate these pathways in various experimental models and clinical samples to confirm their potential as therapeutic targets.

Ubiquitin-like modifications (SUMOylation): synergistic control of immune checkpoint expression

Ubiquitin-like modifications, which involve the covalent attachment of ubiquitin-like proteins to target proteins, include SUMOylation, NEDDylation, and ISGylation24,84–86. Current studies in cancer immunity have reported the interaction between ubiquitination and SUMOylation. To date, only crosstalk between ubiquitination and SUMOylation has been reported in cancer immunity87,88, representing a current research limitation in this field.

Ubiquitination and SUMOylation synergistically control PD-L1 levels to facilitate tumor immune evasion. The E3 ligase RanBP2 catalyzes the SuMOylation of IL-33 at Lys54, and modified IL-33 cannot bind to TRAF6 to promote the ubiquitination and degradation of Interferon Regulatory Factor 1 (IRF1)87. Stabilized IRF1 subsequently upregulates PD-L1 expression, impairing the immune surveillance capacity of T cells. Notably, the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM28 exerts a dual regulatory role in PD-L1 upregulation88: it promotes PD-L1 SUMO2 modification to inhibit its K48- and K63-linked polyubiquitination and degradation, and simultaneously catalyzes K63-linked polyubiquitination of TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1), which activates the TBK1/IRF1 and TBK1/MTOR signaling pathways to further enhance PD-L1 expression at both the transcriptional and post-translational levels.

Palmitoylation: competitive modification to inhibit ubiquitin-mediated degradation

Palmitoylation is a vital lipid modification that covalently attaches palmitic acid to specific amino acid residues in proteins via different chemical bonds. It is mainly divided into three types: S-palmitoylation linked to the internal cysteine (forming a thioester bond), O-palmitoylation linked to serine/threonine (forming an oxylester bond), and N-palmitoylation linked to the N-terminal cysteine (forming an amide bond)89. Among them, S-palmitoylation is the most widely studied reversible palmitoylation modification, in which the palmitoyl group (C16 fatty acid) is covalently linked to cysteine residues by DHHC family enzymes to regulate protein localization, stability, and function90,91.

T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 3 (TIM-3) has an essential role in the immune response as an immune checkpoint92. The regulation of TIM-3 by PTMs has not been studied extensively. TIM-3 is palmitoylated at cysteine residue 296 (Cys296) by the DHHC9, which prevents the binding of TIM-3 to the E3 ubiquitin ligase HRD1, this inhibits its polyubiquitination and degradation, leading to immune depletion and impaired anti-tumor immunity93. Similarly, palmitoylation of PD-L1 by the DHHC3 inhibited its degradation by the proteasome94. These findings suggest that dynamic crosstalk between palmitoylation and ubiquitination is essential for immune checkpoint regulation: palmitoylation inhibits ubiquitination through steric hindrance or competitive modification, thereby stabilizing target proteins.

Succinylation competes with ubiquitination for modification sites

Succinylation is a PTM in which succinyl-CoA is attached to lysine residues in a substrate protein under the action of succinyltransferase95–97. Known succinyltransferases include carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex, lysine acyltransferase 2A, and histone acetyltransferase. Succinylation modifications can be divided into two categories: histone succinylation and non-histone succinylation95–97.

Among succinyl-CoA transferases, 3-Oxoacid CoA Transferase 1 (OXCT1) exhibits a unique dual function. It acts as a key rate-limiting enzyme in ketone body catabolism, catalyzing the transfer of the CoA group from succinyl-CoA to acetoacetate to form acetyl-CoA98. However, OXCT1 also has succinyl transferase activity and can catalyze succinylation modification of non-histones99. OXCT1 is involved in immune regulation through its succinylation activity, which leads to the exhaustion of CD8+ T cells and immunosuppressive changes in the immune microenvironment38. Crosstalk between succinylation and other PTMs, such as phosphorylation, promotes tumor progression100. At the molecular level, the K146 residue of phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK1) is a common site for both succinylation and ubiquitination101. OXCT1-mediated succinylation at K146 competitively inhibits E3 ubiquitin ligase recognition and proteasomal degradation of PGK1. Stabilized PGK1 promotes glycolytic reprogramming and PD-L1 upregulation, thereby suppressing T cell cytotoxicity and facilitating tumor immune escape101. This PTM crosstalk is currently supported by only a few studies, representing a limitation that requires further validation.

Lactylation: novel metabolite-driven inhibition of ubiquitin degradation

Lactylation is a novel PTM that was first reported in 2019102. This modification is unique in its dependence on the metabolite lactate, achieved by esterification reactions between lactate and protein lysine residues. Specifically, the lactate molecule covalently binds to the ε-amino group of lysine by forming an ester bond, thereby producing a lactate modification103–105. Similar to other PTMs processes, lactylation is a dynamic and reversible process. It is regulated by specific enzymes that both catalyze the addition of lactate groups and mediate their removal. Lactylation is classified into two types according to substrate protein: histone lactylation and non-histone lactylation103–105. As a metabolite-driven PTM, lactylation is closely related to the metabolic characteristics of tumor cells, and its crosstalk with ubiquitination directly regulates the stability of immune checkpoint proteins.

Lactylation can affect anti-tumor immune response by regulating the expression of the immune checkpoint molecule PD-L1 and changing the composition of immune cells in the TME106–109. For instance, STAT5 drives PD-L1 transcription by promoting histone lactylation109. It is worth noting that there is also a complex crosstalk mechanism between lactylation and ubiquitination, which jointly regulate the disease process. For instance, in the development of cancer, the interaction of these two modifications can promote tumor progression110. In autoimmune diseases, lactylation inhibits the ubiquitination and degradation of the cGAS protein, thereby enhancing the activity of the cGAS–STING signaling pathway and further regulating the immune response111. Notably, this lactylation-ubiquitination crosstalk directly controls the stability of PD-L1: lactate within the TME drives PD-L1 lactylation at lysine 280 via the lactyltransferase AARS1. This modification blocks HUWE1 binding to PD-L1, reducing its ubiquitination and degradation, thus stabilizing PD-L1 and promoting tumor immune evasion112. In line with this, histone lactylation upregulates MVP, which competitively interacts with β‑TrCP to inhibit PD‑L1 ubiquitination and promote its stability in HCC113.

As critical signaling mediators, cell surface molecules undergo dynamic changes to regulate immune cell functions. Within the TME, the functional status of these immune cells directly dictates the effectiveness of anti-tumor immune responses114,115. Crosstalk between ubiquitination and other PTMs in regulating immune cells and the tumor microenvironment

Regulation of immune-promoting cells

Immune-promoting cells, such as cytotoxic T cells (CD8⁺ T cells), recognize and kill cells expressing tumor-specific antigens, whereas DCs ingest tumor antigens and present them to T cells to initiate an adaptive immune response116–118. The crosstalk between ubiquitination and other PTMs mainly regulates the activation, infiltration and antigen presentation ability of immune-promoting cells. Phosphorylation-ubiquitination crosstalk serves as the core regulatory mechanism, and glycosylation and palmitoylation play a secondary role by modulating the stability of immune receptors.

The CD155-CD226 axis is a key positive regulatory pathway for the activation of NK cells and CD8⁺ T cells119–121. Under physiological conditions, CD155, a single-pass transmembrane protein widely expressed on the cell surface, binds to the activating receptor CD226 on immune cells, thereby promoting the activation of NK cells and CD8⁺ T cells and initiating antitumor immune responses119–121. However, tumor cells can disrupt this protective signal through different mechanisms. For example, high expression of CD155 in tumor cells can activate Src tyrosine protein kinase, which leads to the phosphorylation of the Tyr319 site of CD226. This modification results in the recruitment of the E3 ubiquitin ligase CBL-B, which catalyzes the K48-linked polyubiquitination of CD226 and its proteasomal degradation, ultimately inhibiting CD8⁺ T cell activation122.

The phosphorylation-ubiquitination crosstalk mediated by the CDK family has become another key pathway for regulating the infiltration of immune cells. CDK9 phosphorylates the C-terminal domain of lysine-specific demethylase 1 (LSD1), facilitating its recognition by E3 ligase RNF20. Subsequently, RNF20 mediates K29-linked polyubiquitination of LSD1, enhancing its stability without inducing degradation. High expression of LSD1 inhibits the secretion of T cell chemokines through the epigenetic regulation mechanism, which eventually decreases CD8+ T cell infiltration in the TME and promotes immune escape123. Meanwhile, CDK5 phosphorylates PPARγ at Ser273, inhibiting its E3 ligase activity. This prevents PPARγ from mediating ubiquitination and degradation of ESRP1, whose accumulation regulates CD44 alternative splicing, inhibits cytotoxic T lymphocyte and M1 macrophage infiltration, and fosters an immunosuppressive microenvironment124.

Ubiquitination-phosphorylation crosstalk further suppresses immune-promoting cell functions at the levels of intracellular transcriptional regulation and mitochondrial metabolism. Non-classical IκB kinase family member IKKε phosphorylates interferon regulatory factor 1 (IRF-1) at C-terminal sites (Ser215/Ser219/Ser221), inducing its K48-linked polyubiquitination and degradation. The degradation of IRF-1 leads to the loss of its transcriptional activity, which weakens its promoting effect on T cell activation, thereby inhibiting anti-tumor immune response125. Additionally, E3 ubiquitin ligase PJA1 catalyzes the K48-linked ubiquitination of phosphoglycerate mutase 5 (PGAM5) (Lys88), targeting it for proteasomal degradation. PGAM5 depletion causes persistent DRP1 Ser637 phosphorylation, suppressing mitochondrial fission and reactive oxygen species production. This process reduces the release of pyroptosis-related damage molecules (such as High Mobility Group Box-1 protein and calreticulin), weakens the antigen presentation ability of DCs and the activation of CD8⁺ T cells, ultimately impairing the anti-tumor immune response126.

Besides crosstalk between ubiquitination and phosphorylation, glycosylation and palmitoylation also regulate immune-promoting cells through different pathways. Glycosylation can work with ubiquitination to negatively regulate immune responses. The 4-1BB receptor pathway is the most typical example. 4-1BB (CD137/TNFRSF9), a member of the tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily, is a type I transmembrane protein expressed mainly on the surface of activated T cells that acts as a central regulator of the anti-tumor immune response127. Studies have shown that glycosylation of 4-1BB at N138 and N149 promotes the specific recognition of its intracellular domain by the E3 ubiquitin ligase FBXL20. This leads to K48-linked polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of 4-1BB. This negative regulatory mechanism may suppress T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity to some extent128. However, this regulatory pathway is mainly supported by a single study. Further studies are required to confirm this crosstalk between glycosylation and ubiquitination of the immune cells.

Palmitoylation affects immune cell infiltration by regulating the stability of membrane proteins and signaling proteins129,130, and shows a bidirectional regulatory effect on the function of immune-promoting cells. DHHC2 competes with the E3 ubiquitin ligase KEAP1 for binding to the C51 site of Nrf2, and palmitoylation of Nrf2 blocks its ubiquitination and degradation to maintain protein stability, thereby reducing CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and promoting immune escape131. In addition, ZDHHC20 catalyzes the palmitoylation of CD80 at four cysteine residues in its transmembrane (Cys261/262) and cytoplasmic (Cys266/271) domains, which inhibits proteasomal degradation and increases the membrane localization and stability of CD80. Thus, the CD80/CD28 costimulatory signal is further enhanced to promote T cell activation and immune responses132 (Fig. 3b).

Regulation of immunosuppressive cells

Immunosuppressive Cells, such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), are a heterogeneous population differentiated from neutrophils and monocytes under pathological conditions133. They exert critical immunosuppressive effects, including suppressing T-cell and NK-cell function, and promote angiogenesis and metastasis117,134,135. Phosphorylation–ubiquitination crosstalk regulates the differentiation of Tregs. Phosphorylation of TGF-β T282 by PKA enhances its binding to CHIP, leading to K63-linked ubiquitination of TGF-β at Lys315. The reduction of TGF-β directly inhibits the differentiation and activation of regulatory T cells in the TME, releases its immunosuppression on CD8+ T cells, and enhances anti-tumor immune responses136.

Ubiquitination crosstalk with lactylation and acetylation drives imbalanced macrophage polarization. As highly plastic immune cells, macrophage phenotypes and functions are modulated by the TME, noncoding RNAs, and genes, with M1 macrophages inhibiting and M2 macrophages promoting immune escape137. N-myc downstream-regulated gene 1 directly binds lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) and inhibits its K76 ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby stabilizing LDHA. Elevated LDHA enhances glycolysis and lactate production, which induces H3K18 lactylation. This remodels the tumor immune microenvironment by increasing M2 macrophages and reducing CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, promoting immune escape138. Furthermore, SUMO-modified IL-33 promotes M2 macrophage polarization via the IL-8/CXCR1/2 pathway, further suppressing cytotoxic T cell function and reinforcing an immunosuppressive microenvironment87.

These modifications jointly shape the immunosuppressive microenvironment by regulating the activity of key proteins. Specifically, it inhibits the activation of T cells and NK cells, reduces the infiltration of cytotoxic T cells, and weakens the antigen presentation function, which provides new targets and intervention strategies for tumor immunotherapy.

JAK–STAT signaling pathway

The JAK–STAT signaling pathway is an intracellular signal transduction mechanism. It mainly consists of Janus kinases, including JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, and TYK2, and signal transducers and activators of transcription, including STAT1, STAT2, STAT3, STAT4, STAT5A, STAT5B, and STAT6139,140. STAT3 is activated by ubiquitination to inhibit tumor immune responses, thus playing an indispensable role in cancer immunity141–143.

As a key PTM crosstalk mechanism that tightly modulates the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, the interplay between ubiquitination and phosphorylation exerts dominant regulatory control over this pathway and mediates downstream tumor immunosuppressive phenotypes. The E3 ubiquitin ligase TRAF6 catalyzes the K63-linked polyubiquitination of STAT3 through a non- degradative pathway, which promotes the phosphorylation of STAT3 at Tyr705. The activated STAT3 signaling pathway upregulates arginase 1 and inducible nitric oxide synthase in MDSCs, promoting the immunosuppressive functions144. Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) is a pattern recognition receptor that belongs to the TLR family and has dual roles in tumor immunity145. TLR9 activation inhibits PAR hydrolase expression and enhances PARylation (poly-ADP-ribosylation) of PARP1, which in turn promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of PARP1 mediated by the E3 ubiquitin ligase RNF146146. Upon ubiquitination-mediated degradation of PARP1, its inhibition of STAT3 Tyr705 phosphorylation is relieved, leading to STAT3 activation and subsequent PD-L1 transcription, inducing tumor immune evasion146. Similarly, TREM2 binds to the SH2 domain of STAT1 to competitively inhibit TRIM21-mediated ubiquitination and degradation, enhancing STAT1 stability. TREM2 also recruits SYK via DAP12 to promote STAT1 Tyr701 phosphorylation. These two events synergistically enhance STAT1 transcriptional activity, upregulate PD-L1 expression, and promote immune evasion147.

In addition to crosstalk between ubiquitination and phosphorylation, ubiquitination and acetylation also mediate changes in the TME through the STAT signaling pathway, promoting immune escape. Studies have shown that abnormal acetyltransferase activity can contribute to immune dysregulation in the TME148,149. In the TME, IL-4 activates STAT6, which inhibits TRIM24 transcription, reduces CBP’s K63-linked ubiquitination and STAT6 acetylation, promotes M2 macrophage polarization and immune escape150. Another key acetyltransferase, P300, acetylates TRIB3 at Lys240 to block its ubiquitination by SIAH1, maintaining TRIB3 stability and activating the EGFR-STAT3 signaling pathway, which further inhibits STAT1 expression151. As a major transcriptional regulator of CXCL10, a chemokine that recruits CD8+ T cells152, the inhibited STAT1 reduces CXCL10 secretion, hindering CD8+ T cell recruitment and suppressing the immune response151.

Type I interferon signaling pathway

The IFN-I signaling is a signal transduction mechanism mediated by cytokines (IFN-α/β), pattern recognition receptors (cGAS, RIG-I), receptor subunits (IFNAR1/IFNAR2), kinases (JAK1, TYK2), and transcription factors (STAT1, STAT2, IRF9, and IRFs)9,153. IFN-I signaling activation enhances immune effector (NK, DCs, CD8+ T) cell infiltration into the TME, thereby strengthening anticancer responses154. Notably, ubiquitination-mediated degradation of key recognition receptors suppresses antitumor immunity155. The crosstalk between ubiquitination and other PTMs on the IFN-I pathway shows two opposite regulatory effects. Palmitoylation-ubiquitination crosstalk positively regulates the pathway activity and enhances anti-tumor immunity. Carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A recruits ER-localized palmitoyltransferase ZDHHC4 and catalyzes the palmitoylation of mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein at Cys79. This inhibits K48-linked ubiquitination and promotes K63-linked ubiquitination, thereby increasing the stability and activity of mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein and promoting IFN-α signaling and CD8+ T cell infiltration156.

However, this pathway can be negatively regulated by the interplay between ubiquitination and phosphorylation through the following mechanisms. The β-subunit of the IGF-1 receptor phosphorylates β‑arrestin‑2 at Tyr64 and Tyr250, enabling its interaction with the E3 ligase MEX3A157. MEX3A then mediates K48‑linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of RIG‑I, suppressing the RIG‑I/MAVS/IFN‑I axis, reducing IFN‑I production, and inhibiting effector immune cell infiltration in the TME157. Additionally, the E3 ubiquitin ligase UBR7 blocks the phosphorylation of interferon regulatory factor 7 and inhibits IFN-β and its downstream interferon-stimulated genes by ubiquitinating key sites (Lys472, Lys487, Lys507) of the Speckled family protein Sp110; this results in the inhibition of the IFN-β signaling pathway and promotes tumor immune escape158.

NF-κB signaling pathway

The NF-κB signaling pathway is activated by extracellular stimuli and subsequently regulates the transcription of downstream target genes. Its core components include the NF-κB transcription factor family (p65/RelA, RelB, c-Rel, p50, p52), the inhibitory κB proteins (IκB), and the IκB kinase (IKK) complex composed of IKKα, IKKβ, and the regulatory subunit NEMO (IKKγ)159,160. Studies have shown that ubiquitination of the IKKα/β subunits161 and deacetylation of the p65162 mediate the expression of immune checkpoint molecules, thereby suppressing the cancer immune response. Only ubiquitination and phosphorylation crosstalk regulate the NF-κB pathway to promote tumor immune escape through the following mechanism: Serine-threonine kinase 2 phosphorylates GAPDH at Ser151, promoting FBXW10-mediated K63-linked ubiquitination of GAPDH at Lys139 and enhancing GAPDH activity163. Activated GAPDH boosts the classical and non-classical NF-κB pathways via interaction with TRAF2, upregulates PD-L1 expression, and impairs CD8+ T cell function, thus shaping an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Moreover, androgen receptor transcription upregulates serine-threonine kinase 2 expression163.

TLR signaling pathway

The TLR signaling pathway is a pattern recognition receptor signal transduction mechanism primarily composed of Toll-like receptors (TLR1–10), adapter proteins (MyD88, TRIF, TIRAP, TRAM), kinases (IRAK family, TBK1), ubiquitin ligases (TRAF3/6), and transcription factors (NF-κB, IRF3/7, AP-1)164,165. Dysregulation of ubiquitination in the TLR signaling pathway can lead to aberrant activation or suppression of innate immune responses, contributing to the pathogenesis of inflammatory diseases and cancers166,167. AKT1-mediated phosphorylation of RNF115 at Ser132/Ser133 facilitates its association with 14-3-3 proteins and localization to the ER and Golgi, which is essential for RNF115 to regulate TLR trafficking and signaling. RNF115 ubiquitinates RAB1A (Lys49/Lys61) and RAB13 (Lys46/Lys58), preventing their reactivation and thus blocking TLR transport from the ER to the Golgi and cell surface to suppress TLR-mediated immune signaling168. In contrast, Death-Associated Protein Kinase 3 phosphorylates the S863 site of the E3 ubiquitin ligase LMO7 to promote its binding to STING and catalyze K63-linked ubiquitination, which enhances the interaction between STING and TBK1 and activates IRF3 and IFN-β. This promotes cancer immunity through the STING–TBK1–IRF3 signaling pathway169.

In conclusion, the molecular mechanisms of ubiquitination and the formation of various PTMs crosstalk to regulate three aspects of tumor immune responses. Firstly, in the immune checkpoint, the direct occupation of the E3 ligase binding site by phosphorylation, steric hindrance formed by glycosylation, and competitive modifications such as palmitoylation and succinylation precisely regulate protein stability, thereby regulating the immune response. Secondly, in immune cells, PTM directly regulates the abundance and function of surface receptors of key effector cells, and determines the fate of their activation, depletion, or polarization, thus reshaping the cellular composition and functional status of the whole immune microenvironment. Finally, in immune signaling pathways (such as STAT, IFN-1, and NF-κB), PTM crosstalk regulates the expression of immune genes upstream by regulating the activity and degradation of key transcription factors and signal transduction proteins. The above-mentioned molecular mechanism research reveals that tumor cells utilize PTM crosstalk to achieve immune escape and suggests that jointly targeting multiple PTM nodes may be the key to overcoming the compensation effect of a single pathway and achieving effective treatment.

Clinical or therapeutic implications

Recent research into the regulatory networks involving PTMs has uncovered fundamental mechanisms that may lead to clinically viable treatment approaches. The role of various small-molecule inhibitors targeting PTMs in enhancing the anti-tumor immune response has been explored. For example, INHI, a small molecule inhibitor of Nek2, inhibits the growth of tumor cells by abrogating the Nek2-mediated phosphorylation170, but it also reduces the infiltration of inflammatory cells in autoimmune diseases171. The CK2 small-molecule inhibitor CX-4945 blocks PD-L1 phosphorylation, enhances ubiquitin ligase recognition, and reduces PD-L1 stability, thereby improving the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors56. This molecule has progressed from mechanistic studies to clinical trials, and a phase 1b/2 study showed that Silmitasertib (CX-4945) combined with gemcitabine + cisplatin is effective as the first-line treatment of locally advanced/metastatic cholangiocarcinoma172. In addition, it showed promise in the treatment of basal cell carcinoma (NCT03897036)173 and solid tumors (NCT06541262), and is currently being tested in phase I clinical trials174. In terms of glycosylation regulation, NGI-1 and 2-deoxy-D-glucose (glucose analogs) increase sensitivity to immunotherapy by interfering with PD-L1 glycosylation75,175–178. The small-molecule inhibitor A-485 selectively inhibits p300/CBP acetyltransferase activity and suppresses its function179. Both 2-bromopalmitic acid and Benzosceptrin C are inhibitors of palmitoyltransferase that competitively bind to the DHHC domain, producing different reactions. 2-Bromopalmitic competitively binds to the DHHC domain to activate iron death or promote pyroptosis-induced tumor cell death94,130,180. The natural product Benzosceptrin C exerts anti-tumor immunity by binding to DHHC3 protein and inhibiting its palmitoylation of PD-L1 protein, thereby mediating lysosomal degradation of PD-L1 and prompting apoptosis of tumor cells181. The SUMOylation inhibitor TAK-981 activates IFN-1 signaling to promote antitumor immune responses, and it also directly stimulates T cell viability182,183. Summary of small-molecule inhibitors targeting PTMs for cancer immunotherapy

The successful application of small-molecule inhibitors targeting PTMs in clinical practice faces many challenges. One important issue is targeting selectivity. Many PTM-regulated enzymes have a wide substrate spectrum, which may lead to off-target effects and toxic reactions184,185. Drug delivery is another important limitation, especially for inhibitors with activity against specific organelles or protein complexes186,187. Researchers are developing new technologies to enhance therapeutic specificity and effectiveness. For instance, chimeras constructed with cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) enable more precise drug delivery to target cells and reduce off-target toxicity188. CPPs are short peptides (typically 5-30 amino acids) that can efficiently penetrate the Cell membranes and carry biological macromolecules (such as proteins, nucleic acids, and drugs) into cells. CPPs can be combined with other functional molecules, such as anti-cancer drugs, gene editing tools, and antibodies, to construct. CPP-induced chimeras that enhance intracellular delivery efficiency189–191. Shi et al. reported that chimeric molecules constructed via CPPs can target and induce the degradation of DHHC3, thereby inhibiting PD-L1 expression, enhancing the immune response191. Protein degradation Targeting Chimeras (PROTACs) are an emerging targeted protein degradation technology that selectively degrades target proteins by utilizing the natural ubiquitin–proteasome system within cells192,193. Preclinical studies based on PROTAC technology found that targeted protein degradation reduces the expression of PD-L1 and increases the infiltration of immune cells in the TME194–196.

Notably, while overcoming the challenges faced in the clinical application of targeted PTMs, PTMs of immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1 directly affect the accuracy of treatment efficacy prediction. In particular, immune checkpoint blockade therapy is a highly promising cancer treatment strategy based on the reactivation of T-cell immunity. However, predicting its efficacy requires accurate detection of biomarkers such as PD-L1. Existing biomarker detection technologies are limited, and a significant proportion of patients still fail to benefit from this therapy197,198. For example, the glycosylation of PD-L1 directly affects the binding efficiency of the anti-PD-L1 antibody to the target protein in clinical tests, resulting in false-negative results regarding PD-L1 positivity in specific tissues, which in turn affects treatment decisions65. The binding affinity and detection signal intensity of the anti-PD-L1 antibody are significantly improved after the deglycosylation of PD-L1 by enzymatic or chemical methods199. Multiple retrospective studies have similarly shown that PD-L1 deglycosylation enhances the detection of PD-L1 expression200–202. Wang et al. demonstrated that in deglycosylated tumor tissue samples, 84.31% of initially PD-L1-negative cases were reclassified as PD-L1-positive, indicating that glycosylation accounted for 27.92% of the false-negative rate in the triple-negative breast cancer sample202. A systematic review of the translational value of PTM regulation in tumor immunotherapy would provide new ideas for combination therapy and promote the development of personalized precision medicine.

Conclusion and perspective

This article systematically reviews the core role of the complex interaction network between ubiquitination and other PTMs in tumor immune regulation, revealing the molecular mechanism by which it shapes the immune phenotype of the TME by regulating the stability of immune checkpoint proteins, the function of immune cells, and the activity of signaling pathways. Specifically, PTM crosstalk can lead to the upregulation of immune checkpoints (such as PD-L1) and the exhaustion of effector T cell function, thereby contributing to the “cold tumor” phenotype with a lack of immune cell infiltration. Conversely, targeting the above-mentioned crosstalk mechanism is expected to enhance antigen presentation, promote immune cell infiltration and activation, and drive the transformation of the TME into an immune-inflammatory “hot tumor“203. Therefore, targeting the PTM network is regarded as a key strategy to overcome current immunotherapy resistance.

However, in the complex crosstalk mechanism of PTMs, functional compensation between modifications and tissue-cell specificity needs to be considered. For instance, when PTMs occur at adjacent or structure-related sites of a protein, phosphorylation inhibits the acetylation effect at adjacent sites, thereby regulating the progression of cancer204. In addition, PTMs can also depend on the cellular and tissue environment in which they occur. Their functions and regulatory outcomes can vary due to differences in cell types and tissue microenvironments205. For example, in different tumor subtypes or immune cells (e.g., regulatory versus effector T cells), the downstream signal output of PTMs of the same protein differs, resulting in opposite protumor or antitumor outcomes206. In the future, the mechanisms underlying crosstalk between protein PTMs should be explored in greater depth, For example, the mechanism underlying the effect of ADP-ribosylation on the regulation of immune checkpoints and other factors that influence immune responses207, as well as its interaction with ubiquitination to regulate interferon signaling and affect viral replication208, However, whether ubiquitination and ADP-ribosylation interaction can regulate cancer immunity has not yet been studied. Therefore, subsequent integration of multi-omics techniques such as phosphoproteomes and glycosylomes can be used to systematically resolve the dynamic regulatory network of PTMs, providing a more detailed perspective for understanding the immune escape mechanism.

For clinical translational applications, several development strategies are worth pursuing. First, specific small-molecule inhibitors or agonists targeting key modifying enzymes (such as CK2 and STT3A) should be developed. Second, drugs capable of simultaneously regulating ubiquitination and other related modifications (e.g., ubiquitination–phosphorylation) represent a promising avenue for overcoming immunotherapy resistance. At the same time, clinical trials of combination therapy based on PTM regulation, such as the combination of PD-L1 inhibitors and glycosylation blockers, should be promoted and accelerated. Furthermore, it is critical to determine whether PTM characteristics such as PD-L1 glycosylation level can act as biomarkers to predict immunotherapy response and guide precise clinical treatment. These research directions will help understand the core regulatory role of PTMs in tumor immunity and lay the foundation for the development of novel immunotherapy strategies.
