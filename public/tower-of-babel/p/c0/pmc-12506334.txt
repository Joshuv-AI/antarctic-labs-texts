Palliative care needs and health seeking behavior of people in two rural districts of nepal: a house-to-house survey
Authors: Namuna Shrestha, Arati Poudel, Sunita Bhandari, Adipti Pantha, Amrita Thapa, Maya Jane Bates, Farzana Khan, Daniel Munday
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPalliative care is integral to Universal Health Coverage. In Nepal 80% of the population live in rural often mountainous areas. Despite a national commitment to palliative care close to home, services in rural areas remain underdeveloped. We report on the palliative care needs and health seeking behaviour in two rural districts.MethodsA cross-sectional house to house survey was conducted across four municipalities in two rural districts. People in need of palliative care were identified using the general indicators of Supportive and Palliative Care Indicators Tool for Low Income Settings (SPICT-LIS) and recruited. They were questioned about diagnosis and health seeking behaviour and also about symptoms and quality of life using the Nepali Palliative Care Outcome Scale (N-POS). For those unable to respond, the main carer was asked to provide data. Data were analyzed descriptively using MS Excel 2016.ResultsA total of 587 households with 2320 residents were surveyed. Fifty-eight (2.5%) were identified as needing palliative care, ranging from 0.8 to 3.4% between municipalities. Only one child was identified and excluded from this study because their needs are not comparable with adults. The median age of those requiring palliative care was 79 years (Range 21–98); 50% were female. Forty-four/58(76%) had a diagnosis of at least one chronic illness, including hypertension and diabetes, but in only 32/58 (55%) was this a condition potentially leading to palliative care need. Difficulty in walking (58/58;100%), weakness (56/58;97%) and pain (44/58;76%) were the most common physical complaints. Fifty-one/58(93%) reported at least one psychosocial or spiritual need. Of those with a chronic illness diagnosis, 25/44(57%) were receiving ongoing medical care: 16/25(64%) at tertiary hospitals and 5/25(20%) at a local community hospital. Although 41/58(71%) had enrolled in government health insurance, cost remained a commonly reported barrier to accessing health care.ConclusionSignificant numbers of adults with palliative need in rural Nepal exist with a high burden of physical, psychosocial and spiritual suffering. Those receiving follow up mainly travel to distant tertiary hospitals with local services being underutilized. Despite health insurance, costs prevented access to health care. Our findings are informing the development of a rural palliative care model for Nepal.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-025-01895-z.Keywords: Health seeking behavior, Nepal, Palliative care, Palliative care needs, Rural

Background

Nepal, a lower-middle-income (LMIC) Himalayan country, faces unique challenges in rural healthcare delivery due to its mountainous terrain [1]. In common with other LMIC, Nepal’s population has experienced an increased burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) over recent decades driven by demographic and epidemiological transition. Over 71% of deaths are estimated to be due to NCDs [2] and are projected to reach 79% by 2040 [3]. This shift has escalated the need for palliative care services particularly for people with chronic and progressive conditions such as cardiovascular disease, COPD and cancer [4]. Yet Nepal’s health system with palliative care services concentrated in urban centers and primarily focused on cancer, is underprepared to meet the needs of its rural populations, where more than 80% of its people reside [5].

The Government of Nepal, a signatory of the 67th World Health Assembly Declaration [6], has committed to integrating palliative care into the national health system [7] to provide affordable, accessible services close to patients’ homes. Nepal’s primary care services delivered through government health posts, primary health centres and community hospitals have been identified as central to rural palliative care provision [8]. These, despite a newly introduced government insurance system [9], are often poorly utilized with many seeking care in poorly regulated rural private pharmacies [10]. Others attend private hospitals in urban areas [11], where out of pocket expenditure is high.

As Nepal works towards implementing its palliative care strategy and achieving sustainable developmental goal (SDG) number 3.8, which aims to achieve for Universal Health Coverage (UHC) including access to quality health services, a greater understanding of both individuals’ palliative care needs and their health care utilization in rural communities is essential. This can guide development of service delivery models which ensure effective palliative care can be delivered and that no one is left out. This study, part of the Sunita Project’s three-year initiative aimed at improving access to palliative care for people in need, close to their home in rural areas, presents findings from a house-to-house survey in hill districts in Nepal. Itfocuses on palliative care needs and health-seeking behavior to inform a model of rural palliative care provision.

Methods

Study design and participants

A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted in three municipalities in Lamjung and one municipality in West Rukum districts. Three to five communities in two wards in each municipality were purposively sampled in collaboration with the municipalities’ health coordinators, to achieve maximum variation based on location (urban to remote) and economic, ethnic and cultural diversity. All households within the selected communities were screened by the researchers.

Data collection procedure

An adult (18 years and above) was approached in each household to provide information on number of residents, their ages, ethnic group and religion, and whether any household member was either old and frail, had a chronic illness or a severe disability.

If such a person was identified they (or their main carer if they were unable to answer) were questioned using the general indicators of the Supportive and Palliative Care Indicators Tool for Low Income Settings (SPICT-LIS) [12]. The person was identified as needing palliative care if any indicator was present and was consented and recruited into the study. They were then questioned using a structured questionnaire, designed for the study and pre-tested during project development (Supplementary File 1) This covered: socio-demographic details, disease diagnosis and health-seeking behavior. Symptoms and quality of life items were surveyed using the Nepali Palliative Outcome Scale (NPOS) [13]. Data were recorded in Kobo toolbox using android tablets [14].

Data analysis

Data were anonymized and subsequently analyzed using Excel 2016. Results were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were summarized in the form of mean (standard deviation, SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR). Fisher exact test was used for categorical data using the ‘socscistatistics.com’ online calculator.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from Ethical Review Board (ERB) of Nepal Health Research Council (Reference: 515/2022 P).

Palliative care need

Only one child below 18 years of age with palliative care need was identified during the screening process who was hospitalized at the time of study. We anticipated low numbers of children based on the findings from a previous unpublished pilot study. As needs of children differ from those of adults, we did not recruit this child for data collection or analysis and report findings for adults only.

Fifty-eight/2320 (2.5%) individuals were identified as needing palliative care using the general indicators of SPICT-LIS. All agreed to take part in completing the questionnaire. There was marked variation between municipalities in the proportion of the population with palliative care needs, ranging from 0.8% (5/605) in Chaurjahari Municipality, West Rukum to 3.4% (21/625) in Sundarbazar (see Table 2).

All participants (58/58) when questioned using NPOS complained of having difficulty walking at least some of the time and 97% (56/58) of weakness. 76% (44/58) suffered from pain with 34% (20/58) saying this was moderate or severe. Breathlessness was common in people diagnosed with COPD with 33% (3/9) reporting it as ‘a little’ and 67% (6/9) as ‘very much.’

High levels of psychosocial and spiritual need were reported with 93% (51/58) having at least one need, including 84% (46/55) who were worried about their health at least some of the time. 75% (41/58) felt they did not feel at peace with the same number feeling that their life was not worthwhile and also that they were not able to get information they needed at least some of the time. 64% (35/58) felt at least some of the time they could not discuss their illness with their families.

Health seeking behavior of people in need of palliative care

In terms of health and general health care (Table 4), 76% (44/58) had been diagnosed with at least one chronic condition at some point, the commonest incidental conditions being 11/58(19%) hypertension and 3(6%) diabetes. Twenty-five/44(57%) had been diagnosed at a tertiary hospital in a city, distant from their homes and 13(30%) at a local community hospital. Five had had their chronic condition ‘diagnosed’ in a private pharmacy; four of these had hypertension, the other had COPD. Of the six with cancer, five had been diagnosed in a tertiary hospital and one at a local community hospital.

Only 25/58(43%) of patients with palliative care needs were being follow-up by clinical services and only 5/25(20%) of these were followed up regularly.

Of those having follow up for their chronic condition, 16/25(64%) were going to a tertiary hospital outside of the district and 5(20%) were being following up in a community hospital within the district – with only one person being followed up regularly there. Three were followed up in a local private pharmacy; two with hypertension and the third was the one previously diagnosed in the private pharmacy with COPD. Only one person was being followed up through visits from the local government health post staff.

Of the seven followed up regularly, two had cancer (cervical and chronic myeloid leukemia) and received care at a tertiary hospital. The others having regular follow up suffered from COPD (3) and hypertension (2).

60% (35/58) did access the (very basic) free health government services at least occasionally and 41/58(71%) had joined the national health insurance programme. Twenty-six (45%) claimed that worry about costs prevented them from seeking healthcare at least on some occasions.

Age related issues

Based on the absence of diagnosis among elderly participants, we proceeded with a bivariate analysis of age and diagnosis. Given the median age of participants was 79 (Range 21–98) we explored the effects of age on health seeking behaviour. All seven of the participants under 60 years of age had received a diagnosis of a serious illness leading to palliative care need at a tertiary hospital. Those over 75 were statistically less likely (Fisher exact test p = 0.0313) to have had a diagnosis of any chronic condition compared to those 75 or under.

Discussion

Summary of findings

This paper reports findings from the most comprehensive house-to-house survey of palliative care needs in rural Nepal, covering four municipalities within two rural districts. In total 2.5% of the population were identified as needing palliative care, with marked variation between the two districts. Most people identified had no formal education and were involved in subsistence agriculture, as expected for rural Nepal.

The commonest illnesses leading to palliative care need were cardiovascular, respiratory and cancer; however, only around half of people identified had a specific diagnosis. The majority were identified as old and frail, consistent with the elderly nature of the sample (median age 79) and older people were less likely to have been diagnosed with a chronic condition than younger people. NPOS revealed high levels of unmet physical, psychosocial and spiritual need, but none of those identified in this study were receiving palliative care. The majority of those who had been diagnosed with a life-limiting illness had been seen in a city tertiary hospital, often many hours from their homes and very few were being followed up regularly and follow up was mostly in tertiary centres outside the district. Cost often prevented participants from seeking health care despite availability of health insurance. Palliative care was not formally accessed by any participant as it was not available locally.

Comparison with existing literature

Previous studies in Nepal and India using SPICT identified similar results for the proportion of the population with palliative care need; in Makwanpur District, Nepal, a prevalence of 3.04% was found [15] and in Udupi District, North India 4.31% [16]. However, a systematic review in India estimated a prevalence of 0.62% [17]. Here the majority of studies used a more restrictive tool, where a specific diagnosis was required, in contrast to SPICT which will identify people with no formal diagnosis. We would suggest therefore that using SPICT gives a more accurate estimate, particularly in rural areas where people don’t have ready access to health care for investigation and diagnosis [18].

However, even in our survey, fewer people with palliative care needs were identified in Chaurjahari Municipality, Rukum District (0.8%) compared to Lamjung District (3.1%) indicating in-country heterogenicity. The reasons for this are likely to be complex. Firstly, fewer old people live in Rukum compared to Lamjung District [19], with life expectancy 60.39 years and 70.94 years respectively [20]. In addition, Rukum has a lower Human Development Index (HDI) 0.431 compared with 0.507 in Lamjung [20]. Increasing HDI is associated with higher life expectancy and an increased prevalence of non-communicable diseases, due to life-style changes [21, 22]. Populations with lower HDI are likely to have poorer health due to relative lack of access to health care and poorer health literacy, with higher deaths from communicable disease and fewer with long term chronic illness likely to survive into old age. Finally, it is possible that stigma may have contributed to the lower incidence of palliative care needs discovered in Rukum. Whilst there is no direct evidence of this from the study, non-disclosure or concealment of a condition or disease has been reported as a major consequence of NCD related stigma [23, 24]. It is possible that this is more common in an area with a low HDI such as Rukum.

The prevalence of cardiovascular, respiratory disease and cancer leading to the need for palliative care in our study reflects that in others, both in Nepal [15] and India [16, 25]. Levels of holistic palliative care need with three quarters suffering pain are similar to other studies in rural Nepal [15] and worldwide [26]. All complained of weakness and most of difficulty walking, probably reflecting the mountainous terrain in rural Nepal and the challenges those with chronic illness face. Similar to other contexts, psychosocial and spiritual needs were also prominent in this population [27]. With lack of access to health care generally and lack of training and skills in health workers in rural Nepal [28] these health related issues are unlikely to be addressed.

Lack of diagnosis in older people might reflect a tendency in this age group to equate worsening health with old age and a wish to avoid the costs of seeking health care [29]. It was striking that most people in our survey still travel outside the district for follow up in private tertiary centres, rather than attend local not for profit community and government hospitals. This leads to higher direct and indirect costs, including transport and loss of earnings for family members who need to accompany people to cities [11, 30]. Catastrophic health expenditure is an important driver of poverty in south Asia and is known to be higher amongst the poor, the old and those with chronic illness [31, 32].

Interestingly many more respondents (71%) were enrolled for the government health insurance than the overall 22.5% national uptake [33]. However, even some of those with health insurance, said that they would sometimes avoid accessing health care because of cost. Studies in Nepal highlight several issues with the current health insurance system including unavailability of medicines, the need to pay out-of-pocket and seek services from private facilities [9, 33–35].

Need for further research

There is a need for further research to explore the experience of living with life limiting illness in Nepal. There is an urgent need to understand health seeking behaviour and why people do not use their local health services more frequently, choosing to go to cities for follow up of chronic illness. A purposive sample of people identified in this survey was recruited for a multiple comparative case study where these issues were explored and will be published subsequently.

Implications for practice

Aligning with SDG 3 for achieving UHC, the National Strategy for Palliative Care in Nepal (2017) commits to palliative care being delivered close to people’s homes throughout Nepal. This is important to ensure that people with advanced illness can receive high quality care so they can live and die at or close to home and also that palliative care, as part of UHC delivered locally, can reduce out of pocket costs and catastrophic health spending [36]. This study has demonstrated how community hospitals and local health facilities are underutilized in providing care for people with advanced chronic illness. Local facilities were not widely used for chronic disease management, including follow up for hypertension with people preferring to attend the private pharmacies local to their homes. This study is informing the development of a model of rural palliative care (Sunita Project) which is being implemented and will be evaluated. As the population ages and more people in Nepal suffer and die from chronic illness, there is an urgent need for these services to develop.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first population-based survey to be conducted across two rural districts of Nepal providing valuable insights into palliative care needs across these districts. Communities were selected to ensure diverse representation of socio-cultural, economic and geographical contexts. The alignment of the survey demographics with national and district level data further supports the representativeness of the study suggesting, that the findings are likely to truly reflect population characteristics. Moreover, lack of prior research on health seeking behavior within this group highlights the significance of this survey in understanding how individuals with palliative care needs seek and access health care and is crucial for developing effective and responsive health services tailored to their specific needs. Differential levels in palliative care need between districts with varying HDI is an important finding and is of international interest in illustrating how countries cannot be taken as homogenous entities.

However, several limitations are evident. The small sample size limits the reliability of the findings particularly related to specific palliative care needs. The study was conducted in only two districts and did not extend to more remote areas (due to resource and safety issues) where people are likely to face greater challenges in accessing healthcare. The reliance on self-reported diagnosis in people with limited health literacy raises concerns about the accuracy of the data concerning the health conditions of the study participants emphasizing the need for careful interpretation of the results. Finally, this method is not feasible for identifying children with palliative care needs and this should be addressed through other means.

Conclusion

A substantial and varying burden of holistic palliative care need exists within the selected communities of rural districts of Nepal. The findings emphasize the critical gaps in access to care for rural populations who often travel long distances for diagnosis and follow up rather than using local level health services. This highlights persisting critical challenges as Nepal strives towards achieving the goal of UHC. Identification of palliative care need and health seeking behaviour provides a strong basis for developing a rural model of palliative care, expanding the capacity of community level health care facilities to provide these services and training rural health care workers to deliver appropriate care. Ensuring such quality services exist close to patients’ homes will promote equity in healthcare access for underserved rural populations and help to prevent catastrophic health spending through traveling long distances to access care.
