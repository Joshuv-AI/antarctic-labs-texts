PLK1 inhibition enhances gemcitabine-induced apoptosis through PLK1-dependent ERK1/2-Bim and AKT1/Noxa signals in pancreatic cancer cells
Authors: Bing Lu, Hai Li, Dongfeng Deng, Yadong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolo-like kinase 1 (PLK1) is a critical regulator of many cell cycle events, which has been found to be associated with resistance to cytotoxic drugs. In the present study, we investigates how PLK1 regulates the sensitivity of pancreatic cancer cells to Gemcitabine (GEM) and its mechanism. We detected the expression of PLK1 in pancreatic cancer tissues and cell lines and study the effects of PLK1 and Gemcitabine on cell viability and apoptosis of GEM-resistant pancreatic cancer PANC-1 cells and Gemcitabine sensitive BxPC-3 cells; Using inhibitors or siRNA, we further investigate the effects of PLK1 on ERK1/2, AKT1, and pro-apoptotic genes PUMA, Bim, and Noxa; We finally investigated the effect of the combined onvansertib and Gemcitabine on the growth of PANC-1 subcutaneous transplant tumors in nude mice and explored its possible mechanism of action. PLK1 is overexpressed in PDAC tissues and cell lines. Targeting PLK1 inhibits cell viability and induces cell apoptosis in PANC-1 cells in vitro by ERK1/2-dependent Bim and AKT1-dependent Noxa pathway. Gemcitabine activates ERK1/2 and AKT1, leading to acquired Gemcitabine resistance. Targeting PLK1 enhances Gemcitabine sensitivity in PANC-1 cells by upregulating ERK1/2-dependent Bim and AKT1-dependent Noxa expression in vitro. PLK1 re-expression by PLK1 transfection in PLK1 shRNA transfected PANC-1 cells recures Gemcitabine sensitivity by inhibiting ERK1/2-dependent Bim and AKT1-dependent Noxa expression in vitro. Enhanced PLK1 reduces Gemcitabinecitabine sensitivity in BxPC-3 cells by inhibiting Gemcitabine-induced Bim and Noxa expression. BI2536 (a PLK1 kinase inhibitor) treatment recures the Gemcitabine sensitivity in the PLK -transfected BxPC-3 cells by upregulation of Bim and Noxa expression in vitro. The combination of onvansertib and Gemcitabine showed significant anti-tumor effect in vivo. Mechanistically, combined treatment inhibits ERK1/2 and AKT1, and increases Bim and Noxa expression. Targeting PLK1 sensitizes PDAC cells to gemcitabine in vitro and in vivo. This indicates that combination therapy with PLK1 inhibitor may overcome gemcitabine resistance, offering a promising new therapeutic option for the treatment of gemcitabine-resistant human pancreatic cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12032-025-03062-z.Keywords: Pancreatic cancer, Chemotherapy, Gemcitabine, Polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1), ERK1/2, AKT1, Noxa, Bim, Apoptosis

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer is a malignant tumor with poor prognosis, known as the king of malignancy, with a median survival 10–12 months under treatment conditions, 5–6 months without treatment, and its 5-year survival rate is less than 11% [1]. Most patients with pancreatic cancer were diagnosed in advanced stage and had lost the opportunity of surgery. The current standard first-line therapy chemotherapy treatment regimen is folfironox or gemcitabine plus albumin-bound paclitaxel. GEM has used for all stages of pancreatic cancer patients in the last 20 years, but short-term use may lead to GEM resistance [2]. Studies have shown that the occurrence of GEM resistance in pancreatic cancer patients is related to gene mutations, resistant gene activation or sensitive gene reduction [3, 4]. More extensively researched subtypes of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) susceptible to targeted therapies includes BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations [5], microsatellite instability [6], KRAS mutations [7, 8] and FAM 110C protein [9]. Targeting these mutant subtypes can enhance the sensitivity of pancreatic cancer patients to chemotherapy [10–12]. However, the median overall survival is less than 1 year in patients following combined chemotherapy and targeted therapy;in addition, the survival rate has no significant difference in combined therapy groups compared to the GEM alone groups [12–14], highlighting the need for more effective treatments in patients with pancreatic cancer.

The mechanism of GEM resistance is still largely unknown. Research has found that the GEM resistance is significantly associated with overexpression of resistant gene and anti-apoptotic genes, high expression of drug efflux pumps, ERK1/2, and AKT1/2 hyperphosphorylation, et al. [15–17]. In addition, GEM itself can stimulate cells to produce or reactivate glycolysis, EMT, resistance genes and anti-apoptotic genes [18–20]. Therefore, inhibiting the basal anti-apoptotic genes or the reactivated anti-apoptotic genes could increases cell sensitivity to GEM.

The polo-like kinases-1 (PLK1) is a conserved mitotic serine-threonine protein kinase and an important kinase for cell cycle regulation [21]. Enhanced PLK1 can undermine checkpoints and cause excessive cellular division, leading to the development of cancer because of the abnormal cell growth [21]. Recently, it has been found that PLK1 overexpression is associated with cytotoxic drugs resistance, and targeting PLK1 can enhance cell sensitivity to these cytotoxic drugs [22–24]. Mahajan et al. [25] reported that targeting PLK1 inhibited tumor growth and induced cell apoptosis. Zhang et al. has reported that PLK1 inhibition sensitized pancreatic cancer cells to immune checkpoint blockade by inhibiting NF-κB activity and activating the anti-tumor immune response [26]. Li et al. [27] reported that targeting PLK1 with PLK1 inhibitor GSK461364A sensitized GEM-resistant pancreatic cancer cells to GEM.

Although studies have shown that targeting PLK1 benefits pancreatic cancer cells and enhance cell sensitivity to GEM, the mechanism by which PLK1 acts is still unclear. In the study, we first examined the effect of GEM on pancreatic cancer cells and its mechanism. We then examined the effect of PLK1 on pancreatic cancer cells, and how PLK1 inhibition sensitized pancreatic cancer cells to GEMCITABINE. Our data have demonstrated that GEM induced cell apoptosis by upregulation of pro-apoptotic Bim and Noxa. GEM activated ERK1/2 and AKT1, resulted in GEM resistance. PLK1 inhibition regulated Bim and Noxa expression through inhibition of ERK1/2 and AKT1 activity.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culture

The pancreatic cancer cell lines, SW1990, PANC-1, CFPAC-1, BxPC-3, MiaPaCa2, Capan-2 and AsPC-1 were obtained from Shanghai Institute of Cell Research, Chinese Academy of Sciences (ATCC, Shanghai China) and stained at 37 °C and 5% CO2. All the cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% or 20% fetal bovine serum and 100 U/ml penicillin. They were further tested for authenticity by STR profiling. All experiments were conducted by our research groups at the Experimental Research Center, Henan Provincial People's Hospital.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of tissue samples

Surgical resection specimens from 16 cases of PDAC and its adjacent tissues confirmed by pathology were randomly collected. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissues were deparaffinized, rehydrated, heat-induced epitope retrieval and blocked with 10% horse serum. The sections were then incubated with the primary PLK1 (208G4) Rabbit mAb (1:50) (Cell signals Cat. # 4513, Shanghai, China). The staining intensity (negative −, 0; +, 1; ++, 2; +++, 3) and the positive cells (%) was determined.

Chemical reagents, and chemotherapy drugs

SignalSilence® PLK1 siRNA, BimEL siRNA, Noxa siRNA, p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) siRNA, Akt1 siRNA, and SignalSilence® Control siRNA (CN siRNA) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (CST). The concentration of siRNA used for cell transfection was 100 nM and 48 h prior to cell lysis. Silencer Select NOXA siRNA (AMBION, Cat. #4392420, siRNA ID: s10709) were purchased from AMBION (Shanghai, China). The concentration of siRNA used for cell transfection was 20 nM. Primary antibodies (Dilution,1:1000) against Puma (#12450), Bim (#2819), Noxa (#14766), PLK1 (#4513), p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) (#9102), Phospho-p44/42 MAPK (mAb #4370), Phospho-Akt1 (Ser473) (#9018), Akt1 (D9R8K) (#75692), Cleaved PARP (#9541) and β-Actin (#8457) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (CST). Human SDF1α, the ERK1/2 activator was purchased from PeproTech (Guangzhou, China). BI2536 (a PLK1 kinase inhibitor) was purchased from EMD Millipore (Shanghai China). Gemcitabinecitabine was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, Shanghai China). Onvansertib was purchased from Selleck Chemicals (Beijing China). All other chemical reagents were purchased from Sigma (Shanghai China).

Plasmid transfection

The wild-type PLK1 overexpression plasmid pcDNA3-PLK1 and pcDNA3 (Shanghai Borsen Biotechnology Co., Ltd) was transfected into BxPC-3 cells for 48 h using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) according to the manufacture’s instruction. Transfected cells were lysed, and equal amounts of proteins were subjected to immunoblotting analysis after 48 h transfection. For stable pcDNA3-PLK1 transfection, 48 hs post-transfection, aspirate the medium and replace with fresh medium containing puromycin at the appropriate concentration. Approximately every 2–3 days, aspirate and replace with freshly prepared selective media. The efficacy of the pcDNA3-PLK1 clones was measured by Western blotting.

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection

Cells were grown to confluence in 6-cm tissue culture plates, and transfected with one of the PLK1 siRNA, Bim siRNA, Noxa siRNA, p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) siRNA, Akt siRNA and control siRNA (CN siRNA) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen). Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection. For stable PLK1 siRNA transfection, the transfected cells were selected by culturing cells in DMEM medium (with 10% fetal bovine serum) containing 800 µg/ml G418. After 5–7 days of selection, resistant colonies were isolated and expanded in DMEM medium containing 200 µg/ml G418. The blocking efficacy of the siRNA clones was measured by Western blotting.

MTT assay

2 × 103 cells (PANC-1 or BxPC-3) exposed to different concentrations of GEMCITABINE, were cultured in 96-wells plates for 48 h. PANC-1 cells were transfected into PLK1 siRNA for 24 h, then transfected with pcDNA3- PLK1 for 24 h, after that the cells were treated with GEMCITABINE (10 μM) for 48 h; BxPC-3 cells were transfected into pcDNA3- PLK1 or CN for 24 h, then treated with GEM (1 μM) or co-treated with 40 nM of BI2536 and GEM (1 μM) for 48 h. The PLK1 siRNA or CN siRNA transfected PANC-1 cells were transfected into Bim siRNA or Noxa siRNA or co-transfection with Bim siRNA and Noxa siRNA for 24 h, then treated with GEM (10 μM) for 48 h. Cell viability was detected by 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining

After different experimental time points, different groups of cells under different experimental conditions are seeded into 24-well plate containing coverslips. After Incubating with labeling working solution, PBS wash, addition of the DAPI working solution and antifluorescence quenching agent, the slides were sealed. TUNEL assay was performed according to manufacturer’s instructions using Beyotime TUNEL fluorescence kit. The images were collected under a fluorescence microscope, and apoptotic cells were defined as those with red fluorescence in the nucleus.

Western blot assay

Cells were plated in 6-well plate and with given treatment in advance. Cells were lysed with RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Standard Western blotting was performed using whole-cell protein lysates. Western blotting analysis was performed using standard techniques as described previously [28].

In vivo anti-tumor activity

BALB/c-nu mice (4–6 weeks of age, 16-18 g) (Male and female dual-use) were purchased from Shanghai Slack Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (Shanghai China). All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee in Henan Provincial People's Hospital (No:2022-B023).PANC-1 cells (1 × 107) mixed with Matrigel ma were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each 4- to 6-week-old female and male mice. Once the tumors reached the volume of 60–100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into the treatment and control groups. 6 mice(3 Males and 3 females) were randomized to receive vehicles or onvansertib (50 mg/kg p.o. 5 days a week for 4 weeks, or GEM (weekly 50 mg/kg, i.p. for 4 weeks or their combination. Tumor growth was measured once per week using Vernier calipers, and body weight was recorded for up to 4 weeks. 6 mice were randomized to receive vehicles or onvansertib (50 mg/kg p.o. 5 days a week for 7 days), or GEM (weekly 50 mg/kg, i.p. for 1 weeks) or their combination. Removal of tumor tissue was used for western blot assay to pAKT1, pERK1/2, Bim and Noxa expression and PARP cleavage.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS22 software. All results are expressed as mean ± SEM. All experiments were performed in twice. One-way ANOVA, Student's t test were used whenever applicable. Statistically significance was considered as p value < 0.05.

Results

Enhanced PLK1 expression in PDAC cell lines and tissues

Human polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1) has been reported to be upregulated in many malignant tumor and cell lines. Enhanced PLK1 is related to resistance to chemotherapy drugs or cytotoxic drugs. Sixteen cases of pathologically confirmed PDAC tissues and their paracancerous tissue were detected by immunohistochemistry (Fig. 1). The high-intensity positive expression (+++) rate of PLK1 was 6/16, the moderate intensity (++) was 2/16, the low-intensity (+) was 4/16, and the negative expression (−) rate was 4/16 (Fig. 1A), with the overall positive expression rate of PLK1 was 75%. Negative PLK1 expression (−) was shown in paracancerous tissues (Fig. 1B). We detected PLK1 expression by western blot assay in pancreatic cancer cell lines, SW1990, PANC-1, CFPAC-1, Capan-2, BxPC-3, MiaPaCa2 and AsPC-1. As shown in Fig. 1C. PLK1 protein was overexpressed in most of the PDAC cell lines. The highest PLK1 expression was in the PANC-1 cells. The minimum PLK1 expression was in the BxPC-3 cells. So we used PANC-1 and BxPC-3 cells for further study.

Targeting PLK1 increases the sensitivity of PANC-1 cells to GEM

PANC-1 and BxPC-3 cells were exposure to GEM (0, 0.01. 0.1, 1.0, 10, 100 μM) for 48 h, cell viability was measured by MTT. Measurement of cell viability confirmed that GEM’s IC50 was more than 100 µM in the PANC-1 cells, and GEM’s IC50 was less than 10 μM in the BxPC-3 cells (Fig. 2A). Therefore, PANC-1 cells was GEM resistant compared to BxPC-3 cells. We used 1 µM GEM for further study in BxPC-3 cells and PANC-1 cells.

We transiently knockdown PLK1 (siRNA1 and siRNA2) in the PANC-1 cells via siRNA system. The efficiency of PLK1 knockdown was detected by western blotting. The results showed that 95% PLK1 was reduced after 48 h PLK1 siRNA1,2 transfection in the PANC-1 cells (Fig. 2B).

PLK1 knockdown via siRNA for 48 h led to a significant decrease in cell viability in the PANC1 cells (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, apoptotic cells were significant increase by immunofluorescence observations (Fig. 2D). Cleaved PARP was also significantly increased in PLK1 siRNA/PANC-1 cells (Fig. 2E).

PANC-1 cells exposure to 1 µM GEM alone for 48 h did not significantly inhibit cell viability (Fig. 2C) and induce cell apoptosis (Fig. 2D). GEM treatment also did not activate cleaved PARP (Fig. 2E). However, combined GEM and PLK1 knockdown significantly inhibited cell viability (Fig. 2C), enhanced apoptosis (Fig. 2D) and increased cleaved PARP (Fig. 2E).

Enhanced PLK1 expression decreases the sensitivity of BxPC-3 cells to GEM

BxPC-3 cells were transfected with pcDNA.3-PLK1 for 48 h. PLK1 was overexpressed in the BxPC-3 cells after transient pcDNA.3-PLK1 transfection for 48 h (Fig. 3A). Enhanced PLK1 expression did not significantly affect cell viability (Fig. 3B) and cell apoptosis (Fig. 3C) by MTT or immunofluorescence observations, suggesting that exogenous PLK1 has no significant effect on cell growth and apoptosis in BxPC-3 cells. However, when the BxPC-3 cells were transfected with pcDNA.3-PLK1 for 24 h, then treated with 40 nM BI2536 (a PLK1 kinase inhibitor) for 48 h, BI2536 treatment inhibited PLK1 expression (Fig. 3A) and cell viability (Fig. 3B) and promoted cell apoptosis (Fig. 3C).

BxPC-3 cells treated with 1 μM GEMCITABINE for 48 h led to 55% cell growth inhibition (Fig. 3B) and 35% apoptotic cells (Fig. 3C). PLK1 overexpression by pcDNA.3-PLK1 transfection decreased GEMCITABINE-induced BxPC-3 cell apoptosis (Fig. 3C), reduced cell viability (Fig. 3B) and decreased GEM-induced cleaved-PARP activity (Fig. 3D). However, when BxPC-3 cells were transfected into pcDNA3-PLK1, then co-treated with 40 nM of BI2536 and GEM (1 μM) for 48 h, GEMCITABINE-induced cell apoptosis was increased (Fig. 3C) and cell viability was decreased (Fig. 3B).

Targeting PLK1 enhances Bim levels via ERK1/2-dependent way

It has previously reported that pro-apoptotic Bim is phosphorylated by ERK, resulting in enhanced Bim expression and enhanced cell apoptosis [29]. Here we found that p-ERK1/2 was significant decreased after 48 h PLK1 siRNA transfection by western blot assay (Fig. 4A). PLK1 knockdown significantly induced Bim protein expression (Fig. 4B). PUMA and Noxa were also the pro-apoptotic BH3-only proteins as Bim. We found Noxa protein was significantly increased following PLK1 siRNA transfection and no significant change in the expression level of PUMA was shown (Fig. 4B).

The stable PLK1 siRNA transfected PANC-1 cells were transfected with pcDNA.3-PLK1 or control pcDNA.3 for 48 h. Re-expression of PLK1 reduced pERK1/2 expression and inhibited PLK1 siRNA-induced Bim and NOXA expression (Fig. 4C). Re-expression of PLK1 did not affect PUMA expression (Fig. 4C). Therefore, PLK1 inhibition upregulated Bim and NOXA expression, which was PLK1-dependent.

To determine whether Bim and NOXA expression was ERK1/2-dependent, PANC-1 cells following 48 h PLK1 siRNA transfection were treated with SDF1-α (200 ng/ml for 5 min). The results showed that ERK1/2 was reactivated (Fig. 4A), followed by reduced Bim expression, SDF1-α had no obvious effect on Noxa and PUMA (Fig. 4B).

Targeting PLK1 enhanced Noxa levels via AKT1-dependent way

We detected AKT1 phosphorylation in BxPC-3 and PANC-1 cells, both of which exhibited significant levels of AKT1 phosphorylation (Fig. 4B, D). After 48 h PLK1 siRNA transfection, pAKT1 was significant decreased by western blot assay in BxPC-3 (data not shown) and PANC-1 cells (Fig. 4B). PANC-1 cells following 48 h PLK1 siRNA transfection were treated with 2 µg/mL SC79, for 5 min. Exposure to SC79 increased pAKT1 (Fig. 4B) and abolishes NOXA expression induced by PLK1 inhibition (Fig. 4B). SC79 led to a decrease of a small amount of Bim expression, but had no obvious effect on PUMA expression (Fig. 4B). The stable pcDNA.3-PLK1-transfected BxPC-3 cells were transfected with AKT1 siRNA for 48 h, p-AKT1 levels were decreased followed by AKT1 knockdown (Fig. 4D). However, Noxa expression was significantly upregulated, but PUMA and Bim was not significantly affected (Fig. 4D). These data indicated that NOXA upregulation was AKT1-dependent, and Bim was AKT1-independent, AKT1 had no obvious effect on PUMA.

Targeting PLK1 induced apoptosis and enhances GEM sensitivity by Bim-dependent and Noxa-dependent way in PANC-1 cells

PANC-1 cells were transfected with PLK1 siRNA for 48 h, leading to about 25% cell apoptosis. To further verify whether Noxa and Bim contributed to the effect of PLK1 inhibition, PANC-1 cells were co-transfected with PLK1 siRNA/Bim siRNA or PLK1 siRNA/Noxa siRNA for 48 h. NOXA siRNA or Bim siRNA transfection significantly reversed PLK1 siRNA-induced cell apoptosis (Fig. 5). Therefore, downregulation of PLK1 induced cell apoptosis via upregulation of Bim and Noxa expression.

PLK1 expression was significantly increased in the BxPC-3 cells from 0.5 h, and reached the highest point at 6 h, and returned to normal level at 24 h (Fig. 6B). GEM induces Bim-dependent and Noxa-dependent cell apoptosis in BxPC-3 cells

BxPC-3 cells were treated with 1 μM for 5 min, 10 min, 0.5, 1, 3, 6, 12 and 24 h. NOXA was inactivated from 30 min, and activated from 3 h, and reached the highest at 6 h, and returned to normal level at 24 h (Fig. 6B). BimEL was activated from 30 min and reached the highest at 3 h, and returned to normal level at 24 h (Fig. 6B). GEM led to no significant increase of PUMA expression (data not shown).

We investigated whether Bim and Noxa is related to GEM-induced cell apoptosis. We then silenced Bim or Noxa or combined Bim siRNA + Noxa siRNA in BxPC-3 cells for 16 h, then treated with 1 μM GEM for 48 h. GEM-induced cell apoptosis was decreased due to the reduced Bim or Noxa, respectively, but albeit to a relatively minor extent (Fig. 6C). BxPC-3 cells were co-transfected with Bim siRNA and Noxa siRNA against Bim and Noxa for 16 h, then exposure to 1 μM GEM for 48 h. GEM-induced cell apoptosis was significantly decreased compared to siRNA against Bim or Noxa alone (Fig. 6C). Control vector did not affect cell apoptosis. This data indicated that GEM induced BxPC-3 cell apoptosis by upregulation both Bim and Noxa.

BxPC-3 cells were treated with SDF1-α (200 ng/ml for 5 min), the ERK1/2 activator or 2 µg/mL SC79, the AKT activator, for 5 min or SDF1-α + SC79 for 5 min, then treated with 1 μM GEMCITABINE for 48 h. GEMCITABINE-induced cell apoptosis was significantly decreased (Fig. 6C).

Combined PLK1 inhibition and GEM treatment suppresses pancreatic tumor growth

In vitro studies above have showed that inhibition of PLK1 via PLK1 siRNA overcomes the therapeutic resistance of PANC-1 cells to GEM. We then validated the efficacy of the combination of GEM and onvansertib (PLK1 inhibitor) in suppressing the growth of GEM-resistant xenograft tumors in vivo. The mice were injected in the flank with 1 × 107 PANC-1 cells; when tumors were 60–100 mm3 (about 4 weeks), 6 mice were randomized to receive vehicles or onvansertib [30] (50 mg/kg p.o. 5 days a week for 4 weeks), or gemcitabine (weekly 50 mg/kg, i.p. for 4 weeks) [31] or their combination.

Onvansertib alone delayed but did not significantly prevent tumor growth, GEM alone had no obvious effect on tumor growth (Fig. 7A). However, combined treatment significantly reduced tumor size (Fig. 7A). Tumor weights in all groups did not obviously decline (< 10%), and no other signs of toxicity, such as torpor. were observed (not shown).

Discussion

During oncogenesis, cancer not only escapes the body’s regulatory mechanisms, but also gains capabilities to affect local and systemic homeostasis[32]. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) typically develops in an unusually disordered microenvironment, which contributes to its highly aggressive behavior. Gemcitabine is used as a chemotherapy drug for all stages of pancreatic cancer patients, but it will cause drug resistance after short-term use. In addition, some patients with pancreatic cancer have resistance to gemcitabine from beginning to end. The sensitivity of gemcitabine can be improved only when combined with other chemotherapy drugs or targeted drugs [23], and the mechanism is still unknown.

Gemcitabine induces apoptosis in gemcitabine sensitive BxPC-3 cells and shows a significant decrease in cell growth vitality. PANC-1 cells were resistant to gemcitabine treatment. The p53-dependent expression of PUMA has been shown as a key determinant of transcription-dependent apoptosis [33]. In the present study, gemcitabine did not cause significant changes in intracellular PUMA expression, and after targeting Bim or Noxa in combination, gemcitabine-induced cell apoptosis was significantly restored, suggesting that it is Bim and Noxa, but not PUMA, is involved in the toxic effect of gemcitabine on pancreatic cancer BxPC-3 cells. Our study is consistent with that of Hill’s study, PUMA does not play a major role in the effect of gemcitabine on colon cancer and pancreatic cancer cells [34].

The primary resistance is related to the presence of intrinsic resistance genes within cells, while acquired resistance is related to the activation of certain resistance genes by drugs [35, 36]. PI3K/AKT/mTOR and RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK are frequently activated in malignant tumor cells, sometimes coexisting in the same cell. The former has a higher activation frequency, leading to changes in cellular biological behavior [37].

Gemcitabine resistance is related to the activation of PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways [38–40]. Here we shown that there is high phosphorylation of AKT1 and ERK1/2, especially AKT1. ERK1/2 phosphorylation increases further within 10 min of exposure to gemcitabine, reaches its peak at 3 h, and decreases to basal level within 6 h. AKT1 is also activated, but the degree of activation is not significant, which may be related to the presence of endogenous AKT1 high phosphorylation. Therefore, endogenous or acquired AKT1 and ERK1/2 phosphorylation is associated with gemcitabine resistance. There are different opinions on the signal that AKT1 and ERK1/2 affect the sensitivity of pancreatic cancer cells to gemcitabine [41]. In our study, upon reactivation of ERK1/2 or AKT1, gemcitabine-induced apoptosis was significantly reduced, suggesting that activation of ERK1/2 or AKT1 is relative to gemcitabine resistance.

The research on enhancing gemcitabine sensitivity of pancreatic cancer or combined application of multiple chemotherapy drugs or combined immunity, radiotherapy and chemotherapy has been carried out for more than 20 years, which has raised the 5-year survival rate of pancreatic cancer patients from 2% to less 11% [1]. In the present, we showed that gemcitabine mediates its anti-cancer effects via activation of pro-apoptotic Bim and Noxa, the resistance to gemcitabine is associated with endogenous AKT1 and ERK1/2 activation, as well as acquired AKT1 and ERK1/2 activation leading to inhibition of Bim and Noxa expression. Therefore, there is an urgent need for new treatment options or blocking AKT1 and ERK1/2 activation.

Polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1) is highly conserved, and enhanced PLK1 is associated with resistance to cytotoxic drugs, tumor progression and poor prognosis. Targeting PLK1 can inhibited MEK-ERK-RSK1 signal, resulting in cell cycle arrest [42]. Enhanced PLK1 contributes to development of chemoresistance by activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, and targeting PLK1 can reverse chemotherapy resistance by inhibiting PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling [43]. Similar observations were found in the present study.

PLK1 was overexpressed in PDAC tissues and cell lines. There may be a significant correlation between excessive expression of PLK1 in pancreatic tumor tissue and the biological behavior of the tumor. Due to limited specimen data, the clinical and pathological characteristics of PLK1 and PDAC, as well as their relationship with chemotherapy drug resistance, have not been explored.

In this study, we investigated the sensitivity of PLK1 to gemcitabine in BxPC-3 and PANC-1 cells. we found that targeting PLK1 has pro-apoptotic and growth inhibitory effects, and can enhance cell sensitivity to gemcitabine. Enhanced PLK1 can inhibit gemcitabine -induced cell apoptosis and decreased the chemosensitivity to gemcitabine, suggesting that PLK1 overexpression is related with chemoresistance to gemcitabine. In addition, gemcitabine induces PLK1 expression in a short period of time, further confirming that gemcitabine acquired resistance is related to gemcitabine-induced PLK1 upregulation.

PLK1 regulates PI3K/AKT/mTOR and MEK-ERK-RSK1 signals, by which affects cell apoptosis and chemotherapy resistance [42, 43]. Our current study has shown that targeting PLK1 reduced both phosphorylation of AKT1 and ERK1/2 levels. Targeting PLK1 upregulated ERK1/2-dependent Bim, and enhances cell sensitivity to gemcitabine. Furthermore, targeting PLK1 upregulated AKT1-dependent Noxa, and enhances cell sensitivity to gemcitabine. After blocking Bim or Noxa, the sensitivity of Bxpc-3 cells to gemcitabine was disappeared, indicating that PLK1 inhibition enhances cell sensitivity to gemcitabine by blocking AKT1 and ERK1/2 signaling. Similarly, overexpression of PLK1 enhances cell resistance to gemcitabine by activating AKT1 and ERK1/2 signaling.

In recent years, inhibitors of PLK1, such as onvansertib, has been undergoing clinical trials in combination with other drugs, and the combination was well tolerated [31, 44–46]. In patient-derived platinum-resistant ovarian carcinomas, combined onvansertib and gemcitabine resulted in a significant increase in survival compared to controls and single drugs [47]. Targeted PLK1 using siRNA enhances the sensitivity of pancreatic cancer cells to gemcitabine in vitro. Next, we studied the anti-tumor effect of targeted PLK1 using onvansertib on transplanted tumors in vivo. Herein, we showed that onvansertib significantly potentiates gemcitabine-associated cell growth in the PANC-1 xenograft tumors, similar synergistic effect as in vitro. It has found that phosphatidylinositol-5-phosphate-4-kinase-type-2-A(PIP4K2A) is the potential volasertib (the small molecular PLK1 inhibitor) off-targets, which may be related to the side effects seen in volasertib-treated patients, but PIP4K2A, however, was not affected by another PLK1 inhibitor onvansertib [47]. In our study, measurement of mouse weight in all models showed a less than 10% loss of body weight in response to ONV or the combination therapy. Whether potential off-targets are affected by PLK1-small molecule inhibitor onvansertib in future onvansertib patients need further investigation. We observed here that AKT1 and ERK1/2 was significantly inhibited in the onvansertib and gemcitabine treated tumors, following by the upregulation of pro-apoptotic Bim and Noxa, and increased PARP cleavage. This observation suggests that onvansertib enhances gemcitabine sensitivity by inducing cell apoptosis.

Our in vitro and in vivo data showed that targeting PLK1 using siRNA or PLK1 inhibitor inhibited AKT1 and ERK1/2 activity and upregulated AKT1-dependent Noxa and ERK1/2-dependent-Bim, by which promoted cell apoptosis, inhibited cell growth, and enhances cell sensitivity to gemcitabine. The high expression of endogenous AKT1 and ERK1/2 is associated with gemcitabine resistance, while acquired gemcitabine resistance is associated with PLK1 reactivation or AKT1 and ERK1/2 reactivation, which affected the expression of pro-apoptotic genes. Overall, our study demonstrates the important roles of targeting PLK1 or onvansertib in combination with gemcitabine in inhibiting tumorigenesis in vitro and vivo. Therefore, combining gemcitabine and onvansertib might be a better method used for gemcitabine-resistant PDAC patients.
