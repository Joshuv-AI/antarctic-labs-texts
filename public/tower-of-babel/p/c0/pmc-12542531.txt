Trends and factors associated with modern contraceptive use among sexually active female youths in Tanzania, 2004–2022
Authors: Farida Ollomy, Sia E Msuya, Jim Todd, Sylvia Meku, Ednah Loishiye Samwel, Jackson Lutobeka, Erick A Mboya, Douglas Ndeki, Innocent B Mboya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe study aimed to assess the trends and factors associated with modern contraceptive use among female youths (15–24 years) in Tanzania from 2004 to 2022.DesignWe performed secondary analysis of cross-sectional data from four consecutive Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) in Tanzania: 2004, 2010, 2015/2016 and 2022.SettingTanzania.ParticipantsSexually active female youths (n=8659).Primary outcomeThe use of any modern contraceptive method, coded as Yes or No.ResultsThe sample had a median age of 21 (IQR 18–22 years), with 63.7% aged 20–24 years. Modern contraceptive use among female youths increased from 24.6% in 2004 to 32.1% in 2022. Consistent across surveys, modern contraceptive use was positively associated with secondary or higher education level, having one or more live births and engaging in sexual activity within the month preceding the survey. Additionally, adolescents (15–19 years) demonstrated a weak protective association, indicating a reduced likelihood of modern contraceptive use compared with young women (20–24 years). In 2015/2016 and 2022 surveys, primiparous, multiparous and married/cohabiting women were more likely to use modern contraceptives than nulliparous women.ConclusionsModern contraceptive use among female youths increased gradually in Tanzania between 2004 and 2022 Tanzania DHS. To sustain these upward trends and reduce the risk of unwanted pregnancies and sexually transmitted infections, public health policies should consider the factors positively associated with modern contraceptive use, particularly among young women aged 20–24 years, married/cohabiting, who have initiated childbearing and those with high education levels.

Introduction

The sexual and reproductive health (SRH) of female youths remains a key component of the global burden of poor sexual health, resulting in a detrimental effect on their transition into adulthood.1 In many African countries, the need for SRH services among youths is marginalised, despite their established necessity and urgency.2 Unwanted pregnancies are among the SRH issues that remain a significant public health concern among female youths aged 15–24 years in developing countries. Approximately 14 million unintended pregnancies are reported annually in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA).3 In Tanzania, the trend of unwanted pregnancies has been increasing from 19.4% to 30.9% among adolescents (15–19 years), and 22% to 31% among young women (20–24 years) from 2004 to 2016, respectively.4 5 Unintended pregnancies at a young age are linked to adverse health, educational, social and economic outcomes, consequently increasing the risk of maternal deaths, illnesses and disabilities such as obstetrical fistula, complications of unsafe abortion, as well as sexually transmitted infections, including HIV and health risks to infants.468

Modern contraceptive methods are widely used to promote safer sexual practices, reduce unwanted pregnancies and unsafe abortions and help women control their fertility, contributing to controlling population growth.9 Family planning programmes are also crucial in reducing maternal and child mortality rates through delaying, spacing or limiting pregnancies with various methods.10 Globally, the correct use of modern contraceptives may reduce 2.1 million unplanned and unintended births, 3.2 million abortions and 5600 maternal deaths each year. Most importantly, it provides youths with an opportunity to be confident in deciding when they want to have their children, prevent sexually transmitted infections and improve their chances to complete education and achieve their career goals.1113 Contraceptive prevalence among female youths in SSA varies by country, ranging from 3.5% in Central Africa to 20.7% in East Africa.1315 These estimates are less than the global sustainable development goals target of 75% by 2030. Also, the prevalence of modern contraceptive use among female youths is generally lower than that of adult women.1315 In Tanzania, modern contraceptive use among young women (15–24 years) was 16.0% in 2022.5 Studies have shown that low contraceptive use and high fertility rates are related, and these are highly correlated with the risk of unwanted pregnancies.16 17 In Tanzania, the fertility rate per 1000 adolescent girls and young women has been declining steadily from 132 in 2004 to 112 in 2022.18 19

Additionally, in Tanzania, policies and regulations support the implementation of adolescent and youth-friendly SRH services. Adolescents and young women who use these services can have access to free contraception services, receive family planning education and complete SRH education at all facilities and communities.20 21 This is to ensure that the country meets the adolescent fertility rate target of <90 births per 1000 adolescents and 54% of female youths having access to modern contraceptives by 2030.22 23 Despite the efforts, just about a quarter (26%) of female youths in 2022 had access to modern contraceptives compared with adult women (31%).18 Studies among female youths are essential as they do not have the same needs for or access to contraceptives as adult women, while female adolescents are usually under-represented despite facing disproportionate medical, social and economic consequences of unwanted pregnancies.24 It is well known that reproductive choices made by female youths have an enormous impact on their prospects for health, schooling and employment as well as their overall transition to adulthood.25

The study aimed to assess the trends and factors associated with modern contraceptive use among sexually active female youths in Tanzania. This will provide evidence to support interventions to promote universal access to SRH services, achieve gender equality and empower all women and girls.

Methods

Data source, setting and study design

We performed secondary analysis of cross-sectional data from four consecutive Tanzania Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS): 2004, 2010, 2015/2016 and 2022. The surveys aimed to collect demographic and health information on marriage, fertility, family planning, reproductive health, child health and HIV/AIDS to assist policymakers and programme managers in evaluating and designing programmes and strategies to improve the country’s population health.18 26

Tanzania is a country in East Africa, covering an area of 945 000 square kilometres, 60 000 of which is inland water. The country has over 61 million people with an annual population growth rate of 3.2%.18 Usual residents of the selected households or those who slept in the selected household on the night before the survey were eligible to participate in the survey. All eligible married and unmarried female youths who reported ever having sex were eligible for inclusion. The total sample analysed from the four Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey (TDHS) was 8659 female youths, after excluding respondents who never had sex, had no desire for a child in the next 2 years, were currently pregnant and declared infecund (figure 1).

Variables

Outcome variable

The outcome variable in this study was modern contraceptive use, which was measured by asking participants, ‘Are you or your partner currently doing something or using any method to delay or avoid getting pregnant?’. The modern contraceptive methods for this study include male and female sterilisation, injectable, intrauterine devices (IUDs), oral contraceptive pills, implants, male and female condoms, emergency contraception and lactation amenorrhoea method. Modern contraceptive use was dichotomised as ‘Yes’ if female youths were currently using any modern contraceptive method and ‘No’ if otherwise.

Independent variables

The independent variables consisted of socio-demographic and reproductive health characteristics, and exposure to family planning information. The socio-demographic characteristics were woman’s age (15–19, 20–24 years), partner’s age (15–24, 25–34, 35+years), geographical zones (Western, Northern, Central, Southern Highlands, Southern, Southwest Highlands, Lake, Eastern, Zanzibar), area of residence (urban, rural), respondent and partner education level (no education, primary, secondary and above), marital status (unmarried/cohabiting, married), wealth index (poor, middle, rich) and working status (not working, working). Reproductive health characteristics were age at first sexual intercourse (<15, 15–17, 18–24 years), parity (nulliparous, primiparous, multiparous), decision-maker for contraceptive use (woman, partner, joint decision), fertility desire (wants a child after 2+years, wants a child but unsure of timing, undecided, want no more), pregnancy termination (Yes, No). Exposure to family planning information included having had a household visit from a family planning field worker in the 12 months preceding the surveys, family planning media exposure (television, radio or newspaper) and modern contraceptive knowledge, all of which required binary responses (Yes, No). Some variables, such as education level, fertility desire and family planning media exposure, were categorised based on previous literature for better interpretation and comparability between studies.9 27 28 Wealth index criteria, the households were given scores based on the number and kind of consumer goods they own, ranging from television to bicycle or car and housing characteristics such as source of water, toilet facilities and flooring materials.

Statistical analysis

Data cleaning and analysis were performed using Stata V.14.0. Data analysis accounted for the complex design of DHS by considering the primary sampling units (clusters), strata (rural vs urban residence) and survey weights for both descriptive and inferential statistics. The Cochran-Armitage test was used to assess the trends of modern contraceptive use among female youth across the survey years.

Generalised linear models with the Poisson family and log-link function (also referred to as log-linear regression models) were used to determine the factors associated with modern contraceptive use among sexually active female youths. Crude analysis was done separately for each survey year to estimate the unadjusted relative risk (RR) and 95% CIs for the association with modern contraceptive use. To account for the confounding effect, multivariable log-linear regression models were used to estimate the adjusted RR estimates of modern contraceptive use determinants adjusted for covariates. All associations were considered statistically significant at a p value of <0.05.

Ethical consideration

Permission to use DHS data was granted by the DHS Measure Evaluation programme. Detailed methodology and ethical considerations for the DHS programme are published in the DHS reports.18 21

Patient and public involvement

No patient or public was directly involved in the development of the study design, protocol, data collection, results and dissemination of the study.

Results

Participant characteristics by survey years

The socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents are shown in table 1. The median age (IQR) of 8659 total respondents analysed was similar across all four TDHS, that is, 21 (18–22) years, and most were aged 20–24 years (63.7%), whose proportion slightly increased from 64.3% in 2004 to 67% in 2022. Most respondents had a primary education level, but the proportion slightly decreased across the surveys (63.8% in 2004 to 50.2% in 2022). The percentage of female youths with secondary education or higher increased over time from 9.9% in 2004 to 36.9% in 2022. About half of the study respondents were not married; 50.5% in 2010 to 50.4% in 2022, while about one-third resided in rural areas, but the percentage decreased slightly from 67.5% in 2004 to 63.8% in 2022.

Trends of modern contraceptives by the survey years

The overall proportion of modern contraceptive use among sexually active female youths has significantly increased (p value<0.001) from 24.6% in 2004 to 34.6% in 2015/2016, then decreased slightly to 32.1% in 2022. Across age categories, the prevalence of modern contraceptive use among adolescents (15–19 years) increased from 16.9% in 2004 to 27.3% in 2010, then declined to 21.9% in 2015/2016 and remained stagnant (21.8%) in 2022 (p value=0.004). Among young women (20–24 years), a significant increase in modern contraceptive use was observed from 28.8% in 2004 to 35.9% in 2022 (p value<0.001) (figure 2). Additionally, trends in specific modern contraceptives revealed that across all four TDHS rounds, the use of implants increased rapidly and consistently from 0.9% in 2004/2005 to 17.8% in 2022, p value=0.02. Overall, on average, the commonly used methods of modern contraceptives in this population, specifically contraceptive pills, IUDs, injectables and condoms, declined between survey periods (table 3).

Factors associated with modern contraceptive use

In the crude analysis, across the four survey rounds, we observed a higher RR of modern contraceptive use among female youths with primary and secondary education compared with no education, married/cohabiting, with at least one (primiparous) or more live birth (multiparous) and who recently engaged in sexual activity (ie, within 1 month preceding the survey).

In the adjusted analysis, adolescent girls (15–19 years), from low socio-economic status, with no exposure to family planning information through media, with primary and secondary or higher education and those who recently engaged in sexual activity had a significant positive association with modern contraceptive use (table 3). Also, sexually active female youths visited by a family planning field worker in the last 12 months, being married/cohabiting with a partner, having no occupation and having had one or more births had a significant association with modern contraceptive use in the 2015/2016 and 2022 survey years.

Although a strong negative association between age categories (15–19 vs 20–24 years) with modern contraceptive use was observed in the 2004 and 2015/2016 survey rounds, weak associations were observed in the 2015/2016 and 2022 survey rounds. The effect of women’s secondary education or higher on modern contraceptive use decreased across the three surveys from (RR 1.68, 95% CI 1.13 to 2.50) in 2004 to (RR 1.39, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.85) in 2015/2016, then increased to (RR 1.64, 95% CI 1.23 to 2.19) in 2022. Similar patterns were observed for primary education level, with the positive association declining from (RR 1.72, 95% CI 1.29 to 2.29) in 2004 to (RR 1.59, 95% CI 1.20 to 2.108) in 2022.

The positive association between parity and modern contraceptive use remained significant in the 2015/2016 and 2022 survey rounds. For instance, after controlling for other factors, sexually active female youths with two or more live births (multiparous) had over three times significantly higher RR of modern contraceptive use (RR 1.52, 95% CI 1.19 to 1.92) in the 2015/2016 survey, an association that almost doubled in the 2022 survey (RR 3.99, 95% CI 2.81 to 5.65) survey.

Moreover, the positive association between recent sexual activity became weaker over the years, declining from (RR 3.13, 95% CI 2.46 to 3.99) in 2004 to (RR 2.31, 95% CI 1.96 to 2.73) in the 2022 survey (table 4). We found little indication of confounding by any of the related factors on modern contraception. Likewise, findings (not reported) from decomposition analysis to assess the determinants of changes in the trends of modern contraceptive use over time were not meaningful for interpretation.

Discussion

The study aimed to assess the trends and factors associated with changes in modern contraceptive use among female youths aged 15–24 years in Tanzania from 2004 to 2022. Modern contraceptive use increased by 7.5% over the 17 years from 24.6% in 2004 to 32.1% in 2022. A higher proportion of modern contraceptive use was observed among young women aged 20–24 years compared with adolescent girls aged 15–19 years. The most common modern contraceptive method used was injections and implants, which had steadily increased trends across surveys. In all survey years, modern contraceptive use was positively associated with secondary or higher education level, having one or more live births and engaging in sexual activity within the month preceding the survey.

The current study demonstrated an increasing trend of modern contraceptive use over the past 17-year period. Similar rising trends have been reported from a study conducted in Ethiopia.29 In Tanzania, the use of implants has increased drastically in this population, while that of oral contraceptive pills, injections and male condoms decreased during the study period, which may reflect the increased access and availability of long-acting reversible contraceptives among female youths in Tanzania. Our findings are consistent with a previous study conducted using DHS data in Ethiopia and Sierra Leone, where the use of injections and implants had increased among female adolescents.30 31 The observed increase in the modern contraceptive prevalence could be attributed to the efforts by the government of Tanzania to recognise female youths’ needs for family planning services in policies and interventions.32

The study found a lower likelihood of modern contraceptive use among adolescent girls (15–19 years) than among young women (20–24 years). This is similar to a previous DHS comparative study and a community-based cross-sectional study conducted in Ethiopia and Ghana, respectively.27 33 These findings could be explained by the fact that female youths between the ages of 20 and 24 are thought to recognise the effects of engaging in sexual activity without using contraception better than those aged 15–19 years.34 Also, this could be an indication of suboptimal access to family planning services, including adolescent-friendly SRH services, coupled with inadequate knowledge on where and when to obtain these services.35

Female youths living in rural areas and with low socio-economic status were less likely to use modern contraceptives than their counterparts. These findings are in agreement with previous studies.28 36 37 This could be attributed to the fact that female youths from high socio-economic status have more economic power to access modern contraception in terms of costs, such as emergency contraceptive pills, which are expensive for them to afford over the counter, as they are not provided for free.

We further observe that women who were working had a higher likelihood of modern contraceptive use than those who were not. The finding is consistent with previous studies that showed a similar relationship.12 35 One possible explanation is that working women are too busy with work-related activities to have babies, hence they choose to use modern contraceptives. Additionally, female youths who are working are likely to be more educated, have exposure to family planning information and may be able to afford modern contraceptives.12 35 Positive association with modern contraceptive use were observed among women who were educated than those without any formal education. These women are more likely to be aware of the advantages of existing contraceptive methods for the prevention of sexually transmitted infections and unintended pregnancies. Therefore, family planning services should be tailored to address the needs of this younger population by promoting their general awareness and understanding of basic SRH issues and contraceptive choices for informed decisions.

The findings from this study also revealed that female youths with media exposure to family planning information and having received a household family planning visit had null associations with modern contraceptive use, which is contrary to previous studies.37 This finding may imply that the provision of family planning information through media and home visits is not an efficient platform among this population in Tanzania. The findings could also be an indication that young people mostly prefer to receive family planning information and services from trained healthcare providers in primary healthcare settings. To fully comprehend the observed link, mixed methods studies are necessary.

The strength of this study was that it benefitted from a nationally representative large dataset, which ensured adequate power and provided an opportunity for generalisation of our findings. Several limitations need to be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the study was prone to reporting and social desirability bias, which resulted in under-reporting of sexual activity among unmarried female youth and the use of modern contraceptive methods. Additionally, the DHS employed a cross-sectional design, hence incapable of inferring a causal-effect relationship between modern contraceptive use and covariates of interest.

Conclusion

Our study highlighted a steady increase in modern contraceptive use among sexually active female youths in Tanzania from 24.6% in 2004 to 32.1% in 2022. This increase was mostly driven by changes in modern contraceptive use behaviours, particularly those aged 20–24 years, married/cohabiting, who initiated childbearing, with high education levels, with one or more live births and with recent sexual activity. Despite the increasing trends, modern contraceptive use is still low and far behind the national target of 54% by 2030. To sustain these upward trends and reduce the risk of unwanted pregnancies and sexually transmitted infections, public health policies should consider inequalities and barriers to modern contraceptive use in this population. An inter-sectoral collaboration in promoting parent–child communication on SRH to prevent unwanted pregnancy and its adverse outcomes is paramount. More studies are needed to explore the barriers and gaps in youths’ SRH services, the effectiveness of family planning interventions and communication programmes among youths.
