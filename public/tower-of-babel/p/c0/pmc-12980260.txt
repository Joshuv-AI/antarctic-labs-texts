Hydrothermal Carbonization of Sucrose into Hydrochar: Insights into Surface Chemistry for Efficient Removal of Cationic Dyes
Authors: Pacharaphon Khaopueak, Kanokorn Wechakorn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Synthetic dyes, which are extensively used in textile and other industries, produce wastewater that is toxic, recalcitrant, and non-biodegradable. As such, environmental concerns are drawing attention to sustainable adsorbents of the dyes. In this study, hydrochar was green-synthesized using table sugar (sucrose) via hydrothermal carbonization (HTC) at 200 °C for 12 h without toxic additives. The hydrochar was analyzed in terms of elemental, thermal stability, and point of zero charge (pHPZC ≈ 6). Adsorption studies were performed using cationic dyes, methylene blue (MB), rhodamine B (RhB), and rhodamine 6G (R6G), as well as anionic dyes, methyl orange and eosin blue, for comparative purposes. The hydrochar showed high adsorption for MB (2.0747 mg/g), RhB (3.5063 mg/g), and R6G (1.8268 mg/g), while nearly no anionic dyes were adsorbed. The kinetics data were well described by a pseudo-second-order model, and the equilibrium data were well described by a Langmuir isotherm, indicating that monolayer adsorption was occurring on homogeneous surface sites. The reusability evaluations revealed stable MB adsorption and moderate desorption (in the case of RhB and R6G) through several cycles, indicating practical utility. Moreover, the high adsorption capacity and good recyclability of the sucrose-derived hydrochar demonstrated its potential as an effective, low-cost, and environmentally friendly adsorbent for cationic dye removal, contributing to the application of sustainable water treatment in the context of a circular economy.

Introduction

Synthetic dyes from the textile, pharmaceutical, cosmetic, and food sectors are becoming an ever greater global concern due to their release into aqueous environments. , They are not only a aesthetic pollutants in that they change the color of water, but they are also dangerous chemicals with high stability against natural decomposition and conventional processes for wastewater treatment. , In particular, several cationic dyes, such as methylene blue (MB), rhodamine B (RhB), and rhodamine 6G (Rh6G), have significant biological impacts and are resistant to treatment. RhB is a xanthene dye derivative with a high water solubility. It is often used as a colorant and tracer, and its lethal concentration for aquatic species (LC50) value of 14–24 mg/L makes it more toxic than other dyes. Even at levels as low as 1.0 mg/L, the release of RhB from industrial activities into wastewater makes the water harmful for both humans and aquatic life since it is a neurotoxin, a carcinogen, and a mutagen, leading to irritation of the skin, eyes, and respiratory tract. , The dye Rh6G, which is structurally related to RhB, is used in fluorescence and textile processing applications; however, it raises concerns regarding environmental persistence. MB, a cationic thiazine dye, is even more disturbingit is carcinogenic, mutagenic, and very persistent as a risk to the health of human beings as well as ecosystems. At dosages greater than 5 mg/kg, the monoamine oxidase inhibitory properties of MB dye can cause lethal serotonin poisoning in humans, in addition to being a risk to aquatic species.

Nevertheless, conventional wastewater treatment technologies, such as advanced oxidation processes, membrane filtration, and biodegradation, continue to be energy-intensive, costly, and susceptible to operational inefficiencies. , Moreover, advanced treatments are liable to cause secondary pollution. Hence, the adsorption technique has been preferred for dye remediation due to its simplicity, cost-efficiency, and high removal efficiency. However, activated carbon, as the most employed adsorbent, has a high cost and often unsustainable production, requiring chemical activators and fossil feedstocks. , Therefore, sustainable carbon-containing adsorbents have been the focus of research and development, particularly hydrochar, produced by hydrothermal carbonization (HTC). , HTC is a green thermochemical technique that transforms biomass into a carbon-containing solid product in water at 180–280 °C without drying or the need for distinct chemical sources. The HTC technique retains 50–80% of the biomass carbon in the solid product, uses water as the primary reaction medium, and yields hydrochar with acceptable surface appearance; these functional groups include the hydroxyl, carbonyl, and carboxyl clusters suited for adsorption purposes. ,

Among potential feedstocks, sucrose (C12H22O11) represents a chemically pure, inexpensive, and renewable disaccharide species that may be useful as both a model precursor and a method for studying the hydrothermal formation of hydrochar. Its predictable decomposition in HTC conditions has allowed the production of well-defined hydrochar particles, often as carbon microspheres with high surface activity (good functionalization) and moderate porosity. Recently, hierarchical activated carbon was synthesized using a molten state of sucrose at high temperatures, and a dual-templating technique with mild activators (K2C2O4·H2O and CaCO3) was used for dye removal. Compared to complex lignocellulosic biomass, sucrose can be precisely and reproducibly synthesized without using any external catalysts or harmful additives, which is entirely in accordance with green chemistry principles.

The objective of this study is to develop a sustainable and environmentally friendly hydrochar produced from table sugar through HTC for use as an efficient dye adsorbent. This synthetic procedure is in line with green synthesis, as it does not use any toxic additives or chemical reagents. Although the prepared hydrochar has preferential adsorption toward cationic dyes, it provides a pathway for biomass valorization and demonstrates the potential of saccharide-derived hydrochars as efficient and low-cost materials for efficient, renewable water reuse systems and sustainable water technology in the framework of a circular economy.

Materials and Methods

Reagents and Instruments

Table sugar was purchased from a commercial market. All chemicals were of analytical grade and required no further purification. Methylene blue and methyl orange were purchased from Daejung (Korea). Rhodamine 6G was purchased from Thermo Scientific (China). Eosin B was purchased from Thermo Scientific (India). Rhodamine B was purchased from Loba Chemie (India).

UV–vis absorption spectra were measured on a Peak UV-1601 spectrophotometer. CHNS analysis was obtained from a FlashSmart Thermo Fisher Scientific elemental analyzer (Italy). Fourier-transformed infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed by using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet is5/iD7 ATR instrument. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was obtained by using a D/teX Ultra 250 X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku) with Cu Kα radiation in the range of 5–80° at a scan rate of 0.8°/min and step width of 0.02°. Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were observed using a JEOL JSM7800F instrument (Japan). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was measured by a Kratos AXIS Ultra DLD instrument (Shimadzu). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed on a STA 449F5 Jupiter thermogravimetric analyzer (NETZSCH) from 30 to 1200 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C min–1 under nitrogen gas. Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface areas were detected using the 3Flex 3500 series with a SmartVacPrep analyzer.

Synthesis of Hydrochar (HC) from Sugar

The solution of sugar (15.0 g) in deionized water (200 mL) was transferred to a Teflon-lined hydrothermal autoclave and heated at 200 °C for 12 h. After that, the resulting mixture was separated by using filter paper to obtain a solid HC. The filtered solid was washed several times with deionized water until the filtrate was colorless and then dried in a hot air oven at 80 °C for 6 h and ground by a mortar to obtain the dark-brown HC solid as a powder (4.0 g, 26.7% yield).

Dye Adsorption Capacity of HC

20.0 mg of HC powder was mixed separately with dye solutions with varying concentrations (each solution 1.0 mL) in order to measure the adsorption capacity of HC. To separate the adsorbents, the mixtures were filtered with filter paper. UV–vis absorption spectroscopy was used to examine the supernatants both before and after the HC was added. HC dosage, dye concentration, and incubation time were all examined in order to maximize the adsorption conditions.

The adsorption efficiency percentage and adsorption capacity of HC were determined using the following equations: Adsorption(%)=Ci−CeCi×100 Adsorptioncapacity(q)=(Ci−Ce)VW where C i and C e are the dye concentrations (in mg/L) before and after the addition of HC, respectively, V is the volume of the dye solution (L), and W is the amount of HC (g).

Adsorption Kinetics

Adsorption kinetic experiments were conducted to establish the duration required to achieve the adsorption equilibrium. The adsorption kinetics of various cationic dyes, including RhB, Rh6G, and MB, were evaluated using 20 mg of HC with 1 mL of aqueous dye solutions (50 mg/L) at ambient temperature. Adsorption kinetic data was analyzed using pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order adsorption models according to the following equations: pseudo‐first‐order:ln(qe−qt)=ln⁡qe−k1tpseudo‐second‐order:tqt=1k2qe2+tqe where q e (mg/g) indicates the adsorption capacity at equilibrium, q t (mg/g) indicates the adsorption capacity at time t, k 1 (min–1) indicates the rate constant of pseudo-first-order adsorption, and k 2 (g/mg·min) indicates the rate constant of pseudo-second-order adsorption.

Point of Zero Charge of HC

The pH drift method was used to determine the point of zero charge (pHPZC) of HC. Solutions with pH values of 2 to 12 were prepared using HCl or NaOH. HC (20 mg) was added to each initial solution (1 mL), and the mixtures were sonicated for 15 min and then mixed within 1 h. Each mixture was then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. Finally, the final pH values after the filtration of HC were measured. The initial pHi and final pHf values were used to calculate the pHPZC. The change in pH (ΔpH = pHf – pHi) was plotted against pHi to determine pHPZC, defined as the point where the values are equivalent (ΔpH = 0) to the point of intersection on the x-axis.

Reusability Experiments for Dye Removal

The adsorption–desorption experiments were elucidated using an HC dosage of 20 mg and a dye concentration of 30 mg/L in deionized water (1.0 mL). HC (20 mg) was dispersed in deionized water (500 μL), followed by ultrasonication for 15 min. The stock solution (60 mg/L) of several dyes, including RhB, Rh6G, and MB (500 μL), was added to the suspension at room temperature. After incubation for 1 h, HC was separated by centrifugation at 8,000 rpm for 10 min. To determine the adsorption performance of HC, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 555, 527, and 627 nm for RhB, Rh6G, and MB, respectively. For dye desorption, the HC residue was washed with methanol, and the resulting dye solution was then removed by centrifugation at 8,000 rpm for 10 min. For 10 consecutive adsorption–desorption processes, the HC without drying was reused to absorb these dyes under the same conditions.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of Hydrochar

The HC was easily prepared from table sugar via the hydrothermal method at 200 °C for 12 h through a multistep reaction mechanism, including dehydration, polymerization, and aromatization pathways (Figure ). To study the chemical composition and the surface characteristics of HC, the field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM) technique was used (Figure S1). The HC illustrated spherical morphologies with an average size of HC being 0.58 ± 0.09 μm, in good agreement with previous reports for carbon microspheres of sugars fabricated via HTC. The surface morphology of the HC particles was quite smooth (Figure a). The presence of these microspheres shows that the sucrose solution underwent polymerization and carbonization, forming spherical microparticles of hydrochar. The chemical compositions of HC were carbon (75.4%) and oxygen (24.6%). Additionally, the chemical components of HC were studied using CHNS elemental analysis, which showed mostly the carbon element (64.8%) and a moderate content of hydrogen (4.3%), with no detectable nitrogen and sulfur elements, suggesting that the HC has a higher degree of aromatic condensation with fewer oxygenated functional groups. The functional groups of HC were further characterized using XPS and FTIR methods. The survey XPS spectrum of HC exhibited C 1s (285 eV) and O 1s (533 eV) (Figure b). The high-resolution C 1s spectra of HC showed several binding energies at 285.0, 286.1, 287.2, and 288.1 eV, which are attributed to C–C, C–O, CO, and OC–O bonds, respectively (Figure c). For the high-resolution spectra of O 1s, the four binding energies were found at 531.5, 532.3, 533.2, and 534.0 eV, corresponding to CO, C–O, O–H, and O–CO, respectively (Figure d). Moreover, the FTIR spectrum showed C–O stretching (1162 and 1296 cm–1) and O–H stretching (3368 cm–1), assigned to the hydroxyl group of HC (Figure e). The CO stretching showed a peak for the carboxylic group at 1700 cm–1. The conjugated carbon framework presented several peaks such as C–H stretching (2914 cm–1), CC stretching (1604 cm–1), and C–H bending (796 cm–1). From XPS and FTIR analysis, it was found that the surface of HC is abundant in oxygen-containing functional groups, including hydroxyls and carboxylic groups. Moreover, the crystal structure of HC was determined by using XRD analysis (Figure f). The XRD pattern exhibited a broad peak at 2θ = 19.2°, suggesting the amorphous graphite structure of HC owing to the oxygen-surface functional groups.

The thermogravimetric (TGA) and derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) analyses of HC were also discussed (Figure ). The TGA and DTG curves exhibited a two-step mass decomposition from room temperature to 1200 °C. A first weight loss of 4.66 wt% at 120 °C was observed, which is assigned to the physisorbed moisture and weakly bound volatiles. , The major decomposition process occured at 860.6 °C with the total mass-loss of 43.60 wt% since this step involved cleavage of oxygenated surface groups (−OH, −COOH, CO) and scission of aliphatic chains and continues with the progressive aromatization/condensation of the hydrochar framework. At 1200 °C, the residual mass remaining was 50.19 wt% owing to a rich fixed carbon content of HC and good thermal stability. The large residue corresponds to the CHNS composition (C = 64.76 wt%, H = 4.33 wt%, N ≈ 0, S ≈ 0), indicating the characteristic of hydrochars derived from saccharides.

Adsorption Capacity of Hydrochar

The adsorption capacity of hydrochar was tested against different cationic organic dyes, such as rhodamine B (RhB), rhodamine 6G (R6G), methyl orange (MO), and methylene blue (MB), as well as anionic dyes such as eosin B (EB) (Figure ). To achieve the optimal adsorption conditions, the hydrochar doses were normalized to 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 mg/mL. It was concluded that with the rise of hydrochar weight, the adsorption efficiency of dye increased. In this experiment, a hydrochar weight of 20 mg/mL was selected as it demonstrated the highest dye adsorption efficiency.

(a, e) UV–vis absorption spectra and (f) % removal plots with different HC doses in the presence of various dyes (100 μg/mL), including (a) MB, (b) MO, (c) EB, (d) RhB, and (e) R6G.

Adsorption Isotherm of Hydrochar

Hydrochar demonstrated excellent removal efficiencies for RhB and R6G, with percentage adsorptions of 98.5% and 99.8%, respectively (Figure ). The HC showed lower adsorption of methylene blue (85% removal) due to MB’s planar structure and smaller size. In concentrations higher than 100 mg/L, HC showed dye removal of less than 20%. The bulky xanthene ring system of RhB creates different interaction mechanisms (e.g., π–π stacking). Whereas the low adsorption was observed for anionic dyes such as MO and EB. These trends clearly demonstrate that sucrose-derived hydrochar exhibits preferential adsorption toward cationic dyes, such as MB, RhB, and R6G.

Percentage removal of several dyes in the concentration range 20–200 mg/L using HC (20 mg/mL).

The Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin isotherm models were employed to describe the adsorption equilibria of MB, RhB, and R6G on HC (Figure ). The Langmuir model considers a homogeneous adsorbent surface with a finite number of uniform sites and monolayer coverage with no multilayer adsorption. The Freundlich model considers a heterogeneous surface interaction between adsorbed molecules with different energies, facilitating multilayer adsorption and equilibrium. The Temkin isotherm assumes that the adsorption heat decreases linearly with surface coverage and that the binding energies are uniformly distributed up to a maximal value, taking into account adsorbent–adsorbate interactions. The above procedure was primarily done for RhB, Rh6G, and MB, which showed very high adsorption to quantify their capacities (Figure ). The isotherm curves of MO and EB could not be obtained due to low adsorption. The Langmuir model of MB, RhB, and Rh6G provided higher correlation coefficients (R2 ranging from 0.9846 to 0.9923) than the Freundlich and Temkin models, with R2 ranges of 0.701–0.9687 and 0.6687–0.9133, respectively. These results indicated that the adsorption of the three dyes by HC primarily displays monolayer adsorption behavior, which occurs on homogeneous adsorption sites, as predicted by the Langmuir model (Table ). The maximum adsorption capacities (q max) obtained from the Langmuir model fitting were 2.0747, 3.5063, and 1.8268 mg/g for MB, RhB, and R6G, respectively, illustrating that the HC has strong adsorption. The RL values (0.01–0.3) for these dyes were within the favorable adsorption range, confirming the suitability of the Langmuir model. The sucrose-based HC showed effective adsorption for several cationic dyes compared with the previously reported biomass-based hydrochars (Table ). The different q max may result from the different physicochemical properties, such as the chemical compositions, the morphology, or the particle sizes. Thus, these results indicated a uniform surface location on the HC and promised the application of the adsorptive removal of dyes. From the characterization of HC, the polar surface functional groups are significant for adsorption, as they can form hydrogen bonds or electrostatic interactions with dye molecules. Despite the adsorbent’s limited surface area, aromatic π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding between phenol −OH groups and carboxylic acid groups of dyes result in high adsorption efficiency. From the FTIR results, graphitic layers of the hydrochar could interact with the aromatic rings of RhB and Rh6G via the π–π stacking.

Adsorption Kinetics of Hydrochar

Adsorption kinetics analysis is crucial for understanding the rate of adsorbate uptake as well as the effective contact time to reach equilibrium. The adsorption capacities of HC in the presence of cationic dyes, such as MB, RhB, and Rh6G were studied (Figure ). The adsorption time for dye removal showed that HC adsorbed MB and RhB within 5 min. While HC for Rh6G removal showed that the adsorption time was higher than 30 min to reach the equilibrium. Adsorption kinetics of these dyes with the different concentrations, such as 10, 30, and 50 mg/L, were then explored using the pseudo-first-order model and the pseudo-second-order model (Figure ). MB, RhB, and Rh6G show strong correlations to the pseudo-second-order model with R2 = 0.9898–0.9993, 0.9939–0.9983, and 0.998–0.9994, respectively (Table ), whereas the R2 values of the pseudo-first-order model for these dyes were less than the pseudo-second-order model. The experimental adsorption capacities (q e, exp) of the kinetic model were found to be nearly in correspondence with the calculated adsorption capacities (q e, calc), indicating that the adsorption is the pseudo-second-order kinetic model. The results suggest that the dye adsorption on HC occurs through a chemisorption mechanism and the rate of reaction depends on the concentrations of dyes.

The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area of HC was 1.60 m2 g–1, its Langmuir surface area was 0.70 m2 g–1, and its single-point pore volume was 0.0026–0.0051 cm3 g–1 at p/p0 = 0.99, confirmed by the nitrogen physisorption tests. A micropore volume of 0.00179 cm3 g–1 and a negative external surface area confirmed that microporosity was minimal, despite the t-plot approach showing a micropore area of 5.26 m2 g–1. With typical pore diameters ranging from ∼6.6 nm (BET 4 V/A adsorption) to >100 nm (BJH/D–H), BJH and D–H analyses indicated cumulative pore surface areas <0.20 m2 g–1; the latter were probably artifacts linked to very low adsorbed volumes and interparticle spaces rather than actual macropores. With only a very tiny percentage of mesopores in the 6–13 nm range, HC was essentially nonporous overall. Therefore, the surface structure and chemical composition of HC played significant roles in the adsorption of dye. Studies of adsorption isotherms revealed that the process of adsorption takes place at the surface. The surface groups of HC contained oxygen functional groups (−COOH, −OH), which makes the surface negatively charged and attracts the positively charged dye through electrostatic interaction. The polar functional groups on HC may form hydrogen bonds with the polar functional groups of the dye molecules. For instance, the amine or carbonyl groups of RhB have hydrogen bonds with the hydroxyl groups of hydrochar.

The Point of Zero Charge

The surface charge property of HC was determined by using the point of zero charge (pHPZC). Figure shows that at low pH (pHi = 2–4), the ΔpH values are positive, which implies that positive surface charges of HC are predominant under acidic conditions. Even though the HC does not contain any nitrogen-containing groups, the protonation of surface oxygenated functionalities, including carboxyl and hydroxyl groups, under acidic conditions decreases the density of negative sites, resulting in an apparent positive or near-neutral surface potential. By contrast, ΔpH was negative when pHi > 6, indicating that the negative surface charges are under the basic conditions. The intersection where ΔpH = 0 represents the pHPZC value, which was found to be approximately pH 6.0. The value of pHPZC is important in order to explain the adsorption mechanism because surface charge plays an essential role in the interaction with ionic dyes. For instance, at pH < pHPZC, electrostatic attraction favors anionic dye adsorption, while at pH > pHPZC, the adsorption of cationic dyes is enhanced.

Reusability of HC for Dye Adsorption

The reusability and regeneration capacity of an adsorbent are key factors that determine its feasibility for practical applications in wastewater treatment, as they are associated with economic effectiveness, sustainability, and environmental impact. The reusability of HC was demonstrated in over ten consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles of MB, RhB, and R6G (Figure ). RhB showed excellent performance with over 90% removal achieved by the eighth cycle. MB and R6G also showed stable removal, with the former increasing from 65% to 75% by the eighth cycle, while the latter maintained an almost constant removal of 55–60%. The reusability of MB was observed over 70%, indicating a higher long-term use potential. However, RhB removal declined drastically to ∼30% after the eighth cycle. The reduction in the adsorption capacity over the cycle may be attributed to the continuous saturation of adsorption sites, the irreversible capture of dye molecules, and partial changes in the structure of the hydrochar surface during regeneration. The results demonstrated that the capacity of HC to maintain a higher adsorption performance for MB and relatively stable removal for RhB and R6G through many cycles indicates its application value as a reusable adsorbent for the removal of cationic dyes.

Conclusion

The hydrochar was successfully produced from commercial sugar via hydrothermal carbonization at 200 °C in the absence of any chemical agents. The adsorption ability of the obtained hydrochar was tested for both cationic and anionic organic dyes in water. The resulting hydrochar demonstrated excellent adsorption performance toward three dyes, with removal efficiencies of 98.5% for Rhodamine B (RhB), 99.8% for Rhodamine 6G (Rh6G), and 85% for Methylene Blue (MB). The adsorption equilibrium results fitted well with the Langmuir isotherm model, suggesting that monolayer adsorption takes place on a homogeneous surface of hydrochar. In addition, the point of zero charge (pHPZC ≈ 6) showed that the hydrochar surface becomes negatively charged at pH > 6. Therefore, these results revealed that primary physicochemical driving forces govern the adsorption of dyes on hydrochar, which are electrostatic and π–π interactions. The cationic dye ion was directly attracted to the negatively charged surface due to −COO– groups. In addition, the aromatic stacking interactions and hydrogen bonding of the dye with hydrochar stabilized the adsorbent. Reusability showed that the hydrochar could be used multiple times since the efficiency remained at a high level for eight cycles, particularly for MB. Compared to other biomass-derived hydrochars, sugar-derived hydrochar exhibits competitive adsorption capacities and strong potential as a low-cost, eco-friendly adsorbent for wastewater treatment applications.
