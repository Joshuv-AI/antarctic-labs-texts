Motivation to Participate in a Nutrition and Physical Activity Intervention During Chemotherapy for Breast Cancer: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Leah S. Puklin, Maura Harrigan, Courtney McGowan, Leah M. Ferrucci, Brenda Cartmel, Michelle Zupa, Maryann Deyling, Tara Sanft, Mona Sharifi, Melinda L. Irwin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo explore the motivations for participating in a combined nutrition and exercise intervention starting during chemotherapy for breast cancer.MethodsThe Lifestyle, Exercise, and Nutrition early after Diagnosis (LEANer) trial randomized 173 women newly diagnosed with stage I‐III breast cancer to a yearlong nutrition and exercise intervention (N = 87) or usual care (N = 86) during chemotherapy to assess the effect on chemotherapy adherence. Upon completion of the trial, using a stratified purposeful sampling method, a subset of women randomized to the intervention arm completed a one‐time, individual, semi‐structured interview. Interviews were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsAmong the 29 interviews, we identified four key themes related to women's motivations for participating in a lifestyle intervention during chemotherapy. These included (1) Proactivity – a pursuit of opportunities to improve and prioritize health; (2) Altruism – a desire to help others with breast cancer and advance the science around lifestyle factors and treatment outcomes; (3) Lack of perceived harm – the benefits outweighed risks; and (4) Oncologist recommendation – an endorsement from their clinical provider played a critical role.ConclusionsWomen with breast cancer were motivated to participate in a nutrition and exercise intervention initiated during chemotherapy because of factors relating to optimizing their own health, altruistic desires, perceived lack of harm, and their oncologist's recommendation. Compared to post‐treatment, the opportunity to be proactive and having an oncologist recommendation emerged as unique motivators. Understanding these drivers of motivation can enhance recruitment and promote engagement and adherence in future interventions during active cancer treatment.

Introduction

Physical inactivity and poor diet quality are highly prevalent among women diagnosed with breast cancer and often worsen during active treatment [1, 2, 3]. These modifiable factors are consistently associated with increased risk of comorbidities, cancer recurrence, and all‐cause mortality [4, 5, 6, 7].

A breast cancer diagnosis is often a pivotal moment when women are eager to seek out information, particularly related to lifestyle modifications. This interest often translates to an increased motivation to engage in and adopt healthier behaviors, presenting an opportunity for intervention [8, 9]. However, most behavioral interventions for cancer survivors promoting physical activity and healthy eating have been delivered later in the cancer trajectory after active treatment has ended. This is partly due to the unique challenges faced at diagnosis and during chemotherapy, such as symptom burden and emotional distress, which may hinder engagement and adherence to interventions targeting behavior change [10, 11, 12]. To date, post‐treatment nutrition and exercise interventions for breast cancer survivors have increased physical activity levels, improved diet quality, and positively affected body weight, quality of life, as well as serum metabolic and inflammatory biomarkers [13, 14, 15]. Several qualitative studies have described breast cancer survivors' motivations to engage in these trials, such as a desire to reverse adverse changes in weight, diet, and exercise habits that occurred during treatment, an opportunity to reconnect with their body and regain control, and a chance to reduce the risk of recurrence [16, 17, 18].

However, far fewer behavioral lifestyle interventions have been conducted during chemotherapy for breast cancer, limiting our understanding of what factors may drive participation in a behavior change program delivered earlier in the cancer care continuum. The Lifestyle, Exercise, and Nutrition early after Diagnosis (LEANer) trial randomized women newly diagnosed with breast cancer to a yearlong physical activity and nutrition intervention or usual care that started at the initiation of chemotherapy treatment to assess the effect on chemotherapy completion rates [19]. While the LEANer intervention did not affect chemotherapy completion, the intervention was associated with a higher pathologic complete response in patients with hormone receptor‐positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2‐negative and triple negative breast cancer undergoing neoadjuvant chemotherapy [20, 21].

As one of the first trials of nutrition and physical activity during chemotherapy for breast cancer, the LEANer trial offers a unique opportunity to understand breast cancer patients' motivations for participating in behavioral lifestyle interventions earlier in their cancer care. In this qualitative sub‐study of the parent LEANer trial, we aimed to characterize breast cancer patients' motivations for participating in a combined physical activity and nutrition intervention that was initiated during chemotherapy. Gaining insights into participants' motivations can inform the design and delivery of future trials during this phase of care, enhancing recruitment and engagement by ensuring that interventions are aligned with patients' values, needs, and readiness for change.

Methods

LEANer Trial

The LEANer trial was a two‐arm, yearlong randomized nutrition and physical activity intervention (N = 87) versus usual care (N = 86) examining the effect on chemotherapy adherence, measured as relative dose intensity [19, 20]. The intervention included 16, 30‐min individual counseling sessions delivered in‐person, telephone, and/or video (four weekly sessions in month one, then two biweekly sessions in months two and three, and monthly sessions thereafter), focused on gaining knowledge and skills to increase exercise and improve diet quality. Participants were provided materials to pursue a home‐based exercise program with the goal of increasing physical activity to 150 min/week and participating in twice weekly strength training. The dietary goals included a nutrient dense dietary pattern high in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains and limited red and processed meats and added sugars [22, 23, 24]. The intervention started before a participant's second chemotherapy infusion and lasted 1 year. The interventionists were Registered Dietitians (RDs), who were Certified Specialists in Oncology Nutrition (CSO), with additional training in exercise science. Session content was based on the LEAN study [13], adapted from the Diabetes Prevention Program [25], and grounded in the Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) [26], with modifications to address active treatment. Women randomized to usual care had access to an RD and survivorship clinics at any time throughout the trial per usual clinical care. At the end of the trial, usual care participants were provided study intervention materials and offered an individual counseling session with the study RD. The study was approved by the Yale Human Investigation Committee (HIC #2000031514) and the Dana Farber Cancer Institute Institutional Review Board (IRB# 23–004).

Qualitative Study Sample

We used a stratified sampling approach to invite a subset of the 86 women in the intervention arm of LEANer who had completed the yearlong intervention to participate in a one‐time, semi‐structured interview. Participants were categorized as adherent or non‐adherent to the physical activity goal of 150+ min/week of moderate‐to‐vigorous‐intensity physical activity at 1 year. Physical activity was assessed via an interviewer‐administered validated physical activity questionnaire (PAQ) at 1 year (i.e., end of the intervention) on type, frequency, and duration of activities over the past 3 months [27]. To minimize the strata used in our sampling framework, we limited our adherence to the aerobic exercise goal and did not include strength training. Participants were categorized as adherent or non‐adherent to the goal of improving diet quality over the year‐long intervention. Dietary intake over the past 3 months were collected using a food frequency questionnaire developed by the Nutrition Assessment Shared Resource (NASR) of Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center, Seattle, Washington [28, 29]. Nutrient calculations were performed using the Nutrient Data System for Research (NDSR) software version (2020), developed by the Nutrition Coordinating Center, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN [28, 29]. We calculated the Healthy Eating Index‐2015 (HEI‐2015) with a positive change in HEI from baseline to 1‐year reflecting improvement in diet quality [30]. These adherence measures were then combined to create four categories: (1) “High Adherence:” meeting both the physical activity goal and improving diet quality over 1 year; (2) “Mid Adherence‐Physical Activity only:” meeting only the physical activity goal; (3) “Mid Adherence‐Diet Quality only:” meeting only the diet quality goal; or (4): “Low Adherence:” meeting neither goal or missing 1‐year data. Women were selected at random from within each stratum and invited to participate in an interview. Data collection was discontinued when thematic saturation was met within each of the strata. We determined that we had reached saturation when we began to hear repetitive comments from participants within each strata, with few new data or themes generated.

The interviewer (L.S.P.) did not have prior contact with participants or access to any private discussions from the LEANer counseling sessions. Interviews were conducted between April 2022 and May 2023. The interview guide has been previously published [31]. Briefly, the interview questions were designed to elicit attitudes and experiences throughout the LEANer trial, with specific inquiry about motivations for participating in the trial. The questions were informed by the SCT [26] to understand the personal, environmental, and behavioral factors that influenced behavior change as well as the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability [32] to explore participants perceptions of acceptability of the intervention and its components. Interviews were audio‐recorded via Zoom and transcribed using Zoom automatic transcription and then hand‐reviewed by the interviewer for accuracy (L.S.P.). Verbal consent was obtained at the start of each interview.

Research Team

The interdisciplinary research team included a PhD candidate in public health (L.S.P.) with experience in qualitative research who conducted the interviews and analysis, an MPH student (M.D.) who assisted with the analysis, and a health services and implementation researcher (M.S.) with mixed methods experience who provided guidance and mentorship. In addition, clinicians (T.S., M.S.), epidemiologists (M.L.I., L.M.F., B.C.), research staff (M.Z.), and staff who delivered the LEANer intervention (M.H., C.M.), with collective expertise in nutrition, breast cancer, and lifestyle interventions, refined the interview guide, reviewed the codebook, discussed the themes, and reviewed the manuscript.

Data Analysis

De‐identified transcripts were imported into Dedoose (Version 9.0.90, Los Angeles, CA: SocioCultural Research Consultants LLC) for analysis. Two coders (L.S.P. M.D.) conducted inductive thematic analysis using an open‐coding process to develop the initial codebook [33]. The two coders individually reviewed each transcript. They iteratively revised the codes and codebook after each transcript and discussed and resolved any differences in coding applications. The first version of the codebook was reviewed by the full research team to improve validity [34]. Upon establishing codebook validity, one coder (L.S.P.) re‐coded each transcript, and the other coder (M.D.) reviewed code application with discussion to resolve differences. Themes were derived from the final coded transcripts, and illustrative quotes were selected. We followed reporting guidelines described in the Standard for Reporting Qualitative Research [35].

THEME 1: Proactivity

Many women described their motivation to participate in the trial was driven by a desire to take a proactive approach to improving and prioritizing their health during chemotherapy.

Subtheme: Desire for Health Education & Prevention Strategies

For some women, this proactivity emerged as a desire for credible information and education about improving health behaviors because they had never received guidance on nutrition or physical activity before.To have somebody educate you and expand your knowledge, especially around food and what's healthy and what's not healthy, I’ve never had that before.‐ID 406 (Low Adherence)

To have somebody educate you and expand your knowledge, especially around food and what's healthy and what's not healthy, I’ve never had that before.

However, some women shared that they already led relatively healthy lifestyles prior to their diagnosis. These women often reported wanting more tailored information on how to maintain a healthy diet and stay physically active while managing side effects of chemotherapy. This interest appeared to be rooted in a desire to feel prepared for what lay ahead and be proactive about mitigating physical declines during chemotherapy.I thought of myself as pretty healthy to begin with… but I also was just looking at something to help ground me during that really difficult time with chemo and everything that was following. You know, just that not knowing what was gonna happen, and I thought well, this could just help me even get healthier.‐ID 411 (Mid Adherence‐Physical Activity only)

I thought of myself as pretty healthy to begin with… but I also was just looking at something to help ground me during that really difficult time with chemo and everything that was following. You know, just that not knowing what was gonna happen, and I thought well, this could just help me even get healthier.

Subtheme: Catalyst for Behavioral Lifestyle Changes

For many participants, the motivation to participate in the LEANer trial stemmed from their breast cancer diagnosis prompting a critical reflection on their health and lifestyle. The LEANer trial was a timely opportunity that could act as a catalyst for change. Some women who described longstanding struggles with weight or health‐related behaviors viewed the diagnosis and the LEANer trial as an opportunity to become healthier, even in the face of their new diagnosis.I’ve been overweight all my life. I was kind of excited to learn more and to eat better and try to help myself, because I knew with you know the chemo I wasn't going to really be feeling well.‐ID 405 (Mid Adherence‐Diet Quality only)

I’ve been overweight all my life. I was kind of excited to learn more and to eat better and try to help myself, because I knew with you know the chemo I wasn't going to really be feeling well.

Subtheme: Providing a Sense of Autonomy

Lastly, some women also felt that participating in the LEANer trial would provide a constructive outlet. They described the trial as something actionable they could do and have control over while navigating their diagnosis and treatment.It gave me a different focus than just the cancer. You know, again it took my mind off of that and I focused more on how to get through it being healthy.‐ID 402 (Mid Adherence‐Physical Activity only)

It gave me a different focus than just the cancer. You know, again it took my mind off of that and I focused more on how to get through it being healthy.

THEME 2: Altruism

Altruism, or the selfless desire to be part of something that could benefit women diagnosed with breast cancer in the future, was a key theme influencing women's motivation to participate.

Subtheme: Giving Back

Many participants described their involvement in the LEANer study as an opportunity to “give back” and to turn a challenging personal experience into something more positive for others. For several participants, altruism was shaped by personal experiences, including having family members or friends who had been diagnosed with cancer and had benefited from other research studies. However, a few participants who did not discuss personal connections, also expressed a strong sense of responsibility to support future cancer patients.To help other women that are going through this. Because you're going through this one way or the other, you may as well try to share your experience and help someone down the line.‐ID 433 (High Adherence) I was eager to turn a negative experience into a positive for somebody else down the road.‐ID 413 (Mid Adherence‐Physical Activity only)

To help other women that are going through this. Because you're going through this one way or the other, you may as well try to share your experience and help someone down the line.

I was eager to turn a negative experience into a positive for somebody else down the road.

Subtheme: Scientific Advancement

Some women described this altruistic motivation as specifically tied to being in a research study that would contribute to the science of lifestyle factors and treatment outcomes.It's nice to participate in studies because we all learn from it, and future people that are affected will benefit from the studies that we participate in.‐ID 437 (Low Adherence)

It's nice to participate in studies because we all learn from it, and future people that are affected will benefit from the studies that we participate in.

A few women extended this sentiment beyond the stated goal of the LEANer trial (to improve treatment adherence) by describing broader hopes that their participation might contribute to cancer prevention or reduce the future need for chemotherapy altogether. Additionally, a few participants with professional backgrounds in healthcare or science described their connection to clinical care and scientific research as a driver of participation.

THEME 3: Lack of Perceived Harm

Another commonly reported motivator for participation was a perceived lack of harm in participating.

Subtheme: Lack of Safety Concerns

A few participants described their openness to the LEANer trial was the lack of safety concerns associated with a nutrition and exercise intervention. This was often described in comparison to a greater perceived risk of a research trial on drugs or medications.And this isn't like including any research drugs or anything like that. If I could have the intervention dietitian and like the exercise that's going to be good for me anyways, so I think I thought like it wouldn't hurt me in any way.‐ID 435 (Mid Adherence‐Diet Quality only)

And this isn't like including any research drugs or anything like that. If I could have the intervention dietitian and like the exercise that's going to be good for me anyways, so I think I thought like it wouldn't hurt me in any way.

Subtheme: Perceived Benefit Only

Some participants also viewed the LEANer trial as largely beneficial with little to no potential risk. Many participants described that they expected the program would only have a positive effect on them physically and mentally. This emerged, in part, because the trial was designed using the SCT as a behavior change strategy. The study emphasized educating participants on health lifestyle behaviors as a tool to increase self‐efficacy and ultimately translate to the adoption of behavior changes. As a consequence, participants described the emphasis on education was a motivating factor because they perceived learning and gaining knowledge as a purely beneficial outcome of participation.You're already going through something that is life changing and everything and learning something new is not going to hurt, it's going to be more beneficial to you.‐ID 425 (Mid Adherence‐Diet Quality only)

You're already going through something that is life changing and everything and learning something new is not going to hurt, it's going to be more beneficial to you.

In other cases, having the oncologist mention the trial prompted the idea that making lifestyle changes could help manage side effects and adhere to chemotherapy. Discussion

This qualitative study identified several themes related to factors that motivated women newly diagnosed with breast cancer to participate in nutrition and physical activity intervention during chemotherapy. Participants described being motivated because of factors relating to having an opportunity to improve and prioritize their own health, altruistic desires, the lack of perceived harm, and their oncologist's recommendation.

In the current qualitative study, we found that many women reported participating in the LEANer trial because of a desire to use their experiences to help women diagnosed with breast cancer in the future and to advance the science around healthy lifestyles and cancer outcomes. This theme was similarly described in a post‐treatment study involving 54 women with a history of breast cancer who participated in two physical activity and psychological interventions to promote quality of life [17]. This study reported that women's interest in participating was to contribute to scientific studies that could benefit future breast cancer survivors [17]. Taken together, regardless of the phase of cancer care, many patients are motivated to participate in a research study on behavior change to contribute data and evidence that could benefit future breast cancer survivors. This highlights an important messaging strategy that could enhance participation and engagement in lifestyle trials across all phases of treatment. However, it is important to note that the theme of altruism as a motivating factor was tied specifically to participation in a research study and would not be as relevant to promoting behavior change during and after chemotherapy for breast cancer in a routine care setting. Instead, messaging around the benefits of engaging in these health behaviors in a clinical setting would likely need to emphasize the individual benefits of adopting these behaviors on managing treatment‐related toxicities and enhancing quality of life.

Our current study found that many women were motivated to participate in the LEANer trial because it offered an opportunity to proactively improve and prioritize their health. Specifically, the cancer diagnosis acted as a catalyst to focus on their health and well‐being. The lifestyle program also offered a sense of control during a time when they felt a loss of control over their own health. Motivating factors related to health promotion have also emerged in lifestyle trials delivered in the post‐treatment setting. However, in the post‐treatment setting, the themes surrounding health promotion were more often described in a reactive way compared to the proactive themes that emerged from the LEANer trial. In the post‐treatment setting, motivations related to health and well‐being were focused on reversing declines in physical health that occurred during treatment [16, 18]. For example, one study interviewed 14 women who had completed a 12‐month weight loss intervention following cancer treatment [18]. Participants reported that their primary motivation for enrolling was to lose weight following their diagnosis and to reduce the risk of recurrence [18]. Participation in the trial was seen as a way to reverse the adverse dietary and physical activity changes that occurred during or following treatment that led to weight gain [18]. Similarly, a study that interviewed 9 women who completed a dietary and physical activity‐based weight loss intervention following treatment for breast cancer reported a key motivator for participating was to lose weight following their breast cancer diagnosis [16]. In addition, themes surrounding concerns of elevated risk of comorbidities as a consequence of treatment, having a safe way to improve overall health, and a desire to do everything they can to prevent cancer recurrence also emerged [16]. Taken together, participants are motivated to engage in a lifestyle intervention to improve their health and well‐being in trials delivered both during treatment and post‐treatment. However, studies focused on recruiting and engaging participants earlier in the treatment setting should consider emphasizing the potential benefits of proactively engaging in these health behaviors rather than reactively adopting these behaviors in the post‐treatment setting.

In our study, the prioritization of health was described as a desire for information and education. This aligns with studies reporting that a breast cancer diagnosis represents a pivotal moment when women are eager to seek out information, particularly related to lifestyle modifications. One large survey found that breast cancer survivors have a strong interest in diet‐related (57%) and exercise‐related (56%) programs, with most (52%) preferring these to be initiated at the time of diagnosis or soon after [8]. This desire for information and education early after diagnosis highlights a key strategy that could be employed to enhance clinical trial recruitment efforts during this phase of cancer care delivery. Women in the LEANer trial also reported that they were motivated to prioritize their health so they could feel prepared for what lay ahead related to side‐effects and physical declines during chemotherapy. This finding demonstrates that women were motivated to make behavioral changes when it offered a sense of agency and was focused on addressing their fears and concerns about the anticipated effects of their treatment. Therefore, interventions delivered during this phase of cancer care could benefit from framing as educational and by integrating content focused on addressing treatment‐related side‐effects, even if not the primary study outcome.

Our current study also identified the lack of anticipated harm and having a recommendation from their oncologist as additional themes related to women's motivation to participate. Since many drug trials come with risks, the perceived lack of harm of a lifestyle intervention emerged as a key factor driving motivation to engage. It is also possible that being approached about a program focused on healthy eating and exercise may seem low risk when offered around the initiation of chemotherapy, suggesting this may represent an optimal time for discussion and intervention on behavior change. Having the study recommended by their oncologist played a significant role in supporting women's decision to participate in the LEANer trial. This driver of participation may be particularly relevant to trials delivered at diagnosis and during treatment, as the trial is introduced during a formative period when patients are building trust with their provider. This highlights the potential value of a clinician endorsement as a recruitment strategy for trials delivered early in the cancer care continuum.

Study Limitations

Our findings must be considered in the context of several limitations. This qualitative study was conceptualized upon completion of the LEANer trial, thus we do not have interviews among individuals who did not participate in the parent study. As a result, the participants who were eligible and agreed to be interviewed are likely more highly motivated than those who chose not to participate or complete the trial. Future efforts to qualitatively capture reasons for non‐participation in trials should be considered to complement data such as ours to best tailor future interventions. These findings may also be specific to the experiences of patients with breast cancer treated with chemotherapy, which could differ from individuals undergoing other treatments or diagnosed with other cancer types. Interviewed participants were predominantly non‐Hispanic White, highly educated, and privately insured, reflecting the demographics of the parent trial. However, our findings may not capture the full range of motivations among more diverse patient populations. Lastly, the interviews were conducted an average of 1.8 years after women had completed the LEANer intervention, thus the initial motivation for participating may be hard to recall or influenced by their experience in the trial. However, given the paucity of behavioral lifestyle interventions delivered during chemotherapy for breast cancer, our study provides novel evidence on motivations for participating during this critical time. Other strengths of this study included our large qualitative sample and our use of stratified purposeful sampling to ensure the interviewed participants captured varying levels of intervention adoption.

Clinical Implications

These findings provide critical insights for supporting recruitment and participation in lifestyle interventions for patients newly diagnosed with cancer. Based on this study, potential strategies for enhancing recruitment and participation in lifestyle trials at cancer diagnosis and during treatment include (1) leveraging the treating oncologist as a trusted source; (2) highlighting the educational component of the intervention as a tool to support behavior change; (3) emphasizing proactivity and the potential benefit on treatment‐related side‐effects; and (4) communicating the added layer of support and autonomy the intervention can provide. Aligning intervention content and recruitment methods with these strategies may help optimize participation and support retention of future nutrition and physical activity interventions delivered during this phase of cancer care.

Conclusions

Taken together, our study offers unique insights into the motivations driving participation in a combined nutrition and physical activity intervention initiated during chemotherapy for breast cancer. Factors related to prioritizing health, altruistic desires, perceived lack of harm, and oncologist's recommendation served as catalysts for participation. Compared to post‐treatment, the opportunity to be proactive to improve health and mitigate side‐effects of treatment as well as having an oncologist recommendation, emerged as unique motivators. Understanding these drivers can enhance recruitment and promote engagement and adherence in future lifestyle interventions delivered during active cancer treatment.
