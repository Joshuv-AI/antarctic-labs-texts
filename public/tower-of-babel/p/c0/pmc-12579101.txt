Restoring Vision with Noninvasive Brain Stimulation: A Narrative Review
Authors: Xinyan Duan, Yulian Zhou, Peng Zhang, Wen Wen, Chen Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVision impairment resulting from defects in the visual pathway was once considered irreversible. The main barrier is the limited plasticity of the visual system after the critical period. However, as our understanding of noninvasive brain stimulation (NIBS) deepens, its ability to enhance neural plasticity and regulate the neural system indicates a transformation in treatment and rehabilitation strategies. To verify the potential of NIBS in visual restoration, this review elaborates on the mechanisms underlying the immediate effects and aftereffects of NIBS with proofs from molecular, cellular, and systematic levels. The concept of neural oscillation is especially emphasized as it contributes greatly to cognition and can be interfered with by NIBS. Meanwhile, it discusses recent clinical findings on the use of NIBS, with or without visual perceptual learning, focusing on two key questions which have not been specified in previous reviews: (1) the duration of behavioral improvements, and (2) the optimal treatment period required for different visual functions and NIBS modalities. On the basis of the inconsistency in existing studies, this review is also devoted to optimizing the practical application of combining NIBS with VPL and concludes that the location, timing, and form of NIBS are crucial to achieving satisfying complementary effects.Keywords: Noninvasive brain stimulation, Visual rehabilitation, Perceptual learning, Amblyopia, Neuromodulation, Low vision

Key Summary Points

The current review demonstrates the prospect of noninvasive brain stimulation (NIBS) as an advanced tool for visual rehabilitation.This review elaborates NIBS mechanisms in a gradual and structured manner, and focuses on diverse clinical applications of NIBS relating to vision science.It is devoted to explaining variable clinical outcomes from a comprehensive perspective and attempts to reach more appropriate matches between diseases and methodologies.It focuses on the duration of behavioral improvements and the optimal treatment period required for different visual functions and NIBS modalities.Intensive attention is paid to the location, form, timing, and time effect of NIBS.

Background

Vision plays an important role in people’s interactions with both the natural environment and the social environment. Compared with other senses, it captures abundant information with rapid input and forms multidimensional perception with precise processing [1]. Vision impairment, therefore, has a much more profound influence on a patient’s overall health and well-being. Vision impairment is associated with various hazards, including an elevated all-cause mortality rate [2], an increased incidence of dementia [3], and mental disorders [4], as well as reduced job opportunities [5] and social participation [6].

The leading causes of vision impairment can be divided into three main categories: (1) Reversible diseases, such as uncorrected refractive error and cataracts, may achieve impressive improvement through surgical intervention or optical correction [7]. (2) Irreversible diseases, such as pathological processes in glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy, and age-related macular degeneration, always disturb the visual pathway. Traditional therapies (medicines and surgeries) can delay the progression but are weak in restoring the already lost sight [8]. (3) Diseases with reversibility of vision and visual functions decrease drastically with age, such as amblyopia and heterotropia, the predominant causes of vision abnormalities in children [9]. The lowered plasticity of the neural system after the critical period underlies much of this unfavorable situation.

The critical period is a stage in which rapid development of the visual cortex occurs and complex visual functions are established [10]. While such high neural sensitivity and plasticity endow the young visual system with incredible adaptation to embrace the colorful world, it is also susceptible to unfavorable conditions such as monocular deprivation, which may result in irreversible vision impairment [11]. Since the critical period ends in early childhood in humans (normally by age 2, but possibly extending to the age of 9 considering several specific visual functions), even if the adverse conditions are eliminated then, it is difficult for the cortex to regain manipulation of normal sight input, especially in adult patients [12]. Hence, we are in urgent need of visual rehabilitation methods that manifest powerful effects even beyond this critical period.

Feasibility of Vision Restoration

Studies have revealed potential visual cortical plasticity in both healthy adults and patients out of the critical period with retinitis pigmentosa [13], strabismus [14], amblyopia [15], congenital cataracts [16], and keratoconus [17], though limited and short-term. Therefore, reactivation and extension of cortical plasticity accompanied by favorable guidance could be a prospective alternative to promote correct neural connections, enhancing vision in diseases that are resistant to current treatments [18] and also paving the way for new medications to work more successfully.

Visual perceptual learning (VPL) and noninvasive brain stimulation (NIBS) are two representative methods of inducing neural plasticity, contributing to improved performance in various visual tasks. VPL refers to the process by which the brain improves its ability to interpret and recognize visual stimuli through repeated exposure and practice to challenging tasks [19]. Through well-designed training models, VPL can facilitate specific visual functions and is thus studied broadly. Traditional training tools have long been utilized to enhance vergence, visual acuity, contrast sensitivity, etc. [20, 21]. In recent years, digital training technologies such as dichoptic (wearing specialized glasses to enable different visual input between eyes) games have gained popularity in restoring binocular vision [22]. Their therapeutic effects have been proven to be stable and enduring [20], however the prolonged treatment and slow improvement require great patience and compliance from patients (Fig. 1A). In addition, the Pediatric Eye Disease Investigator Group (PEDIG) study on dichoptic treatment for amblyopia was not as successful as patching (occlusion) [23]. In sharp contrast, NIBS triggers significant progress through limited sessions of electrical or magnetic stimulation applied to the scalp, although the outcomes are sometimes ephemeral and not always as expected [24, 25] (Fig. 1B). Despite being a young technique, scientists have devoted enormous efforts to exploring its ultimate application and hold an optimistic attitude toward its ability to restore impaired visual functions. Previous reviews have focused on the safety and effectiveness of NIBS in different eye diseases [26]. They have also proposed combining NIBS with VPL for the sake of introducing a more fast-acting, long-lasting, and effect-guaranteed therapy into visual rehabilitation [27] (Fig. 1C). However, the time effect of NIBS on visual functions and whether they can bring meaningful changes to daily life require further understanding [28], which is discussed in this review through comprehensive comparisons of the relevant findings from various clinical studies.

In brief, this review addresses the possible mechanisms of NIBS and provides information about recent clinical studies that employ single or combined techniques. First, the popular mechanisms of various NIBS modalities are introduced and compared. Second, the effectiveness of specific techniques in clinical settings is evaluated by bridging the gap between behavioral outcomes and neurophysiological mechanisms. Third, more reasonable frameworks for this prospective treatment are proposed. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Mechanisms of NIBS

NIBS, also known as transcranial brain stimulation, delivers an electric current, magnetic field, or ultrasonic wave to target areas via an intact scalp [28]. Despite being a relatively new technique with many unknowns, transcranial brain stimulation fascinates scientists with its ability to induce immediate behavioral and electrophysiological changes [29]. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and transcranial electrical stimulation (tES) are two commonly used NIBS methods in clinical practice. The efficient relief induced by TMS upon major depression marks a milestone in its clinical application and initiates the widespread deployment of NIBS in neurological and psychiatric disorders [30]. Some visual defects, similar to mental disorders, arise from dysfunctions in neuronal activity, and therefore can be potential targets for NIBS [31]. Several NIBS studies have reported improvements in visual functions including contrast sensitivity, visual acuity, and stereoacuity [32–34]. However, applying this technique to ocular diseases [24, 35] does not always result in stable and pleasant outcomes as seen in depression [30], prompting a need to revisit the underlying principles and explore more effective methodologies.

Mechanisms of TMS

TMS can be technically categorized into single-pulse TMS, paired-pulse TMS, repeated TMS (rTMS), and theta burst stimulation (TBS). In general, low-frequency rTMS and continuous TBS (cTBS) inhibit neurons [36], whereas high-frequency rTMS and intermittent TBS (iTBS) excite neurons [37]. rTMS can be further classified into standard rTMS and accelerated rTMS, with the primary distinction being the frequency and duration of the stimulation. Standard rTMS typically involves a longer treatment period with lower stimulation frequencies, whereas accelerated rTMS employs more densely scheduled sessions, which aims to enhance efficiency and benefit more patients [38]. Since single-pulse TMS and paired-pulse TMS are more of a diagnostic tool in neuropathy than a therapy [39], here, we will focus on the mechanisms of rTMS and TBS.

Transmembrane Potential

The common basis of TMS is the indirect introduction of electric currents into the superficial cortical area beneath the coil through electromagnetic induction, which changes the transmembrane potential, driving or preventing action potentials [40]. Its role as a neural modulator has been reconfirmed by functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies that record enhanced local blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) activity during stimulation to the visual [41] and motor [42] cortex.

Apart from their transient effects, rTMS and TBS produce lasting aftereffects similar to long-term potentiation and long-term depression. In simple terms, long-term potentiation strengthens synaptic connections while long-term depression weakens them. These processes are critically dependent on the redistribution of two types of glutamate receptors: N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) and α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptor (AMPAR), which are believed to affect neuroplasticity, regulate synaptic plasticity, and ultimately induce structural changes underlying learning [40]. In the following sections, we provide some evidence regarding the impact of TMS on these processes.

Gene Expression

Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) is a crucial protein that supports neuron survival and growth [43]. It also contributes to synaptogenesis and neuroplasticity by activating signaling pathways mediated by tropomyosin-related kinase B (TrkB) [44]. In both healthy and ischemic rodents, an increase in BDNF expression and its affinity for TrkB have been observed following rTMS and TBS [45, 46]. This enhanced expression of BDNF is concomitant with an increase in AMPAR levels, which are observed as long-term effects induced by high-frequency rTMS [47]. Such outcomes are in accord with previous studies indicating that AMPAR agonists facilitate the secretion of BDNF and the synthesis of protein necessary for plasticity [48]. Besides, the upregulation of NMDAR is also observed after high-frequency rTMS and TBS, which possibly results from the facilitation of aforementioned BDNF/TrkB pathways [49, 50]. Consequently, NMDAR-dependent calcium influx, which is closely associated with long-term potentiation, is enhanced, thereby boosting synaptic transmission and improving learning outcomes [51].

Neurotransmitters

While long-term potentiation and long-term depression can be modulated by the upregulation or downregulation of glutamate receptor expression, direct alterations in the release of key excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters, glutamate and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), also play critical roles. A series of studies have reported the modulation of their distributions induced by TMS. Specifically, TMS has been shown to help restore the disrupted balance between GABA and glutamate in pathological conditions [52, 53]. It is important to note that, in healthy subjects, the regional ratio of glutamate/GABA is positively correlated with the within-network functional connectivity [54]. Additionally, glutamatergic excitation and between-network functional connectivity are found to underlie the improvement of VPL [55]. Studies on TMS applied to the motor cortex have reached consistent conclusions regarding successful GABA modulation [56, 57]. However, its effect on the visual cortex exhibits some inconsistency across different TMS modalities. In healthy human subjects, a reduction in GABA levels following accelerated low-frequency rTMS applied to the visual cortex can persist for up to 1 week, despite the absence of observable changes in basic visual performance, while the application of standard rTMS did not induce any behavioral or neurochemical changes [58]. Nevertheless, another study has discovered more significant and broader changes of functional connectivity between the visual cortex and important nodes when standard rTMS, instead of accelerated rTMS, is delivered [59]. But these changes produced by both rTMS protocols are correlated with the altered concentration of GABA and glutamate, which may briefly reconstruct a treatment period under pathological circumstances. Contrary to that, a single session of TBS to V1 seems to alter neither the local concentration of GABA and glutamate nor the local excitability, though the correlation between GABA and glutamate only exists after stimulation [60]. Other TBS studies provide some insights into this unexpected result: (1) GABA changes may take place in distant regions connected to the stimulated site instead of the originally stimulated site [61]; and (2) the complicated causal chain involved in NIBS may introduce significant intermediate variables, which prevent the direct observation of theoretical effects [62]. Hence, it can be inferred that the complex global interaction between neurotransmitters, rather than their local concentrations, possibly mediates cortical plasticity.

Mechanisms of tES

Typical forms of tES include transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS), and transcranial random noise stimulation (tRNS). The common procedure of tES involves attaching two or more electrodes onto the scalp to generate electrical currents between them. Although these currents are typically low (usually 1–2 mA), they are sufficient to permeate the brain and modulate neural activity.

Transmembrane Potential

Similar to TMS, tES depolarizes or hyperpolarizes neurons to modulate cortical excitability, but the current delivered is much lower and unable to arouse action potentials [63]. The one-way direct current of tDCS flows from the anode to the cathode and leads to increased neural firing at the anodal electrode but decreased neural firing at the cathodal electrode [64]. It is consequently divided into anodal-tDCS (a-tDCS) and cathodal-tDCS (c-tDCS) depending on the polarity of the electrodes located in the targeted area.

Neural Oscillation

tACS and tRNS work in different manners by delivering alternating currents with a periodical sinusoidal frequency and random frequency, respectively. The advantage of alternating current is that it can be manipulated to mimic or intervene in the natural rhythms of the brain, which is also known as cortical oscillation [65]. It is important to note that cortical oscillation can benefit various stages of perceptual learning [66]. For example, it refines the sensory input through coherent summation of specific neural activities, which aids in the detection and amplification of weak signals [67]. Besides, it consolidates learned information and stabilizes the synaptic modifications induced by learning [68]. It is therefore tightly connected with cognition and attention.

One probable theory, entrainment, is attributed to the immediate effects of tES [69]. Entrainment refers to the synchronization of internal oscillatory activities via external adjustment [70] and has been recognized since the advent of tACS. Studies in rodents have demonstrated increased neural oscillation induced by weak electric fields [71, 72]. However, the endogenous oscillation does not shift to align with the stimulation frequency. This phenomenon suggests that the effect of tACS is to enhance the endogenous neural activity rather than control it [71]. Additionally, a significant increase in neural oscillation amplitude is observed only when the stimulation frequency matches the endogenous frequency [72]. Single-neuron recordings from awake monkeys not only solidify the theory of entrainment in primates but also reveal elevated entrainment with increasing stimulation intensity [73, 74]. Another interesting phenomenon observed recently is the competition between tACS and neural oscillation over the synchronization of neurons [75]. In simple terms, it reflects the rule of “first come, first served”: if the neuron is already actively engaged in an ongoing oscillation, the application of tACS will surprisingly disrupt the entrainment, and vice versa (Fig. 2A). This discovery may offer an explanation for the unstable outcomes of human participants: while tACS at specific frequencies has been shown to enhance oscillatory activity within the corresponding frequency band in some studies [76, 77], other studies have failed to induce such an increase [78] and, in some cases, have even observed a transient decrease in oscillatory activity [79].

Visualized multimodality proofs from humans have enriched the understanding of tES. For instance, after a 10-min tRNS over the motor cortex, intensified motor-evoked potentials are detected compared with those at baseline, indicating a dose–effect relationship between current frequency and excitability, likely owing to frequent activation of sodium channels [80]. Additionally, tACS alters visually evoked steady-state responses in line with visual perception in a phase-dependent manner [81]. The fMRI results agree with this conclusion [82] and demonstrate an increased task-related BOLD activity when the frequency of tACS and the visual flicker are matched [83]. Resting-state BOLD activity [84] and functional connectivity [85] in the targeted area also increase when tACS and tDCS are applied to the higher-order cortex. However, opposite to TMS and other tES, the BOLD activity during high-frequency tRNS is unexpectedly decreased despite increased task performance [86, 87]. Considering the entrainment theory, this can be interpreted as an enhanced efficacy of neural activity due to synchronized oscillations, which reduces metabolic demands.

Although tDCS, tACS, and tRNS seem to share the mutual virtue of boosting cortical activity, functional connectivity, and even cognitive or behavioral performance, their patterns in practice conflict. An up-to-date report carefully compared the neurophysiological effects of tDCS and tRNS, suggesting that tDCS benefits the generation of new neural pathways and strengthens neural connections, whereas tRNS speeds up transmission [88]. The weakened effect induced when attempting to combine tDCS and tRNS further validates their distinct mechanisms. Hence, how the same mechanism (entrainment) operates diversely under each form of tES, as well as their possible unknown mechanisms, remain to be clarified.

Gene Expression and Neurotransmitter

Another popular theory more universally underlies all kinds of tES: the spike-timing-dependent plasticity, which is related to the aftereffects persisting for hours [70]. Actually, this corresponds to the long-term potentiation and long-term depression mentioned in TMS and is likewise supported by the evidence of NMDAR functioning, BDNF expression, and GABA/Glx reversing. Increased BDNF [89, 90] and decreased GABA [91–93] predict behavioral improvement and remain consistent as a result of tDCS, tACS, and tRNS. Moreover, the patterns of GABA fluctuations are comparable between those that originate from tACS (artificial entrainment) and those that originate from specific tasks (spontaneous entrainment), illustrating the participation of tACS in neural oscillations from a novel perspective [91]. The impact of NMDAR is somewhat contradictory. Both the immediate and aftereffects of tACS fade after the administration of NMDAR blockers [94], whereas partial NMDAR agonists can reverse the drug-induced inhibitory plasticity in tDCS [95]. However, tRNS is another story. Its modulation of neural plasticity is influenced by sodium channel antagonists rather than NMDAR modifiers [96], echoing the aforementioned frequent activation of sodium channels during stimulation.

Recent Clinical Results with NIBS

The application of NIBS in visual rehabilitation has not yet become a routine part of clinical practice because most studies to date have been limited by small sample sizes [33, 97, 98]. However, it is encouraging to see progress in the treatment of ocular diseases that were once considered untreatable, such as dry age-related macular degeneration and untreated amblyopia in adulthood [98, 99]. Among these conditions, amblyopia has garnered particular attention because the neural pathways involved in the disease, though impaired, remain intact and may be amenable to restoration with noninvasive interventions. In this section, we explore the potential of NIBS for improving various visual functions.

Contrast Sensitivity

Early studies found a temporary enhancement of contrast sensitivity function (CSF) in adult amblyopic eyes induced by single-session a-tDCS instead of c-tDCS [100]. BOLD analysis was available for several participants and revealed that the lateralization of the neural response toward fellow eyes was lessened. However, behavioral improvement was observed in merely half of the participants, which may require more methodological advancements. Trials with larger samples have reproduced the improved CSF at low and high frequencies, while detecting an elevation of visual evoked potentials (VEP) amplitude in amblyopic eyes immediately after a 20-min a-tDCS to the occipital cortex, which reflects improved processing of visual input [99, 101]. These effects are stable for at least 30 min after one session [100, 101]. In children, the improvement in CSF gained from five sessions of a-tDCS treatment is still significant at the 1-week follow-up, which may be a result of higher plasticity [99]. Besides, enhancement of CSF (only at high spatial frequency) derived from TMS accumulated during the first two sessions and did not diminish for at least 2.5 months in a study of five adult subjects [33]. This raises the following question: will repetitive NIBS therapy amplify or prolong the beneficial effects? It is confusing that a tRNS study surprisingly gives negative answers. Significant improvement of CSF does occur in amblyopic eyes after stimulation on the first day but is attenuated in the following days, denying the possibility of accumulation and elongation through successive stimulation [102]. The author supposed that an invariable intensity of tRNS might not fit the already changed cortical excitability and thus produce minimal effects. However, whether the adoption of intervals between sessions or other forms of NIBS will add to the effectiveness remains unclear.

Interestingly, although a-tDCS improves CSF in the case of amblyopia, it demonstrates subtle benefits in healthy people across a wide range of spatial frequencies [24, 103]. However, it is observed that participants with relatively weaker initial CSF have achieved greater progress, which suggests the determinate role of baseline performance in the outcomes of a-tDCS [103] (Fig. 2B). In contrast, tACS enhances contrast sensitivity at various frequencies from 0.5 cpd to 9 cpd, particularly when applied in a manner resembling α oscillation (10 Hz), as indicated by a specific elevation in α power observed in resting EEG shortly after stimulation [104]. This may reflect the entrainment of spontaneous neural rhythms for a higher signal-to-noise ratio. However, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have yet investigated the use of tACS alone in populations with ocular diseases.

Visual Acuity

Visual acuity (VA) improvement in amblyopic eyes is likewise only observed as a consequence of a-tDCS, along with a reduced p100 latency (an indicator of faster early-stage visual processing) that persisted for 1 week after five consecutive days of stimulation; whereas c-tDCS causes a transient suppression of CSF without harming VA [99]. Longer follow-up data are available in the tRNS study mentioned above [102]. Different from CSF, repetitive application of tRNS induced the accumulation of enhancement in uncrowded VA in the first 3 days, which persisted throughout the 4 weeks. However, neither transient effects nor aftereffects were observed in crowded VA. Although the attachment of the cathodal electrode to the occipital midline ends up with transient weakening of CSF and VEP amplitudes [99, 101], c-tDCS targeted at V1 contralateral to amblyopic eyes unexpectedly facilitates amblyopic eyes in VA and VEP at high contrasts [97]. It has been proposed that unilateral c-tDCS might modulate transcallosal interactions by inhibiting the GABAergic neurons of the stimulated hemisphere because ipsilaterally decreased but contralaterally increased VEP amplitudes are observed in both amblyopic and healthy participants. Another possible assumption, grounded in fMRI evidence, involves modulation in the parvocellular layers of the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) through indirect interhemispheric pathways, as parvocellular processing of high-contrast stimuli is impaired in amblyopia, with reduced geniculocortical connectivity being a key factor contributing to the loss of VA [105, 106]. Coincidentally, the improvement in VA here is detectable only in the case of high contrast. Besides, after 1 h, the excitatory effects only last in amblyopes. In addition, c-tDCS also improves VA in diabetic retinopathy, possibly by inhibiting the neuronal noise rather than facilitating the functional transmission [107] (Fig. 2C). These phenomena further describe the modulation as specifically normalizing the abnormal neural interaction.

Similar conclusions arise from TMS research focusing on interocular suppression. A single session of cTBS has been found to improve suppressive imbalance and VA in amblyopic and binocular-asymmetric participants [32, 35, 108]. The lack of changes in visually normal subjects [35] is consistent with a study that measured the resting-state functional connectivity after TBS [60]. Together, they advocate that an imbalanced system is prone to alteration because inactive neurons are more sensitive to TBS [109]. However, existing TMS data concerning the cumulative improvement in VA are rare and almost disagree with this idea [32]. In detail, amblyopes who receive two sessions of cTBS do not benefit more than those who receive a single session in VA or interocular imbalance. Nevertheless, multiple treatments indeed extend the time effect of visual improvements, which is encouraging.

Stereopsis

Compared with monocular functions, binocular functions such as stereopsis are less commonly evaluated in present studies, although they are quite susceptible in cases of amblyopia and strabismus [110]. Only one study has mentioned the significant improvement of stereo acuity immediately after one session of cTBS, which may have inhibited GABA transmission and thus reduced interocular suppression [108]. Histological and biomolecular alterations in the visual cortex after delivery of NIBS to amblyopic rodent models are also consistent with re-aroused neuroplasticity [111, 112]. In animal studies, repeated sessions of tDCS [34, 112], rather than rTMS [111], appear to hold greater potential in the recovery of stereopsis. Although clinical data from human subjects are still lacking, there is reason to believe that certain forms of NIBS may offer promising improvements of binocular vision.

Other Visual Defects

NIBS is a versatile technique. As well as reversing the visual defects of neurodevelopmental ocular diseases, it also offers compensation for organic vision loss by maximizing the efficacy of residual undamaged neurons [98, 113, 114]. For example, orientation of rTMS to areas adjacent to brain infarction provides additional improvement for visual field defects [113]. Besides, a-tDCS has been shown to alleviate peripheral collinear inhibition [98] and crowding (although only for English but not Chinese characters) [114] in macular degeneration. Further investigations of normal older subjects have also come to the conclusion that one session of tDCS is unlikely to improve the peripheral recognition performance of single Chinese characters [25]. The high degree of within-character crowding of Chinese characters seems to be a great barrier to the behavioral benefits for Chinese users. However, this finding reminds us to consider the cultural background when evaluating the efficacy and practicability of this novel treatment. Another interesting application of c-tDCS is to eliminate visual hallucinations in severe vision loss, also known as Charles Bonnet syndrome, in a short 4-day treatment [115]. The effects are more pronounced in those who exhibit higher activity in the occipital cortex, indicating the considerable impact of baseline conditions on NIBS outcomes.

Although diverse forms of NIBS present common effectiveness in the enhancement of vision, inhibitory NIBS functions through the reduction of abnormal electrical noises (and sometimes even normal signals), whereas excitatory NIBS functions through the purification of useful signals [97, 99]. Therefore, severe visual defects, which are further deteriorated by a lowered signal-to-noise ratio, possibly benefit more from proper inhibition, and vice versa [107] (Fig. 2). Future studies should answer the question of suitable pairing among diseases, stimulation forms, procedures, and locations.

Combination of VPL and NIBS

Another innovative application of NIBS lies in boosting the effectiveness of existing clinical therapies, especially VPL. As mentioned above, VPL and NIBS share a potential to produce better visual performance [22, 28]. However, it is crucial to emphasize that, thus far, the efficacy of VPL has only been superior to the gold standard in one multicenter study, restricted to amblyopic children aged less than 9 years [116]. The commonly used training programs are convenient and generally well accepted, but they may not awake such considerable neural plasticity to correct the ongoing disorders in adult brains because the abnormal neuronal connections are already firmly anchored, and can be quite resistant to changes. Increasing the intensity is a possible solution. However, a regular VPL routine always requires more than 2 months of daily training with at least 1 h a day [117]. If the duration doubles, a steep decline in compliance can be predicted. In contrast, one session of NIBS normally takes approximately 20 min and is usually powerful enough to induce obvious improvement [97]. Besides, day-by-day application is not always necessary considering that the outcomes of consecutive treatment are not so ideal [102]. For instance, clinicians are urgently looking for potential therapies for patching-resistant amblyopes. They have combined patching with dichoptic VPL and found significant improvements in visual acuity, with an average gain of 0.31 ± 0.35 logMAR after 6 months of treatment in patching-resistant children [118]. Creative integration of patching and bilateral tDCS while performing a reading task by amblyopic eyes also significantly facilitates the VA of adult amblyopes in just a 30-min session [119]. Hence, it is theoretically feasible to combine VPL and NIBS together to complement each other and provide a shortcut to visual rehabilitation.

Contrast Sensitivity and Visual Acuity

Interactions exist between the improvements of CSF and VA in some studies, so we will discuss these two visual functions together. The application of hf-tRNS to the amblyopic occipital cortex while performing contrast detection tasks monocularly has not only improved the CSF of amblyopic eyes but also unexpectedly resulted in significantly better VA in both eyes [120, 121]. Notably, contrast sensitivity is enhanced across a wide range of spatial frequencies, despite only one frequency being practiced. Although controls who only undergo VPL show similar gains in CSF, their VA remains unchanged [120]. The transfer of enhancement between the eyes and different visual functions observed here is interesting because, to some extent, high-frequency tRNS seemingly eliminates the specificity of VPL, extending the effects to broader dimensions possibly via stochastic resonance, which refers to the phenomenon that an appropriate amount of external noise can improve performance [122]. In addition, only eight brief sessions are needed to improve the VA of adult amblyopes by nearly two lines in amblyopic eyes, which remains stable at the 6-month follow-up [120]. Similar outcomes regarding uncorrected VA and uncorrected CSF have been reported in subjects with mild myopia [123, 124]. An additional group that merely receiveds high-frequency tRNS is available in this study. However, only the combined group achieved greater uncorrected VA and CSF. This further proves the superiority of coupling the two techniques over utilizing either one of them.

Nevertheless, the advantage in transfer does not occur in another subgroup of patients who suffer from macular degeneration, although task-related reduced lateral masking and improved contrast sensitivity are indeed more pronounced after simultaneous exposure to VPL and tRNS [125]. The slight difference between the tasks used among studies and the distinct neural injury mechanisms of diseases might contribute to this contradiction. On the one hand, the patients included in this study were older adults, who might have a lower neural plasticity [126]. However, this idea needs to be reevaluated according to a recent study in which short-term monocular occlusion has been applied to the aged (60–81 years) [127]. On the other hand, patients with macular degeneration are susceptible to the glare induced by the bright white background in the distant VA test because their performance improves in a near VA test based on a dark background [128].

Visual Field Defects

The co-application of VPL and NIBS is also a popular topic in the rehabilitation of visual field defects after brain injury or cerebrovascular accidents. An early study applied a-tDCS to the occipital area of the hemianopia during training; as a result, extensions of the visual field were greater in the stimulation group than in the sham group [129]. The interference of compensatory ocular movement has been excluded, suggesting that extra improvement is restricted to the facilitation of visual function. In fact, deliberate training of visual searching based on compensatory ocular movement is one alternative to the rehabilitation of partial visual field defects. Scientists have therefore combined multisensory training with a-tDCS and improved the response time in visual detection [130, 131]. The improvement also differs with respect to the brain lesion locations and stimulation sites. The lack of enhancement in accuracy in the first study might have resulted from the limited amount of neural modulation conducted via a single session of stimulation [130] because the author applied multiplied treatment sessions in a second study and produced an ideal result, which turns out to be a viable plan for patients to improve their quality of life [131]. A recent case report examined the combined modality in traumatic peripheral optic nerve injury, in which dramatic expansion of the visual field range was acquired after going through 6 weeks of a-tDCS and training [132]. It is quite intriguing how stimulation to the visual cortex compensates for the distance impairment located at the early stage of the visual pathway. Perhaps by elevating the BDNF level, retrograde facilitation of structural damage repair is feasible in an induced highly plastic environment [133]. Since the structural improvement of the retina is also observed in amblyopic rodents receiving tDCS [34], it is possible that NIBS combined with VPL could partially preserve vision in optic neuropathy or retinopathy concerning nerve fibers. However, it should be noted that these findings are based on rodent studies and case reports, and further research in humans is necessary to confirm this hypothesis.

One study doubts the effect of a-tDCS. In contrast, an amazing acceleration of visual recovery in cortical blinds following tRNS has been observed [134]. In terms of learning efficacy, VPL combined with tRNS also outperforms VPL (with a-tDCS or alone) by 2–3 times, which shortens the training course from months to weeks or even days. Notably, although the stimulation is targeted at V1, which is usually ruined in the cortically blind, satisfactory outcomes do exist. One explanation is that spared “islands” of functioning V1 [135] could be enhanced through tRNS, which also appears to induce widespread modulation across the residual V1 and intact V2 and V3, whereas tDCS tends to have a more focal effect. Moreover, it is argued that the repetitive application of tDCS on consecutive days might trigger the inhibitory homeostatic mechanism that protects the brain from overactivation [136]. Importantly, there is consistency in the long-lasting benefits gained through combined therapy despite an apparently lower amount of training.

Factors Influencing Combined Therapy

There are three fundamental questions we need to address here to construct a practical regimen for combined therapy (Fig. 3).

Key elements of combined therapy left to be explored. The location, timing, and form of noninvasive brain stimulation (NIBS) are crucial factors determining the outcomes of combined therapy, which can be artificially controlled. The prefrontal cortex (PFC), one of the higher-order areas, is related to top–down attention, cognition, and decision-making and contributes to the feedback mechanism. The human middle temporal complex (hMT+) functions in a relatively late stage and is closely related to motion perception. V1, as the primary visual cortex, is fundamental in vision processing and manifests deficiency in many eye diseases. Location of Stimulation

In healthy participants, applying a-tDCS to the left human middle temporal complex (hMT+) during VPL of motion detection usually results in better performance than VPL alone [137, 138]. One study discovered similar amounts of enhancement within and between sessions among groups [137]. Hence, the extra improvement might result from the direct effect of tDCS instead of its influence on the process or consolidation of VPL. However, in another study, the facilitation was significant only in the first three consecutive days of training and the plateau of improved performance was not elevated, which indicates that a-tDCS mainly benefits the early stage of learning [138]. A similar conclusion has been reached when a-tDCS is targeted at other higher-order cortexes such as the prefrontal cortex (PFC) [139]. Nevertheless, by applying tES to the occipital cortex, the facilitation of VPL persists throughout the entire trial [134, 140, 141]. According to one possible mechanism of VPL, plasticity predominates in higher visual areas during the early stage of learning and then shifts to primary visual areas [19]. Therefore, alternation of the stimulation location during the course of VPL might sustain the improvement (Fig. 3A).

Form of Stimulation

tES is the most favored form of NIBS adopted in combined trials, probably owing to its simplicity and convenience. Although TMS is capable of stimulating deeper brain areas, relatively superficial layers of the cortex are more commonly targeted in visual rehabilitation, within the stimulation depth accessible to tES. In addition, tES is a rather gentle technique that causes milder side effects and less discomfort [142]. More importantly, portable tES devices are being designed and manufactured to support home-based, self-administered (still under the guidance of professionals) treatment, following the trend of out-of-office visual rehabilitation. However, distinct results exist even among different types of tES when looking over the aforementioned research. Scientists have endeavored to compare the efficacy of different tES techniques and identify the most suitable one for combined therapy. In a large sample of healthy individuals, both high-frequency tRNS and 10-Hz tACS (especially tACS), when applied concurrently with VPL, induce markedly steeper learning curves and lead to better performance outcomes [141]. In sharp contrast, a-tDCS demonstrates subtle superiority over sham stimulation, which echoes the unsatisfactory results from cortical blinds [134]. The author has also tested the efficacy of tACS at various frequencies or locations, observing maximal facilitation at 10 Hz, specifically in the occipital cortex [140] (Fig. 3B). Unsurprisingly, enhanced α oscillations are recorded in the visual cortex after training, which aligns with our understanding of the entrainment theory [72]. Moreover, this study highlights the crucial role of α oscillations in VPL and recognizes tACS as a promising tool for modulating them.

Timing of Stimulation

The mainstream approach to combined therapy employs online stimulation rather than offline stimulation. In “online” approaches, NIBS is delivered along with VPL, and immediate effects such as entrainment play key roles in boosting VPL. This is a reliable method, usually optimizing the existing training program. However, for a-tDCS, as discussed above, the results are confusing [134, 141]. One possible explanation could be that tDCS functions mainly through aftereffects. A previous study proposed that the enhancement of VPL is viable only when a-tDCS is applied to V1 before training and when tRNS is applied during training [143]. Even so, tRNS outperforms a-tDCS. Another hypothesis states that the concurrent application of a-tDCS may disturb the overnight consolidation of VPL [136]. However, if a-tDCS is exerted immediately after training, it surprisingly encourages the awake consolidation [144, 145], whereas overnight consolidation is blocked again, although the underlying mechanism remains unclear [145]. Interestingly, early consolidation is facilitated when the stimulated site is shifted to the left hMT+ [146], and pretraining stimulation fails to improve the performance [137], suggesting that the mechanism differs among cortexes. In addition, more accurate application of NIBS is always being pursued. For example, exerting gamma tACS to the trough of alpha oscillation outperforms that to the peak in the improvement of dynamic VA [147] (Fig. 3C). Therefore, closed-loop NIBS, which simultaneously monitors endogenous neural activity and triggers stimulation when an ideal brain wave is captured, should receive increased attention in the field of visual science. In conclusion, the interaction between the form, location, and timing of stimulation cannot be ignored. A comprehensive consideration of their configuration to achieve maximum efficiency is key to combining VPL with NIBS to promote visual rehabilitation.

Conclusions

Visual rehabilitation is an indispensable part of eye care and is in urgent need of development. The transformation from conservative therapies based on VPL to innovative therapies involving NIBS is promising. Modulation of the transmembrane potential, neurotransmitters, gene expression, and cortical oscillation enables NIBS to awaken neural plasticity and normalize visual pathways. Recent clinical outcomes have primarily confirmed the benefits of NIBS in visual rehabilitation. The combination of NIBS and VPL is not only supported by their overlapping mechanisms but also seems to outperform either of them in practice (Fig. 4). However, more studies need to be carried out to assess the time effects of the improvements achieved through combined therapies. Besides, current studies predominantly focus on primary visual functions, such as VA and CSF, whereas a significant gap remains in research aimed at complex visual functions such as stereopsis. Such visual defects continue to be a major challenge in clinical settings. Nevertheless, in light of the progress thus far, there is growing promise in the combination of NIBS with VPL as a facilitator of vision recovery (e.g., in amblyopia), a supplement to postoperative rehabilitation (e.g., in strabismus), and even a remedy for refractory visual defects (such as macular degeneration and diabetic retinopathy). A better understanding of the mechanism underlying the interaction between NIBS and VPL is essential for developing optimal treatment protocols and tailoring individualized therapies on the basis of the severity of the condition and the physiological status of the neural system.
