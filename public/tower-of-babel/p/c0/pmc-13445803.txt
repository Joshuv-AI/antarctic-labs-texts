Phosphorylated TYK2 orchestrates the pathogenic program of CD4 + T cells in the development of CNS autoimmunity
Authors: Hao Wu, Zhiqiang Xiao, Zhiqing Mai, Guanyu Chen, Xiaoshuang Luo, Zuoyi Li, Minzhen Wang, Xiaoqing Chen, Zhuang Li, Dan Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe imbalance between pathogenic Th1/Th17 cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs) is a central mechanism in central nervous system (CNS) autoimmune diseases, including autoimmune uveitis (AU) and multiple sclerosis. Tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2) mediates signaling downstream of multiple cytokines implicated in CD4 + T cell differentiation, yet its subset-specific activation and therapeutic potential in CNS autoimmunity remain unclear. Here, we investigated the subset-specific activation of TYK2 and evaluated the therapeutic potential of selective TYK2 inhibition in CNS autoimmune diseases.MethodsTYK2 phosphorylation was examined in CD4 + T cell subsets from AU patients and from experimental autoimmune uveitis (EAU) and encephalomyelitis (EAE) models. The therapeutic effects and mechanisms of a selective TYK2 inhibitor were assessed using flow cytometry, single-cell RNA sequencing, adoptive transfer, in vitro cellular assays, and ex vivo stimulation of patient peripheral blood mononuclear cells.ResultsTYK2 phosphorylation was preferentially activated in Th1 and Th17 cells compared with Tregs in both patients and disease models. TYK2 inhibition significantly reduced clinical and histopathological scores in EAU and EAE, suppressing Th1/Th17 differentiation and production of IFN-γ and IL-17A, while Treg proportion and function remained intact. Mechanistically, IFN-α/β, IL-12, and IL-23—but not IL-2—induced TYK2 phosphorylation in CD4 + T cells. Accordingly, TYK2 blockade selectively inhibited STAT1/2/3/4 activation downstream of these cytokines without affecting IL-2-induced STAT5 phosphorylation in Tregs. These lineage-selective effects were confirmed in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from AU patients.ConclusionTYK2 acts as a lineage-selective therapeutic target in CNS autoimmunity. Its inhibition suppresses pathogenic Th1/Th17 responses while preserving Treg proportion and function by targeting cytokine-specific signaling pathways, thereby rebalancing the effector–regulatory immune axis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03934-9.

Introduction

Central nervous system (CNS) autoimmune diseases, such as multiple sclerosis (MS) and autoimmune uveitis (AU), predominantly affect neural tissues, including the brain, spinal cord, and retina [1, 2]. These disorders share a convergent immunopathogenesis characterized by the breakdown of immune privilege and loss of self-tolerance [3, 4]. Current studies have revealed that the aberrant activation and expansion of pro-inflammatory T helper subsets, notably Th1 and Th17 cells, coupled with impaired regulatory T cell (Treg) function, drive destructive immune responses against self-tissues [5–7]. While corticosteroids and conventional immunosuppressants remain first-line therapies in these CNS autoimmune diseases, their clinical utility is frequently hampered by broad immunosuppression, incomplete remission, and a spectrum of systemic toxicities [8–11]. These challenges underscore the urgent need for more selective immunomodulatory strategies capable of suppressing pathogenic inflammation while preserving immune homeostasis.

Tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2), a unique member of the Janus kinase (JAK) family, is emerging as a promising therapeutic target due to its potential for ideal selectivity [12–15]. Unlike other JAK family members, which are ubiquitously expressed and involved in diverse physiological functions, TYK2 displays a distinct evolutionary and biological profile. Crucially, TYK2 is not essential for core physiological processes such as hematopoiesis, erythropoietin signaling, or growth hormone regulation, which are predominantly mediated by other JAK family members [16, 17]. This functional divergence underpins the superior safety profile of selective TYK2 inhibitors compared to traditional JAK inhibitors. While JAK inhibition is frequently associated with dose-limiting toxicities, including cytopenia, thromboembolism, and metabolic disturbances, targeting TYK2 offers a wider therapeutic window by minimizing these class-related adverse effects [18–20]. Besides, the biological significance of TYK2 is further reinforced by profound human genetic data. Large-scale genome-wide association studies have consistently mapped multiple TYK2 single-nucleotide polymorphisms as key susceptibility loci for a broad spectrum of autoimmune and inflammatory disorders [21, 22]. Driven by these unique biological and genetic characteristics, TYK2 inhibition has rapidly moved into clinical application. A landmark success is the recent FDA approval of deucravacitinib for plaque psoriasis, which demonstrates potent therapeutic efficacy and a superior safety profile of oral TYK2 targeting [23–25]. Currently, the therapeutic landscape of TYK2 inhibition continues to expand, with clinical investigations extending into diverse conditions such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [26]. This progress reinforces its role as a high-confidence cornerstone for next-generation precision immunotherapy. Previous research reported that TYK2 knockout mice exhibit an impairment in the pathogenic features of Th1 and Th17 cells [27]. In addition, reducing TYK2 can attenuate the progression of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) by suppressing the inflammatory activation of microglia [28]. However, the expression profiles and subset-specific phosphorylation patterns of TYK2 across CD4 + T-cell lineages, whether TYK2 inhibition can ameliorate CNS autoimmunity through CD4 + T-cell-intrinsic mechanisms, and the potential therapeutic impact of TYK2 in CNS autoimmune diseases remain insufficiently explored.

The biological impact of Type I interferons (IFN-α/β), IL-12, and IL-23 is particularly significant in orchestrating the differentiation and effector functions of CD4 + T cell subsets. These cytokines serve as indispensable initiators of the inflammatory cascade, constituting a hallmark signaling signature that dictates the commitment and expansion of pathogenic lineages in neuroinflammation [29, 30]. Although the individual contributions of these cytokines to autoimmunity are well established, and the effect of TYK2 on signal transduction processes of downstream proteins was also reported [31], it remains to be elucidated whether their collective impact on CNS autoimmune disease progression is channeled specifically through the TYK2 signaling axis in a lineage-selective manner. Furthermore, whether these cytokines selectively trigger TYK2 activation within specific immune subsets, and the extent to which such subset-specific signaling orchestrates the pathogenic effector programs, has yet to be determined.

To address these unresolved questions, we systematically investigated the subset-specific activation and regulatory role of TYK2 in CD4 + T cell-driven CNS autoimmune disease using models of Experimental Autoimmune Uveitis (EAU) and EAE. Our results reveal that TYK2 acts as a preferential mediator, with its phosphorylation preferentially upregulated in pathogenic Th1 and Th17 cells. We show that selective TYK2 inhibition achieves functional decoupling of anti-inflammatory efficacy from immune-regulatory impairment in both EAU and EAE models, which was further validated in clinical samples from patients with AU. These findings provide novel mechanistic insights into the cell-type-specific effects of TYK2 and establish a strong rationale for targeted TYK2 inhibition as a potential therapeutic strategy for T cell-mediated CNS autoimmune diseases.

Materials and methods

Subjects

The study included 21 patients with active Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada (VKH) syndrome, a typical type of AU [32, 33], and 16 healthy controls (HCs). Clinical and demographic characteristics of all participants are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. All participants were between 25 and 55 years of age. VKH patients were diagnosed in accordance with the criteria established by the Standardization of Uveitis Nomenclature Working Group, based on clinical signs, anterior chamber/vitreous inflammation, and imaging features [34].

Mice

C57BL/6J female mice, aged 6 to 8 weeks, were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (China). All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Laboratory Animal Center of Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center, Sun Yat-sen University. All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center, Sun Yat-sen University, and conducted in accordance with the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

Induction and assessment of EAU and EAE

EAU was induced in 6- to 8-week-old C57BL/6J female mice by active immunization [35]. Briefly, on day 0, mice were immunized subcutaneously at two sites in the lower abdomen and one site on the back with a total of 200 µg of human Interphotoreceptor Retinoid-Binding Protein peptide 651–670 (IRBP651–670) (GL Biotech, China). The peptide was emulsified in an equal volume of complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA) containing 2.5 mg/mL Mycobacterium tuberculosis (BD, USA). Additionally, 200 ng of pertussis toxin was administered intraperitoneally on the day of immunization (day 0) and again on day 2. On day 14, clinical signs of EAU were evaluated using a Micron IV retinal imaging microscope (Phoenix Research Laboratories). At the experimental endpoint, draining lymph nodes (dLNs), spleens, eyeballs, peripheral blood, liver, and kidneys were collected for histopathological and flow cytometric analyses. Clinical signs and retinal Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining were performed as described [36].

EAE was induced in 6- to 8-week-old C57BL/6J female mice by active immunization [37]. Briefly, 200 µg of Myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein peptide 35–55 (MOG35−55) (GL Biotech) was emulsified in CFA containing 2.5 mg/mL of Mycobacterium tuberculosis. A total volume of 200 µL of the emulsion was administered via multiple subcutaneous injections along the dorsal flank near the spine. On the day of immunization (day 0) and again on day 2, 200 ng of pertussis toxin was injected intraperitoneally. At the experimental endpoint, dLNs, spleens, and spinal cords were collected for histopathological and flow cytometric analyses. Clinical signs were scored daily, and spinal cord inflammation and demyelination were evaluated by H&E and luxol fast blue staining, respectively, using established scoring systems [38].

Drug administration

For therapeutic intervention, mice received the selective TYK2 inhibitor (TYK2i) deucravacitinib (BMS-986165; Selleck, China) (5 mg/kg) or an equal volume of vehicle (2% DMSO, 5% Tween-80, 20% PEG, 73% PBS) via daily intraperitoneal injections according to the predetermined experimental schedule until the endpoint.

Proliferation assay

Following isolation, CD4 + T cells were stained with Tag-It Violet Cell Tracking Dye (1µmol/L, BioLegend, USA; 10 min, 37 °C). For the assay, stained cells (2 × 105 per well) and anti-mouse CD3/CD28 beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) (0.5 × 10⁵ per well) were co-cultured in 96-well plates. The culture was maintained for 72 h in the presence of TYK2i (250 nM) or DMSO control. Proliferation was analyzed by flow cytometry.

T cell apoptosis assay

Isolated CD4 + T cells were plated at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well in 96-well plates and activated with anti-mouse CD3/CD28 beads at a bead-to-cell ratio of 1:8. The cells were co-cultured with either vehicle control or specified concentrations of the TYK2i for 72 h. Subsequently, cells were harvested and stained for apoptosis using an Annexin V-FITC/PI detection kit (Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Flow cytometric analysis was then performed to quantify Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide staining.

T cell polarization

Naive CD4 + T cells (CD4 + CD44- CD62L+) were isolated from lymph nodes and spleens of naive mice using the CD4 + T cell isolation kit (StemCell Technologies, CA). The purity of sorted naive CD4 + T cells was assessed by flow cytometry. Cells with purity exceeding 95% were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well for Th1 and Th17 differentiation, and 2 × 105 cells per well for Treg differentiation, into 96-well plates pre-coated with 5 µg/mL anti-CD3 antibody (BioLegend). For Treg polarization, cells were cultured in the presence of TGF-β (10 ng/mL, Bio X Cell, USA), IL-2 (10 ng/mL, BioLegend), and anti-CD28 antibody (5 µg/mL, BioLegend). Th17 differentiation was induced using IL-1β (50 ng/mL, BioLegend), IL-23 (5 ng/mL, BioLegend, USA), IL-6 (50 ng/mL, R&D Systems, USA), TGF-β (1 ng/mL, Bio X Cell), anti-IFN-γ (10 µg/mL, BioLegend), anti-IL-4 (2 µg/mL, BioLegend) and anti-CD28 antibody. For Th1 differentiation, cells were stimulated with IL-12(p70) (5 ng/mL, BioLegend), anti-IL-4 (2 µg/mL) and anti-CD28 antibody.

IRBP T cell stimulation assay

Single cells were harvested from dLNs of EAU mice and cultured with IRBP651−670 (10 µg/mL) alone or in combination with TYK2i (250 nM) for 72 h at 37 °C. Cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry.

Adoptive transfer experiment

Single cells were isolated from dLNs of EAU or EAE mice and stimulated with IRBP651−670 (10 µg/mL) or MOG35−55 (10 µg/mL), respectively, in the presence of TYK2i (250 nM) or DMSO for 72 h. CD4 + T cells were isolated from the restimulated cultures, washed three times with PBS, and then intravenously transferred into naive C57BL/6J mice at a dose of 4 × 106 viable cells per mouse. On day 14 post-transfer, naive mice were subjected to comprehensive clinical, histopathological, and flow cytometric assessments.

Flow cytometry analysis

Tissue samples were mechanically dissociated and homogenized in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 2% FBS, then filtered through 40 μm cell strainers. After washing, cells were stained with the Zombie NIR™ Fixable Viability Kit (BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

For the detection of phosphorylated signaling molecules in cytokine stimulation assays, cells were incubated in serum-free RPMI 1640 medium for 2 h at 37°. Following this starvation period, cells were stimulated with specific cytokines for 15 min at 37°C prior to staining. In both mouse and human cells, phosphorylated signal transducer and activator of transcription(p-STAT) 1 (Tyr701), p‑STAT2 (Tyr690), and phosphorylated‑TYK2 (p-TYK2; Tyr1054/Tyr1055) were detected following stimulation with either IFN‑α (100 ng/mL; Abclonal, China) or IFN‑β (100 ng/mL; Abclonal). For in vitro naive mouse CD4 + T cells, p‑STAT3 (Tyr705) and p‑TYK2 were assessed following IL‑23 stimulation (50 ng/mL; BioLegend) after a 3-day pre-activation with CD3/CD28 T cell Activators in the presence of 100 ng/mL IL-2. p‑STAT4 (Tyr693) and p‑TYK2 were detected following IL‑12 stimulation (50 ng/mL; BioLegend) under the same pre-activation condition for in vitro naive mouse CD4 + T cells. p‑STAT5 (Tyr694) and p‑TYK2 were detected following IL‑2 stimulation (40 ng/mL; BioLegend for mouse cells; 40 ng/mL; Abclonal for human cells). Following stimulation, cells were immediately fixed and permeabilized using a standardized protocol (BD Phosflow Fix/Perm system). Subsequently, intracellular staining for phosphorylated proteins (p-TYK2, p-STAT1/2/3/4/5) was performed.

For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were stimulated for 5 h with Cell Activation Cocktail (with Brefeldin A; BioLegend) following the recommended protocol. Following stimulation, cells were fixed using Fix/Perm Buffer (BioLegend) and permeabilized with Permeabilization Buffer (Invitrogen, USA). Subsequent intracellular staining was performed as previously described [39]. Detailed information regarding the antibodies used in this study is provided (Supplementary Table 2). Samples were acquired on a Cytek flow cytometer (Cytek, USA), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.4).

Single cell RNA sequencing

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from dLNs (three mice per group). After three washes with PBS, the cells were filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer. Cell count and viability were assessed prior to library preparation. Single-cell RNA sequencing libraries were constructed using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3ʹ Reagent Kit (10× Genomics) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, barcoded RNA from encapsulated cells was reverse-transcribed into cDNA, which was then used to construct Illumina sequencing libraries. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw sequencing data were quality-controlled with FastQC and processed through Cell Ranger (v5.1.0; 10× Genomics) to generate FASTQ files.

Differential expression analysis (DEGs) was performed for each cell type separately between groups using the FindMarkers function of the Seurat package (v4.0.4). Significant DEGs were identified based on the criteria of |Log2FC|> 0.25 and adjusted P < 0.05.

To identify molecular pathways associated with autoimmune and inflammatory disorders, functional enrichment analyses of the DEGs were performed. Specifically, Gene Ontology (GO) biological process enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment were implemented using the enrichGO and enrichKEGG functions, respectively (within the clusterProfiler R package).

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed to evaluate coordinated gene expression changes. Reference gene sets were retrieved from the MSigDB database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org). The analysis was executed and visualized using the msigdbr and fgsea R packages.

PBMCs stimulation assay

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained from blood samples via Ficoll density gradient centrifugation (Cytiva, Sweden). Cells were then plated in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and stimulated with anti-human CD3/CD28 beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a ratio of 1:4 (beads to cells). During stimulation, cells were treated with either vehicle control or TYK2i. The cells were collected and subjected to flow cytometric analysis after 72 h.

Statistical analysis

Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, one-way ANOVA, or two-way ANOVA followed by post-hoc multiple comparisons test were applied as appropriate based on experimental design. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Specific statistical tests used, n values (biological replicates), and replicate information are detailed in the figure legends. Significance levels are denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, ns, not significant. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5).

Results

TYK2 is selectively activated in pathogenic CD4 + T cell subsets in CNS autoimmunity

We first investigated the TYK2 activation status in the context of AU. Analysis of PBMCs from patients with VKH syndrome revealed that p-TYK2 levels were significantly elevated in CD4 + T cells compared to HCs (Fig. 1A). This increase was not due to altered total TYK2 levels, which remained comparable between groups (Fig. 1A). Notably, in both VKH patients and HCs, p-TYK2 levels were lowest in naive CD4 + T cells (CD45RA+CCR7+), while the Th1 (CD4 + T-bet+) and Th17 (CD4+ RORγt+) subsets reached the highest levels of activation, both of which were significantly higher than those in the respective Treg (CD4 + Foxp3+) subsets (Fig. 1B). p-TYK2 levels were significantly elevated in all CD4 + T cell subsets in VKH patients relative to HCs. Importantly, within the VKH group, the phosphorylation signal was most prominent in Th1 and Th17 cells, where it significantly exceeded that in naive CD4 + T cells and Treg cells (Fig. 1B). In contrast to the phosphorylation status, total TYK2 protein levels remained comparable among the naive CD4 + T cells, Th1, Th17, and Treg subsets within both the VKH and HC groups. Furthermore, no significant differences in total TYK2 expression were observed within any of these three subsets when comparing VKH patients with HCs (Fig. 1B).

To determine if this signaling signature is recapitulated in mouse models of uveitis, we analyzed T cells from EAU mice. Consistent with the human data, CD4 + T cells from the dLNs of EAU mice exhibited significantly increased p-TYK2, but not total TYK2, compared to naive mice (Fig. 1C-D). Notably, while p-TYK2 levels were elevated across all CD4 + T cell subsets in the EAU context, the phosphorylation signal was most prominent in Th1 and Th17 cells. Specifically, p-TYK2 levels were lowest in naive CD4 + T cells (CD4 + CD44- CD62L+), while in the Th1 and Th17 subsets, phosphorylation levels were significantly higher than those observed in the corresponding Treg compartment (Fig. 1C). In contrast to the phosphorylation status, total TYK2 protein levels remained comparable among the naive CD4 + T cells, Th1, Th17, and Treg subsets within both the EAU and naive groups (Fig. 1D). Similar results were obtained from EAU splenocytes (Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). Thus, the preferential upregulation of TYK2 within pathogenic T helper lineages is a consistent characteristic of AU, faithfully reflected in the murine EAU model.

To investigate whether this selective TYK2 activation represents a hallmark of CNS autoimmune responses, we further examined the EAE model, another classical model of CNS autoimmunity. In line with our observations in EAU, CD4 + T cells from the dLNs and spleens of EAE mice showed a similarly upregulation of p-TYK2 in all subsets relative to healthy controls (Fig. 1E-F; Supplementary Fig. 1C-D). Within the EAE mice, p-TYK2 levels were lowest in naive CD4 + T cells, with Th1 and Th17 subsets exhibiting the most robust activation that again surpassed the levels seen in the Treg subset (Fig. 1E-F; Supplementary Fig. 1C-D). These data suggest that selective TYK2 activation in pathogenic T helper lineages is a fundamental characteristic of neuroinflammatory responses.

We further explored whether the observed TYK2 activation patterns are directly coupled to the differentiation programs of specific CD4 + T cell subsets. In vitro differentiation of naive CD4 + T cells confirmed that Th1 or Th17 polarization significantly elevated p-TYK2 levels compared to non-polarizing (Th0) conditions. In contrast, p-TYK2 levels in induced Tregs were comparable to those in Th0 cells and remained significantly lower than those in polarized Th1 or Th17 cells (Fig. 1G). Total TYK2 levels remained unchanged across all polarization states (Fig. 1H).

Collectively, these data establish that heightened TYK2 activation is a defining feature of inflammatory CD4 + T cell responses, exhibiting a consistent upregulation in Th1 and Th17 cells compared to Tregs in neuroinflammation.

TYK2 inhibition alleviates neuroinflammation in EAU and EAE

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of TYK2i in CNS autoimmunity, we utilized the EAU model (Fig. 2A). Fundus photography and fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) demonstrated that TYK2i substantially alleviated retinal edema, vasculitis, and vascular leakage compared to vehicle controls (Fig. 2B-C). Histopathological evaluation confirmed preserved retinal architecture and reduced inflammatory scores (Fig. 2B-C). Flow cytometric analysis of the retina revealed a significant decrease in the proportions of CD45 + leukocytes and CD4 + T cells; notably, among ocular CD4 + T cells, the production of IL-17A, IFN-γ, and TNF-α was markedly reduced, while the proportion of Tregs remained intact (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, initiation of treatment after the onset of clinical symptoms (Supplementary Fig. 2A-D, Supplementary Fig. 8E-F) effectively attenuated intraocular inflammation and decreased systemic pro-inflammatory cytokines without affecting the Treg compartment.

To validate the broad applicability of these neuroprotective effects, we extended our investigation to the EAE model (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Selective TYK2 inhibition significantly attenuated clinical disease severity and reduced the peak clinical score (Fig. 2F). It also mitigated disease-associated weight loss in EAE mice (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Histological examination of the white matter within spinal cord sections confirmed that TYK2 blockade markedly diminished inflammatory cell infiltration and preserved myelin integrity (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, to address the high translational value of our findings, we assessed the efficacy of TYK2 inhibition when administered starting from day 14, at which point the clinical score had reached 1 (Supplementary Fig. 4A). In this regimen, TYK2i treatment significantly mitigated disease-associated weight loss and successfully restricted further disease progression (Supplementary Fig. 4B,4D). Moreover, TYK2i administration effectively attenuated neuroinflammation and decreased systemic markers, including pro-inflammatory cytokines as well as chemokines and activation surface markers, resulting in reduced inflammatory cell infiltration (Supplementary Fig. 4C, 4E-H, Supplementary Fig. 8I-J).

These results indicate that the therapeutic benefits of TYK2i extend across different contexts of CNS neuroinflammation.

TYK2 inhibition rebalances the peripheral T cell landscape while maintaining regulatory circuitry

To elucidate the potential mechanisms driving tissue protection, we characterized the CD4 + T cell landscape in peripheral lymphoid organs and circulation (Fig. 2A). In the EAU model, flow cytometric analysis confirmed that TYK2 inhibition significantly decreased p-TYK2 levels in CD4 + T cells, with an average reduction of 28% in the dLNs and 25% in the spleen as measured by gMFI, without altering total TYK2 expression (Fig. 3A and E), while body weight and the histology of the liver and kidneys remained unaffected (Supplementary Fig. 5). This was accompanied by a profound phenotypic shift: TYK2 inhibition reduced the proportion of activated (CD4 + CD69+) and effector (CD4 + CD44+ CD62L-) CD4 + T cells, with a concomitant increase in the proportion of naive CD4 + T cells (CD4 + CD44- CD62L+) (Fig. 3B and F). Additionally, treatment led to a significant downregulation of the chemokine receptors CXCR3 and CCR6 (Supplementary Fig. 6A-B). Consistent with the EAU findings, these biochemical and phenotypic shifts were recapitulated in the EAE model (Supplementary Fig. 3C-D), confirming a common systemic response to TYK2 blockade in CNS autoimmunity.

Further analysis of effector functions in EAU mice demonstrated a comprehensive reduction in pathogenic responses, including significant decreases in IFN-γ + and IL-17A+ CD4 + T cells, as well as IFN-γ + IL-17A + and GM-CSF+ CD4 + T cells in the dLNs and spleen (Fig. 3C and G, Supplementary Fig. 8C-D). Mirroring these results, TYK2 inhibition in the peripheral blood of EAU mice (Supplementary Fig. 6E) and in the peripheral immune organs of the EAE model (Supplementary Fig. 3E-F, Supplementary Fig. 8G-H) similarly suppressed the proportions of IFN-γ+, IL-17 A+, and IFN-γ + IL-17 A+ CD4 + T cells.

Importantly, the regulatory T cell program remained robust. In the EAU model, the proportion of Tregs remained stable in all systemic compartments (Fig. 3D and H), and further functional assays confirmed that the expression of key Treg markers, including CTLA-4, ICOS, and Helios, was not compromised (Supplementary Fig. 6C-D). Consistent with these observations, the Tregs proportion in the peripheral blood of EAU mice (Supplementary Fig. 6E) and in the peripheral immune organs of the EAE model (Supplementary Fig. 3E-F) also remained unaltered by the treatment.

These data collectively indicate that TYK2 is required for pathogenic CD4 + T cell activation and differentiation but is not required for the maintenance of regulatory T cells, a lineage-selective profile that holds true across different autoimmune contexts.

TYK2 inhibition curbs Th1/Th17 differentiation and attenuates the pathogenicity of CD4 + T cells

To determine whether the immunomodulatory effects of TYK2 inhibition were linked to changes in T cell proliferation or apoptosis, we first examined these processes. Notably, cell proliferation assays revealed no significant differences in the proliferative capacity of CD4 + T cells between the vehicle and TYK2i-treated groups (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, flow cytometric analysis showed that TYK2 inhibition did not alter the percentages of early or late apoptotic CD4 + T cells (Fig. 4B). Additionally, we investigated whether TYK2 inhibition intrinsically affects the lineage commitment of naive T cells. Naive CD4 + T cells were polarized under Th1-, Th17-, and Treg-inducing conditions (Fig. 4C). TYK2i treatment significantly impaired the differentiation of both Th1 and Th17 cells (Fig. 4D). In contrast, Treg differentiation remained unaltered (Fig. 4D). These results confirm that TYK2 signaling is selectively required for pro-inflammatory T helper cell lineages but dispensable for regulatory T cell commitment.

Building on these cellular findings, we further evaluated the direct effect of TYK2 inhibition on antigen-specific CD4 + T cell responses. Single-cell suspensions from the dLNs of EAU mice were stimulated with IRBP651−670 in the presence or absence of TYK2i. After 72h, TYK2 inhibition significantly reduced the proportions of IFN-γ+, IL-17 A+, IFN-γ + IL-17 A + and GM-CSF+ CD4 + T cells, while the proportion of Foxp3 + Tregs remained unaffected (Fig. 4E, Supplementary Fig. 8A).

To ultimately determine the functional consequence of these modulatory effects in vivo, we performed an adoptive transfer experiment. Cells harvested from the dLNs of EAU mice were restimulated with IRBP651−670 for 72 h in the presence of vehicle or TYK2i. Subsequently, CD4 + T cells were isolated from these restimulated cultures and then transferred into naive mice (Fig. 4F). Naive mice receiving TYK2i-treated CD4 + T cells exhibited significantly lower clinical scores, characterized by reduced retinal vascular leakage (as shown by FFA), and less severe disc swelling compared to the vehicle group (Fig. 4G-H). Histopathological analysis confirmed a marked reduction in retinal inflammation and lower H&E scores in the TYK2i-treated group (Fig. 4G-H). Consistent with the clinical improvement, flow cytometric analysis of the retina and dLNs showed significantly reduced proportion of IFN-γ + and IL-17 A+ CD4 + T cells in the naive mice, while the Foxp3 + Treg proportion remained stable (Fig. 4I-J). As it remains unclear whether the reduction of TYK2 specifically in CD4 + T cells affects EAE progression, we further conducted an EAE adoptive transfer experiment (Supplementary Fig. 7A). Naive mice receiving TYK2i-treated CD4 + T cells exhibited significantly lower clinical scores and reduced weight loss (Supplementary Fig. 7B,7D). This clinical improvement was accompanied by markedly attenuated spinal cord pathology and a significant decrease in the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the dLNs (Supplementary Fig. 7C,7E). These results demonstrate that TYK2 inhibition directly suppresses the intrinsic pathogenicity of CD4 + T cells during neuroinflammation.

Together, these findings demonstrate that TYK2 inhibition effectively restricts the pathogenic potential of autoimmune CD4 + T cells by targeting their differentiation and effector programs without affecting their proliferation and apoptosis, or the regulatory T cell proportion.

TYK2 blockade reprograms the pathogenic transcriptional landscape of CD4 + T cells

To assess the transcriptional changes and cellular heterogeneity upon TYK2 inhibition, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on cells isolated from the dLNs of EAU mice treated with either vehicle or TYK2i (Fig. 5A). t-SNE clustering identified T cells and B cells as the predominant immune populations (Fig. 5B). Focusing on the CD4 + T cell compartment, differential expression analysis revealed that TYK2i treatment significantly downregulated a cluster of inflammation and autoimmune-related genes, including Fosb, Cd74, H2-Aa, Syk, Cxcr4, and Dock2. Conversely, it significantly upregulated Tpt1 and mt-Atp6, which are key genes involved in cellular fitness and mitochondrial metabolism, respectively (Fig. 5C). Supporting these findings, GO, KEGG, and GSEA enrichment analysis demonstrated a robust suppression of core inflammatory pathways, including Leukocyte cell-cell adhesion, Th17 cell differentiation, and T cell activation (Fig. 5D-E).

To achieve higher resolution of the T cell response, we further analyzed the Th17 cell subset through sub-clustering (Supplementary Fig. 9A-B). TYK2 inhibition selectively modulates pathogenic cytokine signaling

The cytokine–STAT signaling axis plays a pivotal role in determining the differentiation fate of CD4 + T cells, orchestrating the delicate balance between pathogenic and regulatory immune programs. As TYK2 serves as a critical signaling node downstream of diverse cytokine receptors, we sought to elucidate its specific contribution to this immunological decision-making process. By systematically characterizing how TYK2 relays distinct extracellular cues, we aimed to uncover the molecular basis for its ability to selectively modulate pro-inflammatory pathways while potentially sparing homeostatic signals essential for immune tolerance.

We first assessed which cytokines trigger TYK2 activation in CD4 + T cells isolated from naive mice. Stimulation with IFN-α/β, IL-23, or IL-12 induced the p-TYK2, whereas IL-2 stimulation did not (Fig. 6A-E). Consistently, a selective TYK2 inhibitor significantly reduced the phosphorylation of downstream STAT proteins in response to TYK2-dependent cytokines.

In CD4 + T cells stimulated with IFN-α/β, TYK2i significantly decreased the phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT2 (Fig. 6A-B). This suppressive effect was similarly observed in the Th1 (CD4 + T-bet+) and Th17 (CD4+ RORγt+) subsets (Fig. 6A-B). We further examined IL-23 and IL-12 signaling, which are critical for Th17 and Th1 responses, respectively. IL-23-induced phosphorylation of STAT3 was significantly attenuated by TYK2 inhibition in CD4 + T cells and particularly within the Th17 subset (Fig. 6C). Similarly, TYK2 inhibition decreased IL-12-induced phosphorylation of STAT4 in CD4 + T cells and the Th1 subset (Fig. 6D). These results demonstrate that TYK2 inhibition directly attenuates key signaling pathways in inflammatory T helper cell populations.

Critically, and in contrast to the above pathways, IL-2 stimulation induced STAT5 phosphorylation in CD4 + T cells, which was unaffected by TYK2 inhibition. Further analysis focused on the Treg compartment—the primary responders to IL-2—confirmed that p-STAT5 levels in Foxp3 + cells were preserved following treatment (Fig. 6E). This preservation is consistent with our finding that IL-2 does not trigger TYK2 activation, underscoring the lineage-selective modulatory profile of the inhibitor.

To confirm the physiological relevance of these molecular findings, parallel analyses were performed on lymph node cells from EAU mice. In vivo TYK2 inhibition resulted in significant reductions in the phosphorylation of STAT1/2/3/4 within the CD4 + T cell population, while p-STAT5 levels in both CD4 + T cells and Tregs remained unaltered (Supplementary Fig. 10A-E). These findings reinforce the mechanism by which TYK2 inhibition selectively modulates pathogenic T cell signaling while maintaining the homeostatic IL-2/STAT5 axis in regulatory cells.

TYK2 inhibition selectively suppresses pathogenic effector functions while sparing the regulatory T cell compartment in AU patients

To assess the functional impact of TYK2 inhibition on human disease, we stimulated PBMCs from patients with active VKH syndrome with anti-CD3/CD28 in the presence of a selective TYK2 inhibitor or vehicle. TYK2 inhibition significantly reduced the production of key inflammatory cytokines by CD4 + T cells, including IL-17 A, IFN-γ, IFN-γ + IL-17 A + and GM-CSF (Fig. 7A, Supplementary Fig. 8B). Notably, while the effector response was suppressed, the proportion of CD4 + Foxp3+ Tregs remained stable under the same treatment conditions (Fig. 7A). These results confirm that TYK2 blockade selectively targets inflammatory effector functions without compromising the regulatory compartment in a clinical context.

Moreover, we characterized the STAT phosphorylation patterns within the CD4 + T cell population of PBMCs derived from VKH patients. Recapitulating our findings in murine models, TYK2 inhibition significantly reduced the phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT2 following IFN-α/β stimulation (Fig. 7B-C). This suppressive effect was similarly observed within the Th1 and Th17 subsets (Supplementary Fig. 11A-B).

Furthermore, IL-23-induced phosphorylation of STAT3 was significantly attenuated by TYK2 inhibition in CD4 + T cells and the Th17 subset (Fig. 7D, Supplementary Fig. 11C). Similarly, TYK2 inhibition significantly decreased IL-12-induced phosphorylation of STAT4 in CD4 + T cells and the Th1 subset (Fig. 7E, Supplementary Fig. 11D). These results demonstrate that the lineage-selective modulatory profile of TYK2 inhibition is conserved in human AU.

In striking contrast to the above pathways, IL-2-induced STAT5 phosphorylation remained unaffected by TYK2 inhibition in both CD4 + T cells and the Foxp3 + Treg population (Fig. 7F, Supplementary Fig. 11E). This confirms that the precise suppression of pathogenic STAT signaling and the preservation of the homeostatic IL-2/STAT5 axis are maintained across species, providing a strong molecular basis for the therapeutic efficacy of TYK2 inhibition.

Discussion

The present study identifies TYK2 kinase as a critical and selective driver of pathogenic CD4 + T cell responses in CNS autoimmunity. We demonstrate that TYK2 phosphorylation is specifically upregulated within Th1 and Th17 subsets, in both clinical patients and experimental models, and further recapitulated through in vitro polarization assays. This study characterizes the subset-specific phosphorylation pattern of TYK2 across CD4 + T cell lineages in CNS autoimmunity, demonstrating that p-TYK2 levels were lowest in naive CD4 + T cells and highest in Th1 and Th17 cells, both significantly higher than in Tregs. Notably, this study establishes the therapeutic effect of TYK2 inhibition in CNS autoimmunity. Targeted inhibition of TYK2 effectively ameliorates disease severity in EAE and EAU models by suppressing Th1 and Th17 cell responses while leaving the Treg compartment intact. Furthermore, using adoptive transfer experiments in both EAU and EAE models, we demonstrate that TYK2 inhibition ameliorates CNS autoimmunity through CD4 + T-cell-intrinsic mechanisms. Mechanistically, the efficacy of TYK2 inhibition stems from a nuanced modulation of cytokine signaling: TYK2 inhibition selectively dampens the activation of STAT1/2/3/4, while preserving the integrity of the IL-2/STAT5 axis. These findings establish TYK2 as a potential therapeutic target, offering a compelling therapeutic strategy for CD4 + T cell-driven CNS autoimmunity through the targeted silencing of pathogenic helper T cell programs.

The pathological hallmark of CNS autoimmunity, as represented by AU and MS, is rooted in the functional dysregulation of pro-inflammatory and regulatory CD4 + T cell subsets [39–41]. Consequently, the dynamic equilibrium between Th1/Th17 and Treg cells is pivotal for controlling disease progression. Conventional therapies, such as corticosteroids and broad-spectrum immunosuppressants, often yield suboptimal outcomes. While these agents are clinical mainstays, their indiscriminate suppression of the immune system—including both effector and regulatory compartments—inevitably compromises physiological immune homeostasis and impairs host defense. Consequently, there is an urgent need to identify selective immunomodulators that can precisely target pathogenic Th1 and Th17 lineages while sparing the vital regulatory circuitry. Our study demonstrates that TYK2 inhibition effectively alleviates disease severity in both EAE and EAU models. This therapeutic efficacy is characterized by high cellular selectivity: in vivo, treatment significantly reduced the proportion of Th1 and Th17 cells, along with their associated effectors (e.g., IFN-γ, IL-17 A) and chemokine receptors (CXCR3, CCR6), while the Treg compartment and its functional markers (e.g., Foxp3, CTLA-4, Helios, and ICOS) remained intact. In vitro mechanistic assays further revealed that, at therapeutic concentrations, TYK2 inhibition does not impair the proliferation, apoptosis, or Treg differentiation of CD4 + T cells; rather, it specifically disrupts the differentiation programs of naive CD4 + T cells into Th1 and Th17 lineages. Thus, our findings identify TYK2 inhibition as a differentiation-oriented, selective immunomodulatory strategy that restores immune homeostasis by selectively targeting pathogenic developmental pathways.

The JAK family, comprising JAK1/2/3, and TYK2, serves as a critical conduit for a broad spectrum of cytokine signaling pathways. Upon cytokine engagement with their cognate receptors, JAKs are activated and subsequently phosphorylate STAT proteins [17, 42]. These phosphorylated STATs form dimers and translocate into the nucleus, where they bind to specific promoter regions to orchestrate the transcription of cytokine-responsive genes. This canonical JAK-STAT signaling axis represents a fundamental regulatory mechanism essential for coordinating immune responses and maintaining systemic homeostasis [43–45]. A fundamental distinction between TYK2 and other JAK family members (JAK1/2/3) lies in their respective roles in IL-2-driven signaling. The JAK1/3 axis is indispensable for the expression of Foxp3, the lineage-defining transcription factor of Treg [46, 47]. While TYK2 is non-essential for this cascade, JAK inhibitors significantly impair IL-2 signal transduction [48, 49]. Given the paramount role of Treg in maintaining immune tolerance in CNS autoimmunity and other autoimmune diseases [50, 51], JAK inhibition may inadvertently hinder long-term tolerance induction by compromising the Treg compartment. Indeed, clinical trials of JAK inhibitors in patients with rheumatoid arthritis or myeloproliferative neoplasms have reported a failure to improve, or even a reduction in circulating Treg frequencies [52–55]. In contrast, because TYK2 does not participate in IL-2 signaling, its targeted inhibition offers a therapeutic window to suppress inflammatory pathways while sparing Treg integrity. Our mechanistic studies provide evidence for this selective efficacy. We demonstrate that within the autoimmune milieu, pathogenic CD4 + T cells utilize distinct TYK2-dependent signaling cascades upon exposure to specific cytokines. Specifically, during Th1 differentiation and effector function, IL-12 signals through its receptor (associated with TYK2 and JAK2) to activate STAT4, which induces T-bet and drives pro-inflammatory IFN-γ production [56–58]. Our data show that IL-12 stimulation markedly enhances TYK2 phosphorylation and downstream STAT4 activation—a pathway effectively neutralized by TYK2 inhibition. Similarly, Th17 cell maintenance, governed by IL-23-dependent TYK2/STAT3 signaling [59, 60], was potently suppressed. Furthermore, the elevated levels of Type I interferons (IFN-α/β) typically found in autoimmunity, which amplify inflammation via the TYK2/JAK1-STAT1/2 axis [61, 62], were significantly attenuated. Crucially, this inhibition remains highly selective; our experiments confirm that the IL-2/STAT5 pathway remains unaffected. Therefore, by selectively targeting Th1/Th17-driven inflammation while preserving Treg maintenance signals, TYK2 inhibition recalibrates the immune balance. This mechanistic bifurcation establishes it as a functionally selective therapy.

However, this study also has limitations. As AU and MS are largely mediated by CD4 + T cell-driven inflammation, we primarily focused on the role of TYK2 in CD4 + T cells and their subsets. Nevertheless, TYK2 is also implicated in SLE, where B cells serve as the core pathologic cells, and the cytokine-STAT signaling axis is known to regulate the activation and pro-inflammatory functions of B cells, monocytes, and dendritic cells [63–65]. These findings suggest that TYK2 may modulate a broader spectrum of pro-inflammatory immune cells, which merits further investigation. Additionally, the scRNA-seq analysis in the present study was limited to a sample size of n = 3 per group, and validation in larger cohorts will be required to further substantiate these observations in future studies.

In conclusion, this study establishes the critical role of TYK2 in CNS autoimmunity, where its kinase activity is specifically activated in pathogenic Th1 and Th17 cells. Selective inhibition of TYK2 effectively alleviates CNS autoimmunity, an efficacy stemming from the precise suppression of pathogenic CD4 + T cell differentiation while leaving the lineage and function of Treg intact. Mechanistically, TYK2 inhibitors achieve this selectivity through the differential regulation of downstream STAT signaling. Consequently, our research not only elucidates the specific role of TYK2 across CD4 + T cell subsets but also demonstrates that targeting this kinase can suppress pathogenic immune responses while maintaining regulatory immune function, thereby achieving precision intervention in autoimmune processes. This provides a novel target and mechanistic rationale for developing therapeutic strategies centered on correcting the Th1/Th17/Treg imbalance in autoimmune diseases.

Conclusions

This study identifies TYK2 as a potential lineage-selective therapeutic target in CNS autoimmunity, with its inhibition suppressing Th1/Th17 responses while preserving Treg proportion and function. These findings provide a mechanistic rationale for targeting TYK2 in Th1/Th17-driven autoimmune diseases.
