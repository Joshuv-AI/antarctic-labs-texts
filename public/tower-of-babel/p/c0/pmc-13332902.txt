Tight contact structures on a family of hyperbolic L-spaces
Authors: Hyunki Min, Isacco Nonino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe classify tight contact structures on various surgeries on the Whitehead link, which provides the first classification result on an infinite family of hyperbolic L-spaces. We also determine which of the tight contact structures are Stein fillable and which are virtually overtwisted.Mathematics Subject Classification: Primary 57K33, Secondary 57K32

Introduction

A cooriented contact 3-manifold (M,ξ) is overtwisted if it contains an overtwisted disk, which is an embedded disk tangent to the contact planes along the boundary, and tight if it does not contain an overtwisted disk. Since Eliashberg [11] classified overtwisted contact structures, more recent studies focus on the classification of tight contact structures.

Since tight contact structures respect the prime decomposition of 3-manifolds by the work of Colin [3] (see also [9, 34]), it is sufficient to consider the classification of tight contact structures on prime manifolds. The geometrization of 3-manifolds implies that a prime 3-manifold is either Seifert fibered, toroidal or hyperbolic. For Seifert fibered spaces with two singular fibers, tight contact structures were completely classified, see [12, 15, 27, 32]. For Seifert fibered spaces with three singular fibers, there have been several classification results, see [20, 23, 24, 55, 56] for example. For toroidal case, tight contact structures were classified on torus bundles over S1 and S1-bundles over a surface, see [26–28, 33, 38]. Honda, Kazez and Matić [35] showed that there exist infinitely many tight contact structures up to isotopy on toroidal manifolds and Colin, Giroux and Honda [5] showed that a closed orientable irreducible 3-manifold supports infinitely many tight contact structures up to isotopy if and only if it is toroidal.

For hyperbolic manifolds, much less is known although most prime 3-manifolds fall into this category [53]. Honda, Kazez and Matić [36] partially classified tight contact structures on hyperbolic surface bundles over S1 when the contact structures have an extremal relative Euler class, meaning that the Euler class of the contact structure evaluated on a fiber surface is maximal. Later, Conway and the first author [7] classified tight contact structures on (most) surgeries on the figure eight knot in S3, which contain infinitely many hyperbolic 3-manifolds.

In this paper we will consider hyperbolic L-spaces. Recall that an L-space is a rational homology sphere such that the order of the first singular homology group equals the free rank of its Heegaard Floer homology group. In general, it is hard to construct a universally tight contact structure on an L-space because an L-space does not admit a taut foliation, which can be perturbed into a universally tight contact structure. There are some non-hyperbolic L-spaces (e.g. lens spaces) that support a universally tight contact structure while it is not known for hyperbolic L-spaces yet. Thus it is natural to ask whether there is a hyperbolic L-space that supports a universally tight contact structure, which can be considered as a contact topology version of the L-space conjecture.

Conjecture 1.1

A hyperbolic 3-manifold is an L-space if and only if it does not support a universally tight contact structure.

Notice that none of the hyperbolic manifolds mentioned above are L-spaces, and they all admit taut foliations so support universally tight contact structures. In this paper, we classify tight contact structures on an infinite family of hyperbolic L-spaces. Although we could not show that they are all virtually overtwisted, we show that most of them are.

Contact topology preliminaries

We assume that the reader is familiar with basic 3-dimensional contact topology [16], including Legendrian knots [17], contact surgery, open book decomposition [18], convex surface theory [14, 32], and Heegaard Floer homology [49, 50]. In this section, we briefly review the results that we will frequently use.

Convex surfaces

Let (M,ξ) be a contact 3-manifold, where ξ=kerα. Recall that a contact vector field is a vector field of which the flow preserves ξ. An orientable surface Σ is called convex if there exists a contact vector field transverse to Σ. If Σ has boundary, then we assume that ∂Σ is Legendrian with negative twisting number with respect to the surface framing. According to Giroux’s flexibility theorem [25] (the boundary case is proven by Kanda [38]), for any embedded surface Σ there is a C∞-small isotopy (rel boundary) that isotopes Σ into a convex surface.

Let Σ be a convex surface with a dividing set ΓΣ, ΓΣ≠∅. We call a properly embedded graph G in Σ non-isolating if G intersect ΓΣ transversely and every component of Σ\G intersects ΓΣ.

Theorem 2.2

(Legendrian realization principle, Giroux [25], Kanda [38], Honda [32]) If G is a properly embedded non-isolating graph on a convex surface Σ, then Σ can be isotoped relative to ∂Σ such that the image of G under this isotopy is Legendrian.

In particular, if Σ\G is connected, G can always be realized as a Legendrian graph. This implies any non-separating curve on Σ can be realized as a Legendrian curve.

Honda [32] showed that if two convex surfaces intersect along a Legendrian curve L, the dividing curves of the two surfaces interleave and we can obtain a new convex surface by rounding the edge, see Figure 2.

We can modify a dividing set in a systematical way. Let Σ be an oriented convex surface with a dividing set ΓΣ and D be a half-disk with boundary given by two legendrian arcs a and b, i.e ∂D=a∪∂b with tbD(a)=-1 and tbD(b)=0. Suppose α=D∩Σ intersects ΓΣ in three points {p,q,r} and ∂α={p,r} are elliptic singularities of D. By Giroux’s flexibility theorem, we can C∞-small isotope D for q to be a unique hyperbolic singularity. We call D a bypass and the sign of the hyperbolic singularity is called the sign of a bypass.

Honda proved in [32] that there is a one-sided neighborhood Σ×[0,1] of Σ∪D such that Σ×{0,1} is convex and the dividing set on Σ×{1} is modified as shown in Figure 3. We say Σ×{1} is obtained from Σ×{0} by a bypass attachment along α. Note that a bypass can be on either side of a surface and it gives a different effect on the dividing set.

Remark 2.4

In Theorem 2.3, we use the slope convention pq=pq since meridianlongitude is our slope convention for a solid torus. Theorem 2.9

(Honda [32]) There are exactly two basic slices up to isotopy fixing the characteristic foliation on the boundary. These two tight contact structures are distinguished by their relative Euler class. We now review Giroux torsion. Let k∈12N. Consider the contact structure ξk=ker(sin(2πkz)dx+cos(2πkz)dy) on T2×[0,1], where (x, y) are the coordinates on T2 and z is the coordinate on [0, 1]. We call ξk a Giroux k-torsion layer and if we have a contact structure (M,ξ) into which (T2×[0,1],ξk) embeds, we say (M,ξ) contains Giroux k-torsion. We will use the phrase (M,ξ) has exactly Giroux k-torsion to the situation where one can embed (T2×[0,1],ξk) into (M,ξ) but not (T2×[0,1],ξk+12). If k=1/2, then we call ξk a half Giroux torsion. We say (M,ξ) has no (half) Giroux torsion if (T2×[0,1],ξk) does not embed in (M,ξ) for any k∈12N.

Now we consider (T2×R,sin(2πkz)dx+cos(2πkz)dy) and perturb T2×{0} and T2×{1} so that they become convex with two dividing curves of slope 0. Let ξck be the resulting contact structure on T2×[0,1]. We call this ξck a convex Giroux k-torsion layer. Notice that inside of (T2×[0,1],ξck) there is a basic slice with one boundary component agreeing with T2×{0} and having boundary slope 0 and ∞. This will either be a positive or a negative basic slice. By reversing the coorientation of ξck, if necessary, we can assume it is positive.

Theorem 2.10

(Honda [32]) For k∈12N, there are exactly two convex Giroux k-torsions ±ξck, up to isotopy rel. boundary, on T2×[0,1] with convex boundary having two dividing curves, both of slope 0. The two contact structures are contactomorphic.

Remark 2.11

In this paper, since we only consider contact manifolds with convex boundary, we just use the term Giroux k-torsion for convex Giroux k-torsion. However, readers should notice that the original Giroux torsion has pre-Lagrangian torus boundary.

Contact surgery

Let L be a Legendrian knot in a contact 3-manifold (M,ξ) and N a standard neighborhood of L. Contact (pq)-surgery on L is defined as follows: let (μ,λ) be a meridian and contact framing of L, respectively. (M(p/q)(L),ξ(p/q)) is obtained by cutting N from M and re-gluing it via a diffeomorphism of ∂N sending μ to pμ+qλ. Then, extend the contact structure (M\N,ξ) to the entire M(p/q)(L). In general, the extension is not unique since there could be distinct tight contact structures on N with a given boundary condition. If we only focus on the tight contact structures on N, Theorem 2.12 provides the number of all possible extensions.

By repeating the same argument, we can show that there is a unique (possibly) tight contact structure on Cn(∞). In total, there are at most two (possibly) tight contact structures on C(∞). □

Now we will prove Lemma 4.4 and 4.5. The argument will be similar to the proof of [7, Lemma 3.1 and 3.4]. However, since ϕn is right veering, whereas the monodromy of the figure-eight knot is not, the maximal slope to which Cn(s) can thicken is different.

Proof of Lemma 4.4 and 4.5

Since r∈Q-, s∈S(r) and s≠0, we know s<0. Let k∈N such that -1k-1<s≤-1k. Suppose s≠-1k. Then by Proposition 4.16 we can find a bypass for -∂Cn(s) with slope 0 and we can thicken Cn(s) to Cn(s′) where s<s′≤-1k. Thus we can find a bypass again by Proposition 4.16 and repeat this until we obtain Cn(-1k). Let ΓΣ be a dividing set for Σ in Cn(-1k). By Proposition 4.13, we need to consider two cases.

(1) ΓΣ contains one boundary parallel arc without any other dividing curves: First, we fix the sign of the bypass on Σ to be negative. Then the relative Euler class evaluated on Σ is -2. After cutting Cn(-1k) along Σ and rounding the edges, we obtain Σ×[0,1] with a smooth convex boundary. Also, the dividing set consists of a single closed curve parallel to ∂Σ. By choosing convex compressing disks for this handlebody whose Legendrian boundaries intersect the dividing curve exactly twice as shown in Figure 29, we see that there is a unique (possibly) tight contact structure on this handlebody, and thus a unique (possibly) tight contact structure on Cn(-1k), which we denote by ξ-1/k-.

It follows that any tight contact structure on Cn(-1k) whose relative Euler class evaluated on Σ is -2 without boundary parallel half Giroux torsion is actually isotopic to ξ-1/k-. Hence the complement of a standard neighborhood of S-(S-(L)) in (L(n,n-1),ξstd) is isotopic to ξ-1-, where S- is the negative stabilization operator and L is a Legendrian representative of K2 from Lemma 4.17. Therefore, (Cn(-1),ξ-1-) in fact thickens to Cn(1), and Cn(-1)=T-∪Cn(∞), where T-=B-(-1,0)∪B-(0,1) is a union of two negative basic slices with dividing slopes s0=-1 and s1=1. We can further decompose T- into T-k∪B-(-1k,0)∪B-(0,1), where T-k is a minimally twisting contact T2×[0,1] with dividing slopes s0=-1 and s1=-1k. Hence we obtain a tight contact structure on Cn(-1k)=B-(1k,0)∪B-(0,1)∪Cn(1). It is straightforward to check that the relative Euler class evaluated on Σ in this Cn(-1k) is -2, so this contact structure is isotopic to ξ-1/k-. Therefore, (Cn(-1k),ξ-1/k-) also thickens to Cn(1) for all k∈N.

Next, change the signs of the regions of Σ so that the sign of the bypass is positive. In this case, the relative Euler class evaluated on Σ is 2. After repeating the above argument, we get a unique tight contact structure under these conditions, which we denote by ξ-1/k+. Similarly, we can build the tight contact structures isotopic to (Cn(-1k),ξ-1/k+) that thicken to Cn(1).

The above arguments show that these tight contact structures on Cn(-1k) are determined by the sign of the bypass on Σ, which is in turn determined by the sign on the stacks of basic slices B±(-1k,0)∪B±(0,1), and are thus independent of the tight contact structure on Cn(1).

(2) ΓΣ contains one closed curve and one arc with slope ∞: In this case, the relative Euler class evaluated on Σ is 0. Fix the signs of the regions Σ\ΓΣ. We showed in the proof of Proposition 4.16 that there exists an isotopic copy of Σ in Cn(-1k) with one boundary parallel dividing arc, one closed boundary parallel dividing curve and perhaps two closed essential curves, see the bottom drawing of Figure 24 for example. As in the proof of Proposition 4.13, we can remove the closed essential dividing curves and hence we can assume ΓΣ consists of one boundary parallel arc and one boundary parallel closed curve. This implies that the dividing set of ∂(Σ×[0,1]) consists of exactly three dividing curves parallel to ∂Σ. By Cofer [2, Section 3], there exist two tight contact structures on this Σ×[0,1] (with a fixed characteristic foliation). Thus there exist at most two tight contact structures on Cn(-1k).

According to the proof of Proposition 4.16, it is clear that one of these contact structures contains a boundary parallel half Giroux torsion layer. By repeating the same argument with the opposite signs on the regions on Σ\ΓΣ, we see that there are at most two tight contact structures on Cn(-1k) without boundary parallel half Giroux torsion when the relative Euler class evaluated on Σ is 0, and that these contact structures are completely determined by the sign of the bypass on Σ. Denote these contact structures by (Cn(-1k),ξ-1/k′±).

It follows that any tight contact structure on Cn(-1k) whose relative Euler class evaluated on Σ is 0 without boundary parallel half Giroux torsion is actually isotopic (fixing boundary setwise) to either ξ-1/k′+ or ξ-1/k′-. Thus according to Lemma 4.17, the complement of a standard neighborhood of S+(S-(L)) (resp. S-(S+(L))) in (L(n,n-1),ξstd) is isotopic to ξ-1′+ (resp. ξ-1′-). Therefore, (Cn(-1),ξ-1′±) in fact thickens to Cn(1), and Cn(-1)=T±′∪Cn(1), where T±′=B±(-1,0)∪B∓(0,1) is a union of two negative basic slices with dividing slopes s0=-1 and s1=1. We further decompose T±′ into T±′k∪B±(-1k,0)∪B∓(0,1), where T±′k is a minimally twisting contact T2×[0,1] with dividing slopes s0=-1 and s1=-1k. Hence we obtain tight contact structures on Cn(-1k)=B±(-1k,0)∪B∓(0,1)∪Cn(1). It is straightforward to check that the relative Euler class evaluated on Σ is 0 and the sign of the bypass is ±, so these contact structures are isotopic (fixing boundary setwise) to ξ-1/k′±. Therefore, (Cn(-1k),ξ-1/k′±) also thickens to Cn(1) for all k∈N.

The above arguments show that these tight contact structures on Cn(-1k) are determined by the sign of the byfpass on Σ, which is in turn determined by the sign on the stacks of basic slices B±(-1k,0)∪B∓(0,1), and are thus independent of the tight contact structure on Cn(1). □

Fillability and virtual overtwistedness

In this section, we will prove Theorem 1.6 and Theorem 1.7. We begin with the fillability of tight contact structures on M(n, r).

Proof of Theorem 1.6

First, we consider the tight contact structures counted by Φ. In [13], tight contact structures on Sn3(T2,3) are classified and it is shown that every tight contact structure on Sn3(T2,3) is Stein fillable for n≥5. Recall from Section 3 that the tight contact structures counted by Φ can be constructed from the surgery diagram shown in Figure 10, which can be considered as a result of Legendrian surgeries on the knots in some tight contact structure on Sn3(T2,3). Since Legendrian surgery preserves Stein fillability, all tight contact structures counted by Φ for n≥5 are Stein fillable.

Next we consider the tight contact structures counted by Ψ. We start by considering M(n, 2). Observe that there is a unique tight contact structure on M(n, 2) counted by Ψ and denote it by (M(n,2),ξ2). We claim that (M(n,2),ξ2) is Stein fillable and show that any tight contact structure on M(n, r) for r>2 counted by Ψ is obtained by some negative contact surgery on some Legendrian knot in (M(n,2),ξ2). Since negative contact surgery preserves Stein fillability, these will complete the proof.

Lemma 4.5 and the proof of Theorem 4.8 implies that any contact structure on M(n, r) for n≥2 and r≥2 counted by Ψ can be obtained by a positive contact surgery on a Legendrian push-off of the binding of an open book decomposition of (L(n,n-1),ξstd), the unique tight contact structure on L(n,n-1). This in fact implies that any tight contact structure on M(n, r) for r>2 counted by Ψ can be obtained by some negative contact surgery on some Legendrian knot in (M(n,2),ξ2).

Lastly, we will consider the virtual overtwistedness of tight contact structures on M(n, r).

Proof of Theorem 1.7

First, as shown in Figure 1, M(n, r) can be obtained by r-surgery on K2 in L(n,n-1) and the surgery dual knot K∗ represents a nontrivial element in π1(M(n,r)). Thus there is a cover of M(n, r) that unwraps K∗. Let N be a neighborhood of K∗. By Honda [32, Proposition 5.1.(2)], if ξ is a virtually overtwisted contact structure on N, then any cover of (N,ξ) contains an overtwisted disk. Thus if (N,ξ) is virtually overtwisted, then there is an overtwisted cover of (N,ξ) that embeds into some cover of M(n, r).

Now according to the proof of Theorem 4.8, the tight contact structures counted by Ψ can be decomposed into C(1) and Nr(1). By Honda [32, Proposition 5.1.(2)], there are at most two universally tight contact structures on Nr(1). Thus for any r∈R+∪Q-, there are at most two universally tight contact structures on M(n, r) counted by Ψ and all others are virtually overtwisted. Also, the tight contact structures counted by Φ can be decomposed into C(∞) and Nr(∞). By Honda [32, Proposition 5.1.(2)] again, there are at most two universally tight contact structures on Nr(∞). Since there are two tight contact structures on Cn(∞) counted by Φ, there are at most four universally tight contact structures on M(n, r) for r∈R+ counted by Φ, and all others are virtually overtwisted. □
