Situation analysis and prospects for One Health implementation in Zanzibar, Tanzania
Authors: Johanna Pauline Wiethoff, Khadija Noor Omar, Othman Juma Othman, Ali Zahran Moh’d, Talib Saleh Suleiman, Mlenge Hassan Mlenge, Tumu Ali Mussa, Christian Reimer, Christian Menge, Sascha Knauf, Anna Sophie Fahrion
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study provides a situation analysis of Zanzibar's One Health recognition and implementation, assessing animal, public and ecosystem health. It provides a comprehensive overview of the prevailing One Health system, addressing current policies, strategic priorities, and the organization of the sectors. In addition, it assesses the status of implementation of intersectoral collaboration to date. A structured desk review and a consultative process were used for information gathering. The findings were related to existing data and assessments, to specifically address their relevance for One Health implementation in Zanzibar. Key factors contributing to the success of the One Health environment in Zanzibar are a conducive political climate, the presence of guidelines and a legal framework, and the establishment of One Health governance structures. The primary challenges encompass uncertainties about the effectiveness of the legal framework and the effective functioning of the established One Health bodies, inadequate infrastructure and diagnostic capacity, and a paucity of efficient processes for data sharing and early reporting. The evaluation of progress towards the strategic objectives of Zanzibar's One Health Strategy, continued improvement of the laboratory infrastructure and new international long-term partnerships for research and capacity strengthening, as well as the island's geolocation, provide opportunities for Zanzibar to serve as a model case for progressing (One) Health safety in the region.

Introduction

The Zanzibar archipelago, comprising its two main islands Unguja and Pemba, is a semi-autonomous state within the United Republic of Tanzania and governed by the Revolutionary Government of Zanzibar (RGoZ). The human population of currently 1.8 million [1] with the majority (70 %) living in Unguja [2], is increasing steadily. Human life expectancy was 66 years in 2022 [3]. The coastal zone is characterized by rich and diverse ecosystems and the climate is tropical with two rainy seasons. Due to rich soils and rainfalls, agriculture is the most dominant sector of the economy [4]. Livestock production in the region is inadequate to satisfy the local demand for meat, therefore 80 % of beef consumed in Zanzibar is derived from imported cattle [4] from mainland Tanzania. A significant proportion of these animals are slaughtered in Zanzibar upon arrival. Tanzania is among the top three countries in the world for the number of zoonotic disease hotspots [11]. Zanzibar's geographic location and its intense, primarily unidirectional animal trade with mainland Tanzania and neighbouring countries, which have documented evidence of various zoonotic diseases [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]], make the presence of a range of zoonotic pathogens highly probable. This is due to the frequent importation of potentially infected livestock and the ongoing infection cycles on the islands. It is therefore vital to understand that zoonotic diseases pose a serious threat not only to veterinary public health, but also to the livelihoods of animal keepers, especially in resource-poor populations [12]. As elsewhere in the region, epidemiological data on zoonotic diseases are scarce. In Zanzibar, the presence of only a few zoonotic diseases – e.g., Dengue fever, Brucellosis, Leptospirosis and Q-fever – has been confirmed to date [13,14].

The concept of One Health (OH) is based on the recognition that the sectors animal, human and environmental health are interdependent and mutually impactful. The One Health High-Level Expert Panel (OHHLEP) defines OH as an “integrated, unifying approach that aims to sustainably balance and optimise the health of people, animals and ecosystems” [15]. A cross-sectoral approach, which builds on the OH paradigm [16], has proven effective in many settings in terms of successfully strengthening networks and controlling diseases. This results in increased preparedness and response to both endemic and epidemic diseases [[17], [18], [19]].

Zanzibar has recognised the importance of the OH approach and has taken steps to implement it. A key initiative undertaken by the Zanzibar government was the development and publication of a Zanzibar One Health Strategic Plan (ZOHSP), launched in December 2022 [4]. The plan summarizes the current state, reports strengths, weaknesses and opportunities, analyses threats and concludes with a five-year plan to implement OH. Its five thematic areas focus on 1. Capacity development, advocacy and communication; 2. Preparedness and response; 3. Research; 4. Disease surveillance, prevention and control; and 5. Coordination. Strategies include improving communication, forming response teams, conducting collaborative research, strengthening laboratories and developing integrated surveillance systems. The coordination of OH activities falls under the remit of the Second Vice President's Office (SVPO), overseen by the Second Vice President of Zanzibar. The Disaster Management Commission (DMC), operating under the SVPO, is responsible for coordinating the implementation of the ZOHSP in collaboration with relevant stakeholders [4] (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Organizational chart depicting the hierarchy of competent authorities in the implementation and realization of a One Health strategy in Zanzibar (modified from Zanzibar One Health Strategic Plan; the directions of the arrows indicate reporting obligations).Fig. 1

The 2022 launch of the ZOHSP was directly followed by a workshop entitled ‘One Health Think Tank – The Road Map to One Health on Zanzibar’ (OHTT). The objective of the workshop was to specify the next steps and provide support for decision-making to achieve the required system-based OH transformative process in Zanzibar. During the OHTT, a group of workshop participants from different sectors developed the outline for the present overview article, with the objective of answering the following questions: 1. What is the current status of OH implementation on the islands of Zanzibar? 2. How can the sectors involved be characterized? 3. What data exist about OH-relevant health hazards? 4. To what extent is the ZOHSP functional? 5. What are the gaps in the system of cooperation between the sectors? and 6. What are the resulting priorities for research and capacity development?

In preparation for and following the discussions in the workshop, a comprehensive review of international, national and grey literature was conducted, with additional oral communications and further resources also being considered.

Methods

Literature review

This research involved an extensive narrative review of the literature. Articles were identified using the Loeffler Discovery Service (LDS), provided by EBSCO Discovery Service [20]. The LDS searches various databases such as PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, OpenAgrar, local library catalogues, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink and Wiley Online Library. Additionally, manual searches using Google Scholar and the Google search engine were conducted and Zanzibari co-authors identified additional publications outside of peer-reviewed journals, such as reports, presentations, policy papers and legal documents (so-called grey literature). The following search string was used: “Zanzibar” OR “Tanzania” OR “East Africa” AND different combinations of the terms “country profile”, “One Health”, “diseases”, “zoonotic diseases”, “livestock”, “public health”, “animal health”, “environmental health”, “fisheries”, “food safety”, “intersectoral collaboration”, “multidisciplinary approach”, “surveillance”, “risk factors”, “health regulations”, “anthropology”, “sociocultural factors” and “One Health implementation”. Also, local expertise and knowledge were used to inform our review. The review covered various thematic areas, including medical, veterinary, environmental, biological and anthropological sciences.

Only English-language documents were considered. As both Swahili and English are official languages in Zanzibar, all documents are available in both languages. For topics for which data sources were not identified in Zanzibar itself (e.g., data on individual zoonotic diseases), information from mainland Tanzania and nearby countries in East Africa was used. Only literature from 2000 to 2024 was included. Documents not containing topics related to OH were excluded. The selected documents were studied and summarised in order to assess the status of OH implementation in Zanzibar and to identify current challenges.

One Health Think Tank (OHTT) Workshop

The literature review was complemented with information gathered during the OHTT, which took place in Zanzibar, Tanzania in December 2022 and involved OH stakeholders from Zanzibar (n = 25), Germany (n = 8) and mainland Tanzania (n = 4). Thirty-seven participants from animal health, human health and environment, as well as government officials worked together in focus groups, addressing the topics One Health Policy; Epidemiology, Surveillance & Antimicrobial Resistance; Infectious Diseases Research; and Reproduction/Genetics & Animal Nutrition. Each group was led by a Zanzibar-German tandem, and important research subjects and areas for capacity strengthening were identified. The aim was to develop a joint agenda for future activities, closely linked to Zanzibar's One Health strategy.

Results

Central One Health actors

A high-level political leadership for multisectoral coordination exists. The DMC is the central OH actor overseeing and coordinating OH issues in accordance with the ZOHSP, and for all disaster management, preparedness and response activities at the national level. The Department of Preventive Services and Health Education (DPS) within the Ministry of Health (MoH) plays a pivotal role in safeguarding public health threats through a multifaceted approach. Its key responsibilities include providing preventive services and health education to the community, educating the public about epidemics and disseminating crucial information to ensure a well-informed and prepared society. It also provides environmental health education and dedicates efforts to understanding and addressing disease behaviour, particularly in the context of non-communicable and neglected tropical diseases. The Department of Livestock Development (DLD) within the Ministry of Agriculture, Irrigation, Natural Resources and Livestock plays a key role in the prevention, control and eradication of animal diseases, including those of public health concern (zoonoses), by providing veterinary services, diagnostics and extension services. The Zanzibar Environmental Management Authority (ZEMA), which operates under the First Vice President's Office is responsible for addressing environmental issues including, but not limited to, climate change, pollution, terrestrial and marine resources, biodiversity, waste management, integrated water resources management and public health [21]. All efforts across these sectors that require multisectoral collaboration are coordinated under the One Health Desk at the DMC. In the event of an emergency, each sector can take either a technical lead or a supporting role, depending on the nature and scale of the public health threat. Figs. A and B (Supplementary Material) map key institutions and infrastructure of the described sectors.

More than 700 non-governmental organizations (NGOs) operate in Zanzibar, with diverse focus areas. Of these, OH-related NGOs are dedicated to public health (n = 145), agriculture including livestock (n = 27) and the environment (n = 48).2

Public health sector

Basic figures and information

Zanzibar's healthcare system comprises 168 Primary Health Care Units (PHCU and PHCU+3), ten District hospitals, two specialised hospitals (one for maternity care and one for mental health), two regional hospitals, one referral hospital and 156 privately managed health facilities. Primary care is provided by community-based health services, dispensaries and health centres. Secondary care is provided by District and regional hospitals, while tertiary care is provided by referral and specialised hospitals [23,24]. The majority of these facilities are located in Unguja [2].

Surveillance system and information flow

Information flow within the healthcare system in Zanzibar follows a hierarchical pattern (Fig. 2). At the Shehia level, the smallest administrative unit in Zanzibar, health data from the community are sent to health facilities. From there, it is reported to the district level and finally to the MoH on a monthly basis. Technically, community-level health data are reported through text messages and paper-based reports. There, the District Surveillance Office (part of the District Health Management Team) and Disease-Specific Programs (DSPs) operate. As in other parts of Africa, the Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) framework improves the use of disease and laboratory data, helping health managers to identify and respond to major health issues more effectively [25]. There is no computerised system at the district health facilities for weekly IDSR reporting [24] and this data are sent by text message to the Ministry's Epidemiology Unit. The District Health Management Team also links health facilities to the national level by sending monthly reports to the MoH, using the District Health Information Software 2 (DHIS2) electronic system to transmit data to the Ministry's Health Management Information System (HMIS) [24].Fig. 2Information and feedback flow within the healthcare system in Zanzibar. Feedback from the WHO is primarily sent by email to the national and district levels, without reaching the health facilities and the community (Fig. 2).

Contributions to One Health

The DPS ensures that health services are provided at strategic locations, such as harbours and airports. Zoonotic diseases are monitored through surveillance initiatives and the department engages in surveillance on communicable diseases and epidemics (e.g., Covid-19). Information is exchanged between the DPS and existing laboratories at Mnazi Mmoja Hospital in Unguja, the Public Health Laboratory-Ivo de Carneri in Pemba and the National Health Laboratory to detect and confirm diseases and pathogens for disease surveillance [24]. The Zanzibar Food and Drug Agency (ZFDA), which operates under the MoH, conducts training for food inspectors on foodborne diseases, food safety and hygiene. By law, the ZFDA is responsible for monitoring and supervising the safety and hygiene of animal products intended for human consumption in Zanzibar. The agency is also responsible for registering and licensing the import of veterinary antimicrobials.4 The ZFDA raises awareness in the community, analyses different environmental samples and conducts research to protect public health from the effects of unsuitable products. An electronic reporting system (e-IDSR) for food-borne diseases, such as cholera and typhoid, exists.5 Exercises on Ebola, including full-scale simulation exercises [31], have been conducted to test readiness at points of entry [32]. Additionally, most healthcare facilities have preparedness and response teams [24]. On mainland Tanzania, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) established the Tanzania Field Epidemiology and Laboratory Training Program (TFELTP) in 2008 [33], to strengthen the country's outbreak response capabilities [34].

Governance

The government has established various committees and groups in the ZOHSP, including a One Health Management Committee and Rapid Response Teams. These groups involve a wide range of sector representatives. Under the SVPO's Commission for Disaster Management, the Principal Secretary for Health, Secretaries, District Officers, epidemiologists and researchers are actively involved in various groups to protect the health of people, animals and the environment [4]. Meetings within the One Health Technical Working Group (TWG) take place, but not on a regular basis.

Agriculture/veterinary sector

Basic figures and information

People in Zanzibar mainly depend on livestock and poultry for food, fertilizer, draught power and financial security [4]. The agricultural sector accounts for almost 25 % of the gross domestic product (GDP) [23] with a growth rate of 3.3 % [35], while the livestock contribution to the GDP is 4 % [36]. The most commonly kept animals are chickens (3,764,184), cattle (270,998) and goats (111,623) [37]. The main crops are cassava and bananas, while cloves and seaweed are the most commonly exported products [23].

Workforce

The veterinary workforce in Zanzibar consists of veterinarians, Veterinary Para-Professionals (VPPs) and Community Animal Health Workers (CAHW). The Chief Veterinary Officer (CVO) is responsible for all veterinary (laboratory) services. The Revolutionary Government of Zanzibar (RGoZ) has introduced scholarships specifically designed for Bachelor of Veterinary Medicine (BVM) students, with the aim of increasing the number of veterinarians. Nevertheless, there is no formal veterinary science training to bachelor or higher level implemented in Zanzibar. Due to the limited number of veterinarians (Table 3), who have been trained overseas, VPPs provide almost all veterinary services and contribute to veterinary public health by monitoring and surveilling entry points. CAHWs are locally selected livestock keepers who have undergone training in basic animal husbandry, enabling them to provide a range of veterinary services [38]. Training in the animal health sector predominantly focuses on artificial insemination, laboratory services, vaccinations and animal health management [39]. As a recent development, in 2023 and 2024, two field schools entitled ‘Healthy Livestock - Healthy People’ were organised in cooperation between the Zanzibar Agriculture and Livestock Research Institute (ZALIRI), the DLD and the Friedrich-Loeffler-Institut (German Federal Research Institute for Animal Health; FLI). Veterinary personnel were trained in the areas of herd health, case definitions, differential diagnosis, ante- and post-mortem inspection, high-quality sampling, laboratory diagnostics and interpretation of results. Surveillance system and information flow

Data collection within the livestock sector is initiated at the field level and culminates at the ministry level (Fig. 3). Information reaches the next level within a seven-day period, or in case of notifiable diseases within six hours. Zanzibar does not report directly to the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH), because of Zanzibar's affiliation with the Republic of Tanzania. Tanzania's Director of Veterinary Services (DVS) is recognised as the responsible delegate. At present there is no data sharing between DLD and the Tanzanian national authority, which means that data from Zanzibar do not reach WOAH. DLD is currently developing an animal health surveillance system.Fig. 3Overview of the existing and envisioned data flow in the livestock sector (based on information from oral communication with the CVO Zanzibar and the Department of Livestock Development Zanzibar).Fig. 3

Reports issued by DLD provide information on disease outbreaks, the type of disease, the number of animals affected, the quantity of vaccinations administered, activities on quarantine stations and the number of staff [40]. Despite the absence of a livestock traceability system, the DLD maintains records of farmers and the number of livestock they own.6 Availability of veterinary pharmaceuticals is restricted, and access to these medicines remains largely unregulated. This enables farmers and animal owners to procure and administer medications, some of which may be of substandard quality, without seeking professional advice. This practice increases the risk of improper or ineffective use, contributing to antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Consequently, veterinarians are working to limit self-administration of treatments and promote responsible use of veterinary medicines.7

Contributions to One Health

The DLD conducts surveillance and response for zoonotic diseases such as rabies, brucellosis and anthrax. Additionally, it fosters sustainable livestock production practices and guides farmers on safe farming practices, including effective waste management and biosecurity measures. Free annual rabies vaccination campaigns have been implemented in recent years [41]. The Tanzania Veterinary Laboratory Agency (TVLA) is a collaboration partner on mainland Tanzania, providing support to Zanzibar in the domains of sample analysis and training. ZALIRI, established in 2017 under MAINL, coordinates research on animal diseases and livestock production, with a particular emphasis on their public health significance. It collaborates with national and international partners on capacity strengthening initiatives and joint research projects, intending to determine the burden of animal diseases in Zanzibar. The Food and Agriculture Organization's (FAO) Emergency Centre for Transboundary Animal Diseases (ECTAD) [42] was established in Tanzania in 2016 to strengthen the country's veterinary service capacities.8 FAO has implemented an In-Service Applied Veterinary Epidemiology Training (ISAVET) [43], a four-months veterinary training program that provides proficient training for field and laboratory frontline staff, mentors and trainers.

Governance

The DLD represents a pivotal element in the conceptualization of the One Health Desk under the DMC, in which the CVO is involved. Veterinary epidemiologists and officers are members of the OH TWG and further OH committees under the SPVO-DMC. However, there is no veterinary statutory body. Consequently, the chain of command within the department and the roles of VPPs and CAHWs in the provision of animal health services are frequently unclear [38].

Environment sector

Basic figures and information

The coastal zone of the Zanzibar archipelago is rich in natural resources and comprises diverse ecosystems including coral reefs, mangroves, seagrass meadows, algal beds, beaches, dunes, estuaries, rocky shores and coastal forests. The corals of Pemba are considered to be among the healthiest and most diverse coral formations in East Africa [44]. Natural forests are only to be found in protected or specifically managed areas. The terrestrial wildlife is dominated by small mammals, birds, reptiles and butterflies, and monkeys live in protected areas, such as the Jozani Chwaka Bay National Park [4]. Beekeeping is a prevalent supplementary livelihood in coastal villages [45]. Zanzibar's coastal Zone is further characterized [45] by a growing tourism industry, while efforts are being made to preserve and integrate the region's rich traditional culture. Fishing continues to be practiced using traditional methods without having transitioned to commercial production, with fishermen operating within territorial waters up to 12 nautical miles from the coast, using canoes, dhows or skiffs. The most common fishing methods are long lining and purse seining [46]. The total fish catch increased by almost 90 % between 2014 (33,000 tons) and 2022 (62,000 tons) [21], primarily due to an increase in the number of fishermen actively engaged in the industry. However, international fishing off Zanzibar's coast contributes to the decline in fish populations, which poses a significant challenge for the local fishing industry. Common species caught include tuna, swordfish, marlin, kingfish and sailfish [47]. The islands of Zanzibar face a number of additional environmental challenges, including groundwater shortages, intensified by tourism and population growth, climate-change-induced over-extraction and pressure on fisheries resources. Rising sea temperatures harm seaweed and coral reefs [48,49]. The Government of Zanzibar identifies sea-level rise, erosion, marine accidents, strong winds and flooding as threats [32,50]. Deforestation and sand mining contribute to erosion, which has a direct impact on water sources [48]. The construction of hotels too near to beaches is another leading cause of beach erosion [51]. The Zanzibar Water Authority (ZAWA) estimates that 62 % of people in Zanzibar have access to the piped water network, while the remaining 38 % rely on informal water providers. Access to clean sanitation is one of Zanzibar's most pressing problems with little improvement over the last 10 years [52]. Sewage water is often discharged into the sea.9

Workforce

The workforce in the environment sector includes environmental officers, biodiversity specialists, climate change specialists, fisheries officers, marine conservationists, aquatic health specialists, aquaculture specialists, fishermen and seaweed farmers. ZEMA monitors biodiversity, terrestrial and marine ecosystems, the coastal zone, waste management and natural resources.

Seaweed farming is carried out commercially [45] and employs 23,654 people, 13,393 (56 %) of whom are women [53]. Production has fluctuated over the years due to epiphyte infestation and Ice-Ice disease (IID), a disease condition in seaweed, which is caused by changes in salinity, ocean temperature, light intensity or pathogenic bacteria (e.g., Vibrio spp. and Pseudomonas spp.). Typical signs of IID include whitening, progressive disintegration of tissue and loss of biomass [54]. Epiphyte infestation has been associated with warm water temperatures in Zanzibar and appears to subside during the rainy season [53]. The Ministry of Blue Economy and Fisheries (MBEF) relies on a diverse and skilled workforce to cover technical, administrative and supportive roles. Its Department of Marine Conservation is responsible for protecting marine ecosystems, and developing and enforcing conservation strategies. The Department of Fisheries Development (DFD), which operates under the MBEF, is responsible for collecting fisheries data and overseeing all fisheries development activities in Zanzibar.

Contributions to One Health

Environmental officers are part of various teams and committees, including the Rapid Response Team under the SVPO. ZEMA has representatives on the Research and Development TWG, the Surveillance TWG and the Preparedness and Response TWG [4]. They coordinate environmental impact assessments, audits and monitoring, and issue environmental certificates, permits and authorisations. The Zanzibar Fisheries and Marine Resources Research Institute (ZAFIRI), which operates under the MBEF, conducts and coordinates research on fisheries, aquaculture, water quality, marine resources and innovation. It also trains fishermen in the early detection and response to fish diseases. The DFD has established a mechanism to manage fishing camps and migrant fishermen in order to regulate fishing and to control the spread of communicable diseases [55]. The MBEF ensures that seafood is safe to consume by regulating fishing practices and monitoring fish health and the presence of diseases and contaminants that could affect fish populations. They have set up public awareness programs to educate local people about proper handling, processing, packaging and disease prevention [56]. They also repair or build landing sites and fish markets to ensure that they are well-maintained, meet safety standards and have adequate storage facilities. Private companies are employed to monitor the cleanliness of the markets throughout the day [[57], [58], [59]]. The Environmental Health Unit, UNICEF and the U.S CDC are supporting Zanzibar's WASH (Water, Sanitation and Hygiene) Program [60], to improve drinking water quality by chlorinating it at the point of collection [52].

Governance

Environmental management in Zanzibar is coordinated by ZEMA. Its mandate is to coordinate, regulate, monitor and control environmental management issues in Zanzibar [61]. The Department of Environment, which operates under the First Vice President's office, is responsible for promoting and monitoring the implementation of the environmental policy [62]. Public and environmental health matters are managed and protected by the DPS, who fulfils an important role in health education for communities [63]. The Environmental Health Unit is an integral component of the MoH. The DFD under the RGoZ has selected five Marine Conservation Areas (MCA) to ensure the proper management of the marine and coastal environment [55]. In order to ensure the safety of consumption and the sustainable use of marine resources, the RGoZ established ZAFIRI. Its DFD works closely with fishermen's committees to monitor illegal fishing practices and protect fisheries resources, including managing the influx of migratory fishermen by patrolling potential fishing grounds [55]. The management of deep-sea and high seas fishing beyond 12 nautical miles from the Tanzanian coast is shared between Zanzibar and the mainland. This area falls within Tanzania's Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ), where international and industrial fishing companies operate. On the Zanzibar side, the Zanzibar's Deep Sea Fishing Authority (DSFA) is responsible. Community-based management and the involvement of stakeholders in decision-making processes help to ensure the effectiveness and cultural appropriateness of policies [64].

Discussion

Zanzibar has recognised the importance of the OH approach to control diseases and health threats at the interface between humans, animals and the environment. This assertion is reinforced by the formulation of the Zanzibar One Health Strategy by the government and immediate steps towards its implementation, such as the One Health Think Tank workshop (2022). Zanzibar's active engagement in health safety processes such as a recently renewed Joint External Evaluation (JEE) (2023) and National Action Plan for Health Security (NAPHS) (2024), also builds on the OH approach [32,65]. While Zanzibar's inagural JEE (2017) identified “the need for multisectoral coordination between sectors and levels of administration” as one of seven overarching theme priorities [66], the second JEE attested significant progress in this domain. However, the JEE scores in the technical areas, specifically relevant to OH and multisectoral collaboration, imply only minor progress, stagnation, or even degradation for important indicators. OH key areas such as surveillance of and response to foodborne diseases and the biosafety and biosecurity systems, as well as the multisectoral workforce strategy demonstrated remarkably low (1 out of 510) scores in the 2023 JEE. Significant challenges remain to be addressed to ensure effective inter-sectoral collaboration and disease control.

The presence of political will, alongside the recognition of the importance of OH, and the availability of relevant policies, strategies and a legal framework, collectively engender a favourable environment for the institutionalisation of OH. In the United Republic of Tanzania, the national OH platform is hosted at the Prime Minister's office [67,68], and it is recognised at a high level in the country's governmental system. The United Republic of Tanzania has also developed a National One Health Strategic Plan [69]. In Zanzibar, the OH Coordination Unit constitutes the core of the OH implementation framework. This unit is situated within the Disaster Management Commission, which is chaired by the SPVO. The SVPO has comparable authorities in the RGoZ as the Prime Minister of the United Republic of Tanzania. However, the OH Coordination Unit appears to be less recognised in the Commission's organizational structure. The ZOHSP demonstrates a high level of multisectoral involvement into OH, interconnecting actors from diverse areas and (administrative) levels. It can be used to describe the roles of a broad range of ministries to local government, police, non-state actors and civil society, as well as international development partners. However, it is not always clear in how far the plans and prescriptions of the ZOHSP are functional or have advanced since its launch in late 2022. To date, no evaluation has been conducted to measure the level and impact of OH implementation. The development of a monitoring and evaluation plan (M&E) was required by the ZOHSP, along with a list of 67 M&E indicators. According to the ZOHSP, the primary data sources were progress reports, of which two were available from the OH TWG meetings. These were scheduled to occur on a quarterly basis. Cross-sectoral activities such as joint simulation exercises have not yet been realized. Overlapping regulations and roles and poor implementation of policies have been postulated [70] and may still be valid, given the extensive list of legal and regulatory documents applicable to OH (see table in Supplementary Material). The extent to which these documents operate and interact to promote a uniform and comprehensive OH implementation in practice remains unclear. Consequently, the 2023 JEE recommends conducting a comprehensive legal analysis of relevant instruments through a multisectoral approach, with the objective of strengthening the country's preparedness framework.

Regarding the three main sectors by their respective strengths and weaknesses, the public health sector is characterized by a well-structured surveillance system, including IDSR, and a number of well-equipped laboratories. Nevertheless, the functionality of these systems and infrastructures can partly be questioned. Preparedness and response teams have been established at national and district level and in most healthcare facilities. However, a significant number of these teams remain inactive or lack regular meetings, and their activities frequently go unevaluated. This poses challenges in maintaining an effective and responsive healthcare system at the base. In hospitals, standard case definitions for priority diseases are not in place and action thresholds are only available for cholera, malaria, measles, tuberculosis, acute flaccid paralysis, rabies, dengue fever and yellow fever [24,71]. Although Malaria cases were declining from 2021 to 2022, the number of cases has been on the rise since October 2023 [72]. A study by Saleh et al. in 2021 [24] assessed the core and support functions of the IDSR system in Zanzibar and aligns with our findings. Health facilities encounter difficulties in diagnosing priority diseases due to inadequate capacity and insufficient laboratory equipment. Guidelines for specimen collection are lacking in half of the facilities and districts, which is further compounded by issues such as airtime voucher shortages and poor phone networks, hindering timely data submission. Data analysis is a rarity in hospitals. Feedback reports from higher levels are scarce, and supervision is inadequate across all levels. Reference laboratories lack molecular diagnostic capacity, which leads to samples being shipped to the mainland. The authors conclude that the current system is inadequate for the early detection of and response to infectious disease outbreaks.

The infrastructure necessary for the effective delivery of veterinary services is lacking, and there is insufficient knowledge and data on zoonotic diseases and AMR. The presence or absence of zoonotic diseases is often only suspected, as evidenced by the provisional list of pathogens compiled in Table 1. This list is based on evidence and local knowledge, resulting in a very limited number of diseases that can be excluded, suggesting that the definitive list of prevalent zoonotic threats is extensive. The delivery of livestock services in terms of zoonotic diseases control is fragmented and uncoordinated and does not make use of livestock traceability. The awareness among livestock keepers of the importance of disease reporting is low, and there is often a lack of essential equipment and tools, such as sample collection kits and data collection tools [38]. With regard to the laboratory systems, it is noteworthy that animal health laboratories scored 1 in the 2023 JEE, while human health laboratories scored 3. Zoonotic disease indicators (surveillance, response and sanitary animal production) have since 2017 remained at a constant score of 2. Though Zanzibar has a livestock data collection routine [40], it is not possible to access this data. Even if data from Zanzibar's animal health sector are available, they do not reach international databases such as WOAH, and therefore remain neglected. There is a critical need for farmer training on AMR and appropriate antibiotic use across both Unguja and Pemba islands [73], as well as addressing biosecurity and the potentially high number of unofficial livestock imports to the islands. A lot of valuable knowledge is present within the country and can be used, e.g., CAHW in the field are highly experienced with the local conditions, but stocking up on knowledge through special training and capacity strengthening activities would further enhance this positive effect. In response to the aforementioned issues, a long-term partnership was initiated in 2023 between the FLI and Zanzibar, led by ZALIRI on the Zanzibar side. This partnership aims to implement measures to strengthen basic and applied sciences, as well as OH capacities through research and sustainable education and training. The Zanzibar-German collaboration has been advanced by the organization of joint field schools on livestock veterinary medicine and diagnosis in December 2023 (Unguja) and November 2024 (Pemba) that will be continued on an annual basis.

Due to the complexity of the ecosystem as a whole, it is challenging to provide a comprehensive narrative description. Therefore, the present study's findings may not be exhaustive. However, there is evidence to suggest that the challenging overview may also hamper an effective integration of the environment sector into OH. For instance, the ZOHSP does not consider an environmental pillar (see Fig.1). The marine ecosystem is confronted with cross-cutting issues, such as overfishing and over-tourism. These issues further exacerbate environmental issues relevant to OH, such as the sinking of the groundwater level [49]. Furthermore, there is a need to monitor zoonotic pathogens such as Vibrio spp. or harmful algal blooms (HABs), which have the potential to impact both fish and human health. Enhanced collaboration between fisheries, public health, and veterinary services is also required to track disease trends.11 At present, the responsibility for environmental health in Zanzibar is assigned to the MoH and is part of the public health sector.

Our work attempted to provide a comprehensive outline of the legal, competence and functional landscape relevant for OH in the Zanzibar archipelago. The present study provides specific insights into the unique challenges of Zanzibar, which often remain unaddressed in the existing literature. One of the strengths of this study is the access to unpublished documents and domestic expert opinions, which enable a more profound comprehension of the situation on the ground. A major limitation is a paucity of literature and published data pertinent to Zanzibar, a factor that impeded the execution of a systematic analysis. This challenge is exemplified by the narrative, qualitative nature of this study. Due to Zanzibar's affiliation with the Republic of Tanzania and its relatively small size, data reported at the national level only provide a limited representation of Zanzibar (e.g., from surveillance) compared to Tanzania mainland data. Owing especially to its insular location, data from the mainland may not be representative of Zanzibar. This underscores the necessity for continued research and evidence-based policy decision-making on the islands. A further limitation contributing to the incompleteness of this analysis were difficulties in involving qualified and meaningful representatives of the individual sectors, especially in the very broad environmental sector, to obtain a full picture.

Efforts of landscaping OH have also been undertaken in other countries and regions, including the Philippines [74] and the Democratic Republic of Congo DRC [75], which have both revealed partially comparable challenges. Despite the lack of systematics in the present study due to the paucity of data, it provides relevant aspects of the OH landscape of a country in a structured way. The present findings are consistent with studies conducted in mainland Tanzania, which also identified weak (veterinary) infrastructure and coordination in disease control [39,76,77]. The identified gaps in the implementation of the OH approach offer important starting points for evidence-based policy guidance. The implementation of targeted measures to enhance medical infrastructure, systematic communication between sectors and laboratory capacity has the potential to significantly increase the efficiency of the health system. This could contribute to more effective control of (zoonotic) diseases and other health threats, thereby protecting humans, animals and the environment. For instance, in the 2023–24 period, the collaboration between Zanzibar and Germany has already made significant progress in the joint undertaking of activities and infrastructure, including a seroprevalence study on four zoonotic diseases, namely Brucellosis, Leptospirosis, Q-fever and Rift Valley fever, in cattle (manuscript in preparation). The establishment of high-performance laboratory infrastructure for the veterinary sector is currently underway, with shared costs between the partners. This initiative showcases how international support complemented by local political will can unlock resources and accelerate progress. Upcoming projects will contribute to collecting additional data specific to Zanzibar, with a view to further strengthening the prioritisation and effective interventions towards health threats. However, it is important to analyse the long-term impact of the implemented strategies to address the identified gaps.

Conclusion

Despite the fact that Zanzibar demonstrates a high recognition and political determination to implement an OH approach, there are still some sector-specific and cross-sectoral gaps that must be addressed to achieve the full potential of OH collaboration and cross-linkage in surveillance, research, and communication. This includes inadequate or underutilised infrastructure, a paucity of OH research and epidemiological data as well as case definitions, the absence of oversight of the sectors and critical gaps within the workforce. However, the unique island location and a joint local, national and international effort have the potential to enhance Zanzibar's capacity for the effective implementation of the OH approach, thus serving as a model for the region. By demonstrating effective disease control and sustainable livestock management practices, Zanzibar can set a precedent for other areas facing similar challenges.
