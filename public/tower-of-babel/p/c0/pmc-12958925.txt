Neurophobia among the Egyptian early-career physicians: the first national multi-centre report
Authors: Hossam Tharwat Ali, Abdalrahman Salah Shehata, Nada A Al-Shafey, Mohamed M Khalaf, Esraa Reda Abdelaziz, Dalia Atef Abouda, Mustafa Faris Elesway, Sara Hosny El-Farargy, Egyptian Neurophobia Collaborative Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is a shortage of neurologists at a time when brain disorders have become major contributors to disability and death worldwide. This is the first national study of Egyptian early-career physicians, exploring their attitudes towards neurology and neuroscience.MethodsThis cross-sectional study took place in September and October 2024 using a structured, self-administered online questionnaire, comprising Likert-scale, multiple-choice and closed-ended questions. We calculated the mean and SD for the score of each speciality in different characteristics (ie, interest, difficulty, confidence and knowledge). The concept of neurophobia was defined as a combined confidence and difficulty score of ≤4 points. Data analysis was done using R Statistical Software (V.4.4.2, R Core Team).ResultsOur study included 445 participants, comprising 384 interns (86%), 23 primary care physicians (5.2%) and 38 residents (8.5%). Around 51% of interns and primary care physicians were males, compared with 34% of residents. A total of 205 (50.4%) of the interns and primary care physicians exhibited neurophobia, followed by 197 (48.4%) to cardiology, and 132 (32.4%) to geriatrics. The most common factors for neurophobia were trouble with neuroanatomy (48.9%), lack of curative treatment or poor prognosis (35.6%), too many rare diagnoses (34.2%), poor teaching (25.8%) and trouble with basic neuroscience (22.4%). Interns and primary care physicians showed a moderate likelihood of specialising in neurology with a mean (SD) score of 2.55 (1.09).ConclusionsThe current study provided a summary of residents’ perspectives toward neurology and neuroscience as well as suggestions to combat neurophobia.Keywords: NEUROANATOMY, CLINICAL NEUROLOGY, MEDICINE

Introduction

According to the WHO reports, neurological conditions contribute to about 6.4% of global morbidity and 12% of mortality.1 Many such conditions are faced and managed by early physicians and general practitioners. However, there is a shortage of neurologists at a time when brain disorders have become major contributors to disability and death worldwide.2,4

Neurophobia, defined as a fear of neurological sciences, was coined in the 1990s with a rising prevalence among medical students and young physicians, most probably due to difficulty in applying their knowledge of basic sciences to clinical situations.5 Neurophobia negatively affects the choice of career speciality and hence contributes to the shortage of neurologists.6 Neurophobia has been reported in the literature among medical students and junior physicians in different areas and healthcare systems.2,4 The prevalence of neurophobia among medical students and young doctors is notably high, underscoring the need for targeted interventions to address this issue.7 8

To mitigate neurophobia, it is essential to evaluate the underlying reasons within each sector of the healthcare system in country-based investigations, since there have been variable reports and conclusions from different studies.24 7,9 Our national multicentre project aimed to explore the existence of neurophobia among Egyptian medical students and young physicians in their early career.9 10 In our previous manuscript, with a neurophobia prevalence of 35.7% among Egyptian medical students, we managed to identify common reasons for difficulty as well as factors with or against choosing neurology as a career option, and hence built specific recommendations to tackle the phenomenon.10 Furthermore, we contribute to the global research on neurophobia along with recent studies in the literature. To the best of our knowledge, this report constitutes the first to provide an estimate of the prevalence of neurophobia and the perspective of young physicians in the Egyptian healthcare system.

Methods

Study design

Following the strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology statement for cross-sectional studies (online supplemental Table S1),11 we conducted this multicentre cross-sectional study as a part of our neurophobia evaluation project in Egypt,10 to assess the prevalence of neurophobia and identify its associated factors among physicians across various levels of clinical training throughout Egypt in 13 universities using a structured, self-administered online questionnaire designed by the “Google Forms” platform during September and October 2024. This report included post-graduate training levels, including medical interns, residents, non-specialist and specialist physicians, all over Egypt.

Sample size

For this study, a minimum sample size of 385 participants was required for a 95% CI, a 5% margin of error and a 50% response distribution. We used Epi Info software V.7.2.5.0 for sample size calculation. Using convenience sampling, participants were recruited via social media platforms (eg, Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, etc) and professional networks.

Study tool

Data were collected using a self-administered, anonymous, web-based questionnaire. The tool was developed through a rigorous process involving prior literature review, expert consultations and pilot testing to ensure content validity and relevance to the study population.15,8 12 The questionnaire incorporated a mix of Likert-scale, multiple-choice and close-ended questions and was designed to explore the following domains: sociodemographic and professional background including age, gender, current position (intern, primary care physician, resident by year) and speciality for residents; Exposure to neurology including personal or family history of neurological illness, caregiving experience, participation in neurology-related research and involvement in clinical case discussions; Educational experience and speciality perception in which participants rated their confidence, knowledge, interest, perceived difficulty and quality of teaching (both undergraduate and postgraduate) across several medical specialities including neurology; Perception of neurology and neurophobia: which was explored through items assessing reasons for difficulty in neurology (eg, neuroanatomy, limited exposure, lack of integration), as well as confidence in managing neurological cases and likelihood of pursuing neurology as a career; Motivating and deterring factors in which participants selected factors that would influence their decision to pursue or avoid neurology (eg, intellectual challenge, lifestyle, stress, teaching quality, financial reward); For non-neurologists, there were questions focused on reasons for not choosing neurology, perceived difficulty and confidence relative to other specialities; For neurologists, including items on reasons for choosing neurology, satisfaction, comparison with peers in other specialities and whether they considered leaving the field and suggestions for improvement in which participants were invited to recommend improvements in neurology teaching and training, such as more integrated teaching, bedside training, neuroanatomy sessions or enhanced self-learning resources. The questionnaire included conditional logic to tailor questions based on the participant’s role and speciality to enhance clarity and relevance.

Statistical analysis

To assess normality, we used the Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test of normality and QQ-plots. Continuous normally distributed data and Likert-scale elements were reported as means and SD, while non-normally distributed data were reported as median and IQR. Categorical variables were summarised using frequency and percentages. We also calculated the mean and SD for the score of each speciality in different characteristics (ie, interest, difficulty, confidence and knowledge). In this study, and consistently with the definition used by previous studies, the concept of neurophobia was defined as a combined confidence and difficulty score of ≤4 points.10 12 14 20 The same definition was used to calculate the prevalence of phobias in other specialities. While there is a long-standing debate regarding the use of parametric versus non-parametric tests for analysing the Likert scale, studies have shown that both methods yield nearly identical results, with parametric tests demonstrating robust performance.21 Given this and our large sample size, we opted for Welch’s t-test to compare neurology with other specialities across different aspects (eg, interest, difficulty). To control multiple comparisons bias, we applied the Bonferroni correction; therefore, a p value of <0.007 was considered significant. To confirm the robustness of our analysis, we conducted a sensitivity analysis where we used the Mann–Whitney U test in place of Welch’s t-test (online supplemental Data). All analyses were performed in R (V.4.4.2, R Core Team).

Results

Demographic characteristics

Our study included 445 participants, comprising 384 interns (86%), 23 primary care physicians (5.2%) and 38 residents (8.5%), with four being neurology residents and 34 non-neurology residents. The age distribution was represented as median and IQR due to non-normality; 24 (24, 25) years old for interns and primary care physicians, and 28 (27, 29) for residents. The gender distribution showed 51% of interns were males, while 34% of residents were males. Participants were affiliated with various universities across Egypt, with the majority from Alexandria University (31% of interns and 13% of residents), followed by Tanta University (25% of interns and 7.9% of residents), and South Valley University (17% of interns and 13% of residents). Notably, most participants reported no history of congenital or neurological diseases (93% of interns and 100% of residents), no family history of neurological disorders (74% of interns and 76% of residents) and no prior experience caring for individuals with neurological diseases (75% of interns and 82% of residents). However, many interns had engaged in case-based discussions or presentations (58%) and participated in neuroscience or neurology research (66%) (table 1).

Interns’ and primary care physicians’ perspectives

Attitude towards neurology and neuroscience

Participants rated their interest and confidence in various specialities using a 5-point Likert scale (table 2). Neurology ranked fourth in terms of interest, with a mean (SD) score of 3.00 (1.24), following cardiology with 3.03 (1.31), gastroenterology with 3.26 (1.22) and endocrinology with 3.27 (1.20), with p values of 0.7, 0.003 and 0.002, respectively. In terms of confidence, neurology scored moderately (mean=2.60, SD=0.94), while gastroenterology scored the highest (mean=3.26, SD=1.08) and geriatrics scored the lowest (mean=2.34, SD=0.95). Neurology was perceived as a challenging speciality, with a mean difficulty score of 2.03 (SD=0.96), second only to cardiology (mean=1.94, SD=0.86), with an insignificant difference (p=0.1548) while showing significantly more difficulty than the other specialities (p<0.0001).

As per the defined method for neurophobia, 205 (50.4%) of the interns and primary care physicians exhibited neurophobia, 197 (48.4%) showed phobia to cardiology and 132 (32.4%) to geriatrics (online supplemental Figure S1). According to the interns and primary care physicians, the most common factors making neurology difficult were trouble with neuroanatomy (48.9%), lack of curative treatment in most instances or poor prognosis (35.6%), too many rare diagnoses (34.2%), poor teaching (25.8%) and trouble with basic neuroscience (22.4%) (online supplemental Figure S2). Sensitivity analysis is provided in online supplemental Table S2.

Quality of teaching aspects

When rating the quality of undergraduate teaching, neurology scored moderately 2.95 (1.07), ranking fourth significantly after rheumatology 3.22 (1.08), endocrinology 3.30 (1.07) and gastroenterology 3.32 (1.06). For the quality of postgraduate teaching, neurology 2.40 (1.04) was significantly lower than endocrinology 2.58 (1.09) and gastroenterology 2.70 (1.14). Interns and primary care physicians reported that the COVID-19 pandemic has had a bad effect on the quality of teaching of neurology, 2.43 (0.96), and across all specialities without significant differences (table 3). Neurology as a career option for interns and primary care physicians

Many factors persuade interns and primary care physicians to pursue a career in neurology, which were the ability to make a significant impact on patients’ lives (31.4%), financial reward (31.0%), intellectual challenge (28.0%) and prestige (27.5%) (figure 1). In contrast, some factors discourage taking a career in neurology, such as difficulty (42.0%), complex patients (39.1%), stress (33.2%) and lack of training centres (30.5%) (figure 2). Interns and primary care physicians showed a moderate likelihood of specialising in neurology with a mean (SD) score of 2.55 (1.09) and confidence in knowing what neurologists do with a core of 2.92 (0.96) out of 5-point scales (online supplemental Table S4).

Residents’ perspective towards neurology

Out of the 38 residents, four were in the neurology speciality (online supplemental Table S5). Two residents (50%) chose neurology due to possible opportunities for travel and research opportunities, while one resident (25%) chose each of the following: ability to make a significant impact on patients’ lives, competitive field, financial reward, intellectually challenging, passion and teamwork. The neurology residents reported many suggestions for improving neurology education, including more case discussions (100%), more bedside teaching (50%), more lectures on clinical neurology (50%), more neuroanatomy (50%) and neuroscience (50%) lectures. According to the neurology residents, trouble with diagnosis (75%), lack of integrated teaching (50%) and trouble with basic neuroscience (50%) were the reasons for difficulty in neurology (online supplemental Table S6). As for non-neurology residents, reasons for not choosing neurology as a speciality included other career interests (47%), difficulty (27%) and complex patients (12%) (online supplemental Table S7).

Discussion

The present study supports the evidence for the prevalence of neurophobia among early-career physicians, mainly medical interns and primary care physicians in Egypt. With a neurophobia rate of 50.4% among interns and primary care physicians, this figure falls within the upper range of international reports (18%–47%) and reflects the substantial cognitive and educational barriers faced by early-career physicians in neurology training.22 Despite high interest in neurology—comparable to cardiology—it was perceived as difficult and intimidating, which supports the notion that neurophobia is not merely a product of disinterest but stems from structural and pedagogical shortcomings.6 The nature of the term “neurophobia” may seem to reflect specifically emotional aspects; however, it has been used in the literature mostly as a measure of difficulty, disinterest, intellectual and emotional reactions.

The most frequent contributor to neurophobia was difficulty with neuroanatomy (48.9%), corroborating findings from Tarolli and Józefowicz,23 who emphasised the problematic disconnection between basic neuroscience and clinical practice. This isolation of clinical relevance from complex neuroanatomical structures impedes effective learning and contributes to diagnostic insecurity. As Józefowicz noted, the failure to integrate “science” and “art” in neurology renders the former irrelevant and the latter mystical.5 Additional factors such as a perceived lack of curative treatment (35.6%), the abundance of rare diagnoses (34.2%) and poor teaching (25.8%) further reinforce the psychological barriers to neurology. These results align with prior research indicating that neurology is often viewed as an intellectually challenging and diagnostically elite speciality.24 The underrepresentation of common, manageable neurological conditions during clinical rotations also contributes to this perception, as students disproportionately encounter complex inpatient cases rather than routine outpatient presentations.25

Teaching quality was also a concern, with neurology ranking lower than most other specialities in both undergraduate and postgraduate contexts. Participants reported that the COVID-19 pandemic negatively affected medical education in general, but this merely compounded longstanding deficiencies in neurology teaching. This study tried to explore the perspective of neurology residents, where they offered educational suggestions, including case-based discussions, bedside teaching and targeted lectures, mirroring those proposed in the literature to counteract neurophobia through greater vertical integration, earlier exposure and clinical contextualisation of theory.22 23 26

In terms of career orientation, neurology was associated with both motivating and deterring factors. Positive motivators included the potential to impact patients’ lives, intellectual challenge and prestige. However, significant deterrents such as perceived difficulty, complex patient cases and inadequate training infrastructure indicate that neurophobia is reinforced by systemic barriers beyond the classroom. The discrepancy between neurology’s conceptual appeal and practical avoidance highlights an urgent need for reform in both educational content and delivery mechanisms.25 27 It is noteworthy that our previous report found a lower rate of neurophobia among medical students (36.6%), even though numerous studies indicated a low frequency of neurophobia among physicians compared with medical students.9 10 It may be explained by the fact that the quality of education in neurology was lower than that of most specialities, especially in the postgraduate phase. The lack of rotation time and limited exposure to neurological patients may also contribute to the high incidence of neurophobia among physicians, who view neurology as a really challenging topic that requires immediate attention.

Ultimately, our findings align closely with international data and emphasise that neurophobia is deeply embedded in medical training systems. It is augmented by inadequate teaching methods, poor integration of neuroscience and clinical neurology, and a lack of hands-on clinical engagement. These deficits not only compromise educational outcomes but may also affect healthcare systems by contributing to increased specialist referrals and underprepared generalists.7 28

Nationwide, the Egyptian healthcare system exhibits heterogeneity in the quality of care across geographical areas and the private and public sectors, as well as discrepancies in the availability of speciality training and mentorship programmes, especially in subspecialities such as neurology and neurosurgery. Some public tertiary care hospitals do not provide neurological or neurosurgical consultations in the first place. This, in part, can be a consequence of neurophobia, leading to fewer trained physicians and widening the gap in such specialities, which eventually causes certain private centres to provide high-quality but expensive care in such specialities. This is accompanied by the general lack of resources and funding, and workload imbalances for the residency years in most medical specialities.4 29 30

Strengths and limitations

Besides being the first to explore neurophobia among early-career physicians in Egypt, a key strength of this study is its large sample size (n=445), which included a diverse range of early-career medical professionals from various academic institutions across Egypt. This enhances the generalisability of the findings within the national context. Additionally, the study applied robust statistical methods, including normality testing and both parametric and non-parametric comparisons, ensuring the reliability and reproducibility of the results. However, the study also has several limitations. The number of neurology residents was small (n=4) as well as non-neurology residents (n=34), limiting the validity of subgroup analyses for this group. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the relationship between educational experiences and the development of neurophobia. Moreover, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce response bias, particularly in the subjective assessments of confidence and interest.

Future recommendations

Putting our findings in the context of the literature, several actionable recommendations can be made. Medical curricula should adopt a vertically integrated approach that connects neuroanatomy and basic neuroscience with clinical practice from the earliest stages.5 23 26 Greater emphasis should be placed on real-world case discussions, outpatient rotations and bedside tutorials to foster clinical reasoning and demystify neurological examination.22 26 Investing in the pedagogical training of neurology educators and establishing mentorship programmes can increase student engagement and interest in the speciality. Shifting the focus from rare syndromes to common and manageable disorders may reduce intimidation and improve practical competence.25 Addressing the lack of neurology training centres and expanding postgraduate opportunities can help mitigate systemic barriers to career entry in neurology.28 Virtual simulations, 3D neuroanatomy models and interactive modules can improve spatial understanding and engagement with complex content.23 31 Special attention should be given to exploring aspects of neurophobia and types of attitudes toward neuroscience and neurology. Future research should elaborate on whether it is a matter of individual reactions or stigma to reputation.

Conclusions

This study highlights the persistence of neurophobia among Egyptian medical professionals and identifies multiple educational and systemic contributors to this phenomenon. The findings align with international literature and underscore the need for integrative, clinically relevant and learner-centred approaches to neurological education. Addressing neurophobia is not merely an academic priority; it is essential for preparing future physicians to competently manage the rising burden of neurological disease. By reforming medical curricula and investing in teaching infrastructure, the medical education community can begin to reverse this trend and cultivate greater confidence, interest and competence in neurology.
