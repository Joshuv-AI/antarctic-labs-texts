Disturbance-mediated apparent competition across boreal landscapes: the role of primary productivity
Authors: Branden T Neufeld, Clara Superbie, Cheryl A Johnson, Daniel Fortin, Philip D McLoughlin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextDisturbance-mediated apparent competition (DMAC) links landscape disturbance, alternative prey, and predator dynamics in ways that can reduce caribou recruitment and survival. Although DMAC underpins national recovery planning for boreal caribou (Rangifer tarandus caribou), its mechanisms have been evaluated mainly in southern, more productive forests. Understanding how DMAC operates across the full range of productivity in Canada is necessary for landscape-scale conservation.ObjectivesWe tested the hypothesis that net primary productivity (NPP) governs the strength of DMAC by influencing predator–prey assemblages and shaping patterns of disturbance. Our objective was to determine how these NPP-linked factors influence caribou recruitment and adult female survival at a continental scale.MethodsUsing data on caribou recruitment (40 study areas) and survival (30 study areas) from across Canada, along with maps of NPP, disturbance footprints, estimates of moose density, and species richness, we assessed how NPP influences DMAC’s effects on caribou.ResultsMany of the explanatory variables were strongly correlated. Recruitment and survival declined with increasing NPP, anthropogenic disturbance, predator richness, prey richness, and moose density. Higher NPP increased disturbance, which in turn increased prey and predator richness and decreased caribou recruitment. For survival, NPP increased moose density and ultimately decreased caribou survival.ConclusionsAt a continental scale, DMAC diminishes as a potential limiting factor to caribou as NPP declines. Anthropogenic disturbance is concentrated in high-NPP portions of the landscape. High-NPP regions support greater predator and alternative prey species, increasing predation risk for caribou calves. Recognizing the role of NPP in concentrating anthropogenic disturbances and altering trophic interactions is essential for conservation strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10980-026-02336-9.Keywords: Anthropogenic disturbance, Boreal caribou, Disturbance-mediated apparent competition (DMAC), Net primary productivity (NPP), Recruitment, Species richness

Introduction

Apparent competition (AC) is an indirect food-web interaction mediated by a shared predator between prey species, whereby a numerical increase in one prey serves to enhance that of a generalist predator, leading to increased predation risk to other prey in the system (Holt 1977). In some instances, AC can be as strong or stronger than direct competition (Dangremond et al. 2010). Apparent competition can lead to species exclusion, particularly in the context of biological invasions. For example, the invasive grass Ammophilia arenaria increases seed consumption on native plants like endangered lupin (Lupinus tidestromii) by elevating rodent densities (Dangremond et al. 2010). Invasion of white-tailed deer (Odocoileus virginianus) into the mixed-wood forests of western Canada exacerbates predation risk for Threatened woodland caribou (boreal and mountain populations, Rangifer tarandus caribou; hereafter ‘caribou’) by supporting higher numbers of wolves (Latham et al. 2011b; Darlington et al. 2022).

Biological invasions often reshape species interactions, including predator–prey dynamics. In systems where apparent competition occurs, the introduction or expansion of novel prey species can amplify predation pressure on native species (Castorani & Hovel 2015), making landscape disturbances that facilitate such interactions a serious concern for applied ecologists (Lathem et al. 2011b). Within this context, caribou stand as a significant species of interest. Boreal caribou are listed as Threatened on Schedule 1 of the Canada Species at Risk Act (Environment Canada 2012). The species has been the subject of intense research and applied conservation biology now for more than 30 years (Environment Canada 2012; Serrouya et al. 2019). Caribou face multiple threats, but AC is a key mechanism linking landscape alteration to increased predation pressure. Landscape disturbance can facilitate AC by supporting higher populations of prey species, such as white-tailed deer and moose (Alces alces), which in turn sustain predators that also target caribou (Bergerud 2007). Wolves are the primary predator linked with AC (Seip 1992; Serrouya et al. 2015, 2021; DeMars et al. 2019; Fryxell et al. 2020), but other predators—including cougars (Puma concolor), coyotes (Canis latrans), black bears (Ursus americanus), grizzly bears (Ursus arctos), and wolverine (Gulo gulo) —are known to prey on caribou in various systems (Knopff et al. 2010; Andren et al. 2011; Festa-Bianchet et al. 2011; Frenette et al. 2020; Labadie et al. 2021). In addition to supporting higher densities of alternative prey, anthropogenic disturbance in the form of linear features can increase predator travel efficiency and encounter rates with caribou (James and Stuart-Smith 2000; Dickie et al. 2017).

‘Disturbance-mediated apparent competition’ (DMAC) has become increasingly important in the management and conservation of caribou in Canada (Hervieux et al. 2013; Serrouya et al. 2015; DeMars et al. 2019; Serrouya et al. 2019; 2021). As evidence for DMAC has grown, it has become a central consideration in caribou recovery efforts. The 2012 federal recovery strategy for boreal caribou takes a habitat-focused approach, aiming to reduce disturbance within caribou ranges to mitigate multiple threats, including predation risk associated with DMAC (Environment Canada 2012; Environment and Climate Change Canada 2018). This strategy applies across > 2.4 million km2 of boreal forest from Yukon to the Atlantic coast (Fig. 1). While negative effects of disturbance on caribou populations—particularly calf recruitment (calf survival to 10–12 months of age)—have been extensively studied including at the national scale (e.g., recruitment vs disturbance-footprint relationships [Environment Canada 2011; Johnson et al. 2020]), the specific mechanisms supporting DMAC have yet to be explored outside regional studies (Serrouya et al. 2015, 2019; DeMars et al. 2019). Studying ecological patterns and processes at multiple scales is critical because small-scale patterns are often shaped by larger-scale constraints (Levin 1992), The conditions influencing DMAC are not likely to be ubiquitous throughout caribou range. For example, successional trajectories in northern Boreal Shield and Taiga environments differ markedly from southern mixed-wood Boreal Plain and Shield forests (Hart et al. 2019). Predator communities also vary geographically; western Canada supports grizzly bears, cougars, and wolverine in boreal caribou range, whereas these species are absent or at very low density east of Alberta. Outside some regional explorations (e.g., Fortin et al. 2017; Labadie et al. 2021; Serrouya et al. 2021; Neufeld et al. 2021; Tjaden-McClement et al. 2025) the broader-scale ecological linkages that regulate DMAC remain understudied. While disturbance rates have been examined (Environment Canada 2011; Johnson et al. 2020), little is known of how productivity affects caribou demography via DMAC at macroecological scales (e.g., continental scale). Requisite for DMAC to impact caribou are the interlinked dynamics of habitat disturbance, forest succession leading to early seral stages dominated by early seral vegetation, and the numerical responses of alternative prey and predators, which in turn affect caribou demographics. We propose that these elements are associated with the underlying productivity of the landscape. At the heart of this relationship is net primary productivity (NPP), a fundamental measure of the energy available for consumers in an ecosystem, which plays a pivotal role in shaping these dynamics (Scurlock et al. 1999). The productivity we are attempting to represent here is not the fluctuations caused by terrestrial eutrophication (Serrouya et al. 2021) but instead represents the climatic productivity potential of different regions. Human appropriation of NPP (Haberl et al. 2007) provides the mechanism by which higher landscape productivity is expected to increase anthropogenic disturbance and, in turn, stronger DMAC in caribou systems.

Regional forest productivity, and consequently NPP, can have a dual effect on caribou demographics. On one hand, increased productivity may shorten the duration of young seral stages through accelerated succession, potentially reducing the window of heightened vulnerability to predation for caribou (Fortin et al. 2017). Enhanced foraging conditions, a direct outcome of heightened NPP, may improve caribou demographic outcomes (Turunen et al. 2014; Heard and Zimmerman 2021). On the other hand, increased productivity could exacerbate the pressures of AC; the relationship between vertebrate diversity and NPP in terrestrial systems often follows a positive linear or unimodal pattern, suggesting that as productivity rises, so too does the richness of ungulate species and their predators (Waide et al. 1999). Because increased productivity can support greater ungulate and predator diversity, higher alternative prey richness has the potential to introduce new or amplified predation pressures on caribou, contributing to apparent competition. Furthermore, the broader spectrum of predators exacerbates this challenge, as increased predator assemblages undermine caribou’s traditional avoidance strategies due to their varied hunting tactics (Leblond et al. 2016). Consequently, in environments marked by high NPP, the intensified presence of diverse species has the potential to magnify pressures on caribou. It is this latter pathway that forms the focus of our study.

Beyond its ecological role, NPP also influences patterns of human land use, such as forestry, which in turn shape the availability and quality of caribou habitat. Areas of higher productivity are often targeted for their higher merchantable wood volume, indicating a direct link between human economic activities and NPP (Haberl et al. 2007). Interestingly, while fire disturbances are traditionally considered detrimental to caribou habitat by reducing lichen availability (Schaefer and Pruitt 1991; Gustine et al. 2014; Greuel et al. 2021), their impact on caribou via DMAC is less clear-cut. Some studies suggest that fire disturbances have less influence on caribou demographics than anthropogenic disturbances, particularly in low NPP landscapes (Johnson et al. 2020; Stewart et al. 2020; Neufeld et al. 2021; Superbie et al. 2022).

This study pioneers a continent-wide exploration into the effect of NPP on DMAC and its implications for caribou management. We test the hypothesis that NPP influences DMAC by shaping both natural and human-driven ecosystem dynamics. While higher NPP may benefit caribou through increased forage availability and a faster return to mature seral stage forest, it may also contribute to greater predator and alternative prey diversity, leading to a more complex species assemblage that intensifies interactions within the food web. Additionally, we predict that areas of higher NPP are disproportionately targeted for anthropogenic disturbance, such as forestry, supporting the concept of human appropriation of NPP (Haberl et al. 2007). At a continent-wide scale, we propose that DMAC is influenced by NPP, as key variables—boreal succession, species richness, moose density, and human NPP appropriation—are all linked to productivity. Our study represents one of the few large-scale tests of DMAC predictions, examining how predator–prey richness, moose density, and anthropogenic disturbance respond to NPP and, in turn, influence caribou recruitment and survival. By focusing on predator and prey richness as well as moose density, we aim to shed light on the nuanced dynamics of DMAC and its potential implications for caribou populations.

Methods

Data collection

We compiled data from multiple sources to assess the relationships between NPP, anthropogenic disturbance, predator–prey dynamics, and caribou recruitment and survival across study areas in Canada. We obtained data for 40 boreal caribou study areas, which included recruitment information from calf:cow ratios in late winter (March, Fig. 1). This metric represents the number of calves that survived to 10 months of age relative to the number of adult females (i.e. calf:cow ratio; Bergerud et al. 1984). We also collected adult female survival data (annual female adult survival rate) for 35 of the 40 study areas. Study areas delineations were based on COSEWIC (2014). We obtained caribou recruitment, survival, and study area habitat polygons for Northwest Territories, British Columbia, Saskatchewan, Ontario, and Québec directly from Environment and Climate Change Canada (Supplementary Table A1). These demographic data were originally collected for federal government reporting and monitoring purposes. We assumed methodological comparability across jurisdictions because they followed similar data collection and reporting procedures.

We obtained annual NPP values from the MOD17A3 NASA Earth Observing System MODIS Land Algorithm (Running and Zhao 2015). Cross-validation studies confirmed that MOD17 data accurately estimate NPP for boreal forests (Turner et al. 2003). The dataset provided 1 km resolution raster data maps, with NPP values reported in g C/m2/year, covering the period from 2000 to 2015. We used the 15-year average NPP per pixel to represent the mean NPP value. We calculated a long-term mean NPP for each pixel by averaging values across all available years (Fig. 1). Averaging NPP over 15 years reduces interannual variation and better represents the persistent productivity levels that shape caribou habitat use and predator–prey dynamics.

We quantified anthropogenic and wildfire disturbance as the percentage of each study area classified as disturbed, based on datasets from Environment Canada (2011) and Environment and Climate Change Canada (2017). These datasets separately define anthropogenic disturbance (e.g., industrial activities, roads, and forestry) and wildfire disturbance, ensuring consistency across study areas. To maintain comparability across regions, we used their standardized classifications of anthropogenic disturbance, which include industrial development, forestry, transportation networks, energy infrastructure, urban development, and a 500-m buffer applied to linear features such as roads and seismic lines. We also incorporated updates from additional sources for some populations where more recent data were available (Supplementary Table A1; Fig. 2).

We obtained data on species distributions and density of moose (moose/1000 km2) from published scientific papers, published reports, or provided by provincial/territorial governments. Among the species for which data were collected, moose was the only species for which density was available across a significant number of study areas (nSM = 30), allowing us to include it in our analyses. Most moose density values came from direct contact with provincial and territorial agencies. Despite potential differences in monitoring methods, we are working under the assumption that the data is comparable. However, we acknowledge that survey methods for estimating moose density vary across jurisdictions, potentially contributing to differences in precision and detection probabilities.

To assess predator and alternative prey presence, we compiled species distribution data from provincial sources, scientific literature, and Canada-wide species maps. We designated species as present (1) or absent (0) in each of the caribou study areas based on the obtained distribution data. The predators included wolf, cougar, black bear, grizzly bear, wolverine, and coyote while the alternative prey species included moose, white-tailed deer, mule deer, North American elk, and bison (Bison bison; Supplementary Table A1). We supplemented provincial data and national distribution records for coyote (Hody and Kays 2018), white-tailed deer distribution (Heffelfinger 2011), and bison (COSEWIC 2013; ABMI 2018; Fiera Biological Consulting personal communication Feb. 2018). We mapped Canada-wide species distribution maps for predators (Supplementary Fig. B1) and alternative prey (Supplementary Fig. B2) to illustrate species ranges across study areas.

We used the following predictor variables in analyses of caribou calf recruitment (nR = 40): NPP (g C/m2/year), percent anthropogenic disturbance, percent fire disturbance, predator richness, and prey richness (Supplementary Table C1). We used the same variables for study areas with annual female survival data (nS = 35), and included moose density in a subset of 30 study areas where both caribou survival and moose density data were available (nSM = 30). Due to insufficient study areas where both moose density and caribou recruitment data were available, we did not conduct recruitment analyses using moose density as a predictor.

For caribou demographic data, we applied weighting based on the number of years of data collected. This minimized biases from study areas with lower survey effort while allowing us to incorporate long-term trends where available.

Data analysis

We conducted all statistical analyses using the R software environment (Version 4.0.1). We tested for collinearity among predictor variables using Pearson correlation coefficients. Several variables showed strong correlations (r > 0.7; Supplementary Table C1), raising concerns of multicollinearity in standard regression models (Zuur et al. 2007; Dormann et al. 2013; Akoglu 2018; Schober and Schwarte 2018). To address this, we performed a principal component analysis (PCA) for all variables using the package vegan (Oksanen et al. 2019).

To guide exploratory analyses, we used a broken-stick method using package vegan (Oksanen et al. 2019) to identify PCA axes that explained a meaningful proportion of variance. These PCA axes were then used as predictor variables in beta regression models using the BETAREG package (Cribari-Neto and Zeileis 2010) to evaluate direct relationships between ecological gradients and caribou demographic responses. For each response (recruitment and survival), we fit a single global model including the selected PCA axes.

To test the indirect effects of NPP on caribou recruitment and survival, we conducted structural equation modeling (SEM) using lavaan package (version 0.6.15; Grace 2006). SEM allows for the simultaneous estimation of direct and indirect pathways among variables, providing a mechanistic understanding of apparent competition processes. We specified two a priori SEMs to test whether variation in landscape productivity influences anthropogenic disturbance, prey and predator assemblages, and caribou demography.

For calf recruitment, we specified an a priori SEM in which NPP was modeled as a predictor of anthropogenic disturbance and prey richness, anthropogenic disturbance was modeled as a predictor of prey richness and predator richness, prey richness and anthropogenic disturbance were modeled as predictors of predator richness, and predator richness was included as a predictor of caribou calf recruitment. The hypothesis here is that NPP increases the likelihood of anthropogenic disturbance (especially forestry practices), which in turn promotes higher alternative prey richness (Lathem et al. 2011b) and facilitates predator travel efficiency (Dickie et al. 2017); NPP also facilitates prey richness (Waide et al. 1999), while prey richness facilitates predator richness (Leblond et al. 2016). In our models, prey richness is also used as a proxy for the total ungulate biomass of each system (Keith 1983; Fuller et al. 2003; Kuzyk & Hatter 2014), as the data for density of all prey species is unavailable across our study regions. All of these factors are hypothesized to increase predation risk to caribou calves (Lathem et al. 2011b; Leblond et al. 2016).

For adult female survival, we restricted the dataset to the 30 study areas where moose density data were available. We used this limitation as wolves are the main predators of adult caribou (Bergerud & Elliott 1986) and moose are the main prey of wolves in the context of caribou (Seip 1992). We specified an a priori SEM in which NPP was modeled as a predictor of anthropogenic disturbance, both NPP and anthropogenic disturbance were modeled as predictors of moose density, and moose density was included as a predictor of adult caribou survival. Moose density was modeled as a function of NPP and anthropogenic disturbance to evaluate how broad-scale ecological and landscape variables relate to variation in moose population density. Because data on wolf density were unavailable, we instead used moose density as a proxy for predation pressure, as moose and wolf densities have been shown to be highly correlated (Messier 1994; Kuzyk & Hatter 2014). Therefore, in our model moose density represents the total effects of moose density on caribou survival including its effects on wolf density (unmeasured). In the same way, anthropogenic disturbance’s effect on survival was representative of how disturbance facilitates wolf travel efficiency (Dickie et al. 2017) and thereby represents the total effects of anthropogenic disturbance on caribou survival. By including these relationships in the model, we aim to capture the total downstream effects of both moose density and anthropogenic disturbance on how they affect wolves’ ability to prey on adult caribou.

We evaluated SEM fit using multiple complementary goodness-of-fit indices using maximum likelihood in the lavaan package in R. Model χ2 tests the discrepancy between the observed and model-implied covariance matrices, with non-significant values indicating adequate fit. Because χ2 is sensitive to sample size, we also report the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), which compare the specified model to a null model (values ≥ 0.90 indicate acceptable fit), as well as the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), which assesses lack of fit per degree of freedom (≤ 0.05 close fit; 0.05–0.08 reasonable fit), and the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), representing the standardized difference between observed and predicted correlations (≤ 0.08 indicative of good fit) (Hu & Bentler 1999; Browne & Cudeck 1993).

Results

Recruitment

The first two principal components explained 85% of the variance in caribou calf recruitment (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table D1). The beta regression analysis showed that for PC1, caribou recruitment decreased as anthropogenic disturbance, NPP, and predator and alternative prey increased (Supplementary Table D2). Fire disturbance had a weak factor loading (− 0.242) rendering its interpretation ambiguous (+ / − 0.3; Gentleman et al. 2011). These exploratory analyses indicate that variation in recruitment is consistently affected by gradients of primary productivity, where higher NPP is associated with greater disturbance and richer predator–prey assemblages, which is negatively associated caribou recruitment.

The recruitment SEM supported the specified indirect pathway linking NPP to calf recruitment through anthropogenic disturbance, prey richness, and predator richness (Fig. 4, Table 1). In this model, NPP was associated with variation in anthropogenic disturbance, anthropogenic disturbance was associated with increases in both prey richness and predator richness, prey richness further contributed to variation in predator richness, and predator richness was associated with reduced calf recruitment. Overall model acceptable (χ2 = 9.61, df = 4, p = 0.048; CFI = 0.93; RMSEA = 0.187; SRMR = 0.099), indicating that the specified model captured the major covariance structure among variables. Together, these results are consistent with a pathway in which higher NPP indirectly reduces recruitment by structuring patterns of anthropogenic disturbance and associated changes in predator–prey assemblages, consistent with DMAC.

Survival

We employed PCA to assess the predictors’ relationship with adult survival, using the same methods as for recruitment (Fig. 5, Supplementary Table D3). The broken stick model identified PC1 and PC2 as significant factors (explaining 84% of variance). In the beta regression, PC1 revealed a negative correlation between survival and anthropogenic disturbance, NPP, and predator and alternative prey species richness, irrespective of fire disturbance (Supplementary Table D4). Fire disturbance showed weak loading (− 0.082; Gentleman et al. 2011). PC2 showed no significance in the beta-regression (Supplementary Table D4). As with recruitment, adult survival declined along gradients of increasing NPP and anthropogenic disturbance, suggesting that productivity-associated landscape conditions are linked to lower survival.

PC2 showed no significance in the beta-regression (Supplementary Tables D5 and D6). These models were similar to the earlier survival model, but with moose density closely linked to NPP.

The SEM model for survival included moose density and anthropogenic disturbance (Fig. 4; Table 2). Initial models using predator and prey richness for all 35 study areas did not yield significant relationships between any predictor and survival, suggesting that a key ecological driver was missing. The model based on those 30 study areas showed a positive correlation from NPP on anthropogenic disturbance and moose density, with moose density having a negative effect on adult caribou female survival (Table 2). Anthropogenic disturbance had a negative but non-significant effect on moose density. As described in the methods, we used moose density both for itself and as a proxy for wolf density as the two are shown to be highly correlated (Messier 1994; Kuzyk & Hatter 2014). Model fit was strong (χ2 = 1.44, df = 1, p = 0.23; CFI = 0.99; RMSEA = 0.12; SRMR = 0.036). This model indicates that NPP indirectly influences adult survival by elevating moose densities, which in turn are associated with reduced adult caribou survival.

Discussion

Our SEM results reveal that, at the macroecological scale, NPP was associated with both caribou recruitment and adult survival through different predator–prey pathways. In the recruitment model, higher NPP was associated with increased anthropogenic disturbance and greater predator and alternative prey richness, consistent with DMAC. In contrast, the survival model indicated that NPP influenced adult caribou survival through its association with moose density, independent of disturbance. Together, these results highlight how spatial variation in productivity structures the ecological context of caribou populations, shaping both human land use and predator–prey dynamics across the boreal forest. High NPP areas, indicative of high species richness and ecosystem productivity (Waide et al. 1999), correspond with increased anthropogenic disturbance and higher richness of both predators and alternative prey species. These factors, converging predominantly in western Canada, illustrate the role of human appropriation of NPP (Haberl et al. 2007) in shaping the ecological landscapes that influence caribou populations.

The close link between NPP and anthropogenic disturbance emerged distinctly in our analyses (Tables 1 and 2 and Supplementary Tables C1, D1, D3, and D5; Figs. 3, 4, 5 and 6), demonstrating that disturbance is not randomly distributed across boreal landscapes but is concentrated in high-NPP areas. While it is true that our measurements of highly disturbed populations include the results of the disturbance in the measure of NPP, following the terrestrial eutrophication hypotheses (Serrouya et al. 2021), our areas of low anthropogenic disturbance are unproductive due to historic climatic variables. Study areas such as the Saskatchewan Boreal Shield have been shown to remain unproductive even after disturbance events (Hart et al. 2019; Neufeld et al. 2021). This geographic variation in NPP is shaped by boreal forest composition, with higher productivity in mixed-wood forests of the Boreal Plains and lower NPP in the conifer-dominated Boreal Shield (Gower et al. 1997; Hart et al. 2019). This supports the hypothesis that anthropogenic disturbance is disproportionately concentrated in high-NPP areas as it pertains to the human appropriation of NPP hypothesis (Haberl et al. 2007). Moreover, the concentration of anthropogenic disturbance in high-NPP areas is largely driven by forestry practices, which represent a significant component of human-caused disturbance in boreal forests. Forestry operations specifically target high-productivity forests due to their greater merchantable wood volume (Gholz 1982; Garkoti and Singh 1995; Haberl et al. 2007; Urrutia-Jalabert et al. 2015), further reinforcing the link between NPP and disturbance intensity. Together, this implies that anthropogenic disturbance and NPP are strongly associated, where forestry practices are drawn to climatic conditions that favour high NPP, but also that these practices in turn further increase the productivity within the system.

Our results corroborate the notion that high NPP areas, characterized by increased predator and alternative prey richness, pose significant risks of DMAC for caribou recruitment (Table 1, and Supplementary Figure C1 and Figs. 1 and 4, Supplementary Figures B1 and B2; Waide et al. 1999; Malmstrom 2010). The aggregation of multiple alternative prey species elevates ecosystem prey biomass, as observed in studies of white-tailed deer (Latham et al. 2011b) and total ungulate biomass (Keith 1983; Fuller et al. 2003; Kuzyk & Hatter 2014), which, in turn, is linked to rising wolf densities. Furthermore, the broader spectrum of predators exacerbates the predicament for caribou, undermining the effectiveness of their avoidance strategies in the face of diverse predatory threats (Leblond et al. 2016). Consequently, in environments marked by high NPP and consequently high disturbance, the intensified presence of diverse species magnifies the effects of DMAC, matching our observations of NPP’s detrimental impact on caribou recruitment.

Our findings highlight the relationship between NPP, anthropogenic disturbance, and species distribution, demonstrating how these elements collectively influence caribou demographics. The observed northern limit of white-tailed deer (Heffelfinger 2011) and coyotes (Hody and Kays 2018), alongside corresponding levels of anthropogenic disturbance, underscores the adaptability of these species to human-altered environments (Supplementary Figures B1 and B2). This adaptability is crucial in areas where forest succession favors deciduous species, creating optimal conditions for moose (Rempel et al. 1997; Courbin et al. 2009; Serrouya et al. 2011; Best et al. 2024) and, by extension, increasing evidence of DMAC (Hervieux et al. 2013; Peters et al. 2013; Serrouya et al. 2019). Bergerud (2007) highlighted that climate-driven shifts in vegetation structure promote moose and deer populations, which in turn sustain higher predator densities and intensify apparent competition for caribou. Our findings build on this by showing that, beyond climate effects, high NPP areas also drive this process through their influence on disturbance patterns and prey distributions. Our analysis reveals a strong correlation between moose densities and NPP, where high NPP areas have lower adult caribou survival. Here, moose-density covariates were shown to be closely related to NPP (Supplementary Table C1) and PCA analysis showed that moose-density vectors closely related to both NPP and anthropogenic disturbance (Fig. 6). Across models, higher moose density was consistently associated with lower adult caribou survival (Table 2, Supplementary Tables D5 and D6, Figs. 5 and 6). Because wolves are the primary predators of adult caribou (Bergerud and Elliott 1986), moose density also serves as a proxy for predation pressure through its association with wolf density (Messier 1994; Kuzyk and Hatter 2014). The SEM results indicate that NPP influences survival indirectly through its effects on moose density (Fig. 4, Table 2).

Although anthropogenic disturbance was not modeled as a direct driver of caribou recruitment or survival, our results indicate that disturbance plays an important contextual role within DMAC. It is well documented that anthropogenic disturbance negatively affects caribou (James and Stuart-Smith 2000; Sorensen et al. 2008; Latham et al. 2011a; Leblond et al. 2011; DeMars and Boutin 2018), largely through indirect pathways rather than direct mortality. Industrialization of boreal forests has altered forest composition, increasing the prevalence of deciduous species in many regions due to widespread logging and other land-use changes (Cumming et al. 1994; Danneyrolles et al. 2016). For example, Best et al. (2024) showed that in mixedwood ecosystem subtypes, cut-blocks produced elevated browse for moose and bears. These changes create early-seral habitats that favor alternative prey species, contributing to increased predator densities and reinforcing DMAC dynamics (Rempel et al. 1997; Hervieux et al. 2013; Peters et al. 2013; Serrouya et al. 2019).

In conclusion, our study underscores that forest productivity and anthropogenic disturbances, particularly in high-NPP areas, shape caribou conservation challenges by fostering conditions that enhance predator–prey interactions and apparent competition. While high-NPP regions often support greater biodiversity, they also attract higher levels of human disturbance, altering species assemblages and ecosystem dynamics in ways that can disadvantage sensitive species. These patterns are not unique to caribou; many species at risk experience similar pressures where productive landscapes coincide with human land use. As climate change continues to reshape ecosystems, understanding how NPP influences natural and human-driven processes will be critical for effective conservation planning.
