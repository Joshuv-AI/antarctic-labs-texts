Carboxylation of lyocell fabrics using C-1 lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases (DcLPMO9A) for enhanced affinity to cationic dye
Authors: Maximilian Huemer, Olanrewaju Raji, Sebastian España Orozco, Robert H. Bischof, Doris Ribitsch, Georg M. Guebitz, Emma R. Master
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Regenerated cellulosic fibers, such as lyocell fibers, are fully biodegradable and of 100% bio-based origin. With properties like high dry tenacity, they are widely used in many high-quality fiber applications. Yet, targeted surface functionalization could improve their processing such as dyeing. Reactive dyes are the dominant class for dyeing cellulosic fibers because they exhibit high affinity and form covalent bonds with cellulose. By contrast, cationic dyes show poor affinity to unmodified cellulose and are therefore not inherently suitable for lyocell unless additional anionic binding sites are introduced. To address this, we have investigated enzymatic surface functionalization of lyocell fabrics to increase negative charges. The C1-acting lytic polysaccharide monooxygenase (LPMO) DcLPMO9A from Delitschia confertaspora was employed to functionalize lyocell fabrics to increase cationic dye uptake without substantial loss in fabric yield. The C1 oxidation of lyocell fiber was quantified through selective labeling and fluorescence analysis and directly correlated to cationic dye uptake. Remarkably, rhodamine-6G (R6G) dye uptake increased by up to 75% using less than 1% w/w DcLPMO9A loadings. Further analysis of fiber properties and yield demonstrated that DcLPMO9A treatment did not compromise fiber quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10570-026-07125-w.

Introduction

Lyocell refers to regenerated cellulose fibers obtained via direct dissolution of wood pulp in N-methylmorpholine-N-oxide (NMMO) and subsequent solvent spinning, without prior derivatization. These fibers are derived entirely from natural sources, are fully biodegradable, and possess a series of unique characteristics (Ghazal et al. 2024). Structurally, lyocell fibers differ significantly from cotton and viscose fibers, exhibiting a nearly circular cross-sectional shape and a thin layer with a low degree of crystallinity that binds the fibrils together (Jiang et al. 2020). Fibrillation is a common phenomenon in fibers, particularly in lyocell, due to swelling-induced weakening of intermolecular binding forces and high structural ordering, which reduces fibril entanglement and facilitates their separation (Zhang 1999). While fibrillation can be a disadvantage in certain applications, it is advantageous for creating specific finishes, such as the “peach-skin” effect, which refers to a soft, velvety surface texture (Periyasamy and Khanum 2012). Lyocell fibers also possess high mechanical strength, making them suitable for applications in clothing, non-woven materials, and medical fields (Jiang et al. 2020). A further advantage of lyocell is its environmental sustainability. The production process avoids the use of toxic chemicals and achieves a solvent recovery rate of over 99% for NMMO (Rosenau et al. 2001; Zhang et al. 2018).

Color is a critical factor in enhancing the appeal of fabrics, including lyocell, regardless of their structural or functional properties. Consequently, the industrial production and application of dyes for textile dyeing have grown into a massive global industry (Khattab et al. 2020). Synthetic dyes are widely preferred over natural dyes due to their lower cost, broad spectrum of bright shades, and superior color fastness properties (Mirjalili et al. 2011). However, their toxic nature poses threats to diverse ecosystems and so raise significant environmental concerns (Khattab et al. 2020). Dyes also present challenges in wastewater management, not only due to their inherent chemical stability but also due to the auxiliary chemicals and processes used during dyeing. Therefore, effective treatment is essential to remove these pollutants and neutralize effluents. Additionally, the discharge of untreated wastewater into agricultural fields can clog soil pores, primarily due to suspended solids, chemical precipitates, and salts, which reduce water infiltration and soil productivity (Božič and Kokol 2008).

For cellulosic fibers such as cotton and lyocell, reactive dyes dominate because they form covalent cellulose–dye bonds and deliver excellent wet fastness (Amin and Blackburn 2015). Reactive dyeing typically requires substantial additions of salts to promote dye exhaustion, resulting in saline wastewater containing unfixed and hydrolyzed reactive dye (Negi 2025). Covalent linkages can also hinder color removal during textile recycling (Li et al. 2022). By contrast, cationic dyes associate with cellulose mainly through electrostatic and other non-covalent interactions with anionic sites (Saad and Mahmoud 2025). These interactions are, in principle, more reversible, which may facilitate stripping in fiber-to-fiber recycling processes. Whereas cationic dyes exhibit high vibrancy and brilliance, their adherence to cellulosic fibers is low given the few anionic sites on native cellulose. This limitation can be mitigated by oxidation to introduce carboxylate groups on the fiber surface (Bouatay et al. 2014). Bouatay et al. (2016) further demonstrated that surface-modifying agents can significantly enhance the fixation efficiency of cationic dyes on fibers, reduce dye losses in wastewater, and lower the amount of dyes required to dye cotton and jute fibers. However, these methods require additional chemical treatments and consume significant energy, highlighting the need for more sustainable alternatives.

An alternative approach to enhance affinity of lyocell fabrics to cationic dyes presented here involves enzymatic carboxylation. Lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases (LPMOs) have gained significant attention in recent years due to their ability to deconstruct biomass, particularly in the context of biofuel production (Johansen 2016; Marjamaa and Kruus 2018). More recent studies have focused on their potential to introduce carboxylate groups as reactive anchor groups on cellulose surfaces (Mathieu et al. 2023; Solhi et al. 2022). LPMOs are small copper containing metalloenzymes that use a dioxygen co-substrate (O2 or H2O2) and an external reductant (e.g., ascorbic acid) to catalyze the oxidative cleavage of polysaccharides, leaving an oxidized group at the C1- and/or C4- positions (Eijsink et al. 2019; Forsberg et al. 2019; Walton and Davies 2016). Based on their regioselectivity, LPMOs are categorized into three groups: C1-oxidizing, C4-oxidizing, and C1/C4-oxidizing enzymes (Eijsink et al. 2019). Furthermore, LPMOs are classified by their sequence and structure into auxiliary activity (AA) families 9–11 and 13–17 of the carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZy) database (Drula et al. 2022; Levasseur et al. 2013). Many LPMOs also exhibit a multi-modular architecture, including non-catalytic carbohydrate-binding modules, which enhance binding to cellulose and thereby improve the efficiency of oxidative cleavage (CBMs) (Forsberg et al. 2019; Lenfant et al. 2017).

LPMO from Delitschia confertaspora (DcLPMO9A), a member of the AA9 family—the largest family of fungal LPMOs (Consortium 2018), exhibits strict C1-oxidizing activity and was previously reported to introduce carboxylate groups into cellulosic materials while minimizing loss in cellulose yield (Mathieu et al. 2023). In this study, we aimed to evaluate the potential of DcLPMO9A to selectively introduce C1-carboxylate groups onto lyocell fiber surfaces, a substrate that has remained largely unexplored in the context of LPMO applications. We hypothesized that this enzymatic modification would increase the density of C1-carboxylate groups on the fiber surface, thereby enhancing the absorption of the cationic dye R6G—used here for its sensitivity and spectrophotometric convenience—without significant loss of fabric integrity. Materials and methods

Materials

The regenerated cellulosic fabrics used in this study were supplied by Lenzing AG (Lenzing, Austria). These were 100% lyocell woven plain weave fabric, woven from ring-spun Nm 50/1 yarns made from 1.3 dtex lyocell staple fibers. The fabric had a mass per unit area of 125 g/m2. Prior to testing, the fabric was singed and desized. All chemicals were obtained in the highest available purity grade or HPLC grade. Ascorbic acid, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), hexane, hydrochloric acid, lactobionic acid, methanol, N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP), R6G, sodium acetate, sodium chloride, and sodium tetraborate decahydrate were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich/Merck KGaA (Schnelldorf, Germany). N,N-dimethylacetamide (DMAc) was provided by Honeywell International Inc. (Charlotte, USA), while N-(1-naphthyl)ethylenediamine dihydrochloride (NED) and triethylamine (TEA) were obtained from VWR International (Radnor, USA). N,N´-diisopropylcarbodiimide (DIC), 2-hydroxypyridine-N-oxide (HOPO), sodium borohydride (NaBH4), and (trimethylsilyl)diazomethane (TMS-DAM) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (Waltham, USA).

Enzyme production and purification

DcLPMO9A was recombinantly produced according to the method described by Raji et al. (2023); DcLPMO9A was then purified from cultivation supernatant using a two-step protocol previously described by Forsberg et al. (2014). Briefly, the cultivation supernatant was buffer-exchanged to 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.8) and concentrated 56 times from 560 to 10 mL using the Pellicon® 3 Cassette with Biomax® Membrane (5 kDa cutoff) (Merck Millipore, Billerica, USA). Anion exchange chromatography was then performed using an UNO Q-6 6 mL column (Bio-Rad Inc., Hercules, USA) equilibrated with 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.8) (eluent A). The protein was eluted with a 25-column volume gradient to 100% eluent B (20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.8), 500 mM NaCl) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Protein-containing fractions were analyzed by SDS-PAGE, then buffer-exchanged into 100 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.0) and concentrated 6 times from 48 to 8 mL using the Pellicon® 3 Cassette (5 kDa cutoff). Size exclusion chromatography was performed using a HiPrep™ 26/60 Sephacryl™ S-400 HR column (320 mL column volume) (Cytiva, Marlborough, USA). The column was equilibrated with 50 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.0) and the sample was eluted with 2.5-coloumn volumes. Protein-containing fractions were analyzed by SDS-PAGE, and protein concentration was determined using both the Bradford assay and gel densitometry using ImageJ software.

DcLPMO9A treatment of lyocell fabric and fiber properties analysis

The enzyme reactions were performed at 50 °C and shaking (1250 rpm) for 24 h in an Eppendorf Thermomixer (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). The reaction mixtures contained 50 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.0), 1 mM reductant (ascorbic acid dissolved in ultrapure water), and varying concentrations of DcLPMO9A ranging between 0 to 1% w/w of substrate (equivalent to 0 to 42.3 nmol, Mw = 23.64 kDa). Unless stated otherwise, the reactions contained 100 ± 0.5 mg of lyocell fabric. After the 24 h incubation, the lyocell fabric was separated from the supernatant, rinsed with ultrapure water, and air-dried at room temperature for 48h before weighing to determine potential mass loss from the fabric. All reactions were conducted in triplicate.

To evaluate the impact of enzyme treatment on the mechanical properties of lyocell fiber, standardized fiber tests were performed to determine tenacity and elongation under dry and wet conditions. Prior to testing, fibers were conditioned for 24 h at a relative humidity of 65 ± 5% and a temperature of 20 ± 2 °C to ensure consistent environmental conditions. Mechanical properties were measured in accordance with ISO 1973 and ISO 5079 standards, using Vibroskop and Vibrodyn testing machines (Lenzing AG, Lenzing, Austria). Each result was determined as the average of 20 measurements. The tests included tenacity and elongation, evaluated under both dry and wet conditions.

Analysis of carboxy groups via C1-fluorescence labeling

C1-oxidized content in enzymatically treated lyocell fabric was quantified according to Budischowsky et al. (2022) and Budischowsky et al. (2024), via selective amine labeling of C1 moieties. Briefly, 12 mg of washed then air-dried lyocell fabric (treated with 0 to 1% w/w DcLPMO9A) was suspended in 3 mL of 1M sodium borate buffer (pH 8.4) in a 10 mL tube. Sodium borohydride (NaBH4, 85 mg) was added and the mixture was incubated in a shaking bath at 40 °C for 2 h. The treated fabric was then thoroughly washed on a Büchner funnel equipped with blue-ribbon filter paper (Lab Logistics Group GmbH, Meckenheim, Germany) using vacuum filtration. The washing sequence included water, aqueous HCl (0.1M), water, ethanol, and DMAc. In the subsequent step, methylation was performed following the protocol of Tot et al. (2009). The DMAc-wet pulp samples were transferred into 10 mL tubes and suspended in a mixture of DMAc (1.3 mL) and methanol (0.3 mL). TMS-DAM (0.6M in hexane, 16 µL) was added to the tubes, which were then sealed and shaken at 40 °C for 2 h. Prior to NED-labeling, the fabric was thoroughly washed with NMP (3 × 2 mL).

Following NaBH4 reduction and TMS-DAM methylation, the labeling of carboxylate groups was carried out using the method described by Budischowsky et al. (2022). For each sample (12 mg), the NED-labeling solution consisted of HOPO (30.9 mg), NED (16.5 mg), and TEA (1.92 μL) in NMP (0.4 mL). The solution was added to the fabric sample in a 10 mL tube, followed by the addition of DIC (18.0 μL). The tubes were then sealed and incubated with shaking at 40 °C for 7 days.

The excess labeling solution was removed by washing the fabric sample with DMAc (3 × 2 mL). The fabric was then transferred into a fresh HPLC vial and dissolved in a solution of 5% DMSO and 9% w/v DMAc/LiCl (1 mL), following the protocol of Melikhov et al. (2025). After 24 h, the fabric was fully dissolved. The solution was then diluted 1:10 in DMAc and prepared for spectrophotometric analysis. Triplicate 200 µL samples of dissolved NED-labeled lyocell fabric were analyzed using an Infinite® 200 Pro microplate reader (Tecan Group AG, Männedorf, Switzerland) in a black 96-well plate (Greiner Bio-One, Kremsmünster, Austria) with excitation wavelength (λₑₓ) at 350 nm and emission wavelength (λₑₘ) at 420 nm. Data analysis was performed using the Tecan i-control software package (2.0.10.0).

Dye uptake analysis

For the dye uptake assay, the lyocell fabric was transferred to a 25 mL flask and treated with 15 mL of boiling 20 g/L sodium chloride R6G dye solution. The top of the flask was wrapped with aluminum foil; to prevent overboiling of the solution, small holes were poked through the foil cover using a 25-gauge needle. The reactions continued to boil with stirring (100 rpm) for 1 h and any moisture loss was replenished using the 20 g/L sodium chloride solution. After 1 h, the fabric was removed, and excess dye was squeezed out of the fabric before it was transferred to 500 mL of boiling 1% (w/v) Alcojet® (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) detergent solution (in ultrapure water) and washed for 2 min. Following the detergent wash, the fabric was rinsed extensively using ultrapure water, then gently squeezed and air-dried for 48h before weighing.

Dye uptake was measured using a spectrophotometer (Nix Spectro L, Nix Sensor Ltd., Canada), which features a 5 mm aperture for measuring color density and sRGB values. Calibration was performed using a series of R6G dye solutions with concentrations ranging from 12.5–800 µL/mL R6G-dye (supplementary information Fig. S1, Eq. S1.). Furthermore, images of the dyed fabrics were captured to provide a visual representation of the dye uptake as perceived by the human eye.

Sample preparation for microscopy of R6G-dyed fabrics

A total of 10 mg of R6G-dyed fibers were excised from the treated fabric, placed upright in a capsule, which was then filled with an embedding solution. The embedding solution consisted of 1 mL of Technovit®, 10 g of Hardener 1, and 10 µL of Hardener 2 (Mitsui Chemicals, Tokyo, Japan). After 2 h, the solution was fully cured. Following 24 h, the polymer block was tapered and a 20 µm cross-section was prepared from the tapered block. The cross-section was embedded in a drop of glycerin on a microscope slide and covered with a coverslip. Microscopy was performed at 800 × magnification for cross-sections and 200 × magnification for longitudinal sections. The microscope exposure time was maintained at a constant 500 ms.

Results and discussion

Verifying the activity of DcLPMO9A on lyocell fabric

Mathieu et al. (2023) characterized DcLPMO9A as a strict C1-oxidizing LPMO and reported its activity on various cellulosic substrates, though not specifically on cellulose II fibers. High-performance anion-exchange chromatography coupled with pulsed amperometric detection (HPAEC-PAD) was used to analyze the soluble products generated at varying DcLPMO9A loadings (supplementary information Method S1, Fig. S2). The release of soluble C1-oxidized products increased with increasing enzyme concentration, confirming that LPMOs lacking a CBM can act on cellulose II, a property previously observed only for CBM-containing LPMOs (Grellier et al. 2024; Støpamo et al. 2024b). However, the overall yield of soluble products was lower than that reported by Mathieu et al. (2023) for microcrystalline cellulose (Avicel). This difference could be attributed to the higher surface-to-volume ratio of Avicel particles compared to cellulose fibers, which is expected to provide greater enzyme accessibility and facilitate product release.

While several methods exist for determining the carboxy group content in cellulosic materials, particularly for uronic acid groups at the C6 position (Bohrn et al. 2006), the quantification of oxidized C1-terminal aldonic acid moieties remains challenging due to their equilibrium with the 1,5-lactone form (δ-lactone) in aqueous solution. This unique characteristic of C1-oxidized cellulose polymers complicates their analysis. To address this challenge, the NED-labeling method developed by Budischowsky et al. (2024) was adapted for the lyocell fabric, enabling reliable detection of oxidized C1 groups. Briefly, we followed the published workflow to suppress non-C1 signals (i.e., sodium borohydride reduction of carbonyls (C2, C3, C6), methylation of uronic acids (C6) with TMS–DAM, and subsequent NED coupling at C1) and diverged only at the detection step: whereas Budischowsky et al. (2024) quantified NED-labeled samples by SEC/MALS, herein the labeled fabric was dissolved (DMAc/LiCl with a 5% DMSO pretreatment) and NED fluorescence was quantified spectrophotometrically. The NED labeled fabric permitted quantitative assessment of LPMO oxidized materials, which was expected to be in the µmol/g range (Budischowsky et al. 2024; Sulaeva et al. 2024). Increasing DcLPMO9A loadings (from 0.1 to 1% w/w) led to higher C1-carboxylate content in NED-labeled lyocell fabrics (Fig. 2). The highest net increase in C1-COOH content (16.2 µmol/g) was achieved at 1% w/w DcLPMO9A (supplementary information Fig. S3, Eq. S2), corresponding to a 78% increase over the untreated lyocell fabric (20.8 µmol/g). The increase was monotonic, with slight attenuation at the highest loadings, potentially reflecting limited in situ H2O2 production. Herein, the fiber oxidation with DcLPMO9A was performed under the “monooxygenase” conditions used to characterize the enzyme (Mathieu et al. 2023). It is conceivable that H2O2-driven fiber oxidation could further increase the C1-carboxylate content in lyocell fabrics while reducing enzyme and reductant requirements as well as reaction times (Solhi et al. 2022).Fig. 2NED-labeling of DcLPMO9A-treated lyocell fabrics. Impact of DcLPMO9A treatment on fabric yield and mechanical properties

Although LPMO-catalyzed oxidation typically achieves lower oxidation levels compared to non-enzymatic processes like TEMPO oxidation, Marjamaa et al. (2025) demonstrated that LPMOs offer precise control, which can minimize negative impacts of oxidation on the mechanical properties of treated fiber. Consistent with this prediction, DcLPMO9A treatment of lyocell fabric did not lead to detectable loss in fabric yield (Fig. 3). Instead, a slight increase in fabric weight was noted, which could be attributed to the retention of buffer salts, enzyme or water; however, the differences were not statistically significant (supplementary information Method S2, Table S1). Besides retaining high fiber yield, enzymatic oxidation of lyocell fiber surfaces should not reduce fiber tenacity or elongation. Fiber tenacity is a measure of specific strength, defined as the force required to break a fiber (measured in cN) divided by its linear density (tex) (Elmogahzy and Farag 2018). The tex represents the mass (in grams) of 1000 m of fiber, with lyocell having a typical value of approximately 0.14 tex. Elongation, or breaking elongation, is the percentage increase in fiber length at the breaking point, reflecting the fiber’s stretchability (Elmogahzy and Farag 2018). Both tenacity and elongation can be measured in dry and wet states, with values varying significantly depending on the fiber’s origin (Jiang et al. 2020). For lyocell, typical ranges are 11–16% for elongation and 35–46 cN/tex for tenacity, depending on the production scale and the quality of pulp used (Jiang et al. 2020). Lyocell fabric treated with 1% w/w DcLPMO9A retained a dry tenacity of 34.5 ± 3.1 cN/tex, which falls within the expected range for this material (Jiang et al. 2020). No significant difference was observed compared to the untreated reference, which exhibited a tenacity of 35.9 ± 2.8 cN/tex. Similarly, mechanical resistance with SamLPMO10C was tested on cellulose films by Valls et al. (2019). Although the degree of polymerization decreased slightly due to enzymatic oxidation, the dry zero-span index was not significantly affected, indicating that the intrinsic fiber tenacity was retained after LPMO treatment, consistent with our observations. Furthermore, no significant change was observed in wet tenacity.

The impact of DcLPMO9A treatment on the elongation of dry fibers was minimal, with the untreated and treated fibers exhibiting an elongation of 12.3 ± 2.6% and 12.0 ± 2.0%, respectively. In summary, the LPMO assay conditions and DcLPMO9A concentration did not significantly affect the fiber properties, and the overall characteristics of lyocell fibers were retained, ensuring their suitability for subsequent processing steps such as spinning.

Impact of DcLPMO9A treatment on dye uptake by lyocell fabrics

Having demonstrated the ability of DcLPMO9A to oxidize lyocell fabrics without loss of fabric yield and strength, the impact of the oxidation on the uptake of 0.5% w/w R6G dye was investigated using up to 1% w/w of enzyme. R6G dye uptake increased with enzyme loading. At 0.75% w/w enzyme treatment, the dye uptake increased from 1.86 to 3.04 nmol, representing a 63% increase compared to untreated lyocell fabric. The highest increase in R6G uptake appeared at the highest enzyme concentration of 1% w/w, where the dye uptake increased by 75% (Fig. 5a). These gains closely track the increases in C1-carboxylate content (66 and 78%), indicating that higher enzyme loadings introduce more C1-COOH groups and thereby increase the number of dye-binding sites. The improvements in dye uptake were visible by eye, where a pink tint of the fabric was already noticeable at 0.5% w/w DcLPMO9A, suggesting the potential to use different enzyme doses to tune the dyeability of lyocell fabric (Fig. 5b). To support these findings, light microscopy images of the dyed fabric were captured, showing both longitudinal and cross-sectional views of the fibers (Fig. 5c). The top row displays samples treated with 0% w/w DcLPMO9A and 0.5% w/w R6G dye, while the bottom row shows samples treated with the same dye concentration but with 0.75% w/w DcLPMO9A. The microscopy images validate the results presented in Fig. 5a and b, demonstrating significantly enhanced dye uptake after DcLPMO9A oxidation. The cross-sectional images reveal uniform dyeing throughout the fiber, with no evidence of surface-only “ring dyeing”. This suggests that the enzyme facilitates deeper penetration into the fiber or enhances the overall affinity of the lyocell fiber for the R6G dye, consistent with the findings of Abu-Rous et al. (2007) regarding the porosity and dyeing behavior of lyocell fibers.

In the bottom-left image of Fig. 5c, some brighter spots are visible, which can be attributed to small nicks in the fiber caused by processing steps or localized enhanced oxidation by the LPMO. This phenomenon is characteristic of CBM-containing LPMOs, where the carbohydrate-binding module (CBM) immobilizes the enzyme on the fiber surface, increasing the likelihood of repeated oxidation along the same cellulose chain due to the proximity of a previous cut (Courtade et al. 2018; Raji et al. 2023). This localized activity arises from the proximity of a previous cut, as the CBM retains the enzyme in place for a prolonged period. In contrast, LPMOs lacking a CBM, like DcLPMO9A, exhibit greater mobility, resulting in more dispersed oxidation across the cellulose surface (Støpamo et al. 2024a). For uniform dyeing applications, a uniformly oxidized fiber surface is preferred, as it minimizes the release of soluble substrates and ensures consistent dye uptake across the fabric.

The effect of dye loading on R6G dye uptake

Using 0.75% w/w of DcLPMO9A to treat the lyocell fabric, the impact of dye loading was then investigated using 0.25 to 5% w/w R6G, which is within the standard range of reactive dye concentrations (1–3% w/w) used for lyocell fabrics (Taylor and Briggs 2004). At 0.25% w/w R6G, the DcLPMO9A treatment enhanced dye uptake by 42% (Fig. 6). At 0.5% w/w R6G dye, the improvement in dye uptake by DcLPMO9A-treated fabric increased to 60%, which is higher than dye uptake by untreated lyocell fabric using 1% R6G. This indicates that treating lyocell with 0.75% w/w LPMO can reduce the amount of dye needed by 50% to achieve the same or even better dye uptake. The images shown in Fig. 6b underscore this result, showing a visibly darker pink fabric when using 0.5% w/w R6G after DcLPMO9A treatment, compared to using 1% w/w R6G on untreated fabric. Further increasing the R6G concentration above 0.5% w/w had diminishing impacts on relative dye uptake by the DcLPMO9A-treated fabric compared to untreated fabric (Fig. 6a). Nevertheless, the highest improvement in absolute terms was achieved at 5% w/w R6G, where the dye uptake by DcLPMO9A-treated fabric increased by 2.29 nmol relative to the untreated fabric (Fig. 6a); a difference that was visible by eye (Fig. 6b). To quantify color intensity, the Kubelka–Munk equation at the R6G reflectance minimum (520 nm) was applied. Plotting K/S against R6G-dye yielded a linear relationship between both reference and DcLPMO9A-treated fabrics (supplementary information Fig. S4, Eq. S3, Table S2), indicating proportionality of color depth to uptake and consistency with a uniform fiber R6G distribution rather than surface accumulation. Kubelka–Munk color strength increased monotonically with dye dosage in both series, with DcLPMO9A-treated samples exceeding the reference at every dosage and showing the largest relative gains at lower dye levels (0.5–1% w/w R6G). Conclusion

The C1-labeling experiment demonstrated a clear increase in carboxylation with higher enzyme loadings, which directly correlated with improved dye uptake performance in treated lyocell fabric. Notably, the treated fabric exhibited enhanced dye uptake without significant dry weight losses or adverse effects on fiber properties. The higher dye uptake using 0.5% w/w R6G dye after pretreating lyocell with DcLPMO9A compared to using 1% w/w R6G dye without enzyme pretreatment, highlights the potential benefit of incorporating an LPMO process step into existing lyocell dyeing workflows, particularly for dyeing processes that are currently underutilized. Besides lowering initial dye loading, the enzyme pretreatment could minimize the amount of dye that must be recovered from wastewater, a process that is both costly and resource intensive.

To build on these findings, future studies should investigate other dye classes to assess the broader applicability of LPMO treatment and optimize key parameters such as temperature, dyeing time, and dye concentration. Overcoming challenges in enzyme production, particularly regarding cost and large-scale availability, will be critical for scaling up applications. The incorporation of H2O2-driven fiber oxidation offers a promising alternative by reducing enzyme and reductant consumption, while low concentrations of hydrogen peroxide can significantly enhance LPMO reaction rates, optimizing treatment times and ensuring compatibility with existing processes. Furthermore, developing strategies for enzyme recycling and verifying the uniformity of LPMO-mediated carboxylation on lyocell surfaces will be important for successful industrial implementation.
