Flavin-dependent monooxygenases as versatile biocatalysts in biomanufacturing: mechanisms, engineering, and applications
Authors: Yong Li, Ling Zhao, Xin Pu, Biao Geng, Wei Liu, Jie Gao, Xiaowei Peng, Yejun Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFlavin-dependent monooxygenases (FMOs) are versatile oxidative biocatalysts that catalyze a wide array of oxygenation reactions, such as hydroxylation, epoxidation, Baeyer–Villiger oxidation, and halogenation. These enzymes utilize flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) or flavin mononucleotide (FMN) as cofactors to mediate selective incorporation of oxygen into diverse substrates. Owing to their remarkable chemo-, regio-, and stereoselectivity, FMOs have attracted increasing attention as powerful tools for biomanufacturing. Recent advances in enzyme engineering, structural biology, and computational design have expanded the catalytic diversity of FMOs and enabled their integration into biocatalysis frameworks. Moreover, developments in cofactor regeneration, directed evolution, and cell-free biotransformation have improved FMOs’ catalytic efficiency and scalability. Despite these advances, challenges such as limited thermostability, oxygen transfer efficiency, and substrate scope remain obstacles for industrial applications of FMOs. This review summarizes the structural characteristics, catalytic mechanisms, and engineering strategies of FMOs, highlights recent progress in their integration into biocatalysis platforms, and discusses current limitations and possible solutions. Insights into improving FMO catalytic performance and expanding their potential as next-generation biocatalysts for biosynthesis will be provided.Keywords: Flavin-dependent monooxygenase, Biocatalysis, Natural products, Biosynthesis, Cofactor regeneration

Introduction

Flavin-dependent enzymes are widely distributed across plants, animals, and microorganisms. As essential components of oxidoreductases, they play a central role in maintaining the redox balance of life processes [1]. Derived from vitamin B2, flavins function as cofactors in the forms of flavin mononucleotide (FMN) and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), endowing associated enzymatic reactions with versatile redox capabilities [2]. These enzymes are capable of catalyzing a broad spectrum of reactions, including dehydrogenation, oxidation, monooxygenation, halogenation, and so on. Among them, flavin-dependent monooxygenases (FMOs), as key members of the flavin enzyme family, participate deeply in essential biochemical processes such as biosynthesis, metabolism, and detoxification within living organisms [3]. In industrial biocatalysis, FMOs exhibit remarkable potential for catalyzing hydroxylation, Baeyer–Villiger oxidation, sulfoxidation, epoxidation, and halogenation reactions [4], demonstrating broad application prospects in biomanufacturing, drug metabolism, and environmental remediation [5–11].

According to the enzyme classification system of the nomenclature committee of the International Union of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology (http://www.chem.qmul.ac.uk/iubmb/enzyme/), FMOs belong to subclasses 1.13 and 1.14 of oxidoreductases and represent the second most important Phase I metabolic enzymes after cytochrome P450s. Since the first purification of lactate 2-monooxygenase in the mid-twentieth century [12], more than 130 FMOs have been identified and characterized. FMOs have been classified into eight groups (A–H) [4], and subsequent studies revealed a high degree of conservation within the FMO family [13]. The catalytic function of FMOs involving four key steps: flavin reduction, molecular oxygen activation, substrate oxidation, and flavin regeneration. During the catalytic process of most FMOs, FMN or FAD is reduced to FMNH2 or FADH2 by flavin reductases using NAD(P)H as the electron donor [4]. The reduced flavin then reacts with oxygen to form a stable C4a-(hydro) peroxyflavin intermediate [14, 15]. Upon substrate binding to the monooxygenase active site, one oxygen atom from the intermediate is inserted into the substrate, while the other forms a molecule of water [16]. After product release, the oxidized flavin is regenerated and re-enters the catalytic cycle [17, 18]. This catalytic mechanism forms the basis of the essential functions of FMOs in natural product biosynthesis, xenobiotic metabolism, and the degradation of environmental pollutants. For instance, FMOs participate in the biosynthetic pathways of antibiotics [19] and plant hormones [20] through oxidative transformations. In addition, they play key roles in detoxification [21] and bioremediation [22] by converting toxic compounds into forms that can be excreted or further degraded.

With the expansion of industrial-scale applications, the limitations of FMOs have become increasingly evident, including issues related to catalytic efficiency, substrate specificity, stability, and cost-effectiveness. These factors significantly constrain their broader application in the synthesis of pharmaceuticals and natural products. This review summarizes recent advances in FMOs, analyzes the challenges associated with their application in the biosynthesis of selected drugs and natural products, and proposes potential strategies to overcome current limitations. Future development trends and industrial prospects are also discussed, with the goal of providing a conceptual framework to support the broader and more effective deployment of FMOs in biocatalysis.

Biological characteristics of flavin-dependent monooxygenases

Protein structure and catalytic mechanism of flavin-dependent monooxygenases

Single-component FMOs generally exist as monomers with a molecular weight of approximately 60 kDa, consisting of 532–558 amino acid residues (Fig. 1A). Crystallographic analyses have revealed that FMOs contain two structural domains. The larger domain harbors a highly conserved FAD-binding motif (FXGXXXHXXXH), where aromatic residues form π–π stacking interactions with the isoalloxazine ring of FAD, ensuring cofactor stabilization and positioning. The smaller domain binds the NAD(P)H cofactor, in which the nicotinamide moiety establishes a hydrogen-bonding network with the FAD isoalloxazine ring, facilitating efficient electron transfer [23, 24]. Notably, the two domains form a characteristic dinucleotide-binding fold that accommodates both FAD and NAD(P)H, a spatial configuration critical for maintaining enzymatic activity. In two-component FMOs (Fig. 1B), FAD serves as a diffusible flavin cofactor bound to a flavin reductase, where it is directly reduced by NAD(P)H without requiring substrate binding. Once reduced, FADH2 is released and transferred to the monooxygenase component, where subsequent catalytic steps resemble the oxidative half-reaction of single-component FMOs, in which FADH2 is used to hydroxylate the substrate [25].

B The two-component flavin-dependent monooxygenase from Escherichia coli: EcHpaB (top, usually exists as a dimer or tetramer) and EcHpaC (bottom) (PDB ID: 9BKE) [25]

The catalytic cycle of most FMOs begins with the reduction of cofactors FMN or FAD. Within the reductase domain or a separate flavin reductase, NAD(P)H donates electrons to reduce FAD or FMN into FADH2 or FMNH2 [4]. The reduced flavin then reacts with molecular oxygen to generate the key reaction intermediate C4a-(hydro) peroxyflavin [14, 15]. Upon substrate binding at the monooxygenase active site, the intermediate inserts one oxygen atom into the substrate to produce a hydroxylated product, while the other oxygen atom forms a molecule of water [16]. After catalysis, the oxidized flavin dissociates from the enzyme and returns to the reductase system to initiate another catalytic cycle (Fig. 2) [17, 18].

General catalytic process of flavin-dependent monooxygenases. S: substrate

FMOs comprise a diverse group of enzymes that exhibit distinct structural and functional properties. The first comprehensive classification of FMOs was proposed in 2006 [4]. Based on protein composition, FMOs can be divided into single-component and two-component systems [26, 27]. Structurally and functionally, they are categorized into eight major groups (A–H). Group A members primarily catalyze aromatic hydroxylation reactions, particularly regioselective ortho- or para-hydroxylation of phenolic substrates [3]. These enzymes use NAD(P)H as the electron donor and possess a Rossmann-like three-layer ββα sandwich domain for FAD binding (CATH code 3.50.50.60), as well as a β-rich substrate-binding domain (CATH code 3.30.9.10) [28]. Group B includes Baeyer–Villiger monooxygenases and heteroatom oxygenases. Similar to Group A, they contain a Rossmann-like ββα fold for FAD binding but lack a distinct substrate-binding domain. Upon FAD reduction, substrate binding occurs, and NAD(P)H remains associated during substrate oxidation [29]. Group C FMOs are TIM-barrel proteins sharing a luciferase-like TIM-barrel fold that binds FMNH2 [30], and catalyze various oxidative transformations. Group D FMOs possess an acyl-CoA dehydrogenase-like fold and catalyze diverse oxidation reactions using FMNH2 or FADH2 supplied by NAD(P)H-dependent flavin reductases [30]. Group E FMOs are epoxidases with a PHBH (GR-2) fold and obtain reduced FAD from NADH-dependent flavin reductases [31]. Group F FMOs consists mainly of halogenases acting on substrates such as anilines, indoles, azoles, and pyrroles [32, 33]. These FMOs perform regioselective halogenation using molecular oxygen and halide ions [34]. Like Groups A and E, they possess a ββα sandwich fold but differ in their C-terminal sequences [13]. Group G includes FMOs with a monoamine oxidase-like fold that employ amino acid substrates as electron donors. Group H FMOs contain FMN as a cofactor and exhibit a TIM-barrel fold; they catalyze oxidative deamination and oxidative decarboxylation reactions, reducing their flavin cofactors via substrate oxidation [3].

Representative reaction catalyzed by flavin-dependent monooxygenases

FMOs catalyze a variety of oxidative transformations, including hydroxylation, halogenation, epoxidation, heteroatom oxidation, and dehalogenation (Fig. 3) [35–38]. These reactions generally proceed through a conserved flavin-mediated mechanism in which the reduced flavin reacts with molecular oxygen to form the C4a-(hydro)peroxyflavin intermediate. This highly reactive species subsequently transfers an oxygen atom to the substrate, enabling diverse oxidative chemistries with high regio- and stereoselectivity.

Representative types of reactions catalyzed by flavin-dependent monooxygenases. A Hydroxylation reactions. B Halogenation reactions. C Epoxidation reactions. D Heteroatom oxidation and bioluminescent reactions

Hydroxylation is one of the most extensively studied FMO-catalyzed reactions. FMOs can regioselectively introduce hydroxyl groups into specific substrate positions, thereby increasing polarity and facilitating downstream metabolism. For example, phenol 2-hydroxylase catalyzes the ortho-hydroxylation of phenol to catechol, a modification known to enhance water solubility and renal excretion of xenobiotics [39]. Similarly, an FMO from Escherichia coli hydroxylates 4-hydroxyphenylacetic acid at the C3 position to form 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetic acid, an antioxidant metabolite and an important precursor in dopamine and plant phenolic acid biosynthesis [40]. These transformations highlight the importance of FMOs in drug metabolism and natural product modification.

FMOs also participate in selective halogenation chemistry through flavin-dependent oxygen activation. In tryptophan-derived pathways, FMO systems enable site-specific incorporation of halogens such as chlorine or iodine at the C6 position, yielding halogenated tryptophans with enhanced lipophilicity and improved pharmacokinetic properties [41]. Mechanistically, recent studies suggest that in some systems flavin reductases first generate hypohalous acids via the FADH2-O2 reaction, which then act as the true halogenating species rather than direct electrophilic substitution by the enzyme itself [42]. In contrast to halogenation, certain FMOs catalyze oxidative dehalogenation by promoting cleavage of carbon-halogen bonds, a reaction with significant implications for environmental pollutant degradation and detoxification processes [43].

Epoxidation represents another important FMO-mediated transformation. Through the same peroxyflavin intermediate, FMOs catalyze regio- and stereoselective oxygen insertion into carbon–carbon double bonds. A representative example is styrene monooxygenase, which employs an FAD cofactor to convert styrene into (S)-styrene oxide with high enantioselectivity [44]. Such biocatalytic epoxidations are valuable for the synthesis of chiral intermediates and for modulating the biological activity and toxicity of pharmaceutical compounds.

In addition to carbon oxidation, FMOs efficiently oxidize heteroatoms such as sulfur and nitrogen, typically forming sulfoxides, sulfones, or N-oxides via nucleophilic attack of the heteroatom on the C4a-peroxyflavin intermediate [45, 46]. These reactions play central roles in drug metabolism, prodrug activation, and fine chemical synthesis. Notably, a specialized subset of flavin monooxygenases, bacterial luciferases, couple flavin oxidation to bioluminescence. In this system, the C4a-peroxyflavin reacts with a long-chain aldehyde to generate an excited flavin intermediate that relaxes with photon emission [47]. Beyond their ecological function in microbial communication, luciferase reactions have been widely exploited in biosensing, reporter gene assays, and real-time imaging.

Applications of flavin-dependent monooxygenases in biocatalysis

FMOs catalyze a wide range of oxidative reactions with broad applications in biocatalysis particularly in the biosynthesis of antibiotics, secondary metabolites from plants and microorganisms, and certain anticancer drugs.

Role of FMOs in antibiotic biosynthesis

FMOs can catalyze antibiotic biosynthesis by mediating hydroxylation, halogenation, and epoxidation reactions that fine-tune pharmacological properties. For instance, in the biosynthesis of the antitumor antibiotic C-1027, the FAD-dependent monooxygenase SgcC3 hydroxylates the C3 position of (S)-β-tyrosine, forming a functionalized precursor with DNA-cleaving activity. This hydroxylation significantly enhances the antitumor potency of the molecule by strengthening hydrogen bonding interactions with the biological target [48]. In the biosynthesis of the natural product rebeccamycin, the FAD-dependent halogenase RebH catalyzes the site-specific chlorination or bromination of tryptophan at the C7 position (Fig. 4A) [49]. Halogen incorporation increases molecular lipophilicity, facilitating cell permeability and modulating interactions with topoisomerase via steric effects. Similarly, the flavin monooxygenase MonCI is a key enzyme in the biosynthesis of monensin A, a prototypical polyether antibiotic. MonCI successively catalyzes the epoxidation of three double bonds in the polyene precursor premonensin A with exceptional stereospecificity (Fig. 4B). Crystallographic analysis revealed that MonCI constrains substrate conformation in its active site, enabling precise spatial recognition with the C4a-hydroperoxyflavin intermediate (Fig. 4C). Its exceptionally spacious substrate pocket accommodates multiple substrate conformers, allowing iterative epoxidation on the same molecule (Fig. 4D), underscoring the remarkable catalytic versatility of the FMO family [50].

D Structure of the binding pocket of MonCI with C(4a)-(hydro)peroxyflavin adenine dinucleotide (top); Predicted model of the binding of key amino acids in the MonCI pocket with C(4a)-(hydro)peroxyflavin adenine dinucleotide (bottom)

Beyond typical oxidative decorations, FMOs can also perform regio- and stereoselective structural transformations critical for building complex natural product scaffolds. For example, the FMO Kmy13 catalyzes the key step in the biosynthesis of kendomycin B, a polycyclic ansa-type antibiotic distinct from classical ansamycins (Fig. 5A) [51]. Similarly, in the biosynthesis of polyether antibiotic lasalocid A, the FMO Lsd18 catalyzes two successive epoxidation reactions on distinct C=C bonds, forming dual (R,R)-configured epoxide moieties that construct the core polyether scaffold (Fig. 5B). Structural studies demonstrated that Lsd18’s large substrate-binding pocket accommodates polyene substrates, while a subpocket near the active site governs the approach of C=C bonds, ensuring strict stereochemical control (Fig. 5C) [52]. In rifamycin biosynthesis, the FMO Rif-Orf17 mediates a dual oxidation reaction—transforming the naphthoquinone chromophore of rifamycin S into a benzopyran ring, and hydroxylating phenolic groups that trigger the linearization and inactivation of rifamycin SV (Fig. 5D) [53]. This demonstrates that FMOs not only participate in antibiotic biosynthesis but also fine-tune their bioactivity. Moreover, FMOs are also involved in the biosynthesis of thiostrepton [54], taenimycin [55], and other complex antibiotics, enhancing their pharmacological profiles.

C Overall structure of Lsd18 (left), structure of the binding pocket of Lsd18 with C(4a)-(hydro)peroxyflavin adenine dinucleotide (right) (PDB ID: 8UP4). D Rif-Orf17-catalyzed conversion between rifamycin S and rifamycin SV

Recent mechanistic discoveries have expanded the potential of FMOs in antibiotic biosynthesis. For example, a cold-active type II FMO from Janthinobacterium svalbardensis maintains high catalytic activity between 5–25 °C and exhibits broad NADH/NADPH compatibility. It catalyzes monooxygenation and sulfoxidation of various ketones and thioesters with high stereoselectivity, suggesting that increased flexibility of the active site does not compromise catalytic specificity [56]. Additionally, a unique [1, 2]-Meisenheimer rearrangement mediated by the FMO PaxA from Penicillium janthinellum was experimentally validated. PaxA catalyzes N-oxidation of paeciloxazine followed by rearrangement, forming an unprecedented N–O bridge. This finding expands the known repertoire of enzymatic rearrangements in natural product biosynthesis [57]. In Streptomyces viridifaciens, the biosynthesis of valanimycin involves a classical two-component FMO system composed of a flavin reductase (FRED) and an isobutylamine N-hydroxylase (IBAH) (Fig. 6A). FRED reduces FAD using NADPH and directly transfers the reduced flavin to IBAH, which catalyzes the hydroxylation of isobutylamine to isobutylhydroxylamine (Fig. 6C). Thermodynamic analyses show that FRED has higher affinity for oxidized FAD, while IBAH prefers reduced FAD, ensuring unidirectional flavin transfer. The study highlights the molecular basis of efficient electron coupling and substrate oxidation in antibiotic biosynthesis mediated by FMOs [58].

Functional roles of FMOs in metabolic regulation

FMOs serve as biocatalysts in secondary metabolism, modulating metabolite bioactivity via oxidative modification and playing multifaceted roles in growth, stress response, and drug metabolism. In plant growth regulation, FMOs catalyze oxidation of hormone precursors. For instance, FMOs hydroxylate indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) at the C2 position to yield 2-hydroxy-IAA, which promotes root elongation and stress adaptation through transcriptional regulation of growth-related genes [59]. In stress defense, FMOs contribute to the biosynthesis of hydroxylated salicylic acid derivatives that activate systemic acquired resistance [60]. In tomato fruit ripening, the FMO SlTNH1 catalyzes successive N-hydroxylations of tetrahydrothiazolidine-4-carboxylic acid, generating volatile nitrogen-containing compounds that shape tomato aroma (Fig. 7A) [61]. Similarly, in ferns (Phlebodium aureum and Pteridium aquilinum), the first step of cyanogenic glycoside biosynthesis—conversion of amino acids to corresponding aldoximes—is catalyzed not by cytochrome P450s but by a FMO FOS1 (Fig. 7B) [62].

A TNH-dependent biosynthetic model of nitrogen-containing volatiles in tomato fruits. B In ferns, the conversion of parent amino acids to oximes is catalyzed by multifunctional FMOs, whereas in other higher seed plants, this reaction is catalyzed by CYP79-family cytochrome P450 enzymes

In microorganisms, FMOs likewise regulate secondary metabolism. The FMO PhzO from Pseudomonas species converts phenazine-1-carboxylic acid to 2-hydroxyphenazine, enhancing antifungal activity [63]. Co-expression with a flavin reductase (Fre) improves FADH₂ regeneration and enables formation of dihydroxylated derivatives with anticancer potential (Fig. 8A). Other examples include the SorC/SorD system in fungal sorbicillinoid biosynthesis, which achieves stereoselective product control (Fig. 8B) [64], and the CmoO/CmoJ system responsible for S-alkylcysteine metabolism in soil bacteria, employing an unprecedented Pummerer-type rearrangement for C–S bond cleavage (Fig. 8C) [65, 66]. In insects, FMOs are involved in xenobiotic metabolism and pesticide resistance. For example, in the rice pest Chilo suppressalis, FMO3B and FMO3C are significantly upregulated in field populations resistant to insecticides such as chlorantraniliprole and spinetoram. Knockdown experiments confirmed their essential roles in detoxification [67].

A PhzO-catalyzed conversion of PCA to 2-OH-PHZ and 3,4-OH-PCA. B Cooperative catalysis of the flavin monooxygenase SorC and the oxidase SorD in sorbicillinoid biosynthesis. C S-alkylcysteine recycling pathway in Bacillus subtilis

The catalytic versatility of FMOs has driven increasing interest in their biotechnological exploitation. In pharmaceutical and fine chemical synthesis, FMOs are widely used to produce chiral sulfoxides, N-oxides, and epoxides with high chemo- and enantioselectivity under mild conditions, providing greener alternatives to traditional oxidation chemistry. Engineered FMO systems, often coupled with efficient flavin reductases for cofactor recycling, have been implemented in whole-cell biocatalysis platforms to generate hydroxylated aromatics. In agriculture and synthetic biology, plant and microbial FMOs are being incorporated into engineered pathways to enhance the biosynthesis of defense metabolites and construct biosensor or reporter systems. Moreover, the oxidative dehalogenation and heteroatom oxidation capabilities of FMOs make them promising tools for environmental bioremediation of halogenated and sulfur- or nitrogen-containing pollutants.

Applications of FMOs in the biosynthesis of anticancer natural products

FMOs are indispensable catalysts in the biosynthesis of various value-added anticancer compounds, contributing to oxidative modifications that optimize drug efficacy and promote sustainable biosynthetic routes. Paclitaxel (Taxol), one of the most successful anticancer drugs, requires FMO-mediated hydroxylation at the C5 position and epoxidation of 10-deacetylbaccatin III intermediates [68, 69]. In epothilone biosynthesis, FMOs catalyze epoxidation at the C12–C13 double bond, generating the active macrocyclic lactone [70]. FMOs also participate in the oxidation–cyclization of tryptamine precursors during vinblastine and vincristine biosynthesis [71]. In notoamide biosynthesis, FMOs NotI and NotI’ catalyze key epoxidation and semi-pinacol rearrangements forming the chiral spiro-oxindole core, conferring molecular complexity and stereochemical diversity [72]. In fluostatin biosynthesis, FlsO1 performs a three-step oxidation cascade—hydroxylation, epoxidation, and Baeyer–Villiger oxidation—to construct the xanthone scaffold with anticancer activity (Fig. 9) [73]. In Strobilanthes cusia, the plant-derived FMO ScFMO1 oxidizes indole to indoxyl, the precursor for indigoid anticancer compounds, demonstrating FMO’s essential role in plant-derived alkaloid biosynthesis [74]. Likewise, Champase from Coccidioides immitis stereoselectively oxidizes substituted indoles to 3-hydroxyindolin-2-ones (up to 95:5 er) (Fig. 10A) [75]. Another FMO, OxaD, catalyzes N-dioxidation of Roquefortine C, forming Roquefortine L (Fig. 10B) [76]. In E. coli, a six-enzyme pathway incorporating BvnB enabled efficient biosynthesis of brevianamide A and B, with BvnB catalyzing a critical oxidative step in constructing the BDO core (Fig. 10C) [77]. Finally, 4-hydroxyphenylacetate 3-hydroxylase (4HPA3H) from E. coli—a prototypical FMO—has been engineered to broaden its substrate spectrum for hydroxylating bulky phenolic substrates such as ferulic acid. Mutagenesis at Y301 to hydrophobic residues (Ile/Leu) enhanced catalytic preference for guaiacyl-type substrates [78]. Overall, FMO-mediated regioselective oxidations such as hydroxylation and epoxidation are governed by electrostatic complementarity within the substrate pocket. With advances in biocatalysis and enzyme engineering, FMOs are poised to overcome natural yield limitations and drive the scalable biomanufacturing of anticancer drugs.

Conclusions and future perspectives

FMOs exhibit tremendous potential in the field of biocatalysis; however, their practical applications still face a series of challenges. In enzyme stability, FMOs are prone to inactivation under certain reaction conditions, compromising their long-term catalytic performance [18, 79, 80]. For instance, although FMO intermediates such as C4a-hydroperoxyflavin are relatively stable, the enzyme as a whole often generates hydrogen peroxide and other reactive oxygen species (ROS) under oxygen-rich conditions. This can cause nonspecific oxidation, dissipate reducing equivalents, and indirectly compromise overall enzyme stability [18]. High temperature is a common factor contributing to FMO inactivation. For example, studies assessing FMO activity in heat-pretreated human liver microsomes showed that thermal exposure significantly reduces catalytic efficiency toward selective substrates, such as benzydamine. This suggests that elevated temperatures compromise the conformational stability of FMOs, leading to enzyme inactivation. In practical applications, reaction temperatures above the enzyme’s optimal range (typically room temperature to 40 °C) can accelerate this loss of activity [79]. Similarly, pH can strongly influence FMO reactivity and selectivity. Extreme pH conditions-either highly acidic or alkaline-may disrupt the active site or interfere with flavin cofactor binding, resulting in enzyme inactivation. For instance, in certain siderophore-associated FMOs, pH changes can alter O2 activation, indirectly destabilizing the enzyme [80]. Enzyme engineering strategies can improve both thermal and chemical stability. For example, systematic combinatorial mutagenesis of 4-hydroxybenzoate hydroxylase, building upon previously identified single mutations, has been shown to enhance catalytic activity, reaction specificity, and thermal stability [81].

Low soluble expression and improper folding of FMOs in heterologous hosts remain significant challenges, often arising from incorrect cofactor incorporation and protein instability. Many FMOs rely on FAD/FMN and NAD(P)H-dependent redox cycling, and insufficient intracellular flavin availability or inefficient cofactor loading can result in accumulation of inactive enzyme. For instance, recombinant cyclohexanone monooxygenase (CHMO) exhibits limited activity when expressed in E. coli. In contrast, secretory expression in Pichia pastoris markedly enhanced both enzyme yield and catalytic performance, demonstrating that host selection and adequate FAD availability are critical engineering strategies [82].

In substrate specificity, native FMOs typically display a narrow substrate range, limiting their ability to catalyze diverse biotransformations [83]. Computational enzyme design and directed evolution approaches can be employed to engineer FMOs with broadened substrate adaptability and improved catalytic efficiency [84]. In substrate and product related issues, some substrates, such as indole, exhibit cytotoxicity to host cells, restricting their in vivo applications. Developing novel reaction systems—such as cell-free or in vitro biosynthetic platforms—can circumvent these limitations [85]. Certain catalytic products have low water solubility, complicating downstream separation and utilization. This issue can be addressed through chemical modification of products or the design of water-soluble derivatives. For example, a micro-aqueous system was created by adding a small amount of water (e.g., 5 vol% aqueous buffer) to an organic solvent. In this system, a type II flavin-containing monooxygenase (FMO-E) was coupled with horse liver alcohol dehydrogenase (HLADH) to catalyze the synthesis of γ-butyrolactone, achieving a conversion rate of up to 95% while enabling in situ NADPH regeneration. This strategy effectively addressed limitations in oxygen transfer and substrate solubility, resulting in improved space–time yield [86]. In addition, high product concentrations may inhibit FMO activity and decrease reaction efficiency. This can be mitigated by timely product removal using membrane separation, adsorption, or related technologies. Studies have shown that BVMOs and FMOs are often susceptible to product inhibition. Operating at low temperatures can increase oxygen solubility and slow reaction kinetics, thereby mitigating inhibitory effects from product accumulation. Notably, combining low-temperature conditions with a biphasic system facilitates the transfer of products from the aqueous phase into the organic phase, effectively reducing the concentration of inhibitory species in the aqueous phase. This approach has been demonstrated to enhance FMO activity and overall reaction efficiency, offering a promising strategy to overcome product inhibition [56].

FMOs are dependent on the costly cofactors NAD(P)H, which can substantially increase production expenses. Developing efficient cofactor regeneration strategies is therefore critical for cost-effective biocatalysis. In this context, a study demonstrated that light can be used as an energy source and EDTA as an electron donor to replace NADPH in flavin-dependent monooxygenase reactions, allowing the reaction to proceed efficiently in the absence of the expensive cofactor [87]. Moreover, insufficient intracellular cofactor supply can limit catalytic turnover. In addition to cofactor regeneration, overexpression of genes involved in NAD(P)H biosynthesis via genetic engineering can enhance intracellular cofactor availability [88, 89].

Limited oxygen transfer to the FMO active site can restrict reaction rates. Enhancing aeration and agitation or incorporating oxygen carrier materials can increase dissolved oxygen levels and improve transfer efficiency [90]. Additionally, hydroxylation of naphthalene has been achieved via directed evolution of a cytochrome P450 from Pseudomonas putida using hydrogen peroxide as the electron acceptor [91]. This suggests a potential new strategy for FMOs: engineering enzymes to utilize peroxides or organic peroxides as alternative oxygen donors to overcome oxygen supply limitations. Conversely, excessive oxygen may participate in side reactions that generate harmful byproducts, reducing product yield and quality; thus, optimizing reaction conditions such as temperature and pH is necessary to suppress these undesirable reactions [92].

Genetic engineering has been applied to overcome the challenges of narrow substrate scope, limited stability, and suboptimal selectivity of FMOs. Structure-guided mutagenesis, loop swapping, and saturation mutagenesis expanded substrate tolerance and enhanced regio- and enantioselectivity of phenylacetone monooxygenase (PAMO) [93]. Recent work has significantly expanded the available repertoire of FMO/BVMO biocatalysts for enantiodivergent sulfoxide synthesis through the combined use of enzyme mining and protein engineering. By identifying monooxygenases capable of selectively producing either (R)- or (S)-sulfoxides and applying structure-guided optimization, the catalytic efficiency, substrate scope, and enantioselectivity have been improved. Implementation in whole-cell systems further enhanced cofactor utilization and process robustness [94].

In summary, despite multiple challenges, these bottlenecks are expected to be overcome through integrated strategies such as enzyme engineering, computational design, development of novel reaction systems, construction of efficient cofactor regeneration modules, product removal techniques, and optimization of reaction conditions, thereby promoting the application of FMOs in biomanufacturing.
