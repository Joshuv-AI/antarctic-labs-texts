Topological stress regulates replication fork dynamics in unperturbed S phase
Authors: Deepika Jayaprakash, Josie Gannon Patterson, Ruthie Mulvaney, Quinn Dickinson, Courtney Lovejoy, Mads Lerdrup, Juan Carlos Rivera-Mulia, David Cortez, Rahul Bhowmick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate genome duplication requires tight-regulation of replication fork progression, and disruptions to this process are a major source of genomic instability, yet how fork dynamics are controlled during unperturbed S-phase remains unclear. We found replication forks elongate slowly in early S (ES) and faster in late S, independent of transcription or nucleotide availability. Elevated origin firing coupled with low TOP2A in ES generates torsional stress, causing replisome uncoupling, reduced fork speed, and basal ATR-CHK1 activation. Overexpression of TOP2A enhances fork speed and reduces replication stress in ES. Thus, TOP2A is a limiting replication factor during unperturbed ES, and basal ATR-CHK1 signaling is driven by transient replisome uncoupling. Also, TOP2A overexpression suppresses oncogene-driven replication stress. Given that TOP2A is frequently upregulated in cancers, it may function as a compensatory response to oncogene-induced replication stress. Together, these findings establish TOP2A as a central regulator of replication fork dynamics.

Introduction

Precise genome duplication requires tight regulation of replication elongation (fork speed). Insufficient replication fork speed delays S phase completion and yields under-replicated DNA1,2, whereas excessive or poorly controlled speed compromises genome surveillance and increases mutation or breakage3. Fork speed is responsive to developmental cues and cellular stress, varies across differentiation4 and oncogenic transformation5–7, and is increased by PARP inhibitors3, which may contribute to their efficacy. Yet the determinants of fork speed during an unperturbed S phase remain poorly defined.

Fork progression is impeded by compact chromatin, DNA lesions, unavailability of replication factors (nucleotides, etc.) and transcription-replication conflicts8–17. In addition, both in vitro and in vivo studies have shown that the mechanical process of DNA unwinding during replication introduces substantial topological stress18–21. As the replisome advances, it generates positive supercoils ahead of the fork and intertwined nascent chromatids behind it22–24. If unresolved, these constraints perturb replication21,25. Topoisomerases mitigate this burden: TOP1 introduces transient single-strand breaks to relax positive supercoils ahead of forks, while TOP2A introduces transient double-strand breaks to decatenate intertwined DNA both in front of and behind forks25–27. Although TOP2A is essential for replication termination28 and mitotic chromosome segregation29,30, its role in ongoing fork progression is incompletely understood. Unlike TOP1 and TOP2B, TOP2A levels are cell-cycle regulated, with low expression in G1, increasing through late S phase, and peaking at mitosis31–33. This nonuniform availability across the S phase may create a bottleneck under high replication demand.

Human genomes replicate according to a spatial-temporal program in which early replicating regions are gene-rich and transcriptionally active, whereas late-replicating domains are heterochromatic and silent34–39. While this replication timing is tightly regulated, it remains unclear whether fork speed is uniform across these domains. S phase progression is further controlled by the ATR/CHK1-driven intra-S checkpoint40–44. Although the precise mechanisms that trigger intra-S checkpoint activity throughout S phase remain incompletely understood, its role is essential for preserving genome integrity under physiological conditions45,46.

In this study, we found that replication fork speed in human cells intrinsically varies across S phase, with early S phase forks progressing 2-fold more slowly due to elevated origin firing and topological stress from limiting TOP2A. Limited levels of TOP2A induce fork uncoupling, triggering basal ATR/CHK1 activation during unperturbed S phase. Cancer cells overexpress TOP2A47, and one way this can confer a survival advantage is by allowing them to cope with oncogene elevated replication stress, linking TOP2A levels to tumor adaptability. This study demonstrates that torsional stress is not merely a byproduct of DNA unwinding but a key regulator of the replication program and reveals TOP2A overexpression in cancer as both an adaptive mechanism and a therapeutic vulnerability.

Results

Slow replication fork rates in early S phase are accompanied by intrinsic replication stress and basal checkpoint signaling

Although the effects of acute replication stress induced by exogenous agents has been extensively studied48, the intrinsic behavior of replication forks during unperturbed S phase remains incompletely understood. To investigate this, we synchronized transformed (U2OS, HCT116) and immortalized untransformed (RPE-1) cells at the G1/S boundary using thymidine. Cells were then released for 3.5 or 7 h to enrich for early or late S phase, respectively, and replication fork dynamics were assessed using DNA fiber analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B), which labels newly synthesized DNA with nucleoside analogs and visualizes individual forks by stretching DNA fibers on slides and staining the incorporated analogs49. Average fork speed varied more than two-fold, with early S phase forks progressing at less than half the rate of late S phase forks across all cell types, revealing a conserved temporal pattern of fork progression. (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Fork dynamics in sorted asynchronous cells mirrored those in synchronized cells, supporting the conclusion that the stage-dependent fork progression reflects an inherent property of unperturbed S phase (Fig. 1B, C and Supplementary Fig. 1D). Genomic regions replicated in early and late S phase differ in compaction, with early S phase regions forming open euchromatin and late S phase regions forming condensed heterochromatin53. To test whether chromatin structure drives the temporal variation in fork speed, we modulated histone acetylation using histone acetyl transferase (HAT) and histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors PU-139 (to reduce acetylation and promote compaction) and valproic acid (to increase acetylation and promote opening) respectively54 (Supplementary Fig. 1F). These treatments affected overall fork progression, particularly in early S cells, yet the distinct difference in fork speed between early and late S phase persisted, indicating that chromatin compaction modulates fork speed but does not account for the intrinsic shift across S phase (Fig. 1F).

To determine whether intrinsic differences in the replication machinery underlie slower fork progression in early S phase, we examined replication fork asymmetry. If the machinery itself were altered, forks would slow but remain symmetric; if fork stalling were responsible for slower fork speed, asymmetry would increase. DNA fiber analysis revealed that early S phase forks are not only slower, but significantly more (> 50%) asymmetric (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Fig. 1G), suggesting increased fork stalling. Consistently, early S cells exhibited higher levels of single-stranded DNA (potentially exposed at stalled forks), increased chromatin-bound RPA (marking single-stranded DNA at stressed replication forks) and elevated DNA damage markers (Fig. 1H, I and Supplementary Fig. 1H). Since stalled forks expose single-stranded DNA and, upon being collapsed, activate damage responses, we examined these markers. While these global increases could partly reflect the higher number of active replication forks during early S phase55,56, the increase in fork asymmetry, normalized per individual fork, supports frequent fork stalling leading to elevated endogenous replication stress during unperturbed early S.

Together, these findings reveal that the slow replication fork movement in early S phase is not due to transcriptional interference or chromatin compaction but is potentially driven by frequent fork stalling.

High levels of origin usage in early S contributes to slow elongation

To investigate the basis of replication stress in early S phase, we examined origin activity. Consistent with previous studies55,56, significantly more origins fired in early S than in late S (Supplementary Fig. 1I). This led us to hypothesize that elevated origin firing in early S may deplete limiting replication factors, thereby inducing fork stalling, leading to an overall reduction in fork speed. To test this, we modulated origin firing in U2OS cells synchronized in early or late S by treating with a CHK1 inhibitor57 to increase firing or a CDC7 inhibitor58 to suppress origin activation during the final 1.5 hrs. before fork speed analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2A). If replication stress in early S results from limiting replication factors, then reducing origin firing should increase fork speed in early S without affecting late S, while increasing firing should further reduce fork speed in early S and minimally impact late S (Fig. 2A). Consistent with this, CDC7 inhibition significantly restored fork speed in early S with no effect in late S, whereas CHK1 inhibition reduced fork speed in both phases (Fig. 2B). These results indicate that one plausible hypothesis for restrained fork progression in early S phase is the limited availability of replication factors, which may also become limiting in late S phase under conditions of excessive origin firing. While other mechanisms are possible, this interpretation is consistent with several previous studies suggesting that S-phase progression can be modulated by the availability of limiting replication components59,60. Since nucleotides have been proposed as a limiting factor under conditions of high replication demand61, we tested whether exogenous nucleoside supplementation could alleviate fork slowing. Hydroxyurea (HU), a ribonucleotide reductase inhibitor, slows fork progression by depleting nucleotide pools62, and nucleoside supplementation reverses this effect. Elevating PRIMPOL-mediated repriming increases replication fork speed in early S at the cost of genome stability

Given the elevated levels of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) detected by native BrdU staining in ES compared to late S, we next asked whether replication protein A (RPA) availability might be limiting. Previous studies have shown that increased origin firing through ATR checkpoint inactivation can cause RPA exhaustion and replication catastrophe62. To test this, we used SuperRPA (U2OSRPA) cells62, which stably overexpress all three RPA subunits while maintaining correct stoichiometry. Strikingly, RPA overexpression not only increased fork speed specifically in early S but also abolished the stage-dependent differences in fork speed, rendering fork progression comparable between early and late S (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. 2B,). These findings suggest that RPA availability could be a limiting factor during early S under conditions of high origin firing. Consistent with this, reducing origin firing in U2OSRPA cells failed to further increase fork speed, reinforcing the model that fork progression in early S is constrained by RPA availability (Fig. 2E).

We reasoned that if RPA were truly limiting, its overexpression should alleviate replication stress in early S and accelerate S phase progression. Surprisingly, instead of shortening the S phase, RPA overexpression caused a modest delay in completion (Fig. 2F). Moreover, U2OSRPA cells showed increased genome instability, including elevated micronuclei and anaphase bridges, along with a modest rise in DNA damage markers (Fig. 2G, H). Unexpectedly, RPA overexpression also enhanced native BrdU signal, indicating increased single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) accumulation (Fig. 2I). One possible explanation for the ssDNA accumulation is that excess RPA alters the balance between fork reversal and repriming, processes known to modulate replication speed under stress. Increased PRIMPOL-mediated repriming can accelerate average fork speed by enabling more frequent fork restart63–67, but at the cost of leaving persistent ssDNA gaps67. Consistent with this model, PRIMPOL depletion further reduced fork speed in U2OS cells, particularly in early S, and suppressed the accelerated fork progression in U2OSRPA cells, restoring it to parental levels (Fig. 2J and Supplementary Fig. 2C). Similarly, stable PRIMPOL overexpression (U2OSPPOL) eliminated the differential fork speeds between early and late S phase by specifically increasing early S phase fork speed (Supplementary Fig. 2D, E). Like RPA overexpression, the increase in fork speed from PRIMPOL overexpression was unaffected by reduced origin firing (Supplementary Fig. 2F), and origin usage remained unchanged in both U2OSRPA and U2OSPPOL cells, ruling out decreased origin firing as a cause of acceleration (Supplementary Fig. 2G). Similar to RPA, PRIMPOL overexpression also showed modest and statistically insignificant delayed S phase progression (Supplementary Fig. 2H, I), increased genome instability including micronuclei and anaphase bridges (Supplementary Fig. 2J, K), elevated ssDNA accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 2L), and modestly reduced overall proliferation rates (Supplementary Fig. 2M). These data suggest that, surprisingly, just overexpression of either RPA or PRIMPOL promotes repriming during early S, likely due to frequent helicase-polymerase uncoupling, consistent with the elevated ssDNA and ATR signaling observed in the S phase.

Although RPA and PRIMPOL overexpression increased early S-phase fork speeds, it seemed unlikely that they are truly limiting factors under normal conditions. RPA is highly abundant, and previous studies have shown that aberrant origin activation via ATR checkpoint inhibition is required to exhaust soluble RPA pools62. While some studies have reported reduced fork speed in PRIMPOL-depleted cells under unperturbed conditions68, PRIMPOL is predominantly involved in replication fork restart and lesion bypass during replication stress63,65, rather than normal replicative synthesis. We therefore considered the possibility that normalization of synthesis rates throughout S phase by RPA and PRIMPOL overexpression simply bypassed the true restraint that generates elevated replication stress in early S cells. To address this, we first assessed endogenous protein levels of RPA and PRIMPOL across the cell cycle and found no substantial changes in their overall expression (Supplementary Fig. 2N). We then tested whether their chromatin recruitment is altered by modulating origin firing. If these factors were limiting in early S, increasing origin firing should not proportionally increase their chromatin association. Contrary to this, both RPA and PRIMPOL showed increased chromatin recruitment when origin firing was elevated, arguing against the idea that these proteins are limiting during early S phase (Fig. 2K).

CHK1 inhibition under high replication stress increases origin firing, which has been reported to exhaust PCNA and lead to replication catastrophe69,70. However, PCNA overexpression did not affect the temporal variation in fork dynamics in unperturbed S phase (Supplementary Fig. 2O, P). Inducing new origins during early S increased PCNA chromatin localization (Supplementary Fig. 2Q), suggesting that, like RPA, PCNA becomes limiting only when the replication checkpoint is inactivated.

Together, these results indicate that frequent fork uncoupling during early S phase, driven by elevated origin firing, underlies the intrinsic replication stress. While RPA or PRIMPOL overexpression accelerates fork progression through repriming, it does not resolve the fundamental constraints that slow synthesis in early S relative to late S.

Replication fork progression in early S is constrained by limited TOP2A availability

To systematically identify potential limiting factors at the replication fork, we performed iPOND (isolation of proteins on nascent DNA)71 coupled to mass spectrometry in synchronized early and late S cells (Fig. 3A), capturing the dynamic composition of the replication fork proteome across S phase and providing a framework for subsequent hypothesis-driven analysis. Comparing common proteins detected in both biological replicates of early S versus late S iPOND datasets with the published list of replication fork proteins71, we found that the vast majority (61%) showed no change in abundance. Of the remaining proteins, 16% decreased, and 23% increased beyond the field-standard 15% abundance ratio threshold. (Supplementary data 1). TOP2A was notably depleted from forks in early S despite higher fork density (Fig. 3B). In contrast, TOP2B levels remained unchanged, while TOP1 association increased in early S (Fig. 3B, C), suggesting differential regulation of topoisomerases across S phase32, with TOP2A potentially acting as a limiting factor during early S. We detected several proteins, whose differential fork recruitment could potentially alter unperturbed fork speed. The increased association of TOP1 with replication forks in early S is consistent with elevated origin firing and reduced inter-origin distance, which increases fork number and torsional stress ahead of forks, necessitating enhanced supercoil resolution. TOP2A is generally thought to act primarily behind the fork to remove catenanes generated as the replication machinery rotates18. To determine whether reduced fork association of TOP2A in early S reflects restricted recruitment or lower expression, we assessed its protein levels across the cell cycle. Consistent with previous studies32, TOP2A expression is low in G1, rises in early S, and peaks during late S/G2/M, suggesting that lower levels contribute to its reduced fork association in early S (Supplementary Fig. 3A). To test whether TOP2A is limiting during early S, we generated stable TOP2A-overexpressing U2OS (U2OSTOP2A) and RPE-1 cells (RPETOP2A) (Supplementary Fig. 3B, C). Overexpression restored fork speed in early S, supporting the idea that TOP2A availability constrains fork progression at this stage (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. 3D). Conversely, TOP2A depletion further reduced the fork speed in early S but had no effect in late S, indicating a phase-specific requirement (Supplementary Fig. 3E, F). Short-term treatment with the TOP2A catalytic inhibitor73–75 ICRF-187 or Merbarone phenocopied depletion effects, suggesting that TOP2A promotes fork progression via its catalytic activity (Fig. 3E). Reducing origin firing in TOP2A-overexpressing cells did not further increase fork speed, reinforcing that TOP2A is limiting during early S (Fig. 3F). Unlike RPA and PRIMPOL, TOP2A recruitment to chromatin did not increase upon CHK1 inhibition, and soluble nuclear TOP2A levels were minimal, confirming the absence of a substantial reserve pool and highlighting TOP2A as a limiting replication factor in early S phase (Fig. 3G). In the late S phase, chromatin-bound TOP2A increased markedly while the soluble pool remained scarce, indicating that most TOP2A is associated with chromatin (Fig. 3G) and providing only limited buffering capacity when origin firing is further elevated. Consistent with this model, CDC7 inhibition released TOP2A from chromatin into the soluble fraction particularly during late S phase (Fig. 3G), supporting a role for TOP2A partitioning between chromatin-bound and soluble pools in linking origin firing density to replication fork speed, such that excessive origin firing upon CHK1 inhibition also slows late S-phase forks, albeit less than in early S phase (Fig. 2B). Nonetheless, it remains possible that CHK1 and/or CDC7 inhibition directly influences fork progression, or that their activities differ between early and late S phase.

The accelerated fork speed observed with TOP2A overexpression could potentially reflect an effect on origin firing, as previous studies in Xenopus egg extracts demonstrated increased origin firing upon TOP2A depletion76. However, TOP2A overexpression in human cells did not alter origin firing frequency or inter-origin distance, indicating that the observed accelerated fork speed was not due to changes in origin usage (Fig. 3H and Supplementary Fig. 3G). Unlike RPA-overexpressing cells, the increase in fork speed from TOP2A overexpression was also independent of PRIMPOL (Supplementary Fig. 3H, I).

While synchronized cells showed no significant acceleration in overall S phase progression (Supplementary Fig. 3J, K), asynchronously growing TOP2A-overexpressing cells exhibited a modest proliferation increase (Fig. 3I), suggesting a shortened cell cycle. Importantly, these cells displayed reduced DNA damage and genome instability markers, decreased ssDNA accumulation, and lower RPA recruitment to chromatin compared to parental cells (Fig. 3J–L and Supplementary Fig. 3L), along with markedly reduced fork asymmetry (Supplementary Fig. 3M). Thus, unlike RPA or PRIMPOL overexpression, TOP2A overexpression prevents replication stress rather than bypassing the underlying cause of slow forks in early S. These findings support the idea that intrinsic replication stress in early S arises from topological problems caused by high origin firing, which exceeds the capacity of the relatively low levels of endogenous TOP2A, whereas in late S, reduced origin firing and higher TOP2A levels alleviate this topological burden.

To test whether TOP2A uniquely overcomes the torsional stress that slows early S-phase synthesis, we overexpressed TOP1 (Supplementary Fig. 3N). Unlike TOP2A, TOP1 overexpression failed to restore fork speed in early S, indicating that replication stress associated with early S arises not from positive supercoiling ahead of the fork, but potentially from pre-catenanes or other TOP2A-specific substrates (Supplementary Fig. 3O).

Torsional stress generates the endogenous replication stress that maintains basal level of ATR activation in S phase

Having established that limited TOP2A in early S phase constrains replication fork speed, likely by failing to resolve topological stress, we next investigated whether this intrinsic replication stress activates the basal ATR–CHK1 checkpoint under unperturbed conditions41. While low CHK1 activity regulates S phase progression and G2 entry without exogenous insults, the endogenous trigger remains unclear. It is typically attributed to transient RPA-coated single-stranded DNA on the lagging strand. However, given the markedly higher ssDNA in early S and its further increase upon RPA or PRIMPOL overexpression, which promotes fork repriming, we hypothesized that transient fork uncoupling caused by insufficient TOP2A, alongside lagging strand ssDNA, serves as the primary trigger of basal ATR–CHK1 activation during unperturbed S phase.

To test this idea, we quantified post-replicative ssDNA gaps as an indirect measure of fork uncoupling (Fig. 4A). Gaps were significantly more abundant in early S than in late S and further increased by RPA or PRIMPOL overexpression (Fig. 4B). In contrast, TOP2A overexpression, which accelerates fork progression by relieving torsional stress, substantially reduced ssDNA gap formation (Fig. 4B). CHK1 phosphorylation closely mirrored fork uncoupling, being elevated in early S, further increased by RPA or PRIMPOL overexpression, and reduced with TOP2A overexpression (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. 4A). None of these manipulations altered origin firing, supporting the idea that CHK1 activation in unperturbed S phase is driven primarily by transient uncoupled forks rather than just normal lagging strand synthesis. These findings support a model in which high levels of origin firing with limiting TOP2A levels in early S yield excessive torsional stress. Replication programs are also altered by oncogene expression, which increases origin activation and replication stress. To address whether TOP2A overexpression can alleviate oncogene-induced replication stress in untransformed cells, we employed p53-deficient RPE-1 cells with inducible cyclin E overexpression, as p53 wild-type cells undergo apoptosis upon oncogene overexpression. Notably, TOP2A overexpression rescues fork slowing in cells with oncogene-induced stress, implicating elevated topological strain as a source of oncogene-induced replication stress and suggesting that elevated TOP2A levels support stress mitigation (Supplementary Fig. 4B, D). In contrast, TOP2A failed to restore fork progression in cells treated with hydroxyurea, aphidicolin, or cytarabine treatment, which impair replication via nucleotide depletion, polymerase stalling, or chain termination (Supplementary Fig. 4C). These results indicate that TOP2A specifically counteracts stress from excessive torsional strain, not direct replication inhibition, a model further supported by its ability to restore synthesis rates during CHK1 inhibitor-induced excessive origin firing (Supplementary Fig. 4D). This functional specificity underscores that TOP2A is required to resolve topological barriers to fork progression and supports a model in which topological stress and fork uncoupling are central to endogenous replication stress in early S phase. Together, these findings reveal that torsional stress is not merely a byproduct of replication but a fundamental regulator of fork dynamics, checkpoint activation during S phase.

Discussion

In this study, we identified that replication fork speed increases during S-phase progression in both transformed and untransformed human cells. Forks advance 2-3 times slower in early compared to late S phase, although this shift could be a gradual acceleration as S-phase progresses, which is consistent with recent findings77,78. This pattern parallels origin firing. Early S cells have a higher origin density than late S phase. Suppression of origin firing speeds up early S forks, whereas increasing origin firing further slows them. Transcription and nucleotide availability do not account for this behavior. Instead, topological stress is the dominant constraint in early S, where densely spaced origins generate torsional strain, including supercoils ahead of forks and pre-catenanes behind. During replication, together with TOP1, TOP2A likely forms a “torsional stress relief” system. In early S, limited TOP2A underpowers this system, allowing torsional strain to build and constrain fork progression. Because TOP1 overexpression fails to increase early S fork speed, early S likely features torsional structures that slow DNA synthesis and are resolvable only by TOP2A.

We found that fork slowing under these conditions is associated with fork stalling and helicase-polymerase uncoupling, as evidenced by increased fork asymmetry, ssDNA, and post-replicative gaps that drive basal ATR-CHK1 activation in unperturbed S phase. A recent single-molecule study using the T7 replisome demonstrated that accumulated precatenanes can generate back-torque on the replisome, promote helicase-polymerase uncoupling, and reduce fork processivity79. Although similar experiments have not yet been performed with human polymerases, this framework is consistent with our observations that TOP2A overexpression reduces fork asymmetry, ssDNA, gaps, and restores fork speed. We propose that in human cells, precatenanes behind the fork could similarly promote helicase-polymerase uncoupling and fork stalling, thereby restraining the overall speed of fork progression. Fork stalling induced by torsional stress eventually leads to fork collapse in a subset of cases, triggering the modest accumulation of DNA damage markers. In contrast, by late S-phase, fewer active forks and higher TOP2A restore balance and permit faster elongation.

Although basal checkpoint activation decreases upon TOP2A overexpression, origin usage remains unchanged, indicating that the link between origin firing and ATR-CHK1 activation is disrupted upon TOP2A overexpression. Overexpression of PRIMPOL or RPA also rescues early S fork speed by enabling repriming downstream of uncoupled forks, but increases ssDNA gaps, reflecting repeated repriming and polymerase exchange under high torsional stress. These factors are not normally limiting, yet their overexpression induces repriming, suggesting a cell cycle-specific mechanism restrains excessive repriming to protect genome stability. Consequently, PRIMPOL or RPA overexpression promotes genomic instability, underscoring the importance of controlled repriming for replication fidelity under torsional stress65,67,80.

While RPA, PRIMPOL, or TOP2A overexpression each increased early S-phase fork speed, this was not reflected in bulk S-phase progression as measured by flow cytometry. Specifically, RPA and PRIMPOL overexpression caused a mild and statistically insignificant slowing of S-phase progression, while TOP2A overexpression left S-phase progression essentially unchanged. The mild slowing observed with RPA and PRIMPOL overexpression likely reflects checkpoint activation triggered by accumulated ssDNA. In contrast, the lack of any detectable effect with TOP2A overexpression, despite its ability to accelerate fork speed, is likely due to the limited temporal resolution of flow cytometry compared to single-molecule DNA fiber analysis, rather than an absence of a true biological effect as previously noticed in a different context81. Importantly, the 5-day growth curve analysis proved more sensitive in capturing the functional consequences of fork speed modulation: RPA and PRIMPOL overexpression led to slower cell proliferation, while TOP2A overexpression promoted faster proliferation. Together, these results demonstrate that replication fork speed has measurable consequences on cell cycle dynamics and long-term proliferative capacity.

Oncogene-induced replication stress, marked by excessive origin firing and fork slowing, is often attributed to transcription-replication conflicts (TRCs)5. However, TOP2A overexpression substantially restores fork speed under oncogenic stress, highlighting topological strain, in addition to transcriptional interference, as a key barrier. This provides one possible explanation for frequent TOP2A overexpression in aggressive cancers and its association with poor prognosis: TOP2A may shield the replisome from torsional stress caused by deregulated origin firing, though other roles of TOP2A overexpression in cancer progression remain to be explored. Whether the myriads of defects linked to TRCs arise primarily from direct DNA-RNA polymerase collisions or from torsional strain between them remains an open question.

Replication control by limiting factors is well established. Overexpression of CDC45, a known limiting factor60, induces origin firing but arrests cells in early S, potentially due to torsional stress constraining forks and triggering arrest. PCNA/PAF15 and RPA can become limiting upon checkpoint suppression62,69,70,82. However, in unperturbed early S, PCNA and RPA are not limiting, suggesting these factors constrain replication only when origin firing is excessive, such as when the replication checkpoint is inhibited. In contrast, topological stress is the earliest barrier, triggering basal checkpoint activation and restraining fork progression. These data support a hierarchical model in which topological stress provides potentially the initial safeguard, followed by engagement of other limiting factors to preserve genome stability under increasing replication load. The rationale for limiting TOP2A levels in early S-phase remains unclear; however, given that TOP2A is an inherently dangerous enzyme capable of generating double-strand breaks, tight regulation of its expression is likely critical. It would be interesting to determine whether TOP2A overexpression in an animal model confers an overall fitness disadvantage, which could shed light on the selective pressure to constrain its activity during early S-phase.

In summary, we reveal an intrinsic, topologically driven barrier to fork progression in unperturbed S phase arising from high origin density and limited TOP2A, particularly in early S. By relieving torsional stress, TOP2A modulates fork speed, prevents replication stress, and controls ATR-CHK1 activation to maintain genome stability (Fig. 5). Methods

Cell culture

U2OS (osteosarcoma; human, female origin, ATCC, HTB-96), HCT116 (colorectal carcinoma; human, male origin, ATCC, CCL-247), hTERT-RPE-1 (immortalized retinal pigment epithelium; human, female origin, ATCC, CRL-4000; referred to as RPE-1 throughout the manuscript), U2OS cells stably overexpressing N-terminal GFP-tagged PRIMPOL80 (referred to as U2OSPPOL), RPE-1_Cyclin E_P53KO cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Invitrogen, 11960) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; 10500) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Invitrogen, 10378016) under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂, humidified atmosphere). U2OS_Super RPA cells (referred to as U2OSRPA) and HCT116_POLR2A degron cells (referred to as RNAPII degron) were generous gifts from Drs. Jiri Lukas and Masato Kanemaki, respectively, and maintained as previously described. U2OSTOP2A, RPETOP2A and U2OSPCNA cells were generated by stable lentiviral transduction of N-terminal GFP-tagged TOP2A or N-terminal m-cherry-tagged PCNA, respectively, followed by selection with 1mg/mL G418 or 1 μg/mL puromycin. For iPOND experiments, U2OS cells were cultured in SILAC-compatible DMEM supplemented with 10% dialyzed FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A33822; R&D Systems, S12850H) and either isotopically light or heavy 13 C₆,15 N₂-lysine and 13 C₆,15 N₄-arginine. All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination using the Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Abcam, AB289834).

Cell synchronization

For mitotic and G1 synchronization, asynchronously growing cells were treated with 6 μM RO3306 (Millipore, 217699) for 6 hours, washed with pre-warmed media, and incubated at 37 °C for an additional 30 minutes prior to mitotic shake-off to collect prometaphase cells, which then were replated and incubated at 37 °C for 40–45 min for anaphase cells and for 3-4 h for G1. To synchronize cells at specific stages of S phase, asynchronously growing cells were treated with thymidine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A11493.06) for 16 h (2 mM for U2OS and HCT116, 4 mM for RPE-1), washed with warm media, and released into fresh medium for either 3.5 h (early S phase) or 7 h (late S phase). For G2-phase synchronization, cells were first arrested at the G1/S boundary with thymidine as described above, then released into S phase by washing and subsequently (after 6 hrs of release) treated with 6 μM RO3306 for 6 h.

Drug treatment

The following compounds were used at the indicated final concentrations and treatment durations: thymidine (2 mM in U2OS and HCT116, 4 mM in RPE-1) for 16 h; RO3306 (6 μM) for 6–10 h; ICRF-187, (MCE,HY-B0581, 300 nM) for 1.5 hrs; Merbarone, (MCE, HY-19024, 40 µM) for 1.5 h, PU-139 (MCE,HY-124696, 2 μM) for 1.5 hrs; valproic acid (MCE,HY-10585, 2 mM) for 3 h; XL413 (MCE,HY-15260, 10 μM) for 1.5 h; Prexasertib (MCE,HY-18174, 10 nM) for 1.5 hrs, Aphidicolin (Sigma Aldrich, A0781, 0.2 μM) for 1.5 hrs, Cytarabine (MCE, HY-13605, 0.1 μm) for 1.5 hrs, HU (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A10831.06, 0.1 μM) for 1.5 hrs and doxycycline (MCE,HY-N0565, 100 ng/mL) for 48 h.

Antibodies

The following antibodies were used at the indicated dilutions RPA (Abcam, ab2175) at 1:250; PrimPol (Proteintech, 29824-1-AP) at 1:1000; Histone H3 (Abcam, ab10799) at 1:1000; TOP2A (Bethyl, A300-054A) at 1:1000; GAPDH (Millipore, MAB374) at 1:1000; γH2AX (Ser139) (Cell Signaling, 2577S) at 1:1000; 53BP1 (Santa Cruz, sc-517281) at 1:250; Histone H3K9ac (Upstate, 06-942) at 1:500; TOP1 (Sigma, SAB5702042) at 1:1000; RNA Polymerase II (Millipore, 05-952) at 1:1000; Phospho-Chk1 (Ser345) (Cell Signaling, 2348) at 1:250; BrdU (MoBU-1) (ThermoFisher, B35128) at 1:250; BrdU Clone B44 (detects IdU; Becton Dickinson, 347580) at 1:100; BrdU Clone BU1/75 (detects CldU; Abcam, ab6326) at 1:100; Tubulin (B-7) (Santa Cruz, sc-5286) at 1:1000; and Cyclin E (HE12) (Santa Cruz, sc-247) at 1:1000.

RNA interference

Transient transfection of siRNAs was performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 13778030) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were transfected with 10 nM siRNA targeting PRIMPOL (ON-TARGETplus SMARTpool, Horizon Discovery, L-016804-02-0005) or 5 nM siRNA targeting TOP2A and the ON-TARGETplus Non-targeting Control Pool (Horizon Discovery, D-001810-10-05) and incubated for 48 h. Protein depletion was confirmed by western blot analysis.

Plasmid transfection

Human TOP1 and EGFP-TOP2A overexpression plasmids were synthesized by GenScript. Transient plasmid transfections were performed using FuGENE HD (Promega, E2311) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein overexpression was verified by western blotting. Stable cell lines expressing human mCherry-PCNA (Addgene #215117) or EGFP-TOP2A were generated through viral transduction in the presence of polybrene. Virus production was achieved by co-transfection of 293 T cells with the target expression construct and helper plasmids (psPAX2 and pMD2.G). The resulting cell lines were validated by western blotting.

Flow cytometry

For PI staining, cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and fixed in 35% ethanol in PBS on ice for 1 h. Fixed cells were incubated with 10 μg/mL propidium iodide and 8 μg/mL RNase A in PBS for a minimum of 1 h at 4 °C before analysis. For BrdU/PI staining, cells were pulse-labeled with 10 μM BrdU for 30 min, fixed in 35% ethanol, denatured with 2 N HCl for 20 min, and neutralized with 0.1 M sodium borate (pH 8.5). Cells were washed with PBS and stained with FITC conjugated or Alexa flour 488 conjugated anti-BrdU antibody (BD 347580, 1:100) in PBS with 1% BSA and 0.1% Tween-20 for 30–40 min with vortexing every 10 minutes. The pellet was then washed with PBS and counterstained with PI/RNase A as above. Samples were analyzed with a Thermofisher Attune NxT flow cytometer and processed using FlowJo V10 software.

For sorting, logarithmically growing cells were trypsinized, washed with PBS, and fixed in 70% ethanol in PBS on ice for 1 h. After two washes in PBS, fixed cells were incubated with 10 μg/mL propidium iodide and 8 μg/mL RNase A in PBS for a minimum of 1 h at 4 °C. Sorting of cells at different substages of S phase were done using gates indicated in Supplementary Fig. S1D based on PI intensity. At least 10000 cells were sorted for each gate and were resuspended in 250 µl PBS to carry out the DNA fiber assay.

Cell proliferation assay

For proliferation analysis, 8000 cells were seeded per well in 12-well plates in triplicate. Plates were placed in a BioSpa™ 8 automated incubator (Agilent) connected to a Cytation™ 5 Cell Imaging Multi-Mode Reader (Agilent), and cell confluence was monitored over 5 consecutive days using brightfield imaging. Images were acquired daily under consistent exposure and focus settings, and confluence measurements were calculated using Gen5 software (Agilent). Total cell area was plotted against time to generate proliferation curves.

Cell fractionation

After cells were harvested by trypsinization, and equal cell numbers from each condition were resuspended in Buffer A (100 mM NaCl, 300 mM sucrose, 3 mM MgCl₂, 10 mM PIPES, pH 6.8, 1 mM EGTA, 0.2% Triton X-100) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 04693159001) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 04906837001). Samples were incubated on ice for 5 min. Insoluble material (chromatin fraction) was pelleted by centrifugation at 1400 × g for 4 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected as the soluble fraction. Chromatin pellets were washed once with Buffer A (1400 × g, 4 min, 4 °C), then resuspended in Buffer B (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 200 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 0.75% CHAPS, 1% Triton X-100, with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails) and incubated on ice for 20 min with frequent mixing. The resuspended chromatin was then sonicated using a Bioruptor sonicator (Diagenode) for 15 cycles of 30 s on/30 s off. Following sonication, samples were centrifuged at high speed for 5 min, and the resulting supernatants were either stored at – 80 °C or used for downstream analyses.

Immunoblotting

Whole-cell lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4; 300 mM NaCl; 1% NP-40 or Triton X-100; 0.5% sodium deoxycholate; 0.1% SDS; and 1 mM EDTA), supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Lysates were sonicated using a Bioruptor sonicator (Diagenode) for 10 cycles of 30 s on/30 s off, followed by centrifugation at high speed for 5 minutes. Supernatants were either stored at – 80 °C or subjected to protein quantification using bradford reagent and Gen5 software. For immunoblotting, 20–50 µg of whole-cell lysates or equal volumes of chromatin fractions were mixed 1:1 with 2 × Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad, 1610737), boiled for 10 minutes, and separated on Criterion TGX gels (4–15%, 10%, or 7.5%; Bio-Rad). Proteins were transferred to Immobilon®-FL PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPFL00010). Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat dry milk or BSA in TBST (0.1% Tween-20 in TBS) for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in 5% milk or BSA in TBST. Following three 20-minute washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP- or fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 700/520) diluted in 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were then washed four times for 15 min each with TBST. Signal was detected using Clarity™ Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad, 1705060) or via epifluorescence, and images were acquired using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc imaging system.

Immunofluorescence

For detecting chromatin bound proteins, cells in 96-well plates were pre-extracted (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, 50 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 300 mM sucrose, and 0.5% Triton X-100) for 2 mins followed by fixation (4% Paraformaldehyde in PBS) at room temperature for 10 mins or directly fixed. For detecting ssDNA fixed cells were washed with PBS three times and were blocked by incubation in 3% BSA, 0.5% TritonX-100 in PBS for 20 mins at room temperature. After blocking, cells were exposed to different primary antibodies (γH2AXS139 Cell signaling Tech., 2577S, 1:1000; 53BP1, Santacruz Bio., sc517281, 1:250; RPA, Abcam, ab2175, 1:250, phosphoCHK1S345, Cell signaling Tech., 2348, 1:250, BrdU, ThermoFisher, B35128,1:250) diluted in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. Following this, samples were washed three times with blocking buffer, followed by incubation with Alexa fluor488/594/647 conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen., A11037, A11034, A-21245,1:400) and DAPI (0.5 µ/ml; Sigma-Aldrich, D9542) diluted in blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature. In the case of EdU detection, samples were subjected to the Click reaction for 45 mins using the kit according to manufacturer’s instruction between the primary and secondary antibody incubation. Cells were washed three times with PBS before and after the Click reaction. After secondary antibody incubation and three additional washes in 0.25% TritonX-100-PBS, cells were imaged with the ImageXpress at 4x magnification and analyzed using the ImageXpress HCS analysis software package. For anaphase bridge and micronuclei quantification, asynchronously growing cells were enriched at mitosis or early G1 as described in cell synchronization before being fixed and mounted with Vectashield mounting media with DAPI (Vectashield; Vector Labs, H-1200). Images ware captured by a 40X oil objective (Nikon Eclipse Ti) and analyzed using Nikon Elements software.

DNA Fiber analysis

For replication fork speed analysis, both DNA spreading and DNA combing methods were used. For quantification of origin firing, fork asymmetry, and post-replicative gap analysis, the DNA combing method was employed. In both approaches, cells ( ~ 3.5 × 10⁵) were sequentially pulse-labeled with 20 μM CldU (20 min) and 200 μM IdU (20 min) at 37 °C. For DNA spreading, labeled cells were harvested, resuspended in PBS, and 2 μL of the suspension was mixed with 10 μL of spreading buffer (200 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 50 mM EDTA, 0.5% SDS) on a glass slide. After 8 min incubation, slides were tilted by 45o to spread the DNA, air-dried, and fixed in methanol:acetic acid (3:1). DNA was denatured with 2.5 M HCl for 1 h, blocked in PBS with 10% goat serum and 0.1% Triton X-100, and stained with anti-CldU (mouse, IgG1, BD 347580, 1:100) and anti-IdU (mouse, Abcam ab6326, 1:100) for 1.5 hrs in blocking buffer. After three PBS washes, Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-rat secondary antibodies (1:100) were applied for 1 h. For DNA combing, labeled cells were embedded in low-melting-point agarose plugs, treated overnight with proteinase K followed by three one-hour long washes with TE buffer. For post-replicative gap detection, washed plugs were primed with S1 buffer (30 mM sodium acetate, pH 4.6, 1 mM ZnSO₄, 5% glycerol) followed by incubation in S1 buffer with or without 2 U/mL S1 nuclease at room temperature for 45 min. The samples were then washed in cold with TE buffer. DNA was then extracted and stretched onto silanized coverslips using the Genomic Vision molecular combing system, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Coverslips were denatured with 0.5 M NaOH and 1 M NaCl for 8 mins, washed, dehydrated, and blocked as above. DNA fibers were stained with anti-CldU (1:25) and anti-IdU (1:50) for 1.5 hrs in the blocking buffer, followed by the same secondary antibodies for 1 h. For inter-origin distance measurement isotype specific secondary antibodies were used for IdU (Goat anti-Mouse IgG1 Alexa Fluor 488, Invitrogen A-21121) then coverslips were further stained with anti-ssDNA antibody (Mouse anti-single strand DNA, IgG2a, Millipore MAB3034) for 1.5 hrs. and 45 min with isotype specific secondary antibody (Goat anti-Mouse IgG2a, Alexa Fluor 305, Invitrogen A-21241). All slides were mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade and imaged with a 40 × oil immersion objective on a Nikon Eclipse Ti microscope or Zeiss Axio scan microscope. DNA fiber lengths were analyzed using Nikon Elements software or FIJI.

iPOND-SILAC-MS Proteomic changes at nascent DNA during early and late S phase in U2OS cells were analyzed using iPOND-SILAC-MS, as previously described. Briefly, cells labeled with heavy or light lysine and arginine were synchronized to early S and late S (as described above) and pulse-labeled with 10 µM EdU. Cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min, quenched with 1.25 mM glycine, and permeabilized in 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min. Heavy and light SILAC-labeled cells were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and subjected to a 1 hr click reaction to conjugate biotin to EdU-labeled DNA. After sonication, DNA–protein complexes were captured using streptavidin-conjugated C1 Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher), washed, and boiled in sample buffer for 30 min to reverse cross-links. Samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and digested in-gel with trypsin. Peptides were analyzed by MudPIT using an eight-step gradient, and protein identification was performed using MaxQuant.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Unless otherwise indicated in the figure legends, all experiments were performed in biological triplicate. For DNA fiber and single-cell measurements, the median value for each biological replicate was calculated, and statistical comparisons were performed on these replicate medians (n = 3 independent experiments) rather than pooling individual measurements, thereby appropriately accounting for experimental variability. For most experiments, individual data points are shown with the median of each replicate highlighted. Exceptions include Western blots, flow cytometry, where representative images are shown.

Data normality was first assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For pairwise comparisons, Welch’s t test was applied for normally distributed data or the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. For multiple comparisons, one-way ANOVA with Šídák’s multiple comparisons test was used for normally distributed data or Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons test for non-normally distributed data. For normalized data, where values were normalized by control within each biological replicate, the control was set to a constant (1, variance = 0) and was therefore excluded from group-based comparisons. Instead, experimental groups were compared against the expected value of 1 using a one-sample t test (for normally distributed data) or Wilcoxon signed-rank test (for non-normally distributed data). We have ensured consistent application of parametric versus non-parametric tests based on normality assessments throughout the manuscript.

No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size, and no inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Detailed statistical information, including sample sizes, significance values, and specific tests used, is provided in the figure legends and figures.
