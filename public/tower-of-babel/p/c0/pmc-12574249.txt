Correlation between antimicrobial resistance and virulence in carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae at colonization and infection stages
Authors: Junning He, Yijie Yin, Cheng Li, Ning Xie, Peilin Zhang, Guixia Wang, Xianting Liang, Yongfang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCarbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) has emerged as a critical global public health. To investigate the dynamic changes of drug resistance genes and virulence traits in carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae during colonization-infection transitions, supporting clinical prevention strategies.MethodsA total of 18 cases each of CRKP colonization-to-infection and infection-to-colonization were enrolled from ICU patients. Genomic DNA from 72 isolates was used for PCR detection of resistance genes, virulence genes, and capsular serotype genes among the 72 isolates. KPC-positive isolates underwent Sanger sequencing for subtyping, and the string test was employed to assist in defining the hypervirulent phenotype.ResultsNo significant differences were observed in clinical features between groups. All isolates carried blaKPC−2 without other resistance genes. The colonization-to-infection group showed higher virulence gene detection rates (50.00% vs. 27.78%), with 4 cases exhibiting increased virulence genes during infection. Conversely, 2 cases in the infection-to-colonization group showed reduced virulence genes. Capsular serotyping identified three isolates (4.17%) carrying hypervirulence-associated serotypes: a K1 strain from a blood sample (Case 8) showing virulence gene increase during infection, and two K57 strains (Case 34) lacking virulence genes. 11 hypervirulent CRKP (15.28%) were identified, with 9 from the colonization-to-infection group.ConclusionCRKP maintains stable resistance genes during colonization-infection transitions, but dynamic virulence changes indicate higher infection risks when colonization converts to infection. Enhanced monitoring of virulence and resistance is critical to control hv-CRKP spread in ICUs.Keywords: ICU, Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae, Colonization, Infection, Resistance genes, Virulence genes

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae is a leading opportunistic pathogen causing severe nosocomial infections, including respiratory tract infections, bloodstream infections, and urinary tract infections [1, 2]. The global spread of carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP), characterized by multidrug resistance and mortality rates exceeding 25%, poses a critical public health threat [3, 4]. ICU patients face elevated risks due to immunosuppression, invasive procedures, and broad-spectrum antibiotic use [5, 6], with our hospital data confirming ICUs as CRKP epicenters [7]. CRKP intestinal colonization significantly increases secondary infection risks, while infected strains may revert to colonization, heightening recurrence potential [8]. Current research predominantly focuses on colonization-to-infection transitions, neglecting infection-to-colonization dynamics and emerging challenges from hypervirulent CRKP (hv-CRKP)—strains combining carbapenem resistance with enhanced pathogenicity [9]. Molecular characterization of CRKP strains, particularly the detection of key resistance and virulence genes, is crucial for understanding their pathogenic potential and guiding clinical prevention and control. PCR-based targeted gene detection represents a common and effective method for achieving this objective. This study systematically compares clinical profiles, resistance patterns (blaKPC−2 prevalence), and virulence gene dynamics (rmpA, iucA, peg-344) between colonization-to-infection and infection-to-colonization groups. By analyzing strain homology, mobile genetic elements, and virulence plasticity, we aim to elucidate mechanisms driving CRKP transitions and inform targeted infection control strategies against hv-CRKP dissemination.

Materials and methods

Sources of bacterial strains

18 patients each from CRKP intestinal colonization-to-infection and CRKP infection-to-colonization in the neurosurgical ICU (Neurosurgery ICU, NICU), central ICU, and emergency ICU of the Affiliated Hospital of Chuanbei Medical College were selected from June 1, 2020, to January 1, 2023, and the CRKP strains detected in their intestinal colonization and infection sites were collected. Inclusion criteria: (1) From colonization-to-infection: patients whose first CRKP detection was a positive active rectal swab screening result in the absence of clinical symptoms of intestinal infection (thus confirming an asymptomatic intestinal colonization state), followed by a CRKP infection at any other site. From infection-to-colonization: patients who were CRKP infected and had a negative anal swab screen, and then turned positive by anal swab screen during the subsequent observation period to confirm colonization status. (2) Each patient’s first positive anal swab culture result was included, excluding duplicate strains from the same site in the same patient, and patients with multiple sites of infection were included only in the first occurrence of infection with a positive screen result. Number the patients from CRKP intestinal colonization-to-infection group as cases 1–18 and the patients from CRKP infection-to-colonization as cases 19–36. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College.

Methods

Data collection

Relevant information about patients was collected through the hospital’s electronic medical record system, including (1) general information: age, gender, admission time, discharge time, admission department, underlying diseases (e.g., diabetes mellitus, hypertension, malignant tumors, renal failure, etc.), and type of infected specimen; (2) invasive operations: surgery, endotracheal intubation, urinary tract intubation, and central venous catheterization, etc. (3) Prognosis: improvement, ineffective. Improvement means that the patient’s clinical symptoms, imaging and infection indicators (including white blood cells, C-reactive protein, Procalcitonin and other test indicators) improved, and ineffective means that the patient’s clinical symptoms, signs and infection indicators did not significantly improve or death. (4) Others: Time of delivery of positive anal swab screening, time of delivery of positive CRKP detection at other sites of infection.

Anal swab active screening

Active rectal anal swab screening was performed on patients admitted to the ICU within 48 h of admission, 48–72 h and every subsequent week until screening was positive for CRKP or the patient was discharged/dead. A positive rectal swab CRKP screening result in the absence of clinical symptoms of intestinal infection was determined as intestinal colonization.

Bacterial strain identification and drug sensitivity test

For culture, the collected specimens were inoculated onto blood plates or MacConkey’s medium (Chongqing Pangtong Medical Devices Co., Ltd). Bacterial species were identified using mass spectrometry or VITEK-2compact fully automated microbial identification system. The drug sensitivity test was performed using disk n diffusion, and the results were referred to the Performance Standards for Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing developed by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). Klebsiella pneumoniae.

resistant to any of the three carbapenems, imipenem, meropenem, and ertapenem, were recognized as CRKP strains.

Drug resistance gene detection

The genomic DNA of Klebsiella pneumoniae was extracted using the boiling method as the template for amplification by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The 5 most common resistance genes in China were amplified by PCR: blaKPC, blaOXA48, blaNDM, blaIMP, and blaVIM, respectively. The specificity of all primers was confirmed by BLAST analysis. Primers were synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Biological Company, and sequences are listed in Table 1. A no-template control was included as a negative control. The amplified blaKPC-positive products were analyzed by sequencing using the Sanger method to determine the KPC genotype.

Detection of virulence genes and capsular serotype

The genomic DNA of the bacterial strains was extracted using the same method as previously described. PCR was performed to detect virulence genes, specifically amplifying 3 of the most common virulence genes (rmpA, iucA, peg-344) and 6 capsular serotype-specific genes (K1, K2, K5, K20, K54, and K57). Primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech, and the sequences are listed in Table 1. The protocols for negative controls and primer validation were the same as those described in section "Drug resistance gene detection".

The string test

72 CRKP strains were inoculated on blood agar plates and incubated for 18–24 h at 37℃ in a constant temperature incubator. The colonies were gently picked and stretched using a sterile inoculation loop and the length of the pull was observed. If more than 5 mm of slime pulls were formed, the strain was judged to have high mucilage, i.e., positive for the string test [10].

Definition of hv-CRKPI

In this study, hv-CRKP was defined as isolates fulfilling both of the following criteria: carrying ≥ 2 of the specific virulence genes (rmpA, iucA, peg-344) and testing positive in the string test.

Statistical analysis

The data were statistically analyzed using SPSS27.0 software, age and length of hospitalization measurements were expressed as (mean ± standard deviation), and a t-test was used for comparison between groups. Count data were expressed as the number of cases, rate, or constitutive ratio, and comparisons were made using Fisher’s exact probability method test. For all statistical analyses, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General conditions of patients

There was no statistical difference in gender, age, length of hospitalization and departmental distribution between the two groups. Most of the patients in the two groups were from NICU and CICU, and both patient groups exhibited high rates of poor outcomes (33.33% and 38.89%, respectively), with no statistically significant difference between them.(Table 2).

Infection-related factors

Detection of resistance genes and subtypes

A total of 72 CRKP strains were detected in 36 patients in both groups, and it was determined that both colonizing and infecting strains of the same patient carried KPCase-producing resistance genes by resistance gene detection, and none of them were detected with blaNDM, blaIMP, blaVIM and blaOXA−48. Further genetic sequencing was performed on KPCase-producing strains, and a comparative analysis was conducted with the known blaKPC−2 sequence. The results demonstrated that all 72 isolates exclusively carried the blaKPC−2 resistance gene (100%), with no other genotypes identified.

Detection of virulence genes of strains in the two groups

In the colonization-to-infection group, virulence genes were detected in colonized or infected bacteria in 9 cases (50.00%), of which only one virulence gene was detected in 4 patients, two or more virulence genes were detected in 5 patients, and there were 6 cases of carriers at the time of colonization. In the infection-to-colonization group, a total of 5 (27.78%) patients were detected with virulence genes, of which 4 patients were detected with only one virulence gene and 1 patient was detected with two or more virulence genes. Detection of hv-CRKP strains in the two groups

According to the definition provided in section "Definition of hv-CRKPI", a total of 11 (15.28%) hv-CRKP strains were identified among the 72 CRKP isolates. A total of 9 hv-CRKP strains were detected in 36 CRKP strains in the colonization-to-infection group, of which 8 strains were from the colonization stage of Cases 5, 6, 17 and 18 with the subsequent emergence of the infection stage respectively, and one strain was from the infection stage of Case 8, and virulence gene was not detected in the colonization stage of Case 8. Only 2 strains of hv-CRKP were identified in the infection-to-colonization group, with 1 strain each in the colonized and infected stages of case 28. Overall 11 hvKP strains (15.28%) were hv-CRKP strains, and there was no significant difference in the detection of hv-CRKP strains between the two groups.

The string test

Among the 72 CRKP isolates, 15 (20.83%) were positive for the string test. A strong concordance was observed between the string test and our hv-CRKP definition: all 11 strains identified as hv-CRKP (carrying ≥ 2 virulence genes) were string test positive. Of the remaining 4 string test-positive strains, which were not classified as hv-CRKP, 2 belonged to the K57 serotype (Case 34) and 2 others (Cases 27 and 32) did not carry any of the detected virulence genes. Conversely, 11 strains that carried one virulence gene (8 with only peg-344 and 3 with only iucA) were negative in the string test. Capsule-specific serotype

Only three (4.17%) of the 72 CRKP strains amplified capsular serotypes, including one (1.38%) of type K1 showing virulence gene increase during infection, from a blood specimen of case 8, and this isolate was identified as hv-CRKP. Two strains (2.77%) of type K57 were detected from rectal swab and sputum specimens from the colonization and infection phases of case 34, which were positive in the string test but did not carry virulence genes.

Discussion

In recent years, with the widespread use of antimicrobial drugs, the global emergence of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) has attracted much attention due to their high degree of resistance and widespread. In China, CRKP accounts for about 90% of clinical CRE infections [12], and as an important member of CRE, the problem of CRKP colonization and infection is particularly important. Intestinal colonization is a key precursor for CRKP infections in ICUs [13], with most cases arising from prior colonization rather than nosocomial transmission [14]. Post-infection colonization further complicates recurrence risks. CRKP remains prevalent in ICUs, and emerging strains co-harboring carbapenem resistance and hypervirulence genes (hv-CRKP) threaten to cause severe, untreatable infections [15]. In this study, we analyzed the clinical characteristics, drug resistance mechanisms and virulence mechanisms of two groups of patients from CRKP intestinal colonization to infection and infection to intestinal colonization during their admission to the ICU, and we intended to investigate whether there were any differences in the above factors between CRKP colonization to infection and infection to colonization, intending to provide a basis for the prevention and control of CRKP infection.

CRKP colonization and infection involve multifactorial risks, though studies report inconsistent predictors (e.g., antimicrobial exposure, prior ICU admission, comorbidities). In some studies, antimicrobial exposure has been identified as an independent factor for CRKP colonization and infection, and others include prior ICU admission, renal disease, diabetes mellitus, catheter insertion, and anti-pseudomonas penicillin use [16, 17]. This study found no significant differences in age, invasive procedures, comorbidities, or prognosis between colonization-to-infection and infection-to-colonization groups. Both groups exhibited similar hospitalization durations and infection sites (primarily sputum). Regardless of transition direction, CRKP infections consistently correlated with underlying high-risk factors (e.g., immunosuppression, broad-spectrum antibiotic use), suggesting that patient vulnerability—not transition sequence—drives poor outcomes. These findings highlight the complexity of CRKP epidemiology, emphasizing that colonization and infection share overlapping risk profiles, necessitating comprehensive surveillance rather than isolated intervention strategies.

With the widespread use of carbapenem antimicrobial drugs, new mutant isoforms of the gene encoding the KPC enzyme are constantly appearing. CRKP strains carrying blaKPC−2 have formed a general epidemic trend in China [3]. This study detected identical resistance genes (blaKPC−2) in both 2 groups, confirming blaKPC−2 carbapenemase production as the primary resistance mechanism in our hospital. CRKP resistance genes are commonly located on transmissible genetic elements, such as plasmids and transposons, which facilitate horizontal transmission between strains. Notably, colonizing and infecting isolates from the same patient shared blaKPC−2, indicating horizontal acquisition and clonal spread. Prior studies align with these findings: CRKP resistance profiles are highly conserved between colonization and infection stages [18], with blaKPC−2-bearing plasmids exhibiting self-transfer capacity, a key driver of nosocomial outbreaks [19]. This genetic mobility not only sustains CRKP persistence but also elevates infection risks in colonized patients. The colonizing and infecting bacteria from the same patient in this experiment carried the same resistance genes, and the sharing of this resistance mechanism by colonizing and infecting bacteria means that it is highly likely that these strains have acquired blaKPC−2 through horizontal gene transfer, thus causing transmission in different patients and clinical settings.

The analysis of strain resistance and virulence characteristics in this study was conducted against a well-defined background of clonal homology. In homology analysis using ERIC-PCR, strains are generally considered to belong to the same clone when their similarity coefficient reaches or exceeds 0.85. The genetic relatedness of these strains had been previously analyzed using Enterobacterial Repetitive Intergenic Consensus-PCR (ERIC-PCR) and published in our prior work [11]. Among the 14 patients carrying virulence genes, the colonizing and infecting isolates from 13 patients (92.85%) exhibited a similarity coefficient of >0.85. Specifically, isolates from 10 patients (71.42%) were genetically identical (similarity coefficient = 1.00), while those from 3 patients had similarity coefficients between 0.90 and 0.95. This indicates that in the vast majority of cases, colonization and infection were caused by the same clone, providing a reliable genetic foundation for observing the phenotypic changes described in the present study. Only Case 27 exhibited distant relatedness (similarity coefficient < 0.85). These findings suggest virulence genes may be dynamically acquired or lost via plasmids or other horizontal transfer mechanisms without disrupting clonal lineage integrity [20].Such plasticity implies that even genetically conserved CRKP clones can evolve enhanced pathogenicity through mobile genetic elements, complicating infection control. Continuous genomic surveillance is essential to track virulence-resistance co-evolution in CRKP epidemics.

The virulence genes detected in this study play central roles in the pathogenesis of hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKP). rmpA [21–23] is a key regulator for the overproduction of capsular polysaccharide, leading to the hypermucoviscous phenotype; iucA [24, 25] is the core gene of the aerobactin siderophore system and exhibits high specificity for discriminating hvKP; and peg-344 [24, 26] serves as a highly specific marker for the hvKP virulence plasmid, with its encoded metabolite transporter directly contributing to virulence. Comparative analysis revealed higher virulence gene detection rates (peg-344 >iucA >rmpA) in colonization-to-infection cases versus infection-to-colonization. These virulence genes has previously been shown to exhibit >0.95 accuracy in identifying hvKP strains and is associated with a risk ratio of >25 for serious disease and mortality [24]. Among 18 colonization-to-infection patients, 9 carried ≥ 1 virulence gene, with 4 showing gene amplification during infection. Notably, 3 patients (Cases 8,10,16) exhibited virulence gene acquisition exclusively in infection phases. Case 8 demonstrated all three virulence genes (rmpA, iucA, peg-344) post-infection despite negative colonization screening, while Case 18’s infecting strain gained rmpA compared to colonizers. These findings align with prior evidence linking rmpA to hypervirulence. Virulence genes enhance CRKP adhesion and immune evasion, facilitating intestinal persistence and systemic dissemination. The dynamic acquisition of virulence factors—potentially via plasmid transfer—during colonization-infection transitions underscores their critical role in pathogenicity. Such plasticity suggests that even clonal CRKP strains can evolve heightened virulence through genetic mobility, emphasizing the need for dual surveillance of resistance and virulence markers in high-risk settings.

Among infection-to-colonization cases, 5 patients carried virulence genes, with 2 showing peg-344 loss during transition and 3 remaining unchanged. The consistently low prevalence of virulence genes across both stages suggests that CRKP may downregulate virulence through gene silencing or mutations to sustain asymptomatic persistence. This adaptive plasticity—potentially driven by host microenvironment pressures—enables colonization without immune clearance. Current understanding of infection-to-colonization mechanisms remains limited, particularly regarding virulence attenuation pathways. Future studies employing whole-genome sequencing or transcriptomics could explore the mechanisms of virulence dynamics. Such insights are critical to disrupting CRKP’s adaptive strategies and preventing reservoir formation in high-risk populations.

The definition of hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKP) remains contentious. While some studies define hvKP by the presence of iuc gene [15], others propose multi-gene criteria (e.g., rmpA, rmpA2, peg-344, iucA, and iroB) and one phenotype (hmv) for the definition of hvKP. They found that the presence of iucA or iroB yielded 96% and 97% accuracy, respectively [24]. One of the four virulence genes, iucA, iroB, rmpA, or rmpA2, has also been defined as hvKP [27]. However, the clinical relevance of these markers varies across strains. A hyperviscous phenotype, linked to capsular polysaccharide overproduction (e.g., K1/K2), is another hallmark of hvKP [28]. The hyperviscous phenotype in Klebsiella pneumoniae is traditionally linked to virulence plasmids carrying CPS-regulated genes (rmpA/rmpA2)and siderophore clusters. Among 72 CRKP isolates, 15 (20.83%) exhibited hyperviscosity (string test-positive), including 10 rmpA-positive strains and 2 K57 serotype carriers. Notably, 3 strains lacking rmpA and capsular-specific serotypes still showed hyperviscosity. Since the hyperviscous phenotype was first reported, it has often been assumed to be equivalent to hypervirulent. Recent evidence challenges the assumption that hyperviscosity equals hypervirulence [29], that carriage of the virulence plasmid do not necessarily correlate with the expression of the hyperviscous phenotype, and that detection of the hyperviscous phenotype may not be associated with the expression of the hypervirulent phenotype of that organism.

Combining most of the scholars’ criteria for defining hvKP, hvKP was defined as strains carrying ≥ 2 of three virulence genes (rmpA, peg-344, iucA), supported by string test and capsular serotyping in this experiment. Among 72 CRKP isolates, 11 (15.27%) were identified as hv-CRKP, with higher prevalence in colonization-to-infection (9/36, 25%) versus infection-to-colonization groups (2/36, 5.56%). Of the 18 patients in the colonization-to-infection group, 4 patients harbored hv-CRKP in both stages, while Case 8 showed hv-CRKP exclusively during infection. The two hv-CRKP strains in the infection-to-colonization group originated from the same patient, and the remaining 17 patients were colonized or infected by non-hv-CRKP strains. One study [18] identified 34 (14.97%) of 227 CRKP strains as belonging to both CRKP and hvKp, and another study [30] also identified 46 (17%) of 270 CRKP isolates as hv-CRKP. These findings align with prior studies reporting hv-CRKP rates of 14.97–17.97%, confirming the consistent emergence of convergent resistance-virulence strains in clinical settings. Currently, the distinction between CRKP and hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (hvKP) is increasingly blurred due to resistance-virulence convergence [31]. Convergence of carbapenem resistance and hypervirulent occurs when resistance and virulence plasmids coexist in the same cell or through the emergence of hybrid plasmids carrying both traits [9]. It has been shown [18] that plasmids carrying blaKPC−2 can mobilize unconjugated virulence plasmids from hvKp strains to CRKP strains, and that the widespread presence of KPC-producing integrative plasmids contributes to the emergence and prevalence of hv-CRKP strains. hv-CRKP poses a great threat to human health due to combining multidrug resistance, hypervirulence, and rapid spread. To mitigate transmission, proactive measures are essential: (1) For CRKP-colonized patients, implement strict surveillance to prevent progression to infection; (2) For CRKP-infected patients, monitor gastrointestinal colonization via serial fecal/rectal swab analyses to track resistance-virulence evolution and prevent cross-transmission.

In conclusion, there was no significant difference in the clinical characteristics and prognosis of the two groups of patients from CRKP colonization to infection and infection to colonization in our ICU. The colonizing and infecting bacteria in the same patient were highly homologous to each other and carried the same drug-resistant genes and subtypes. In some patients, virulence genes changed in two phases, with an increase in some virulence genes occurring at the time of conversion from colonization to infection and a decrease in some virulence genes at the time of infection to colonization status. Patients with CRKP colonization can be predicted to be at risk of infection by the monitoring of virulence genes, and it is important to strengthen the epidemiological monitoring and prevention and control of drug resistance and virulence genes of CRKP in key departments such as the ICU. Future multi-center studies with larger sample sizes will integrate MLST or WGS to elucidate plasmid-mediated co-transfer mechanisms of resistance and virulence genes and to investigate transmission dynamics at a higher resolution.
