Aetiology and outcomes of emergency admissions for chronic liver disease in England, 2012–2019: a national cohort study using administrative data
Authors: Jessica King, Cori Campbell, Vikram Bains, James Doidge, Jan Van Der Meulen, Kate Walker, William Bernal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveChronic liver disease (CLD) deaths in the UK have risen fivefold since 1970. People with CLD often present with advanced disease. We investigated trends in aetiology, care, and outcomes of patients with a first emergency admission (FEA) for CLD in England.MethodsIn a retrospective cohort study, we analysed national administrative data of hospital admissions (Hospital Episode Statistics Admitted Patient Care data) to identify patients with a FEA for CLD between April 2012 and March 2019. We used negative binomial, logistic and proportional hazards regression to explore time trends in admission numbers according to population size, demographics, aetiology, readmissions, mortality, transplantation, and the treating clinicians’ specialty.ResultsThere were 83 527 FEAs for CLD during the 7-year study period, with numbers increasing each year. There was no evidence of a time trend when population size was taken into account (p=0.51). Of these FEAs, 65.3% had alcohol-related aetiologies. The proportion of FEAs with viral aetiologies decreased (7.3% in 2012–2014 vs 5.6% in 2016–2019; p<0.001) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) increased (5.8–11.3%; p<0.001), respectively. Mortality within 30 days was 17.3% and 37.3% within 1 year. 1-year all-cause mortality improved over the study period (2012–2014 vs 2016–2018 adjusted HR 0.92 (95% CI 0.90 to 0.95)) but 30-day readmission rate increased (1.04 (95% CI 1.00 to 1.09)). Over 40% of patients did not receive care from a hepatologist/gastroenterologist during the FEA and <1% received liver transplant within 1 year.ConclusionThe number of patients with FEA for CLD increased in line with population size between 2012 and 2019. The dominant aetiology was alcohol-related, but the proportion with MASLD nearly doubled over the study period. There were improvements in survival, but over a third of patients died within a year, and over a third of hospital survivors were readmitted. Many patients did not receive specialist inpatient care or evidence-based interventions, suggesting opportunities to improve survival and resource use.Keywords: CIRRHOSIS, LIVER FAILURE, Treatment Outcome, EPIDEMIOLOGY

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This national study demonstrates more than 10 000 first emergency admissions (FEAs) for CLD annually in England with a continued rise over the study period in line with population size, alongside notable shifts in disease aetiology, though alcohol-related liver disease remains the predominant underlying cause. HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

These findings underscore the need for coordinated action to improve outcomes. As well as addressing the persistently high mortality associated with FEA, efforts should focus on reducing these unplanned admissions through improved early detection and community-based management. Future research should prioritise identifying and overcoming barriers to the uptake of effective secondary care interventions. Introduction

Chronic liver disease (CLD) is a major health threat and has an increasing prevalence in many countries.1 2 In the UK, deaths from CLD have risen almost fivefold since 1970, and most people dying with CLD do so before they are 65.3 People with CLD often first come to medical attention with an emergency admission to hospital when their disease is already advanced.4 Outcomes for such patients are very poor and previous studies have estimated that over a quarter of patients die within 60 days of their first emergency admission (FEA), and nearly 70% of patients who survived an FEA die within 5 years.5

The increase in CLD prevalence is likely to be linked to factors including increasing levels of obesity and alcohol consumption.1 2 In contrast, major advances in the treatment of hepatitis C (HCV) are likely to have reduced the incidence of viral hepatitis.1 2 6 How the incidence, aetiologies and outcome of patients with an FEA for CLD to hospital has changed over time is not known and is a key knowledge gap.

Seeking to address the unacceptably poor outcomes associated with emergency presentations of CLD, a number of ‘care bundles’ of evidence-based interventions have been developed in an effort to reduce mortality and shorten hospital stay.7 These include the early use of antimicrobials for empiric treatment of infection, specific diagnostic investigations including paracentesis, prompt gastroenterologist or hepatologist senior specialist review, and appropriate escalation to critical care. However, it is likely that only a small proportion of patients receive these recommended interventions8 and that not all eligible patients receive appropriate escalation to critical care.9 The overall effect of the adoption of these care bundles on clinical outcomes is currently unquantified.

In patients who survive the initial admission, little is known about postdischarge resource use. Unplanned readmission may be common and associated with increased mortality.10,12 For many patients with CLD presenting during an emergency admission to hospital the use of liver transplantation may be the only intervention likely to prolong life, but the proportion of patients undergoing transplantation is unknown.

In this study we used national administrative health datasets over 7 years to address these gaps in knowledge and investigate the care and outcome of patients diagnosed with CLD during an emergency admission in England. We sought to describe trends over time in the number of patients presenting in this way and their outcomes, exploring their demographics, cause of liver disease, clinical characteristics and specific elements of their care.

Methods

Data

This retrospective cohort study used Hospital Episode Statistics Admitted Patient Care data (HES-APC), a national administrative dataset of all day-case and overnight admissions provided by the English National Health Service (NHS).13 Each episode in HES-APC is a continuous period of care under one senior clinician (ie, a ‘consultant’) at one hospital. Every patient has a unique pseudonymised identifier within HES-APC so all their episodes of care can be linked. HES-APC records contain patient demographic information, including age, socio-economic deprivation, and ethnicity, as well as dates and modes of admission and discharge. For each episode, a primary diagnosis is recorded using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10).14 Up to 19 other diagnoses can be coded in secondary diagnosis fields to capture other relevant conditions or comorbidities. Procedures carried out are coded using the Office of Population Censuses and Surveys Classification of Surgical Operations and Procedures, fourth revision (OPCS-4)15 as well as the procedure date. HES-APC data was linked to Office for National Statistics mortality data for all deaths to 20 September 2020, providing date and cause of death.

Population

Patients resident in England aged 18 years and above were eligible for inclusion in our study if they had an FEA for CLD (see below for definition) between 1 April 2012 and 31 March 2019. All patients were followed up for at least 1 year. An admission was defined as being for CLD if the ICD-10 code in the primary diagnosis field indicated CLD, or if the primary diagnosis was a sign, symptom, complication or cause of CLD, and an indication of CLD was among the secondary diagnoses. A full list of the ICD-10 codes that were used is presented in online supplemental table S1, and the coding is described and discussed in more detail elsewhere, where causes of death are also explored.16 The admission was considered to be a first emergency admission (EA) for CLD if there was no hospital admission (emergency or planned) for CLD in the preceding 5 years. Records for the same patient cannot be linked when patient identifiers are not submitted to HES-APC by hospitals for reasons of confidentiality. Changes to confidentiality rules were made by NHS England during the financial year 2017/2018, which temporarily expanded the list of ICD-10 and OPCS-4 codes regarded as legally restricted. These changes were reversed in the following financial year.17

Patient and clinical characteristics

The period of admission was categorised by financial year (1 April to 31 March) for comparing numbers of patients over time, and by era for comparing their demographics, clinical characteristics, care and outcomes over time. We defined three eras a priori: 1 April 2012 to 31 March 2014, 1 April 2014 to 31 March 2016, and 1 April 2016 to 31 March 2019. These eras were defined in order to divide the 7 years (1 April 2012 to 31 March 2019) into three time periods of roughly equal size, while avoiding dividing whole years of data between eras. Age was categorised into 18–34, 35–49, 50–64, 65–79 and ≥80 years. Socio-economic deprivation of patients was described using the Index of Multiple Deprivation, which provides an area-level measure of deprivation derived from information about income, education, employment, crime, and the living environment for areas typically including about 600 households or 1500 inhabitants.18 These areas were grouped into quintiles of the national distribution. The Royal College of Surgeons Charlson Comorbidity Score was used to determine the number of comorbid conditions for each patient,19 excluding liver disease and liver cancer. Complications of interest were ascites, gastrointestinal bleeding, septicaemia, and peritonitis, and these were defined using ICD-10 diagnosis codes (codes are listed in online supplemental table S2) recorded during the FEA. The specific source of septicaemia was not determined. Aetiologies were defined by the presence of diagnosis codes in the FEA, and were categorised into five groups: alcohol, viral, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), autoimmune and other specified aetiologies (online supplemental table S3).16 20 21

Care during FEA

Five processes of care were identified during the FEA. These were paracentesis, endoscopic variceal bleeding treatment, transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) procedure, invasive ventilation, and whether the patient was under the care of a senior hepatologist or gastroenterologist. The first four were defined by the presence of OPCS-4 procedure codes in any episode of the FEA (list of codes given in online supplemental table S4). The final care process was defined using the treatment specialty (specialty in which the senior clinician was working, not contracted) in the HES-APC records of any episodes of the FEA.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were death during the FEA, death within 30 days of the emergency admission, and emergency readmission or any readmission (including planned readmissions) within 30 days of discharge from the FEA. Secondary outcomes were all-cause mortality, CLD mortality, and liver transplantation, all within 1 year of the FEA. Deaths were defined as caused by CLD if the underlying cause was an indication of CLD (diagnosis codes in lists A or B of online supplemental table S1). The underlying cause of death is defined as the disease or injury that initiated the train of events directly leading to death and is coded by the Office for National Statistics from information on the death certificate.17

Statistical methods

We used a negative binomial regression model with financial year of admission as a continuous independent variable adjusting for the midyear estimates of the size of the population aged 18 years and above in England to test for a trend over time in numbers of FEA for CLD.22 χ² tests were used to test whether patient and clinical characteristics in the FEA varied according to era, and logistic regression models to test for trends in primary outcomes by era. For secondary outcomes, we used a Cox proportional hazards model to compare all-cause mortality by era, and a subdistribution hazard model with death as a competing risk to compare liver transplantation by era. We checked for collinearity between the variables in the model by comparing the width of the CIs in the univariable and multivariable models and found that the correlation between the variables was not large enough to affect the width of CIs around any of the HRs substantially, which demonstrated that collinearity was not an issue. In addition, we assessed the proportional hazards assumption for the effect of each era on each outcome using Nelson-Aalen plots and found no clear evidence of non-proportional hazards.

To compare CLD mortality by era, we used a subdistribution hazard model with non-liver-related death as a competing risk. We modelled these long-term outcomes without and with adjustment for patient sex, age category (see above), ethnicity, number of comorbidities, and socio-economic deprivation. We plotted Kaplan-Meier curves for all-cause mortality and cumulative incidence curves for other long-term outcomes.

Role of the funding source

The work was performed as part of the Management of Chronic Liver Disease admitted as an Emergency project (National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR) 132969). The funder of this study (NIHR) had no role in the study design, collection, analysis, or interpretation of data, or the writing or decision to submit the paper for publication.

Of the included patients, 34 578 (41.4%) had ascites coded in the record of their FEA, 30 237 (36.2%) gastrointestinal bleeding, and 1570 (1.9%) peritonitis. In total 54 516 (65.3%) had an aetiology of CLD that was alcohol-related, with no evidence that this proportion changed over time. However, the proportions of patients having aetiologies related to MASLD, autoimmune disorders or other specified aetiologies increased (5.8% to 11.3%, 3.9% to 4.6% and 3.6% to 5.2%, respectively from era 1 to era 3), while the proportion of patients with a viral aetiology decreased over the eras from 7.3% to 5.6% (p<0.001 across eras; table 1).

A total of 27 167 (32.5%) of patients underwent paracentesis, 7577 (9.2%) endoscopic bleeding treatment, and 222 (0.3%) a TIPS procedure during their FEA, with evidence that the proportion of patients receiving each of these treatments decreased over time from era 1 to era 3 (32.8% to 32.1% (p=0.0501), 9.6% to 8.6% (p<0.001) and 0.3% to 0.2% (p=0.056), respectively; table 1). Overall, 44 772 (53.6%) of patients were treated by a senior gastroenterologist or hepatologist, and this proportion increased across the three eras from 50.0% to 56.1% (p<0.001; table 1).

Of the 83 527 included patients, 13 830 (16.6%) died during the FEAs and 14 474 (17.3%) died within 30 days of the FEA (table 2). There was evidence of a small decrease in era 3 compared with era 1 in the odds of dying during the FEA (crude OR 0.94, p=0.01), but not at 30 days (OR 0.99, p=0.826). 24.0% of patients were readmitted as an emergency within 30 days of discharge, and the overall (including planned readmissions) 30-day readmission rate was 34.4%. There was a trend (p=0.065) toward an increase over time in crude odds of emergency readmission within 30 days, in both era 2 (OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.10) and era 3 (OR 1.04, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.09) compared with era 1. There was a similar magnitude of increase in all readmissions (emergency or planned) within 30 days (table 2, p=0.041).

Discussion

We found that since 2012 in England there has been an increasing number of patients with an FEA with CLD. However, we found no evidence of increase in the incidence of FEA when we took the corresponding increase in size of the population aged 18 years and older into account. Mortality during the admission after admission was very high, but on adjusted analysis showed some improvement over time, with modest improvements in all-cause mortality during the emergency admission and within a year. We found that alcohol related CLD remained the most common cause of illness but noted important changes over time in other aetiologies of disease. More recently admitted patients were less likely to have CLD with a viral cause, and more likely to have MASLD.

This increase in the proportion of patients with CLD with FEA reported with MASLD is a significant concern and parallels other national data.2 20 In the UK, there has been an increase in the incidence of metabolic liver disease, resulting in an estimated prevalence of early-stage disease of up to 18% of the adult population, of whom the great majority remain undiagnosed.23 24 This will inevitably lead to an increase in more advanced MASLD-related CLD in the near future.2 25

We assessed elements of the recommended care bundles for acute admissions with CLD and found that the proportion of patients seen by an appropriate specialist—a senior hepatologist or gastroenterologist—increased from 50% in the first era (2012/2013–2013/2014) of admissions to 56% in the latest (2016/2017–2017/2018). However, even in the more recent years, more than 4 out of 10 patients admitted with a complication of CLD were not managed by an appropriate specialist. A further care bundle requirement is to perform diagnostic paracentesis in all patients with ascites to identify spontaneous bacterial peritonitis. We found that paracentesis was performed in only a third of patients with CLD and there was no clinically significant increase in its use over time. Our findings reinforce the recent calls for greater utilisation of care bundles,7 packages of simple interventions that have been shown, when combined, to improve survival. However, there are evidently significant challenges to their implementation as they continue to be used only in a minority of emergency admissions.8 26

Other interventions with established effectiveness were even less commonly used. For example, only a very small proportion (0.3%) of patients had a TIPS procedure during their emergency admission, mirroring the results of a recent national audit and this also decreased over time despite UK guidance recommending its use as rescue therapy for recurrent variceal bleeding.27 28 Recent international guidelines also recommend TIPS for both rescue therapy and prevention of rebleeding in patients considered to be at high risk.29 The reasons that TIPS is only rarely performed in England need to be better understood. Similarly, liver transplantation remained a rare intervention with 0.7% of patients receiving a transplant within a year of their admission. Liver transplantation is the only intervention with unequivocal survival benefit in advanced CLD, and its low utilisation in patients with a very high rate of CLD-related deaths is a concern and is explored in detail elsewhere.30 Potential increases in transplantation use are limited given the relatively low total number of liver transplantations carried out in England (around 750 each year), and the lack of options for their increase, compared with the number of patients with FEA. Focus must therefore be on improvement in non-transplant interventions—particularly prevention and early detection of evolving CLD.

Finally, it was very common for patients to be readmitted quickly following their FEA. Nearly one in four of those who were discharged alive had another emergency admission within 30 days, rising to one in three when planned readmissions were also included. Though studies from outside the UK have indicated that liver-specific postdischarge coordinated care packages may reduce readmission rates and resource use,31 32 their utilisation in in the UK appears sporadic.33

This is one of few studies that captures emergency admissions for CLD on a national scale and for all aetiologies. The administrative hospital data used are of high quality with very good coverage of acute care provided in England, and contain rich clinical information, including key patient characteristics, specialist liver treatments, emergency readmissions, liver transplantations, and mortality outside hospital. A limitation of this work is the duration of the study period which predates the COVID-19 pandemic. Our data are censored in 2020 as trends during the pandemic years would not meaningfully reflect standard of emergency care trends due to the major and prolonged disruption of secondary care during the pandemic. Further, linked data for relevant post-COVID years which would reflect more normal standards of care are not currently available for analysis. There is evidence that in the UK following the pandemic there have been major increases in alcohol-related harm, including deaths from liver disease, and this is likely to have impacted the number and pattern of emergency admissions with CLD.34 35

There are some further limitations in the use of routine hospital data. We are reliant on diagnostic codes, and so cannot identify with absolute accuracy which patients were admitted for their CLD. Admissions were only considered to be for CLD if the primary diagnosis field indicated CLD, or if the primary diagnosis was a sign, symptom, complication or cause of CLD, and an indication of CLD was among the secondary diagnoses. Any choice of combination of diagnostic codes will be a compromise between specificity (minimising the inclusion of patients who were admitted with CLD rather than for CLD) and sensitivity (minimising the exclusion of patients truly admitted for CLD). To address these concerns, we have systematically developed and rigorously validated algorithms to identify these patients, which are discussed elsewhere.16 However, a further limitation of the data source used is that it cannot be used to assess liver disease severity with the granularity of scores such as Model for End-stage Liver Disease (MELD) or the Child-Pugh classification.

We did not link inpatient hospital records to patients’ primary care or outpatient records. This means that we do not have information about a possible diagnosis of CLD solely either made in primary care or at outpatient visits. However, evidence from another national study in England suggests that the majority of patients with a FEA for alcohol-related liver disease do not have a diagnosis made in primary care in the year before their admission.36 It is concerning that the number of patients with a FEA for CLD is increasing, especially because mortality after such a presentation is extremely high, demonstrating the key importance of improving early diagnosis of evolving CLD in primary care. The time trends in aetiology we found reflect wider patterns of change in risk factors for CLD. Although the proportion of patients with an alcohol aetiology has not changed over the study period, it is important to note that the number has progressively increased. The decrease in both the proportion and number of cases with viral disease is likely to reflect the success of UK programmes for early detection and treatment of viral hepatitis, particularly HCV, and the efficacy of the current treatment regimens.6 Data from the UK Heath Security Agency demonstrate that between 2015 and 2020 the prevalence of HCV-related CLD has decreased by 50%, and HCV-related deaths by 31%.37

In conclusion, our results show that since 2012 in England an increasing number of people have FEAs with CLD, but this increase is in line with increases in population size. Mortality during and after FEA is very high, though we found small improvement over time. However, multiple interventions recommended in national evidence-based guidelines continued to be underused. There is thus clear scope to apply these more effectively and address the unacceptably high mortality that continues to affect this patient group.
