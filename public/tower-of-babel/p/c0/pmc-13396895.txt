Effects of operating parameters on bubble collapse temperature and sonochemical product yields in dual‑frequency acoustic cavitation
Authors: Liang Lv, Peng Zheng, Bing Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work presents a single‑bubble model for dual‑frequency acoustic cavitation in an O2 environment. Dual-frequency acoustic cavitation generates extremely high temperatures in liquids, thereby driving complex sonochemical reactions. However, a direct quantitative link between operating parameters and sonochemical product yields has not been fully established. In this study, we systematically quantify the relationships between the maximum bubble temperature (Tmax) and a range of key operating parameters, including acoustic field settings (power allocation ratio, phase difference, frequency combination, and excitation amplitude) and initial bubble conditions (initial water vapor mole fraction and initial bubble radius). Using Tmax as an intermediate variable, we then establish quantitative correlations between these parameters and the yields of chemical products (total chemical species, H2, H2O2, and •OH). Numerical simulations are performed for multiple dual-frequency combinations (20 + 40, 20 + 140, 20 + 200, 40 + 140, 40 + 200, and 140 + 200 kHz) while systematically varying each parameter. The results reveal that the sensitivity of Tmax to the power allocation ratio and phase difference is strongest under low-frequency combinations and weakens under high total acoustic pressure amplitude. A “high-frequency-dominant region” is identified, in which the specific low-frequency value no longer affects Tmax. The initial water vapor mole fraction exerts a noticeable modulating effect on Tmax at low acoustic pressures, but this effect is progressively suppressed as the excitation amplitude increases, except for certain frequency combinations at higher acoustic pressures. Tmax (approximately 3000–10190 K) determines the product yields, which follow distinct patterns. The overall trend of sonochemical yields as a function of Tmax remains consistent across different initial bubble radii, although the specific transition temperatures for each product vary with the initial radius; the initial radius of 2 μm is chosen as a representative case for the subsequent detailed analysis. H2 exhibits a three-stage pattern: “low plateau, sharp jump, slow saturation”, with a threshold temperature of 5818 K and the sharp increase occurring between 5818 K and 7260 K; H2O2 shows a unimodal distribution with a peak at 6210 K; •OH displays a multi-peak oscillation pattern with a peak at 6354 K. At 7237 K, the growth rate of the total yield of chemical species decelerates, marking a transition from reaction control to dissipation control. Based on these findings, we propose model-dependent optimal temperature windows for target products: approximately 7260 K for H2, 6210 K for H2O2, and 6354 K for •OH, with the understanding that these values are derived from the present single-bubble model under an O2 atmosphere and serve as theoretical references rather than universal experimental guidelines. This study provides a theoretical basis for optimizing operating parameters to understand the selectivity of dual-frequency ultrasonic cavitation technology in the context of green hydrogen production and pollutant degradation.Keywords: Dual-frequency acoustic cavitation, Bubble collapse temperature, Operating parameters, Sonochemical products, Model-dependent temperature windows

Introduction

Acoustic cavitation refers to the physical phenomenon in which tiny bubbles undergo formation, growth, oscillation, and violent collapse in a liquid under a strong ultrasonic field [1], [2]. Upon bubble collapse, the inertia of the liquid drives the bubble wall inward at high velocity, generating extreme temperatures and pressures inside the bubble. Temperatures can reach 5000–20000 K, and pressures can range from hundreds to thousands of atmospheres [1], [3], [4]. Such extreme conditions trigger the thermal dissociation of water molecules, producing highly reactive species such as hydroxyl radicals (•OH), hydrogen atoms (H•), and oxygen atoms (O), which in turn initiate a complex chemical reaction network known as sonochemistry [5], [6], [7].

Based on the above mechanism, acoustic cavitation demonstrates broad application prospects across many fields. In environmental remediation, the strongly oxidizing radicals generated by cavitation bubbles can effectively degrade organic pollutants in water, including phenols, dyes, pesticides, and pharmaceutical residues, and thus it is widely used in wastewater treatment and soil remediation [8], [9]. Studies show that sonochemical degradation efficiency increases significantly with decreasing frequency and increasing acoustic pressure amplitude, providing guidance for process parameter optimization [10]. In the energy sector, acoustic cavitation enables hydrogen production via water splitting, offering a novel route for green hydrogen energy [5], [6], [11]. This technique is environmentally friendly, energy-efficient, and sustainable, making it particularly suitable for coupling with renewable energy sources. For further applications of acoustic cavitation, readers are referred to reviews [12], [13], [14].

Nevertheless, conventional single-frequency ultrasonic excitation still faces several critical challenges in practical applications. First, under single-frequency excitation, the dynamic behavior of cavitation bubbles is relatively simple and the energy conversion efficiency is limited, resulting in yields of reactive radicals and target products that are insufficient for industrial-scale applications [5], [7], [11]. Second, the controllability of cavitation effects is poor. Single-frequency ultrasound allows adjustment of only two parameters (i.e., frequency and power) from the perspective of the excitation field, offering limited degrees of freedom for fine control over bubble dynamics and reaction pathways [3], [4], [15]. The size ranges of sonochemically active bubbles vary significantly with frequency [2], [11], indicating that frequency selection plays a decisive role in reaction pathways. However, a single-frequency system cannot simultaneously meet the selective synthesis requirements for multiple products. Third, energy efficiency requires improvement: the spatial distribution of acoustic energy under single-frequency excitation is often inhomogeneous due to pronounced standing wave effects, resulting in uneven cavitation activity within the reactor and thus reducing the overall energy utilization efficiency [16].

To overcome the above limitations, dual-frequency/multi-frequency ultrasonic excitation technology has been developed. Compared with single-frequency ultrasound, dual-frequency ultrasound, by superimposing two acoustic waves of different frequencies, can significantly enhance cavitation effects and generate synergistic gains [17], [18]. The physical origin of this synergy lies in the marked enhancement of the nonlinear characteristics of bubble dynamics under dual-frequency excitation. When two acoustic waves of different frequencies are superimposed, the oscillating behavior of bubbles is no longer a simple linear superposition of single-frequency responses; instead, it exhibits richer dynamic behaviors, such as enhanced nonlinear oscillations [19], subharmonics [20], bubble–bubble interactions [21], and combination frequency responses [22]. Numerical calculations indicate that the acoustic scattering cross-section of bubbles under dual-frequency excitation contains richer subharmonic and ultraharmonic components [23]. These additional frequency components increase the channels for energy exchange between bubbles and the acoustic field, allowing more bubbles to reach resonance conditions over a wider frequency range, thereby enhancing both the probability and intensity of cavitation events [24]. At the same time, dual-frequency excitation can effectively suppress chaotic bubble oscillations, improving system controllability [25]. From an energy distribution perspective, the critical radius for bubble interface instability under dual-frequency excitation depends primarily on the total acoustic pressure amplitude rather than on frequency, and the stable region under dual-frequency excitation is narrower than that under single-frequency excitation [26]. Moreover, the parametric map of bubble sphericity stability under dual-frequency excitation has been constructed using GPU-accelerated large-scale numerical simulations [27].

A wealth of experimental evidence confirms that dual-frequency excitation significantly enhances sonochemical yields. For example, Feng et al. [28] found that a dual-frequency combination of 28 and 870 kHz increased sonochemical yield by a factor of 1.5–3.4, with the synergistic effect more pronounced under orthogonal transducer arrangement. Similarly, a study of different frequency combinations on H2O2 yield showed that the combination of 28 and 970 kHz reached a synergy coefficient as high as 3.04, and this effect was closely related to transducer arrangement and acoustic power [29]. Regarding reactive oxygen species generation, dual-frequency ultrasound at 0.5 and 1 MHz combined with TiO2 nanoparticles significantly enhanced the generation of •OH radicals, offering a new route for sonodynamic therapy [30]. Furthermore, coupling dual-frequency ultrasound with persulfate for the treatment of petroleum-contaminated soil increased the total petroleum hydrocarbon degradation rate by 34.7%, demonstrating the potential of dual-frequency ultrasound in soil remediation [31]. In wastewater treatment, Fletcher et al. [32] employed dual-frequency ultrasound with 20 kHz combined with 37 kHz and 80 kHz to degrade bisphenol A, achieving a removal rate as high as 94.2% within 40 min, with COD removal rates reaching 67.7% and 64.2%, respectively, indicating that dual-frequency ultrasound significantly enhanced degradation efficiency. In the field of food processing, dual-frequency ultrasound also exhibits unique advantages: Jin et al. [33] combined dual-frequency ultrasound with electrolyzed water to treat Agaricus bisporus mushrooms. This treatment reduced the α-helix content of polyphenol oxidase by 6.3% and the β‑turn content by 11.2%. The relative enzyme activity remained stable at approximately 60% over a 7‑day storage period, thereby effectively inhibiting enzymatic browning. In the biomedical field, Pan et al. [34] developed a dual-frequency ultrasound-assisted thrombolysis technique using 300 kHz and 1 MHz, achieving a thrombus dissolution rate of approximately 90% within 15 min while reducing the drug dose to 10% of the conventional level.

Throughout the above-mentioned studies on sonochemical enhancement by dual-frequency ultrasound, a central physical quantity that emerges is the bubble collapse temperature. The influence of temperature on sonochemical product yields constitutes a critical bridge connecting macroscopic operating parameters with microscopic chemical products. Specifically, factors such as the acoustic field parameters, liquid environment, and gas environment first affect the bubble collapse temperature, which in turn modulates the chemical yields. Yasui et al. [35] investigated the yield of oxidizing species in an air bubble and reported an optimal bubble temperature of approximately 5500 K. In their other work [36], [37], the maximum bubble temperature was predicted to range from 1380 to 8950 K. Merouani et al. [1] also observed that the yield of •OH in an oxygen bubble reached a maximum at 5200 ± 200 K. Furthermore, Merouani et al. [38] demonstrated that the optimal temperature for •OH yield in an argon bubble depends on the ultrasound frequency: approximately 4800 K at 213 kHz, 4200 K at 355 kHz, 4000 K at 515 kHz, and 3260 K at 647 kHz. These results indicate that the competition between the formation and consumption reactions of •OH is the core mechanism determining its optimal temperature window. Similarly, the effect of bubble collapse temperature on H2 yield has been investigated. Kerboua and Hamdaoui [4] obtained an optimal temperature of approximately 6000 K for H2 yield in an oxygen bubble. Further work by Rashwan et al. [5], [6] confirmed that the hydrogen production rate is nearly zero below 3000 K and increases significantly at 6000 K; however, very high temperatures (>8000 K) may lead to a decrease or leveling off of the net yield, a phenomenon closely related to the surrounding gas atmosphere. Lv et al. [11] provided the optimal temperatures for hydrogen yield in an oxygen bubble under different ultrasound frequencies: approximately 5267 K at 140 kHz, 4813 K at 213 kHz, 4626 K at 355 kHz, and 3856 K at 515 kHz. For a systematic review of the relationship between bubble collapse temperature and interfacial chemistry, the reader is referred to Yusof et al. [39].

Current theoretical studies on dual-frequency acoustic cavitation are mostly based on equations such as the Keller-Miksis equation, focusing on the effects of ultrasonic parameters and initial bubble radius on bubble dynamics (e.g., combination resonance, stability, Bjerknes forces). However, investigations from the perspective of chemical reaction coupling, specifically analyzing the influence of collapse temperature on yields of products such as •OH and H2O2, remain limited. Kerboua and Hamdaoui [40] developed a dual-frequency oxygen bubble model, analyzed nonlinear responses using kinetic parameters, collapse ratio, and average velocity, and established a relationship between compression ratio and total free radical yield based on nine data points. In another study, they found that pressure work is the primary energy input in a dual-frequency sonochemical reactor, while diffusive heat transfer and enthalpy loss from water vapor condensation are the main dissipation pathways [41]. Lv et al. [7], building on Merouani’s work, developed a dual-frequency sonochemical model to analyze the effects of power allocation ratio, frequency combination, and excitation amplitude on total free radical yield and product fraction, and reported the temperature dependence of the yield, with a focus on the dual-frequency cavitation enhancement effect. However, that study did not analyze the sensitivity to phase difference or initial radius, and the parameter ranges were limited; collapse temperature was considered only between 2400 and 6000 K, which fails to cover the typical values, and the variation of mole fraction with temperature was constrained. Although another study by Lv et al. [42] examined the effects of phase difference and amplitude on maximum bubble radius, oxidant yield, and •OH mole fraction, it did not explore the effect of collapse temperature on yield.

Thus, existing studies remain at discrete stages and have not yet established a structured framework linking operating parameters to product yields. In response to these gaps, this paper aims to: (1) systematically investigate the effects of key operating parameters (including power allocation ratio, phase difference, frequency combination, acoustic amplitude, initial bubble radius, and initial water vapor mole fraction) on bubble collapse temperature over a wide parameter space; (2) use Tmax as an intermediate variable to construct a framework linking operating parameters to products such as H2, H2O2, and •OH; and (3) identify characteristic temperature thresholds for different products and transition points in the reaction network. Based on this framework, we attempt to establish a model-based pathway from operating parameters to target products, providing theoretical reference for parameter optimization of dual-frequency acoustic cavitation in applications such as green hydrogen production and pollutant degradation.

Results and discussion

This work systematically establishes the quantitative relationships between key operating parameters (power allocation ratio, phase difference, frequency combination, excitation amplitude, initial water vapor mole fraction, and initial bubble radius) and the maximum bubble temperature during acoustic cavitation. Using Tmax as an intermediate variable, a correlation framework is constructed linking these parameters to the yields of chemical products (H2, H2O2, •OH). By elucidating the cascade from these operating parameters through bubble temperature to chemical yields, this study provides a quantitative reference for the selection of these parameters in later experimental studies. All numerical calculations in this work are performed over 30 ultrasound cycles. To eliminate the influence of initial transient effects, the data from the first eight cycles are discarded, and only temperatures at the moment of bubble collapse (i.e., at the minimum radius) and the yields of chemical species from the last 22 cycles are used as the basis for subsequent analyses of variation patterns. By extracting and analyzing characteristic parameters from these steady-state cycles, the dynamic behavior of cavitation bubbles under dual-frequency excitation can be more accurately revealed.

The time history of bubble radius under dual-frequency excitation is displayed in Fig. 1(a). Under dual-frequency excitation, the oscillation behavior of the bubble exhibits distinct stages. Initially, the bubble is in a positive pressure region and has not yet expanded. As the negative pressure phase arrives, a pressure gradient forms across the bubble wall, driving gradual expansion. With the continuous increase in ultrasonic pressure, the bubble expansion rate accelerates and the bubble reaches its maximum radius. When the acoustic pressure switches to the positive pressure phase, the bubble rapidly contracts and collapses, and the radius sharply decreases to a minimum. At this moment, the pressure inside the bubble far exceeds that of the surrounding environment, triggering a new rebound expansion, while the internal pressure subsequently drops. When the internal pressure falls below the external pressure, the bubble is recompressed. This cycle repeats, with the bubble sequentially undergoing expansion, collapse, rebound, and recompression. In each oscillation cycle, the peak bubble radius gradually decreases and approaches the initial radius until the next negative pressure phase initiates the dynamic process.

The dual-frequency excitation sonochemical model proposed in this work is based on the study of Kalmár [15], whereas the commonly used dual-frequency excitation sonochemical model is based on the work of Merouani [7], [40], [41]. Fig. 1(a) and (b) show the predictions of the two models for bubble radius and bubble collapse temperature, respectively. The two models yield consistent results, which corroborates the accuracy of the present model. In the following sections, we further validate our model by examining the effect of bubble collapse temperature on the yields of chemical species inside the bubble.

The evolution of chemical species inside the bubble during the main collapse phase of the 10th cycle is depicted in Fig. 1(c). The calculated time is based on the period determined by the lower frequency under dual-frequency excitation. As seen in the figure, the bubble temperature rises to approximately 6150 K at the moment of collapse. This high-temperature environment significantly promotes the thermal decomposition of water vapor and triggers various chemical reactions during the final stage of collapse. As seen in Fig. 1(c), due to the extremely high temperature attained during collapse, the number of moles of water vapor decreases markedly, indicating substantial consumption. At the end of collapse, the main chemical products generated inside the bubble include H2, H•, O, and •OH radicals. Among them, the yield of •OH is 1.11 × 10−15 mol, higher than those of H2 (1.37 × 10−16 mol), HO2• (3.34 × 10−16 mol), O (1.05 × 10−16 mol), and H2O2 (3.41 × 10−16 mol), and even higher than those of O3 (3.98 × 10−17mol) and H• (2.56 × 10−17 mol).

Effects of operating parameters on the maximum bubble temperature

In a dual-frequency excitation field, the maximum temperature at bubble collapse (Tmax) is a key indicator of cavitation efficiency, directly affecting sonochemical yields, material surface treatment outcomes, and biomedical treatment efficacy. Compared with single-frequency excitation, dual-frequency excitation, through the synergistic coupling of two frequencies, significantly enhances bubble nonlinear oscillation and thus achieves higher collapse temperatures. Therefore, it is necessary to thoroughly investigate the influence of these operating parameters on Tmax, to reveal the underlying physical mechanisms, and to provide a theoretical basis for controlling cavitation effects via parameter optimization in applications. This section systematically analyzes the effects of key operating parameters, including power allocation ratio, phase difference, frequency combination, initial bubble radius, and initial water vapor mole fraction, on Tmax.

Effects of power allocation ratio on the maximum bubble temperature

The influence of the power allocation ratio (N) on the maximum bubble temperature (Tmax) is demonstrated in Fig. 2. Under a constant acoustic pressure amplitude Pe = 1.5 atm, the effect of N on Tmax for different frequency combinations is presented in Fig. 2(a). Under 20 + 40 kHz dual-frequency excitation, Tmax first increases slowly with N and then decreases, reaching a maximum of 6148 K at N = 1.06. Overall, the variation of Tmax under this frequency combination is relatively small, fluctuating between 5766 and 6148 K, indicating that under dual-low-frequency excitation, the temperature sensitivity to N is low.

Influence of power allocation ratio (N) on the maximum bubble temperature (Tmax) under different frequency combinations (20 + 40, 20 + 140, 20 + 200, 40 + 140, 40 + 200, and 140 + 200 kHz) and total acoustic pressures: (a) Pe = 1.5 atm, (b) Pe = 2 atm, (c) Pe = 2.5 atm, and (d) Pe = 3 atm, with phase difference φ = 0, and initial bubble radiusR0 = 2 μm.

For combinations containing a high-frequency component (20 + 140, 20 + 200, 40 + 140, and 40 + 200 kHz), Tmax first decreases, then increases, and finally decreases as N increases, with the maximum values all occurring at N = 0.1 (low-frequency dominant): 6155, 6112, 5608, and 5507 K, respectively. This common trend indicates that the energy focusing effect is optimal when the amplitude of the low-frequency component is 10 times that of the high-frequency component.

Under the dual-high-frequency combination 140 + 200 kHz, Tmax first increases and then decreases as N increases, reaching a maximum of 4952 K at N = 0.81. The overall temperature level is approximately 1100–2500 K lower than that of combinations containing a low-frequency component, confirming the decisive role of the low-frequency component in energy focusing.

Further observation reveals that, except for the dual-low-frequency combination (20 + 40 kHz), Tmax curves for other dual-frequency excitations converge at large N. This convergence trend depends mainly on the high-frequency component involved in the coupling, and is herein termed the “high-frequency-dominant region”. Specifically, when N > 2.73, Tmax curves for the 20 + 200, 40 + 200, and 140 + 200 kHz combinations converge in the range of 3290–4255 K; when N > 2.48, the curves for the 20 + 140 and 40 + 140 kHz combinations converge in the range of 4050–5080 K. This indicates that under high-frequency-dominant conditions, the specific frequency value of the low-frequency component has a negligible effect on Tmax.

The influence of increasing excitation amplitude on the variation of Tmax with N is shown in Fig. 2. In general, as the excitation amplitude increases, Tmax shows an overall upward trend. Under 20 + 40, 20 + 140, and 20 + 200 kHz excitations, when N is small (the “enhancement region”), the effect of N on Tmax is stronger; when N is large (the “weakening region”), the effect becomes weaker. The difference between the local maximum and minimum temperatures within the enhancement and weakening regions reflects the fluctuation amplitude of Tmax and the degree to which N influences it: the larger the difference, the more significant the influence, and vice versa.

Taking the 20 + 200 kHz combination as an example, at excitation amplitudes Pe = 1.5, 2, 2.5, and 3 atm, the boundary N values separating the enhancement and weakening regions are 1.52, 2.28, 2.62, and 3.38, respectively, showing a gradual increasing trend. In the enhancement region (N less than the boundary), the differences between the local maximum and minimum temperatures (i.e., the fluctuation amplitude) are 1606, 1783, 2430, and 4483 K, respectively, indicating that the regulatory effect of N gradually strengthens; in the weakening region (N greater than the boundary), the corresponding differences are 782, 329, 127, and 66 K, respectively, indicating that the regulatory effect gradually weakens.

Under 40 + 140, 40 + 200, and 140 + 200 kHz excitations, as the excitation amplitude increases, the influence of N on Tmax gradually weakens, which is manifested as a significant reduction in temperature fluctuation. Taking 140 + 200 kHz as an example, at excitation amplitudes Pe = 1.5, 2, 2.5, and 3 atm, the fluctuations in Tmax induced by N are 1629, 702, 370, and 240 K, respectively.

Effects of phase difference on the maximum bubble temperature

How the phase difference (φ) affects the maximum bubble temperature (Tmax) is illustrated in Fig. 3. Under a constant acoustic pressure amplitude Pe = 1.5 atm, the variation of Tmax with phase difference is presented in Fig. 3(a). Under 20 + 40 kHz dual-frequency excitation, Tmax exhibits a pronounced V-shaped fluctuation with φ, reaching a minimum (≈ 2700 K) at φ = 0.5π and a maximum (≈ 6481 K) between φ = 1.27π and 1.59π, showing the largest fluctuation amplitude. This indicates that dual-low-frequency excitation is highly sensitive to φ, and a proper choice of φ can more than double the temperature.

Influence of phase difference (φ) on the maximum bubble temperature (Tmax) under different frequency combinations (20 + 40, 20 + 140, 20 + 200, 40 + 140, 40 + 200, and 140 + 200 kHz) and total acoustic pressures: (a) Pe = 1.5 atm, (b) Pe = 2 atm, (c) Pe = 2.5 atm, and (d) Pe = 3 atm, with power allocation ratio N = 1, and initial bubble radiusR0 = 2 μm.

Under 20 + 140 kHz dual-frequency excitation, Tmax shows an approximately symmetric pattern about φ = π, with minima at φ = 0 and 2π (≈4033 K) and maxima at φ = 0.28π and 1.51π (≈4835 K). Under 20 + 200 kHz dual-frequency excitation, Tmax fluctuates more dramatically, with a trough near φ = 1.12π (≈3143 K), followed by a recovery and a peak at φ = 1.63π (≈4884 K). Under 40 + 200 kHz dual-frequency excitation, Tmax varies with phase in a cosine pattern, reaching a minimum and a maximum at φ = 0.41π and 1.22π, respectively, with corresponding temperatures of approximately 3343 and 4956 K. In contrast, under 40 + 140 and 140 + 200 kHz dual-frequency excitations, Tmax changes smoothly without pronounced troughs or peaks: the former remains stable between 5253 and 5389 K, while the latter fluctuates around 4915 K.

Effects of the maximum bubble temperature on sonochemical product yields

During acoustic cavitation, the high-temperature environment inside the bubble promotes the dissociation of water molecules and triggers a series of complex chemical reactions, generating various reactive chemical species including •OH, H2O2, and H2. The yields of chemical products inside the bubble do not increase monotonically with Tmax; instead, they are governed by the coupled interplay of reaction pathway competition, water vapor consumption, reverse reaction enhancement, and energy distribution. The response patterns of different chemical species to temperature differ significantly. This work systematically analyzes the influence of bubble collapse temperature on the yields of major chemical species and reveals the underlying kinetic mechanisms of the reaction network, providing a theoretical reference for parameter optimization in sonochemical processes and for understanding product selectivity. Using Tmax as a key parameter, this section analyzes its effects on the total yield of chemical species (excluding O2 and H2O), and the mole fractions of H2, H2O2, and •OH.

It is important to note that the yields of chemical species reported in this section are gas-phase values at the instant of collapse, whereas experimentally measurable product yields (e.g., accumulated H2O2 in the bulk liquid) reflect the macroscopic cumulative effect of products generated from a large number of bubble collapses. These two quantities are fundamentally different in nature. First, the internal yield of chemical species is an instantaneous, local gas-phase value for a single bubble at the moment of collapse, while the experimental yield is a liquid-phase, time-averaged value accumulated over many collapse events from a large bubble population. Second, the internal yield of chemical species only describes the composition of reaction products inside the bubble, whereas the experimental yield involves mass transfer from the bubble to the liquid, stability in the liquid phase, and possible secondary reactions. Moreover, the simulations are based on a single-bubble assumption, while experiments correspond to multi-bubble systems involving complex factors such as bubble–bubble interactions, size distribution, and bubble number density. Therefore, direct quantitative comparison between internal and experimental yields can be misleading. The subsequent analysis in this work focuses mainly on the trends and relative changes of these internal species with temperature, rather than on their absolute numerical values. This approach offers mechanistic insight into the reaction network and identifies characteristic temperature thresholds for different reaction pathways.

In this study, we aim to use Tmax as an intermediate variable to reveal the effects of dual-frequency operating parameters on the sonochemical characteristics inside the bubble, including the total yield of chemical species and the mole fractions of H2, H2O2, and •OH. A sensitivity analysis of R0 shows that the influence of Tmax on the sonochemical yields and product ratios is strongly radius-dependent. Comparison of Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Fig. 9, Fig. 10 reveals that, for different initial radii, the overall variation of sonochemical characteristics with Tmax remains consistent, but the transition temperatures at which the curves change slope differ. Therefore, in the subsequent part of this work, we select an initial radius of 2 μm as a representative case for detailed analysis, to use Tmax as an intermediate variable to elucidate the intrinsic relationships between dual-frequency ultrasonic fields and the sonochemical features inside the bubble.

Effects of the maximum bubble temperature on total yield of chemical species (excluding O2 and H2O)

The variation of the total yield of chemical species (excluding O2 and H2O) inside the bubble with the maximum bubble temperature (Tmax) is presented in Fig. 7. A detailed analysis is carried out for an initial bubble radius of 2 μm, as illustrated in Fig. 7(b). Overall, the total yield of chemical species exhibits a nonlinear growth pattern with increasing Tmax, characterized by a rapid initial increase followed by a tendency toward saturation.

Based on significant differences in the growth rate, the temperature range can be divided into two stages, with a transition point at approximately 7237 K. The first stage spans temperatures from 3052 to 7237 K, where the total yield of chemical species increases rapidly with Tmax. In this stage, gas molecules inside the bubbles undergo intense dissociation and excitation at high temperatures, the generation rates of reactive radicals (such as H•, •OH, and O) increase markedly, and numerous chemical reaction channels are activated, resulting in a rapid increase in chemical yields. The second stage covers the Tmax range from 7237 to 10190 K, where the growth rate of the total chemical species decreases significantly and gradually approaches saturation. Although Tmax continues to rise, the further increase in chemical yields is limited by reactant consumption, enhanced energy dissipation, and competition from reverse reactions at high temperatures, resulting in a saturation trend. This pattern reveals that the chemical reaction efficiency inside cavitation bubbles does not increase linearly with Tmax, but is constrained by multiple factors such as reactant concentration and energy distribution. Notably, the inflection point near 7237 K may mark the transition of the internal chemical process from “reaction control” to “dissipation control”.

In [3], the authors analyzed the relationship between the total amount of free radicals and the bubble collapse temperature. Their results showed that when the bubble collapse temperature ranged from 2850 to 7000 K, the total radical yield increased significantly with temperature; for the range of 9445 to 14210 K, the total radical yield still increased with temperature, but the rate of increase was markedly reduced. Although the interval between 7000 and 9445 K was not covered in [3], the overall trend is consistent with our calculations, supporting the accuracy of our results.

Effects of the maximum bubble temperature on H2 mole fraction

Fig. 8 illustrates the variation of H2 mole fraction inside the bubble as a function of the maximum bubble temperature (Tmax). A detailed analysis is carried out for an initial bubble radius of 2 μm, as illustrated in Fig. 8(b). Based on the differences in the growth rate of the hydrogen mole fraction, the Tmax range can be divided into four stages.

In the Tmax range of 3052–5519 K, the H2 mole fraction is extremely low and negligible (<0.5%). In the transition range of 5519–5818 K, the H2 mole fraction remains at a low level (within about 1.5%) but exhibits an increasing trend. Notably, 5818 K is the critical temperature where a sudden change in H2 mole fraction occurs. In the Tmax range of 5818–7260 K, the H2 mole fraction increases sharply, rising from approximately 2% to 38%. In the range of 7260–10190 K, the growth rate of H2 mole fraction decreases significantly and eventually approaches saturation at about 41%. Although Tmax continues to rise, the net production rate of H2 is suppressed due to the gradual consumption of the key reactant (H2O) and the enhancement of the reverse reaction (H2 + •OH → H2O + H) at high temperatures, forming a saturation plateau. Notably, the Tmax range for the sharp increase in H2 mole fraction (5818–7260 K) partially overlaps with the range for the rapid increase in total yield of chemical species (3052–7237 K); however, the onset temperature for the H2 surge is higher, indicating that H2 is a characteristic product of the high-temperature stage and its formation requires more severe thermodynamic conditions.

The increase in H2 yield with rising Tmax is consistent with the conclusions of [4], [11]. For example, in [4], when Tmax approached 6000 K, the effect of temperature on H2 yield noticeably weakened, which corresponds to the fourth stage summarized in this work, i.e., the slow saturation trend of H2 yield with Tmax. Once Tmax exceeds a certain critical value, its influence on H2 yield diminishes, which supports the conclusions of this work regarding the variation pattern of H2 yield with Tmax. However, the critical value in [4] was 6000 K, whereas in this work it is 7260 K. This discrepancy mainly stems from two factors: first, differences between the models, and second, differences in the characterization parameters for H2 yield. In [4], molar yield was used, whereas the present work uses mole fraction. The results of the present work are also consistent with the conclusions of [5], as both show that bubble collapse temperature is closely related to hydrogen yield. Specifically, the minimum Tmax required to produce a small amount of H2 was 3000 K; when Tmax rose to 6000 K, H2 yield increased significantly. Furthermore, the higher the bubble temperature, the faster the chemical reaction rate, leading to a higher H2 production rate. This relationship was also confirmed by [6].

Effects of the maximum bubble temperature on H2O2 mole fraction

The variation of H2O2 mole fraction inside the bubble with the maximum bubble temperature (Tmax) is illustrated in Fig. 9. A detailed analysis is carried out for an initial bubble radius of 2 μm, as illustrated in Fig. 9(b). Unlike the monotonic increase of H2 mole fraction with Tmax, the H2O2 mole fraction exhibits a typical unimodal distribution, with its peak occurring near 6210 K at a value of 17%. Based on the kinetic characteristics of H2O2 mole fraction with Tmax, the Tmax range can be divided into four stages.

In the low-temperature fluctuation region from 3052 to 4228 K, the H2O2 mole fraction remains below 2% with small fluctuations. This “low plateau” can be regarded as an incubation period for H2O2 formation. In the sharp growth region from 4228 to 6210 K, the H2O2 mole fraction climbs rapidly, rising from about 2% at 4228 K to a peak of 17% at 6210 K. Notably, this temperature range overlaps with the slow growth region of H2 mole fraction (<5818 K), indicating that in the range of 4228–5818 K, the generation pathways of H2O2 and H2 coexist, with H2O2 being dominant; in the range of 5818–6210 K, as Tmax approaches 6210 K, the H2O2 generation rate reaches its maximum while H2 also begins its rapid growth, foreshadowing a shift in the reaction pathway.

In the peak and rapid decay region from 6210 to 7248 K, 6210 K is the critical turning point for H2O2 yield. Above this temperature, the H2O2 mole fraction drops sharply from 17% to 9% at 7248 K. In the high-temperature plateau region from 7248 to 10190 K, the H2O2 mole fraction remains essentially stable at about 9%, no longer varying significantly with Tmax, indicating that under extremely high-temperature conditions, the generation and consumption of H2O2 reach a dynamic equilibrium.

As demonstrated in this work, the H2O2 mole fraction increases sharply within the temperature range of 4228–6210 K and attains its maximum value at 6210 K. This result is qualitatively consistent with the numerical findings of [37], which identified 4000–6500 K as the optimal temperature window for H2O2 generation inside an air bubble, although the absolute values differ due to the different gas environments (air vs. O2).

Effects of the maximum bubble temperature on •OH mole fraction

Depicted in Fig. 10 is the variation of the •OH mole fraction inside the bubble with the maximum bubble temperature (Tmax). A detailed analysis is carried out for an initial bubble radius of 2 μm, as illustrated in Fig. 10(b). Unlike the monotonic increase of H2 and the unimodal distribution of H2O2, the •OH mole fraction exhibits a multi-peak oscillatory pattern with Tmax, first decreasing, then increasing, and decreasing again. This complex evolution reveals the sensitivity of the generation and consumption mechanisms of •OH, as a core radical in the reaction network, to temperature, as well as its role as a “bridge” in multiple competing reaction pathways. Based on the kinetic characteristics of the •OH mole fraction with Tmax, the Tmax range can be divided into four stages.

In the initial high-value region with slow decay from 3052 to 5520 K, the •OH mole fraction is 53% at 3052 K, the second highest value over the entire temperature range, and then declines to 48% at 5520 K. This slow decline indicates that the consumption rate exceeds the generation rate.

In the secondary growth and peak region from 5520 to 6354 K, the •OH mole fraction stops declining and recovers, rising from 48% to a peak of 54% at 6354 K, the maximum over the entire temperature range. Above 6210 K, H2O2 begins to undergo significant thermal decomposition (H2O2 + M → 2•OH + M), supplying additional •OH to the system and partially offsetting other consumption pathways. The peak at 6354 K can be regarded as the “critical equilibrium point” of •OH generation and consumption: before this point, generation dominates; after it, consumption again takes over.

In the rapid decay region from 6354 to 7305 K, the •OH mole fraction drops sharply from 54% to 42% at 7305 K. This sharp decline reflects the collective strengthening of •OH consumption mechanisms at high temperatures, including competition from the H2 generation pathway (•OH + H2 → H2O + H), thermal decomposition of •OH (•OH → O + H), and reorganization of the reaction network. This stage coincides with the rapid growth of H2 and the rapid decay of H2O2, revealing the systematic shift of the reaction pathway from oxidizing intermediates (•OH, H2O2) to the reducing end product (H2). In the high-temperature plateau region from 7305 to 10190 K, the •OH mole fraction decreases more slowly, dropping from 42% to 40% at 10190 K and remaining essentially stable, indicating that under extremely high-temperature conditions, the generation and consumption of •OH reach a dynamic equilibrium.

The multi-peak evolution of the •OH mole fraction with Tmax reflects multiple competing and synergistic pathways within the complex reaction network inside the bubble. The peak temperature of 6354 K is not only the extremum point of the steady-state •OH concentration but also the “watershed” at which the reaction network shifts from dominance by oxidizing intermediates to dominance by reducing end products. In [1], when the bubble collapse temperature fell in the range of 5700–6300 K, the •OH mole fraction reached its maximum. Although the peak temperature differs somewhat between the two studies, the trend observed in [1], where the •OH mole fraction peaked near 6000 K, is essentially consistent with our conclusion that 6354 K is the critical equilibrium point for •OH generation and consumption; both indicate that this temperature range is the key region where •OH generation dominates and the reaction network enriches oxidizing intermediates, thereby providing important support for our delineation of •OH kinetic characteristics and the discussion of reaction pathway shifts.

Model validation against experimental results from the literature

To validate the numerical model developed in this study, we compare its predictions against experimental results reported for dual‑frequency sonochemical systems in the literature. It should be noted that the present model describes the dynamic response of a single bubble under a given acoustic field, whereas macroscopic sonochemical effects arise from the collective and cumulative contribution of a large bubble population over space and time. Consequently, the computed species yields represent molar amounts within a single bubble at the moment of collapse and are not directly comparable to bulk solution concentrations. We therefore limit our analysis to qualitative comparisons of relative trends across different frequency combinations.

To convert the macroscopic calorimetric data into acoustic driving parameters for the model, we use the plane-wave approximation. In a liquid medium, the local acoustic intensity I (W·m−2) and the acoustic pressure amplitude Pe (Pa) are related by: I=Pe2/2ρc. The volume-averaged acoustic power density is linked to the local acoustic intensity Pd (W·m−3) through an effective propagation length L (m): I=Pd·L.

Two independent experimental studies are selected for qualitative validation, each providing well-characterized reaction conditions and clear trends in sonochemical efficiency under both single- and dual-frequency irradiation. Ref. [29] examines H2O2 production in a bench‑scale reactor (3.9 L) over a higher frequency range (584 and 970 kHz). Ref. [32] investigates the degradation of bisphenol A (BPA) in a miniature cylindrical reactor (25 mL) equipped with a 20 kHz probe and a dual-frequency ultrasonic bath operating at 37 or 80 kHz. For each of these references, we adopt the reported experimental parameters (e.g., reactor geometry, liquid height, acoustic power density, and frequency combination) as inputs to our numerical model. Comprehensive comparison and mechanistic analysis of the temperature response

A comprehensive comparison of the temperature response patterns is made for the total chemical yield and for the three specific chemical products (i.e., H2, H2O2, and •OH), taking an initial bubble radius of 2 μm as an example. This comparison clearly reveals the dynamic shift of reaction pathways with temperature in the sonochemical system. The total yield of chemical species, as an overall indicator, provides a macroscopic framework for understanding the behavior of individual products through its two-stage feature of “rapid growth followed by saturation”; the transition point at 7237 K marks the shift from reaction control to dissipation control. Within this framework, each product exhibits a distinct temperature response: H2 shows a threshold-jump response, being negligible below 5818 K, rising rapidly between 5818 and 7260 K, and then saturating; H2O2 shows a unimodal response, peaking at 6210 K and then decaying; •OH shows a multi-peak oscillatory response, peaking at 6354 K, with variations spanning the entire temperature range.

Comparing the characteristic temperature points of various products reveals a series of temperature nodes and their physicochemical significance. 4228 K is the onset temperature for the rapid growth of H2O2 and marks the start of the overlap region between the slow decline of •OH and the rapid growth of H2O2. 5818 K is the threshold temperature for H2 generation, marking the onset of significant production of reducing products, and is near the starting point of the secondary growth region of •OH. 6210 K is the peak temperature of H2O2 and the turning point at which the reaction network shifts from dominance by oxidizing products to dominance by reducing products. 6354 K is the peak temperature of •OH, lying between the onset of H2O2 decomposition (6210 K) and the rapid growth of H2 (5818–7260 K), and represents the dynamic equilibrium between the two competing pathways. 7237 K is the inflection point of the growth rate of the total chemical species, marking the entry into the saturation trend. 7248 K is the starting point of the H2O2 high-temperature plateau, 7305 K is the starting point of the •OH high-temperature plateau, and 7260 K is the turning point where the growth rate of H2 begins to slow.

From the perspective of reaction pathway shifts, the entire temperature range can be divided into seven characteristic stages. In the low-temperature region (<4228 K), primary dissociation dominates, with •OH and H• as the main products, while the generation of H2O2 and H2 is limited. In the lower-middle-temperature region (4228–5520 K), •OH recombination to form H2O2 becomes dominant, the •OH fraction slowly declines, and H2O2 grows rapidly. In the upper-middle-temperature region (5520–5818 K), H2O2 continues to grow and approaches its peak, the •OH fraction continues to decline slowly as H2O2 has not yet decomposed, and H2 remains at a low plateau. In the high-temperature region (5818–6210 K), H2 begins to surge, H2O2 peaks at 6210 K and then starts to decompose, and the •OH fraction begins to recover from H2O2 decomposition. In the higher‑temperature region (6210–6354 K), H2O2 decomposes rapidly, the •OH fraction continues to recover due to this decomposition and reaches its peak at 6354 K, and H2 continues to grow rapidly. In the very‑high‑temperature region (6354–7260 K), the •OH fraction drops rapidly because it is heavily involved in H2 generation reactions, H2 continues to grow rapidly and approaches saturation, and H2O2 drops rapidly to its plateau value. In the highest‑temperature region (>7260 K), the system enters an equilibrium state: H2 slowly increases to saturation, while •OH and H2O2 stabilize at plateau levels.

This series of patterns reveals the systematic evolution of the reaction network inside the bubble: temperature, as the core regulating parameter, drives the shift of reaction pathways from oxidizing intermediates (•OH, H2O2) to dominance by the reducing end product (H2) through influencing the rate constants and equilibrium constants of elementary reactions. In particular, •OH, as a hub radical connecting the various products, exhibits concentration changes that sensitively reflect the direction of the reaction network. These findings provide a theoretical reference for the application of acoustic cavitation technology in fields such as green hydrogen production and pollutant degradation: if H2 is the target product, the temperature range of 5818–7260 K should be prioritized; if H2O2 is the target, the temperature should be precisely controlled near 6210 K; if •OH is the target reactive species, operating conditions near 6354 K should be selected. By appropriately choosing operating parameters to control the bubble temperature, targeted synthesis of desired products and precise control of reaction pathways can be achieved.

Model dependence of predicted sonochemical yields

The selection of chemical reaction mechanisms constitutes a critical source of uncertainty in sonochemical modeling. To systematically assess the model dependence of our predictions under dual-frequency excitation, we compare four distinct chemical mechanisms that are commonly employed in the sonochemical literature [15], namely those of Kamath et al., Yasui et al., Merouani et al., and Kalmár et al., hereafter referred to as Model 1, Model 2, Model 3, and the present model, respectively. All four mechanisms are developed for bubbles initially containing oxygen and water vapor, but differ substantially in their kinetic parameters and modeling approaches. To ensure a fair comparison, all simulations are performed under identical physical conditions within the dual-frequency acoustic field, with only the reaction mechanism being varied. This approach ensures that any observed differences arise solely from the chemical kinetics employed in the dual‑frequency sonochemical model. A detailed comparison of the four mechanisms is presented in Fig. 11.

Comparison of sonochemical yields predicted by four different chemical mechanisms as a function of the maximum bubble collapse temperature (Tmax), under dual-frequency excitations of (a) 20 + 40 kHz and (b) 20 + 200 kHz, at total acoustic pressures Pe = 1.5–3 atm, power allocation ratio N = 1, phase difference φ = 0, with initial bubble radius of 2 μm.

The discrepancies among the four mechanisms arise from several fundamental differences. First, the Arrhenius parameters are drawn from different sources and periods, with rate coefficients for the same reaction sometimes differing by orders of magnitude. Second, the treatment of reverse reaction rates differs fundamentally: Models 1–3 employ independent reverse Arrhenius parameters, whereas the present model calculates reverse rates rigorously via thermodynamic equilibrium constants. Third, pressure‑dependent reactions, which are important given the extreme pressures (up to hundreds of bars) reached during bubble collapse, are accounted for only in the present model. Finally, the present model incorporates duplicated reactions and accurate third‑body efficiency factors, including the enhanced efficiency of water vapor, all of which are absent from the other three mechanisms.

Fig. 11(a) presents a comprehensive comparison of the four mechanisms in terms of the total chemical species yield (excluding O2 and H2O) as a function of the maximum bubble temperature. Overall, the four models exhibit broadly similar trends: at relatively low collapse temperatures, the total chemical yield increases rapidly with Tmax; at higher temperatures, the growth rate gradually decelerates and the curves approach saturation. However, marked quantitative differences emerge among the models across different temperature regimes. In the low-to-intermediate temperature range (3000–5600 K), the predictions of Model 1, Model 3, and the present model are nearly indistinguishable from one another, and all yield substantially higher values than Model 2. In contrast, at elevated temperatures, Model 2 outperforms the other three models, predicting the highest total chemical yield.

Fig. 11(b) compares the H2 mole fraction predicted by the four mechanisms as a function of the maximum bubble temperature. Strikingly, all four models predict qualitatively the same trend: the H2 mole fraction is negligible at low collapse temperatures, undergoes a sharp increase in the intermediate temperature regime, and eventually reaches a plateau at high temperatures, beyond which further increases in Tmax have no further effect on the H2 mole fraction. Despite this qualitative agreement, considerable quantitative discrepancies emerge among the models. At the saturation stage, Model 1, Model 3, and the present model predict H2 mole fractions that are significantly higher than those obtained for Model 2.

Fig. 11(c) presents the H2O2 mole fraction predicted by the four mechanisms as a function of the maximum bubble temperature. For all models, with increasing Tmax, the H2O2 mole fraction first shows a slight decline, then rises sharply to a distinct peak, followed by a gradual decrease before stabilizing at high temperatures. Notably, at lower collapse temperatures, Model 1 predicts substantially higher H2O2 mole fractions than the other three models. In the high-temperature region, Model 2 exhibits pronounced fluctuations in the predicted H2O2 mole fraction. The peak values of H2O2 mole fraction predicted by the four models rank as follows: Model 2 > present model > Model 1 > Model 3, and the corresponding peak temperatures exhibit a similar ordering.

Fig. 11(d) presents the •OH mole fraction predicted by the four mechanisms as a function of Tmax. All four models predict a distinct maximum in the •OH mole fraction, though the optimal temperature corresponding to this peak varies considerably across mechanisms. The optimal temperatures for Model 1 and Model 3 are similar, occurring at approximately 5900 K, which is lower than that of the present model, while Model 2 exhibits the highest optimal temperature among the four.

In the low-temperature regime (below the respective optimal temperature), the present model uniquely predicts a non-monotonic response: the •OH mole fraction first decreases and then increases with rising Tmax, whereas the other three models increase monotonically with Tmax. In the high-temperature regime (above the respective optimal temperature), the •OH mole fraction predicted by Model 1, Model 3, and the present model show an initial decrease followed by a plateau, while Model 2 shows a monotonic decrease with increasing Tmax.

Limitations and future work of the present study

The dual‑frequency, single‑bubble sonochemical model presented here is based on Kalmár's work [15] and employs an O2 gas environment as the baseline condition. We acknowledge that this gas‑composition choice constitutes a fundamental premise of the present study. It is well established that gas composition critically affects both the bubble collapse temperature and the resulting sonochemical product distributions. Merouani et al. [38] revealed a marked frequency dependence in the ranking of •OH production rates among different saturating gases: the order is Ar > O2 > air > N2 for frequencies above 515 kHz, whereas at 213 kHz the ranking changes to O2 > air ∼ N2 > Ar. These findings indicate that the optimal temperature windows identified in this work are specific to O2 and should not be directly extrapolated to other gas environments without appropriate recalibration. More broadly, these temperature windows are model‑dependent predictions that may vary with reaction mechanism selection and multi‑bubble effects, and should therefore be viewed as theoretical reference guidelines rather than absolute targets for experimental systems. A systematic investigation of gas‑composition effects, including air, Ar, He, and other gases, is therefore planned as a key direction for our future work.

Despite these limitations, the model uses validated Arrhenius coefficients and third‑body efficiency factors, accounts for pressure‑dependent reactions and duplicated reactions, and calculates reverse rate constants via precise equilibrium equations. Compared with previous dual‑frequency sonochemical models [7], [40], [41], [47] that did not incorporate these effects, the present model more accurately reflects the significant changes in reaction rate constants induced by peak pressures of up to several hundred bars during bubble collapse. While these improvements are significant, several additional limitations remain and are discussed below. First, the maximum bubble temperature (Tmax) is selected as an intermediate variable to relate operating parameters to sonochemical yields. Although Tmax is widely used as an indicator of sonochemical activity, Rashwan et al. [49] have pointed out that the actual production of chemical species is determined jointly by the maximum bubble temperature and pressure, the amount of trapped water vapor, and the collapse duration. That is, the yield depends not only on temperature but also on the local concentration of reactants (especially water vapor) and the duration for which the temperature is sustained.

To further illustrate this point, we perform additional parametric simulations and present the results in Fig. 12, which shows the variation of the •OH mole fraction with four key dynamic parameters under dual‑frequency excitation. Overall, the trends are broadly consistent across the different parameter sweeps: the •OH mole fraction first decreases, then rises to a maximum, and finally declines again. However, caution is warranted when drawing conclusions from a single parameter in isolation, as nearly identical Tmax can correspond to markedly different •OH yields when other factors vary.

Parametric dependence of •OH mole fraction on four key dynamic variables under dual‑frequency excitation: (a) maximum bubble temperature (Tmax), (b) maximum bubble pressure, (c) mole fraction of trapped water vapor, and (d) bubble collapse duration. Simulations are performed for six frequency combinations (20 + 40, 20 + 140, 20 + 200, 40 + 140, 40 + 200, and 140 + 200 kHz) with total acoustic pressure Pe = 1.5–3 atm, power allocation ratio N = 0.1–10, phase difference φ = 0–2π, initial water vapor mole fraction XH2O,0=0.01-50%, and initial bubble radius of 2 μm.

A clear example is provided in Fig. 12. Under otherwise identical conditions of Pe = 2 atm, N = 1, and a frequency combination of 20 + 140 kHz, two cases are compared. In the first case, the phase difference is set to φ = 0 with an initial water vapor mole fraction XH2O,0=1.35%; in the second case, the phase difference is adjusted to φ = 3π/2 with XH2O,0=40%. Remarkably, both cases yield nearly identical Tmax (≈6700 K), similar water vapor mole fractions (≈99.83%), and collapse durations (≈10.8 μs). Despite these close similarities, the second case produces the maximum collapse pressure that is one order of magnitude higher than that of the first case, resulting in an •OH mole fraction that is approximately 5% higher. This difference can be attributed to the higher collapse pressure, which enhances the dissociation of water vapor and thereby promotes the formation of •OH radicals, while the comparable temperature and collapse duration ensure that the consumption pathways remain similar. Consequently, the net •OH yield is significantly elevated in the high‑pressure case, even though Tmax alone would suggest identical sonochemical activity.

This example clearly demonstrates that Tmax alone is insufficient to predict the final product distribution; rather, the collapse pressure, together with the temporal evolution of temperature and reactant concentrations, must also be considered. Therefore, while Tmax serves as a useful first‑order indicator, future work should aim to develop more comprehensive predictive parameters that integrate the complete temperature–pressure history and collapse dynamics, thereby enabling more reliable predictions of sonochemical yields across a broader range of operating conditions.

Second, the present model neglects chemical reactions in the liquid phase. According to Storey’s work [50], the concentration of chemical species inside the bubble is fairly uniform over most of the bubble interior, with a very steep gradient only near the wall (the wall concentration is three orders of magnitude lower than the centre concentration at the moment of the minimum bubble radius). The thermal boundary layer confines chemical reactions to the inner region, isolating them from the gas–liquid interface. While this simplification is qualitatively reasonable, accurate prediction of the transport of soluble reactants into the liquid phase would require coupling with liquid-phase reaction kinetics in the future.

Third, gas exchange between the bubble and the surrounding liquid is neglected. The characteristic time scale for gas diffusion is τdiff∼R02/D(where D represents the diffusion coefficient, on the order of 10−9 m2/s) [51]. For R0 = 2 μm, this time scale is approximately 4000 μs, which is much longer than the period of a 20 kHz ultrasound wave (50 μs). Therefore, this neglect is justified. However, for smaller bubbles or lower frequencies, diffusion effects may become significant, and a non-equilibrium diffusion term could be introduced if needed.

The most critical limitation is that the present model analyzes sonochemical behavior for a single bubble, excluding bubble–bubble interactions, bubble size distribution, bubble number density, and other factors that are prevalent in industrial sonochemical systems. In a multi‑bubble system, acoustic radiation from neighboring bubbles alters the pulsation characteristics of each bubble, affecting the overall chemical activity. Key issues such as bubble cluster behavior and shape instability are also excluded.

Nevertheless, the single‑bubble model is useful for understanding fundamental mechanisms and can serve as a basis for predicting sonochemical activity when combined with bubble population data. Future work will extend the model to multi‑bubble systems by incorporating Bjerknes forces, acoustic interference, bubble translation, non‑spherical deformation, size distribution dynamics, and more complex chemical kinetics (e.g., liquid‑phase reactions and multi‑component mass transfer). Ultimately, such extensions, combined with experimental calibration, could enable a practical model for sonochemical systems.

Conclusion

This single‑bubble study systematically quantifies the relationships between key operating parameters (acoustic field settings and initial bubble conditions) and the maximum bubble temperature (Tmax) under an O2 environment. Using Tmax as an intermediate variable, a structured correlation framework is constructed that links these operating parameters to the yields of key chemical products (H2, H2O2, and •OH). This single-bubble model elucidates the cascade from acoustic and initial‑state variables to Tmax and chemical products, but its quantitative conclusions are limited by assumptions such as spherical symmetry and the neglect of bubble–bubble interactions.

The effect of the power allocation ratio is strongly frequency-dependent. For frequency combinations that include a low‑frequency component, Tmax peaks at a low power allocation ratio (N = 0.1); for the dual-low-frequency combination (20 + 40 kHz), the optimal power allocation ratio is approximately 1.06. As the excitation amplitude increases, the regulatory role of the power allocation ratio gradually strengthens in the “enhancement region” (small N) but weakens in the “weakening region” (large N). The phase difference most strongly affects the temperature under dual-low-frequency excitation, where the temperature variation can more than double; for combinations containing a high-frequency component, the phase effect is comparatively modest. Moreover, at high excitation intensities, the fine-tuning effect of the phase difference diminishes considerably. The modulating influence of the initial water vapor mole fraction on Tmax is most pronounced at low acoustic pressures, but this effect is progressively suppressed as the excitation amplitude increases, except for certain frequency combinations at Pe = 3 atm.

The overall trend of sonochemical yields as a function of Tmax is consistent across different initial bubble radii, although the absolute transition temperatures for each product vary with the initial radius. As exemplified by the case of an initial radius of 2 μm, the total yield of chemical species exhibits a two-stage pattern of “rapid growth followed by saturation” with increasing Tmax, and the inflection point occurs near 7237 K. The H2 mole fraction displays a threshold-jump behavior, rising sharply from ≈2% to 38% between 5818 and 7260 K. In contrast, the H2O2 mole fraction follows a typical unimodal distribution, peaking at ≈17% near 6210 K. The •OH mole fraction, in turn, shows a multi-peak oscillatory pattern, reaching a maximum of ≈54% at 6354 K.

From an application perspective, these findings provide a single-bubble-based theoretical reference for parameter selection aimed at controlling sonochemical product yields. If H2 is the desired product, the bubble temperature should be preferentially controlled near 7260 K. For H2O2 production, the temperature should be precisely tuned to approximately 6210 K. To maximize •OH as the target reactive species, operating conditions near 6354 K are recommended. However, the proposed temperature windows are model‑dependent predictions obtained under an O2 atmosphere within a single‑bubble framework, and their direct extrapolation to arbitrary experimental conditions should be approached with caution. These windows should therefore be viewed as theory‑derived reference points that require validation and recalibration for specific systems.
