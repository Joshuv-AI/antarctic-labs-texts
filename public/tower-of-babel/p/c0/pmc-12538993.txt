Specific plasma lipid species in children are causally associated with kawasaki disease: a mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Changqing Zhen, Shuo Zhang, Feng Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKawasaki Disease (KD) is an acute pediatric vasculitis and the leading cause of acquired heart disease in children, yet its etiology involving lipid metabolism remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the potential causal associations between plasma lipid species, measured in a pediatric cohort, and the risk of KD using a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) approach.MethodsWe utilized summary statistics from a genome-wide association study (GWAS) of 227 plasma lipid species in 1,149 children and adolescents (median age 11.2 years). Summary statistics for KD were obtained from a meta-analysis of two GWAS datasets comprising 479 cases and 490,361 controls of European ancestry. We employed inverse-variance weighted (IVW) MR as the primary analysis, complemented by nine robust univariable MR methods (UVMR), five multivariable MR (MVMR) methods (MVMR-IVW, -Egger, -PRESSO, -Lasso, -Median), and MR-Bayesian Model Averaging (MR-BMA) to assess causality and account for pleiotropy and lipid correlations.ResultsThe primary IVW analysis suggested nine lipid species associated with KD risk. Robust UVMR analyses provided corroborating evidence, notably showing consistent negative associations for Lysophosphatidylcholine (0:0/20:0) and Triglyceride (52:5) across multiple methods. Subsequent MVMR consistently identified positive causal associations between Phosphatidylcholine (40:8), Phosphatidylinositol (36:4), and Sphingomyelin (d36:3) and increased risk of KD. Furthermore, MR-BMA identified Triglyceride (56:5) as the lipid species with the strongest evidence for a protective effect (highest marginal inclusion probability = 0.275).ConclusionLeveraging genetic data specific to a pediatric population, this MR study provides novel evidence supporting potential causal roles for specific plasma lipid species in the pathogenesis of Kawasaki disease. Background

Kawasaki disease (KD), also known as mucocutaneous lymph node syndrome, is an acute, febrile systemic vasculitis predominantly affecting infants and young children, representing the leading cause of acquired heart disease in pediatric populations in developed countries [1–3]. While the hallmark of KD is systemic inflammation, its most feared complication is the development of coronary artery aneurysms, which can lead to long-term cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [4, 5]. The precise etiology of KD remains elusive, although current evidence suggests a complex interplay between genetic susceptibility and environmental or infectious triggers culminating in an aberrant immune response targeting the vasculature [6, 7].

Lipids, beyond their structural and energy storage roles, are critical mediators of cellular signaling, inflammation, and vascular homeostasis [8]. Lipidomics, the large-scale study of lipids in biological systems, offers a powerful lens to capture detailed perturbations in lipid metabolism and identify specific species potentially involved in disease pathogenesis [9]. However, traditional observational studies linking lipid profiles to diseases are susceptible to confounding factors and reverse causation, limiting causal inference.

Mendelian randomization (MR) utilizes genetic variants, randomly allocated at conception, as instrumental variables (IVs) to probe the causal effect of an exposure (e.g., a specific lipid species) on an outcome (e.g., KD) [10]. By leveraging the principles of Mendelian inheritance, MR can mitigate confounding and establish a more robust basis for causal inference compared to conventional observational approaches [11, 12]. In recent years, MR has been increasingly applied to multi-omics data to dissect causal pathways in complex diseases.

However, a significant knowledge gap exists regarding the specific causal roles of the plasma lipidome in the etiology of KD. Furthermore, most MR studies utilizing lipidomic or other omics data have relied on datasets derived from adult populations, investigating adult-onset diseases [13, 14]. Given that KD is primarily a pediatric condition and that lipid profiles exhibit developmental specificity [15], investigating these associations using genetic data pertinent to children is crucial. The scarcity of large-scale pediatric GWAS for omics traits has limited such investigations.

Therefore, this study leverages summary statistics from a genome-wide association study of the plasma lipidome in a pediatric cohort to investigate the potential causal associations between 227 distinct plasma lipid species and the risk of KD using a comprehensive two-sample MR framework. By employing data specifically from children and adolescents for the exposure, we aim to provide novel insights into the causal contribution of lipid metabolism to the pathogenesis of KD.

Methods

Ethical considerations

This study adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology using Mendelian Randomization (STROBE-MR) guidelines. Detailed adherence is documented in the STROBE-MR checklist [11].

Study design and data sources

Data source for lipidomics

Genome-wide summary statistics pertaining to individual lipid species were sourced from the NHGRI-EBI GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas), accessible under study accession numbers GCST90488134-GCST90488360. The lipidomics data originated from a Danish cohort comprising 1,149 children and adolescents (median age: 11.2 years; 44.8% male). Analysis encompassed approximately 7.1 million genetic variants to evaluate their association with 227 distinct plasma lipid species [15].

Data source for KD

KD was defined according to the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11) Diagnosis Code for Kawasaki disease (4A44.5). Genome-wide association study (GWAS) data for KD were aggregated from two distinct sources: firstly, summary statistics from study GCST90013537 (400 cases, 6,101 controls of European ancestry) obtained via the NHGRI-EBI GWAS Catalog [16, 17]. Genetic variants were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome build utilizing the 'liftover' function within the 'MungeSumstats' R package [18]. Secondly, data were incorporated from the FinnGen R12 release, encompassing 79 cases and 484,260 controls [19]. Subsequent meta-analysis of the GWAS data employed an inverse-variance weighted multiplicative-random-effects model, executed using METAL software [20]. Participants across both contributing GWAS datasets were exclusively of European ancestry, ensuring no significant population overlap.

Selection of instrumental variables (IVs)

Instrumental variable selection adhered to the three core assumptions of MR: relevance, independence, and the exclusion restriction [12]. The relevance assumption was met by selecting SNPs robustly associated with each lipid species. The independence assumption was addressed by linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping to ensure the instrumental variables (IVs) for a given exposure were uncorrelated. The exclusion restriction assumption (i.e., that IVs affect KD only via the lipid species of interest) is addressed by the MR design itself, which mitigates traditional confounding, and was further tested for violations using multiple pleiotropy-robust sensitivity analyses as detailed in subsequent sections. IVs were initially selected based on a stringent genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5 × 10–8) to identify single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) robustly associated with individual lipid species. Where insufficient SNPs met this stringent criterion, a relaxed threshold (P < 5 × 10–6) was adopted to maintain adequate statistical power, aligning with established MR methodologies [21]. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping was subsequently performed using the European 1000 Genomes Project reference panel, applying thresholds of r2 < 0.001 and an LD window of 10,000 kb. A critical step to mitigate weak instrument bias is ensuring all IVs are strongly associated with their respective exposures. Therefore, we calculated the F-statistic for each SNP and excluded any IVs with an F-statistic below the conventional threshold of 10 [22]. The final, robust set of IVs used for each lipid species is detailed in Supplementary Table S1. For potential reverse MR analyses, IVs for KD were subject to identical selection criteria: association threshold P < 5 × 10–6, LD clumping (r2 < 0.001, window = 10,000 kb), and exclusion based on F-statistics < 10.

Univariable MR analyses (UVMR)

In the two-sample UVMR framework, the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was initially applied to estimate the overall causal effect, leveraging all selected IVs [23, 24]. All causal estimates were scaled to represent the odds ratio (OR) of KD per one standard deviation (SD) increase in the concentration of each lipid species. Furthermore, the MR-Egger intercept test was employed to assess potential directional pleiotropy [25]. An intercept P-value exceeding 0.05 was interpreted as evidence against significant directional pleiotropy, thereby strengthening the validity of the causal estimate. Recognizing that the IVW method's susceptibility to bias from even a single invalid instrument (0% breakdown point) and the improbability of all selected IVs being perfectly valid, multiple robust MR methods were implemented to address potential violations of IV assumptions [26]. Specifically, nine complementary robust UVMR techniques were applied: weighted median [26], mode-based estimation (MBE) [27], MR-Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) [28], MR-Robust [29], MR-Lasso [30], MR-Egger [31], contamination mixture [32], MR-Mix[33], and MR-Robust Adjusted Profile Score (RAPS) [34] (details in Supplementary Table S2). These methods encompass three main strategies: consensus-based (weighted median, MBE), outlier-robust (MR-PRESSO, MR-Lasso, MR-Robust), and explicit modeling of invalid IV effects (MR-Egger, contamination mixture, MR-Mix, MR-RAPS) [35]. UVMR analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.2) with the 'TwoSampleMR' (v0.6.9) [36, 37], 'Mendelian Randomization' (v0.10.0) [38], 'MRPRESSO' (v1.0) [28], 'MRMix' (v0.1.0) [33], and 'mr.raps' (v0.2) [34] packages.

Multivariable MR analyses (MVMR)

MVMR extends the UVMR framework by modeling multiple risk factors simultaneously [39]. Unlike UVMR, which assumes IVs influence only a single exposure, multivariable MR (MVMR) accommodates IVs potentially influencing multiple measured risk factors, thereby accounting for measured pleiotropy. We employed MVMR to disentangle the independent effects of lipid species identified as potentially causal in the UVMR analyses, accounting for their potential interactions. The multivariable inverse-variance weighted (MVMR-IVW) method served as the principal analysis, supplemented by MVMR-Egger, MVMR-PRESSO, MVMR-Lasso, and MVMR-Median methods for sensitivity assessment [40]. These MVMR analyses were executed using the 'TwoSampleMR', 'Mendelian Randomization', and 'MR-PRESSO' R packages. Standard MVMR approaches face challenges when applied to high-dimensional, highly correlated lipidomic data [41]. Consequently, the Bayesian model averaging approach (MR-BMA), specifically designed for high-throughput data, was employed to facilitate robust risk factor selection among numerous candidates [42]. MR-BMA was therefore applied to identify lipid species demonstrating the strongest evidence of association with KD risk (details in Supplementary Table S3). MR-BMA analysis parameters included a prior probability ('prior_prob') of 0.1, prior variance (σ2) of 0.25, and 100,000 stochastic search iterations ('max_iter') [42].

Results

GWAS meta-analysis for KD

The meta-analysis encompassed 9,671,413 SNPs (minor allele frequency > 0.01), identifying 24 variants associated with KD at a suggestive significance level (P < 5 × 10−5) but none reaching genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8) (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S4).

Manhattan plot of GWAS meta-analysis for Kawasaki disease. The 24 SNPs identified with P value less than at 5 × 10 − 5 from GWAS meta-analysis are labelled (blue line – P < 5 × 10–5). However, there was no locus reaching genome-wide significance (red line – P < 5 × 10–8)

Univariable MR analyses

The primary IVW analysis suggested potential causal associations between ten distinct lipid species and KD risk (Supplementary Table S5). One species, Phosphatidylcholine (40:5) A, was excluded from further consideration due to evidence of significant directional pleiotropy indicated by the MR-Egger intercept test (P < 0.05) (Supplementary Table S6). Furthermore, heterogeneity testing using Cochran's Q statistic revealed no significant heterogeneity among the instrumental variables for the remaining nine lipid species (all P > 0.05), supporting the stability of the IVW estimates (Supplementary Table S7). For the remaining nine lipid species identified in the primary IVW analysis, the range of mean F-statistic for the instrumental variables was 22.3 to 31.3, indicating strong instrument strength (Supplementary Table S8). The nine lipid species demonstrated associations with KD (Fig. 3): five showed positive associations (Odds Ratio [OR] > 1)—Phosphatidylcholine (40:8), Phosphatidylcholine (P-38:6)/Phosphatidylcholine (O-38:7), Phosphatidylethanolamine (40:7), Phosphatidylinositol (38:3), and Sphingomyelin (d36:3); four showed negative associations (OR < 1)—Lysophosphatidylcholine (0:0/20:0), Triglyceride (56:6), Triglyceride (52:5), and Triglyceride (56:5). To corroborate these findings, nine distinct robust UVMR methods were applied to assess the consistency of the observed causal effects (Fig. 4, Supplementary Tables S9-17). Notably, Lysophosphatidylcholine (0:0/20:0) exhibited a consistent negative association (beta < 0) across seven robust methods (P-values: weighted median = 0.014, PRESSO = 0.010, Robust = 0.005, Lasso = 0.007, Egger = 0.033, contamination mixture = 0.006, RAPS = 0.011), while Triglyceride (52:5) showed consistent negative association across six methods (P-values: weighted median = 0.013, PRESSO = 0.001, Robust = 0.009, Lasso = 0.001, contamination mixture = 0.003, RAPS = 0.004). The MBE method did not yield statistically significant associations for any lipid species (P > 0.05). Collectively, the IVW and nine robust UVMR analyses identified 58 lipid species demonstrating at least nominal significance (P < 0.05 in at least one method) as potential exposures for subsequent MVMR analysis (Supplementary Table S18). Instrument selection for reverse MR analysis (KD as exposure) failed to identify any variants meeting the predefined significance threshold (P < 5 × 10–6). Consequently, reverse MR analyses were not performed.

Multivariable MR analyses

MVMR analyses were subsequently conducted to evaluate the independent associations of the identified lipid species while accounting for potential confounding and interactions among them. This multivariable framework enabled the assessment of the independent contributions of the 58 candidate lipid species identified in UVMR (Supplementary Table S18). Instrument variables for MVMR were curated according to the 'TwoSampleMR' package guidelines, involving extraction for each exposure, aggregation, LD clumping, re-extraction post-clumping, and harmonization to ensure consistent effect alleles across all IVs. A final set of 229 SNPs served as instruments for the MVMR analyses, satisfying the condition that the number of instruments exceeds the number of exposures (Supplementary Table S19). The initial phase of MVMR analysis utilized five distinct methods: MVMR-IVW, MVMR-Egger, MVMR-PRESSO, MVMR-Lasso, and MVMR-Median. Results indicated that Phosphatidylcholine (40:8), Phosphatidylinositol (36:4), and Sphingomyelin (d36:3) consistently demonstrated significant positive associations with KD risk across all five MVMR methods (Fig. 5, Supplementary Table S20). To further refine the findings within this high-dimensional exposure set, an MR-BMA analysis was conducted. In the MR-BMA results, Triglyceride (56:5) emerged as the leading candidate, exhibiting the highest marginal inclusion probability (MIP = 0.275) and a negative model-averaged causal effect (MACE = −0.118); all other lipid species displayed MIPs below 0.2 (Table 1). Considering individual model configurations, the model including only Triglyceride (56:5) possessed the highest posterior probability (PP = 0.017) with a corresponding model-specific causal estimate (MSCE = −0.452); posterior probabilities for models including other individual lipids were all less than 0.01 (Table 2). Sensitivity analysis using the MR-BMA 'diagnostics' function did not identify any outlier or influential genetic variants (evaluated at posterior probability > 0.01).

Discussion

This study employed a comprehensive two-sample Mendelian randomization approach, uniquely utilizing pediatric lipidome GWAS data, to investigate the causal relationships between plasma lipid species and KD. Our findings provide novel genetic evidence suggesting potential causal roles for specific lipids in KD pathogenesis. The multivariable MR analyses conducted in this study revealed significant positive associations between genetically predicted levels of Phosphatidylcholine (40:8), Phosphatidylinositol (36:4), and Sphingomyelin (d36:3) with an increased risk of Kawasaki disease. While the effect sizes for a per-SD change are modest, they may signify clinically relevant shifts in disease susceptibility at a population level, highlighting these lipids as potential biomarkers for risk stratification. This suggests that inherently higher levels of these specific lipid species during childhood may causally contribute to a greater susceptibility to developing KD. The consistency of these associations across multiple MVMR methods strengthens the evidence supporting a direct causal link between these lipid species and the disease. Conversely, the MR-BMA analysis highlighted a negative association between Triglyceride (56:5) and the risk of KD, exhibiting the highest marginal inclusion probability among the analyzed lipid species. This finding indicates that genetically predisposed lower levels of this particular triglyceride in children might be causally related to a higher risk of developing KD. The MR-BMA approach is particularly well-suited for analyzing high-dimensional and often correlated data [42], such as lipidomics datasets, making this specific finding particularly noteworthy.

Phosphatidylcholines (PCs) are the most abundant phospholipids in cellular membranes and play key roles in membrane integrity, lipoprotein metabolism, and inflammatory signaling [43]. Our finding of a positive association with PC (40:8) may reflect an underlying pro-inflammatory state in children susceptible to KD. As KD is a systemic vasculitis, alterations in PC composition could directly impact the vascular endothelium. For example, changes in membrane fluidity and structure might enhance endothelial activation, increase the expression of adhesion molecules, and facilitate leukocyte infiltration into the vessel wall—all critical early steps in KD pathogenesis [3]. Furthermore, specific PC species can be hydrolyzed to produce potent inflammatory mediators like lysophosphatidylcholine and arachidonic acid, potentially amplifying the intense inflammatory cascade driven by cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, which are well-known biomarkers of acute KD [44]. This is consistent with lipidomics studies in pediatric KD cohorts, where elevated oxidized phosphatidylcholines have been linked to coronary arteritis through activation of endothelial inflammation and monocyte adhesion [45]. Similar associations have been observed in adult vasculitides, such as ANCA-associated vasculitis, where dysregulated lipid profiles correlate with disease activity and inflammation [46], suggesting a broader role for PCs in vascular inflammatory pathways across age groups.

Phosphatidylinositols (PIs) are signaling lipids that play crucial roles in various cellular processes, including cell growth, survival, and the regulation of immune responses [47]. The positive association identified between PI (36:4) and KD might point to a role for this lipid species in the immune dysregulation that is a characteristic feature of KD [3]. It is plausible that increased levels of PI (36:4) are linked to the enhanced activation of immune cells and the subsequent production of pro-inflammatory cytokines that are observed in the pathogenesis of KD. Mechanistically, PIs may contribute to vasculitis by modulating phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase pathways, which regulate innate immune responses; this aligns with findings in IgA vasculitis (another pediatric vasculitis), where lipid metabolism disruptions influence inflammation and cell apoptosis [48].

Sphingomyelins (SMs) are another class of structural lipids found in cell membranes, known to be involved in cell signaling pathways and the process of apoptosis [47]. The positive association of SM (d36:3) with KD might suggest its involvement in the vascular inflammation or the endothelial dysfunction that are key pathological features of the disease. Higher levels of SM (d36:3) could potentially contribute to the vascular damage that is a significant concern in KD, particularly its predilection for the coronary arteries, where alterations in sphingolipid metabolism could have detrimental effects on vascular health. This is supported by lipidomic profiling in IVIG-resistant KD children, where dysregulated sphingolipids correlate with treatment response and vascular inflammation [49], and extends to adult vasculitides like ANCA-associated vasculitis, where lipid metabolism shifts are tied to monocyte activation and disease severity [46].

The negative association observed between Triglyceride (56:5) and KD is less readily explained through direct inflammatory mechanisms, as triglycerides (TGs) primarily function as the body's main form of energy storage. However, alterations in triglyceride metabolism have been consistently reported in KD [50], and lower levels of specific TGs might reflect shifts in energy utilization or broader metabolic changes that are associated with the disease process. In the context of KD, an acute inflammatory illness, significant impacts on the body's metabolism are expected, and the observed negative association of TG (56:5) might serve as a marker of such metabolic dysregulation or altered energy demands. For instance, studies in acute KD have shown elevated triglycerides linked to hypercoagulation and cardiovascular risk, suggesting that reduced specific TG species could exacerbate metabolic stress during inflammation [51].

A major strength and distinguishing feature of this study is the use of exposure GWAS data derived exclusively from children and adolescents [15]. Given that KD primarily manifests in childhood and that lipid metabolism undergoes significant developmental changes [47], leveraging pediatric data provides a more biologically relevant context for investigating causal factors for this disease compared to extrapolating from adult datasets, which dominate the current MR-omics landscape [13, 14]. Furthermore, we employed a rigorous analytical strategy, including a meta-analysis to increase the power of the KD GWAS, the use of multiple complementary UVMR and MVMR methods to test the robustness of findings against pleiotropy [35, 40], and the application of MR-BMA suited for high-dimensional exposure data [42].

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while we employed multiple methods to detect and adjust for pleiotropy, the possibility of residual or unmeasured pleiotropy influencing the results cannot be entirely eliminated. The core MR assumptions, particularly the exclusion restriction criterion (IVs affect the outcome only via the exposure), remain critical and may not be fully met. Second, our analyses relied on summary-level GWAS data from populations of European ancestry, potentially limiting the generalizability of findings to other ethnic groups, which is particularly relevant given the known variations in KD incidence across different ancestries [52]. Third, while our meta-analysis increased the sample size for Kawasaki disease (KD) to 479 cases and 490,361 controls, the relatively small number of cases still limits statistical power to detect small-to-modest causal effects with high confidence. This limitation meant we could not identify any genetic variants that reached the significance threshold to serve as strong instruments for KD. Consequently, we were unable to perform reverse MR analyses. This prevents a formal test of reverse causation (i.e., whether the onset of KD alters the plasma lipidome), and we cannot definitively rule out a potential bidirectional relationship between certain lipids and KD. Future MR studies will require KD GWAS with substantially larger numbers of cases to improve statistical power and the robustness of the findings. Fourth, summary data prevent the exploration of potential non-linear relationships or interactions between lipids and other factors. Fifth, in our MR-BMA analysis, the posterior probabilities for the top-ranking models were low, reflecting considerable model uncertainty when selecting from a large number of correlated lipid species. While this is an expected finding in a high-dimensional setting, it suggests that no single lipid model is overwhelmingly superior to others. However, the MR-BMA approach inherently addresses this by averaging across all plausible models, which provides more robust evidence for the prioritized lipids (e.g., Triglyceride (56:5)) than would be achieved through single-model selection, thereby controlling for false positives. Sixth, our use of summary-level data prevented sex-stratified analyses. Given that KD exhibits a male predominance and that lipid profiles differ by sex, particularly during adolescence, investigating potential sex-specific causal effects is an important avenue for future research using individual-level data. Finally, plasma lipid levels may not fully reflect lipid metabolism within specific tissues directly involved in KD pathology, such as the coronary arteries.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this Mendelian randomization study, leveraging unique pediatric plasma lipidome GWAS data, provides novel evidence supporting potential causal associations between specific lipid species—notably Phosphatidylcholine (40:8), Phosphatidylinositol (36:4), Sphingomyelin (d36:3), and Triglyceride (56:5)—and the risk of KD. Our findings highlight the importance of investigating pediatric-specific exposures for pediatric diseases and suggest that targeted perturbations in lipid metabolism may play a causal role in KD pathogenesis. While further validation is required, these results open new avenues for understanding KD etiology. The translation of these findings into clinical practice will necessitate future research, including replication in larger and more ethnically diverse pediatric cohorts and mechanistic studies. Critically, a larger GWAS of KD is needed to identify robust genetic instruments that would facilitate future bidirectional MR analyses. Furthermore, integrating these findings with transcriptome-wide or proteome-wide MR could help elucidate the downstream biological pathways linking these lipids to KD pathogenesis. Such investigations, including prospective studies that correlate specific plasma lipid species from clinical lipid panels with disease activity and outcomes in KD patients, will be crucial for determining whether these lipids could serve as biomarkers or targets for novel preventive or therapeutic strategies in Kawasaki disease.
