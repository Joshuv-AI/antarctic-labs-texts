Model test study on centroid frequency evolution of reservoir landslide under water level fluctuations
Authors: Zhixiang Wu, Guobao Zhang, Mowen Xie, Xiaoliang Cheng, Jiabin Zhang, Jiasheng Hao, Peng Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the evolution of centroid frequency in reservoir landslides under cyclic fluctuations of reservoir water levels, using both theoretical analysis and physical model tests. Theoretical investigations examined the attenuation characteristics of longitudinal waves, shear waves, and Rayleigh waves in viscoelastic media. Physical model tests simulated landslide evolution under different water-level fluctuation rates, and the centroid frequency variations throughout the landslide process were analyzed. The results show that water-level fluctuations do not directly alter centroid frequency values. However, the associated decline in landslide stability leads to pronounced frequency depression in centroid frequency. The evolution of centroid frequency is significantly affected by the fluctuation rate: the faster the fluctuation, the lower the landslide stability and the more noticeable the frequency variation. Sensitivity to landslide changes also varied across monitoring locations, with more significant changes observed near the landslide toe and on the surface compared to deeper sections. Notably, the downward trend in centroid frequency occurred prior to landslide failure, with changes exceeding 7 Hz, indicating its potential as an early-warning indicator. Nevertheless, the reliability and general applicability of centroid frequency as a warning metric require further validation. This study provides new insights and methods for understanding the dynamic evolution of reservoir landslides and improving landslide monitoring and early-warning systems.Keywords: Reservoir landslide, Water level fluctuations, Centroid frequency, Evolution pattern, Early warningSubject terms: Engineering, Hydrology, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

With the intensification of global climate change and human activities, frequent reservoir water level fluctuations have posed a significant threat to the stability of reservoir landslides1,2. As a common geological hazard, reservoir landslides not only pose serious risks to the safety of nearby residents and property3,4, but may also trigger secondary disasters such as river blockage and disruption of navigation5–7. Therefore, investigating the evolutionary processes and early warning indicators of reservoir landslides holds significant scientific and practical value for landslide disaster prevention and mitigation.

In recent years, with continuous advancements in monitoring technology, dynamic indicators have attracted increasing attention. Spectral analysis enables the transformation of recorded waveform data from the time domain to the frequency domain. Compared with time-domain features, spectral methods can reveal many phenomena that are difficult to detect in the time domain, particularly those related to source-type characteristics. Among various spectral parameters, natural frequency and centroid frequency are widely used. Natural frequency reflects the dominant frequency at which acoustic emission energy is released, while centroid frequency, distinct from natural frequency, represents the distribution of high-frequency and low-frequency energy in a signal8.

Centroid frequency has been applied in fields such as seismic analysis9, porosity evaluation of materials10,11, and rock instability studies12. For example, several scholars have investigated the relationship between centroid frequency and rock failure13,14. Zhang et al.15 analyzed the distribution of centroid frequency during the deformation and failure process of granite specimens to explore failure mechanisms and precursory features. Zhao et al.16 used average centroid frequency to identify precursors of critical failure in red sandstone under varying moisture content. While spectral methods reveal phenomena invisible in the time domain, current applications of centroid frequency are predominantly confined to rock mass instability and seismic attenuation. For reservoir landslides, monitoring still relies heavily on surface displacement. There is a significant research gap in understanding how centroid frequency responds to the micro-structural degradation caused by cyclic wetting-drying in reservoir slopes.

Studies have shown that reservoir water level fluctuations induce repeated wetting and drying cycles within landslide masses, which affect the mechanical properties and structural stability of the soil. This process can cause micro-deformation and lead to the initiation of new cracks and the expansion of pre-existing fractures17–19. Since centroid frequency is sensitive to internal damage changes in materials, it is of research interest to use centroid frequency to assess landslide stability.

In this study, physical model tests were conducted to investigate the evolution pattern of centroid frequency in reservoir landslides. The tests simulated landslide development under different rates of water level rise and fall, and analyzed the changes in centroid frequency. The potential of centroid frequency as an early warning indicator was also examined. The findings contribute to a better understanding of the dynamic evolution mechanisms of reservoir landslides and provide a novel approach and methodology for landslide early warning.

Analysis of the evolution mechanism of landslide centroid frequency

As the moisture content of the landslide soil increases, lubrication layers form between soil particles, leading to a reduction in the soil’s shear modulus. Macroscopically, the soil exhibits viscoelastic behavior, showing certain viscous or plastic characteristics. These changes in the mechanical properties of the soil significantly affect the propagation of mechanical waves within the landslide medium, particularly by intensifying energy attenuation in the viscoelastic medium.

Landslide model test

The theoretical analysis in Sect. 2 indicates that wave energy attenuation is influenced by the shear modulus and other mechanical properties of the soil. Centroid frequency is considered an effective indicator for characterizing wave energy attenuation9,21. Therefore, analyzing the centroid frequency provides insight into energy dissipation and can be used to assess landslide stability. Physical model testing represents a fundamental method in geotechnical studies. The basic concept is to employ scaled models to explore the mechanisms governing complex processes. Landslide model tests make it possible to reproduce the full evolution of landslides in a controlled and repeatable manner within the laboratory, overcoming the difficulty of capturing such processes directly in the field5,22,23. Details of the test apparatus, model materials, testing procedures, and monitoring strategies used in this study can be found in Wu’s research24.

Overview of the test

The landslide model used in the test has a height of 1 m and a slope angle of 35°. The sliding mass has a density of 2.01 g/cm³, cohesion of 1.1 kPa, and internal friction angle of 19.5°. The sliding zone soil has a density of 1.81 g/cm³, cohesion of 0.5 kPa, and internal friction angle of 15.1°. The sliding bed was constructed using stacked bricks and stones.

The vibration signals analyzed in this study were captured from the ambient noise and environmental micro-tremors present within the laboratory setting. These signals primarily originate from the mechanical operation of the water circulation system and background environmental vibrations. To ensure the reliability of the centroid frequency shifts, the background noise spectrum was monitored and confirmed to be stable during the pre-test phase, providing a consistent baseline for identifying changes induced solely by the landslide’s internal structural degradation.

Accelerometers were embedded at three cross-sections of the landslide model: the upper, middle, and lower sections. At each section, accelerometers were installed both on the landslide surface and above the sliding zone. Two types of accelerometers with different frequency response ranges were used. The first type was used to calculate the natural frequency, while the second type—used in this study to calculate the centroid frequency—is shown in Fig. 1. Its performance parameters are listed in Table 1. The tests were divided into four groups, simulating reservoir water level fluctuations at rates of 1 m/d, 2 m/d, 3 m/d, and 4 m/d, respectively. Each simulation involved a total fluctuation height of 27 m and consisted of three complete water-level rise and fall cycles.

As a result, two cracks developed in the landslide body, but no landslide failure occurred.

As a result, five cracks developed in the landslide, and two landslide failures occurred during the second and third water-level drawdown phases, respectively.

Figure 4 shows the centroid frequency data from the entire process of the second group of tests. A pronounced frequency depression in centroid frequency is observed at Sects. 1 and 2 during the third water level fluctuation cycle. The amplitude of centroid frequency variation reached 9.79 Hz at Sects. 1 and 8.55 Hz at Sect. 2. This indicates that the landslide mass entered an unstable state following the water-level fluctuations. However, since the test was limited to three cycles, the landslide did not progress to actual failure. As centroid frequency changes tend to precede landslide failure, early signs of instability were captured in the data, even though no visible failure occurred during the test.

A pronounced frequency depression in centroid frequency was observed at Sect. 3 during all three fluctuation cycles, which deviates from the patterns observed in the other three test groups. Further analysis revealed signs of structural damage at the top of the landslide model (corresponding to the orange dashed box in Fig. 2b). This damage likely resulted from insufficient compaction during the construction of the model’s upper portion, representing a pre-existing flaw from the start of the test. Therefore, the observed failure was caused by a construction error, and the frequency depression in the centroid frequency data for Sect. 3 is considered a test artifact.

Figure 5 shows the centroid frequency data from the entire process of the third group of tests. A pronounced frequency depression in centroid frequency was observed at Sect. 1 prior to the landslide failure. The amplitude of centroid frequency change on the landslide surface was greater than that above the slip zone, with a maximum variation of 7.17 Hz. Sections 2 and 3 exhibited minor fluctuations without clear patterns, likely because the failure zone was located near Sect. 1—specifically around cracks 1 and 2—while Sects. 2 and 3 were situated farther from the failure zone, resulting in less pronounced changes.

Centroid frequency of the third group of tests. a Sect. 1, b Sect. 2, c Sect. 3.

Figure 6 shows the centroid frequency data from the entire process of the fourth group of tests. The centroid frequency at Sect. 1 exhibited three pronounced frequency depression, all occurring during the water level rise stages. The observed downward frequency depression in centroid frequency can be theoretically explained by Eq. (21). As reservoir levels rise, water infiltration reduces the soil’s shear modulus G. According to the derived relationship α ∝ 1/G, a decrease in G leads to a sharp increase in the energy absorption coefficient α. Since this attenuation is positively correlated with frequency ω, high-frequency components dissipate faster than low-frequency ones. This causes the spectral energy to shift toward the lower end, manifesting as the significant drop in centroid frequency observed prior to failure. The centroid frequency variations at the slope surface were greater than those above the slip zone. Specifically, the amplitudes of the three variations at the slope surface were 8.81 Hz, 11.32 Hz, and 8.69 Hz, respectively. Notably, the second drop displayed a significantly greater amplitude and range than the other two. Sections 2 and 3 showed some fluctuations in centroid frequency during the second and third water-level fluctuation cycles. This is because the landslide failure zone was located closer to Sect. 1, leading to more significant centroid frequency changes there. In contrast, Sects. 2 and 3 were farther from the failure zone, resulting in less noticeable variations.

Discussion

Analysis of central frequency differences at different monitoring positions.

Analysis of central frequency differences at different monitoring positions.

A comparative analysis of the monitoring results from the four test groups revealed that centroid frequency can effectively reflect the dynamic characteristics of landslide evolution. However, the sensitivity of centroid frequency to landslide changes varies significantly across different monitoring locations. This variation can be mainly attributed to the following factors:

①Cyclic fluctuations in water level cause the soil within the landslide body to repeatedly undergo swelling and shrinkage. This process gradually weakens the cementation between soil particles, leading to cumulative structural damage. As the damage accumulates, the mechanical properties of the soil deteriorate, resulting in changes in centroid frequency. The mid and lower sections of the landslide body are more affected by water level variations, exhibiting more pronounced structural degradation and thus greater sensitivity in centroid frequency.

The mid and lower sections of the landslide body are more affected by water level variations, exhibiting more pronounced structural degradation and thus greater sensitivity in centroid frequency.

②In loose media, the propagation of vibration waves is accompanied by energy attenuation, which is further amplified by the presence of cracks. As the propagation distance increases, the high-frequency components of the signal are more severely attenuated, increasing the relative proportion of low-frequency components. In areas near the toe of the landslide, the shorter wave propagation path results in less energy loss, making changes in centroid frequency more reflective of cumulative damage. In contrast, at the upper part of the landslide, where waves travel farther, the stronger attenuation of high-frequency components can mask the frequency changes caused by damage accumulation.

This leads to repeated cycles of soil swelling and shrinkage, resulting in more severe structural alterations and more noticeable changes in centroid frequency. This non-uniform infiltration is a key factor contributing to the differences in sensitivity of centroid frequency across monitoring locations.

(2)Comparative analysis of different monitoring data.

Comparative analysis of different monitoring data.

Taking the fourth group of tests as an example, a comparative study was conducted between the centroid frequency and other monitoring data from different test groups24. The comparison results are shown in Fig. 7.

Overall comparison of different monitoring indicators in the fourth test group.

The static indicators, such as displacement and inclination angle, exhibited continuous changes throughout the landslide process and showed sharp increases shortly before failure, making them key indicators for imminent landslide warnings. The dynamic indicator, namely the natural frequency, also experienced a sudden shift prior to the landslide failure. Notably, the first-order natural frequency changed earlier than the actual failure, indicating its potential as a critical early warning indicator. In the inundated zone (where failure occurred), the centroid frequency displayed a clear downward frequency depression during the water level rise phase. This frequency depression coincided with or even preceded the change in natural frequency. Furthermore, the magnitude of the concavity increased significantly before failure. These findings suggest that the centroid frequency can serve as a supplementary indicator for early warning in landslide monitoring.

(3)Complexity and influencing factors of centroid frequency evolution.

Complexity and influencing factors of centroid frequency evolution.

The test results revealed that the evolution of centroid frequency under different conditions is complex. In the first test group with a slower water level fluctuation rate, the centroid frequency remained generally stable. In contrast, in the second, third, and fourth test groups with faster fluctuation rates, the centroid frequency exhibited varying degrees of change, such as frequency depression and fluctuations. These findings indicate that the rate of water level fluctuation is a key factor influencing the evolution of centroid frequency. When the water level rises or falls slowly, the swelling–shrinkage behavior of the landslide mass is gradual, resulting in slower structural degradation and less variation in centroid frequency. In contrast, rapid fluctuations accelerate this process, leading to faster structural damage accumulation and more evident changes in centroid frequency.

Additionally, the sensitivity of centroid frequency to changes in landslide stability varies across different monitoring locations. This variation is closely related to several interacting factors, including the degree of influence from water level fluctuations at different positions, the propagation paths and energy attenuation of vibration waves, and the non-uniformity of water infiltration. These factors collectively determine the evolution pattern of centroid frequency, resulting in complex variations.

(4)Potential and limitations of centroid frequency as an early warning indicator.

Potential and limitations of centroid frequency as an early warning indicator.

The centroid frequency exhibits distinct variation patterns during the evolution of reservoir landslides. This is particularly evident under conditions of rapid reservoir water-level fluctuations, where its changes correlate with the dynamic characteristics of landslide instability, indicating its potential as an early-warning indicator. For instance, in the fourth test, a downward trend in centroid frequency was observed during the water level rise in the inundated area (where the slide occurred). The timing of this drop coincided with or preceded the variation in the first natural frequency, and its amplitude significantly increased prior to landslide failure. This phenomenon suggests that changes in centroid frequency could serve as an early-warning signal for impending landslides. The test results indicate that when the centroid frequency variation exceeds 7 Hz, it signifies a notable decrease in landslide stability, suggesting that early warning measures can be initiated.

The anomaly observed at Sect. 3 in the second test group—initially attributed to insufficient compaction—highlights a significant capability of the centroid frequency metric. This sensitivity suggests that beyond monitoring moisture-induced instability, centroid frequency can serve as an effective indicator for internal structural flaws or heterogeneous soil density. In engineering practice, this implies that spectral analysis could potentially be used for the quality assessment of landslide remediation works (e.g., compaction of backfill) or for identifying pre-existing weak zones within a slope.

However, the use of centroid frequency also presents certain limitations. Its sensitivity to landslide stability varies across monitoring locations, requiring data from multiple sensors for more accurate assessment. Additionally, the evolution of centroid frequency is influenced by a complex interplay of factors, and its quantitative relationship with landslide occurrence remains uncertain and requires further investigation. Moreover, measurement accuracy is affected by instrumentation precision and environmental noise, which could compromise the reliability of centroid frequency as a standalone early-warning metric.

(5)Limitations and future perspectives.

Limitations and future perspectives.

This study presents an initial exploration of the evolution pattern of centroid frequency in reservoir landslides under fluctuating water levels. While some meaningful findings were obtained, several limitations remain. For example, due to test constraints, the physical model scale is relatively small compared to real-world reservoir landslides, which limits the extrapolation of results. Additionally, the test only considered the water level fluctuation rate, while real landslides are influenced by multiple factors, such as rainfall, earthquakes, and geological structures. Future studies should incorporate these variables into the analysis.

The 7 Hz early-warning threshold identified in this study is subject to scaling effects. Future research will incorporate more field-scale monitoring data to refine and adjust this value for practical engineering applications.

Moreover, the measurement and analysis methods for centroid frequency require further refinement to enhance the accuracy and reliability of its use in early warning systems. Future work could involve larger-scale physical models with more complex boundary conditions, integration of numerical simulations, and multi-factor coupling analyses to establish a quantitative relationship between centroid frequency and landslide failure. These efforts would provide more scientifically grounded support for early warning of landslides.

Conclusions

This study investigated the evolution patterns of centroid frequency in reservoir landslides through physical model tests and explored its potential as an early warning indicator. The main conclusions are as follows:

Reservoir water level fluctuations drive centroid frequency evolution by modulating the soil’s viscoelastic properties. The reduction in shear modulus during saturation intensifies high-frequency energy attenuation, providing a physical basis for using centroid frequency as a stability indicator.

Reservoir water level fluctuations drive centroid frequency evolution by modulating the soil’s viscoelastic properties. The reduction in shear modulus during saturation intensifies high-frequency energy attenuation, providing a physical basis for using centroid frequency as a stability indicator.

A centroid frequency drop exceeding 7 Hz is identified as a robust precursor to landslide instability. This dynamic indicator often precedes or coincides with natural frequency shifts, offering a wider lead time for emergency response compared to traditional displacement-based metrics.

This study demonstrates that centroid frequency is highly sensitive to early-stage structural damage. Incorporating spectral monitoring at the landslide toe can significantly enhance the reliability of multi-parameter early-warning systems for reservoir geo-hazards.
