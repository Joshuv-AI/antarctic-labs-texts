Plant-based synthesis of silver nanoparticles using aqueous leaf extracts of Cinchona calisaya Wedd. and Cinchona pubescens Vahl: physicochemical characterisation and biological activities
Authors: Justin B. Safari, Victoire S. Ushindi, Fortunat C. Andema, Pacifique M. Hamuli, Emmanuel B. Irenge, Benny M. Vuangi, Eunice N. Zola, Bénédicte K. Baraka, Concilie M. Bilamirwa, Vivianne M. Matabaro, Franck P. Angbongbo, Victoire Nsabatien, Josué Zanga, Emery M. Metelo, Rui W. M. Krause, Aistė Balčiūnaitienė, Patrick B. Memvanga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Green synthesis approaches to produce silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have gained considerable attention recently due to their eco-friendliness and diverse applications in biotechnology, healthcare and environmental management. The present study explores the biogenic synthesis of AgNPs using aqueous extracts of Cinchona calisaya Wedd. and Cinchona pubescens Vahl. The research focuses on the physicochemical characterisation of AgNPs using several analytical techniques such as UV-visible spectroscopy, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), dynamic light scattering, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD), and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy. The obtained nanoparticles exhibited diverse sizes ranging from 23 ± 3 to 50 ± 9 nm, with a spherical shape observed on TEM images and with elemental composition containing Ag, N, C, O, etc. FTIR data indicated that chemicals containing carboxylic, hydroxyl, alkanes and carbonyl groups are bonded on the surface of AgNPs, while PXRD data indicated that the AgNPs were crystalline. Furthermore, a deep evaluation of the biological activities of the AgNPs was conducted. AgNPs exhibited moderate antioxidant, antibacterial and antifungal activities. Additionally, the produced AgNPs exhibited a dose-dependent potency for environmentally friendly vector control (larvicidal action) at the concentrations ranging from 200 to 400 ppm. The total phenolic content of C. calisaya and C. pubescens extracts and AgNPs was between 288.73 ± 20.42 mg/100 mL and 464.47 ± 16.44 mg/100 mL. This comprehensive investigation provides valuable insights into the potential of C. ledgeriana and C. pubescens leaf extracts as reducing and capping agents for AgNPs synthesis. The outcomes of this study have far-reaching implications for green nanotechnology, eco-friendly materials science and biomedicine. The diverse properties of AgNPs facilitate innovative healthcare and environmental sustainability applications, highlighting their potential to tackle significant global issues.

Introduction

In recent years, nanotechnology has revolutionised the scientific and technological landscape. Indeed, nanotechnology offers innovative solutions to address multifaceted challenges across diverse fields, including materials science, environment sciences and medicine [1–3]. Among these advancements, the preparation of nanoparticles through green routes has garnered substantial attention from researchers due to its eco-friendly and sustainable nature [4–6].

In this context, using plant extracts as reducing and capping agents in synthesising metallic nanoparticles is a promising way to reduce environmental impacts [7]. Adding to that, plant-based synthesis of nanoparticles enhances the efficacy of the produced nanoparticles [7–11]. Among the numerous nanomaterials that have gained considerable attention, AgNPs that are greenly prepared have emerged as promising candidates. This is because AgNPs have a wide range of applications in diverse domains, and they are easily prepared [12–14]. Additionally, AgNPs have demonstrated exceptional biological properties as well as prominent applications for environmental sustainability [15]. Therefore, AgNPs are invaluable in the realm of healthcare, biotechnology, and environment [15, 16]. This work explores the plant-based synthesis of AgNPs using aqueous leaf extracts of Cinchona calisaya Wedd. and Cinchona pubescens Vahl, two plant species belonging to the Rubiaceae family. It also investigates the physicochemical properties and biological activities (including antioxidant, antibacterial, antifungal, and larvicidal activities) of the produced AgNPs.

In the traditional medicine of the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), these species of Cinchona are used for their antidiabetic, anti-obesity, anti-inflammation and anti-malarial properties [17]. Indeed, C. calisaya and C. pubescens are well known by the scientific community for their content in quinine, quinidine, cinchonine and cinchonidine, which have several biological activities [18]. Although the traditional use of Cinchona species in the DRC as well as scientific data does not mention any antibacterial, antifungal and larvicidal activities, the oxidation of their phytochemicals (alkaloids, polyphenols, etc.) in the presence of silver ions may confer to these plants antioxidant, antibacterial, antifungal and larvicidal activities [19]. In addition, these phytochemicals can play a pivotal role in the reduction and stabilisation of AgNPs [20–22]. The use of plant extracts not only facilitates the synthesis of AgNPs but also imbues them with biocompatibility, making them suitable for biomedical applications [23].

This study presents a pioneering exploration of the green synthesis of AgNPs using leaf extracts of two species of Cinchona. Additionally, it offers a comprehensive analysis of the physicochemical attributes and a deep evaluation of the antibacterial, antifungal, antioxidant, and larvicidal activities of the AgNPs synthesised using Cinchona plant-mediated techniques. Doing so contributes to advancing green nanotechnology and developing novel strategies for addressing pressing global challenges, such as infectious diseases and environmental sustainability.

Materials and methods

Materials

Plant materials

Fresh leaves of C. calisaya and C. pubescens species were collected in Panzi (Bukavu, South Kivu, DRC) in August 2021 (2°31’59.8’’S 28°51’42.6E). These species were identified at the Department of Botany in the Faculty of Science of the Official University of Bukavu (DRC). The leaves were washed with tap water to remove any dust on the leaves and air-dried away from the sunlight at room temperature until no weight loss was recorded (after four weeks). The dried leaves were crushed using a wooden pestle and mortar and then sieved until a fine powder was obtained. The powder was stored in a brown glass container at room temperature in a dry place.

Chemicals and reagents

Silver nitrate (98%), ABTS (2, 2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulphonic acid)), Trolox (6-hydroxy-2, 5, 7, 8-tetramethylchroman-2-carboxylic acid), Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and sodium bicarbonate were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Ethanol (96.3% v/v) was obtained from Stumbras, AB (Kaunas, Lithuania). DPPH (2, 2-Diphenyl-1-(2, 4, 6-trinitrophenyl) hydrazin-1-yl), gallic acid and potassium bisulphate were purchased from Alfa Aesar GmbH & Co KG (Karlsruhe, Germany). At last, Mueller Hinton was purchased from Oxoid Limited (London, UK). All chemicals used were of analytical grade.

Biological materials

Microbiological materials used in this study included laboratory and clinical strains of bacteria, fungi and larvae. The bacterial strains were Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923, Proteus vulgaris ATCC 8427, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853, Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 51299, Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 13883, Proteus mirabilis ATCC 29906, and Escherichia coli ATCC 25922. The fungal strains comprised Candida albicans ATCC 32354 and clinical isolates of Candida albicans and Trichophyton spp. The larvicidal potency of AgNPs was evaluated using three strains of larvae, notably Anopheles gambiae Kisumu, Anopheles gambiae Wild strain and Culex quinquefasciatus Say. These larvae strains were collected from stagnant water in Kinshasa (DRC) during the rainy season. After collection, they were brought to the Bioecology and Vector Control Laboratory (BIOLAV) of the School of Public Health at the University of Kinshasa (Kinshasa, DRC). Once in the laboratory, larvae were stored in dechlorinated tap water and raised at 25 ± 3 °C with a relative humidity of 75 ± 5% and a 12:12 h light/dark cycle.

Methods

Preparation of extracts

Aqueous leaf extracts were obtained by decoction. Briefly, 5 g of each leaf powder was mixed with 100 mL of distilled water. The mixture was heated to ebullition, let to boil for 15 min and cooled at room temperature. After cooling, the mixture was filtered using the Whatman No. 01 filter paper, then centrifuged (4000 rpm, 15 min). The supernatant was collected and used for AgNPs synthesis [10].

Green synthesis of AgNPs

The method reported by Kambale et al.. (2020) was used with slight modifications [23]. In test tubes, 4 mL of each plant extract was mixed with 6 mL of 3.33 mM silver nitrate solution (to obtain 2 mM silver nitrate). The mixtures were incubated in a water bath (1XGN 1/1 220 V-MONO, Milan, Italy) at 70 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the mixtures were centrifuged (5000 rpm, 45 min). The precipitate consisting of AgNPs was collected and washed thrice with distilled water. Washed AgNPs were dried in an oven (UN 260 –256 L, Milan, Italy) at 50 °C for 24 h. After drying, AgNPs were stored in the dark at room temperature for further analysis.

Physicochemical characterisation

Visual observation and UV-vis spectroscopy

Progressive colour changes in the reaction medium visually noticed the formation of AgNPs. UV-vis spectroscopy analysis was performed to confirm the formation of AgNPs. For this, the biosynthesised AgNPs were dispersed in distilled water (0.5 mg/mL) and then sonicated for 30 min [23]. The spectra were recorded from 250 to 700 nm and operated at a resolution of 1 nm by using a Perkin Elmer Lambda 25 UV/Vis spectrophotometer (California, USA).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM Zeiss Libra-120KV (Berlin, Germany) was used to visualise the AgNPs. The sample prepared for UV-vis analysis was dropped onto carbon-coated copper TEM grids. Excess liquid was absorbed using filter paper, and the grids were left to air dry overnight at room temperature before visualising the particles [23]. The ImageJ software was used to visualise the TEM images to get information on the size distribution of the nanoparticle samples.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS)

The size distribution of AgNPs was investigated using a Zetasizer nano ZEN-3600 MAL 1,043,132 from Malvern Instruments (London, UK). The diluted samples prepared for UV-Vis were used to perform dynamic light scattering (DLS). The sample containing AgNPs in water was added to a cuvette and introduced into the sample holder of the zeta sizer. The hydrodynamic size of AgNPs was determined by analysing the modulation of scattered light intensity depending on time [24].

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

A PerkinElmer Spectrum 100 FTIR Spectrometer (California, USA) was used for the FTIR analysis. The infrared spectra were obtained using the attenuated total reflection method. The sample consisted of the powder of dried nanoparticles, which was placed on the crystal of the instrument. For each experiment, 16 scans were performed in the frequency range from 600 to 4000 cm− 1 [25].

X-ray diffraction spectroscopy (XRD)

XRD was used to examine the crystallinity of the AgNPs. Samples were placed on a metal sample holder. The sample holder was inserted in a Diffractometer X-ray, D2 Phaser, 2nd generation instrument (Bruker, USA). Diffractograms were recorded in the two theta (2θ) range from 5 to 60° with a scan rate of 2 °/min and a slot width of 6.0 mm [25].

Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX)

Elemental analysis of nanoparticles was determined by using an INCA PENTA FET coupled to VAGA TESCAM (Libusina, Czech Republic) energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). The biosynthesised nanoparticle samples were fixed on aluminium stubs, coated with gold, and then the EDX image was recorded at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV [26].

Determination of total phenolic content

The total phenolic content (TPC) in the plant extracts and AgNPs was assessed using the Folin-Ciocalteu method [27]. The absorbance of the samples was measured using a Cintra 202 (GBC Scientific Equipment, Knox, Braeside, VIC, Australia) spectrophotometer. Briefly, 100 mL of the plant extracts or the resulting quantity of AgNPs in suspension were mixed with 0.75 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (previously diluted 10-fold with distilled water) and let react at room temperature for 5 min. Then, 0.75 mL of sodium bicarbonate (60 mg/mL) solution was added and thoroughly mixed. After 90 min at room temperature, the sample was analysed by UV-vis spectrophotometer at 765 nm [28]. A gallic acid calibration curve with a concentration range of 0.25-5 mg/mL was used to estimate the TPC. The total phenolic content was expressed as mg of gallic acid equivalent (GAE) per 100 mL of samples (plant extracts or AgNPs).

Evaluation of the antioxidant activity of AgNPs

The antioxidant activity was assessed by ABTS*+ radical cation scavenging and DPPH* free radical scavenging assays. The concentrations of nanoparticles used in the antioxidant activity assays ranged from 25 to 200 µg/mL, selected based on preliminary screening for biological activity and literature precedence. The IC₅₀ values were calculated using non-linear regression analysis (sigmoidal dose–response curve) performed in GraphPad Prism (v9) by plotting percentage inhibition against the log-transformed concentration values.

ABTS*+ radical cation scavenging assay

The parent ABTS*+ radical solution was prepared by oxidising an aqueous solution of ABTS (2 mM) with a solution of potassium persulfate (0.7 mM). The prepared solution was stored for 16–17 h in the dark at room temperature until the reaction of ABTS and potassium persulfate was complete [29].

The working ABTS*+ solution was prepared by diluting the parent ABTS*+ solution with distilled water to 0.8 ± 0.02 absorption units at the wavelength of 734 nm. In a quartz cuvette, 20 µL of each sample (AgNPs suspension and plant extracts) prepared previously were mixed with 2 mL of working ABTS*+ solution. After 30 min, a spectrophotometer Cintra 202 (GBC Scientific Equipment, Knox, Braeside, VIC, Australia) was used to determine the decrease in sample light absorbance at 734 nm. The antiradical activity was expressed as Trolox equivalent (TE) (µmol/mL of the sample) based on the Trolox calibration curve. Trolox concentration range 8000–24,000 µM.

DPPH* free radical scavenging assay

The DPPH* free radical scavenging activity was assessed using the method developed by Brand-Williams [30]. In brief, 20 µL of each sample (AgNPs suspension and plant extracts) obtained previously were mixed with 2 mL of DPPH*solution in 99.0% v/v ethanol in a quartz cuvette. After 30 min, the decrease in light absorbance was determined at 515 nm using a Cintra 202 (GBC Scientific Equipment, Knox, Braeside, VIC, Australia) spectrophotometer. Antiradical activity when applying the DPPH* method was expressed as µmol of the Trolox equivalent (TE) per mL of sample. Trolox concentration range was 400–20,000 µmol [31].

Evaluation of the antibacterial activity of AgNPs

The in vitro antibacterial activity of AgNPs was evaluated using the Agar diffusion test [30]. Briefly, 0.5 McFarland unit density suspension (108 CFU/mL) of bacterial strain was inoculated onto the cooled Mueller-Hinton Agar using sterile cotton swabs. Wells of 6 mm diameter were punched in the agar and filled with 50 µL of the AgNPs suspension (or plant extracts). After incubation of Agar plates at 37 °C for 24 h, inhibition zones were measured and tabulated. Three replicates were carried out for each sample.

Evaluation of antifungal activity of AgNPs

Antifungal activity of the biosynthesised AgNPs was also investigated using the Agar diffusion test [30]. For this purpose, Mueller Hinton Agar was prepared and sterilised at 121 °C for 15 min using an autoclave (CertoClav EL 181,115/121 °C, Milan, Italy). After sterilisation, the Agar was cooled, and 0.5 McFarland unit density suspension (108 CFU/mL) of fungal strain was inoculated onto the Agar using sterile cotton swabs. After this, 50 µL of the AgNPs suspension (or plant extracts) were added into wells of 6 mm diameter (previously punched in the agar). Agar plates were incubated at 37 °C for 72 h, and inhibition zones were measured and tabulated. For each sample, three replicates were performed.

Evaluation of larvicidal activity of AgNPs

The larvicidal potency of AgNPs was evaluated following the protocol proposed by the World Health Organisation [31] with some modifications. Twenty larvae at 3rd and 4th larval instars of different strains were added to 100 mL of AgNP suspension at 200, 300 and 400 ppm concentrations. Larvae were brought into contact with AgNPs suspension for 24 h at 25 ± 3 °C. Results and discussion

Physicochemical characterisation of AgNPs

Observation of colour change and UV-visible characterisation

The mixture of silver nitrate solution with C. calisaya or C. pubescens leaf extracts led both to a yellowish solution (Fig. 1a). After 24 h of incubation, the colour of the solution became brownish (Fig. 1b ). The manifestation of colour change indicated the reduction of silver ions into metallic silver, which testified to the formation of AgNPs; several researchers had observed and reported similar observations [23]. It is well known that silver ions get reduced into AgNPs in the presence of plant extracts through the reducing properties of certain secondary metabolites (polyphenols, alkaloids, terpenoids, etc.), and this expresses itself by a change in colour [32]. Interestingly, when added to the silver nitrate solution, the aqueous extracts of the two plants turned into an identical colour. This suggests that similar phytochemicals might have reacted with silver ions, indicating that C. calisaya and C. pubescens may have similar phytochemicals.

Observation of colour change: a 1: CC extract + AgNO3 and 2: CP extract + AgNO3 before reaction and b 3: CC extract + AgNO3 and 4: CP extract + AgNO3 after reaction

To further investigate the formation of AgNPs, UV-vis spectroscopy was carried out. Figure 2. presents the result of this characterisation. It is depicted that the maximum absorbance (λ max) values for the synthesised AgNPs using C. pubescens and C. calisaya leaf extracts were detected at around 448 nm and 481 nm, respectively. These results agreed with other research reporting that AgNPs exhibit maximum UV-Vis absorption in the range of 400–500 nm, depending on their size distribution [23, 3334]. Hence, the UV-visible results confirmed the effective synthesis of AgNPs using C. calisaya and C. pubescens leaf extracts. Additionally, the maximum absorbance values obtained for the synthesised AgNPs at around 450 nm suggest the presence of spherical-shaped AgNPs [23].

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), being the fundamental characterisation for the study of materials at the nanometric scale, was used to determine the size, morphology, and polydispersity of the synthesised AgNPs [35]. Figure 3 presents the TEM results of AgNPs synthesised using C. calisaya and C. pubescens leaf extracts. It is apparent from this figure that the AgNPs were of a size less than 100 nm (average of 22.6 ± 3.2 nm for AgNPs-CC and 49.9 ± 7.9 nm for AgNPs-CP). Additionally, the TEM micrographs showed the presence of individual and spherical-shaped nanoparticles, thereby confirming the implication of their absorbance values and the role of phytochemicals in preventing the formation of aggregates [23, 25]. Finally, using the size distribution data of the TEM image, the polydispersity was calculated and found to be 4.15 % and 25.04 % for AgNPs-CC and AgNPs-CP, respectively.

Dynamic light scattering

The size of the synthesised AgNPs was also assessed using dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis. Figure 4 shows that the average size of AgNPs mediated by C. calisaya (A) and C. pubescens (B) was found to be 11.7 ± 4.8 nm and 50 ± 9.3 nm, respectively. Both the AgNPs obtained using C. calisaya and C. pubescens had an average size of less than 100 nm, with AgNPs-CL being smaller than AgNPs-CP. DLS result corroborates the TEM results, which is rarely the case due to the difference in the behaviour of the nanoparticles when in water (DLS) or in the dried state (TEM), as well as the irregularity of the surface of AgNPs attributed to the presence of phytocompounds (capping agents) [36].

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The functional group characteristics of AgNPs were further investigated through FTIR spectroscopy. Figure 5 shows a wide range of stretching vibration bands for the AgNPs, confirming the presence of organic molecules. Indeed, the presence of vibration bands at around 3315–3188 cm− 1, 2913–2823 cm− 1, 1635–1615 cm− 1, and 1050–1025 cm− 1 showed on the FTIR spectra correspond to the vibrations of alcoholic (O–H), alkanes (C-H), carbonyl (C=O and C–O) stretches, respectively [37, 38]. Therefore, the FTIR results confirmed some phytochemicals on the surface of the synthesised AgNPs. Interestingly, stretch bands related to (N–O) are not noticed on the FTIR spectra, thereby indicating the absence of traces of nitrates, which could come from the precursor salt. Thus, the silver nitrate was completely reduced to AgNPs. Furthermore, both AgNPs prepared using C. calisaya and C. pubescens exhibited almost the same range of vibrations. As suggested by the manifestation of an identical colouration when the two plant extracts were added to the silver nitrate solution, the outcome of FTIR might confirm the presence of similar biomolecules in the two extracts involved in the reduction of silver. Further bioprospecting studies might be needed to confirm or reject this hypothesis. In addition, several studies revealed that species from the cinchona gender have a variety of secondary metabolites, including polyphenols (phenolic acids, anthocyanins, tannins and flavonoids), terpenoids, saponins, quinoline alkaloids, and indole alkaloids, which contain the functional groups observed in the FTIR spectrum [39, 40]. Moreover, another recent study demonstrated that phytochemicals such as flavonoids, terpenoids, anthocyanins, and coumarins were present in the capping agent on the surface of silver nanoparticles synthesised by plant extracts [10], which corroborates that these groups of compounds intervened in the reduction of silver.

X-ray diffraction spectroscopy

The crystallinity of the synthesised AgNPs was studied using X-ray diffraction spectroscopy (XRD). Figure 6 exhibits the XRD patterns of both AgNPs obtained using C. calisaya and C. pubescens leaf extracts. On the diffractograms of both AgNPs, two distinct crystalline peaks were depicted at 2θ values of 38.2° and 44.4º. From which the Miller indices values (hkl) were calculated using the indexing approach and were found to be (111) and (200), corresponding to 38.2° and 44.4º, respectively. These values are aligned with the crystal planes (111) and (200) according to the International Centre for Diffraction Data (ICDD) card no. 04–0783. These peaks and hkl values are characteristic of AgNPs, as reported by similar results using different plants [11, 41, 42]. In addition, the crystallinity of both nanoparticles was calculated and was found to be 58.2 % and 47.5 % for AgNPs CP and AgNPs CC, respectively. On the other hand, the Debye-Scherrer equation was utilised to calculate the crystalline size, which was respectively 14.8 and 12.8 nm for AgNPs CP and AgNPs CC.

Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX)

The surface elemental composition of the synthesised AgNPs was evaluated using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDX). Figure 7 shows the results of this investigation. The signal at 3 keV indicates the existence of elemental silver reduced by plant extracts. The presence of signals of the other elements (C, O, Cl and N) together with silver gives once more an idea of the phytochemical composition of plant extracts. Remarkably, although the FTIR spectra did not show a stretch band related to (N–O), the outcome from EDX analysis reveals the presence of nitrogen (N) on the surface of AgNPs. Moreover, EDX elemental composition of AgNPs-CC indicated that it contained 16.3, 28.6, 5.3, 49.4 and 0.25 % of C, Ag, N, O and Cl, respectively; while AgNPs-CP is composed of 17.5, 36.2, 6.2, and 40.1 % of C, Ag, N, and O, respectively. It is obvious that the nitrogen (N) noticed on EDX spectroscopy indicates the presence of alkaloids, and not of nitrates, on the surface of AgNPs. Indeed, Cinchona species contain more than twenty sorts of alkaloids (15 %) in which the nitrogen is directly linked to carbon [16], thereby explaining the presence of carbon and nitrogen on the surface of AgNPs. Hence, the FTIR results are consistent with EDX records concerning the existence of organic compounds (capping agents) on the surface of AgNPs, responsible for reducing silver ions.

Antioxidant activity and total phenolic contents of AgNPs

Antioxidant activity cannot be adequately assessed using only one method. Indeed, it is recommended to use at least two different methods to determine antioxidant activity in plant extracts [29]. For this reason, the antioxidant activity of AgNPs derived from C. calisaya and C. pubescens was evaluated using two different in vitro spectrophotometric methods, namely ABTS and DPPH methods. According to Fig. 8a, which presents these ABTS and DPPH radical scavenging activities, it is apparent that the synthesised AgNPs exhibited strong antioxidant potency compared with the corresponding plant extracts. Thus, in DPPH assay, both AgNPs derived from C. calisaya and C. pubescens exhibited a slightly higher antioxidant activity (IC5O 19.31 ± 2.36 µmol/mL and 21.66 ± 4.33 µmol/mL, respectively) than the corresponding plant extracts (IC50 24.01 ± 1.08 µmol/mL and 28.79 ± 1.44 µmol/mL, respectively) at the same concentration (p˂0.05). The outcome of the ABTS assay also suggested that the radical scavenging activity of AgNPs (IC50 24.08 ± 2.32 µmol/mL for AgNPs-CL and 40.95 ± 4.17 µmol/ml for AgNPs-CP) was somewhat more significant in comparison with plant extracts (IC50 38.11 ± 2.14 µmol/mL and 44.65 ± 3.62 µmol/mL, respectively) (p < 0.05). Notably, AgNPs derived from C. calisaya exhibited excellent antioxidant potency than AgNPs derived from C. pubescens. This is probably due to the size of AgNPs and the content of phytochemicals in the two species.

Furthermore, from Fig. 8b, it can be depicted that both plant extracts and AgNPs were richer in phenolic compounds expressed as GAE per mg/100 mL. Indeed, the average TPC values of C. calisaya and C. pubescens aqueous extracts were 364.01 ± 20.37 mg/100 mL and 464.47 ± 16.44 mg/100 mL, respectively. On the other hand, AgNPs derived from C. calisaya and C. pubescens contained approximately 288.73 ± 20.42 mg/100 mL and 419.57 ± 20.07 mg/100 mL of TPC, respectively. The TPC of AgNPs suspension confirms the presence of biochemicals containing phenolic compounds on the surface of the AgNPs. Previous research using different plants has reported similar results [36].

Antimicrobial activity of AgNPs

The antimicrobial activity of AgNPs derived from C. calisaya and C. pubescens leaf extracts was investigated against some Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacterial strains and fungi. The results presented in Table 1 show that C. calisaya and C. pubescens extracts presented no activity for almost all the studied microorganisms except for Staphylococcus aureus and Enterococcus faecalis. These results confirm why Congolese traditional medicine does not use Cinchona species to treat microbial infections. However, the prepared AgNPs exhibited moderate antimicrobial activity against all the microbial strains in this study. It can be concluded that AgNPs interact actively with the bacterial membrane and disrupt their functions. In addition, the activities do not significantly change either when we have gram-positive or negative strains or against the fungi.

Antimicrobial activity of AgNPs

Based on all these results, we concluded that AgNPs greenly synthesised using Cinchona species showed much greater antimicrobial activity than the plant extracts. These results support data previously reported by Kambale et al. [23]. Indeed, nearly all the studied microbial strains resisted C. calisaya and C. pubescens leaf extracts, whereas the synthesised AgNPs were active in all cases.

Nevertheless, it should be pointed out that thanks to chemical hemisynthesis, phytochemicals (alkaloids) from Cinchona species are today used as precursors in the synthesis of new molecules likely to have additional biological properties, including antibacterial and antifungal activities [18].

The mechanism by which silver nanoparticles act against microbes is not yet fully understood. However, in case of antibacterial activity, studies have shown that when they come into contact with bacterial membranes, a change occurs in their structure (such as an increase in permeability) and function (disruption of membrane transport), leading to cell death [43]. This action is presumed to be due to a dual interaction: firstly, an electrostatic interaction between Ag + ions and the negatively charged cell membrane of bacteria, and secondly, an interaction between silver nanoparticles and the disulphide groups of bacterial membrane proteins [43, 44]. Other intracellular mechanisms after penetration into the bacterium, such as action on certain key organelles of bacterial metabolism such as mitochondria and ribosomes, are also reported [15, 43, 45]. Moreover, AgNPs could also interact with bacterial DNA either directly by binding to their phosphate groups or by producing reactive oxygen species that cause DNA modification and denaturation and cell death [46]. In general, AgNPs are more active on Gram-negative bacteria than on Gram-positive bacteria. This is thought to be due to the fact that Gram-positive bacteria have a thicker membrane than Gram-negative bacteria, acting as a barrier to the penetration of AgNPs [43, 45, 46].

On the other hand, the antifungal mechanisms of AgNPs presumably include membrane disruption, interactions with DNA, destruction of hyphae and spores, and the generation of reactive oxygen species [16, 47]. In addition, another mechanism regulating genes and controlling specific fungal protein levels after exposure to AgNPs could be responsible for disrupting the homeostasis of fungal organisms [47].

Larvicidal activity of biosynthesised AgNPs

The percentages of larval mortality observed in all bioassays are presented in Fig. 9. It is apparent from this figure that the suspension of AgNPs derived from C. calisaya and C. pubescens exhibited a dose-dependent toxic effect against larval strains. However, it was noted that the larvicidal potency of the AgNPs suspension and the plant extracts was almost identical at the tested concentrations (p˂0.05, data not shown). Furthermore, the larval toxicity of AgNPs and plant extracts was noticed only at concentrations higher than 200 ppm, suggesting that C. calisaya and C. pubescens may have no excellent larvicidal activity. Nevertheless, it should be noted that the apparent weak larvicidal activity of AgNPs was probably due to their weak solubility in water. Hence, to improve the solubility of AgNPs, (ultra)sonication- or microwave-assisted treatment could be done in further studies. Noteworthy, these results confirm one more why Congolese traditional medicine does not use Cinchona species as vector control agents but only for a curative treatment by oral administration [48].

Conclusion

The studied AgNPs were synthesised using an eco-friendly plant-based method using C. calisaya and C. pubescens aqueous leaf extracts. The results suggest that the plant extracts affected the size of AgNPs. Indeed, the average size of AgNPs mediated by C. calisaya and C. pubescens ranged between 10 and 50 nm. Moreover, the FTIR and EDX results concerning the existence of organic compounds on the surface of AgNPs indicate that phytochemicals from plant extracts acted as capping and stabilising agents. The antimicrobial investigation revealed that AgNPs greenly synthesised had much greater antimicrobial activity than the corresponding plant extracts. Furthermore, the biosynthesised AgNPs exerted a dose-dependent larvicidal potency and moderate antioxidant properties. Therefore, additional research should be carried out to identify the phytochemicals that bind to AgNPs and to enhance the solubility of AgNPs in water so that they can be adequately used for biological purposes or environmental sustainability.
