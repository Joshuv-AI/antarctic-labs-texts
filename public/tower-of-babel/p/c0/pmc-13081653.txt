Youth perspectives on distracted driving and hands-free laws
Authors: Carson Downer, Renée St. Louis, Xochitl Amaro, Jennifer Zakrajsek, Rohit Agarwal, Marika Waselewski, Tammy Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMotor vehicle crashes remain a leading cause of death among youth in the United States, and distracted driving—particularly related to cell phone use—is a key contributing factor. Hands-free laws have been enacted in many states to reduce this risk by limiting handheld phone use while driving. However, little is known about how youth perceive these laws, their level of awareness, or whether they believe such policies are effective in changing behavior. This study explores young people’s knowledge and perspectives on these laws and identifies potential strategies from the youth perspective to prevent distracted driving.MethodsThis study used the MyVoice nationwide text message poll of youth aged 14–24 years, recruited through community outreach and social media advertising, to reflect national benchmarks. In March 2024, youth were sent five open-ended questions on distracted driving, including cell phone use, state laws, and prevention strategies. Responses underwent qualitative content analysis with an iteratively developed codebook; two researchers independently coded all data and resolved discrepancies through discussion. The coding team included survey experts, topical experts, and youth advisors. Demographics were summarized with descriptive statistics.ResultsA total of 756 youth were invited to participate; 577 (RR = 76.3%) responded to at least one question. Respondents were 50.4% female, 52.2% non-Hispanic White, and 67.8% lived in a state with a hands-free law. Among respondents, 74.6% reported phones as a significant distraction, and 63.9% viewed phone use while driving as dangerous. While 80.3% of respondents were aware of their state’s distracted driving laws, 62.7% perceived hands-free laws as only somewhat or not effective. As one response noted, hands-free laws “makes it more risky as they will try to not get caught and still do the texting.” Youth suggested improved education (25.1%), enhanced phone settings (20.8%), and advanced car technology (19.6%) as more effective approaches.ConclusionsYouth recognize the dangers of distracted driving but continue to engage in phone use while driving, suggesting that laws alone are insufficient to change youth driving behaviors. Youth-centered strategies, including improved education, technological innovation, and engagement with existing smartphone tools, may more effectively reduce distracted driving and improve safety.

Background

Motor vehicle crashes are a leading cause of death among youth in the United States. Youth exhibit a higher prevalence of risky driving practices that contribute to serious crashes, including speeding, running red lights, and distracted driving [1]. In recent years, approximately 10% of teen drivers involved in fatal crashes were distracted at the time [2]. Cell phone use is a common distraction while driving, with studies showing young drivers frequently use their cell phones while driving [3].

In response, many states have enacted hands-free laws, which prohibit or limit holding a phone while driving [4]. However, the effectiveness of these laws remains uncertain. Some research suggests they reduce phone use [5], while others find minimal or no impact [6, 7], particularly among youth. Despite the reach of such legislation, little is known about how young drivers perceive these laws or what strategies they believe are most effective. This study explores youth perspectives on cell phone use while driving, their awareness and perceptions of hands-free laws, and their ideas to reduce distracted driving.

Methods

This study used the MyVoice nationwide text message poll of youth aged 14–24 years. This age range aligns with the United Nations’ definition of youth and reflects the diverse developmental stages of young people [8]. Recruitment was conducted through community outreach and targeted social media advertising using nationally representative benchmarks for age, gender, race, and geographic region using the American Community Survey [9]. Eligible participants provided consent and completed a demographic survey online. Participants received $1 per weekly response and $5 for completing the demographic survey. Further information about MyVoice survey procedures is published elsewhere [10].

In March 2024, five open-ended questions were sent to 756 youth across the United States: (1) What are some things that take your attention away from the road while driving? If you don’t drive, what are things you have seen that distract drivers? (2) What do you think about using a cell phone while driving? (3) Does your state have a law against using a cell phone while driving? If yes, tell us about it. (4) Hands-free laws typically make it illegal to hold a phone with your hands, arms, or shoulders while driving. How do you think these laws influence young people’s phone use while driving? (5) What do you think would help young people not use their phones while driving?

Responses were analyzed using qualitative content analysis [11, 12]. The research team iteratively developed a codebook and two researchers independently coded every response, resolving discrepancies through discussion. Researchers included survey experts, topical experts, and youth, and were not necessarily affected by distracted driving. Nonsensical responses (< 5%) were excluded. Descriptive statistics were calculated using Excel 2021 and SAS. The University of Michigan IRB approved all protocols, and the study follows EQUATOR qualitative research guidelines [13]. Parental consent was waived for minors, and all participants provided consent or assent online at the time of study enrollment.

Results

Of the 756 youth participants, 577 (76.3%) responded to at least one survey question. The average age was 20.2 years (SD = 2.6), 50.4% identified as female, 52.2% non-Hispanic White, and 22.9% reported low socio-economic status (current or previous SNAP use). Respondents represented 501 zip codes across 49 states, and 67.8% lived in a state with a hands-free law. Full demographic data can be found in Table 1. Code frequencies and representative quotes are presented in Table 2.

Discussion

This study found that a large majority of youth (74.6%) reported that cell phones are a major distraction while driving, with texting, music, phone calls, and notifications frequently cited. This is consistent with another study of 18-year-old high school student drivers in the United States who report making frequent and prolonged glances toward a phone while driving [14]. Despite widespread awareness of distracted driving laws, 62.7% of youth felt that hands-free laws are only somewhat or not effective in reducing phone use while driving. Youth acknowledged that distracted driving is dangerous yet still described frequent use. These findings highlight a continued disconnect between the awareness of the risks and distracted driving behavior among youth, suggesting that recent efforts, including hands-free laws, may not be sufficient to address youth phone use while driving.

Youth proposed a range of strategies to reduce distracted driving, including education, public service campaigns, and improvements to phone and in-car technology. These findings suggest the value of youth-centered approaches that make non-distracted driving easier or more automated. However, a minority spontaneously mentioned using current phone tools that already exist for this purpose [15], suggesting gaps in awareness, accessibility, or perceived usefulness. Notably, only 16.1% of responses called for more laws or harsher punishments, and just 7.2% emphasized better enforcement. As one participant explained, “Young people will do what they want regardless of the consequences,” reflecting widespread skepticism toward punitive approaches. As cell phone use continues to be central to all aspects of life, parents, healthcare providers, and other youth-serving individuals may also serve a critical role as models and educators on strategies for safe driving among youth.

Limitations

While this study used a large nationwide sample, it is not nationally representative. It was limited to youth with text messaging access. Our data is also subject to desirability bias and inaccuracies, and the automated text message format prevented clarification or follow-up. We did not ask if participants were drivers, and differences in knowledge about state laws or enforcement may have shaped responses. Additionally, we did not assess how frequently youth engage in cell phone use while driving or how this behavior relates to their perceptions of danger; future research could examine the relationship between perceived risk and self-reported driving behaviors to inform education and policy efforts.

Conclusions

Youth in our nationwide sample recognized the dangers of distracted driving and were aware of hands-free laws, yet many reported continued phone use behind the wheel. These perceptions suggest that current laws alone are not enough to shift behavior—particularly when enforcement is limited or laws are poorly understood. Youth-proposed solutions emphasized education, improved car technology, and tools like “Do Not Disturb” mode on phones that make reducing distraction automated and easier to use. Centering youth in the design of education campaigns and tech interventions may improve effectiveness and acceptance.
