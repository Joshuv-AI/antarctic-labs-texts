Regulation of physicochemical properties of wheat starch by common natural Pectins: Insights into gelation characteristics and interaction mechanisms
Authors: Kaiyue Liu, Jinfeng Bi, Xiang Zhang, Jianyong Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The regulatory effects of four common natural pectins-apple pectin (AP), citrus pectin (CP), lemon pectin (LP), and sugar beet pectin (SP)-on the physicochemical properties of wheat starch (WS) were systematically compared at an equal mass addition level of 1.0% (w/v), and the intermolecular interaction mechanisms were investigated. The introduction of all four pectins was found to enhance the gelatinization, rheological, and thermal properties of WS. Notably, the most pronounced improvements were observed in AP, where the peak viscosity and viscoelasticity were augmented by 4.72 times and 15.82 times, respectively (p<0.05). The WS-AP composite gel likewise exhibited superior swelling power (16.13 g/g, p<0.05). Further analyses showed hydrogen bonding dominated WS interactions with AP, CP, and SP, while electrostatic forces were secondary. In contrast, the opposite was observed for LP. These findings provide theoretical support for the modulation of WS characteristics by common pectins from diverse sources.

Introduction

As the primary dietary source of carbohydrates, wheat starch has been widely employed as a stabilizer and emulsifier in the food industry due to its abundant availability and renewability (Wang et al., 2023). However, its limited thermal stability, low pasting viscosity, and poor swelling capacity have constrained the broader application of wheat starch gels (Zhao et al., 2021). To address these limitations, various modification strategies for wheat starch have been actively explored. Compared with physical complexation of wheat starch with non-starch polysaccharides and hydrophilic gums, chemical modification may result in residual organic reagents, posing risks to human health and the environment, while enzymatic modification is associated with high costs. Consequently, interactions between starch and non-starch polysaccharides or hydrophilic gums have been highlighted as a research focus in recent years (Lu et al., 2025; Mahmood et al., 2017).

As a natural polysaccharide present in plant cell walls, pectin is composed of galacturonic acid, rhamnose, galactose, and arabinose, and comprises three structural domains: homogalacturonan (HG), rhamnogalacturonan-I (RG-I), and rhamnogalacturonan-II (RG-II). Interactions between pectin and starch are predominantly governed by noncovalent forces, including hydrogen bonding, van der Waals interactions, and electrostatic interactions (Chen et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2022). These molecular-level interactions are known to modulate the macroscopic physicochemical properties of starch gels. The interaction mechanisms in starch-non-starch polysaccharide systems are generally ascribed to three aspects: competitive adsorption of free water molecules, encapsulation of starch granules by polysaccharides, and chain entanglement and cross-linking between polysaccharide and starch molecules (Zhuang et al., 2024).

It has been reported that pectin can elevate the pasting temperature, pasting viscosity, and viscoelasticity of starch (Chen, Williams, et al., 2023). Yin et al. (2021) has shown that RG-I pectin can suppress the swelling of starch granules and the leaching of amylose. Furthermore, in comparison with low pectin concentrations, high pectin concentrations have been found to be more favorable for increasing the peak viscosity during starch pasting (Ma et al., 2019). In our previous work, pectin with a high degree of esterification and pectin with high molecular weight were both found to enhance the thermal stability and viscoelasticity of starch (Liu, Bi, Ma, & Yi, 2026). In recent years, many studies have examined the interactions between pectin (and other non-starch polysaccharide hydrocolloids) and starch; however, most have focused on the mutual effects of starch with pectin of varying concentrations, structural features or modification treatments (Pan, Chen, & Xie, 2025; Wang et al., 2024). Systematic evaluations addressing how natural pectin from different botanical sources regulate starch gelatinization, rheology, and thermal behavior, as well as their underlying interaction mechanisms, have remained limited. The most recent study (Chen, Williams, et al., 2023) exploring the effects of pectin from different sources on starch physicochemical properties was published in 2023, wherein Citrus pectin, apple pectin, and creeping fig seeds pectin were assessed for their impacts on starch pasting and rheological properties, together with adsorption capacities of different pectin, from the perspective that pectin can be adsorbed within starch granules. Nevertheless, effects on gel viscoelasticity, thermal characteristics, swelling power, and microstructure were not characterized, nor was the strength and nature of noncovalent interactions such as hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions further resolved.

Based on the aforementioned facts and considerations, the present study was primarily conducted to investigate the effects of four common pectins from different sources-apple pectin, citrus pectin, lemon pectin, and sugar beet pectin-on the physicochemical properties of WS. The interaction mechanisms between the four pectins and WS were further elucidated by the addition of NaCl and urea, as well as by isothermal titration calorimetry. Initially, the monosaccharide composition, molecular weight, degree of esterification, and structural features of the four pectins were characterized. Subsequently, the effects of the four pectins on the gelatinization, rheological, and thermal properties of WS, along with the swelling power, were reported, and changes in the functional groups and microstructure of the composite gels were assessed. Finally, to clarify the molecular-level interaction mechanisms, the complexation processes between WS and the four pectins were further visualized through the addition of NaCl and urea and by isothermal titration calorimetry. The findings of this study have provided a deeper understanding of the interactions between common natural pectins and starch, and a theoretical foundation has been established for the targeted modification of the physicochemical properties of starch using various pectins to enhance the utilization of starch-based foods.

Materials and methods

Materials

Pectin from apple (Classic APA170; DM = 75 ± 3%; ash <1%) and citrus (Classic APC104; DM = 64 ± 2%; ash <1%) was kindly supplied in dry powder form by DSM Andre Pectin (Shandong Andre Group Co., Ltd., Yantai, China). Lemon pectin was kindly provided in dry powder form by Chongqing Mengtai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Chongqing, China). The sugar beet pectin (GR10002805; Ash <1%) was purchased from Azeles International Trading Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Native wheat starch (S25804; purity >90.00%; amylose, 25.62%; amylopectin, 70.33%) was obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade. Deionized water was used throughout the experiments.

Pectin purification

To remove soluble low-molecular weight sugars, the powdered pectin was purified with 80% (v/v) ethanol/water solution (Feng et al., 2023).

Monosaccharide composition of pectins

The monosaccharide composition and contents of pectin were determined by trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) hydrolysis coupled with high-performance anion-exchange chromatography (HPAEC; ICS-3000, Dionex, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) equipped with a Dionex CarboPac PA20 column and a pulsed amperometric detector (PAD). Samples were hydrolyzed with 10 mL of 2 mol/L TFA at 120 °C for 1.5 h, diluted 100 times, and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane prior to injection. A mixed standard containing D-(−)-fucose (Fuc), L-rhamnose (Rha), D-(+)-arabinose (Ara), D-(+)-galactose (Gal), D-(+)-xylose (Xyl), and galacturonic acid (GalA) at various concentrations (0.01–5 mg/L) was employed for qualitative identification and quantitative calibration. The proportions of the homogalacturonan (HG) and rhamnogalacturonan I (RG-I) domains of pectin were calculated according to the following equations. Each sample was subjected to independent hydrolysis treatment in triplicate.(1)HGmol%=GalAmol%−Rhamol%(2)RG-Ⅰmol%=2Rhamol%+Aramol%+Galmol%

Molecular mass of pectins

The molecular weight distribution of pectin was analyzed by high-performance size-exclusion chromatography (HPSEC) coupled with multi-angle laser light scattering (MALLS; Dawn Heleos II, Wyatt, USA) and a UV detector (L2400, Wyatt Corp., USA). Separation was performed on a TSK gel G4000PWxl column (Tosoh, Japan) using 0.1 mol/L NaCl as the mobile phase at 25 °C with an elution rate of 0.5 mL/min. Molecular weight was computed using ASTRA software (version 5.3.4; Wyatt Technology, Santa Barbara, CA, USA), with the refractive index increment (dn/dc) set to 0.135 mL/g (Ma et al., 2024). All samples were processed in three independent replicates to ensure experimental reproducibility.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) of pectins

Powdered pectin samples were measured by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (TENSOR 27, Bruker, Germany). Pectin powder was mixed with KBr at a mass ratio of 1:100 to prepare pellets, and spectra were collected over 4000–400 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1 with 64 scans (Shi et al., 2024). The degree of esterification (DE) was calculated using the following equation:(3)DE%=A1754A1754+A1635

A1754 and A1635 denote the absorbance intensities of the bands at 1754 cm−1 and 1635 cm−1, respectively, corresponding to esterified and non-esterified carboxyl groups.

Pasting properties of composite gels

Powdered WS (6%, w/v) was dispersed in 25 mL of distilled water at 25 °C. Subsequently, apple pectin, citrus pectin, sugar beet pectin, and lemon pectin were added, respectively, at 1.00% (w/v) and thoroughly mixed. Pasting properties were measured using a Rapid Visco Analyzer (RVA-Tec M, Perten Ruihua Scientific Instruments, Beijing, China). The temperature profile followed a previously established protocol (Liu K. et al., 2025): the sample was held at 50 °C for 1 min, heated to 95 °C at 12 °C/min and maintained for 2.5 min, then cooled to 50 °C at the same rate and held for 2 min. The paddle speed was set to 960 r/min for 10 s and then reduced to 160 r/min for the remainder of the run. Viscosity parameters were extracted, including peak viscosity (PV), trough viscosity (TV), final viscosity (FV), breakdown (BD), setback (SB), and pasting temperature (PT).

Rheological properties of composite gels

A rheometer (Physica MCR 301, Anton Paar, Austria) equipped with aluminum parallel plates (40 mm diameter, 1 mm gap) was employed to evaluate rheological behavior. The gelatinized samples obtained above were allowed to stand for 2 min, cooled to 25 °C, and then subjected to measurement.

(I) Apparent viscosity: At 25 °C, the apparent viscosity was recorded over a shear rate ramp from 0 s−1 to 100 s−1 (Xu et al., 2023).

(II) Dynamic rheology: Dynamic frequency-sweep measurements were performed to determine the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″). Frequency sweeps were carried out from 0.1 Hz to 10 Hz at a constant strain of 1%.

Thermogravimetric analysis of composite gels

Thermogravimetric (TG) and derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) profiles were acquired using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA 5500, Waters, USA). An empty aluminum pan was used as the reference to minimize instrumental bias. Freeze-dried RVA-prepared samples (5 mg) were placed in aluminum pans and heated from 30 °C to 550 °C under nitrogen at a heating rate of 20 °C/min.

Swelling power of composite gels

After preparation as described in Section 2.6, samples were cooled to room temperature and centrifuged at 25 °C, 4000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was discarded. The wet mass of the precipitate (m1) was recorded, followed by oven-drying to constant weight to obtain the dry mass (m2) (Liu K. Y. et al., 2026). Swelling power was calculated according to the following equation:(4)SPgg=m1m2

FT-IR of composite gels

Structural characteristics were examined using an FT-IR spectrometer (TENSOR 27, Bruker, Germany). Freeze-dried samples were thoroughly mixed with KBr at a mass ratio of 1:100, pressed into pellets, and scanned from 4000 to 400 cm−1 at 4 cm−1 resolution with 64 scans (Ma et al., 2025). Degree of order (DO, calculated as the absorbance ratio at 1047/1022 cm−1) reflects the short-range ordered structure, while degree of double helix (DD, 1022/995 cm−1) represents the extent of double-helical content.

Interaction force

Firstly, composite solutions of WS and each pectin were prepared according to the method described in Section 2.6, and NaCl (1 M) and urea (1 M) were subsequently added to each composite solution, respectively. The storage modulus (G′) of the samples was then measured at 25 °C using a 1% strain within the frequency range of 0.1–10 Hz (Gong et al., 2025).

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC)

Thermodynamic parameters governing the interactions between WS and pectin of different botanical origins were determined using isothermal titration calorimetry (Nano ITC, TA Instruments, Delaware, USA) at 25 °C. A WS solution (0.3 mg/mL, 300 μL) was loaded into the sample cell and stirred at 350 r/min. Aliquots (40 μL) of each pectin solution (1 mg/mL) were injected at 120 s intervals for a total of 20 injections. For each pectin, control titrations into the corresponding blank solvent were conducted in parallel. Data were fitted using the Independent model, and the Gibbs free energy (ΔG) was calculated from the fitted parameters.(5)ΔG=−RTlnKa=△H−T△S

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) of composite gels

The microstructure of gel samples was observed using a scanning electron microscope (SU8010, Hitachi Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. Prior to imaging, specimens were sectioned, and the sections were sputter-coated with gold using an ion sputter coater (MCI000, Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Images were acquired at 200× magnification.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in SPSS 26.0, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Experiments were conducted in triplicate, and results are reported as mean ± standard deviation.

As a critical parameter reflecting the molecular properties of pectin, molecular weight (Mw) affects both gel strength and solubility. As shown in Table 1, the Mw values of the four pectins followed the order: AP > CP > SP > LP. Among them, LP presented the lowest Mw (2.04 × 105 g/mol), which can be explained by a more vigorous extraction process in addition to its naturally low degree of polymerization (Gentilini et al., 2014). It is worth noting that pectins with higher molecular weights possess longer molecular chains, resulting in an increased number of intermolecular cross-linking sites and enhanced chain entanglement, thereby promoting the effective formation of gel networks (Zdunek et al., 2021). The dispersity of polymer samples is typically characterized by Mw/Mn, which describes the breadth of the molecular weight distribution. A higher Mw/Mn value indicates a broader molecular weight distribution and a greater coexistence of both long-chain and short-chain molecules (Feng et al., 2023). As presented in Table 1, SP displayed the highest Mw/Mn value (1.76) among all samples, suggesting the most heterogeneous distribution. This phenomenon can be explained by the higher proportion of RG-I regions in SP compared to the other pectins, resulting in an increased coexistence of shorter and longer pectin chains and, consequently, a larger Mw/Mn.

As shown in Table 1, the degree of esterification (DE) values for AP, CP, LP, and SP were determined to be 68.31%, 66.49%, 57.82%, and 60.26%, respectively; thus, all four pectins can be classified as high-methoxyl pectins. It was observed that AP and CP presented closely similar DE values, consistent with data previously reported by Fiedot et al. (2025). LP exhibited the lowest DE (57.82%), which may have resulted from the accelerated de-esterification under conditions of higher acid concentration and extraction temperature (Karim et al., 2022).

FT-IR of pectins

The structures of four pectins were characterized by FT-IR spectroscopy, as shown in Fig. 1. Absorption peaks in the range of 3200–3600 cm−1 were primarily assigned to O—H stretching vibrations both within and between pectin molecules (Ma et al., 2025). The absorption peak at 2935 cm−1 resulted from C—H stretching vibrations, which is characteristic of polysaccharide compounds (Gnanasambandam & Proctor, 2000). Peaks observed at 1745 and 1643 cm−1 were associated with esterified carboxyl groups (-COOR) and free carboxyl groups (-COO-), respectively. These two peaks are commonly used to calculate the degree of methoxylation in pectin. Compared with the other pectins, AP exhibited much stronger absorption at both 1745 and 1643 cm−1, further corroborating the higher degree of esterification in this sample. Absorption peaks in the range of 1300–800 cm−1 are typically recognized as the “fingerprint region” for pectin (Manrique & Lajolo, 2002). The absorption bands at 1095 and 1012 cm−1, assigned to the stretching vibrations of C-O-H and C-O-C, respectively, are reflective of the content of pectin backbone regions (Han et al., 2025). Notably, SP displayed very weak absorption at both 1095 and 1012 cm−1, suggesting a lower proportion of HG regions and a higher presence of RG-I, which is consistent with the monosaccharide composition results in Table 1.Fig. 1FT-IR spectra of all pectin samples.Fig. 1

Pasting properties of composite gels

The pasting properties of each sample were characterized using a Rapid Visco Analyzer (RVA). The RVA profiles and pasting parameters of the pure WS gel and the various WS-pectin composite gels are presented in Fig. 2A and Table 2. Compared with the pure WS gel, the addition of all four pectins resulted in substantial increases in peak viscosity (PV), trough viscosity (TV), and final viscosity (FV). This enhancement in viscosity can be explained by several factors. Firstly, the incorporation of pectin facilitates the formation of a hydrated membrane around WS granules, which promotes water absorption and swelling of the starch. This increase in swelling volume subsequently leads to higher viscosities in the composite gels (Liu et al., 2024; Liu, Xiao, et al., 2019). Secondly, entanglements between WS and pectin molecules reduce the free space within the system, further contributing to viscosity elevation (Huang et al., 2023). Among the four composite gels, those containing AP and CP exhibited the highest pasting viscosities, whereas the lowest values were observed in the WS-LP composite gel. The higher-DE and Mw of AP and CP are believed to promote stronger interactions with WS, by contrast, the lower DE and Mw of LP lead to weaker interactions with WS (Gong et al., 2025; Liu, Yi, et al., 2025). Interestingly, although a significant difference in Mw was present between AP and CP, comparable PV values were obtained. Hence, PV was governed predominantly by DE rather than by chain length. As demonstrated in our previous work (Liu, Bi, Zhang, & Yi, 2026), DE determines the charge density and hydrophobicity of pectin, thereby controlling the intensity of water competition and electrostatic repulsion between pectin and starch at the early stage of gelatinization. In other words, because similar DE values were shared by AP and CP, nearly identical interference with granule swelling was exerted, resulting in similar PV and steady apparent viscosity profiles (Fig. 2B). Breakdown viscosity (BD) is used to evaluate the stability of starch granules under conditions of heat and shear (Liu et al., 2018). As shown by the results, BD values for all composite gels were higher than that of the pure WS gel (10.00 mPa·s). This effect may be driven by the presence of pectin, by which the pasting viscosity is enhanced; consequently, WS granules are swollen and disrupted, and greater shear forces are sustained (Han et al., 2024; Lv et al., 2025). Setback viscosity (SB) reflects the degree of starch retrogradation after heating and cooling, as well as the rearrangement capability of amylose molecules (Chan et al., 2009). Upon addition of the four pectins, significant increases in SB values were observed in all gels, suggesting that the short-term retrogradation of WS was enhanced. This enhancement is believed to result from the considerable thickening properties inherent to pectin. Through the incorporation of pectin, the mobility of amylose molecules is restricted, leading to increased aggregation of starch granules and strengthened intermolecular interactions, by which short-term retrogradation is promoted (Hu et al., 2025; Lv et al., 2025).

Rheological properties of composite gels

Steady shear rheological property

The apparent viscosity curves of the pure WS gel and composite gels are presented in Fig. 2B. It is evident that the apparent viscosity of all samples decreased with increasing shear rate, displaying a typical shear-thinning behavior; thus, all gels can be classified as pseudoplastic fluids (Naji et al., 2012). Furthermore, all composite gels exhibited higher apparent viscosity compared to the pure WS gel, which is consistent with the pasting results. Among the composite gels, the lowest apparent viscosity was observed in the WS-LP system. This result may be associated with the higher-Mw and longer molecular chains of AP, CP, and SP, which facilitate stronger entanglement and aggregation with WS molecules (Gong et al., 2025; Liu, Bi, Ma and Yi, 2026). Moreover, the higher-DE of AP, CP, and SP was considered another factor contributing to the higher apparent viscosity of their respective composite gels. As reported in Liu, Yi, Ma and Bi (2025), pectin with higher-DE can establish stronger hydrogen bonding and form more compact gel network structures with starch, thereby resulting in increased apparent viscosity.

Dynamic rheological property

The storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were employed to evaluate the effects of four pectins on the elasticity and viscosity of WS gels. The dynamic rheological curves for all samples are summarized in Fig. 2C and D. Both G′ and G″ were found to increase with rising angular frequency for the pure WS gel as well as for the pectin-WS composite gels, with G′ consistently exceeding G″ throughout the measurement range. In other words, all gel samples exhibited marked elasticity and solid-like behavior, which is characteristic of weak gel structures (Yang et al., 2021). The incorporation of four common natural pectins was found to enhance both the G' and G“ of WS gels. At identical concentrations, more significant increases in G' and G" were observed when AP and CP were incorporated, compared to the other pectins. This improvement is believed to result from a greater degree of molecular entanglement and intermolecular cross-linking formed between WS and these pectins, leading to a denser gel network (Lin et al., 2025). In addition, stronger hydrogen bonding and other intermolecular interactions between AP or CP and WS were suggested to further strengthen the gel matrix (Lin et al., 2025). The inherent thickening effect of pectin itself was also considered to contribute to the overall enhancement of the network structure, thereby improving the viscoelastic properties of the composite gels (Liu, Hu, McClements, Jin and Chen, 2025).

Thermogravimetric analysis of composite gels

To elucidate the effects of four common natural pectins on the thermal stability of WS gels, the thermogravimetric (TG) and derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) curves of all gels are presented in Fig. 3A and B. The thermal degradation of all samples was characterized by four distinct stages. In the first stage (30–150 °C), the mass loss was mainly ascribed to the evaporation of water from the gel systems (Liu, Yang, Ma and Zhang, 2019). The second stage, occurring between 150 and 220 °C, was characterized by relatively smooth curves for all gels, reflecting good thermal stability within this temperature range. A pronounced degradation occurred during the third stage (220–350 °C), accompanied by a significant increase in mass loss, which resulted from the extensive decomposition of carbohydrate polymers and volatilization of the degradation products (Xue et al., 2025). In the fourth stage (350–550 °C), further mass loss was caused by the carbonization of starch-based materials. The influence of four different pectins on the thermal stability of WS gels was most evident in the third stage. As depicted in Fig. 3B, all composite gels exhibited higher temperatures of maximum mass loss and lower maximum mass loss rates compared to the pure WS gel, suggesting that the introduction of the four pectins conferred enhanced thermal stability. Among these, WS-AP and WS-CP composite gels displayed lower maximum mass loss rates, reflecting superior thermal stability. This enhanced stability may primarily be explained by the following factors. Firstly, denser and more stable three-dimensional network structures were formed via hydrogen bonding and other non-covalent interactions between WS and pectin with higher-DE, higher-Mw (Liu, Yi, Ma and Bi, 2025, Liu, Bi, Ma and Yi, 2026). As a result, the structural compactness and thermal stability of the composite gels were considerably strengthened (Kong, Song, et al., 2024). Secondly, crosslinking and molecular entanglement between higher-Mw pectin and WS effectively restricted the migration and leaching of amylose, promoting the orderly rearrangement of starch molecules and further improving the structural integrity and thermal stability of the gels (Xue et al., 2025).Fig. 3Thermogravimetric (A) and corresponding DTG (B) curves of WS gel and composite gels.Fig. 3

Swelling power of composite gels

The swelling power of starch is considered an important indicator for evaluating its interaction with water molecules (Majeed et al., 2017). The results of swelling power for WS gel and composite gels are presented in Table 3. A significant enhancement in the swelling power of WS gel has been observed after incorporation of four pectins, increasing from 13.01 g/g to 16.13 g/g. This improvement can be explained by two main reasons. Firstly, as hydrophilic hydrocolloids, pectins possess a strong affinity for water, which facilitates the migration of additional water molecules into the composite gel matrix, thereby promoting the interaction between WS and water molecules and ultimately increasing the swelling power (Zhang et al., 2024). Secondly, it has been reported by Shrivastava et al. (2018) that the presence of hydroxyl groups in food hydrocolloids enhances the migration and penetration of water molecules between starch granules, thereby improving the swelling power of starch gel. As further support, Yan et al. (2025) reported that flaxseed gum, also classified as a hydrophilic hydrocolloid, was able to improve the swelling power of WS gel. Notably, a higher swelling power was exhibited by the WS-AP and WS-CP composite gels, which was consistent with their higher peak viscosity. That is, a greater swelling capacity was shown by starch gels with a higher peak viscosity; this statement was also supported by Yan et al. (2025). In infrared spectroscopy, the region of 1200–800 cm−1 is recognized as the fingerprint region for starch. Within this range, the absorbance ratio at 1047 cm−1 to 1022 cm−1 is defined as the degree of order (DO), which is used to characterize the short-range ordered structure of starch. Simultaneously, the 1022/995 ratio is also applied as the double helix content (DD), reflecting the proportion of amorphous regions to ordered carbohydrate domains within the starch (B, B, B and B, 2021). The DO and DD values for all samples have been summarized in Table 3. Compared with the pure WS gel, slightly increased DO values have been observed in composite gels with pectin addition, suggesting that the presence of pectin may suppress leaching of amylose to some extent, thereby promoting the molecular rearrangement and enhancing the proportion of short-range ordered structures (Hu et al., 2025). In contrast, a slight decrease in the DD value has been detected, implying that the binding between starch molecules was disturbed through the formation of hydrogen bonds and other interactions with pectin, resulting in a reduction in the proportion of ordered carbohydrate domains within the overall structure (Yu et al., 2025).

Interaction forces

During the interaction between starch and polysaccharides, hydrogen bonding and electrostatic forces are considered the major driving factors (Kong, Shen, et al., 2024). To further elucidate the types of interactions between WS and four common pectins, NaCl and urea were employed in this study to selectively perturb electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding, respectively. The G′ value, which reflects the structural characteristics of the gel network, was monitored under various treatment conditions, as shown in Fig. 5. As presented in Fig. 5A, the introduction of 1 M urea resulted in significant differences in G' among the four composite gel samples. Specifically, the highest G' values were observed in WS-LP-1 M Urea and WS-SP-1 M Urea, followed by WS-CP-1 M Urea, whereas WS-AP-1 M Urea exhibited the lowest G' value. Consistency was observed with the results obtained from FT-IR and ITC analyses of the composite gels. Notably, G' values of WS-AP-1 M Urea and WS-CP-1 M Urea were significantly reduced when compared to those of WS-AP and WS-CP composite gels, respectively. In contrast, a marked increase in G' was observed for WS-LP-1 M Urea, while minimal changes were detected for WS-SP-1 M Urea. These findings suggest that, when hydrogen bonding is predominant in the composite gel network, the introduction of urea can substantially weaken the structural integrity of the gel. Consequently, the strongest hydrogen bonding was considered to occur in WS-AP composite gel, followed in order by WS-CP and WS-SP, whereas WS-LP exhibited relatively weaker hydrogen bonding. It should be emphasized that, under identical concentration conditions, the impact of urea on G' of WS-AP, WS-CP, and WS-SP composite gels was considerably greater than that of NaCl, further supporting the central role of hydrogen bonding as the main interaction force between WS and AP, CP, and SP. By contrast, in the case of WS-LP composite gel, an increase in G' upon the addition of urea was observed, suggesting that, upon substantial weakening of hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions may have become the dominant driving force. This shift is assumed to have promoted the rearrangement of molecular chains and the formation of a stronger network structure, thereby leading to an increase in G'. Similar results have also been reported by Yu et al. (2025). It should be noted that these inferences were derived from differences in relative responses among samples measured under identical treatment conditions, and secondary effects of urea on polymer-chain conformation and solvent structuring cannot be fully excluded; therefore, the present conclusion is confined to relative, rather than absolute, hydrogen-bond interaction strength.Fig. 5Interaction forces of pure WS gel and pectin-WS composite gels: changes in G′ of all composite gels after addition of urea (A); changes in G′ of all composite gels after addition of NaCl (B).Fig. 5

Interaction forces of pure WS gel and pectin-WS composite gels: changes in G′ of all composite gels after addition of urea (A); changes in G′ of all composite gels after addition of NaCl (B).

As illustrated in Fig. 5B, after NaCl addition, the differences in G' among the samples were minimized and the profiles of all samples became comparable. This observation suggests that electrostatic interactions were not regarded as the dominant factors responsible for the differences in composite gel network structures. In addition, a significant enhancement in G' was observed in WS-LP composite gels with NaCl treatment. This phenomenon could be explained as follows: both WS and pectin possess negatively charged surfaces, and the presence of negative charges gives rise to certain electrostatic repulsions between them. The addition of positively charged Na+ ions was considered to reduce this electrostatic repulsion, consequently increasing the degree of intermolecular binding. Moreover, electrostatic interactions between sodium ions and hydroxyl groups of pectin restrict intermolecular repulsion and free space, thereby facilitating stronger associations between pectin and WS (Hu et al., 2025; Kong, Shen, et al., 2024). Importantly, the G' value of WS-LP-1 M Urea was found to be higher than that of WS-LP-1 M NaCl, revealing that, compared with hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions dominated the interactions between WS and LP in the current system.

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) analysis

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) has been widely utilized as a technique for analyzing intermolecular interactions and their corresponding thermodynamic parameters, enabling the characterization of binding features between two molecules (Arijaje et al., 2015). In the present study, the thermodynamic binding behaviors between WS and different pectins were analyzed using ITC. As shown in Fig. 6, the titration of WS with all four pectins resulted in a series of negative exothermic peaks, demonstrating that exothermic binding reactions were formed between pectin and WS. With the progression of titration, a gradual reduction in exothermic peak intensity was observed, suggesting that the binding sites within the system approached saturation. The obtained isothermal binding curves (Fig. 6, bottom) were fitted using a “one-site binding" model, and the thermodynamic parameters presented in Table 4 were determined. Among these, the binding constant (Ka) reflects the molecular affinity. The results showed that the affinities between WS and the four pectins followed the order AP > CP > SP > LP (Table 4), suggesting that AP associates more readily with WS, which is in accordance with previous results from gelatinization and rheological experiments. It should be noted that only minor differences in the binding stoichiometry (N) between the four pectins and WS were found. Negative enthalpy changes (ΔH) and Gibbs free energy changes (ΔG) were observed for all samples, demonstrating that the interactions between the four pectins and WS are dominated by non-covalent forces and proceed spontaneously (Pan et al., 2025). SEM of composite gels

To further investigate the effects of four pectins on the structure of WS gels, the microstructures of both WS gel and all composite gels were examined by SEM. As shown in Fig. 7, the pure WS gel, in the absence of pectin, exhibited a relatively uniform porous “honeycomb-like” network structure. This observation was consistent with the structure of pure WS gel previously reported in the Yan et al. (2025). Notably, no starch granules were observed in any of the samples, suggesting that complete gelatinization of the starch had been achieved. In all composite gels of WS and pectin, a denser and more ordered layered network structure, resembling a “framework” was observed. This phenomenon may be ascribed to the enhancement of the compactness and stability of the three-dimensional gel network through intermolecular interactions such as hydrogen bonding formed between WS and pectin (Kong, Song, et al., 2024). These observations were found to be in high alignment with the results obtained from the dynamic rheological measurements mentioned above. In addition, larger pore structures were observed in the composite gels compared with the pure WS gel. This finding suggests that both the hydrophilic nature of pectin and the intermolecular interactions with WS contributed to the improved water-holding capacity and distribution of water molecules within the gel (Xue et al., 2025). Similar microstructural features have also been documented in composite gels consisting of wheat starch and hawthorn seed polysaccharide or flaxseed gum (Yan et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025).Fig. 7SEM images of WS gel and composite gel.Fig. 7

Discussion

Based on the findings presented above and our previous conclusions, the intrinsic structural factors of pectin that have been identified to influence the physicochemical properties of WS and the strength of their mutual interactions are summarized as follows:

Firstly, the DE of pectin was confirmed as one of the key structural parameters for modulating pectin-WS interactions, with the major influence being exerted on the swelling and rupture behaviors of starch granules during WS gelatinization. Specifically, granule swelling during WS gelatinization was controlled more strongly by DE than by Mw. In accordance with our previous study (Liu, Bi, Zhang and Yi, 2026), a higher-DE corresponds to a greater substitution of carboxyl groups by methyl ester groups, through which stronger hydrophobicity and a relatively weaker water-binding capability are imparted to pectin. Consequently, fewer water molecules were sequestered by the pectin molecules, allowing more free water to be made available for migration into the WS granules; thus, water uptake, swelling, and granule rupture during gelatinization were promoted, accompanied by enhanced amylose leaching, through which a higher viscosity of the system was ultimately achieved (Chen, Cui, et al., 2023; Liu, Yi, Ma and Bi, 2025). With increased amylose leaching, more binding sites were provided for non-covalent interactions between pectin and WS, by which a denser and more stable network was more readily constructed, leading to improved pasting- and gel-related properties. In addition, with higher-DE pectin, the surface negative charge density was reduced, so electrostatic repulsion against WS was alleviated to some extent; consequently, closer approach to starch molecules was facilitated and participation in composite structure formation was enhanced (Liu, Bi, Zhang, & Yi, 2026). In the present work, very similar DE values were measured for AP and CP (Table 1); therefore, similar regulatory effects on WS swelling and rupture during gelatinization were exerted, and similar peak viscosity values (Table 2) as well as comparable steady apparent viscosity curves (Fig. 2B) were obtained.

Secondly, the Mw of pectin was also involved in regulating pectin-WS interactions; however, its contribution was mainly manifested in the interaction strength and the structural integrity of the composite gel. Compared with lower-Mw pectin, more pronounced improvements in WS pasting characteristics and gel performance were achieved by higher-Mw pectin. A longer backbone was provided by higher-Mw pectin, through which more binding sites were offered for interaction with WS and stronger hydrogen-bonding interactions were established. Moreover, a larger hydrodynamic volume was occupied by higher-Mw pectin, so physical entanglement and crosslinking with leached amylose were facilitated, resulting in a more compact and stable gel network and, consequently, improved integrity and stability of the composite gel structure (Gong et al., 2025; Liu, Bi, Ma, & Yi, 2026).

Finally, in this study, a considerably higher RG-I region content and distinct monosaccharide composition were measured in SP compared to other pectins. However, variations in the backbone (HG) and side chain (RG-I) proportions of pectin, as well as the accompanying differences in monosaccharide composition, have not been identified as decisive factors influencing the physicochemical properties of WS. It should be emphasized that this investigation did not aim to systematically resolve the causal relationships linking pectin monosaccharide structure or backbone-to-side-chain ratios to the modulation of WS physicochemical properties. As such, the conclusions drawn here remain somewhat limited in scope. More targeted and variable-controlled experiments need to be conducted to elucidate the precise role of pectin structural features-including HG and RG-I ratios, as well as monosaccharide composition and branching patterns-in regulating the physicochemical properties and interactions of WS. It should also be noted that the starch-only control (6%, w/v) was not matched to the composite systems (total solids 7%, w/v) in terms of either total solids or viscosity; accordingly, the enhancements in pasting and rheological responses may partially arise from the intrinsic thickening capacity of pectin and/or the higher solids content. Nevertheless, the presence of specific non-covalent interactions between wheat starch and pectin was supported by the ITC-derived thermodynamic parameters together with the selective perturbation tests using urea/NaCl and the corroborating FT-IR/SEM observations, demonstrating that the observed enhancements cannot be rationalized solely by thickening effects or solids differences.

Conclusion

In this study, the regulatory effects and intermolecular interaction mechanisms of four common natural pectins (AP, CP, LP, SP) on the physicochemical properties of WS were systematically compared at an equal mass addition level of 1.0% (w/v). Firstly, the introduction of all four pectins was found to improve the pasting properties, rheological behavior, thermal stability, and swelling power of WS, although the extent of modulation varied among the different pectins. More pronounced enhancements in pasting characteristics, viscoelasticity, and thermal stability were observed with the incorporation of AP and CP, as compared to LP and SP. Specifically, the WS-AP composite gel exhibited the highest pasting viscosity, swelling power, and a more stable three-dimensional network structure, followed in descending order by WS-CP, WS-SP, and WS-LP composite gels. Secondly, interaction analyses suggested that hydrogen bonding served as the primary driving force in the interactions between WS and AP, CP, and SP, while electrostatic interactions played a secondary role. In contrast, the regulation conferred by LP was predominantly governed by electrostatic interactions, with hydrogen bonding as a complement. Additionally, ITC experiments further confirmed a higher binding affinity between AP and WS, in accordance with the findings of the interaction analyses. Lastly, structural characterization by FT-IR and SEM revealed that the presence of AP facilitated the formation of a more ordered and compact network within the WS system, resulting in enhanced overall structural stability. In summary, natural pectins, especially AP, demonstrated outstanding potential in modulating the physicochemical and structural properties of starch-based systems. These findings provide new perspectives and insights into the application and selection of natural pectins, and offer a theoretical basis for the functional regulation and innovative application of high-quality starch-based foods.
