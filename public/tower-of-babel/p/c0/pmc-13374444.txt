Time to diagnosis in large vessel vasculitis: insights from a retrospective study at a tertiary rheumatology centre
Authors: Anna Kernder, Pauline Bussmann, Uta Kiltz, Hilal Kavruk, Philipp Sewerin, Johanna Mucke, Diana Vossen, David Kiefer, Ioana Andreica, Johanna Heuser, Judith Erkenberg, Panagiotis Ermeidis, Ralph Brinks, Xenofon Baraliakos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the diagnostic pathway of patients affected by large vessel vasculitis (LVV) and to determine factors associated with the time to diagnosis including referring and previously consulted medical specialties.MethodsThis retrospective single-centre study enrolled patients diagnosed with giant cell arteritis (GCA) and Takayasu arteritis (TAK) between January 2014 and May 2025. Clinical, laboratory and referral data were retrieved from medical records. Time to diagnosis was defined as the interval between the onset of first LVV-related symptoms and the confirmed diagnosis. Factors associated with diagnostic delay were analysed by multivariate Cox regression. Temporal differences were visualised using Kaplan–Meier curves.ResultsIn total, 501 patients were included (GCA n=488; TAK n=13). Compared with GCA (89 days (IQR 31–184)), the median time to diagnosis for TAK (245 days (IQR 31–1308)) was longer. Presentation with typical cranial symptoms was associated with a shorter diagnostic interval (HR 1.27, p=0.022), involvement of the aorta and its major branches was linked to prolonged delay (HR 0.64, p<0.001). Prior medical consultations by rheumatologists were associated with longer diagnostic delay (HR 0.73, p=0.004), whereas referrals by ophthalmologists (HR 2.40, p=0.001) and cardiologists (HR 3.81, p=0.008) were associated with earlier diagnosis.ConclusionDiagnostic delay varies by subtype and clinical presentation. Time to diagnosis was longer in TAK compared with GCA, whereas typical cranial symptoms facilitate earlier diagnosis. Increasing awareness is crucial to minimise diagnostic delay and prevent organ damage.

Introduction

Large vessel vasculitis (LVV) is characterised by inflammation of the aorta and its major branches.1 2 According to the 2012 revised Chapel Hill Consensus Conference nomenclature, LVV comprises the major subtypes giant cell arteritis (GCA) and Takayasu arteritis (TAK).1 GCA constitutes the most prevalent vasculitis, with an incidence ranging from 5.8 to 10.1 per 100 000 individuals aged >50 years in southern Europe and 16.7–43.6 per 100 000 in northern Europe.2 Within Europe, the incidence of TAK is 1.11 per million person-years.3 GCA generally manifests in individuals older than 50 years, whereas TAK typically occurs in those aged 10–40 years.1 2 Women are more frequently affected by LVV.2 3

Depending on the vascular segments involved, the clinical manifestations are diverse. Involvement of the temporal artery in GCA commonly results in headache, while ophthalmic artery inflammation leads to visual disturbances.2 4 In TAK, an early systemic phase with non-specific symptoms is often followed by features resulting from vascular stenosis, such as arterial claudication and pulse abnormalities, particularly in the upper extremities.2 5 Inflammatory activity may be accompanied by fatigue, fever and weight loss.2 This heterogeneity of clinical presentation may contribute to delayed diagnosis. Prolonged diagnostic latency is clinically significant, as it increases the risk of irreversible organ damage and mortality.6 7 Conversely, early identification allows for less intensive immunosuppressive therapy and may reduce treatment-related adverse effects, including infection and cardiovascular complications.8 Current data from other studies on diagnostic delay are inconsistent due to differences in the definition of study populations and time period until diagnosis.6 Among patients with suspected GCA,7 a Dutch study reported a median time of 31.0 days (IQR 13.0–108.8 days) from symptom onset to the first fast-track clinic visit.96

Previous studies have identified multiple determinants of diagnostic delay in vasculitis, such as extended referral intervals to specialised care, misdiagnosis at presentation, limited recognition of early disease manifestations and non-specific symptoms.6 10 Although diagnostic algorithms have been proposed to promote timely identification,6 considerable heterogeneity in clinical implementation persists, particularly across healthcare systems. Moreover, a Finnish study demonstrated a direct association between diagnostic delay and increased healthcare use.11 To date, no systematic data from Germany, as one of Europe’s most populous nations, have evaluated diagnostic delay, referral structures and the spectrum of involved medical specialties in patients with LVV. In patients with anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies-associated vasculitides, data from a related study has recently been published by our working group.12

In Germany, primary care is provided by general practitioners, who assess symptoms and refer patients to outpatient specialists or hospital services when necessary. Hospitals are organised into primary, secondary and tertiary levels, with tertiary centres offering highly specialised multidisciplinary care. As a result, patients with atypical or multisystemic conditions often pass through several outpatient and inpatient providers before reaching tertiary care, which can contribute to diagnostic delay.

Understanding the diagnostic pathway is fundamental to identifying modifiable determinants and enhancing early diagnosis. Accordingly, the objective of this study was to analyse the duration between the onset of first LVV-related symptoms and the confirmed diagnosis, and to characterise the referral structures of patients with newly diagnosed LVV at a tertiary rheumatology centre.

Methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective study included patients diagnosed with the LVV subentities TAK and GCA at a tertiary rheumatology centre. Eligible patients received their diagnosis between 1 January 2014 and 30 May 2025. Clinical data were retrieved from the hospital information system, and diagnoses were subsequently verified within the scope of this study by a panel of rheumatology specialists in accordance with the 2022 American College of Rheumatology/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (ACR/EULAR) classification criteria.

Patients with a pre-existing LVV diagnosis prior to hospital admission or before 1 January 2014, as well as those in whom vasculitis was excluded, were omitted from the analysis. Additional exclusion criteria included incomplete or non-verifiable medical documentation.

Data collection and definitions

Retrospective data acquisition was done between 1 May 2024 and 30 June 2025. The diagnostic interval was defined as the period between patient-reported onset of initial LVV-related symptoms and the date of confirmed diagnosis. When the exact day of symptom onset was uncertain, the 15th day of the reported month was used; if only the year was known, 30 June of that year was recorded (this concerns 16 (3.3%) of the patients with GCA and 3 (23.1%) of those with TAK). Symptoms were considered to be the initial manifestation if they were typical manifestations of GCA or TAK according to the 2022 ACR/EULAR classification criteria.1 The onset of the disease was defined as the date of the first documented clinical manifestation that could be attributed to the disease and was recorded in the medical records. Imaging findings without corresponding clinical symptoms were not considered sufficient to determine the onset of the disease. For patients with a history of polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR), the date of disease onset was based on the earliest symptoms that the treating physicians considered to be part of the disease spectrum.

Demographic parameters (age at diagnosis, sex) and potential influencing variables, such as symptoms, number and type of previously consulted specialists and referring physicians, were documented. Both medical and surgical specialties (eg, vascular surgery) were included. Disease activity at diagnosis was evaluated using the Birmingham Vasculitis Activity Score (BVAS) V.3.0.13 In cases where BVAS was not documented, it was reconstructed from clinical records. Only confirmed organ involvements were included in the analysis.

Information on prior immunosuppressive or anti-inflammatory therapy (eg, glucocorticoids, methotrexate or other immunosuppressants) and on pre-existing rheumatological diagnoses (eg, rheumatoid arthritis, fibromyalgia syndrome or Raynaud’s phenomenon) was extracted from patient records. Laboratory parameters relevant for disease classification and activity assessment (including C reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and blood cell count), as well as findings from radiological imaging such as ultrasound, MRI or CT scans were obtained from medical records.

In patients with suspected GCA, duplex ultrasound of the cerebral arteries was routinely performed as the initial imaging study. In cases where the findings were inconclusive but clinical suspicion remained high, additional imaging studies using MRI or CT were performed. In cases with clinical features suggestive of PMR, an MRI was additionally performed to assess involvement of muscles and tendons. In patients with suspected TAK, CT angiography was the primary imaging method. Further imaging studies were performed as clinically indicated based on the patient’s individual clinical presentation and symptoms.

Statistical analysis

Cox proportional hazards regression was used to identify factors influencing the time to diagnosis. Each model was adjusted for age and sex, with the onset of symptoms defined as the start of observation and the date of diagnosis as the event. Separate models adjusted for age and sex were fitted for each variable.

In addition, a Cox proportional hazards regression that was adjusted for sex and age and also included CRP and ESR as covariates was performed.

Variables comprised previously consulted and referring specialties, disease activity (BVAS, CRP) and organ involvement. A definition of rare organ involvement can be found in the supplementary material (online supplemental table S16). ORs were used to determine the strength of association between consultation of specific medical specialties and different organ involvements. For patients who had imaging findings suggestive of vasculitis prior to referral, an exploratory subgroup analysis was conducted to investigate the association with a delay in diagnosis. Data distribution was assessed visually. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean±SD; non-normally distributed variables are shown as median and IQR. Categorical data are summarised as absolute numbers and percentages (n (%)). CIs are reported at the 95% level (95% CI). A two-sided value of p<0.05 was deemed statistically significant. Temporal differences in diagnostic duration among LVV subtypes were visualised using Kaplan–Meier curves. Statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio (V.2025.09.1+401) with the R packages survival, dplyr, ggplot2, survminer, scales, shiny, flextable and officer.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 501 patients were included in this study, comprising 488 (97.4%) with GCA and 13 (2.6%) with TAK. The mean age at diagnosis was 71±9.2 years in GCA and 45±11.9 years in TAK. Female patients represented 61.0% (n=299) and 62.0% (n=8) of the respective subgroups. Prior to confirmed diagnosis of LVV, 61.7% (n=301) of patients with GCA and 38.5% (n=5) of those with TAK had received immunosuppressive therapy, predominantly glucocorticoids.

The mean BVAS was 2.7±2.0 in GCA and 1.3±1.5 in TAK at diagnosis. Corresponding CRP levels were 5.5±7.2 mg/dL and 3.4±5.0 mg/dL, respectively (table 1).

Initial symptoms primarily included typical cranial symptoms (n=236, 48.5%), shoulder and pelvic girdle pain (n=220, 45.8%) and B symptoms, including unexplained fever, night sweats and/or unintentional weight loss of >10% of body weight within 6 months (n=124, 25.8%) in patients with GCA. In patients with TAK, they primarily included ophthalmic involvement (n=6, 46.2%), joint involvement (n=3, 23.1%) and elevated inflammatory markers without a focus (n=4, 30.8%). However, many patients initially already exhibited a combination of various symptoms (online supplemental table S17).

Time to diagnosis

The median time between the onset of LVV-related symptoms and confirmed diagnosis was significantly longer in TAK (245.0 days (IQR 31.0–1308.0)) than in GCA (89.0 days (IQR 31.0–184.0)). The Kaplan-Meier analysis (figure 1) confirmed the significantly extended diagnostic delay among patients with TAK compared with those with GCA (log-rank p<0.05).

The median time to diagnosis in patients with GCA who present with typical cranial symptoms was shorter (61.0 days (IQR 31.0–181.0)) than in those without such symptoms (92.0 days (IQR 31.0–214.0)).

In patients with GCA and visual impairment, the time to diagnosis was shorter in those who developed permanent visual loss (n=5, 31.0 days (IQR 31.0–118.0)) than in those who did not (n=46, 58.5 days (IQR 16.0–113.0)).

Data on imaging studies, especially CT, performed prior to referral that suggest vascular inflammation are available for only a small number of patients (6 patients with GCA and 2 patients with TAK); in this subgroup, a tendency towards a longer diagnostic delay was observed in patients with prior imaging studies compared with the entire group, although the statistical significance was limited. In patients with GCA and prior imaging studies the median time to diagnosis was 106.0 days (IQR 68.25–235.25 days) and in patients with TAK 1097.0 days (IQR 1082.5–1111.5 days).

Referring specialties and prior consultations

At the time of diagnosis, the most frequent referring specialties for both GCA and TAK were general practitioners, specialists in internal medicine and rheumatologists.

Patients typically consulted multiple medical specialties (mean 2.38, SD 1.31); those with TAK consulted more specialties (mean 3.54, SD 1.29) compared with those with GCA (mean 2.34, SD 1.29) (online supplemental table S2). The most commonly visited specialties prior to diagnosis included general practitioners (50.0% (n=251) of all cases), internists (45.1%, n=226), rheumatologists (25.9%, n=130), ophthalmologists (21.8%, n=109) and neurologists (21.6%, n=108) (table 2, online supplemental table S1, figure S1).

Specialist consultations and organ involvement

Determination of OR showed associations between consultation of specific specialties and organ involvement. Patients consulting ophthalmologists prior to initial diagnosis were more likely to have typical cranial symptoms (OR 2.04, 95% CI 1.24 to 3.44, p=0.006) and ocular involvement (OR 44.34, 95% CI 20.09 to 112.91, p<0.001) compared with patients not visiting ophthalmologists. Patients who had consulted outpatient rheumatologists prior to diagnosis showed a trend towards having a lower chance of typical cranial symptoms (OR 0.82, 95% CI 0.53 to 1.27, p=0.380).

The Cox regression adjusted for CRP and ESR in addition to sex and age showed that with only minimal changes the overall direction and statistical significance of the associations remained consistent (online supplemental table S10–S15).

Predictors of diagnostic delay

Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified several independent predictors of diagnostic delay (figure 1, online supplemental table S3–S9). The presence of typical cranial clinical presentation, characterised by headache, visual disturbance or jaw claudication, was associated with a shorter time to diagnosis (HR 1.27, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.55, p=0.022). In contrast, the involvement of the aorta and its major branches such as the axillary, subclavian, iliac or femoral arteries (HR 0.64, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.79, p<0.001) and cutaneous involvement in the form of skin ischaemia (HR 0.32, 95% CI 0.12 to 0.88, p=0.027) were linked with longer diagnostic delays.

Notably, patients referred by rheumatologists (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.57 to 0.92, p=0.008) or who had been previously assessed by rheumatologists (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.59 to 0.90, p=0.004) also experienced a significantly prolonged diagnostic interval. Referrals from ophthalmologists (HR 2.40, 95% CI 1.46 to 3.92, p=0.001) and cardiologists (HR 3.81 95% CI 1.41 to 10.28, p=0.008) were associated with a shorter time to diagnosis.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we examined the diagnostic pathway of patients with LVV and identified factors associated with the time to diagnosis. Diagnostic delay was significantly longer in TAK compared with GCA.

Patients presenting with typical cranial symptoms were diagnosed significantly earlier, most likely because visual impairment and severe headache require prompt evaluation,1416 and jaw claudication is quite specific for GCA.17 Consequently, we observed a shorter diagnostic interval among patients referred by ophthalmologists, especially among patients who developed a permanent visual loss. In contrast, symptoms involving other vessels (as the aorta) often develop more gradually with unspecific symptoms and without pain, thereby contributing to delayed referral. A multicentre study of 549 patients compared those with and without large vessel involvement in GCA. Patients with large vessel involvement were more often under 70 years old and complained less frequently of cephalic signs, including ophthalmic involvement, and were less likely to have polymyalgia rheumatica.18 Thus the absence of typical early symptoms makes early diagnosis difficult. In patients with GCA without typical cranial involvement, there is a longer delay in diagnosis; however, this delay is significantly shorter than in patients with TAK. In the absence of typical symptoms, diagnosis is more difficult. Due to the rarity of TAK, diagnosis is further complicated in these cases.

Many patients with GCA initially presented with typical symptoms, which could facilitate diagnosis—unlike in TAK, where non-specific symptoms were more common. However, the presence of B-symptoms in a quarter of the patients with GCA in our study opens up a broad range of differential diagnoses, which could also contribute to a more difficult and delayed diagnosis.

It is important to note that we observed a longer diagnostic interval in the presence of involvement of the aorta and its major branches as previous studies showed that large vessel involvement in GCA is associated with higher mortality, a potentially greater risk of relapse and higher cumulative glucocorticoid exposure.19 20

Prior consultations with outpatient rheumatologists were associated with a longer diagnostic interval, possibly due to our observation that patients with atypical or multisystem presentations were referred to our tertiary centre. These patients tended to have a lower probability of typical cranial symptoms. Collectively, these findings highlight the ongoing challenge of recognising all forms of LVV in its early stages.1 15 16

Direct comparison of diagnostic delay between studies is difficult, as definitions of ‘time to diagnosis’ and inclusion criteria vary.6 A study including multiple vasculitis entities reported a median delay of 7 months among predominantly Caucasian participants.6 However, the interpretation in regard to large vessel vasculitis is limited, as this group only accounted for 10% of the study population.6 Another study from the Netherlands showed a median time from onset of symptoms to first fast-track clinic visit of 31.0 days (IQR 13.0–108.8 days) in patients with suspect GCA, but not all patients received a final diagnosis of GCA.7 A meta-analysis with data from inception to November 2015 described a mean time between GCA symptom onset and diagnosis of 9 weeks; they also observed longer diagnostic delay in patients with non-cranial GCA (17.6 weeks) than in those with cranial GCA (7.7 weeks).9

There are only a few studies that have examined diagnostic delay in patients with TAK, and recent studies are particularly lacking. A study published in 2013 examined a US cohort of 126 patients with TAK between 1984 and 2009. It found a median delay between symptom onset and diagnosis of 17.5 months (IQR 7–41.8 months).21 Another US study examined patients with TAK between 1970 and 1990 and found a median delay between symptom onset and diagnosis of 10 months (range, 0 to 13 years).22 A recent case report describes a Japanese patient who was diagnosed 50 years after the onset of symptoms.23 The range of delay is wide, with the median delay in our study being slightly shorter than that reported in older studies. However, the interpretability is limited due to the small sample size. Larger, recent studies are needed to more closely examine the duration of diagnostic delay in patients with TAK.

Diagnostic delay has repeatedly been linked to increased morbidity and mortality in LVV.24 25 Auanassova et al and Sreih et al analysed factors associated with diagnostic delay, without distinguishing between different entities of vasculitis. They identified referral delay, initial misdiagnosis and limited disease awareness as key contributions to delayed diagnosis.6 10 Prior et al identified non-cranial manifestation in GCA as a factor for diagnostic delay.9 The prolonged diagnostic interval in TAK could be due to the rarity of the disease1 3 and the often non-specific manifestation.2 10 24

Our findings align with these observations and highlight that timely recognition of LVV remains heavily reliant on clinical presentation. The noted association between prior rheumatology consultation and extended diagnostic interval probably reflects the complexity of distinguishing early LVV from other autoimmune or inflammatory disorders.2

In an additional exploratory analysis, we examined the delay in diagnosis in the subgroup of patients who had undergone imaging studies prior to referral to our centre, the findings of which suggested vascular inflammation. Contrary to expectations, there was a tendency towards a longer delay in diagnosis; however, the number of cases meeting these criteria was small. Consequently, the statistical significance of this analysis is limited, and the results should be interpreted with caution. One explanation for the longer time to diagnosis in these cases could be that the findings were considered in isolation and not in the context of other symptoms. Further studies with larger cohorts are needed to better assess the impact of prereferral imaging on the time to diagnosis.

Prompt initiation of immunosuppressive treatment helps to prevent irreversible organ damage, particularly vision loss, and vessel stenosis and reduces therapy-related toxicity by avoiding unnecessarily prolonged high-dose regimens.8 25 26 Conversely, diagnostic delay leads to disease progression and contributes to heightened risks of infection and cardiovascular morbidity. Educational initiatives and structured referral pathways—modelled on those already implemented in other chronic rheumatic diseases—could be adapted to vasculitis in order to raise awareness among non-specialist physicians.27 28

The retrospective, single-centre design restricts the generalisability of our findings to other healthcare settings. As our centre serves as a tertiary rheumatology care, patients treated exclusively in primary care or never referred to specialised units are not included. Consequently, the longest diagnostic delays among undiagnosed or misdiagnosed individuals remain unquantified, introducing potential selection bias inherent to retrospective data sets.6 7 The definition of disease onset in this study was based on retrospectively documented clinical symptoms rather than on isolated imaging findings. This approach may have led to some uncertainty, particularly in patients with subclinical involvement of large vessels or a history of polymyalgia rheumatica, in whom distinguishing between early signs of the disease and related conditions can be difficult. Consequently, the calculated diagnostic delay may have been influenced by variations in the recognition and documentation of early symptoms.

Although our study defines diagnostic delay as the period from the onset of the first symptoms to the formal diagnosis, this measure may underestimate clinically significant delays in cases with rapidly progressing complications, such as vision loss in GCA. These cases illustrate that even a short diagnostic interval can indicate delayed recognition of high-risk manifestations, underscoring the importance of early vigilance in patients with potentially serious conditions.

Although each organ-specific variable was analysed in a separate, age-adjusted and sex-adjusted Cox model, testing multiple variables may increase the risk of a type 1 error. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as exploratory and indicate potential associations rather than definitive causal relationships. This approach allowed us to control for important demographic confounders while simultaneously examining the influence of organ involvement on diagnostic delay.

In addition, differences in healthcare organisation and referral structures between countries limit the validity of direct cross-national comparison. Conversely, our rheumatology centre functions as a major regional tertiary-referral centre for patients presenting with complex rheumatological disorders. Prospective multicentre studies encompassing both primary and secondary care are needed to substantiate these findings and to delineate system-level barriers to timely diagnosis.

Despite these limitations, our study provides meaningful insights into the diagnostic pathway of LVV within a tertiary rheumatology setting. Identifying clinical and structural determinants of delayed diagnosis is crucial for developing targeted educational, diagnostic and organisational strategies designed to enable earlier detection and improve patient outcomes.
