Infant anaphylaxis and epinephrine use: Can we improve acute management?

Anaphylaxis is a serious systemic hypersensitivity reaction that is rapid in onset and may cause death. The true prevalence of infant anaphylaxis is unknown, but such cases may be increasing in presentation to emergency departments, with studies that evaluate health-care utilization after implementation of early introduction guidelines that report an increase in the use of emergency department for food-related visits as well as an increase in epinephrine prescriptions for infants. Reasons for these increases may include early food introduction as well as therapeutic interventions such as early life or preschool oral immunotherapy. Infant anaphylaxis presents many diagnostic challenges, including poor recognition attributable to confusing signs and symptoms that may be age specific, and risk for inadequate acute management (Table 1).

Infant symptoms and signs in anaphylaxis

Intramuscular epinephrine is first-line treatment of anaphylaxis in all ages, including infants. Epinephrine can be given intraosseously in this age as well. Unfortunately, community use of epinephrine for anaphylaxis at any age is suboptimal at best and unacceptable at worst, issues that may be further compounded by hesitancy to use this medication in the infant or toddler. Studies that span multiple countries have shown a concerning trend that many infants with anaphylaxis fail to receive epinephrine.1–3 A recent U.S. national survey of 264 probable cases of anaphylaxis in preschool children showed that only 39% of these infants received epinephrine for a severe allergic reaction.4 However, the odds of receiving epinephrine increased significantly in children previously diagnosed with a food allergy and who had an anaphylaxis action plan already in place.4

Does potentially suboptimal management of infant anaphylaxis impact outcomes? Most allergic reactions in infants are reported to be mostly mild or moderate, rather than severe. An older, prospective observational study of 512 infants ages 3–15 months old with egg or milk allergy, followed up for a 36-month follow-up period, noted ∼0.81 reactions per year occurred, with 70% of these classified as mild, 18% as moderate, and 11.4% as severe.5 The investigators noted that the severe reactions were undertreated in terms of epinephrine use.5 It is assumed, due to the lack of data to the contrary, that infants tend to spontaneously recover even without receiving epinephrine (which is similar to European Anaphylaxis Registry6 data from older children and adults), and there are no known reported infant anaphylaxis fatalities to date. Still, this is suboptimal management, which may increase morbidity, regardless of mortality rates, and undertreating anaphylaxis needs to be discouraged. A recent single-center report that evaluated infant and toddler anaphylaxis described two infants (7% of the infant study cohort) who experienced severe anaphylaxis, one of whom received three doses of epinephrine.7 The risk for severe anaphylaxis is further supported by data from other centers, with a proportion of infants noted to experience significant cardiovascular (hypotension) and neurologic symptoms during anaphylaxis episodes.1,2 In one retrospective analysis of the Allergy Vigilance Network from 2002 to 2018, 3% of food anaphylaxis reaction occurred in infants.2 Of these, hypotension and neurologic manifestations were described in 21% and 26% of infants experiencing anaphylaxis.2

To improve the use of epinephrine, multiple existing barriers require addressing (Fig. 1). Poor recognition of a severe allergic reaction in infants (by both caregivers and health care professionals), the lack of the availability of epinephrine at the time of the acute allergic reaction, fear or inability of administration of epinephrine autoinjectors, and concerns about potential adverse effects are all potential factors that negatively impact optimal management. In addition, there have also been concerns with regard to autoinjector needle length, resulting in an accidental intraosseous injection, although this seems to be a rare occurrence. The emergence of a 0.1-mg device (approved in the United States for infants who weigh 7.5 to 15 kg) may mitigate this rare but serious risk, but the device may be difficult to obtain (due to availability and insurer requirements). Recent expert consensus has recommended that either the 0.1-mg or the 0.15-mg dose may be used to treat anaphylaxis in young infants weighing < 15 kg because the benefits of treatment far outweigh the risks.8 The authors of the recent anaphylaxis practice guidelines highlight the lack of data to determine optimal epinephrine dosing and needle length in this age group.8 Similarly, these issues have been elucidated previously in an expert consensus paper that defines principles of infant and toddler anaphylaxis, in particular, highlighting the difficulty in assessing hypotension in this age due to shifting cuff size and normal reference values.9

Infant anaphylaxis and epinephrine use.

The first-line treatment of infant anaphylaxis is epinephrine. To improve administration in this population, we need a multifaceted approach to address barriers and educate health-care professionals and caregivers. Allergists can help by providing comprehensive teaching on how to recognize signs and symptoms of anaphylaxis specific to infants, training on how and when to use epinephrine autoinjector devices in this population, reassuring that the use of epinephrine is safe and effective, and providing an easy-to-follow anaphylaxis action plan. It remains to be seen whether or not development and use of infant-specific plans would improve anaphylaxis management. Alternate routes of epinephrine administration, including intranasal and sublingual, are currently under development and will increase options to treat anaphylaxis, although it may take additional time after initial approval for additional approved indications down to the infant age range. While more research is needed to further examine current knowledge gaps in the epidemiology, diagnosis, management, and patient outcomes of infant anaphylaxis, we can and must partner with families of infants with food allergy to empower and prepare them to provide timely management of anaphylaxis in the community.
