LncRNA IRENA promotes peripheral T-cell lymphoma progression through scaffolding ARHGEF1 and FMNL1 to modulate RHOA GTPase/MAPK signaling
Authors: Cong Wang, Ming-Ci Cai, Shu Cheng, Song Hu, Yao-Hui Huang, Liang-Juan Zhao, Yao Qin, Yu-Ran Qiu, Hong-Jing Dou, Ye-hui Tan, Su-Jun Gao, Jie Xiong, Wei-Li Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Peripheral T-cell lymphoma (PTCL) is a highly heterogeneous group of lymphomas, characterized by aggressive behavior and poor outcomes. Investigating the key regulatory long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) is helpful to refine current prognostic models and identify novel therapeutic targets in PTCL. Using clinical and transcriptomic data from 172 patients (training cohort) and 36 patients (validation cohort) with newly diagnosed nodal PTCL, this study identified LINC01727 (also known as IRENA) as an independent prognostic marker associated with poor outcomes. Functional assays demonstrated that IRENA significantly promoted tumor growth and inhibited T-lymphoma cell apoptosis, underscoring its oncogenic role in PTCL. Mechanistically, cytoplasmic IRENA acted as a scaffold, enhancing the interactions between Rho guanine nucleotide exchange factor 1 (ARHGEF1) and Formin-like protein 1 (FMNL1), as well as their binding to RHOA. Multi-omics analysis revealed that the scaffolding activity of IRENA established a positive feedback loop, thereby provoking RHOA/MAPK activation. In the murine cell-derived xenograft model, targeting IRENA with an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) effectively suppressed T-lymphoma growth. This study identifies IRENA as an independent prognostic biomarker for nodal PTCL patients, revealing its role in the self-organizing activation of RHOA GTPase. Targeting the IRENA/RHOA/MAPK signaling axis by ASO may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for PTCL.

Introduction

Peripheral T-cell lymphoma (PTCL) is a highly heterogeneous malignancy with poor outcomes [1]. Due to its clinical and biological diversity, the World Health Organization (WHO) classifies PTCL into over 30 distinct subtypes [2], among which nodal PTCL entities represent the majority of the patients. Nodal PTCL mainly includes systemic anaplastic large cell lymphomas (ALCL), either anaplastic lymphoma kinase positive (ALK+) or negative (ALK-), nodal T-follicular helper cell lymphomas (nTFHL), and PTCL, not otherwise specified (PTCL-NOS) [2, 3]. Current treatment for nodal PTCL involves CHOP or CHOEP for CD30-negative cases and brentuximab vedotin plus CHP for CD30-positive cases, followed by consolidation with autologous stem cell transplantation [4]. Standard first-line therapy shows limited efficacy, with 68% of patients experiencing relapsed or refractory (R/R) disease and a 3-year overall survival (OS) rate less than 30% [5]. Given the high incidence of R/R disease and its strong association with poor outcomes, identifying R/R-associated biomarkers with therapeutic potential has become the keystone of PTCL research.

Genome-wide investigations have significantly accelerated the discovery of new biomarkers, molecular signatures, and genetic lesions in PTCL [6]. The nTFHL subtype is characterized by frequent epigenetic mutations, including TET2, DNMT3A, and IDH2, as well as driver mutations like RHOAG17V [7]. The stepwise tumorigenesis driven by these mutations indicates a potential therapeutic benefit from hypomethylating agents. The PTCL-NOS can be divided into two subtypes based on transcriptomic profiling: PTCL-TBX21 and PTCL-GATA3, characterized by dysregulation of NF-κB, JAK/STAT, and PI3K/mTOR signaling pathways, as well as MYC over-activation, and genomic instability [1]. These molecular differences guide potential targeted therapies, with JAK inhibitors or microenvironment modulators for PTCL-TBX21 and PI3K inhibitors for PTCL-GATA3 [1]. Although recent studies [8, 9] have shown promising improvements in remission rates with biomarker-guided therapies, there is still a long way to go in identifying distinct molecular entities and effectively incorporating novel agents to optimize therapeutic strategies. Recently, the role of the non-coding genome in PTCL pathogenesis has attracted attention, identifying PTCL molecular subgroups through genome-wide miRNA expression profiling [10, 11]. However, researches on long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) remain limited in PTCL.

LncRNAs, defined as transcripts longer than 200 base pairs without protein-coding potential, have emerged as key regulators in orchestrating oncogenic processes [12–14]. Antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) drugs have gained increasing attention due to their ability to target diverse RNAs, including lncRNAs, with several ASO products already approved for clinical use [15]. However, the functional landscape of lncRNAs in PTCL remains elusive, and their therapeutic potential as targets for ASO-based therapies needs to be fully explored. Recent transcriptomic profiling has uncovered three PTCL-associated lncRNAs with functional significance. In ALK- ALCL, lncRNA MTAAT was shown to promote tumor progression by restraining mitophagy via chromatin reorganization [16]. Another ALK- ALCL-associated lncRNA, BlackMamba, was found to be regulated by STAT3 and to interact with the DNA helicase HELLS, thereby contributing to the maintenance of the neoplastic phenotype [17]. Beyond ALCL, the lncRNA TCLlnc1 was reported to be overexpressed across major PTCL subtypes, where it serves as a modular scaffold for HNRNPD and YBX1 to activate TGF-β signaling, promoting lymphoma cell proliferation and migration [18]. These findings highlight the emerging relevance of lncRNAs as key drivers and potential therapeutic targets in PTCL.

In the present study, we integrated clinical and transcriptomic data from 172 patients (training cohort) and 36 patients (validation cohort) with newly diagnosed nodal PTCL. LINC01727, also known as IRENA, was highly expressed in R/R PTCL and independently predicted poor prognosis. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated IRENA as a scaffold for Rho guanine nucleotide exchange factor 1 (ARHGEF1) and Formin-like protein 1 (FMNL1) proteins, creating a positive feedback loop to amplify the activation of the RHOA GTPase and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway, ultimately driving PTCL progression. Therapeutic targeting IRENA with ASO effectively suppressed its tumor-promoting effects, providing a promising therapeutic approach for PTCL.

Materials and Methods

Patients

A total of 172 patients with newly diagnosed nodal PTCL, including ALK+ ALCL (n = 22), ALK-ALCL (n = 14), nTFHL (n = 102), and PTCL-NOS (n = 34), were enrolled in this study as the training cohort. Among them, 109 patients were classified as relapsed/refractory (R/R) and 63 patients as durable remission based on treatment response. The prognostic value of IRENA was further validated in an independent cohort of 36 patients with nodal PTCL from our previous study [19]. Pathological diagnoses were established according to the WHO classification [2].

Cell lines

Karpas299 (Human ALK-positive anaplastic large cell lymphoma), H9 (Cutaneous T cell lymphoma), Jurkat (Human T lymphocyte), and HEK 293 T (Human embryonic kidney cell) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). DL40 (Human ALK-negative anaplastic large cell lymphoma) was purchased from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources Cell Bank (JCRB, Catalog No. JCRB1337). All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling and tested for mycoplasma contamination. Karpas299 and DL40 served as the primary PTCL models for core functional and mechanistic studies, while H9 and Jurkat were included as auxiliary T-cell lines for loss-of-function and gain-of-function assays, respectively. Karpas299, H9, and Jurkat cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. DL40 was cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 20% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. HEK 293 T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All the cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using FastPure ®Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). For cDNA synthesis, 1 μg of total RNA was treated with 4 μl of 4× gDNA wiper mix (Vazyme) in a 20 μl reaction volume and incubated at 42 °C for 2 min to remove residual genomic DNA. The reaction was then mixed with 4 μl of 5× HiScript III qRT SuperMix (Vazyme) and incubated at 37 °C for 15 minutes followed by 85 °C for 5 sec to synthesize cDNA. RT-qPCR was performed using the synthesized cDNA as templates, with Chamq Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) and the corresponding primers listed in Table. S1.

RNA-sequencing analysis

Tumor biopsies collected at diagnosis were subjected to RNA-seq analysis. RNA extraction, library construction, paired-end sequencing, and read alignment were performed as previously described [19, 20]. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the limma R package to compare R/R and durable remission patients in the training cohort. Genes upregulated in the R/R group were identified based on a threshold of log2 fold change (FC) > 1.0 and adjusted p < 0.05. To identify genes associated with poor progression-free survival (PFS), we performed univariate Cox regression analysis using gene expression (log2(TPM + 1)) as a continuous variable, along with log-rank tests based on stratification by the mean expression level of each gene. Genes with a hazard ratio (HR) > 1 and p < 0.05 in both analyses were considered prognostically significant. For the Karpas299 cell line overexpressing IRENA compared to the vector control, differential expression genes were analyzed with edgeR package and subjected to KEGG pathway and GO enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler package.

Whole exome sequencing and targeted sequencing

Whole exome sequencing (WES) and targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) were performed on genomic DNA samples obtained from Shanghai Ruijin Hospital. All sequencing procedures and subsequent bioinformatic analyses were conducted according to the protocols established in our previous study [19]. This study included 55 WES and 99 tNGS data. A total of 73 genes with recurrent mutations (frequency > 2%) were selected to assess their association with IRENA expression level (quantified by RNA sequencing), as summarized in Table. S4.

5’ and 3’ rapid amplification of cDNA Ends (RACE)

The SMARTer RACE 5’/3’ Kit (Takara Bio, Japan) was used to generate full-length IRENA cDNAs according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 1 μg of total RNA extracted from DL40 was reverse transcribed to cDNA using CDS primer and SMARTScribe Reverse Transcriptase. The cDNA was then amplified by nested PCR to increase specificity. The gene-specific primers used for PCR amplification were listed in Table S1. The PCR products were separated by agarose gel electrophoresis, purified, and cloned into the pRACE vector. The recombinant plasmids were then transformed into Stellar Competent E.coli cells, positive clones were selected, and the inserted RACE products were sequenced using M13 F/R primers.

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

RNA Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization experiments were performed using the RNA in situ hybridization kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The probe (5’-GTGCTTCTGAATCCAGCTTCTGTGCTGCCC-3’) was designed and synthesized by Servicebio. Briefly, DL40 and IRENA-overexpressing Karpas299 cells (5 × 104) were centrifuged onto glass slides using a cytospin. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and treated with protease K solution for protein digestion. Pre-hybridization was conducted with hybridization buffer at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by overnight hybridization at 37 °C either with the scrambled RNA probe or with the probe targeting IRENA. Post-hybridization washes were done with SSC buffers, then HRP-labeled anti-DIG antibody was applied and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Tyramide signal amplification (TSA) solution (FITC-Tyramide, Servicebio) was then added and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 10 min. Cells were counterstained with DAPI, and slides were mounted and imaged using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany).

Nuclear & Cytoplasmic RNA Isolation

Nuclear and cytoplasmic RNA were isolated from DL40 cells using Cytoplasmic & Nuclear RNA Purification Kit (Norgen Biotek Corp, Canada) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were lysed with lysis buffer J, and cytoplasmic RNA was separated by centrifugation. The supernatant was carefully transferred to a new 1.5 ml tube, processed with buffer SK and ethanol, and centrifuged through a spin column. The nuclear pellet was resuspended with buffer SK and ethanol, and also processed through a spin column. Both RNA fractions were washed with wash solution A, eluted with elution buffer E, measured for concentration, and used for downstream RT-qPCR analysis. GAPDH was used as the cytoplasmic marker and U6 as the nuclear marker, with primer information listed in Table S1.

Ectopic expression and gene knockdown by shRNA

IRENA 1649nt full-length cDNA was synthesized according to 5’ and 3’ RACE results and cloned into the pLXT-SFFV-puro vector. Two shRNA sequences targeting IRENA were individually cloned into the pLKO5-GFP-puro vector. shRNA targeting sequences are as below: sh#1 GCCAGGAGTGTCTGTTCTAAC; sh#2 AGGATGGTGAAG GTCTCTTTA. Lentiviruses were produced by transient PEI transfection of HEK 293 T cells with the psPAX2 and pMD2.G virus packaging plasmids, as well as the lentiviral expression vector that contained the sequence of interest. Virus was harvested by passing through a 0.45 mm filter and used directly to infect Karpas299, Jurkat, and DL40, H9 for ectopic expression and knockdown of IRENA, respectively. Infected cells were selected with puromycin at 0.5 μg/ml.

Cell viability assay

CCK-8 Cell Counting Kit (Vazyme) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells from gain- and loss-of-function assays (Karpas299 VCT/OE, Jurkat VCT/OE, DL40 NT/shRNA1/shRNA2, H9 NT/shRNA1/shRNA2) were seeded into 96-well plates. After incubation for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, 10 μl of CCK-8 solution was added to each well. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an Infinite 200 Pro Microplate Reader (Tecan, Switzerland).

Cell apoptosis

Cell apoptosis was conducted using the Annexin V-PE/7-AAD Apoptosis Detection Kit (Vazyme) following the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 5 × 105 cells obtained from gain- and loss-of-function assays were harvested and stained with 5 μl of Annexin V-PE and 5 μl of 7-AAD Staining Solution. After a 10-minute incubation in the dark at RT, 400 μl of 1 × binding buffer was added to each sample. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, CA, USA). Cells positive for Annexin V-PE alone were classified as early apoptotic, while those positive for both Annexin V-PE and 7-AAD were classified as late apoptotic or necrotic.

Cell cycle

A total of 1 × 106 cells from gain- and loss- of function assays were harvested and stained with propidium iodide using the Cell Cycle Staining Kit (MultiSciences Biotech, Hangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell cycle analysis was performed using a Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and the data were analyzed by the FlowJo software.

Cytoskeletal and cytokinesis analysis

Karpas299 and DL40 cells from gain- and loss-of-function assays were seeded onto coverslips pre-coated with 0.1% poly-L-lysine, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin. F-actin was stained with phalloidin conjugated to Alexa Fluor 633 (MCE, Cat# HY-D1819, 1:200), and α-tubulin was detected using a primary antibody against α-tubulin (CST, Cat# 3873, 1:1000) followed by an Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam, Cat# ab175473, 1:2000). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and fluorescent signals were imaged using a confocal microscope. For quantitative analysis, at least 200 cells per condition were examined across four independent experiments. Cytokinetic cells were characterized by condensed nuclei and the presence of a visible midbody. Multinucleated cells were defined as those containing three or more nuclei.

In vitro transcription

In vitro transcription of IRENA RNA was performed using the MEGAscript®T7 Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with PCR products as DNA templates. DL40 cDNA was used as the template for PCR amplification with primers containing the T7 promoter sequence to synthesize sense and antisense IRENA, as well as truncated IRENA. All the primers used for in vitro transcription were listed in Table S1. The PCR products were purified using the Gel Extraction Kit (Vazyme) and used as DNA templates for in vitro transcription. The purified PCR products were added to transcription reactions and incubated at 37 °C for 6 h. The synthesized RNA was purified by lithium chloride precipitation. RNA integrity and size were assessed using the Denaturing RNA Electrophoresis Kit (MesGen, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA pulldown and mass spectrometry

Pierce Magnetic RNA-Protein Pull-down Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for RNA pulldown assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In vitro transcripts, including sense, antisense IRENA, and truncated IRENA, were labeled with desthiobiotin at the 3’ end and incubated overnight at 16 °C. The labeled RNA was then purified using the GeneJet RNA Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Subsequently, 50 pmol of labeled RNA was incubated with 50 μl of streptavidin magnetic beads at room temperature for 30 min. The RNA-bound beads were then incubated with DL40 protein lysates at 4 °C for 1 h with rotation. Following incubation, the beads were washed, and RNA-protein complexes were eluted. The eluted proteins were boiled at 100 °C for 10 min before further analysis.

Following RNA pulldown, the protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE. Silver staining was performed using a silver staining kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) to visually compare band differences between the IRENA sense group and the anti-sense control group. Additionally, a portion of the protein samples was also separated by SDS-PAGE, running for approximately 15 minutes until the proteins reached the interface between the stacking gel and the separating gel. The gel was then stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (Byotime, Shanghai, China), and the desired bands were excised for mass spectrometry analysis (Novogene, Tianjin, China). Both silver staining and Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining were performed according to the manufacturers’ protocols. The remaining RNA pulldown samples were subjected to western blot analysis to confirm the interactions between IRENA and its target proteins.

RNA immunoprecipitation

The RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay was performed using the PureBinding® RNA Immunoprecipitation Kit (Geneseed Biotech, Guangzhou, China) in DL40 cells. A total 1 × 107 of cells were lysed with an ice-cold lysis buffer A containing protease and RNase inhibitors. After reserving 50 μl of the lysates for protein input and 100 μl for RNA input, the remaining lysates were pre-cleared with Protein A + G beads. A total of 5 μg specific antibodies were incubated with 200 μl Protein A + G beads to form antibody-bead complexes, using rabbit IgG antibody as a negative control. The antibody-bead complexes were then incubated with the lysates at 4 °C overnight with rotation to capture RNA-protein complexes. Following incubation, the beads were washed. During the last wash, 10% of the beads were reserved for protein elution and subsequent western blot to verify successful pulldown of the target proteins. The remaining beads were used for RNA extraction via column purification for subsequent RT-qPCR analysis.

Co-immunoprecipitation

A total of 5×106 cells were lysed in ice-cold Pierce IP® Lysis Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. To detect RHOA interactions, cells were serum-starved overnight and then stimulated with 10% FBS for 20 min prior to lysis. The cell lysates containing 1000 μg protein were then incubated with 5 μg primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. 25 μl PierceTM Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added and incubated for an additional 1 h at RT with gentle rotation. After washing the beads thoroughly with lysis buffer, the bound proteins were eluted in 1 × Loading buffer (Epizyme, Shanghai, China), resolved by SDS-PAGE, and analyzed by immunoblotting. All the antibodies used in co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays were listed in Table. S2.

Western blot

Cells were lysed in 500 μl of Pierce IP® Lysis Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were measured using the Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime). A total of 15 μg of proteins were loaded and separated on SDS-PAGE gels, followed by transfer to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing using 1 × TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Proteins were detected using ImmobilonTM Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Millipore) and visualized with a chemiluminescence imaging system. Bands' intensities were quantified using ImageJ and normalized to ACTIN. All the antibodies used in this study are listed in Table S2.

RNA FISH and sequential immunofluorescence

Tissue sections from the Karpas299-OE cell-derived xenograft (CDX) tumors were dewaxed and rehydrated through a graded series of ethanol, followed by antigen retrieval in citrate buffer and digestion with Proteinase K. Subsequent steps for IRENA RNA FISH were performed as previously described. Following RNA FISH, sequential immunofluorescence was conducted as follows: the samples were blocked in 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature to prevent nonspecific binding. Two primary antibodies, anti-rabbit ARHGEF1 (Proteintech, Cat# 11363-1-AP) and anti-mouse FMNL1 (Santa Cruz, Cat# sc-81274), were mixed and diluted at a ratio of 1:500, and the mixture was incubated with the samples overnight at 4 °C. The samples were then washed and incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. After final washes, coverslips were mounted with a fluorescence-compatible mounting medium containing DAPI (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and images were captured using an Ortho-fluorescent microscopy (Nikon Eclipse CI).

Multicolor immunofluorescence staining

Tissue sections from Karpas299-OE CDX tumors were deparaffinized and rehydrated using graded ethanol, followed by antigen retrieval and Proteinase K digestion. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and sections were blocked with 5% BSA. Sequential immunofluorescence staining was performed using primary antibodies against ARHGEF1 (Proteintech, Cat# 11363-1-AP, 1:500), FMNL1 (Santa Cruz, Cat# sc-81274, 1:500), and RHOA (CST, Cat# 2117, 1:1000), with overnight incubation at 4 °C in a humidified chamber for each antibody. After washing, secondary antibodies and TSA signal amplification solutions were applied. Between each staining cycle, antigen retrieval was repeated using citrate buffer. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and fluorescent signals were imaged using a confocal microscope. All the antibodies used in this study are listed in Table S2.

RHOA activation assay

The RHOA activation assay was conducted using the Rho Activation Assay Biochem Kit (Cytoskeleton, Inc., CO, USA). Cells were first subjected to 24-hour serum starvation, followed by stimulation with 10% FBS for 20 minutes immediately prior to lysis. This process ensures that the cells are in a “responsive state” for optimal RHOA activation. Cells were then lysed rapidly on ice using an ice-cold lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. The lysates were clarified by centrifugation, and protein concentration was measured and equalized to 0.5 mg/ml. For each sample, 10% of the lysate was reserved as input for a subsequent western blot to determine total RHOA levels. The remaining 800 μg of lysates were incubated with Rhotekin-RBD beads at 4 °C for 1 h with agitation. The beads were then washed and centrifuged, and the bound active RHOA was eluted by boiling in 2×Laemmli sample buffer. The eluted samples were analyzed by western blot using anti-RhoA monoclonal antibody (Cytoskeleton, Cat# ARH05, 1:1000). Quantification of RHOA activation was determined by comparing the signal intensity between different groups and normalizing with the total RHOA levels from the input samples.

Cell transient transfection

HEK 293 T cells were seeded in 12-well plates at 70–90% confluence. For ectopic overexpression of IRENA, ARHGEF1, FMNL1, and their truncated variants, the cells were transfected with appropriate plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For RHOA knockdown, Karpas299 cells were transfected with a negative control (siNC) and three siRNAs targeting RHOA using Lipofectamine 3000. Plasmids encoding HA-tagged-ARHGEF1, Flag-tagged-FMNL1, and their truncated variants, as well as siRNAs, were obtained from Xitu bio lnc (Shanghai, China).

In vivo xenograft murine models

Six-week-old female NOD/SCID mice (Strain NO. T001492) and NCG mice (Strain NO. T001475) were purchased from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China) and housed under SPF conditions at the Experimental Animal Center of Ruijin Hospital affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. For xenograft models, 5 × 106 Karpas299 cells and 1.5 × 107 DL40 cells suspended in 200 μl PBS were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of NOD/SCID and NCG mice, respectively. When the tumor volume reached 100 mm³, mice from the IRENA OE group were paired by similar tumor volume. Within each pair, mice were randomized into ASO and vehicle arms. ASO (#GAGCTGAACACGTAAATGCC) was purchased from RiboBio Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) and given by intratumoral injection (10 nmol each mouse) every other day. Tumors and mice's body weight were measured every other day, and the tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: total tumor volume (mm3) = 0.5 × length × width2. No blinding was performed during the experiment or outcome assessment. After the final administration, all mice were sacrificed. Subcutaneous tumors and organs were harvested for further experiments.

Hematoxylin-eosin and immunohistochemical staining

Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining were performed on 4 μm formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections. Sections were baked at 62 °C for 1 h, followed by deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through a graded ethanol series. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with hydrogen peroxide for 10 min at room temperature. Heat-induced antigen retrieval was performed in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) at boiling temperature. After blocking with 5% BSA for 20 min, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies Ki67 (abcam, Cat# ab66155, 1:500) and Cleaved caspase-3 (CST, Cat# 8202, 1:1000). The following day, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 30 min. 3,3’-Diaminobenzidine was used for chromogenic detection, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Slides were dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. Tissue sections were scanned using an Aperio GT 450 slide scanner (Leica Biosystems), and images were analyzed using QuPath software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10) and RStudio (version 4.3.0). Kaplan-Meier analysis with the log-rank test was used to compare PFS between patients stratified into high- and low-expression groups according to the optimal cutoff value of log₂(TPM + 1) determined by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted to elucidate the prognostic significance of lncRNA IRENA, with the hazard ratio (HR) threshold set at > 1. For comparisons between two groups, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied. For multiple comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was employed for data with equal variances, whereas the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc test was used. Two-way ANOVA was applied for repeated-measures data, such as tumor growth curves and cell variability assays. For the analysis of associations between categorical variables, we utilized the Pearson’s Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Sample sizes (3–6 biological replicates in vitro; 5-8 mice per group in vivo) were chosen based on common practice in the literature, without prespecified effect size or power analysis.

Results

LncRNA IRENA is a predictor of PTCL progression

To identify lncRNAs with prognostic significance, we conducted differential expression and prognostic analyses in the training cohort, followed by validation of their prognostic value in the independent validation cohort (Fig. 1A). R/R nodal PTCL patients (n = 109) exhibited a significantly poorer prognosis compared with patients in durable remission (n = 63), with 3-year OS rates of 29.4% and 96.6%, respectively (p < 0.001, Fig. S1A). Differential gene expression analysis between the R/R and durable remission subgroups identified 28 genes that were upregulated in the R/R subgroup (cut-off: log2FC > 1.0 and adj. p < 0.05, Fig. 1B, Table S3). To further assess the prognostic relevance of these 28 upregulated genes, we performed univariate Cox regression and log-rank tests using a threshold set as HR > 1 and p < 0.05. These analyses identified 9 genes that were both upregulated in R/R patients and significantly associated with poor clinical outcomes (Fig. 1C, Table S3), among which two were lncRNAs, namely LINC01727 (IRENA) and LOC101927708. As shown in Fig. 1D, both IRENA and LOC101927708 exhibited higher expression levels in R/R nodal PTCL patients. When further evaluating their prognostic significance using optimal cutoffs for patient stratification, IRENA was consistently prognostic for PFS in both the training and validation cohorts (Fig. 1E), whereas LOC101927708 did not reach statistical significance in the validation cohort (Fig. S1B). The 2-year PFS rate was 30.1% in the IRENA-high group, compared to 50.3% in the IRENA-low group (Fig. 1E). Additionally, IRENA exhibited larger area under the curve (AUC) for predicting both 3-year PFS (Fig. S1C) and R/R risk (Fig. S1D), and retained its prognostic stratification ability across various nodal PTCL subtypes (Fig. S1E, F). Based on these results, IRENA was selected for further investigation. As shown in Table 1, 172 patients were categorized into IRENA-low (N = 109) and IRENA-high (N = 63) groups based on the optimal cutoff value derived from ROC curve analysis (cut-off: log2(TPM + 1) = 2.7). The IRENA-high group was associated with adverse clinical features, including poor prognostic pathological subtypes (p = 0.008), advanced Ann Arbor stage (p = 0.003), a high prognostic index (PIT) score for PTCL (p = 0.037), and a higher proportion of R/R disease (p = 0.001). IRENA showed variable expression across four major pathological subtypes, with significantly higher levels in ALK- PTCL compared to ALK+ ALCL (Fig. 1G). The expression pattern was further confirmed by RNA FISH using an IRENA-specific-probe in patient-derived tumor tissue sections (Fig. 1H). Although IRENA has been previously reported to be expressed in tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in breast cancer [21], its cell of origin in PTCL remained undefined. To ascertain whether IRENA is expressed intrinsically by tumor cells in PTCL, we performed CIBERSORT deconvolution analysis on our bulk RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data. The results revealed a distinct correlation profile: IRENA expression correlated most strongly with the estimated proportion of CD4+ T cells (particularly T follicular helper cells), which represent the cell-of-origin for most PTCLs (Fig. 1I, J). Conversely, IRENA expression was negatively correlated or not correlated with the proportions of canonical tumor microenvironment (TME) cells, including CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and macrophages (Fig. S2A). This correlation pattern suggested that the IRENA signal primarily originates from malignant T cells rather than TME components. To visually confirm this hypothesis, we performed combined immunofluorescence and RNA FISH on a primary PTCL tumor section, which showed co-localization of IRENA RNA signals with CD4+ Ki67+ tumor cells, providing direct spatial evidence of its expression within proliferative malignant T cells (Fig. S2B). We next interrogated the genomic landscape of PTCL patients in relation to IRENA expression. The expression level of IRENA was not significantly associated with gene mutation frequencies in 154 patients with available DNA-sequencing data (Fig. S2C, Table S4). Besides, for the three most frequently mutated genes-TET2 (61.7%), RHOA (39.0%), and KMT2C (24.0%), no significant differences in IRENA expression were observed between mutant and wild-type groups (Fig. S2D).

IRENA promotes PTCL progression both in vitro and in vivo

IRENA was located in the forward strand of chromosome 20 at position 21,557,367-21,688,006 with 24 alternative splice variants. Since exon 3 was universally shared across all transcripts, we designed primers targeting exon 3 and performed both 5’ and 3’ RACE using RNA extracted from DL40 cell line to confirm the full-length sequence of IRENA (Fig. 2A). RACE results identified that IRENA was consisted of 4 exons and spanned 1649 nucleotides (nt) (Fig. S3A), which aligned with the ENST00000687963 transcript. Exon-spanning primers were then used for PCR amplification, and subsequent gel electrophoresis and Sanger sequencing confirmed the RACE-identified transcript (Fig. 2B). We used the NCBI open reading frame (ORF) finder online tool to identify ORFs in the IRENA sequence, revealing 11 ORFs with the potential to encode peptides (Fig. S3B). However, PhyloCSF analysis showed a negative score, indicating that these ORFs were unlikely to encode a functional protein (Fig. S3C). Furthermore, the coding potential assessment tool (CPAT) predicted a coding probability for IRENA as 0.03, with a negative Hexamer score (Fig. S3D). The expression of IRENA was further assessed in different PTCL cell lines. Gel electrophoresis of RT-PCR products was first performed to verify primer specificity and product size, which revealed no visible bands in Karpas299 and Jurkat cells (Fig. 2C). Quantitative RT-qPCR analysis subsequently confirmed this expression pattern, showing that DL40 cells had the highest expression level, over 70-fold greater than Karpas299 cells (Fig. 2D). Besides, the subcellular location of IRENA determined its mode of regulation and the potential mechanisms through which it functions [22]. We used lncLocator (Fig. S3E) and iLoc-LncRNA (Fig. S3F) to predict the subcellular localization of IRENA based on its sequence information, revealing a high probability of cytoplasmic localization. The subcellular localization of IRENA was further confirmed by the nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation with GAPDH and U6, referred to as controls for cytoplasmic and nuclear localization, respectively (Fig. 2E). Moreover, we performed RNA FISH using a specific probe targeting IRENA, with a scrambled RNA probe serving as a negative control. The results demonstrated that both endogenous IRENA in DL40 cells and exogenous IRENA overexpressed in Karpas299 cells were predominantly localized in the cytoplasm (Fig. 2F).

To investigate the pathogenic role of IRENA in PTCL, we established lentiviral-mediated overexpression in Karpas299 cells and shRNA-mediated knockdown in DL40 cells. Compared to vector control (VCT), the overexpression (OE) group showed dramatically increased IRENA expression in Karpas299 cells (Fig. 3A) and significantly enhanced cell viability (Fig. 3B). Apoptosis was assessed by flow cytometry based on early (Q3: PE single positive) and late (Q2: PE and 7-AAD double positive) apoptotic populations (Fig. S5A), and the percentage of apoptotic cells was significantly reduced in the OE group compared to the VCT group (Fig. 3C). Additionally, IRENA overexpression facilitated cell cycle progression, as evidenced by a decreased G0/G1 fraction and an increased S-phase population (Figs. 3D; S5B). Conversely, shRNA-mediated knockdown of IRENA in DL40 cells reduced IRENA expression by more than 50% compared to the non-targeting control (shNT) (Fig. 3E), decreased cell viability (Fig. 3F), increased apoptosis (Figs. 3G; S5E), and induced G0/G1 cell cycle arrest (Figs. 3H; S5F). To validate IRENA-induced tumor progression in vivo, 6-week-old NOD-SCID mice were subcutaneously implanted with Karpas299 cells from either the IRENA OE group or the VCT group (Fig. 3I). Tumor tissues isolated from the OE group exhibited nearly 1000-fold overexpression of IRENA (Fig. 3J), which was correlated with accelerated tumor growth (Fig. 3K) and increased tumor weight (Fig. 3L) compared to the VCT group. Furthermore, IRENA overexpression promoted tumor proliferation and inhibited cell apoptosis in vivo, as evidenced by increased Ki-67 staining and decreased cleaved caspase-3 (CC3) positivity in the OE group (Fig. 3M, N). These results were corroborated by flow cytometric analysis of mean fluorescence intensity (Fig. S4I, J) and apoptosis (Fig. S4K, L) in dissociated tumor tissues. These findings were further validated in a cell-derived xenograft (CDX) model established in 6-week-old NCG mice subcutaneously inoculated with DL40 cells from the loss-of-function assay (Fig. 3O). Tumors from the shIRENA groups showed effective downregulation of IRENA (Fig. 3P), delayed growth kinetics (Fig. 3Q), reduced tumor weight (Fig. 3R), as well as decreased Ki-67 positivity and elevated CC3 expression (Fig. 3S, 3T). Together, these findings indicated that IRENA induced a pro-tumorigenic phenotype both in vitro and in vivo.

IRENA scaffolds ARHGEF1 and FMNL1 to facilitate protein interaction

As a cytoplasmic non-coding RNA, IRENA has been reported to exert its oncogenic function in breast cancer by binding to protein kinase R and inducing its dimerization, thereby activating the downstream oncogenic signaling pathway [21]. To address the protein interactome of IRENA in PTCL, we performed RNA pulldown-mass spectrometry analysis in DL40 cells using in vitro-transcribed RNA. The antisense IRENA is a complementary sequence to the sense IRENA and serves as a control to account for non-specific binding. Silver staining showed differential bands in the IRENA sense group compared to the antisense group, with no bands observed in the polyA negative control group, confirming the specificity of the assay (Fig. S6A). A total of 465 proteins were detected by mass spectrometry analysis, with 62 specifically present in the IRENA sense group. According to the UniProtKB database, 40 proteins were localized in the cytoplasm (Fig. 4A and Table S5), which functionally enriched in pathways related to small GTPase binding and regulation of Rho protein signaling (Fig. 4B). Of note, three proteins emerged as key candidates due to their close association with small GTPase activity: ARHGEF1, an essential activator of the RHOA small GTPase; FMNL1, a downstream effector of RHOA; and protein kinase C alpha type (PRKCA), which directly phosphorylates and activates ARHGEF1. To further verify their interactions with IRENA, we conducted an IRENA pull-down assay followed by western blot analysis. Specific binding for ARHGEF1 and FMNL1 was observed in the IRENA sense group compared to the antisense group, confirming their interaction with IRENA (Fig. 4C). These findings were supported by computational predictions using catRAPID (computational analysis of RNA-protein interactions and dissociation), with interaction propensities for ARHGEF1 and FMNL1 above 80% (Fig. S7A). LncRNAs can regulate protein function by modulating their stability or interactions with other molecules [22]. qPCR and western blot analysis showed no change in ARHGEF1 and FMNL1 expression in IRENA overexpression or knockdown cells (Figs. 4E, S6B, C). Co-immunoprecipitation analysis revealed that IRENA enhanced the interaction between ARHGEF1 and FMNL1. In IRENA-overexpressing Karpas299 cells, the amounts of FMNL1 co-immunoprecipitated by ARHGEF1 and ARHGEF1 co-immunoprecipitated by FMNL1 were both significantly increased compared to the VCT group (Fig. 4F). Consistent results were obtained in DL40 cells, which endogenously express high levels of IRENA, where knockdown of IRENA reduced the interaction between ARHGEF1 and FMNL1 (Fig. 4G). These findings support the role of IRENA as a scaffold that promotes the interaction between ARHGEF1 and FMNL1. RNA FISH of IRENA, coupled with immunofluorescence staining of ARHGEF1 and FMNL1, also revealed their cytoplasmic colocalization (Fig. 4H). Since ARHGEF1 and FMNL1 function as upstream regulators and downstream effector of RHOA GTPase [23–25], respectively, RHOA co-IP was also increased by IRENA (Fig. 4F, G). Tissue sections from Karpas299-OE CDX tumors were subjected to multicolor immunofluorescence staining to detect ARHGEF1, FMNL1, and RHOA, with the analysis revealing their colocalization, as shown in Fig. 4I. We hypothesized that IRENA acts as a scaffold, bringing ARHGEF1 and FMNL1 into proximity and facilitating their interaction with RHOA.

To validate the hypothesis, we investigated the specific domains involved in their interactions. It is well known that the dbl homology (DH) domain of ARHGEF1 activates RHOA by converting it to its GTP-bound form [26]. The activated RHOA then binds to the GTPase-binding domain (GBD) of FMNL1, relieving autoinhibition of the FH2 domain and allowing it to engage downstream targets [27, 28]. Using catRAPID, we predicted potential interaction sites between IRENA and these proteins based on their sequences. The analysis indicates that the 5’ end of IRENA, highlighted by the nucleotide index in Fig. S7B, C, is most likely to interact with the RNA-binding domains of ARHGEF1 and FMNL1. To validate these predicted domains, four truncated IRENA variants were transcribed in vitro, with full-length (FL) IRENA as a positive control (Fig. S6D), followed by RNA pulldown assays. As shown in Fig. 4J, fragments 1 and 2, which contain the 5’ end of IRENA, interacted with ARHGEF1 and FMNL1, but did not directly bind to RHOA. To identify the binding domains in the proteins, we generated three truncated ARHGEF1 mutants (A1, A2 and A3). As shown in Fig. 4K, mutants A2 and A3 bound to FMNL1, indicating that the DH domain-responsible for RHOA binding and activation, was essential for the IRENA-mediated interaction with FMNL1. Similarly, we constructed three truncated FMNL1 mutants (F1, F2, and F3) which were stably expressed in HEK 293 cells. Co-immunoprecipitation analysis confirmed that the GBD domains were responsible for interacting with ARHGEF1 (Fig. 4L). To further characterize the domains required for RHOA binding, we performed co-immunoprecipitation assays using these truncated constructs. As shown in Fig. S6E, full-length ARHGEF1 and FMNL1 both interacted with RHOA. Notably, deletion of any functional domain in ARHGEF1 abrogated its binding to RHOA, whereas FMNL1 bound RHOA specifically through its GBD domain. These results provide deeper mechanistic insight into the domain-specific interactions between IRENA, ARHGEF1, FMNL1, and RHOA. Together, IRENA acted as a scaffold via its 5’ end, aligning the RHOA-binding domains of ARHGEF1 and FMNL1, facilitating their interaction with RHOA.

ARHGEF1 and FMNL1 are the specific activator and effector of RHOA GTPase [23–25], we thus assessed RHOA activity in IRENA-overexpressing Karpas299 cells and IRENA-knockdown DL40 cells. RHOA activation assays showed that the expression level of IRENA influences RHOA activation, as indicated by RHOA-GTP levels (Fig. 5B). Quantification of the gray values for the RHOA-GTP/total RHOA ratio normalized to ACTIN revealed that overexpression of IRENA enhanced RHOA activation in Karpas299 cells, while knockdown of IRENA reduced RHOA-GTP levels in DL40 cells (Fig. 5C). Given the established role of RHOA GTPase in regulating F-actin cytoskeletal dynamics during cell division, adhesion, and migration, we investigated whether IRENA affects cytoskeletal organization and cytokinesis by performing immunofluorescence staining for F-actin and α-tubulin in IRENA-overexpressing and knockdown cells. As shown in Fig. 5D, in Karpas299 cells overexpressing IRENA, cytokinesis was enhanced (yellow arrows), with the proportion of dividing cells increasing from 2.51% to 5.15% (Fig. 5F, upper panel), accompanied by an increased F-actin signal at the cleavage furrow (white arrows). In contrast, in DL40 cells with IRENA knockdown, the proportion of cytokinetic cells (Fig.5E, orange arrows) decreased from 5.50% to 3.45% (Fig. 5F, middle panel), along with a reduced F-actin signal at the cleavage furrow (Fig.5E, white arrows). Besides, an increase in multinucleated cells (green rectangles) was observed in IRENA-knockdown DL40 cells, from 1.70% to 4.02% (Fig. 5F, lower panel), indicating cytokinesis failure. Collectively, these results demonstrate that IRENA modulates RHOA-dependent cytoskeletal organization and cytokinesis in a manner consistent with its regulation of RHOA activity. Additionally, IRENA activated the downstream MAPK pathway, as evidenced by elevated phosphorylation of JNK and p38 (Fig. 5G).

Emerging evidence suggests that Rho GTPase activation is regulated through a self-organizing pattern involving positive and negative feedback loops, rather than a simple linear process [29]. Active RHOA itself [30] and its downstream effectors [31] or targets [32] can directly bind to and activate upstream GEFs, generating positive feedback and sustaining continuous GTPase cycling. To determine whether IRENA establishes a positive feedback loop by scaffolding the downstream effector FMNL1 and ARHGEF1, we performed a series of RHOA activation assays in HEK 293 T cells. Since ARHGEF1 is the key regulator of RHOA activation, we ectopically overexpressed HA-tagged ARHGEF1 in HEK 293 T cells (Fig. 5H, Group 2) and evaluated its effects on RHOA activation and downstream MAPK signaling pathways under various conditions: with additional overexpression of IRENA (Fig. 5H, Group 3), FLAG-tagged FMNL1 (Fig. 5H, Group 4), and the simultaneous overexpression of ARHGEF1, IRENA and FMNL1 (Fig. 5H, Group 5). Group 1, transfected with three control plasmids, served as the negative control. As shown in Fig. 5H, the levels of activated RHOA-GTP gradually increased from Group 1 to Group 5, while total RHOA expression remained relatively stable compared to ACTIN. Co-overexpression of IRENA (Group 3) and FMNL1 (Group 4) further enhanced RHOA activation compared to ARHGEF1 alone (Group 2). Notably, the highest level of RHOA activation was observed when IRENA, ARHGEF1, and FMNL1 were simultaneously co-overexpressed (Group 5). In parallel, as RHOA activation increased progressively from Groups 1 to 5, a corresponding enhancement in MAPK pathway activation was observed (Fig. 5I). Therefore, IRENA scaffolded ARHGEF1 and FMNL1 to establish a positive feedback loop that amplified RHOA and MAPK signaling activation (Fig. 5J). To ascertain whether the oncogenic role of IRENA is mediated through RHOA, we performed a rescue experiment in IRENA-overexpressing Karpas299 cells. Molecular silencing of RHOA was performed with three independent siRNAs, which significantly reduced RHOA expression at both the mRNA (Fig. S8F) and protein levels (Fig. S8G) compared to the non-targeting control (siNC). Crucially, the enhanced cell variability conferred by IRENA expression was effectively abolished upon RHOA depletion, whereas the control siRNA had no effect (Fig. S8H). These results demonstrate that the pro-tumorigenic function of IRENA is functionally dependent on RHOA.

ASO treatment inhibits IRENA-induced tumor progression

To validate the therapeutic potential of targeting IRENA, we designed a targeted ASO, which effectively degraded IRENA in vitro upon transfection into IRENA-overexpressing Karpas299 cells, resulting in decreased IRENA expression (Fig. 6A) and suppressed cell viability (Fig. 6B). To further validate the therapeutic efficacy of the ASO in vivo, we used IRENA-overexpressing Karpas299 cells to establish xenograft tumors in six-week-old NOD-SCID mice, as this model enables rapid and consistent tumor formation, a critical feature for time-sensitive therapeutic intervention. When tumors reached approximately 100 mm³ (Fig. 6C), the mice were randomly divided into two treatment arms (ASO or Vehicle). ASO treatment successfully degraded IRENA in vivo (Fig. 6D) and significantly inhibited tumor growth (Fig. 6E) without affecting overall body weight (Fig. 6F). In the ASO treatment arm, tumor cell proliferation was inhibited, as evidenced by decreased Ki-67 positivity, while cell apoptosis was enhanced, indicated by increased cleaved caspase-3 positivity, compared to the vehicle arm (Fig. 6G). As a mechanism of action, the ASO treatment disrupted the interaction between ARHGEF1 and FMNL1 by degrading IRENA (Fig. 6H), and inhibited IRENA-induced MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 6I). As an oncogenic factor, IRENA promoted tumor growth in vivo, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target. Discussion

LncRNA IRENA serves as a baseline predictor of R/R risk and an independent prognostic factor for poor outcomes in PTCL. Through gain- and loss-of-function assays in vitro, IRENA was demonstrated to enhance cell viability, reduce apoptosis, and promote cell cycle progression across multiple PTCL cell lines. As a cytoplasmic lncRNA, IRENA interacted with ARHGEF1 and FMNL1, thereby enhancing their binding to RHOA and establishing a positive feedback loop that amplified RHOA GTPase and MAPK pathway activation. Therapeutic targeting IRENA with ASO disrupted RHOA activation and inhibited tumor growth both in vitro and in vivo. Our study demonstrated the scaffolding role of lncRNA IRENA in establishing a self-organizing pattern of RHO small GTPase activation, which underscored its potential to identify high-risk PTCL patients who may benefit from targeted therapeutic approaches.

LncRNAs often exhibit context-dependent expression patterns. For example, lncRNA MALAT1 is expressed in over 16 cancer types, including solid tumors and hematologic malignancies, demonstrating that lncRNAs can function in diverse cellular contexts [33]. Consistently, IRENA was first reported to be expressed in TAMs in breast cancer, where it acts as an IFN-induced cytoplasmic lncRNA that activates NF-κB signaling by promoting protein kinase R dimerization, subsequently modulating macrophage polarization [21]. In our study, IRENA is predominantly expressed in CD4+ T cells in PTCL. Although the cell-of-origin concept in PTCL is debated, current evidence indicates that nodal PTCLs are primarily derived from CD4+ T cells, with only a small subset of PTCL-NOS potentially originating from CD8+ T cells [3, 34]. Thus, our findings suggest that in nodal PTCL, IRENA originates from the tumor cells themselves rather than from microenvironmental cells, further illustrating its context-dependent expression across different malignancies. Therefore, we broadened the understanding of IRENA in PTCL progression and proposed a novel regulatory mechanism, wherein IRENA acted as a scaffold to regulate RHOA GTPase activity.

LncRNAs can function as scaffolds to provide structural platforms that bring related proteins into close proximity, promoting their interactions and optimizing downstream signal transduction [35–37]. For instance, the lncRNA HOTAIR acts as a scaffold by binding distinct histone modification complexes to coordinate chromatin modifications, ultimately regulating gene expression [37]. A recent study [38] has also found that long mRNAs can act as signaling scaffolds, bringing FXR1 molecules together to activate RHOA signaling-induced actomyosin reorganization by maintaining spatial proximity between kinases and their substrates. This highlights a broader structural role for RNA molecules serving as scaffolds in the activation of the RHOA signaling pathway. Besides, the self-organizing pattern of RHO GTPase is often tightly coordinated in space and time, driven by positive and negative feedback loops involving GEFs, effectors, and targets, allowing for more dynamic and adaptable control. In our study, RHOA activation also involved a similar self-organizing process, facilitated by the scaffolding of effector FMNL1 and ARHGEF1 by IRENA. This type of positive feedback loop between effectors and GEFs is well-established in previous studies [31, 39, 40]. The interaction between FMNL1 and ARHGEF1 mirrors the mechanism observed with Dia1 and LARG [31]. Dia1, a downstream effector in the formin family, functions similarly to FMNL1 by releasing its autoinhibition upon RHOA activation, thereby exposing its FH2 domain. This exposed domain not only activates downstream targets but also interacts with the upstream RhoGEF, LARG, enhancing its GEF activity. This interaction creates a positive feedback loop that further amplifies RHOA activation. Thus, FMNL1 similarly interacts with ARHGEF1, enhancing its GEF activity and establishing a positive feedback loop that further activates RHOA. Notably, co-overexpression of IRENA with ARHGEF1 and FMNL1 in HEK 293 T cells led to the strongest activation of both RHOA and the MAPK signaling pathway. This suggested that IRENA amplifies the positive feedback loop between FMNL1 and ARHGEF1 by spatially organizing these components, thereby enhancing the RHOA GTP-GDP cycle and driving sustained RHOA activation.

The Rho family of small GTPases, including RHOA, RAC, and CDC42, regulates key cellular processes in PTCL, including cell morphology, migration, proliferation, apoptosis, and cytokinesis. In ALK⁺ T-cell lymphomas, the NPM-ALK fusion oncoprotein drives malignant phenotypes through activation of GTPase and MAPK/mTOR signaling via its kinase activity [41–43]. Recent work has demonstrated that in ALK⁻ ALCL, the DNA helicase HELLS transcriptionally activates RhoA, RhoU, and their effector Pak2, thereby promoting cytokinesis and proliferation [44]. These findings illustrate that Rho GTPase signaling can be engaged through both ALK-dependent and -independent mechanisms. In our study, we showed that IRENA, a scaffold lncRNA overexpressed in ALK⁻ PTCL, integrates the upstream RHOA activator ARHGEF1 and the downstream effector FMNL1, thereby activating RHOA GTPase and the downstream MAPK pathway. IRENA-mediated RHOA activation represents an alternative, ALK-independent route to regulate GTPase-driven oncogenic pathways. Conversely, the low IRENA expression in ALK⁺ ALCL may reflect functional redundancy, as NPM-ALK itself already potently drives downstream signals.

In PTCL, studies on RHOA primarily focus on its G17V mutant, identified in 53-71% of nTFHL cases and 8-18% of PTCL-NOS cases [7, 45]. In cellular assays, the RHOAG17V mutant showed impaired GTP binding and could disrupt wild-type RHOA activity by sequestering the function of GEFs, classifying it as a dominant-negative mutant [7]. Although in vitro studies suggested a dominant-negative function for the G17V mutant, it could still facilitate tumorigenesis by activating alternative pathways in vivo [46]. Notably, recent evidence attributes this oncogenic behavior to a novel gain-of-function mechanism: the RHOAG17V mutant synergizes with CD28T195P to enhance CD3/CD28-induced NFAT transcriptional activity by binding p300 and stimulating its histone acetyltransferase activity, thereby driving lymphomagenesis [47]. Thus, RHOA and its associated GTPase signaling pathway are not intrinsically tumor-suppressive in PTCL. Instead, aberrant RHOA signaling in cancers frequently results from RHOA overexpression, gain-of-function mutations, or hyperactivation due to the dysregulation of its upstream regulators [48–50]. Enrichment analyses based on our RNA-seq data indicated that IRENA upregulation was associated with the positive regulation of small GTPase and JNK signaling pathways, and the RHOA activation assay further confirmed that IRENA activated both RHOA and its downstream MAPK pathways in vitro. The tumor-promoting effects of IRENA on RHOA activation observed in our study are independent of the RHOAG17V mutation status, as no correlation was observed between IRENA expression and RHOA mutation status. The RHOAG17V mutation and IRENA overexpression may represent two distinct yet convergent mechanisms promoting lymphomagenesis. The RHOA G17V mutant disrupts canonical RHOA activation while acquiring alternative gain-of-function properties that drive downstream oncogenic pathways. In contrast, IRENA enhances canonical RHOA activation irrespective of mutation status, potentially leading to distinct signaling outputs. Both mechanisms ultimately contribute to a pro-tumorigenic state. This model is consistent with the strong prognostic value of IRENA observed across multiple PTCL subtypes, not all of which exhibit high frequencies of RHOAG17V mutation.

Small GTPases have historically been considered “undruggable” due to the absence of pharmacologically targetable pockets in both their wild-type and mutant forms, presenting a major challenge for drug development. For a protein family with over 150 members, only two drugs targeting KRAS G12C have been clinically approved, while the progress of targeting Rho, Arf, and Rab falls far behind Ras [51]. Recently, a cryptic allosteric pocket in the switch II region of the small GTPase family has been discovered, creating new opportunities for designing small-molecule inhibitors that specifically target these GTPases [52]. Despite this progress, translating these findings into effective therapeutic strategies for PTCL remains a time-consuming challenge. In our study, targeting IRENA with ASO treatment offers a strategic advantage by inhibiting RHOA activation at an upstream level, thereby bypassing the challenges associated with directly targeting RHOA GTPase. ASO therapies have already proven to be safe and effective, with several ASO-based products approved for clinical use [15, 53, 54]. Here, IRENA-targeting ASO administered in CDX models successfully inhibited tumor growth by disrupting the interactions between ARHGEF1 and FMNL1 and suppressing the MAPK signaling pathway. Building on these, applying ASO treatment to target IRENA in PTCL patients with high IRENA expression offers a promising approach to potentially improve prognosis by directly suppressing the pro-tumorigenic effects of the RHOA/MAPK signaling pathway.

Conclusions

This study identified the pathogenic role of lncRNA IRENA in PTCL, which functioned as a scaffold that drives a self-organizing positive feedback loop in RHOA GTPase signaling. ASO therapy targeting IRENA directly counteracted small GTPases and was a promising strategy to inhibit IRENA-RHOA/MAPK-driven PTCL progression.
