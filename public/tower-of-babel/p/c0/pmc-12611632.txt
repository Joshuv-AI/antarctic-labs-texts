Late-onset lupus nephritis: what is the evidence?
Authors: Sophie Green, Henry HL Wu, Arvind Ponnusamy, Rajkumar Chinnadurai
Published: 2025

Late-onset lupus nephritis (LN) (i.e., diagnosis at age ≥50 years), adding to the already established late-onset systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) entity, is an issue of growing clinical importance with an aging global population. Shabaka et al. [1] noted that the age at which patients with SLE are subsequently diagnosed with LN has progressively increased over the past three decades (from 1994 to 2019). Most patients diagnosed with late-onset SLE were found to have higher serum creatinine and lower estimated glomerular filtration rate compared to patients diagnosed with SLE at a younger age, and the prevalence of end-stage kidney disease upon diagnosis of LN is also significantly higher [1–3].

Whilst there may be concerns in relation to the risks versus benefits in performing kidney biopsies for older patients, a kidney biopsy is required to determine the classification and severity of LN. General consensus from the nephrology community remains in favor of older patients undertaking a kidney biopsy to confirm LN status, given adverse events associated with kidney biopsy are not frequent and findings from kidney biopsy are instrumental in informing specific therapeutic strategies for more than 50% of LN patients [4].

Based on available published data, histopathological assessment of kidney biopsy samples revealed class IV LN was less frequently observed in late-onset SLE in comparison to cases where SLE were diagnosed at a younger age [1]. Tian et al. [2] noted no differences in the presence of active changes (i.e. crescents, glomerular capsule adhesions, subendothelial hyaline deposits and tubular necrosis) between kidney biopsy samples obtained from patients with childbearing age-onset and late-onset LN. Otherwise, evaluation of LN-associated kidney biopsy samples also reported chronic histopathological changes amongst late-onset cases [3].

It is recognized that the prognosis of patients with late-onset SLE is poorer compared to those diagnosed with SLE at a younger age. This is mainly attributed to an increased number of comorbidities and an increased rate of progression towards end-organ damage, secondary to age-associated senescence and heightened exposure to cardiovascular risk factors. Despite having a poorer overall prognosis, late-onset LN typically exhibits a milder trajectory of disease progression compared to childbearing age-onset LN [2]. Tian et al. [2] highlighted that whilst a higher baseline serum creatinine is more commonly observed in patients diagnosed with late-onset LN, there were no significant differences in kidney outcomes between patients diagnosed with late-onset and childbearing age-onset LN.

Numerous studies evaluated differences in the use of immunosuppressive therapy between patients with late-onset and childbearing age-onset LN. Patients with late-onset LN are less likely to be prescribed multiple classes of disease-modifying medications (including antimalarials, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), and calcineurin inhibitors) [5]. This reflects the lower severity of the disease course in late-onset LN. In relation to corticosteroid use, Mongkolchaiarunya et al. [6] found that patients with late-onset LN received a higher proportion of moderate-to-high dose corticosteroids in comparison to other immunosuppressive drugs. Meticulous prescription of immunosuppressive drugs is now recommended for the older LN population, considering a higher risk of infective complications [3]. Either complete steroid avoidance or following the management approach as described from the AURORA and the recently published REGENCY clinical trials, involving rapid tapering of corticosteroid dose to 5 mg by 24 weeks of commencing the treatment course (i.e., for the AURORA trial, the treatment course included voclosporin on a background of MMF and corticosteroids; for the REGENCY trial, the treatment course included obinutuzumab on a background of MMF and corticosteroids), may be appropriate for the late-onset LN population to reduce risk of complications [7,8]. Furthermore, there are newly approved biological therapies for LN treatment, which have shown particular relevance in the context of late-onset LN. For one, belimumab demonstrated a high level of safety and efficacy in a post hoc integrated analysis of data from six randomized, placebo-controlled belimumab clinical trials (BLISS-76, BLISS-52, BLISS-SC, Northeast Asia study, LBSL02, and EMBRACE) and the BASE study for adults above the age of 65 years with LN [9].

There remain knowledge gaps for further investigation within the topic of late-onset LN. A clearer distinction to define the pathophysiology and clinical trajectory between late-onset SLE cases presenting subsequently with LN and LN diagnosed after the age of 50 years in those with long-standing SLE is needed. Much of the current data relating to this topical area are from retrospective studies, which may lack specific clinical and histological information on study participants. A greater insight into the global epidemiology of late-onset LN based on multicenter prospective observational studies is also required. Frailty, an age-related clinical syndrome characterized by a decline in physiologic reserve and an associated decreased ability to respond to stressor events, has now been used as a clinical measure to prognosticate outcomes and to guide treatment strategy in late-onset SLE [10]. Perhaps frailty status could be utilized similarly within the context of late-onset LN to inform optimized treatment strategies for this patient population.
