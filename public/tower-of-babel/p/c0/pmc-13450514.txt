Papulovesicular eruption after administration of zoledronic acid: A rare cutaneous manifestation of an acute phase reaction
Authors: ShengYang Bertrand Lian, Michelle Weiting Liang, Yee Kiat Heng
Published: 2026

Zoledronic acid is an intravenous bisphosphonate that inhibits osteoclastic bone resorption, thus reducing fracture risk. It is used in the treatment of osteoporosis and cancer-related bone complications. Acute phase reactions that include systemic complaints of fever, flu-like symptoms, and myalgia are commonly encountered within the first 3 days of administration.1 While cutaneous side effects have been reported as uncommon (frequency of ≥1 in 1000 to <1 in 100),2 these have not been reported during acute phase reactions1 and do not demonstrate any consistent distinct morphologic patterns. We report a case of a papulovesicular eruption arising concomitant to an acute phase reaction after administration of zoledronic acid.

The patient is a 67-year-old man with a history of osteoporosis, hepatitis C (completed treatment), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. He had no prior dermatologic history. One dose of intravenous zoledronic acid was administered for osteopenia with a high Fracture Risk Assessment Tool score after consultation with his endocrinologist. He was bisphosphonate-naïve, and there were no other concomitant drugs administered. Within 2 days of administration, he experienced fever and musculoskeletal pain. A papulovesicular rash then developed in the patient 3 days after, and he was subsequently reviewed by our department (Figs 1 and 2). The rash was pruritic and demonstrated a centripetal spread. Blood investigations revealed a normal complete blood cell count, renal function, and liver function. The C-reactive protein level was also normal. A skin biopsy of the lesion was performed and showed spongiotic dermatitis with microvesiculation and areas of basal vacuolar alteration. There were numerous eosinophils in the superficial perivascular infiltrate (Fig 3, A-C). Causality was assessed using the Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale, yielding a score of 7, consistent with a probable adverse drug reaction to zoledronic acid. In view of the acute systemic manifestations, drug latency, and absence of other culprit drugs, a diagnosis of zoledronic acid acute phase reaction with a papulovesicular eruption was made. Zoledronic acid was withdrawn, and he was given a course of tapering prednisolone over 2 weeks with near-complete resolution. Zoledronic acid–associated acute phase reactions are seen in up to 42% of patients and have been categorized into 5 groups: fever, musculoskeletal, gastrointestinal, ocular inflammation, and general symptoms. These symptoms characteristically arise within 3 days of administration and are short-lived, generally resolving by day 11.1,3 Notable risk factors for acute phase reactions include a younger age, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug use, and bisphosphonate-naïve patients.1,4 Cutaneous manifestations have not been reported as part of this constellation of features. The exact pathogenesis of the acute phase reaction has yet to be fully elucidated, but in vitro studies have shown the ability of nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates to activate CD3+ γδ T cells via inhibition of farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase in the mevalonate pathway.5 Increased secretion of cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor, IFN-γ, and interleukin 6 have been demonstrated and are believed to drive such reactions.5,6 Additionally, zoledronic acid has suppressive effects on T regulatory cells, which can upregulate T helper (Th) 17 inflammatory pathways.7 Dysregulation of tumor necrosis factor and IFN-γ (prototypical Th-1 inflammatory cytokines), together with Th-17 pathways, have been described as key pathogenic drivers of allergic contact dermatitis.8 The overlap between the cytokine signature of the acute phase reaction and that implicated in allergic contact dermatitis provides a mechanistic basis for cutaneous involvement in susceptible patients. A papulovesicular eruption developed in our patient; histologic findings were compatible with drug-induced eczematous dermatitis. We propose that this represents a systemic contact dermatitis-like reaction arising from the abnormal Th-1 and Th-17 inflammatory cascades that characterize the acute phase reaction, though further studies are needed to substantiate this hypothesis. Most zoledronic acid-induced acute phase reactions are short-lived and self-limited, only requiring symptomatic treatment.1 Attenuation of the acute phase reaction with repeat dosing further suggests that recurrence of cutaneous involvement is less likely should further infusions be necessary.1 Earlier studies have also demonstrated efficacy of prophylactic nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs in reducing incidence of acute phase reactions.9 Such measures can be discussed with patients at risk of these reactions. In conclusion, we report the case of a papulovesicular eruption as part of an acute phase reaction to zoledronic acid. Awareness of this entity will help clinicians avoid attributing causality to other concomitant drugs and allow appropriate reassurance regarding future dosing.
