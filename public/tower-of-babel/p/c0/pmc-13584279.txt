Clinical malaria recurrence and associated factors in infancy following IPTp-DP versus IPTp-SP in Busia, Uganda; a secondary analysis using the Conditional Frailty model
Authors: Edward Ikoona, Adoke Yeka, Charles Natuhamya, Samuel Munyole, Fiona Agweng, Paul Mubiri, Josephine Tumuhamye, John Ssenkusu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn sub-Saharan Africa, malaria remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in children under five. Recurrent malaria not only increases the risk of severe anaemia and developmental delays in infants, but also presents unique challenges for statistical modelling, as episodes are often correlated. Studies have explored malaria dynamics in infants but gaps remain in contextualizing specific risk factors and how they influence malaria recurrence using appropriate modelling techniques that account for correlation. This study compared malaria recurrence among study infants and explored associated risk factors.MethodsThis was a secondary analysis of data from the PROMOTE trial conducted in Busia district, Uganda. A total of 640 infants born to women who received IPTp-DP or IPTp-SP were followed-up for 12 months. These infants experienced a total of 997 malaria episodes. To also assess dependence in malaria episodes and between-infant heterogeneity, the conditional frailty model was used. The PWP-gap time and Frailty only models were used to confirm the true underlying type of event dependence.ResultsIPTp-DP was associated with a modest but non-statistically significant reduction in recurrent malaria hazard compared with IPTp-SP (HR = 0.94, 95% CI 0.82–1.07). Compared with infants whose mothers had no education, infants born to mothers with primary, secondary and higher education had 21%, 42%, and 48% lower hazards of malaria recurrence. Infants whose mothers were 30–45 years had a 28% lower hazard of malaria recurrence compared to those of mothers 16–19 years (HR = 0.72, 95% CI 0.53–0.97). Multigravida mothers had 36% increased hazard of malaria recurrence in their infants compared with primi-gravida mothers (HR = 1.36, 95% CI 1.02–1.80). Infants living 3–5 km from a health facility had 40% greater hazard of malaria recurrence compared with infants in less than 1km (HR = 1.40, 95% CI 1.11–1.77). Malaria episodes were correlated but there was weak non-statistically significant evidence of residual between-infant heterogeneity.ConclusionsCompared with IPTp-SP, IPTp-DP did not significantly reduce malaria recurrence in infants. Maternal education, maternal age, and distance to health center were associated with the hazard of malaria recurrence in infants. Recurrent malaria episodes were correlated, with evidence of event dependence across successive episodes.

Introduction

Malaria remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among children under five years of age in sub-Saharan Africa [1]. In countries with persistently high malaria transmission such as Uganda, repeated clinical malaria episodes in the first year of life contribute substantially to childhood anaemia, growth faltering and neurodevelopmental deficits [2–4], imposing a continuous burden on families and health systems. Preventing malaria during pregnancy is a cornerstone strategy to protect both pregnant women and their off-springs. Intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp) has the potential to improve birth outcomes and to modify an infant’s early risk of malaria [5], however, the optimal IPTp regimen for translating these pregnancy-period benefits into reduced infant malaria remains uncertain [6, 7]. The standard World Health Organization, WHO recommended regimen, sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine (SP), has demonstrated benefits for maternal and perinatal outcomes where parasite resistance is limited [8, 9], but, increasing resistance has prompted investigations of alternative regimens such as dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine (DP). Studies in East Africa have compared IPTp-DP with IPTp-SP on reduction of placental malaria and adverse birth outcomes, but evidence on infant malaria outcomes is mixed [10–14]. Placental malaria and poor fetal growth are associated with infant malaria susceptibility [15] and a regimen that better suppresses maternal parasitemia could reduce early life exposure and consequent risk.

The determinants of recurrent malaria in infancy are multifactorial. Maternal characteristics, infant factors, household attributes and climatic drivers interact to shape exposure and susceptibility [16, 17]. Empirical studies have repeatedly shown protective associations of maternal education, older maternal age, and elevated risk among first-time mothers and infants born small-for-gestational-age or with low birth weight [18–20]. These risk pathways are salient when assessing whether antenatal interventions alter infant outcomes, but any observed treatment effects may also be confounded or modified by these contextual factors, necessitating careful covariate assessment in analyses aimed at estimating possible effect of IPTp on infant malaria recurrence.

In high‑transmission settings, infants often experience multiple malaria episodes during the first year of life, and the risk of a new episode is likely influenced by prior infections and by unmeasured, infant‑specific susceptibility [2, 21]. Recurrent‑event methods are useful in accommodating both event dependence and within‑infant correlation. The conditional frailty model, which combines stratification by event order with a subject‑specific frailty term, is well suited to this context because it allows separate baseline hazards for successive episodes while accounting for residual heterogeneity between infants [22], yielding more robust estimates.

Given this background, rigorous empirical assessment of infant malaria recurrence after maternal IPTp requires; (i) high-quality prospective follow-up of infants born to randomized mothers; (ii) comprehensive measurement of maternal, infant and household covariates; and (iii) recurrent-event methods that explicitly account for event dependence and heterogeneity. This analysis aimed to compare malaria recurrence among infants born to mothers randomized to IPTp-DP or IPTp-SP and to explore associated risk factors.

Methods

Study design

This was a secondary analysis of data from the PROMOTE trial, a randomized controlled phase III trial to compare malaria recurrence rates among infants born to mothers randomized to IPTp-DP or IPTp-SP and explore associated risk factors.

Data source

The PROMOTE study was a double-blind randomized controlled phase III trial carried out at Masafu hospital in Busia district, Uganda, between 2016 and 2018. A total of 782 HIV-negative pregnant women aged 16 years and older, were randomized in equal proportions to receive either IPTp-SP or IPTp-DP. Infant malaria episodes were detected through a combination of scheduled clinic visits at 1, 4, 6 and 8 weeks and every 4 weeks thereafter, and passive surveillance where caregivers would bring the child to the study clinic whenever the child was ill. This study is described in detail by Kajubi, Ochieng [14].

Study population

This study was carried out among infants born to mothers who completed the PROMOTE study. Infants were enrolled onto the study at birth and were followed up until 12 months of age.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All infants with available follow-up data were retained for analysis. Exclusions were limited to twin records, infants without follow-up after delivery, and malaria episodes that did not meet the definition of a new episode for this recurrent event analysis.

Study variables

The dependent variable was time (in days) to recurrent malaria episodes; defined as number of days after 14 days of treatment of a previous malaria episode. Incident uncomplicated malaria episodes were treated with weight‑based artemether–lumefantrine according to Uganda Ministry of Health guidelines, while severe malaria was managed with parenteral artesunate or quinine as appropriate. Only new episodes experienced by the infants were considered.

Independent variables included; IPTp regimen (DP/SP), maternal factors (including age at enrollment, education, gestation age at enrollment, gravidity), infant factors (low-birth-weight, infant sex, small-for-gestation-age, sleeping under a treated mosquito net the previous night), wealth index (constructed from household asset and living-standard variables using principal component analysis), CHW_present (presence of a community health worker who distributes malaria medicines in the village or community) and distance (km) from the health facility.

Outcome definition and time at risk

The primary outcome was time to recurrent clinical malaria in infants during the first year of life. Clinical malaria was defined as the presence of fever (axillary temperature ≥ 37.5 °C or history of fever in the previous 24 h) together with a positive thick blood smear for asexual Plasmodium falciparum parasites. For the first malaria episode, time at risk begun at birth, while for recurrent episodes, a new malaria episode was defined as any clinical malaria event occurring more than 14 days after the diagnosis and treatment of a previous episode. Episodes occurring within 14 days of a prior malaria treatment were considered part of the same episode and were excluded from the recurrent event dataset. In this analysis using the conditional frailty model, the 14‑day window was treated as a non–risk period, so, time during these 14 days did not contribute to the risk set. The 14‑day interval reflects the expected parasite clearance and early recrudescence window after artemisinin‑based combination therapy in this setting and follows prior longitudinal malaria analyses that distinguish new infections from early treatment failures using a 14‑day clearance period.

Recurrent malaria episodes were modeled using this conditional frailty Cox model in gap-time form. The multivariable model was fit in R and it included study arm and other covariates. A subject-specific gamma frailty term was included for “infant ID” to account for within-infant correlation, and the baseline hazard was stratified by “episode interval” using strata(interval) to allow different hazards across recurrent events. The model was fitted on the long-format start–stop data set, with time defined as gap-time since 14 days post-treatment of the previous malaria episode. In this specification, episode represented the recurrent event outcome in the survival object, while time represented the gap time between episodes.

Independent variables were included in the model primarily on the basis of their biological importance to malaria risk in infants. The statistical significance of effects and parameters was at P-values less than 0.05.

Results

Participant flow

A total of 782 HIV‑negative pregnant women were randomized in the PROMOTE trial, with 391 allocated to monthly IPTp‑SP and 391 to monthly IPTp‑DP. Among these pregnancies, 666 resulted in live births, 7 were macerated stillbirths, and pregnancy outcome was unavailable for 109 women. Of the 666 live‑born infants enrolled and followed; 26 were excluded from the present analysis, including 13 twins and 13 infants with no follow‑up data after delivery. The final recurrent malaria analysis therefore included 640 singleton infants as shown in Fig. 1Fig. 1Participant flow

Participant flow

Background characteristics

A total of 640 infants were included in this study, 325 (50.8%) females and 315 (49.2%) males. The average age of the study mothers was 24 years, with 176 (27.5%) adolescents aged 16–19 years, 210 (32.8%) young adults aged 20–24 years, 130 (20.3%) early adults aged 25–29 years and 124 (19.4%) older adults 30–45 years.

Among the mothers, 433 (67.7%) had attained primary education, followed by 141 (22%) with secondary education, 53 (8.3%) with no education and 13 (2%) with higher education. More than half of the study mothers, 334 (52.2%) were multi-gravidae (> = 3), 155 (24.2%) were secundi-gravidae and 151 (23.6%) were primi-gravidae. Most of the infants (275,43.5%) lived in households moderately close (1–2 km) to the nearest health facility, 208(32.5%) lived in households moderately distant (3–5 km), 79 (12.4%) lived very close (< 1 km) and 38 (5.9%) lived far (> = 6 km). The average number of malaria episodes per infant was 1.6 and the median was 1 episode. Because the survival curves for the DP and SP arms overlapped and the log-rank test P-value (0.59) was greater than 0.05, this suggested non-statistically significant difference in time to first malaria episode between infants in the two study arms.Fig. 3Survival to first malaria episode by study arm

Modelling results in Table 2 revealed that infants whose mothers received IPTp-DP had a 6% lower hazard of recurrent malaria episodes compared to those whose mothers received IPTp-SP (HR = 0.94; 95% CI 0.82–1.07). Compared with infants born to mothers with no education, infants born to mothers who attained primary education had a 21% lower hazard of recurrent malaria episodes, (HR = 0.79; 95% CI 0.63–0.98), those born to mothers who attained secondary education had a 42% lower hazard of recurrent malaria episodes (HR = 0.58; 95% CI 0.45–0.76), and infants born to mothers who attained higher education had a 48% lower hazard of recurrent malaria (HR = 0.52; 95% CI 0.27–0.99). Compared with those born to mothers 16–19 years old, infants born to mothers 30–45 years old had a 28% lower hazard of recurrent malaria (HR = 0.72; 95% CI 0.53–0.97), however the associations for infants born to mothers aged 20–24 years old and those born to mothers aged 25–29 years were not statistically significant. Compared with infants born to primi-gravida mothers, those born to multi-gravida (> = 3) mothers had a 36% increased hazard of recurrent malaria episodes (HR = 1.36; 95% CI 1.02–1.80). Compared with those who lived in households less than 1 km from the closest health facility, infants who lived in households 3–5 km from the closest health facility had a 40% greater hazard for recurrent malaria episodes (HR = 1.40; 95% CI 1.11–1.77). The conditional frailty model separately identifies whether the underlying correlation is due to event dependence or due to between-infant heterogeneity. For this model, the plot of cumulative hazards for the different malaria episodes showed evidence of event dependence while the near zero frailty variance with a non-significant P-value and a plot of infants’ frailties which was not wide spread, proved that there was limited residual between-infant heterogeneity. The PWP-gap time model also showed that there was event dependence from its plot of the cumulative hazards. The frailty-only model on the other hand showed existence of between-infant heterogeneity. Notably, it falsely captured and represented event dependence as residual between-infant heterogeneity, because the random effect for frailty absorbs any event dependence and presents it as between-infant heterogeneity, since this model doesn’t stratify by event order.

Discussion

This study assessed recurrent clinical malaria episodes during the first year of life of infants born to mothers who received either IPTp-DP or IPTp-SP during pregnancy in Busia district, a high malaria transmission setting in Uganda. Conditional frailty modelling was used to account for correlations/dependence in recurrent malaria episodes and between-infant heterogeneity.

Compared with infants whose mothers received IPTp-SP, infants whose mothers received IPTp-DP experienced a slightly lower hazard of recurrent malaria, but this difference was not statistically significant. This finding is consistent with previous work in East Africa [27, 28], where IPTp-DP has shown clear benefits for reducing maternal and placental malaria and improving birth outcomes, but has only modest or imprecise effects on infant malaria incidence and recurrence. Randomized trials in Uganda and Kenya reported that IPTp-DP substantially reduced maternal parasitaemia and placental malaria compared with IPTp-SP, yet the impact on infant malaria outcomes was small and often not statistically significant [10–12, 14]. The biological rationale for a downstream effect remains strong, given the established association between placental malaria, adverse birth outcomes and subsequent infant susceptibility [20], but results from this study suggest that in this setting, any incremental benefit of IPTp-DP on infant malaria recurrence beyond that of IPTp-SP is likely modest. This finding re-echoes another prior report [5] indicating minimal postnatal benefits of IPTp-DP on infant malaria outcomes despite its superior efficacy during pregnancy. However, this study’s limited statistical power implies that the non-significant result should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as hypothesis generating and perhaps supportive of further studies, rather than definitive evidence of no effect.

In addition to the IPTp regimen, maternal sociodemographic characteristics and healthcare access were significantly associated with the hazard of recurrent malaria in infancy. Maternal education emerged as protective, with infants born to mothers who attended primary, secondary, and higher education programs experiencing progressively lower hazards of malaria recurrence than infants whose mothers had no formal education. This aligns with the literature indicating that maternal education positively influences health-seeking behaviors, proper use of preventive measures, and timely treatment, thereby reducing infant malaria risk [19]. Older maternal age (30–45 years) was also protective against infant malaria recurrence compared with adolescence, which is consistent with evidence that repeated exposure to malaria leads to partial immunity and improved pregnancy outcomes, which can indirectly lower the risk of malaria in infants [29, 30]. However, infants born to multigravida mothers (≥ 3 pregnancies) had a significantly greater hazard of recurrent malaria than those born to primi-gravidae did, a pattern that differs from classical observations that primi-gravidae are at the highest risk of malaria during pregnancy [31, 32]. This counter-intuitive association may reflect residual confounding by unmeasured factors such as maternal or household exposure patterns, complex infant and maternal immunology, or differential health-seeking behavior among higher gravidity women in this high transmission setting, and thus warrants further investigation.

Geographical access to healthcare services also emerged as an important determinant of recurrent malaria episodes in infants. Compared to those living within 1 km, infants residing within 3–5 km from the nearest health facility had a 40% greater hazard of recurrence, whereas infants residing within 1–2 km and those residing more than 6 km from the closest health facility also had elevated although not statistically significant hazard of malaria recurrence. These findings align with broader literature showing that greater distance to health facilities is associated with delayed treatment, lower uptake of preventive services, and worse malaria outcomes in children [33, 34]. In high transmission rural settings such as Busia, strengthening community based case management, improving transport and referral networks, and ensuring the reliable availability of diagnostics and anti-malarials at peripheral facilities and the community health worker level may be critical for reducing recurrent malaria in infancy.

Advanced modelling using the conditional frailty model was instrumental in appropriately capturing the correct recurrent nature of malaria episodes in this study. The data showed strong evidence for event dependence with very limited statistically non-significant residual between-infant heterogeneity. This finding suggests that recurrent malaria hazard in this infant population was associated with the history of prior episodes, which likely reflects biological immune dynamics or exposure patterns. The conditional frailty model provides a flexible framework for accounting for event dependence and within-infant correlation producing greater accuracy and interpretability over models that ignore such dependencies or heterogeneity, hence offering valuable guidance for future malaria research employing recurrent event methodologies. These methodological insights reinforce concerns about the use of simpler approaches such as time-to-first-event Cox models, Poisson regression or negative binomial regression for recurrent malaria data, which either discard information on later episodes or ignore within-subject correlation and event ordering [35, 36]. Such approaches may yield less efficient or potentially biased estimates in settings with strong within-subject correlation, especially in high transmission settings where multiple episodes per child are common [2, 37]. The conditional frailty model used in this study exploited all available recurrent event data, accounted for event dependence, and provided interpretable hazard ratios for maternal, infant and household factors. Future malaria cohort analyses should routinely consider this model, especially when the biological plausibility of event dependence is strong and the underlying correlation structure is uncertain.

The strengths of this study included the following; (i) use of high quality secondary data from a rigorously conducted, double-blind randomized phase III trial, ensuring that the maternal IPTp-DP and IPTp-SP groups were comparable with respect to baseline maternal and infant characteristics, and (ii) use of the conditional frailty model, a statistical method more suitably tailored to recurrent event data.

Important limitations in this study should be acknowledged; most critically, although the original PROMOTE trial was adequately powered for its primary maternal outcomes, this secondary infant recurrent-event analysis was underpowered to detect modest differences in recurrent malaria risk between IPTp regimens, increasing the risk of type II error. Consequently, the non-significant findings regarding IPTp-DP versus IPTp-SP should be interpreted cautiously. Second, ascertainment of infant malaria episodes relied primarily on facility‑based surveillance, with diagnoses made when caregivers brought infants to the study clinic for routine scheduled visits or febrile illness. Although missed routine visits triggered home tracing and encouragement to attend the clinic, all episodes that resolved without care‑seeking could not be captured. Consequently, the observed association between greater distance to the health facility and higher hazard of recurrent malaria may partly reflect differential case detection rather than only true differences in malaria incidence, and some degree of outcome ascertainment bias cannot be excluded. Third, this was a secondary analysis of trial data and was therefore limited to variables available in the original PROMOTE dataset. Several potentially important factors including maternal adherence to IPTp dosing, detailed household vector control practices, infant breastfeeding patterns, maternal and infant immunological markers, and time-varying climatic exposures such as rainfall and temperature were unavailable. Absence of these variables may have resulted in residual confounding and may have influenced the observed associations between maternal, household, and infant factors and recurrent malaria risk. Finally, because the study was conducted in a single high-transmission setting in eastern Uganda, findings may not be generalizable to lower-transmission settings, urban populations, or regions with different malaria epidemiology, health system structures, or antimalarial resistance profiles.

Conclusion

Compared with infants born to mothers who received IPTp-SP during pregnancy, there was only a slight reduction in the hazard of recurrent malaria episodes among infants born to mothers who received IPTp-DP, but this difference was not statistically significant. Maternal education, maternal age, gravidity and distance to health facilities were significantly associated with recurrent malaria episodes in infants, underscoring the importance of sociodemographic and health system factors alongside chemo preventive regimens. The conditional frailty model was useful for characterizing within-infant correlations in recurrent malaria episodes, illustrating presence of event dependence in this study rather than residual between-infant heterogeneity. These findings suggest the importance of integrated malaria control approaches that combine effective antenatal chemoprevention with improved access to care and maternal health education as well as appropriate statistical handling of recurrent-event data in future research.
