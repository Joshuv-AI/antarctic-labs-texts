Damping behavior of adaptable shoe under torsional loading at varying angular velocities: replicating the effects on cutting maneuvers
Authors: Md Samsul Arefin, Chien-Ju Lin, Hsiao-Feng Chieh, Kai-Nan An, Ying-Chun Huang, Fong-Chin Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Damping is a vital mechanical feature for a dynamic system like shoe that is a specialized sports equipment designed to protect and enhance human movement performance. This study aimed to investigate the damping characteristics of adaptable shoe configurations to simulate the real-world cutting effects. To achieve this, repetitive cyclic torsional loading tests were conducted at different angular velocities (25°/s, 50°/s, 75°/s, 100°/s, 125°/s, and 150°/s) with a torsion angle range of 0–30°. Experimental conditions were: (a) control shoe (CS), which are adaptable air cushion shoe, (b) midpart adapted shoe (MAS), and (c) forepart adapted shoe (FAS), both altered in sole construction with adjustable elastomeric spacers. A torsion testing machine with a specially designed fixture system held the test shoes. Then, the shoes underwent repetitive torsional loading and unloading with angular displacements from 0° to 30° to simulate inversion motion. Results revealed an inverse correlation between damping coefficient (DCoeff) and angular velocities. Notably, at the highest angular velocity 150⁰/s, all shoe conditions demonstrated the lowest DCoeff, indicating that shoes retained most of their energy during twisting motion, resulting in relatively low energy dissipation. This might result in higher twisting forces on foot-shoe system and ankle, might impact on ankle stability. Similarly, low mechanical damping at higher velocity in the shoe forepart may reduce energy dissipation. This could exert greater force on the metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint of the forefoot, potentially compromising its stability. Study findings may provide preliminary insights into the damping behavior of shoes at increasing angular velocities to assist in the development of athletic footwear for sports performance, and further studies are needed optimized damping.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41715-9.

Introduction

Damping is a universal phenomenon found in various materials e.g., solid, liquid, or gas, and it involves the loss of mechanical energy when materials undergo time-dependent changes in stress or strain, usually converting this energy into heat1. The overall damping behavior is influenced by structures made from different components1. Damping is crucial when conducting simulations to predict or optimization of the system’s dynamic behavior1. The human foot experiences recurrent impact forces due to increased speeds and the extent of loading during walking and running2. The foot-shoe system is a similar mechanical system3 that undergoes repetitive mechanical loading during everyday walking, running, turning and cutting in sports. The shoe materials exhibit viscoelastic behavior and the damping of shoe material need to considered4. Hence, damping of shoe is important as a dynamic system to optimize. The damping behavior was evaluated as the damping coefficient (DCoeff) that characterized as the energy dissipation inside a mechanical system and is commonly quantified under repetitive cyclic loading conditions5.

Footwear design represents a complex area in sports biomechanics and ergonomics which effects in gait and athletic performance6. A traditional athletic shoe consists of three basic and important parts including upper, midsole and outsole7. The primary function of the shoe midsole is to deliver cushioning to the shoe7. Generally, polyurethane (PU) and ethylene vinyl acetate (EVA) were used as midsoles, but due to technological development, gas, gel and water also used to increase cushioning of foams7. And these midsole materials have viscoelastic properties, which offer a spring-like features along with capabilities of motion damping and heat dispersion8. Material damping arises from the energy absorbed by the material in each loading cycle and because the material properties of shoe midsole are load-rate dependent, the loading should be at a rate as near to realistic9. Earlier studies revealed about the shoe soles that when a low-damping material is positioned beneath the foot, it does not effectively diminish the impact force. Conversely, when a higher-damping material is placed under the foot, it significantly reduces the impact peak10,11. The shoe’s damping mechanism absorbs a portion of this energy, and it can be quantified by analyzing the pattern of the load-deformation curve during cyclic loading9. Turning or cutting in sports is a maneuver that involves a decrease and followed by an increase in velocity to change the velocity12. The change of direction in running requires the application of additional force to the ground to redirect the initial momentum of straight running into a new direction13,14. Hence, shoes as well as foot undergo additional twisting or torsional forces during cutting movements. To optimize the design of sports shoe, evaluation of torsional mechanical properties of shoe materials and structure is important due to its effect on cutting movements in sports during frequent directional changes15.

The torsional stiffness and DCoeff of materials and structures are important mechanical characteristics in analyzing dynamic systems. Although numerous shoe studies focused on biomechanical evaluation16–22 no studies have yet investigated the damping behavior of shoes under cyclic torsional loading. Air cushion shoes, which feature adjustable sole construction with air-filled compartments replacing the regular midsole, exhibit a distinct design. Due to increasing usage and potential benefits of these shoes in athletic activities, an investigation into their dynamic mechanical characteristics, such as damping, is warranted. Evidence revealed that the angular velocities of the rear foot running at 4 m/s are about 287–860⁰/s21. A study evaluated the energy cost of turning during running investigated different running velocities from 0.83 m/s to 2.22 m/s23. Another study21 found, the angular velocities at 0.83 m/s to 2.22 m/s might be equivalent to 60⁰/s to 160⁰/s. Therefore, this study conducted the torsion test for a range of angular velocities such as 25⁰/s, 50⁰/s, 75⁰/s, 100⁰/s, 125⁰/s, and 150⁰/s in the frontal plane of movement. Because of the paucity of studies, a necessity exists to investigate how torsional loading influences the material mechanics of adaptable sole constructions across a range of angular velocities. Hence, the aim of this research was to characterize the damping behavior of an adaptable shoe construction under cyclic torsional loading with a range of increasing angular velocities and simulate their impact on real-life cutting maneuvers. It was hypothesized that the damping coefficients of adaptable shoes under repetitive cyclic torsional loading may decrease with higher range of angular velocities.

Materials and methods

Experimental conditions

This study investigated experimental conditions (Fig. 1) including (a) control shoes (CS), that is air cushion shoe (Dr Air Inc, Taiwan), (b) midpart-adapted shoe (MAS), and (c) forepart-adapted shoe (FAS), where MAS and FAS were adjusted by adding elastomeric and partially flexible vertical spacers in the cavities between the compartments in the sole construction. Test shoes feature a unique sole design that incorporates air compartments and hollow cavities within the sole structure. Air is introduced into the compartments via an air valve to work as cushioning material. Please see the shoe conditions in the article15 for further details.

Experimental set-up and test procedures

The footwear underwent mechanical testing using a torsion testing machine (Yang Yi Technology, Taiwan) in accordance with EN 409 standard. A specially designed fixation system was utilized to hold the shoe within the torsion testing machine (Fig. 2). Upper part of the fixture was used to grip the shoe back part, while the lower part of the fixture was used to secure the shoe fore part. Upper part of the fixture was fixed, while the lower part was capable of rotation about the axis formed between the shoe’s forepart and back part. The shoe’s forepart was subjected to alternating clockwise and counterclockwise rotations through the rotary head of the machine, which induced movement against the stationary rear part. These movements are stated as inversion and eversion movement, respectively, and replicate the foot–shoe inversion/eversion motion in the frontal plane of movement based on previous studies24,25. The experimental arrangement and test machine employed in this investigation were identical to those illustrated in the article15 and can be read for further details.

During the experiment, specific test parameters were input into the software to facilitate customized tests. These parameters included a range of angular velocities (25°/s, 50°/s, 75°/s, 100°/s, 125°/s, and 150°/s), a sampling rate of 500 Hz15,26, an inversion angle ranging from 0 to 30°, and fifteen of complete test cycles. For each shoe condition, the test consists of 15 repeated loading and unloading cycles. Torsional moments, torsion angles, timestamps, and step cycles data were collected in this experiment for subsequent data analysis. The damping coefficients were computed from the rates of change of torsional moments and angular velocities. The time stamps indicated the elapsed time for changes in angular distance (referred to as torsion angles), and the loading and unloading cycles were identified by the number of step cycles during torsional movements.

Data analysis

The raw data processing was accomplished with custom-written MATLAB scripts (R2022a, The MathWorks Inc, USA). Prior studies15,29 conducted torsion tests where data were filtered with cut-off frequencies between 3 and 5 Hz to reduce noise. In this study, a fourth-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a 3 Hz cutoff frequency was applied to the torsion test data. This choice followed pilot tests with a range of cut-off frequencies that preserved signal features though diminished the noise. Test for each shoe condition consists of fifteen loading and unloading cycles of data. The first five cycles of testing data out of fifteen cycles were excluded from analysis for each footwear condition30, and the DCoeff was computed from the last ten test cycles (6th – 15th consecutive cycles) for inversion torsional movements. The DCoeff were calculated from the loading curve of torsional moments versus angular velocities for angular displacements ranging from 15–30°. To facilitate data reduction and analysis, the five curve points, corresponding to torsional moments, were extracted at the torsional angles (15°, 19°, 23°, 27°, and 30°) to represent equal intervals along the loading curve for data reduction. The process was repeated for 10 test cycles for each angular velocity tested (25°/s, 50°/s, 75°/s, 100°/s, 125°/s, and 150°/s). Next, a second-order polynomial curve fitting (Fig. 3) was performed, and the DCoeff was determined through best-fit analysis following equation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(4)$$\end{document} described in the formulation section.

Results and discussion

This study examined the damping characteristics under cyclic torsional loading for three shoe conditions: CS, MAS, and FAS, with a range of angular velocities of 25°/s, 50°/s, 75°/s, 100°/s, 125°/s, and 150⁰/s. Results indicated in Fig. 4; Table 1 that the DCoeff decreased with increasing angular velocities for all shoe conditions. Additionally, the results revealed reduced DCoeff for the MAS and FAS conditions in compare with CS condition, with the exception of the MAS condition above 125°/s. Notably, the FAS exhibited the lowest the DCoeff across all shoe conditions and angular velocities. Data presented in Table 1 is also visualized in Fig. 4 to highlight trends more clearly.

Damping is an important characteristic that needs to be considered while investigating dynamic systems. This study applied relevant torsional speeds (angular velocity) to get the impact of cutting movement that often happens in real-life sports9. The material’s DCoeff might differ for various structures and materials. For higher velocities, a low DCoeff is advantageous31 and shoes with minimal or no damping might be beneficial for reducing the occurrence of potentially detrimental vibrations in soft tissues according to a previous study32. This study agrees with the previous findings for all shoe conditions that showed the lowest DCoeff with the highest angular velocity for this inversion torsional loading (Fig. 4). Hence, this study revealed a reciprocal relationship between the DCoeff and the angular velocities, which shows the DCoeff decrease with increasing angular velocities for all shoe conditions undergoing torsional loading (Fig. 4). These findings were also confirmed by an earlier study showing that the DCoeff of a urethane foam named CONFORM foam decreases with increasing speed compared to near-constant rubber damping31. Another study observed identical findings that reported that ball bearing’s DCoeff were reduced with rising angular speeds33. Differences in DCoeff were revealed at angular velocities when comparing DCoeff across three shoe conditions (Fig. 4). The data results for the damping versus angular velocity curves are plotted for the range of angular speeds from 25 to 150⁰/s. Because below 25⁰/s speed, the DCoeff seem to be high, whereas above 150⁰/s, they might be impractically low. Moreover, this study was focused on evaluating the damping behaviors of adjustable shoes with a higher torsional speed of 150⁰/s because this torsional speed might evaluate and replicate the impact of this speed on real-life cutting speed during sports maneuvers, especially during sidestep cutting.

Study results also found that DCoeff at the highest angular velocity were the lowest for all shoe conditions. Earlier study findings corroborated these results, explaining that typical engineering structure’s DCoeff is near about 0.01. During treadmill running, when the athlete was running at twice the pace of the run/walk transition, the DCoeff of the entire leg and shoe system was approximately 0.134. This low DCoeff means that the shoe retains most of its energy during the twisting motion and experiences relatively low energy dissipation. The torsional movement might exert significant forces on the foot-shoe system and the ankle joint during cutting movements. The low damping in the shoe might contribute to less energy dissipation, leading to higher forces being transmitted to the foot-shoe system or at the ankle. This might affect ankle stability35, especially if the shoe does not adequately support the ankle during the cutting motion. Conversely, the DCoeff at the lower torsional speed are higher; hence, energy dissipation will be higher, leading to lower force transmission to the foot-shoe system or the ankle, hence, might not affect ankle stability. Nevertheless, according to previous studies found that a shoe sole with a lower-damping material could not effectively reduce impact force, whereas a higher-damping material could significantly reduce it10,11. However, the low DCoeff might impact the durability of the shoe’s materials, as cyclic torsional loading with limited energy dissipation can lead to material fatigue. According to a study investigating the impact dynamics of a basic mass-spring-damper system when subjected to an impulse force, damping values falling within the range of 0.2 to 0.5 can decrease the peak response by as much as 70%36. Based on the current study results and previous studies, it might be summarized that the DCoeff of shoes might be considered as an important factor that might control the effect of twisting force on the foot-shoe system to control ankle stability. Therefore, optimization of damping of shoe materials is important and need further studies.

Regarding the forepart adapted shoe condition, it is important to note that the forefoot area of footwear has received limited attention in previous research on sports performance; specifically, no study has focused on forefoot shoe damping. However, few relevant shoe studies have focused on the forepart features of shoes. The forepart modification of the shoe in this study covered the metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint area, which is crucial for forefoot and overall foot movement. When wearing shoes, the foot and footwear interact to regulate the mechanical behavior of the first MTP joint37. This study’s results revealed that the damping coefficient for the FAS was the least across all test conditions at 150⁰/s angular velocity, and that the damping coefficients showed an inverse relationship with angular velocity. Hence, the low damping at the shoe forepart may result in reduced energy dissipation, resulting in greater force transmission to the forefoot, especially at the MTP joint, which may compromise the MTP joint stability if the foot cannot neutralize the force. A relevant study investigating the addition of elastic material in the forefoot region of shoes revealed that increased bending elasticity reduces the dissipation of applied forces during gait and running, thereby altering movement performance38. The same study also noted that the shoe forepart is not only linked to performance but is associated with various sports injuries38. In comparison, another study found that slicing or cutting material in the shoe’s forefoot region altered movement of the MTP joint39. The MTP joint acted as a mechanical damper across all tested velocities, with its damping behavior increasing as the velocity augmented40. However, there are no studies directly exploring shoe forepart damping for comparison. Based on the above findings and the results of this study, it is possible that shoe forepart damping may influence the forefoot region and, consequently, foot movement. Further investigation is needed to comprehensively understand the effect of forepart damping at various torsional velocities, which might help in sports footwear design.

It is important to note some limitations of this study. The torsion test in this study was performed without inserting a shoe last or foot model into the tested shoes. This study focused on shoe samples that differ from traditional shoes, as their soles were replaced with air-cushion chambers. In addition, there are various sports shoes that differ in their materials and constructions. As a result, the findings of this study may not be generalized to all shoe designs. Therefore, further studies are warranted to examine other shoe constructions made from different materials, providing more insights to enhance athletic shoe design and improve performance, and reduce injury risk.

Conclusions

This study revealed an inverse correlation between the damping coefficients and angular velocities for all shoe conditions. Importantly, all shoe conditions exhibited the lowest damping coefficients at the highest angular velocity. This suggests that shoes retained most of their energy during the twisting motion, leading to relatively low energy dissipation. This might result in higher twisting forces on the foot-shoe system and ankle, which might affect ankle stability. The damping coefficient of the forepart-adapted shoe was the least among the shoe conditions. Low damping of the shoe forepart might cause less energy dissipation, which might exert greater force on the forefoot, particularly on the metatarsophalangeal joint. This might affect the stability of the metatarsophalangeal joint, which is important for foot movement. This study may offer preliminary insights into shoe damping behavior to advance sports footwear design for enhanced sports performance, and more studies are essential to optimize damping behavior.
