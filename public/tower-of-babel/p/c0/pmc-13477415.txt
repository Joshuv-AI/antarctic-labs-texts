Giant Pelvic Hydatid Cyst Causing Bilateral Hydroureteronephrosis: A Case Report and Review of Literature on an Uncommon Cause of Progressive Abdominal Distension and Obstructive Uropathy
Authors: Manish Kumar, Abhishek Chhabra, Varun Gowda, Abhay S Gaur, Suresh K Goyal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydatid disease of the pelvis is a rare form of echinococcosis infection and seldom occurs in bilateral ureteric obstruction resulting in hydroureteronephrosis. A male patient in his 50s presented with a history of progressive abdominal distension and pain since two months. The ultrasonography revealed a large multiloculated cystic lesion in the pelvis, resulting in the compression of bilateral ureters and thus leading to bilateral hydroureteronephrosis. The case presents a rare yet clinically relevant manifestation of hydatid disease of the pelvis and points to the necessity to consider hydatid cyst along with the list of differentiating cysts of the pelvis causing obstructive uropathy.

Introduction

Hydatid disease results from the parasitic infection transmitted by Echinococcus granulosus, which usually affects the liver and lungs [1-3]. Primary pelvic hydatid disease is very rare, as they constitute not more than 2% of all reported cases [4]. Pelvic hydatid cysts usually manifest late because of the delayed mass effect on the surrounding organs [4]. The extrinsic compression of the urinary bladder or ureters usually causes urological symptoms, which rarely result in bilateral hydroureteronephrosis and functional impairment of the kidneys [5,6]. Pelvic hydatid disease often shows nonspecific signs and symptoms, leading to the incorrect diagnosis of the disease as mesenteric, ovarian, or retroperitoneal cystic pathology due to its rarity [4,7]. Primary pelvic hydatid disease is very uncommon and is believed to spread by hematogenous or lymphatic dissemination or as a secondary event after subclinical rupture of a primary hepatic cyst [8]. We present a rare case of a giant pelvic hydatid cyst causing bilateral ureteric obstruction and progressive abdominal distension.

Case presentation

A male patient in his 50s presented with complaints of progressive abdominal distension and dull aching abdominal pain for two months. He reported no fever, hematuria, lower urinary tract symptom (LUTS) history, bowel disturbances, or weight loss. There was no prior history of hydatid disease or abdominal surgery.

On examination, the abdomen was distended with a palpable, non-tender, cystic mass arising from the pelvis and extending into the lower abdomen (Figure 1). There was no evidence of overall renal impairment with a serum creatinine level of 1.1 mg/dL and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in the normal range on routine laboratory investigations. All other baseline evaluations were normal.

Clinical photograph of the patient showing marked abdominal distension due to a large pelvic mass extending into the lower abdomen: (a) lateral view and (b) anterior view.

Ultrasonography revealed a large multiloculated cystic pelvic lesion with multiple septa and daughter cysts, suggestive of hydatid disease (Figure 2). Both kidneys showed dilatation of the pelvicalyceal systems and ureters, consistent with bilateral hydroureteronephrosis. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) demonstrated a giant, well-defined, multiloculated cystic mass occupying the pelvis, displacing bowel loops and compressing both distal ureters, resulting in bilateral hydroureteronephrosis (Figure 3). The imaging characteristics were highly suggestive of a hydatid cyst.

The patient underwent an exploratory laparotomy. Laparoscopic intervention was avoided because of the large size of the cyst, its compressive effect on the bilateral ureters, and the critical requirement for maintaining a clear operative field, avoiding any intraperitoneal spill. Once the peritoneal cavity was entered, the bowel loops and peritoneum around the cavity were carefully isolated and packed with surgical mops to avoid any leakage or seeding into the cavity. The giant pelvic cyst was entered initially by controlled aspiration and followed immediately by instillation of povidone-iodine as a scolicidal agent. After allowing for an appropriate contact time, open surgical deroofing was performed. A systematic evacuation of multiple daughter cysts and germinal membranes was performed (Figure 4). After full evacuation and adequate irrigation, the residual cyst cavity was managed with a closed suction drain.

Intra-operative photograph showing multiple hydatid daughter cysts and germinal membranes evacuated following open deroofing of the pelvic hydatid cyst, confirming the diagnosis of hydatid disease.

Discussion

Liver (50%-70%) and lungs (20%-30%) are the most common sites of hydatid disease [2,3]. Pelvic localization is rare and usually secondary to intraperitoneal dissemination following rupture of a primary hepatic or splenic cyst [4]. Primary pelvic hydatid disease is exceptionally uncommon and is believed to occur via hematogenous or lymphatic spread [4]. CT scan remains the gold standard investigation as it assists in defining the extent, anatomy, compression effect and resultant hydronephrosis [4,6,8]. Ureteric compression leading to unilateral or bilateral hydroureteronephrosis is distinctly rare, with only isolated case reports described in the literature (Table 1).

Imaging plays a vital part in diagnosis. Usually, ultrasonography can reveal a clear cystic or many daughter cysts with internal septations. CECT remains the gold standard investigation as it assists in defining the extent, anatomy, compression effect and resultant hydronephrosis [5,10,11].

The major mode of treatment is surgical excision while taking utmost care to prevent the spillage of the cysts [1,8,9]. It is also recommended to prescribe albendazole before and after surgery to reduce the recurrence. Temporary urinary drainage interventions like double J (DJ) stenting or percutaneous nephrostomy might be needed in selected cases.

Conclusions

The postoperative period was uneventful, with immediate improvement in abdominal distension and pain. The patient was started on adjunctive albendazole therapy. Over a follow-up period of one year, the patient remained completely asymptomatic, and follow-up imaging revealed a complete resolution of hydroureteronephrosis with no clinical or radiological evidence of disease recurrence.

When evaluating the differential diagnosis of any pelvic cystic mass with multiple septations, particularly in endemic areas, pelvic hydatid cyst must be taken into consideration. Though very uncommon, it may cause severe urological complications such as bilateral hydroureteronephrosis. Early diagnosis and proper planning of surgery are crucial to avoid irreversible renal damage. Surgical deroofing of daughter cysts and adjunctive albendazole therapy in our case led to tremendous symptomatic improvement and relief from abdominal distention.
