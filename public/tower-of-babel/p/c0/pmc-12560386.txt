Knowledge, barriers, attitude, perception, and ability of public and private institution graduating pharmacy students toward deprescribing and polypharmacy: a cross-sectional survey study in Northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Muluken Adela Alemu, Yared Andargie Ferede, Abraham Nigussie Assefa, Eshetie Melese Birru, Assefa Kebad Mengesha, Taklo Simeneh Yazie, Getaye Tessema Desta, Tesfagegn Gobezie Yiblet, Tesfaye Yimer Tadesse, Woretaw Sisay Zewudu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolypharmacy is a simultaneous use of multiple drugs (five or more drugs for general polypharmacy and more than two drugs for psychotropic polypharmacy). If unmanaged, it may lead to an increased incidence of problems like adverse drug reactions which in turn resulted initiation of deprescribing as a solution. For effective implementation of deprescribing, equipping pharmacy students with adequate knowledge and training through a well-structured curriculum is important. Evaluating both the attitudes and perceptions of students for polypharmacy and deprescribing is crucial to modify the curriculum and shape educational strategies. Therefore, the objective of this study was to assess the attitudes and perceptions of graduating pharmacy students toward polypharmacy and deprescribing in Colleges and Universities in northwest Ethiopia.MethodA cross-sectional survey study was conducted among graduating degree pharmacy students from licensed governmental and non-governmental higher institutions from 15 May to 30 November 2024 using both online and paper based structured questionnaires. Data werecollected from 205 graduating students. The collected data were analyzed using SPSS version 24 using ANOVA, Post Hoc, and t-tests. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered significant.ResultThe majority (58%) of the participants were not familiar about deprescribing while 85.9% heard about the term polypharmacy. Less than half of participants (42.9%) identified the definition of deprescribing, while 45.4% and 18.5% were able to identify the minimum number of drugs to declare general polypharmacy and psychotropic polypharmacy respectively. About 17.1% of students reported they got curriculum based education on deprescribing while 64.4% on polypharmacy. Near to three-fourth (74.2%) and 75.2% of the students reported that they got inadequate education on deprescribing and polypharmacy respectively. Students from government institutions reported that their school curriculum prepared them to deprescribe medications better as compared with students from private institutions (2.86 ± 0.12 vs. 1.15 ± 0.03, p < 0.001). Similarly students from government institutions declared they have better ability to deprescribe medications than private institution students (p < 0.001). Furthermore, government institution students have better attitude and confidence (p < 0.001) to deprescribe medications as compared to private institution students. The common barriers for deprescribing are related to problems in the curriculum, inadequate preparation of graduating pharmacy students, and patients’ willingness.ConclusionThis study highlighted inadequate knowledge and practice of students on deprescribing and polypharmacy and a need for curriculum reform to equip students with appropriate knowledge and skill about deprescribing and polypharmacy in Ethiopian pharmacy schools. The reform should also include securing appropriate content in the curriculum, curriculum standardization across institutions, and fostering active learning strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08106-6.Keywords: Deprescribing, Polypharmacy, Knowledge, Attitude

Introduction

Polypharmacy is the use of several medications for the treatment of different diseases, which is common among geriatric patients and patients with chronic diseases [1]. It is the use of five or more drugs by one person simultaneously. Even though there may be many conditions where polypharmacy is obligatory, there are often unintended and harmful medications included in polypharmacy treatment regimen. Globally, it is a smoldering issue due to its association with different health problems [1–3]. Owing to the increased incidence of chronic diseases, it has become a common problem, specifically in poor countries where resources are scarce in health care settings. In Ethiopia, increased prevalence of chronic diseases adds much to the increased occurrence of polypharmacy [4]. Polypharmacy may lead to an increased incidence of problems like adverse drug reactions, non-adherence, and increased healthcare costs [1]. These problems prompted clinicians to initiating deprescribing so as to reduce patient harm and improve outcomes.

Deprescribing is the process of reducing the dose or discontinuing medications for optimum patient outcome [5]. It is “a systematic process of identifying and discontinuing medications in instances in which existing potential harms outweigh potential benefits within the context of an individual patent’s care goals, the current level of functioning, life expectancy, values, and preferences” [5, 6]. Deprescribing helps to reduce medication burden, promote medication safety, and enhance quality of life [7]. It is a collaborative work requiring different health professionals to work in concert. It requires the collaborative involvement of pharmacists and physicians to make certain that prescribed drugs are periodically evaluated and then adjusted to the continuously evolving demands of the patient. As an expert in pharmacotherapy, pharmacists are expected to lead deprescribing processes by identification of drug therapy problems and equipping patients with appropriate knowledge [8].

Understanding attitudes and perceptions of graduating pharmacy students toward polypharmacy and deprescribing is essential, as they are the future workforce of pharmacy sector of the health system. Knowledge of students’ perspectives on polypharmacy and deprescribing provides clues on their ability of deprescribing leading to improvement of pharmaceutical care. Moreover, it shows the potential discrepancies of pharmacy curricula and pharmacy education that needs to be dealt with. Prior studies pointed out that, pharmacy students appreciated the probable disadvantages of polypharmacy and the advantages of deprescribing. But they pointed out that they feel insufficient to implement deprescribing decisions owing to inadequate clinical attachment and absence of formal education on this concept, negative patient anticipations and scarcity of resources [9, 10].

In a country Ethiopia, where financial resources are scarce, deprescribing adds value by reducing treatment cost, optimizing treatment outcomes, reducing mortality, preventing severe medication reactions, shortening hospital stays and readmissions [8, 11]. However, there is limited information on the attitudes, perceptions, and readiness to practice among Ethiopian pharmacy graduating students about polypharmacy and deprescribing. Owing to increasing acceptance and role of pharmacy professionals in medication management, evaluating and comparing students’ attitudes and perceptions on such issues ensures whether pharmacy graduates are sufficiently equipped to take part in pharmaceutical care including deprescribing. This cross-sectional study is focused on assessing and comparing attitudes and perceptions of Ethiopian graduating pharmacy students toward polypharmacy and deprescribing. The results of this survey could provide important information to structure the future aspects of pharmacy practices in the health system.

Methods and materials

Study design, period, and participants

A cross-sectional survey study was conducted among bachelor degree graduating pharmacy students from public and private higher education institutions from 15 May to 30 November, 2024. Both online and paper based structured questionnaire were used to collect the data. Sufficient paper copies were printed and provided to some of the study participants while the study link leading to online survey instrument was sent to the email addresses of each of many of the study participants through their representatives. Survey reports which were incomplete were excluded from the study. Study participants were asked for their consent of participation for the study. To keep the data confidential, personal identifiers were excluded from the questionnaire.

Questionnaire development

Both paper and web based structured self-administered survey instrument (Google forms) was adapted from the previously published articles. Once developed, the survey instrument was reevaluated for its readability and validity before pilot testing. Pilot testing was then conducted on twenty participants to ensure the instrument’s clarity, acceptability, relevancy, reliability, and validity. The participants who were involved in the pretest were excluded from the main study. The final study instrument was then distributed to each of the participants. The survey instrument contains three sections; (1) socio-demographic information of participants (2) questions asking students’ knowledge, perceptions, abilities, barriers, attitudes, and confidence of deprescribing, and (3) questions on knowledge and attitudes of students toward polypharmacy.

Sample size

This survey study was conducted among all graduating students of the participating institutions. A total of 205 graduating students, 96 from government institutions and 109 from private institutions were participated.

Inclusion criteria

All students enrolled in the final year of the pharmacy program who have completed all clinical internship/rotation components of their education were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Students who have not completed their clinical internships and who did not consent to participate were excluded from the study.

Data analysis

Responses from the survey instrument were fed into Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 24 and analyzed using ANOVA, Post Hoc, and t-tests. Frequencies and percents were reported as a part of descriptive statistics.

Knowledge of polypharmacy

Regarding educational exposure, multiple (64.4%) students reported that they had received curriculum-based education on polypharmacy. When asked about the inclusion of polypharmacy into the curriculum, a significant proportion (73.2%) of respondents preferred it to be incorporated as a required course, while very few (2, 1%) favored it as both elective and required course (Table 12).

Over half (51.7%) of the respondents need polypharmacy to be taught through lecturing, while 36.1% preferred patient-centered case-based learning or clinical attachments.

Even though the majority of respondents reported they received curriculum-based education about polypharmacy, a larger proportion of students reported the education as very inadequate (45.9%) and 29.3% stated they get an inadequate education.

Discussion

This study showed the gaps of graduating pharmacy students’ knowledge, attitude, confidence, ability and barriers on deprescribing and polypharmacy. The findings indicate that pharmacy education in the country - especially in private institutions - requires urgent amendments to enhance necessary competencies of future pharmacists graduating in these institutions.

Although greater than half (62.9%) of the students had professional exposure in hospital pharmacy, community pharmacy or both settings, lower than half (42.9%) could correctly identified the definition of deprescribing, and only very few (17.1%) reported that they got deprescribing education as part of the curriculum. These results are in line with other published works, which shows that deprescribing gets less emphasized in pharmacy curricula; particularly the problem is more in developing countries [12, 13]. Near to half of participants (44.9%) reported they get a “very inadequate” education on deprescribing which shows a curricular deficiency.

The study showed that the most preferred method of instruction for deprescribing was interactive lecturing (60%) followed by patient-centered case problems or attachments (20.5%). These inclinations indicate the need for implementing experiential, integrated, and interprofessional learning to build appropriate competences and confidences in deprescribing [5]. Providing deprescribing education using more practical and interactive learning strategies such as clinical attachments, simulations, interdisciplinary discussions can improve not only the knowledge but also the communication skill and decision making skill of students [14].

Most of graduating students perceived that pharmacists are professionals in charge of conducting deprescribing. The majority (74.6%) strongly agreed that pharmacists are experts in identification of medications that are unnecessary or harmful, and 93.7% believed that deprescribing should be implemented in concert with physicians, patients, and other health professionals, proving its interdisciplinary nature. These results align with researches conducted in older adults with multimorbidity and polypharmacy [15, 16]. Such perceptions are important to involve pharmacists’ clinical activities and enhance the growing clinical role of pharmacists in implementing deprescribing.

Nonetheless, an alarming discrepancy was reported between awareness of the students on their professional responsibilities and the education their institutions provided to address these. Only very few (1.5%) of graduating students strongly agreed that their school’s curriculum had sufficiently prepared them to implement deprescribing, while greater than half (53.7%) strongly disagreed. These results have similar patterns with researches from Australia and Canada, showing pharmacy graduates insufficiencies in performing deprescribing interventions [17, 18]. Although students have motivation to deprescribe medications, their school did not equip appropriate and necessary skills which may result significant implications in their future career.

This condition was complicated by additional institutional and systemic barriers. The most common problems to implement deprescribing included resistance from other healthcare professionals, limited clinical knowledge, understaffed health facilities, and ambiguous legal frameworks. These problems are more in Low- and Middle-Income Country (LMIC) settings where pharmaceutical care is not fully recognized [19]. To address these challenges appropriate guidelines indicating the scope of clinical practice and workforce should be needed.

This study revealed that graduating students from governmental institutions reported better preparedness, knowledge, attitudes, and confidence in deprescribing than students from private institutions. For example, students from government institutions scored significantly higher knowledge on tapering medications and recommending deprescribing for elderly patients (mean = 3.59 vs. 1.57, p < 0.001). They also have better ability in recommending deprescribing to physicians (mean = 3.43–3.63) as compared to students from private institutions (mean = 2.21).

These differences may show variations in quality of institutions, curriculum implementation, and access to clinical training environments. Similar studies reported equivalent differences in other countries. A report from UK reported that students exposed to geriatric care and professional training during their stay in academic institutions felt more confident while students without such experiences reported lower confidence [20].

Furthermore, the study revealed the majority (85.9%) of students had heard of polypharmacy while less than half (45.4%) could accurately define it. On the other hand, only 18.5% of students could identify the number of drugs to declare for psychotropic polypharmacy.

Even though 35% of respondents stated that they got curriculum-based deprescribing education, only 13% considered this training was adequate. This gap may be because of lack of active learning strategies, inappropriate content, and lack of clinical context. The report indicated that 73.2% of students call for polypharmacy to be given as a required coursework. Their preference for experiential and case-based methods is in line with other reports that emphasize applied learning in optimization of medications [21, 22].

Despite the reported gaps, many participants believed polypharmacy is an important issue in patient care. The majority (84.4%) agreed that polypharmacy may increase the risk of adverse drug reaction, drug interaction, cost, and decreased patient compliance. This indicates that students are aware of the real implications of polypharmacy, especially in aging populations.

Conclusion

This study highlighted inadequate knowledge of graduating pharmacy students on deprescribing and polypharmacy and an urgent need for curriculum reform to equip students with appropriate knowledge and skill about deprescribing and polypharmacy in Ethiopian pharmacy schools. The reform should also include securing appropriate deprescribing content in the curriculum, curriculum standardization across institutions, and fostering active learning strategies. Without such interventions, the future pharmacists may have inadequate knowledge and skill to combat an increasing challenge of irrational medication use in complex patient situations. This issue is particularly important in Ethiopia’s evolving healthcare system, where pharmacists are increasingly being involved in patient safety and clinical decision-making.
