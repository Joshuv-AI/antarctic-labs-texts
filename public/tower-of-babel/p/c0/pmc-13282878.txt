Efficacy of intraoperative topical Sapylin (OK-432) for preventing lymph leakage following inguinal lymph node dissection in melanoma: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Kebin Ning, Guangfu Shi, Xinlian Xie, Zhenchao Yuan, Zhenjie Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate whether intraoperative topical Sapylin (OK-432) reduces postoperative lymphatic leakage after inguinal lymph node dissection (ILND) in melanoma patients.MethodsThis retrospective study included 105 melanoma patients who underwent unilateral ILND (2022–2025). The study group (n = 51) received intraoperative topical Sapylin; the control group (n = 54) received saline. Both groups underwent Sartorius muscle flap coverage. Lymphatic leakage incidence, recovery indicators, and complications were compared. Logistic regression was used to identify risk factors.ResultsLymphatic leakage was significantly lower in the Sapylin group (9.8% vs. 25.9%, P = 0.03). Multivariable analysis confirmed age ≥ 60 years as an independent risk factor (OR = 4.93, P = 0.01) and Sapylin use as an independent protective factor (OR = 0.30, P = 0.04). The study group also showed reduced total drainage volume, shorter drain removal time, faster wound healing, and shorter hospital stay (all P < 0.05). No significant between-group differences were observed in fever or surgical site infection.ConclusionIntraoperative topical Sapylin is safe and effective for reducing postoperative lymphatic leakage and accelerating recovery after inguinal lymph node dissection for melanoma.Trial registrationThis trial was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR) on September 23, 2025 (Registration Number: ChiCTR2500109640; URL: https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=287559).Keywords: Sapylin (OK-432), Melanoma, Inguinal lymph node dissection, Lymphatic leakage, Postoperative complications

Introduction

Melanoma is one of the fastest-growing malignancies in China [1], and most patients are diagnosed at stage III–IV [2]. Inguinal lymph node dissection (ILND) is a key procedure for managing regional metastasis in lower extremity or truncal melanoma [3–5]; however, the incidence of postoperative lymphatic leakage ranges from 8% to 56% [6–11]. This is largely due to extensive disruption of lymphatic vessels during lymphadenectomy [8–10], which prevents effective closure of the severed ends. Lymphatic leakage increases the risk of wound infection (by 2.7-fold) and impaired healing (by 1.6-fold) [12], and significantly prolongs hospital stay and healthcare costs. Although molecular markers such as SGSM2 have been associated with melanoma prognosis [13], delays in adjuvant therapy caused by postoperative complications remain a critical factor affecting outcomes [14].

Current preventive strategies have shown limited efficacy. Lymphatic leakage rates remain as high as 48.8% despite preservation of the great saphenous vein [15]. Negative-pressure wound therapy and collagen-fibrin sealants have not consistently reduced the incidence of lymphatic leakage [16–19], and lymphaticovenous anastomosis may even increase the risk of tumor progression [20]. Thus, safe and effective preventive approaches are needed. The strategy of repurposing existing drugs has shown promise in melanoma—for example, the antidepressant maprotiline enhances antitumor immunity by inhibiting PDL1 [21]—providing inspiration for exploring new indications for Sapylin.

Sapylin (OK-432) is a lyophilized preparation of group A Streptococcus pyogenes that induces local aseptic inflammation, promoting fibrin deposition and tissue fibrosis [22–25]. It has demonstrated efficacy in reducing seroma formation after axillary lymph node dissection for breast cancer [26, 27], as well as in treating lymphatic malformations [28–30] and lymphoceles [23, 24, 31]. Bioactive components of traditional Chinese medicine with multi-target effects, such as cucurbitacin B (CMSP) [32], and the heterogeneous regulation of fibroblasts in the tumor microenvironment [33], have emerged as new directions in cancer therapy research. However, its role in preventing lymphatic leakage following ILND for melanoma has not yet been reported.

This retrospective study aimed to evaluate the clinical value of intraoperative topical Sapylin in preventing lymphatic leakage after inguinal lymph node dissection.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethics approval

This single-center retrospective cohort study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Guangxi Medical University Cancer Hospital (Approval Number: KY2025986). All patients were informed preoperatively of the potential intraoperative use of Sapylin or normal saline and provided written informed consent. Because anonymized retrospective clinical data were used, the ethics committee approved a waiver of re-consent for this study. This study adhered to the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. The study protocol has been registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR; Registration Number: ChiCTR2500109640).

Patient and public involvement

Due to the retrospective nature of this study, patients or the public were not involved in the design, implementation, reporting, or dissemination plan of the results of this study.

Study methods

Inclusion criteria

① The primary lesion of melanoma is located on the lower extremity or the trunk; ② It meets the criteria of stage III in the 8th edition of the AJCC staging system; ③ It is the first time for the patient to undergo unilateral inguinal lymph node dissection; ④ The medical records contain detailed information about the patient’s general condition, surgical process, and postoperative treatment.

Exclusion criteria

① History of previous surgery or radiotherapy in the ipsilateral inguinal region; ② Allergy to penicillin or streptococcal preparations; ③ Presence of severe underlying diseases (such as grade III-IV cardiac function, liver and kidney failure, etc.); ④ Incomplete medical records. All patients received the same postoperative management plan (including drainage tube care, tube removal criteria, complication handling, etc.).

Trial design and grouping

This study followed the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational studies. According to the wound irrigation plan determined by the surgical team during the operation, patients were non-randomly assigned to the study group (n = 51) or the control group (n = 54). All patients received standardized postoperative care, including: (1) Wound care at the drainage site: Disinfect the drainage outlet area daily to prevent infection; (2) Drainage tube removal criteria: Consider removal when the drainage volume is less than 50 ml for 3 consecutive days; (3) Complication management: Use physical cooling or acetaminophen for fever (axillary temperature > 37.3 °C); for surgical site infections (SSIs), antibiotics were used based on the results of drug sensitivity tests.

Sample size determination

As an exploratory retrospective cohort study, we included all consecutive eligible melanoma patients who underwent unilateral inguinal lymph node dissection in our hospital from January 2022 to March 2025. This time-limited consecutive enrollment strategy was adopted to minimize selection bias and reflect the true clinical experience of that period. A post hoc efficacy analysis was conducted based on the observed lymphatic leakage incidence (9.8% in the study group and 25.9% in the control group), and the results indicated that the sample size obtained (n = 105) provided > 80% statistical power (α = 0.05, two-sided) to detect this difference.

Surgical methods

To reduce bias, all surgeries in this study were performed by the same group of experienced surgeons. After successful anesthesia, routine disinfection and draping were carried out. A longitudinal incision of 12–15 cm centered on the femoral vessels was made, with the upper boundary extending beyond the inguinal fold (Malgaigne line), and 6–8 mm of subcutaneous tissue was preserved to protect the blood supply. The anatomical operation started from the inner side, clearly identifying the long adductor muscle, sartorius muscle and deep femoral vein fascia, and ligating the deep femoral vein; an incision of 2–3 cm on the medial side of the long tendon membrane was made, extending upward and downward to the intersection of the sartorius muscle and the inguinal ligament, and lymph nodes were systematically disinfected from the inside out until the femoral vein was exposed and its inner and outer sides were completely exposed; on the outer side, the sartorius tendon membrane was cut along its entire length to expose the femoral artery surface, and along the femoral vein, the great saphenous vein arch was found and ligated and cut. Then, the dissection continued along the anterior surface of the femoral arteries to the inguinal ligament, dissecting the femoral canal and removing the Cloquet lymph nodes. During lymph node dissection, all visible lymphatic vessel stumps were ligated using 3 − 0 silk sutures. Small lymphatic stumps were sealed with electrocautery or an ultrasonic scalpel. For lymphatic vessels that were difficult to ligate directly, bipolar coagulation was applied to achieve precise hemostasis and closure. After completion of the dissection, the surgical field was thoroughly irrigated 2–3 times with warm saline. Hemostasis was then ensured by a combination of compression (dry gauze pressed for 1–2 min) and spot electrocautery, with stepwise inspection from the femoral vessels outward to confirm the absence of active bleeding. After hemostasis was confirmed, a 2–3 min observation period was allowed to rule out recurrent bleeding.The sartorius muscle was then dissected free and its origin at the inguinal region was severed. It was transposed to cover the inguinal defect, and the proximal end was sutured to the surrounding fascia. The surrounding fascia and adjacent skin flaps were used to cover the wound surface. A 10- or 15-pore drainage tube was placed along the femoral vascular axis and clamped; in the study group, 20 mL of Sapylin solution (prepared by dissolving 10 vials of Sapylin (Streptococcus pyogenes preparation for injection, Shandong State-owned Luya Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., National Drug Approval Number S19980002, 1 KE per vial) in 20 mL of normal saline) was applied topically to the wound surface, while the control group received the same volume of normal saline. The incision was closed in layers (2 − 0 absorbable suture for subcutaneous tissue, 3 − 0 non-absorbable suture for intermittent skin suturing). The drainage tube was opened 30 min after incision closure. Patients were instructed to lie flat, avoid vigorous coughing, and keep the drainage tube unobstructed. The drainage tube was removed when the drainage volume was less than 50 mL for three consecutive days (Fig. 1).

Outcome measures

Data were extracted from medical records:

Primary outcome

The incidence of postoperative lymphatic leakage was defined as drainage fluid volume ≥ 100 mL/day with a pale yellow and foamy appearance, persisting for at least two consecutive days starting from postoperative day 3.

Secondary outcomes

① Total drainage volume, measured daily until drain removal; ② Time to drain removal, defined as the duration (days) from surgery to drain removal; ③ Wound healing time, defined as the number of days from surgery to complete epithelialization; ④ Length of hospital stay, defined as the number of days from surgery to discharge.

Harms assessment

All adverse events were monitored and recorded. These included fever (defined as an axillary temperature > 37.3 °C within 24 h and requiring intervention) and surgical site infection (SSI). Surgical site infection was diagnosed based on clinical signs (erythema, swelling, fever, pain, purulent discharge) or positive bacterial culture results. To reduce assessment bias, the outcome assessors (nurses recording drainage conditions and surgeons assessing wound healing) were unaware of the groupings.

Subgroup analysis

To evaluate the consistency of Sapylin efficacy across patients with different lymph node burdens, a subgroup analysis based on the number of positive lymph nodes was prespecified. According to clinically relevant thresholds and the sample distribution, and to enhance statistical power, patients were categorized into three groups: low burden (1 positive lymph node), intermediate burden (2 positive lymph nodes), and high burden (≥ 3 positive lymph nodes).

Follow-up and data collection

Long-term safety assessment

Chronic lower limb lymphedema was assessed at six months postoperatively, as lymphedema typically peaks at this time point. A composite criterion was used for diagnosis, combining objective signs (≥ 2 cm increase in limb circumference at the same anatomic level compared with the contralateral side, or pitting edema on pretibial compression) with subjective symptoms (patient-reported swelling, heaviness, or tightness, and need for professional lymphatic drainage at least weekly). Diagnosis was made if either an objective sign or a subjective symptom was present.

Data recording and management

All follow-up data were entered into the electronic medical record system by dedicated personnel and double-checked to ensure accuracy. For patients lost to follow-up, the date of the last follow-up and the reason for loss to follow-up were recorded.

Statistical analysis

The statistics were conducted using SPSS 27 (Chicago, USA) and GraphPad Prism 10 (San Diego, USA). The measurement data were expressed as x ± s and were compared between groups using the independent sample t-test; the non-normal distribution data were represented by the median (interquartile range) (Q1-Q3) and were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. The count data were expressed as frequency (percentage) and were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact probability method. Binary Logistic regression was used for univariate analysis to screen potential factors related to postoperative lymphatic leakage. To ensure the stability of the multivariate model and avoid overfitting, the number of variables included in the model was strictly limited. The inclusion of variables followed the following strategy: variables with P < 0.10 from the univariate analysis and important variables based on clinical knowledge that were considered potentially related to lymphatic leakage were included in the multivariate Logistic regression model. The results were expressed as OR and 95% CI. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of baseline data between the two groups

A total of 110 melanoma patients who underwent unilateral inguinal lymph node dissection in our hospital from January 2022 to March 2025 were consecutively identified through the electronic medical record system (Fig. 2). After excluding 5 patients with incomplete data, 105 patients were included in the final analysis and divided into two groups based on intraoperative Sapylin application: the study group (n = 51) and the control group (n = 54) (Fig. 2). All patients were followed up for 6 months (Fig. 2). No significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed between the two groups (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Total drainage volume

The median total drainage volume was significantly lower in the study group (557 ml, IQR 323–900 ml) compared to the control group (797 ml, IQR 420–1430 ml) (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3).

Box plot comparing total drainage volume between groups

Postoperative recovery indicators

The study group had significantly shorter median time to drain removal [8 days (IQR 7–10) vs. 11 days (IQR 7–13), P = 0.003], shorter median hospital stay [10 days (IQR 9–12) vs. 14 days (IQR 11–17), P < 0.001], and shorter median wound healing time [12 days (IQR 11–14) vs. 15 days (IQR 13–17), P < 0.001] compared to the control group (Fig. 4).

Postoperative complications

The incidence of lymphatic leakage in the study group (9.8%, 5/51) was significantly lower than that in the control group (25.9%, 14/54) (P < 0.05) (Table 2, Fig. 5). Fever was the most common complication in both groups. In the study group, 7 patients (13.7%) experienced postoperative fever (defined as fever), with the highest temperature reaching 39.0 °C; in the control group, 9 patients (16.7%) experienced postoperative fever, with the highest temperature reaching 39.6 °C. No significant difference was observed in the incidence of postoperative fever or body temperature between the two groups (P > 0.05) (Table 2). The incidence of surgical site infection (SSI) in the study group was 2.0% (1/51), and in the control group was 7.4% (4/54) (P = 0.40) (Table 2).

At the 6-month follow-up, the study group had 10 patients lost to follow-up and one death, and the control group had 11 lost to follow-up and three deaths, leaving 40 patients in each group with complete follow-up (Fig. 2). Subgroup analysis of Sapylin efficacy by positive lymph node burden

To further evaluate the preventive effect of Sapylin in patients with different lymph node burdens, patients were divided into low-burden (1 positive lymph node, n = 56), intermediate-burden (2 positive lymph nodes, n = 16), and high-burden (≥ 3 positive lymph nodes, n = 33) groups according to the number of positive lymph nodes. The incidence of lymphatic leakage was compared between the study and control groups within each subgroup. The results (Table 5) showed that in the low-burden group, the incidence of lymphatic leakage was 10.0% (3/30) in the study group and 30.8% (8/26) in the control group, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.05). In the intermediate-burden group, the incidence was 14.3% (1/7) in the study group and 33.3% (3/9) in the control group (P = 0.59). In the high-burden group, the incidence was 7.1% (1/14) in the study group and 10.5% (2/19) in the control group (P = 1.0).

Interaction test for consistency of efficacy across subgroups

A binary logistic regression model with interaction terms was constructed to assess whether the efficacy of Sapylin varied according to the number of positive lymph nodes. The number of positive lymph nodes (1, 2, or ≥ 3) was included as a categorical variable, Sapylin use as the intervention, and lymphatic leakage as the dependent variable. As shown in Table 6, no statistically significant interaction was observed between Sapylin use and the number of positive lymph nodes (overall P = 0.74), indicating that the protective effect of Sapylin remained consistent across different lymph node burden subgroups. The main effect of Sapylin use was significant (OR = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.05–0.89, P = 0.03), further confirming its independent protective role in the overall population. No significant association was found between the number of positive lymph nodes and lymphatic leakage (overall P = 0.20), although patients with ≥ 3 positive lymph nodes showed a trend toward lower risk (OR = 0.22, 95% CI: 0.04–1.18, P = 0.08).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, local application of Sapylin significantly reduced the incidence of lymph leakage after inguinal lymph node dissection for melanoma (9.8% vs. 25.9%), while also decreasing the total drainage volume, shortening the recovery time, and not increasing the risk of complications. The mechanism of action may be as follows: ① Sapylin can induce the elevation of many cytokines (such as interleukin 1α, interleukin 6, tumor necrosis factor, interferon-γ, etc.), creating an environment with antibacterial activity [23, 25]; ② Activating neutrophils, macrophages, T cells, and lymphocytes, increasing collagen synthesis, and accelerating the closure of lymphatic vessel ends [22, 23]; ③ Upregulating growth factors (VEGF, bFGF), HUVEC, HFL1, enhancing the proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis of vascular endothelial cells, thereby improving blood supply at the wound site [34]. The results of this study are similar to those of Sapylin in preventing subcutaneous effusion after axillary lymph node dissection for breast cancer, verifying its feasibility in another lymphatic system-rich area, the groin region.

The incidence of postoperative lymphatic leakage in the study group (9.8%) was significantly lower than the 48.8% reported in the literature for the treatment using the great saphenous vein preservation method, indicating that Saperlin can reduce the occurrence of lymphatic leakage at its source. Moreover, the study group performed significantly better than the control group in terms of total drainage volume, time for removing the drainage tube, hospital stay, and wound healing time, and had more advantages over the collagen fibrin sealant (which has been proven to have no significant effect). Additionally, a single intraoperative topical application of Sapylin to the wound surface suffices for therapeutic effect, markedly reducing surgical complexity and offering superior clinical adoptability compared with lymphaticovenous anastomosis.

Another clinical application direction of Sapylin that we cannot overlook is its use in treating lymph leakage that occurs after preventive intervention, or in high-risk patients who have not received prevention. Through inducing local inflammation and promoting fibrin deposition, Sapylin can also be used as a therapeutic method. It is also effective for low-flow lymph leakage or lymph cysts that have formed after surgery. Recent studies have shown that ultrasound-guided intracystic injection of Sapylin can reduce the size of lymph cysts by more than 95% [24], which is consistent with the fibrotic effect observed in this study. Compared with secondary surgery, ultrasound-guided percutaneous injection of Sapylin after surgery has advantages such as less trauma, repeatability, and economy. However, we also need to fully consider its limitations: Delayed intervention may lead to an increase in infection rate and prolongation of hospital stay, which overall negates the therapeutic benefits [35]; if lymph leakage has formed a sinus tract or local infection, the inflammatory stimulation of Sapylin may aggravate the condition, and it is necessary to control the infection first before considering its use.

The safety of Sapylin is a key concern in clinical practice. Fever is the most common adverse reaction of Sapylin [23, 36]. In this study, the fever rate in the study group (13.7%) was not significantly different from that in the control group (14.8%), and the fever was relatively mild (the highest body temperature was 39.0℃). After physical cooling or drug treatment, the body temperature returned to normal. This is consistent with the characteristic of Sapylin inducing a mild and controllable inflammatory response. However, it should be noted that if the postoperative body temperature remains high, further observation is still needed to check for the occurrence of local infection or other complications. It is worth noting that in terms of wound infection rate, the incision infection rate in the study group was lower than that in the control group (2.0% vs. 7.4%), but the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.40), indicating that the local application of 10KE dose of Sapylin is safe and does not increase the risk of infection.

In addition to its favorable safety profile, the rationale for the Sapylin dosage used in this study (10 KE/20 mL) merits further explanation. Based on these considerations, the dosage was determined for the following reasons. First, the manufacturer’s instructions recommend an intracavitary maintenance dose of 5–10 KE, placing 10 KE within the safe range. Second, this dose has been shown to be safe and effective in previous studies on the prevention of seroma formation after axillary lymph node dissection for breast cancer [26]. Third, a 10 KE dose has demonstrated favorable safety and efficacy in the local treatment of malignant serous cavity effusions and solid tumors [37]. Finally, preliminary clinical experience at our center has shown that this dose achieves adequate drug concentration in the wound bed without any dose-related serious adverse events.

In addition to the significant clinical benefits, the results of this study indicate that Sapylin may possess potential clinical economic advantages. Based on its ability to reduce the risk of postoperative lymph leakage by 62% and shorten the median hospital stay by approximately 4 days, the application of Sapylin expected to reduce the consumption of additional medical resources due to lymph leakage and its complications. Compared to treating already existing lymph leakage, cost-effective preventive interventions are a better strategy [38, 39]. As a relatively economical intervention measure, the effects of Sapylin in reducing the risk of lymph leakage and shortening hospital stay can theoretically be converted into savings in costs associated with prolonged hospital stays, frequent outpatient follow-ups, and treatments for lymph leakage (such as repeated wound care, long-term maintenance of drainage tubes, and antibiotic use after secondary infections). This “low investment, high preventive benefit” strategy not only helps to alleviate the economic burden on patients but also optimizes the efficiency of medical resource allocation by reducing subsequent treatment needs. It has particularly promising application prospects in grassroots medical institutions with relatively limited resources. Future research needs to conduct a formal cost-benefit analysis to confirm its economic value.

Multivariate logistic regression identified age ≥ 60 years as an independent risk factor for lymphatic leakage (OR 4.93; 95% CI 1.46–16.69; P = 0.01), whereas intraoperative Sapylin application emerged as an independent protective factor (OR 0.30; 95% CI 0.09–0.96; P = 0.04).Although Sapylin can reduce the risk of lymphatic leakage, elderly patients may have additional factors that increase the risk, and these factors are not affected by Sapylin intervention: ① The proportion of patients with vascular sclerosis is higher, and local microcirculation disorders may weaken the local effect of Sapylin or promote the generation of lymphatic leakage fluid, thereby increasing the risk of infection and indirectly prolonging the postoperative lymphatic leakage course; ② Patients often have poor nutritional reserves (low albuminemia, decreased prealbumin) and low immunity, leading to a decrease in tissue repair ability. Even if all patients receive sartorius muscle transposition, meticulous ligation of lymphatic vessel ends during the operation, and the promotion of lymphatic vessel closure by Sapylin, local tissue healing may still be poor, increasing the duration of leakage. Therefore, we should fully realize that in actual clinical applications, no intervention measure can eliminate the influence of all risk factors. The local application of Sapylin as an intervention method has a limited scope of action, and age, as a systemic risk indicator, often has multi-dimensional influences. For elderly patients, even if Sapylin is used, targeted perioperative management (such as correcting low protein before surgery, controlling blood sugar [40]; avoiding overly frequent use of blunt instruments such as forceps or fingers to create gaps in the tissue space during the operation, and using energy devices such as electrosurgical knives and ultrasonic scalpels to close invisible lymphatic channels [11, 12]; appropriately extending the drainage tube retention time after surgery [11, 12, 41]) is necessary to reduce the risk of lymphatic leakage at multiple levels. In the context of an increasingly aging population in China, this finding provides more evidence-based guidance for clinical decision-making that is more in line with the characteristics of Chinese patients.

The multivariate analysis of this study did not reveal a significant association between the total number of lymph node dissections and the risk of postoperative lymphatic leakage (OR = 0.98, P = 0.69), which is different from some previous studies [12, 42]. We analyzed the possible reasons for this result as follows: Firstly, all surgeries were performed by the same group of experienced surgeons, following standardized surgical procedures, and systematic and meticulous ligation of lymphatic vessel ends was carried out during the operation, and the sartorius muscle flap was used to cover the femoral sheath. This standardized operation may have weakened the association between the total number of lymph node dissections and the risk of lymphatic leakage to a certain extent. Secondly, the baseline levels of lymph node dissections in the two groups of patients in this study were comparable and there was no statistically significant difference (Table 1), and the variability may not be sufficient to detect significance in the statistical model. Moreover, the occurrence of lymphatic leakage is more directly dependent on the number of unsewn lymphatic vessel ends during the operation, rather than the number of dissected lymph nodes themselves. The number of lymph nodes is only an indirect surrogate indicator, and the two are not completely linearly correlated. Finally, in this study, age ≥ 60 years and the Sapylin use were factors with strong effects (OR values were 4.93 and 0.30 respectively), which may to some extent mask the relatively weak influencing factor of the number of lymph nodes.

Regarding the number of positive lymph nodes, we systematically evaluated this through subgroup analysis and interaction testing. In the low-burden group (1 positive lymph node), Sapylin significantly reduced the risk of lymphatic leakage (P = 0.05), whereas in the intermediate- and high-burden groups (≥ 2 positive lymph nodes), the effect did not reach statistical significance but showed a direction consistent with the overall analysis (OR = 0.48). More importantly, the logistic regression model incorporating interaction terms revealed no significant interaction between treatment assignment and the number of positive lymph nodes (overall P = 0.74), indicating that the protective effect of Sapylin remained consistent across patients with different lymph node burdens. This finding supports Sapylin as a broadly applicable prophylactic strategy for lymphatic leakage after inguinal lymph node dissection, from which patients may benefit regardless of their lymph node metastasis burden.

A noteworthy observation was that among patients with a high burden of positive lymph nodes (≥ 3), the incidence of lymphatic leakage in the control group (10.5%) was paradoxically lower than that in the low-burden group (30.8%). This counterintuitive finding may be attributed to differences in surgical technique—surgeons tend to perform more thorough ligation of lymphatic vessels and more extensive coverage with the sartorius muscle flap in patients with a high lymph node metastatic burden, thereby reducing the baseline risk of lymphatic leakage. Sampling variability due to the small sample size in the high-burden group (n = 33), as well as unmeasured confounders (such as nutritional status and the use of energy devices), may also have contributed. This finding underscores the importance of accounting for variations in surgical technique when evaluating the efficacy of prophylactic interventions.

Chronic lymphedema following inguinal lymph node dissection is an important long-term complication that significantly affects patients’ quality of life [43, 44]. Its pathogenesis involves irreversible disruption of the lymphatic network and impaired lymphatic drainage. Reported incidence rates vary widely (10%–50%) depending on the assessment criteria, follow-up duration, and patient population [44–46]. Lymphedema typically peaks around six months after inguinal lymph node dissection and then declines [47]; therefore, we selected this time point for long-term safety assessment to obtain a reliable estimate of its true incidence.

In this study, the incidence of lymphedema at six months post-operatively was 22.5% in the Sapylin group and 25.0% in the control group, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.793). This negative finding carries important clinical implications. First, it further confirms the safety of the intervention. Although Sapylin prevents early lymphatic leakage by inducing local aseptic inflammation and fibrosis, there was a theoretical concern that this mechanism might aggravate lymphatic outflow obstruction and increase the risk of long-term lymphedema. Our results indicate that such concern is unwarranted—local Sapylin application did not translate into an increased risk of chronic lymphedema. Second, this finding demonstrates the coexistence of short-term benefits and long-term safety: Sapylin significantly reduced the incidence of early postoperative lymphatic leakage (9.8% vs. 25.9%) and shortened hospital stay without increasing the risk of long-term lymphedema. This pattern of “short-term benefit with long-term safety” further supports the clinical value of Sapylin in inguinal lymph node dissection. Although the difference between groups was not statistically significant, the 22.5% incidence in the Sapylin group suggests a potential trend toward benefit. Moreover, the two groups were well balanced with respect to baseline factors known to influence lymphedema development, such as age, sex, extent of dissection, and BMI (Table 1), ruling out confounding by these variables.

Our discussion of the mechanisms underlying Sapylin’s effects draws on existing evidence from studies on axillary lymph node dissection for breast cancer and the treatment of lymphatic malformations. It must be acknowledged that currently, there is a lack of molecular-level research on the local inflammatory and fibrotic processes after the application of Sapylin in the groin area. This warrants further investigation in future studies. However, we believe that extending these mechanisms to the groin area has biological rationality. Firstly, the sterile inflammation triggered by Sapylin and the subsequent fibrotic reaction is a fundamental pathological process that is not affected by different anatomical locations. Its core components (such as inflammatory cell infiltration, cytokine release, and collagen deposition) are universal in different tissues. Secondly, the significant clinical effects observed in this study, namely the reduced incidence of lymph leakage, decreased total drainage volume, and shortened healing-related time in the study group, provide strong indirect evidence for the same mechanism to be at work in the groin area. The generation of these effects must have a biological basis, and the mechanisms that have been verified in other parts are the most reasonable explanation at present.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. It is a single-center retrospective cohort study, and therefore the generalizability of our findings to broader populations requires further validation. Additionally, the retrospective, non-randomized design introduces potential selection bias and operator bias. Patient allocation was based on intraoperative decisions by different surgical teams rather than random assignment. For instance, surgeons may have decided whether to use Sapylin based on subjective assessments of preoperative conditions (such as BMI or nutritional status). Although we statistically adjusted for known baseline confounders using multivariate logistic regression, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be completely excluded, which somewhat weakens the strength of causal inference. Moreover, the definition of lymphatic leakage relied on clinical criteria and drainage volume, without biochemical confirmation (e.g., triglyceride measurement).

It should be noted that the definition of lymphatic leakage in this study was based on clinical criteria (drainage volume and fluid characteristics) rather than on biochemical confirmation, such as triglyceride measurement. This approach was adopted for several reasons. Currently, no universally accepted definition of postoperative lymphatic leakage exists, and diagnostic criteria vary considerably across studies, ranging from specific drainage volumes to the number of days of leakage [48]. Using clear, objective, and easily applicable clinical criteria helps improve the comparability and reproducibility of research findings. Moreover, recent studies have demonstrated that early postoperative drainage volume is a key predictor of lymphatic leakage and its severity [49], supporting the rationale for using drainage volume as a core diagnostic criterion in this study. Furthermore, in postoperative wound exudate, the biochemical composition of lymphatic fluid may overlap with that of hematoma absorption fluid or seroma (e.g., triglyceride levels) [50, 51], making biochemical differentiation not entirely reliable. Therefore, the clinical criteria used in this study represent a practical and acceptable approach within the current state of the field [52]. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the lack of routine triglyceride measurement in drainage fluid is a limitation of this study, as it may have introduced some imprecision in the calculation of lymphatic leakage incidence. This should be addressed in future prospective studies.

To address these limitations and further validate the clinical value of Sapylin, a prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled trial (RCT) is warranted based on the findings of this study. Such a confirmatory study could be conducted in collaboration with multiple melanoma treatment centers across China, using a blinded design (especially blinding of outcome assessors), standardized surgical procedures, and a uniform Sapylin intervention protocol (with dosage and administration consistent with the present study). The diagnostic criteria for lymphatic leakage should incorporate biochemical confirmation, such as triglyceride measurement, to improve objectivity. Multicenter collaboration would not only increase the sample size to enable more reliable subgroup analyses (e.g., in elderly patients or those with high BMI) but also test the robustness and generalizability of our findings across different healthcare settings and surgical teams. To directly confirm the proposed mechanism of Sapylin in the groin region, future studies could measure cytokine concentrations (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) in postoperative drainage fluid or perform histological analysis of surgical specimens. Furthermore, future studies could explore dose-response relationships by testing different Sapylin doses to identify the optimal regimen that maximizes clinical benefit. If the results of such a multicenter RCT are consistent with those of the present retrospective study, they would provide higher-level evidence to support the inclusion of this safe, simple, and cost-effective strategy in clinical practice guidelines, ultimately benefiting a broader population of melanoma patients.

Conclusion

The intraoperative topical application of Sapylin to the wound surface following melanoma lymph node dissection in the groin is a safe, simple, and effective technique. It may accelerate wound healing through mechanisms such as inducing inflammatory responses, promoting collagen synthesis and vascular proliferation and repair. This provides an innovative and feasible solution for preventing lymph leakage.
