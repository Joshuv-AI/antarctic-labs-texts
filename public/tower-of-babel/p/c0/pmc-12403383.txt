Anxiety, depression and fear of disease progression among Chinese parents of children with retinoblastoma
Authors: Yilin Yu, Yiyi Feng, Yanping Han, Jie Yang, Xuyang Wen, Xianqun Fan, Jiayan Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecent studies indicate parents of childhood cancer survivors experience high psychological burden. However, there is limited knowledge regarding the mental health of parents of children with retinoblastoma (RB) in China. Thus, the purpose of this study was to assess anxiety, depression, fear of disease progression (FoP), and family function among parents of children with RB, and to analyze associated factors.MethodsThis cross-sectional study enrolled 151 RB patients and 185 of their parents. Parents completed the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), Fear of Progression Questionnaire-Short Form/Parent version (FoP-Q-SF/PR), and Family APGAR Index to assess the levels of anxiety, depression, FoP, family function. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to identify associated factors.ResultsClinically relevant anxiety and depression were reported in 34% and 41% of parents of children with RB. FoP-related dysfunction was observed in 68.1% of parents. More than half of the families (54.9%) exhibited family dysfunction. Anxiety level demonstrated positive correlations with urban residency and BDI scores. The increase in the number of intravenous chemotherapy (IVC), tumor recurrence, urban residency and BAI scores were associated with parental depression levels. Additionally, multivariate analysis included IVC, religious belief, ethnic minority groups and BDI scores as independent risk factors for FoP.ConclusionsAnxiety, depression and FoP represented substantial burdens for parents of children with RB, which were relate to IVC, age at diagnosis, tumor recurrence, place of residence, and education level of parents. Consequently, during treatment of RB, particular attention needs to be paid to the psychological health of parents, psychological interventions should be provided when necessary.

Background

Retinoblastoma (RB) is the most common intraocular malignant tumor in children, which is the main disease that causes blindness and disability in childhood [1, 2]. Globally, RB affects approximately 1 in 16,000–18,000 live births annually, with ~ 8,000 new cases diagnosed each year; 95% of these occur before age 5 [1]. China reports the world’s second-highest RB incidence after India [3], with ~ 1,100 annual diagnoses, of whom 84% present with high-risk intraocular disease [4, 5]. Primary treatment objectives prioritize life preservation, followed by vision salvage and ocular conservation [1, 6]. Current therapeutic modalities for RB include systemic intravenous chemotherapy (IVC), intra-arterial chemotherapy (IAC), focal therapy (laser therapy or cryotherapy) and enucleation. Over the past 30 years, the Chinese survival rate of RB patients have significantly increased from 81–91% [5], resulting in increasing number of survivors. Nonetheless, survivors frequently experience impaired vision, monocular vision, or blindness, with consequent impacts on physical, psychological and social health.

Cancer not only affects the patient, but can also impact their family [7, 8]. The treatment and care of children with cancer is a long-term process, which requires not only doctors and nurses, but all family members. A meta-analysis of 85 studies revealed a high prevalence of depression among caregivers of cancer patients [9]. Previous studies also showed that parents have to cope with long-term psychosocial burden (e.g., anxiety, depression and post-traumatic stress) after their children are treated for cancer [10–12]. In a national cohort of parents of children with cancer, the study reported that these parents were found to be at higher risk for psychiatric disorders in the first year after their child was diagnosed with cancer, and notably, this risk became higher over time [13]. In addition, recent studies demonstrated that fear of progression (FoP) is a great burden for parents of paediatric cancer survivors [14, 15]. These results indicate that parents of childhood/adolescent cancer survivors are likely to experience a long-term psychosocial burden during or after treatment of their children. In fact, the connection of psychological functions between children and parents is complex and bidirectional [16]. A study had showed that anxious parents had different parenting characteristics which affected the socio-emotional development of their children [17]. High prevalence of parental depression was negatively associated with children’s adherence to treatment [18]. Therefore, it is of great importance to focus on the psychological functioning of parents, especially parents of children with chronic illnesses. However, in clinical practice, we often neglect the psychological state of parents. There is limited knowledge regarding the mental health of parents of children with RB, especially in China.

Therefore, our study focused on the Chinese parental anxiety, depression, FoP and family function of children with retinoblastoma, and to analyze the risk factors.

Methods

Participants

Patients with RB treated in Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital of Shanghai JiaoTong University School of Medicine from January 2017 to September 2022 were included in this study. The inclusion criteria were children aged 0–18 years who were clinically diagnosed with RB and their parents could accurately understand the questionnaire. Parents who could not read or use electronics were excluded. Self-report questionnaires were completed by parents of children with RB through face-to-face interviews or electronic questionnaires.

Sociodemographic and medical data

Parents reported sociodemographic information through questionnaire. Demographic details of parents included sex, age, ethnicity, place of residence, marital status of parents, education level, religious belief, health condition, personal living habits and family financial burden. Medical information of children with RB was reviewed and collected through medical records.

Beck anxiety inventory (BAI)

The anxiety levels of parents were measured by the Chinese version of the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI). The BAI is a common screening tool to measure anxiety levels in adults aged 17–80 years [19]. The tool has been used to distinguish between anxious and non-anxious diagnostic groups in a variety of clinical populations (Cronbach α = 0.74–0.87) [20–22]. The BAI questionnaire consists of 21 items covering anxiety symptoms that have occurred in the past week. Each answer is scored on a scale of 0 (not at all) to 3 (severe). Higher total scores represent more severe anxiety symptoms. A total score of 0–7 indicates normal or minimal anxiety; 8–15 indicates mild anxiety; 16–25 indicates moderate anxiety; and 26–63 indicates severe anxiety. Scores above the minimal level considered to be associated with clinically relevant anxiety, and people with moderate and severe scores may warrant timely intervention.

Beck depression inventory (BDI)

The depression levels of parents were measured by the Chinese version of the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI). The BDI is one of the most commonly used self-report tools for depression screening, which is now widely used in clinical and scientific research, and the reliability and validity of the Chinese version of the BDI have been confirmed (Cronbach’s α = 0.846) [23–27]. Previous studies had demonstrated the BDI was capacity to discriminate between depressed and non-depressed subjects [28–31]. The BDI questionnaire contains 21 questions and takes approximately 5 min to complete. Each item includes a list of statements about a particular symptom of depression, which is scored on a scale of 0 (not at all) to 3 (severe). Total scores of 0–13 indicate minimal depression; 14–19 indicate mild depression; 20–28 indicate moderate depression; and 29–63 indicate severe depression. Scores above the minimal level considered to be associated with clinically relevant depression, and people with moderate and severe scores may warrant timely intervention.

Fear of progression (FoP)

FoP is defined as the “fear that the illness will progress with all its biopsychosocial consequences, or that it will recur” [32], involving both the survivors and their caregivers. In this study, FoP was measured with the Chinese version of the Fear of Progression Questionnaire for the parental perspective (FoP-Q-SF/PR) (Cronbach’s α = 0.89) [14]. The FoP-Q-SF/PR is an adaptation of the FoP-Q-SF for adult patients with 12 items, which has proven to be reliable and valid [33, 34]. Parents used a five-point scale (from never (1) to very often (5)) to score the degree of FoP. Higher scores indicate a higher level of FoP. A cut-off of ≥ 34 to differentiate functional from dysfunctional levels of FoP was suggested by Herschbach et al. [35], which has also been used in an previous study on FoP in parents of childhood cancer patients [32].

Family APGAR index

The Family APGAR Index measures satisfaction with family function, which was originally developed by Smilkstein [36]. The Family APGAR Index had been proven to be a valid and reliable instrument [37]. We used the Chinese-translated version with 5 items and each item ranged from “never (0)” to “always (3)”, with a total score range of 0–15. The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.843 and the 2-week test–retest reliability was 0.724 in the Chinese version. Higher scores indicate greater satisfaction with family function.

Statistical analysis

Demographics, clinical information, psychosocial outcomes and family APGAR Index were summarized using means, standard deviations (SD), medians or proportions, frequencies, and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences in the characteristics between groups were examined by the Mann–Whitney U test or two-sample t-test for continuous variables according to the distribution of the data. For ordinal categorical variables, we used the Mann–Whitney U test to compare differences in characteristics between groups. Categorical variables were analyzed by the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Univariate analyses were performed and variables with p value < 0.1 were included in subsequent multivariate models to identify independent factors associated with anxiety, depression and FoP. Statistical significance for all analyses was defined as two-tailed p value < 0.05. Analyses were carried out using the software package SPSS 19.0 for Windows.

Ethical considerations

The research was carried out according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [38]. Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital Ethics Committee approved the study. Informed consent has been obtained from all participants in this study. All selected subjects were informed of the purpose and content of the study, and were also promised that their personal information would not be disclosed.

Assessment of parental depression, anxiety, FoP and family function

Among 185 parents of children with RB, 34.0% of parents exhibited clinically relevant anxiety (mild 17.8%, moderate 8.1%, severe 8.1%) (Fig. 1A), while 41.0% reported clinically relevant depression (mild 17.8%, moderate 17.8%, severe 5.4%) (Fig. 1B). Psychological dysfunction from FoP affected 67.9% of parents (Fig. 2). 45.1% parents reported family dysfunction (moderate 31.8%, severe 13.3%) (Fig. 3).

A comparison between the enucleation and globe salvage groups was also conducted (Table 3). Among parents of children undergoing enucleation (N = 67), clinically relevant anxiety was observed in 28.4% (mild 13.4%, moderate 9.0%, severe 6.0%), while clinically relevant depression occurred in 43.3% (mild 16.4%, moderate 23.9%, severe 3.0%). Univariate analysis of anxiety revealed significant correlations with child’s age at enucleation (p = 0.029), tumor recurrence (p < 0.001), urban residency (p = 0.003), parents from non-Han ethnicity (p = 0.030), parents with religious belief (p < 0 0.001), BDI scores (p < 0.001), and FoP-Q-SF/PR scores (p < 0.001).

Univariate analysis of depression revealed significant correlations with child’s age at diagnosis (p < 0.001), number of IVC cycle (p = 0.043), tumor recurrency (p = 0.018), parents with religious belief (p < 0.001), education level of parents (p = 0.006), family financial burden (p < 0.001), BAI scores (p < 0.001), FoP scores (p < 0.001) and APGAR scores (p < 0.001).

Univariate analysis of FoP revealed significant correlations with child’s age at diagnosis (p = 0.003), IVC (p < 0.001), PPV (p = 0.007), child’s age at enucleation (p < 0.001), sex of parents (p = 0.02), parents from non-Han ethnicity (p = 0.025), parents with religious belief (p = 0.001), education level of parents (p < 0.001), smoking (p = 0.006), BAI scores (p < 0.001), and BDI scores (p < 0.001).

Multivariate analysis

Multivariate analysis was conducted to explore independent factors for parental anxiety, depression, and FoP. Variables with p < 0.100 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate models. Table 5 showed that urban residency (OR = 23.04, p = 0.005), and BDI scores (OR = 1.69, p < 0.001) were independent risk factors for parental anxiety level. Number of IVC cycle (OR = 1.90, p < 0.001), tumor recurrence (OR = 381.55, p = 0.002), urban residency (OR = 3.25, p = 0.018), and BAI scores (OR = 2.79, p < 0.001) were independent risk factors for parental depression levels (Table 6), while child’s age at diagnosis (p = 0.001), education level of parents (p < 0.001), parents with religious belief (p < 0.001), and APGAR scores (p = 0.005) were protective factors (Table 6). In addition, IVC (OR = 708.77, p = 0.041), parents from non-Han ethnicity (OR = 2275.71, p = 0.011), parents with religious belief (OR = 616.80, p < 0.001) and BDI scores (OR = 1.53, p = 0.011) were independent risk factors for parental FoP (Table 7).

Discussion

This study assessed the parental anxiety, depression, FoP and family function of children with RB in China, and evaluated the associated factors of parental anxiety, depression and FoP levels. The results showed Chinese parents of RB children were experienced heavy burden of anxiety, depression and FoP, which were related to IVC, age at diagnosis, tumor recurrence, place of residence, and education level of parents.

Anxiety is a common psychiatric disorder in the world and is characterized by intense, excessive and persistent worry or fear. Depression is characterized by depressed mood, loss of interest, poor eating and sleeping, etc., which has a high disability rate and a low cure rate. Today, an increasing number of people are suffering from mental problems such as anxiety and depression, which has become a neglected global health crisis [39]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), more than 350 million people worldwide are affected by depression, with a global average incidence of 4.4% [40, 41]. In an epidemiological study on the prevalence of mental disorders in China published in 2021, the prevalence of anxiety disorder was 7.6% in adults [41], and the prevalence of depressive disorder in China was as high as 6.8% [41, 42]. In our study, we found that 34% of parents had clinically relevant anxiety (mild 17.8%, moderate 8.1%, severe 8.1%), 41% of parents reached clinically relevant levels of depression (mild 17.8%, moderate 17.8%, severe 5.4%). It suggested that the prevalence of anxiety and depression of parents of children with RB were higher than the national prevalence (anxiety 34% vs. 7.8%, depression 41% vs. 6.8%). A study on family caregivers of children with RB in America also reported that 26.7% of family caregivers suffered depression, and 35.8% had anxiety [43]. Compared with data from the USA, our findings indicated that Chinese parents of children with RB have a higher prevalence of clinically relevant depression (41.0% vs. 26.7%). As previous studies have shown that caregivers’ education level were related to their mental health [32]. Our data showed that the education level of Chinese RB parents were lower than that of the United States, which may lead to an insufficient understanding of the disease, and resulting the incidence of depression. Furthermore, recent research showed that only about 9.5% of patients with depression have received mental health services in China [41], while the proportion in the United States is about 65% [44, 45]. Therefore, many Chinese parents of children with RB who are in the early stage of clinical depression may not receive the mental health services promptly, progressing to depression. Additionally, hospitals in China mainly focus on the treatment of children with RB, with low attention and support for the mental health of their parents. However, to our knowledge, this is the first report on parental FoP and family function of children with RB. Our study showed that majority of Chinese parents (67.9%) of children with RB suffered psychological dysfunction from FoP, and nearly half of the families (45.1%) had impairment in family function. These findings suggested that the diagnosis of RB in children has a significant impact on the mental health of their parents and families.

A large number of studies have confirmed that depression and anxiety were closely related to each other among parents of children with cancer [46–48]. Peikert ML et al. also reported that FoP was significantly associated with depression [32]. Here, our results also showed that the level of parental depression was positively correlated with anxiety and FoP, severe anxiety symptoms increase the level of depression. According to the diagnostic criteria for anxiety and depression in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11), it also confirmed that the symptoms of anxiety and depression could interpenetrate each other. Notably, our data showed that increasing number of IVC cycles was an independent risk factor for parental depression (OR = 1.90), and IVC treatment increased the risk of psychological dysfunction from FoP (OR = 708.77), which indicates that children undergoing IVC had a significant impact on parental mental health. This may be due to the severe systemic side effects of systemic IVC in children, including bone marrow transplantation, nausea and vomiting, and diarrhoea. The increasing number of IVC may indicate the poor control of tumor, which indirectly causes a huge psychological burden to parents. Our previously study has showed that the younger the diagnosis age, the more likely the child was to be in an advanced stage, and the risk of undergoing enucleation increased [5]. In this study, we found that age at diagnosis of children with RB was a protective factor for parental depression levels. Thus, parents of children with RB may have more negative feelings and a greater risk of depression. The correlation between APGAR and BDI scores also revealed that good family function may alleviate the depressive symptoms of parents. Family living in urban was an independent risk factor for anxiety and depression, and the level of education attained by parents was also negatively associated with depression. These results suggested that the social demographic information of parents had a certain predictive value for psychological dysfunction. Hence, in the clinical work of RB, parents living in urban or with low educational should be paid more attention.

In this single-centre study, there were several sources of bias or limitations: single centre (in which the sample size was restricted and selection bias was inevitable), Chinese population (reflecting results only from one part of the wider population), cross-sectional study (lacked psychological changes at different points in time), lack psychiatric assessment (lacked a semi-structured or structured measurement tool). Hence, the findings do not represent the overall psychological status of all parents of children with RB in China. In addition, due to the national situation in China, most of the RB parents included in this study were without religious belief, so the findings of this study are not generalizable globally. In future studies, we will use more structured psychometric tools to further assess the psychological functioning of children with RB and explore the interconnectedness of psychosocial outcomes between children and parents in a large series from multiple ocular oncology centres with long-term follow-up. Future research will include a longitudinal assessment of parental psychological functioning to better assess the impact of various variables, including treatment adherence.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study found parents and families of children with RB bear the heavy burden of anxiety, depression, fear of disease progress and assessed the associated factors. Therefore, in clinical practice, more attention needs to be paid to the psychological health of parents in order to identify early symptoms and provide mental services if necessary.
