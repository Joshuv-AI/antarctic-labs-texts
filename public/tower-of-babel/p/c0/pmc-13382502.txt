Misdiagnosed acute Q fever due to Coxiella burnetii in Guangxi China: A case report and literature review
Authors: Xingling Tan, Jinfeng Liao, Zuokun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Coxiella burnetii (C. burnetii), a Gram-negative obligate intracellular bacterium, is the etiological agent of Q fever. Timely diagnosis and initiation of appropriate therapy are critical for favorable clinical outcomes, particularly in cases of acute Q fever. This report describes a case of C. burnetii infection in a 35-year-old male electrical grid engineer who presented with a 4-day history of febrile illness, peaking at 40.0 °C. Associated symptoms included night sweats, chills, anorexia, nausea, non-productive cough, and chest tightness. During hospitalization, the patient also developed headache and vomiting. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of peripheral blood identified C. burnetii as the causative pathogen. The patient was treated with oral doxycycline (100 mg twice daily) for 14 days and levofloxacin (500 mg once daily) for 5 days. Following antimicrobial therapy, the patient's symptoms resolved rapidly, with no clinical evidence of relapse or progression to chronic Q fever during follow-up. This report describes a case of Q fever diagnosed by mNGS. The case highlights the diagnostic value of mNGS in patients presenting with febrile illness of unknown origin. It also serves as a reminder to clinicians that, even in regions where Q fever is enzootic in animals but no human cases have been reported, the possibility of human infection should not be overlooked.

Introduction

Coxiella burnetii is a Gram-negative, obligate intracellular bacterium classified under the genus Coxiella, family Coxiellaceae, and order Legionellales. Commonly referred to as the etiologic agent of Q fever, it was historically associated with the Rickettsiae due to similar intracellular lifestyles [1]. C. burnetii exhibits considerable environmental resilience, remaining viable for extended periods, ranging from months to years, in harsh conditions such as dry tick feces, soil, and dust. This environmental persistence plays a crucial role in the pathogen’s transmission dynamics and the epidemiology of Q fever [2]. Q fever is a globally distributed zoonotic disease caused by C. burnetii, with confirmed cases reported on every continent except New Zealand and Antarctica [3], [4], [5], [6]. In China, Q fever has been documented in 28 provinces, including Heilongjiang, Beijing, Xinjiang, Yunnan, Guangdong, and Zhejiang [7], [8]. However, the disease remains underreported in Guangxi, with only sporadic or isolated cases identified [9].

This case report describes the clinical presentation, diagnostic process, laboratory findings, and therapeutic approach for a patient with C. burnetii infection in Guangxi. Additionally, relevant literature is reviewed to elucidate the clinical significance of this case and to enhance awareness and diagnostic accuracy of Q fever in regions where it remains underrecognized.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old male was admitted to Baise People's Hospital, Guangxi, on January 4, 2025, with a 4-day history of fever. The patient had no prior medical history of surgery, trauma, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or hepatitis. He worked as a power grid engineer and had been engaged in infrastructure installation in rural regions of Baise for the preceding three months. His occupational activities included exposure to cattle, sheep, and their feces, as well as consumption of potentially contaminated food. He had no history of travel outside Guangxi, and no known outbreaks of rickettsial diseases had been reported in his residential area.

The illness began on January 1, 2025, with a high-grade fever peaking at 40 °C, accompanied by night sweats, chills, nausea, anorexia, intermittent dry cough, and occasional chest tightness. He denied chest pain, abdominal pain, palpitations, or peripheral edema. Physical examination at presentation was unremarkable. Initial treatment was sought at a local county hospital, but details of the management were unavailable due to the absence of accessible medical records. Owing to the lack of clinical improvement, he was transferred to Baise People's Hospital on January 4, where his presenting symptoms remained unchanged and physical findings were again within normal limits.

Upon admission, laboratory investigations revealed elevated procalcitonin (PCT) at 0.61 ng/mL, leukocytosis with a predominance of neutrophils, and increased high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) (Table 1). Chest computed tomography (CT) demonstrated: (1) inflammatory infiltrates in the right upper and bilateral lower lobes; (2) bilateral pleural thickening (Figs. 1A, 1B; Figs. 2E, 2F). Electrocardiography findings were normal. Based on clinical and radiological findings, a preliminary diagnosis of non-severe community-acquired pneumonia was established. On January 5, 2025, laboratory investigations revealed evidence of hepatic and renal dysfunction (Table 1). A broad etiological workup for febrile illness was conducted. All of the following results were within normal reference ranges or tested negative: antistreptolysin O (ASO), rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibody (CCP-Ab), hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C virus IgG antibody (HCV-Ab), human immunodeficiency virus antibody (HIV-Ab), Treponema pallidum antibody (anti-T.P.), fecal examination for malarial parasites, dengue virus NS1 antigen, nucleic acid amplification for influenza A and B viruses, respiratory syncytial virus, human rhinovirus, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, antinuclear antibody (ANA) panel, anti-tuberculosis antibody (TB-Ab), liver fluke IgG antibody, Aspergillus galactomannan antigen, and fungal (1−3)-β-D-glucan.On the same day, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) was performed on a peripheral blood sample. Concurrent testing of respiratory pathogen nucleic acids revealed a positive result for adenovirus DNA (Table 1). Based on the working diagnosis of bacterial pneumonia complicated by adenoviral respiratory infection, the patient was empirically started on oseltamivir (75 mg orally, twice daily) in addition to ongoing ceftriaxone therapy.

By January 8, the patient continued to exhibit intermittent low-grade fever (Table 2), now accompanied by new-onset headache, nausea, and vomiting. The mNGS results returned on that day revealed 1152 unique reads mapped to the Gram-negative genus Coxiella, with a relative abundance of 78.38%. Species-level identification confirmed C. burnetii with 1152 sequences and a confidence score of 99% (Fig. 3). Based on these findings, the diagnosis was revised to acute Q fever. Antimicrobial therapy was promptly adjusted: ceftriaxone and oseltamivir were discontinued, and oral doxycycline(100 mg twice daily) was initiated as the recommended first-line treatment.Following three days of oral doxycycline therapy, the patient's body temperature normalized, and systemic symptoms, including headache, nausea, chills, and anorexia, had resolved. HsCRP levels decreased, and serum creatinine returned to within normal reference range. Follow-up chest computed tomography demonstrated significant resolution of the pulmonary inflammatory infiltrates (Figs. 1C, 1D; Figs. 2G, 2H). To mitigate hepatic injury, the patient was administered intravenous compound ammonium glycyrrhizinate (80 mg once daily for three days), in combination with oral glutathione (400 mg daily, divided into three doses). On January 15, repeat laboratory tests demonstrated improvements in hepatic transaminases, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and γ-glutamyltransferase (GGT). However, there was a notable increase in white blood cell (WBC) count and monocyte percentage (Table 1), suggesting that the therapeutic effect of doxycycline is not satisfactory. In order to enhance therapeutic effect, oral levofloxacin (500 mg once daily for five days) was initiated. The patient was subsequently discharged with instructions for outpatient follow-up and continued clinical monitoring.

On January 24, following a full 14-day course of doxycycline, the antibiotic was discontinued, and the hepatoprotective medications were also terminated. At the outpatient follow-up, the patient reported complete resolution of all initial symptoms. Laboratory results showed normalization of WBC count, neutrophil percentage, HsCRP, AST, ALT, and serum creatinine (Scr). Additionally, the monocyte count, platelet count (PLT), HsCRP, and GGT had decreased compared to earlier measurements. However, serum uric acid levels were found to be gradually increasing.

On March 9, during the follow-up visit, the patient reported mild pain in the left wrist joint but denied symptoms such as fever, chills, nausea, vomiting, cough, or chest tightness. Laboratory results revealed a slight elevation in WBC and monocyte count, while uric acid levels continued to rise. Notably, the neutrophil percentage, PLT, AST, ALT, GGT, and Scr remained within normal limits. No clinical or laboratory signs of acute Q fever recurrence or chronic infection were observed. The mild left wrist pain, along with the elevated WBC and monocytes, and further increase in serum uric acid levels on March 9, were attributed to gout rather than a relapse or chronic phase of acute Q fever.

Discussion

C. Burnetii is the etiological agent of acute Q fever, a zoonotic disease characterized by significant environmental resistance. The bacterium can persist in soil, water, and milk for extended periods, ranging from months to years, and exhibits moderate tolerance to ultraviolet light and common disinfectants [10]. Transmission occurs primarily via inhalation of contaminated respiratory aerosols, direct contact with infected animals, or the fecal-oral route [3]. For instance, when animal excreta contaminated with C. burnetii contaminates water sources or soil, and individuals ingest the pathogen after handling contaminated materials without proper hand hygiene, transmission through the digestive system may occur [4], [11]. Populations at increased risk include farmers, laboratory personnel, agricultural workers, and individuals in close contact with livestock [12].

Acute Q fever often presents with nonspecific symptoms, which can lead to misdiagnosis [13]. Key clinical features include fever, chills, headache, myalgia, fatigue, and profuse sweating. Pulmonary involvement may manifest as cough and expectoration. The severity of symptoms varies, typically correlating with the extent of pulmonary inflammation, and may be accompanied by hepatocellular injury, evidenced by elevated transaminases and bilirubin levels, with jaundice occurring in some cases [11]. On physical examination, relative bradycardia is common, and rashes are infrequent and short-lived. Without appropriate treatment, acute Q fever can be fatal, with reported mortality rates ranging from 1% to 2% [14]. Delayed treatment may result in progression to chronic Q fever, which is associated with prolonged treatment regimens, recurrent episodes, and a significantly higher mortality rate [3].

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has gained increasing favor in the diagnosis of acute Q fever, largely due to its broad-spectrum pathogen coverage and high sensitivity in detecting febrile illnesses of unknown origin, enabling accurate identification of the causative pathogen [15], [16].

In the present case, the patient initially presented with symptoms including fever, night sweats, chills, nausea, anorexia, dry cough, and chest tightness. During the course of treatment, additional symptoms such as headache, nausea, and vomiting developed. Relative bradycardia was noted, but there were no signs of scleral or skin jaundice or rash. Upon admission, laboratory results revealed elevated WBC, HsCRP, ALT, AST, GGT, and Scr. The adenovirus nucleic acid test (ADv) was positive (Table 1). Chest CT imaging demonstrated inflammatory exudation at various levels (Fig. 1A, B; Fig. 2E, F). Blood metagenomic pathogen nucleic acid high-throughput gene sequencing results report: Gram-negative bacteria, C. burnetii (Fig. 3). Although the patient's clinical symptoms were non-specific, the epidemiological history revealed exposure to cattle, sheep, and their feces. Combined with the aforementioned abnormal test results, the final diagnosis was acute Q fever.

During treatment, we initially misdiagnosed the condition as non-severe community-acquired pneumonia complicated by adenovirus infection. Both antimicrobial and antiviral medications were administered without improvement. Acute Q fever was not initially considered, as no prior cases of human infection had been reported in Baise. This may be attributable to the widespread use of antibiotics, which could have masked the clinical presentation and obscured the true incidence [17]. Alternatively, it remains possible that human infections had occurred but were not recognized or reported by clinicians at the time. Doxycycline is currently recognized as the most effective treatment for acute Q fever, with its efficacy widely acknowledged [18]. In our case, after one week of doxycycline treatment, the patient showed improvement but developed elevated white blood cell and monocyte counts. Follow-up chest CT scans revealed resolved pulmonary infiltrates, ruling out worsening pulmonary inflammatory exudation. We hypothesized that doxycycline alone did not yield the expected or previously reported efficacy. Therefore, we added levofloxacin tablets for 5 days. One week later, follow-up tests showed normalized white blood cell counts, significantly reduced monocyte counts, and no recurrence of fever.

Conclusion

This case report describes a laboratory-confirmed Q fever infection caused by Coxiella burnetii, which was definitively diagnosed by metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) in Baise City, Guangxi. The patient had a clear history of livestock exposure and showed significant clinical improvement following treatment with a combination regimen of doxycycline and levofloxacin. This case has dual teaching value. Firstly, it underscores that the absence of reported human Q fever cases in a region should not diminish clinical suspicion, particularly in areas with prevalent antibiotic overuse, which may have masked the true incidence of the disease. Secondly, it highlights the valuable role of mNGS in the rapid and accurate identification of fastidious pathogens such as C. burnetii, providing a powerful diagnostic tool for atypical infectious diseases.
