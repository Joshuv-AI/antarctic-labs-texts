Burnout among Polish midwives: mediating role of satisfaction and dispositional optimism
Authors: Agnieszka Zborowska, Joanna Furtak-Pobrotyn, Piotr Pobrotyn, Dorota Krówczyńska, Mariola Głowacka, Stanisław Manulik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Continuous stress, emotional tension, the pace of work, and constant contact with sick people exposes nurses and midwives to professional burnout. Until now, the level of burnout among nurses has been assessed, but research among midwives with an analysis of sociodemographic determinants, including attitudes toward work and life, is still limited. The aim of the study was to assess factors influencing the level of professional burnout among midwives, taking into account job satisfaction, life satisfaction, and dispositional optimism. This cross-sectional observational study was conducted on a group of 98 midwives. Standardized research tools were used to assess professional burnout (Maslach Burnout Inventory, MBI), dispositional optimism (Life Orientation Test, LOT-R), life satisfaction (Satisfaction with Life Scale, SWLS), and job satisfaction (Job Satisfaction Scale, JSS). Depersonalization was significantly higher in individuals with 21 years or more of work experience than in midwives with 0–5 years of seniority (p = 0.023). Lack of job satisfaction was significantly higher among individuals with low life satisfaction than among those with medium and high levels of life satisfaction (p < 0.001), among pessimists compared with neutral individuals (p < 0.001), among those working in a shift system (p = 0.045), and among individuals with less than 21 h of free time per week (p = 0.013). Independent predictors of lower emotional exhaustion burnout were employment in two workplaces (17.98 points). Independent predictors of lower reduced personal accomplishment were job satisfaction (1.638 points) and life satisfaction (24.385 points). An optimistic outlook on life reduced the overall level of professional burnout (13.449 points). The study found that the overall level of professional burnout among midwives was moderate. Professional burnout was associated with individual, interpersonal, and organizational factors. Higher levels of burnout were observed among midwives with a pessimistic outlook.

Introduction

Professional activity can be a source of satisfaction and self-fulfillment for individuals, but it can also lead to frustration and dissatisfaction. The phenomenon of burnout affects those who work in professions involving close interpersonal contact1. One such profession is that of a midwive. This type of professional activity involves providing assistance to others, caring for them, and entails a high level of responsibility. According to some researchers, the so-called mission of the profession can contribute to professional burnout2.

The first person to describe professional burnout was Freudenberger3, who defined burning out as “experiencing failure, depleting one’s strength, and reaching a state of exhaustion due to excessive demands in relation to one’s energy, strength, and resources.” A new definition formulated in collaboration with Richelson indicates that burnout is a state of fatigue or frustration resulting from dedicating oneself to a cause, lifestyle, or relationship without yielding the expected reward4.

However, according to Pines, it is essential to distinguish professional burnout from stress, as stress can lead to depression, routine, and fatigue, but not necessarily burnout5. According to this view, burnout is a response to unmet expectations related to professional work. Competencies play an important role in professional burnout6. Thus, burnout is a consequence of competency gaps in the workplace. Professional burnout occurs not only as a result of stress but also as a response to stress undefined by one’s own coping activity7.

The most frequently cited theory regarding professional burnout in the literature is the concept described by Maslach and Jackson8. According to these researchers, professional burnout is a psychological syndrome of emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment that occurs in individuals working with people in a specific way1,9. As defined by Maslach and Jackson, the burnout syndrome encompasses three dimensions: emotional exhaustion (EE), depersonalization (DP), and reduced personal accomplishment (PA)1.

Professional burnout manifests itself in adverse effects on the work environment. Employees experiencing professional burnout usually exhibit lower performance in comparison to their capabilities and competencies. Moreover, they tend to make more mistakes, become less diligent, and, above all, less engaged, resulting in a range of negative consequences for the institution they work for, as well as for themselves9. Professional burnout is accompanied by a range of symptoms, both physical and psychological, related to work10.

Various sociodemographic and other factors can influence professional burnout, including job satisfaction levels11–13, life satisfaction12,14, and dispositional optimism15. Although this field is not well-explored, there is an increasing effort to identify models pointing to the causes of professional burnout among employees. When searching for causes, the entire work system should be considered, especially in professions with a social mission, such as the nursing and midwivery professions. Mission-driven professions are characterized by a high work ethos, a set of norms, and ideals that individuals are expected to adhere to, in other words, to act in accordance with ethical standards. People working in mission-driven professions demonstrate a high sense of social mission realized in their work2.

Nurses and midwives, as a professional group, are particularly susceptible to professional burnout, as their work is accompanied by continuous stress, emotional tension, quick decision-making, and constant contact with other people7,12,16. Only recently has professional burnout among representatives of mission-driven or helping professions, including nurses and midwives, become a serious issue explored by researchers, especially in the face of the growing shortage of medical personnel. The aim of this study was to assess the factors influencing the phenomenon of professional burnout among midwives, taking into account job satisfaction, life satisfaction, and dispositional optimism.

Methods

Participants and settings

The study employed a convenience sampling strategy. Questionnaires were distributed to midwives attending specialization training, qualification courses, and specialist courses at the European Postgraduate Education Center in Wrocław, Poland. A total of 103 questionnaires were distributed, of which 98 were returned and eligible for analysis, resulting in a response rate of approximately 95%. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Inclusion criteria for the study were a professional experience of more than 1 year and voluntary and informed consent to participate. Exclusion criteria included a lack of consent to participate in the study and less than 1 year of work experience.

Research tools

The study utilized standardized research tools to assess professional burnout – Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), dispositional optimism – Life Orientation Test-Revised (LOT-R), life satisfaction – Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS), and job satisfaction – Job Satisfaction Scale (JSS).

MBI, developed by Maslach and Jackson in 19818, was used in the study with the Polish adaptation by T. Pasikowski17. The tool consists of 22 items related to three aspects of burnout: emotional exhaustion (EE), depersonalization (DP), and reduced personal accomplishment (PA). Results for each subscale are expressed on a scale of 0–100, where a higher score indicates a higher level of professional burnout. Additionally, a general burnout index, the average of the three subscales, is calculated. High scores in emotional exhaustion and DP subscales and low scores in the personal accomplishment subscale suggest professional burnout.

LOT-R, developed by Scheier, Carver, Bridges18, consists of 10 statements, of which 6 are diagnostically relevant, used to assess dispositional optimism. It is designed for both healthy and ill individuals. The internal consistency of the LOT-R test was established on a sample of 174 individuals aged 20–55 years and is 0.76 (Cronbach’s alpha).

SWLS by Diener et al.2 comprises 5 statements evaluated on a seven-point scale. The respondent assesses the extent to which each statement applies to their life. The measurement result is an overall index of subjective life satisfaction.

JSS is a tool used to measure overall job satisfaction19. The instrument consists of five statements related to the work sphere. Responses to each statement are given on a seven-point scale: 1 – strongly disagree; 2 – disagree; 3 – somewhat disagree; 4 – neither agree nor disagree; 5 – somewhat agree; 6 – agree; 7 – strongly agree. The score ranges from 5 to 35 points, with higher scores indicating greater job satisfaction. There are no norms for the JSS questionnaire to determine whether the obtained score is high or low. The JSS scale has a “midpoint”: 20 points (4 points in each question), indicating that the respondent is neither satisfied nor dissatisfied with their job.

Data collection

The questionnaires used in the study were successively distributed to participants in qualification and specialization courses at the European Postgraduate Education Center in Wrocław, Poland. Participation in the study was voluntary and anonymous. Participants were informed by the researcher about the purpose of the study. The researcher personally distributed and collected the completed questionnaires. Subsequently, they were verified for completeness. Among all surveys, 5% were rejected as incomplete.

Ethical considerations

Study participants were informed about the assumptions and course of the study and provided informed consent to participate. Standardized research questionnaires were used for the study. The research project was accepted and approved by the Bioethics Committee at the Wroclaw Medical University (approval no. KB – 208/2018). This study was not registered on a clinical trials registry platform because it was designed a cross-sectional observational study (original research) rather than a clinical trial, and therefore it does not have a clinical trial number. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice.

Data analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), version 3.4.320. Comparison of qualitative variable values (responses to survey questions) between groups was performed using the chi-square test (with Yates’ correction for 2 × 2 tables) or the exact Fisher test where low expected frequencies appeared in the tables. Since the analyzed quantitative variables (questionnaire results) did not have a normal distribution, a comparison between two groups was made using the Mann-Whitney test, and a comparison between three or more groups was made using the Kruskal-Wallis test. If it showed statistically significant differences, post hoc analysis (Dunn’s test) was performed to determine which specific groups differed from each other. Medians, quartiles, and ranges of variable values were presented on the charts. The correlation between them was determined using the Spearman coefficient. Multifactorial analysis of the independent influence of multiple variables on a qualitative variable (MBI questionnaire result) was performed using linear regression. Only variables that proved to have a significant impact in single-factor analyses were included in the multifactorial analysis. Results were presented as regression model parameter values with a 95% confidence interval. The normality of variable distributions was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The significance level was set at 0.05. Thus, all values of p < 0.05 were interpreted as indicating significant relationships.

Results

Characteristics of the studied sample

A total of 98 midwives participated in the study. The largest group consisted of individuals aged 51–60 years (42.86%). The majority of participants were in a relationship (93.88%). Nearly half of them obtained a master’s degree in midwivery (42.86%), while every third person had a bachelor’s degree in midwivery (32.65%). Medical studies were completed by 22.45% of the participants. Most individuals lived in urban areas (approximately 74%) and reported family support with no family problems (79.59%). Influence of individual variables on the level of occupational burnout among midwives

Among socio-demographic variables, age was a significant factor differentiating the level of emotional exhaustion. Midwives aged 51–60 were more emotionally exhausted than those aged 23–30 (Table 3). Emotional exhaustion significantly depended on the ability to relax and life satisfaction. Midwives unable to relax were more emotionally exhausted than those who could relax, and midwives who lacked time for relaxation. Emotional exhaustion was significantly higher among midwives with average life satisfaction than those with high scores on the SWLS scale. Emotional exhaustion significantly depended on work experience, the number of workplaces, and the level of computer and Internet skills. To verify which response categories significantly differed in terms of emotional exhaustion, post-hoc analysis using the Dunn’s test was conducted. Emotional exhaustion was significantly higher among midwives with 21 years and more of work experience than those with 0–5 years of experience. Midwives working in one workplace were more emotionally exhausted than those working in two places. Midwives reporting good and insufficient computer and Internet skills were more emotionally exhausted compared to those with excellent and very good skills in this area. Midwives expressing a need for burnout prevention workshops were more emotionally exhausted than those not expressing such a need. Midwives maintaining neutral relationships with doctors were more emotionally exhausted than those having positive relationships with them. Midwives declaring less than 21 h of free time per week were more emotionally exhausted than those with more than 21 h of free time from work.

Multiple regression analysis, a method for assessing the independent impact of multiple variables on the results of the MBI questionnaire, was used to analyze the various dimensions of burnout. Only variables that were found to have a significant impact in intergroup comparison analyses were included in the regression models. The results are presented as regression model parameter values with a 95% confidence interval. In the case of depersonalization, only years of work experience differentiated the participants, so this dimension was not considered in the multiple regression analysis.

The linear regression model showed that the only independent predictor of the level of emotional exhaustion burnout was the number of workplaces (p < 0.05) (Table 5). Compared to having one job, having two jobs decreased the level of burnout by an average of 17.98 points. The remaining 49.83% depended on variables not included in the model and random factors.

Discussion

The well-being of healthcare workers is primarily associated with job satisfaction levels21. Job dissatisfaction can lead to various disorders, including burnout syndrome. The aim of the study was to assess the level of occupational burnout and determine the factors influencing the burnout of the surveyed midwives. Reduced PA, as measured by the MBI, was significantly associated with job satisfaction assessed using the JSS, indicating that occupational burnout and job satisfaction represent related but distinct constructs.

In our study, the MBI questionnaire was used to assess the level of occupational burnout, yielding an overall burnout score of 29.37. Paul et al.‘s study22 showed that moderate or high levels of personal burnout and work-related burnout were present in 48.3% and 38.2% of midwives, respectively. Burnout related to clients was less common, with 23.3% of midwives experiencing moderate or high levels. On the other hand, De la Fuente-Solana’s study23 found that 17% of participants presented high levels of EE, 16.6% high levels of DP, and 55.1% a reduced PA. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Suleiman-Martos et al.24 based on 14 articles covering a total of 8,959 midwives showed that midwives presented a moderate level of occupational burnout. Most midwives exhibited personal and work-related burnout, indicating a high risk of burnout development. The highest level of occupational burnout was reported in the lack of job satisfaction subscale, followed by the EE subscale, with the fewest participants showing DP symptoms. Based on the results, it can be concluded that the surveyed groups were slightly dissatisfied with job satisfaction assessed by the JSS questionnaire. In contrast, regarding life satisfaction, most participants indicated high life satisfaction, but 37.05% rated it as low.

Personality characterizes the cognitive and emotional assessment of professional work, which can affect the subjective level of job satisfaction. One important personality trait in this context is optimism25. Moreover, individuals with dispositional optimism are characterized by more effective work strategies, focusing on practical problem-solving rather than problem searching. An optimistic employee is more willing to seek support from others and has a higher level of trust not only in people but also in the organization they work for, which, in turn, translates into the effectiveness of care. This is because an optimistically oriented and satisfied individual will actively seek solutions in problematic situations and, therefore, less frequently apply avoidance or withdrawal mechanisms26. In the context of these data, the assessment of dispositional optimism in our analysis is interesting, where 34.20% of the participants tended toward pessimism, and 28.53% toward optimism. The remaining group had a neutral orientation. Optimism significantly decreased the level of occupational burnout, as confirmed by the findings from the studies mentioned earlier.

The results obtained in the study confirm the alarming phenomenon related to the growing aging process among medical personnel, including midwives. Currently, the midwivery profession is rapidly aging, with less interest in this profession among young women and men27. In the analyzed group, age had a significant impact on the level of midwive burnout, with burnout being significantly higher in the older group (51–60 years). A meta-analysis by Suleiman-Martos et al.24 showed that young, less experienced, and lone midwives exhibited higher levels of occupational burnout, possibly related to low practical skills and a lack of emotional support. Additionally, factors influencing a higher level of occupational burnout were a lack of resources, work environment, and the care model employed. Paul et al.‘s study22 found that midwives with a moderate or high level of occupational burnout were more likely to be aged 25–34 compared to all other age groups. Previous systematic research reviews24,28 indicate that a higher level of occupational burnout is common among younger midwives. Personal factors and working conditions should, therefore, be taken into account when assessing the risk profiles of midwive occupational burnout.

Shift work can affect the quality of life, both personally and professionally29,30. Twelve-hour shifts and irregular working hours are considered factors predisposing to the development of occupational burnout30. While shift work is often considered a potential risk factor for DP, the present findings did not confirm a statistically significant association between work system and DP levels in the studied group. Furthermore, midwives working in a shift system experienced a lack of professional satisfaction and were more professionally burned out. Shift work, especially the 12-hour workday, is considered a predisposing factor for the development of professional burnout and is even referred to as a stressogenic factor31. Work experience was a factor influencing professional burnout in the domain of lack of professional satisfaction. Individuals most burned out in the domain of lack of professional satisfaction were those with work experience of up to 5 years. Additionally, Haor et al.32 suggest that an increase in work experience leads to a decrease in positive attitudes toward work and life satisfaction. On the contrary, other studies analyzing the impact of work experience on professional burnout show that the longer the work experience, the lower the professional burnout, aligning with the results of our own research33. The analysis of previous studies may suggest that younger individuals with a shorter work experience may experience symptoms of professional burnout more quickly, possibly due to confronting reality and having expectations without professional experience. These data may indicate that individuals with longer work experience may develop defense and adaptive mechanisms.

Lack of support from superiors, poor relationships with superiors, lack of support, and good collaboration with colleagues can contribute to the development of professional burnout. Team relationships are mentioned as factors influencing professional burnout. The mentioned determinants contributed to an increase in the level of professional burnout in our study in almost every subscale of the MBI. In the surveyed group of midwives, the number of workplaces influenced emotional exhaustion. Having two workplaces was associated with lower burnout levels. On the other hand, lower satisfaction from the JSS questionnaire, family problems, relationships with superiors, and the lack of opportunities for improvement in verbal and non-verbal communication had a negative impact on professional burnout in the DP subscale. Among midwives, only work experience influenced DP. Work experience, where experience exceeding 20 years reduced the level of professional burnout in this domain, influenced the lack of professional satisfaction. Furthermore, improvement in verbal and non-verbal communication, relaxation skills, and dispositional optimism reduced the level of professional burnout. Studies conducted in the United Kingdom34 and Australia35 showed that midwives who left the profession expressed dissatisfaction with employment conditions and workload. In our study, the impact of weekly working hours on the level of professional burnout was not confirmed. Different results are presented in the study by Paul et al.22, where compared to midwives with a low level of professional burnout, midwives with a moderate or high level of professional burnout worked more hours per week, more often experienced a significant reduction in the number of working hours and workload, or did not work as midwives in the last 5 years.

In summary, a variety of factors can influence the occurrence of professional burnout. Its causes are related to the nature of the work performed, which is particularly relevant in nursing and midwivery professions. Often, workers themselves are blamed for the professional burnout they experience, and therefore, appropriate preventive actions are not taken. Typically, corrective actions are only initiated in crisis situations and when the process of burnout intensifies. These actions focus on strengthening individual coping mechanisms with stress, without attempting to change what triggers professional burnout. This results in burnout affecting the most dedicated workers and brings a range of negative consequences for them, the institutions they work for, and the service recipients, i.e., the patients.

There is still a large area not only for research on professional burnout but also for raising awareness and strengthening individual and organizational resources that can be a remedial measure. The present study showed that actions in this field should still be undertaken. The study by Doherty et al.36, aimed at examining the concept of professional burnout among midwives and determining their perspectives on how to reduce it, showed that midwives participating in thematic workshops on professional burnout thoroughly examined the emotional nature of midwivery practice and its impact on the level of professional burnout among midwives. It can be concluded that midwives require a high level of self-awareness to identify external demands that make them more susceptible to burnout and to use their own positive coping mechanisms. However, without the involvement of healthcare systems to reduce chronic work overload, the level of professional burnout will remain high, negatively affecting midwives and the patients under their care.

The findings of this study have important implications for healthcare policy and organizational interventions aimed at improving midwives’ well-being. Strategies focusing on enhancing job satisfaction, promoting positive workplace relationships, and supporting psychological resources such as optimism may contribute to reducing burnout levels. Organizational measures, including workload management, improved shift scheduling, access to mental health support, and structured burnout prevention programs, should be considered by healthcare administrators. Furthermore, fostering supportive leadership and team-based work environments may mitigate emotional exhaustion and disengagement among midwives.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size and the use of a convenience sample recruited from a single educational center may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of midwives in Poland and other healthcare systems. Future studies should involve larger, multi-center samples to enhance external validity. Second, the cross-sectional observational design of the study does not allow for causal inferences regarding the relationships between burnout, job satisfaction, life satisfaction, and dispositional optimism. Longitudinal research is needed to better understand the temporal dynamics and potential causal pathways among these variables. Third, the reliance on self-reported questionnaires may introduce response bias, including social desirability bias and recall bias. Additionally, participation was voluntary, which may have resulted in selection bias, as individuals experiencing either particularly high or low levels of burnout may have been more motivated to participate. Finally, the findings should be interpreted within the cultural and organizational context of the Polish healthcare system. Differences in healthcare structures, working conditions, and cultural perceptions of work-related stress may limit the direct transferability of the results to midwives in other countries. Further cross-cultural studies are recommended to examine the universality of the observed relationships.

Conclusions

The study found that the overall level of professional burnout among midwives was moderate. Professional burnout was associated with individual, interpersonal, and organizational factors. Higher levels of burnout were observed among midwives with a pessimistic outlook.
