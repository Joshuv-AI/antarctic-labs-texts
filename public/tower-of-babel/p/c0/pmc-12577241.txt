The Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS): psychometric properties of a Mongolian version in university students
Authors: Dorjnambar Balgansuren, Binderiya Bayanmunkh, Hongru Zhu, Naidan Bat-Ulzii, Enkhjin Bat-Erdene, Bayarmaa Tsend, Nobuyuki Sudo, Nomin-Erdene Shirchin, Battuvshin Lkhagvasuren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding affective states is essential in psychology and psychiatry, yet few validated scales remain for evaluating affect in Mongolia. The Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) is widely used globally, but cultural and linguistic differences necessitate its validation for specific populations. This study aimed to assess the psychometric properties of the Mongolian PANAS among university students, a group navigating academic and socio-emotional challenges where emotional well-being is crucial.MethodsAfter a comprehensive translation and back-translation procedure with expert validation, we administered the 20-item Mongolian PANAS to 480 university students (mean age = 20.5, SD = 2.0; 54% female). We used exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess structural validity, Cronbach’s alpha to evaluate internal consistency, and test-retest reliability over 14 days to determine temporal stability. Item-scale correlations were examined to test construct validity. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26, Amos version 29, and RStudio.ResultsThe factor analysis supported a two-factor structure, consistent with the original PANAS, which explained 44.9% of the total variance and demonstrated a moderate model fit (CFI = 0.905, RMSEA = 0.066). The scale exhibited good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.788) and strong temporal stability (ICC = 0.806–0.831), confirming its reliability in measuring affect among Mongolian university students.ConclusionThe Mongolian PANAS is a psychometrically sound tool for assessing affective states in university students, which supports its use in research and educational settings. However, additional refinement may be needed to enhance its cultural sensitivity and applicability. Future research should explore its use in clinical populations and longitudinal studies to assess its predictive validity over time.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03514-1.

Background

Emotions are crucial to the human experience, shaping our perceptions, behaviors, and general well-being. The systematic study of affective states has become increasingly important in psychological research, particularly through the lens of Watson et al.‘s two-factor model, which identifies positive affect (PA) and negative affect (NA) as core dimensions of emotional experience. The Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS), derived from this theoretical framework, has evolved as one of the most extensively used tools for evaluating various affective dimensions across diverse populations [1]. While first thought to be bidimensional, research shows more complicated structures, including a bifactor model and differences depending on valence and activation [2, 3]. Cross-cultural studies affirm PANAS’s reliability, but cultural differences can affect factor structures and item interpretations [4, 5]. Given that emotional expression and interpretation are shaped by cultural norms, psychometric validation is crucial when adapting such measures to new contexts [6, 7]. Given Mongolia’s distinct socio-cultural framework, validating PANAS within this population is essential to ensure its reliability and applicability.

Understanding affective states is particularly important in university students, as this group faces unique emotional challenges shaped by academic demands, shifting social roles, and cultural expectations [8]. Emotion regulation has been identified as a critical factor in maintaining psychological well-being during such transitions [9]. In Mongolia, research has highlighted significant mental health concerns, with 14.5% of college students experiencing clinically significant depression and nearly half reporting suicidal ideation, underscoring the urgent need for psychological assessment tools [10]. Moreover, evidence shows that structured emotional regulation strategies can improve emotional control and reduce distress, reinforcing the necessity for culturally appropriate affect measures [11].

Adolescence and early adulthood are key times for learning emotional control abilities. New study connects poor emotion control throughout these years to psychiatric problems, such as behavioral addictions [12], emphasizing the value of reliable evaluation techniques for early detection and intervention. Given the high prevalence of depression and suicidal thoughts among Mongolian students, validated assessments like PANAS may aid to early screening and focused therapies in this population [10]. Several research studies validating culturally adapted psychological measures have highlighted the need to guarantee reliability and validity within specific linguistic and cultural contexts [11, 13]. The contemporary context of increasing digitalization has introduced new complexities to emotional experiences, with excessive online engagement and cyber-related stressors contributing to negative affective states among young adults [14]. Mindfulness and psychological toughness have been highlighted as particularly crucial for university students handling these new problems in addition to traditional academic and social expectations [12]. The COVID-19 pandemic’s influence on student mental health emphasizes the significance of having validated tools to reliably assess emotional states during moments of heightened stress [15]. A validated PANAS could be useful in identifying at-risk students and assessing the efficacy of specialized interventions, such as stress management or mindfulness programs designed specifically for Mongolian youths.

The PANAS instrument specifically addresses the need for robust affect measurement through its well-established two-factor structure [16, 17]. It is a brief but comprehensive and user-friendly scale with two sets of 10 items, each measuring a distinct emotional dimension. PA items include interested, excited, strong, enthusiastic, proud, alert, inspired, determined, attentive, and active. The Negative affect items consist of distressed, upset, guilty, scared, hostile, irritable, ashamed, nervous, jittery, and afraid [1].

Originally developed in North America, PANAS has demonstrated strong psychometric properties across cultural and linguistic contexts [18–21]. Validations in China, Canada, India, Italy, Norway, Brazil, Romania, Russia, Serbia, and Spain have generally supported its two-factor structure, internal consistency, and external validity [16, 17, 20, 22–26]. While cross-cultural studies support the PANAS’s general adaptability, direct translations may not fully capture intended emotional constructs [27]. This highlights the importance of cultural adaptation and rigorous validation in Mongolia, where language, social conventions, and patterns of emotional expression may influence responses. Population-specific validation remains necessary to ensure reliability and conceptual accuracy.

In recent years, Mongolia has made progress in validating psychological assessment tools, though gaps remain. Various psychometric measures have been successfully developed to fit Mongolian populations, indicating the country’s rising interest in mental health [28]. Among these tools, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) has adequate internal consistency (α = 0.76) and substantial associations with anxiety, depression, and quality of life variables [29]. Similarly, the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) has strong reliability, with α values of 0.82 for anxiety and 0.80 for depression, indicating a solid assessment of mental distress [30]. The WHOQOL-BREF, a popular scale for evaluating quality of life, has excellent internal consistency (α = 0.89), indicating its usefulness [31]. Additionally, the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) has demonstrated moderate discriminant validity, making it a suitable instrument for screening child mental health in the Mongolian environment [32].

Despite these advancements, Mongolia still lacks a validated measure of fundamental affective states, limits research on emotional well-being and clinical applications. While instruments such as the PSQI, HADS, and WHOQOL-BREF have been effectively adapted, there is no culturally validated tool for measuring affective states. PANAS has been verified among university students in several countries, including China, Germany, and the United States, thus, its adaption for Mongolian students would cover a crucial gap in both research and mental health evaluation [33–35].

A validated PANAS for Mongolian university students would offer a crucial tool for research and mental health screening. As digitalization and socio-cultural changes reshape student experiences, understanding affective states becomes increasingly important. The effective adaption of PANAS could allow for longitudinal studies on emotional development, cross-cultural comparisons, and the evaluation of mental health therapies targeted to Mongolian young people. As a result, this study aims to describe the PANAS’s psychometric features in a sample of Mongolian university students, addressing a crucial demand for accurate and validated emotional assessment instruments in the Mongolian setting.

Methods

Participants

The study involved 585 students from the Mongolian National University of Education (MNUE) and the University of Internal Affairs of Mongolia (UIAM) in Ulaanbaatar, Mongolia. The initial recruiting was conducted using a non-random, convenience sampling method. This sample method was chosen based on practical issues such as student availability and participation willingness. We worked with two big, accessible universities, where professors helped with recruitment. These universities were chosen because of their large student numbers and accessibility to the study team, resulting in efficient data collecting.

The final sample comprised 480 participants (54.2% women; age: 20.5 ± 2.17 years). The following inclusion criteria were used in the study: (1) being over 18 years of age; (2) being a student at a university in Ulaanbaatar, Mongolia; (3) speaking Mongolian fluently; and (4) understanding and accepting the contents of the written informed consent, expressed by signing it. The exclusion criteria were (1) having a known neurological or mental disorder, and (2) being unable to offer informed consent [28]. The sample size was determined following established psychometric guidelines. Factor analysis recommendations suggest an item-to-participant ratio of at least 10:1, meaning a minimum of 200 participants for the 20-item PANAS scale [36, 37]. However, to improve generalizability and ensure reliable factor structures, a larger sample size of 500 was targeted.

Additionally, as the study involved a test-retest reliability component, potential attrition was considered. Psychological studies often report attrition rates of 10–20% [38], which could impact ICC estimates. Prior research suggests that 200 + participants are needed for stable ICC estimates [39, 40]. Therefore, we initially recruited 585 participants to ensure a final sample of approximately 500 after accounting for exclusions due to absence, illness, or refusal to complete the retest.

We conducted a post-hoc power analysis using GPower 3.1, using parametric tests (t-tests for two groups, ANOVA for ≥ 3 groups) as benchmarks. For moderate effects (d = 0.5 for t-tests, f = 0.25 for ANOVA) with α = 0.05 and 80% power, the analysis indicated that 478 participants were needed. Data collection

Data were obtained from the aforementioned universities during February and March 2024. Participants were given the option to complete the PANAS online (Google Forms) or on paper. Both formats used identical item wording and instructions, and paper responses were later entered into the same database for analysis. Of the 480 eligible participants, 70 completed the questionnaire online and 410 on paper. Because both formats employed identical items and instructions, no differences between administration modes were expected. Before data collection, all research assistants received standardized training on survey administration processes, informed consent protocols, and uniform responses to participant questions. This training ensured consistent implementation across formats and locations [28].

To ensure data quality, all questionnaires were conducted in controlled classroom environments with minimal external interruptions. Trained research assistants were present during data collection to assist participants and answer questions. Each completed questionnaire was checked for missing or conflicting information, and double data input was used to minimize errors. Within two weeks following the initial administration, all 480 participants completed the PANAS questionnaire again to evaluate test-retest reliability.

Measures

The original PANAS questionnaire, developed by Watson et al., consists of 10 items measuring positive emotional states and 10 items measuring negative emotional states, rated on a 5-point Likert scale [1]. The negative and positive subscales are mostly independent. The 20 items are rated on a scale ranging from 1 (“not at all”) to 5 (“always”). The participants were asked to rate how they felt (a) “right now (at the present moment)”; (b) “today”; (c) “over the past few days”; (d) “over the past weeks”; (e) “over the past few weeks”; (f) “over the past year”; and (g) “in general, or on average” [41]. This study used the “during the past weeks” time frame reference (see Additional file).

Mongolian translation of the PANAS

The translation process aims to guarantee that the translated scale has the same meaning and context as the original. We translated the PANAS into Mongolian according to the criteria for cross-cultural adaptation using the following steps: Two translators separately translated the items into Mongolian (one with an MA in psychology and another with a PhD in linguistics), and the translation was compiled into a single version with an emphasis on word choice that best suited the original version [28]. A pilot test was conducted on December 26, 2023, with 50 undergraduate students to evaluate their understanding of the questions, ability to respond, and the overall applicability of the scale. Two independent translators, who were unfamiliar with the original scale, did a back-translation, which revealed no major differences. During the review of pilot test results and back-translation, the team identified that Item 7 (“scared”) translated as “айсан” and Item 20 (“afraid”) as “айж түгшсэн” conveyed nearly identical meanings of fear in Mongolian, failing to distinguish the nuanced differences in the original scale where “afraid” encompasses broader concepts including chronic worry and outcome apprehension. After expert committee discussion, Item 20 was retranslated as “санаа зовох, түгших” to capture this full meaning better. Based on the pilot test and back-translation findings, an expert committee analyzed the item translations before completing the Mongolian version of the PANAS (M-PANAS) for use in the research [28].

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, as it is suitable for larger sample sizes and helps determine whether the data deviate significantly from a normal distribution [42]. To assess between-group differences, we employed the Kruskal-Wallis H test (for comparisons of ≥ 3 groups) or the Mann-Whitney U test (for two groups), as the data did not meet the assumptions for parametrics tests (normality and homogeneity of variances). Non-parametric tests are more suitable for ordinal data or when data distributions are skewed or contain outliers [43]. All variables were analyzed using descriptive statistics such as frequencies (n), percentages (%), means, and standard deviations (SD) [42].

We tested the reliability of the PANAS by checking its internal consistency, where Cronbach’s α ≥ 0.7 or higher was considered acceptable [44]. We also looked at how well each item related to the overall construct through item-scale correlation analysis. A high item-scale correlation implies that the item consistently measures the construct, whereas a low item-scale correlation indicates that it does not [45]. Test-retest reliability was assessed over two weeks using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) using a two-factor mixed-effects model, with ICC values interpreted under published recommendations (e.g., moderate reliability: 0.5–0.75) [46].

Additionally, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted using principal axis factoring with varimax rotation, as this method is appropriate for identifying underlying factors while minimizing cross-loadings [47]. Suitability for EFA was confirmed using Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < 0.001) and the KMO measure of sampling adequacy (KMO >0.65) [48]. Factors with eigenvalues exceeding 1 were retained for further analysis [49]. To validate the factor structure, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed using maximum likelihood estimation, These thresholds are commonly accepted for ensuring adequate model fit in psychological research [47]. The model’s fit was tested using the chi-square test and fit indices, including RMSEA (< 0.05), CFI (≥ 0.8), and TLI (≥ 0.8) [28, 50].

For general statistical procedures, all analyses were carried out using JAMOVI (version 2.2.5). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated using SPSS (version 26), and the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was done using Amos (version 29). The sample was split into subsets using RStudio. Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05 for two-tailed tests [51].

Results

Participant characteristics and preliminary analysis

The total sample consisted of 480 Mongolian university students, comprising 220 men and 260 women, with a mean age of 20.46 years (SD = 1.68; range: 18–38). Most participants were unmarried (96.7%) and had completed secondary or vocational education (76.7%) before entering university. The sample included students from all academic years, with the largest proportion in their second year, followed by fourth, third, and first years. The majority were majoring in education sciences (58.3%) or internal affairs (36.5%), while a smaller group (5.2%) studied military study major. In terms of socioeconomic status, students reported a range of family income levels, with the largest group identifying as middle-income. Living conditions also varied, with most residing in apartments, while others lived in traditional Mongolian dwellings (gers) or dormitories. Post-hoc confirmatory factor analysis

Since the initial models showed poor fit, modifications were made by allowing error terms of theoretically related items to correlate. Specifically, items with similar content—such as “excited” and “proud” (positive affect) and “distressed,” “irritable,” “nervous,” and “ashamed” (negative affect)—were permitted to correlate, improving model fit. These items also met the requirements for detecting causes of model mismatch, with modification indices exceeding the essential threshold of p < 0.05. The model suggested acceptable fit (χ² = 372.67; CFI = 0.904; TLI = 0.891; RMSEA = 0.072; SRMR = 0.083; see Fig. 2B for factor loadings).

Bootstrapping analysis

Multivariate normality was assessed using Mardia’s test, which revealed significant non-normality (multivariate kurtosis = 96.829, critical ratio = 25.284). To address this, we applied bootstrapping with 2,000 resamples to generate robust parameter estimates and standard errors, ensuring the reliability of the CFA results. Results showed stable maximum likelihood (ML) discrepancy between the implied and sample covariance matrices (Mean = 581.993, SE = 1.260). The Kullback-Leibler (K-L) overoptimism values also remained acceptable (stabilized mean = 155.727, SE = 1.309). These findings suggest that the model modifications did not lead to overfitting and that parameter estimates were robust despite minor deviations from normality.

Furthermore, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to determine how well the two subscales represented the PANAS’s latent structure. Convergent and Discriminant validity

Convergent validity was not supported. Both PA (ω = 0.42, AVE = 0.42) and NA (ω = 0.46, AVE = 0.46) fell below recommended thresholds (ω ≥ 0.70, AVE ≥ 0.50). In contrast, discriminant validity was supported. The square root of AVE for PA (0.65) and NA (0.68) was higher than their correlation (– 0.27), and the HTMT ratio (0.29) was well below the cutoff of 0.85.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the psychometric characteristics of the Mongolian version of the 20-item PANAS among university students. This is the first study to assess the validity of the PANAS in a Mongolian university context, contributing to the country’s growing body of psychometric research.

The Mongolian version of the PANAS showed strong content validity, as indicated by expert evaluations. A broad consensus among assessors supported the conclusion that the translation and adaptation process was successful, ensuring the items were culturally relevant and easily understood.

Psychometric analysis revealed robust measurement characterictics. Inter-item correlations were within acceptable ranges, indicating that items within each subscale reliably measured the same underlying constructs. Parallel exploratory factor analysis supported the hypothesized two-component model of affect, comprising distinct PA and NA dimensions. This aligns with the theoretical framework proposed by Watson et al., reinforcing the bidimensional structure of affect. The scale demonstrated strong internal consistency in our study (Cronbach’s α: PA = 0.91, NA = 0.88), aligning with previous findings (e.g., Lee et al., : PA = 0.91, NA = 0.94) [16, 52]. Confirmatory factor analysis supported the established two-factor structure, consistent with findings from international studies [20, 52]. The moderate factor loadings for PA (mean = 0.61) and NA (mean = 0.68) items further support its structural integrity. The scale also showed good test-retest over 14 days, consistent with reports from other populations (e.g., Argentine athletes: PA = 0.91, NA = 0.87) [53], supporting its suitability for longitudinal and clinical use.

While item-scale correlations confirmed subscale specificity, the moderate Cronbach’s alpha suggests response variability, potentially attributable to age-related affective fluctuations in our young sample [20, 52].

Furthermore, we find the negative correlation between PA and NA (r = −0.32, p < 0.01), which contrasts with Watson and colleagues’ original finding of orthogonality between these two dimensions. The negative correlation observed in this study is consistent with findings from several cross-cultural studies, though notable differences exist in the strength and interpretation of this relationship. For instance, Lee et al. reported a weaker negative correlation (r = −0.09) in Singaporean and American samples, supporting the view that PA and NA are largely independent but can exhibit slight inverse relationships depending on cultural context [52]. Similarly, Vera-Villarroel et al. (2019) found weak to moderate negative correlations in Chilean non-clinical samples (r = −0.066 to r = −0.042) and Spanish adolescents and young people with depressive symptomatology [17, 20], suggesting that PA and NA remain mostly independent in general populations but may become more interrelated in clinical groups.

In contrast, the negative correlation in our sample implies that cultural characteristics, such as limited emotional expressiveness, may influence affective experiences. This is consistent with the findings of an Italian research [16], where a weak negative correlation (r = −0.09) was attributed to cultural differences in emotional expression.

The negative correlation aligns more closely with Russell’s (1980) circumplex model, which suggests that emotional dimensions interact rather than stay orthogonal [54, 55]. This interaction may be influenced by Mongolian cultural norms, where emotional regulation plays a significant role in maintaining social harmony [56]. In collectivist societies, both positive and negative emotions may be perceived as interconnected rather than as separate, unidimensional constructs [57]. Further support for this interpretation comes from studies by Mesquita and Markus (2004), who found that in collectivist societies, the regulation of both positive and negative emotions is intertwined [58]. Experiencing high levels of positive affect may require greater emotional effort to regulate negative affect, potentially leading to a negative correlation. Similarly, research in other East Asian cultures, such as Japan and China, has shown that emotional experiences are less dichotomous [59], with positive and negative emotions coexisting in a more interrelated manner.

Our study observed differences in affective responses between genders. Male students reported stronger positive affect and lower negative affect than female students. The gender differences observed in our study are consistent with findings from international research [60, 61]. A large-scale study in 37 countries reported significant differences in affective experiences between genders, with women generally reporting more NA than men do [62]. Moreover, differences in affective responses between university majors appear to be influenced by gender imbalances and academic workload [63]. Notably, education majors (mostly female in our sample) reported much higher negative affect than male-dominated internal affairs disciplines. While this pattern might indicate either (1) gender composition differences, (2) discipline-specific stress variables, or (3) their interplay. Future studies should address these potential mechanisms more directly.

Despite these findings, the study has certain limitations that should be addressed in future research. First, while the sample size was adequate for initial validation, it was very small and confined to university students, limiting the results’ generalizability to other groups. Future research should strive for a bigger and more varied sample that better represents other demographic groups, including non-student populations, in order to improve the external validity of the results. Because this study only included students from two national institutions, the findings may not be generalizable. As a result, caution should be given when applying these findings to different age groups, social circumstances, or cultural contexts. Second, convenience sampling involves possible selection bias, which may fail to capture the diversity of experiences and emotional expressions in the general population. This might have had an influence on the factor structure’s resilience as well as the strength of the observed correlations. Future study should focus on random selection procedures to achieve a more representative and unbiased sample.

Third, the gender imbalance in our sample—predominantly female education majors—may have affected the results, as female students often face higher academic stress and may report different affective responses compared to their male counterparts. Cultural norms around emotional expression in Mongolia could further shape responses. Future research should ensure balanced sampling and examine gender-specific stress and cultural effects. Fourth, the moderate Cronbach’s alpha coefficient suggests some variability in item responses, which may reflect the developmental stage of the sample. This fluctuation might be attributed to the naturally varying emotional states of younger people, which may affect the scale’s reliability. Fifth, convergent validity was not supported in this study, as composite reliability and average variance extracted for both PA and NA fell below recommended thresholds. While the scale showed good discriminant validity, the weak convergent validity suggests that some items may not strongly represent their intended factors. To improve the construct validity of the scale, future studies should re-examine the factor structure and improve the item wording in larger, more varied populations. Overall, these limitations, together with our findings, underline the importance of further refining and validating the PANAS in order to adequately capture the complexities of affect across varied cultural settings.

Conclusion

The Mongolian PANAS demonstrates strong potential as a reliable and valid measure of affective states in university student populations. Its factor structure and temporal stability support its adoption for research and educational applications. While not yet validated for clinical use, this instrument offers researchers a psychometrically sound tool for assessing emotion in Mongolian youth. Future studies should expand validation to more diverse samples and cultural contexts to strengthen its utility.
