A Preliminary Exploration of the Behavioural Criteria of Ethical Care With Dementia
Authors: Yuqin Pan, Ruyi Chen, Guiyuan Huang, Jia Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo develop a format of the behavioural criteria of ethical care with dementia so as to support nursing pedagogy and ethical practice in dementia care.DesignLiterature review, group workshop and Delphi method.MethodsThis project underwent three stages of literature review, initial construction and Delphi method from June 2020 to June 2023. Nine databases were searched with key words for articles relevant to ethical care with dementia that were published before 2022. Johns Hopkins Evidence Level and Quality Guidelines were employed for quality appraisal. A process of highlighting, comparing, merging and categorising was employed by two reviewers for data synthesis. The original behavioural criteria of ethical care with dementia were constructed by team members based on literature review and group discussions. Fifteen experts were invited and two rounds of consultation were completed. Statistics were generated for comprehensive evaluations. Revisions were made with expert suggestions to obtain a final format.ResultsA total of 3299 titles and abstracts were initially retrieved, 242 full text articles were reviewed and 15 articles were included, with evidence type of I to V and evidence level b. Ten ethical principles and 12 categories of ethical issues closely relevant to dementia care were achieved by syntheses. An initial format of the behavioural criteria was developed for the Delphi method. Modestly acceptable correspondence was obtained by two rounds of expert consultation. The final format of the criteria included 10 ethical principles, 37 ethical issues and their corresponding cases, scenarios and three modalities (the recommended, the obscure and the false) of ethical care behaviours with dementia. Therefore, this format of the case‐based narrative behavioural criteria could be useful for training the nurses' competencies of ethical reasoning and setting the professional boundaries of nurses' ethical care behaviours, which could facilitate nurses in their pedagogical and clinical practice of ethical care with dementia.Patient or Public ContributionsNo patient or public contributions.Keywords: behaviour, criteria, dementia, ethical care, nurses

Introduction

The prevalence of dementia globally remains high with accelerated ageing and increased longevity, and the number of dementia cases will be tripled to 152.8 million in 2050 (Collaborators 2022). Dementia is a disease characteristic of progressive cognitive decline which ultimately results in the loss of independence and decision‐making capability among clients. Numerous ethical issues such as negligence, abuse and violation of privacy and autonomy have been reported in the contexts of dementia care, which led to negative health consequences for the clients, undermined the professionalism of care services and brought about adverse social impacts (Götzelmann et al. 2021). Debates on the ethical practice in dementia care have been ongoing and generated useful suggestions. Nevertheless, practical norms or criteria of ethical care conduct with dementia are lacking in supporting nurses to deal with ethical issues or conflicts (Schou‐Juul et al. 2023; Soofi 2022). Therefore, the ethical care for people with dementia has raised a great challenge to clinical nurses which needs urgent attention.

Ethical theories or principles are often abstract or even contradictory when being applied in scenarios of dementia care, leaving the experienced nurses in disputes and the novices in puzzling (Byers 2021; Howes and Gastmans 2021; Tanaka 2023). For example, tube feeding for the terminal clients with dementia is advocated from the perspective of deontology which focuses on the carers' behaviours attaching to their duties. Whereas, consequentialism proposes on the outcomes of the carers' behaviours, and an opposite ethical decision of minimising the clients' suffering may be reached (Chetna et al. 2021). Similarly, the applications of the four ethical principles as non‐maleficence, beneficence, justice and respect have never been straightforward. For example, had justice been upheld for the clients with dementia during a shortage of care resources? Had refraining family members from visiting the clients reflected beneficence during the pandemic of COVID (Cipriani et al. 2022; Cousins et al. 2021)? Surely, no clear‐cut answers could be given due to the complexity of ethical scenarios in dementia care, and the ethical theories are underpinned with different philosophical stands. Thus, nurses should be able to negotiate and balance among various ethical considerations so as to initiate proper ethical care behaviours.

Despite the theoretical barrier, individual, organisational or systemic factors may also hamper the nurses' choices of ethical conducts to the clients with dementia (Hanssen and Tran 2019). For example, Portacolone et al. (2020) indicated that the values or beliefs of the clients, family members or nurses affected the processes of ethical care in research participation and in the use of companion robots or electric tracking devices. Frequent use of restraints among the clients with psycho‐behavioural symptoms may be attributed to the overriding organisational regulations or the cultures for safety and efficiency (Chakanyuka et al. 2022; Crooks et al. 2021; Gunawardena and Smithard 2019). Moreover, ethical care procedures with advanced directives could be either legitimate or illegal among different systems (Kollisch et al. 2021). Therefore, multiple factors may influence nurses' ethical care behaviours. Practical strategies must be explored to facilitate nurses' ethical decisions and specify their ethical care approaches to the clients with dementia.

Contemporary literature of the field informed that numerous efforts have been made to assist nurses to deal with the ethical issues in dementia care. Guidelines, frameworks or case studies have been reported to promote nurses' ethical practice (Bolt et al. 2021; Chiong et al. 2021; Lauridsen et al. 2023). Decision‐making tools may as well have facilitated the processes of ethical care (Iaboni et al. 2021). Besides, Grealish and Bloomer (2020) forwarded that appropriate communications had played a key role in dissolving daily ethical crises in dementia care. In spite of these contributions, it is still in deficit of more concrete approaches to enhance nurses' understanding in ethical theories and smoothen their applications in dementia care. Nurses need to be guided further to improve their competencies of ethical reasoning and be sensitive to their ethical conducts in daily care. Thus, it is essential to take a step forward to develop the behavioural criteria of ethical care to boost nurses' confidence in response to the ethical scenarios with dementia.

In short, the ethical care with dementia has been controversial and a conundrum hindering nurses' ethical practice. A critical link was absent to bridge between ethical theories and clinical applications in the ethical contexts of dementia care. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to develop the behavioural criteria of ethical care with dementia to facilitate nurses' ethical care decisions and set boundaries for nurses' ethical conducts. The narrative behavioural criteria will delineate specific ethical cases and scenarios, demonstrate the applications of ethical principles, and define the patterns or modalities of ethical care behaviours, which will serve both didactic and practical purposes by providing nurses with a format of ethical reference to support their clinical practice in dementia care.

Methods

This funded research project was initiated from the end of June 2020 and completed at the end of June 2023 (Project approval number: 21NDJC187YB). Eight members with both educational and clinical experiences in dementia care were selected to build up the research team and were involved in the whole process of this project. This study underwent three stages: Stage 1. Literature review; Stage 2. Initial construction; and Stage 3. Delphi method.

Literature Review

Search Method

In this review, key words of ‘Dementia’ OR ‘Alzheimer's disease’ OR ‘Cognitive dysfunction’ AND ‘Nursing ethics’ OR ‘Ethical care’ AND ‘Behavioural criteria’ OR ‘Standard’ OR ‘Norm’ OR ‘Guideline’ were used in the search through the databases of Scopus, PubMed, CINAHL, Science direct, Researchgate, Foreign Academic Resources Retrieval System (FARS, including 28 databases such as Nature, Elsevier, Springer link, John Wiley, Sage, Taylor & Francis, Oxford, Cambridge) and three domestic databases of CNKI, VIP and Wangfan in China. The articles published between 2010 and 2022 (updated) which met the eligible criteria were selected.

Inclusion Criteria and Exclusion Criteria

The articles were included if they were: (1) in relevance to nursing or medical ethics; (2) covering the theories or the practices of ethical care with dementia; (3) studies with all types of research methodology, reviews, guidelines or position statements; and (4) published in English or in Chinese. The articles were excluded if they were: (1) irrelevant to ethical care with dementia; (2) not comprehensive in scope (fewer than three ethical principles or three ethical issues were discussed); (3) providing incomplete or unfitting information to the topic; and (4) commentaries, posters, abstracts, pamphlets or infographics.

Data Extraction

As the reviewers, two team members independently screened the titles and abstracts of the articles with the inclusion criteria, then reviewed their full texts and selected articles based on the exclusion criteria. Furthermore, the ethical principles and the ethical issues in dementia care that were explicitly or implicitly discussed in the articles were respectively extracted and tabulated by the reviewers. All the differences in extractions between the reviewers were resolved through consensus. The data extracted from each article included: (1) author name and publication year; (2) the research designs of the studies or the nature of the articles; (3) ethical principles; and (4) ethical issues. The preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta‐analyses statement (PRISMA) checklist was used as a reference for the review and charting the diagram of literature search (Page et al. 2021).

Quality Appraisal

The Johns Hopkins Evidence Level and Quality Guidelines (Philbrick 2013) were used to select the robust evidence in the literature. By employing appraisal tools based on the type of evidence, two reviewers again assessed the included articles respectively and a third experienced researcher was consulted when inconsistency arose. The articles with evidence type I to V and evidence quality above level b would be included in this review.

Data Synthesis

The same two reviewers independently analysed the data that were extracted. After the data were carefully studied, they were synthesised using the processes of highlighting, comparing, grouping or categorising, which best served the purpose of the study at this stage, because it was targeted to obtain the frequently discussed ethical principles and ethical issues in dementia care (Tay et al. 2023). Similar contents regarding the ethical principles and ethical issues were merged. All the differences in syntheses between the two reviewers were also resolved through consensus.

Construction of the Initial Delphi Draft

The ethical principles and the ethical issues that were frequently identified in the review were adopted as the basis for further discussions. Opinions or suggestions on the topic were welcomed among the eight team members with their professional experiences, and the ethical principles and the ethical issues in dementia care were formed or categorised with consensus. Subsequently, the each team member was assigned one or two categories of the ethical issues in dementia care, typical cases, scenarios and modalities of ethical care behaviours were preliminarily constructed by each team member after consulting the experienced clinicians from dementia care units. The criteria of ethical care behaviours were set with three modalities: the recommended, the obscure and the false. Finally, all the written cases, scenarios and modalities of ethical care behaviours were brought to the team conference for further review. Revisions were made till an initial draft was settled with agreement for the Delphi method.

Delphi Method

Recruitment of Delphi Expert Panel

A Delphi method involves multiple rounds of expert consultation by questionnaires so as to aggregate expert opinions of the experts and finally reach a consensus (Guerreiro et al. 2021). In this study, 15 nursing experts from a Southeast province of China were referred to and invited to participate in the survey through correspondence. The inclusion criteria for the experts were: (1) Holding a bachelor's degree or higher in nursing; (2) Holding a professional title of intermediate level or higher; (3) Having 10 or more years of working experience in nursing; (4) Being self‐confident in the knowledge of ethical care with dementia; (5) Willing to participate and complete rounds of rating. Those nursing professionals who did not have clinical experience in dementia care or did not focus on the ethical care of dementia were excluded.

Survey Consultation

The experts were instructed via emails to independently fill the survey questionnaires with three parts: (1) demographics; (2) evidence of judgement and degree of familiarity with the research content; and (3) rating on the draft criteria of ethical care behaviours with dementia including three indicators, (a) ethical principles; (b) ethical issues; and (c) blocks of ethical cases, scenarios and criteria of ethical care behaviours. The degree of importance for the former indicators was rated by a Likert scale with 5 as the most important and 1 as the least important; and the degree of appropriateness for the third block indicator was similarly rated with 5 as the most appropriate and 1 as the least appropriate. To ensure the research quality, the experts were required to complete the consultation within 10 days. They were otherwise reminded by emails. After the first round was done, the revised rating scale was used for the second round of expert consultation. The experts were also provided with a summary of the first‐round consultation and the explanations of the corresponding changes. This process was repeated 2 weeks later till expert consistency was accepted.

Data Analysis

SPSS 25.0 was used for statistical analysis. Frequency and percentage were used for descriptive data. Mean (M), standard deviation (SD), Kendall's correlation coefficient (r) and p values were used for measurement data (Li et al. 2023). A higher percentage of an effective responding rate indicated a stronger experts' attention to the project. The composite reliability (Cr) was employed to indicate the authority degree of the expert consultation, which was calculated by averaging the coefficient of judgmental evidence (Ca) and the degree of the experts' familiarity with research contents (Cs). The Ca score was the sum of the assigned values for the four evidence sources as practical experiences, knowledge of theories, literature review and intuitive feeling, respectively corresponding to their levels of high, medium or low. Cs was rated by a Likert scale with 5 as the most familiar and 1 as the least familiar. A Cr score of ≥ 0.7 or > 0.8 revealed respectively an acceptable or a great reliability of the experts' opinions in the field (Wan et al. 2023). An indicator would not be included when its average mean of the experts' ratings was < 4. Coefficient of variation (CV) was used to judge the variations of the experts' ratings on an indicator with the acceptable criteria of < 0.25 (Li et al. 2023). Kendall's coefficient of concordance (w) was employed to analyse Kendall's correlation coefficient (r) among the experts' ratings with a range between 0 and 1. A higher Kendall's r indicated a higher consistency in the experts' ratings (Wan et al. 2023). And, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical Approval

Ethical approval was not required for this study as no information of the clients was involved. However, the experts were fully informed of the purposes, significances, contents and methods of this study. They were respected for their freedom to participate in or to withdraw from the study at any time; their rights and interests were well protected by being permitted to quit at any stage of this study without any consequences. Also, anonymity was made to protect the experts' confidentiality in the process of this study.

Demographics

The research team of this project consisted of eight female members (Table 1). A half of them aged > 40 years old, and 62.5% of them owned a master or a doctor degree. All of them had experiences in both clinical practice and nursing education, with 75% of them working more than 15 years and 50% of them earning an advanced professional title. And, the 15 panel experts were females as well (Table 1). 66.7% of them aged > 40 years old and earned a bachelor degree. 53.3% of them had > 15 years of work experience and owned an advanced professional title. 80.0% of them had both clinical and educational experiences in nursing while the rest primarily had clinical experience.

Characteristics of the Articles Included

The authors of the 15 articles originated from seven countries including four teams from the United States, three teams from Germany, two teams from The United States, Norway and China, and one team from Switzerland and Israel respectively (Table 2). Seven articles were published in the recent 3 years, and the rest were published between 2013 and 2019. Eight articles were traditional reviews (Chao et al. 2019; Daly and Fahey‐McCarthy 2014; Hine et al. 2022; Johnson and Karlawish 2015; Mitchell 2019; Parker 2020; Strøm and Engedal 2021; Zhou and Wang 2021), the other seven articles included two systematic qualitative reviews (Strech et al. 2013; Götzelmann et al. 2021), three qualitative studies (Hochwald et al. 2021; Knüppel et al. 2013; Lundberg 2017), one position statement (Chiong et al. 2021), and one guideline (Swiss Academy of Medical Sciences 2018). Among them, the study of Knüppel et al. (2013) was a guideline assessment based on the findings from a systematic review of the ethical guidelines for dementia care.

Evidence Quality

By the two reviewers' appraisals of the 15 articles with Johns Hopkins Evidence Level and Quality Guidelines, five articles including two systematic qualitative reviews and three qualitative studies were evaluated as evidence type III, the other two articles of one guideline and one position statement were rated as evidence type IV, and the eight traditional reviews were evaluated as evidence type V. Moreover, 12 articles of the total were rated as level a and the rest three articles (Chao et al. 2019; Strøm and Engedal 2021; Zhou and Wang 2021) were rated as level b. Thus, the 15 articles with evidence of above level b were all qualified to be included in this review. The evidence quality of these articles was listed in Table 2.

The Ethical Principles and Ethical Issues Synthesised

Based on the ethical principles and ethical issues extracted from the articles included, 10 ethical principles and 12 dimensions of ethical issues were merged and categorised (Tables 2, 3, 4). Among the 10 ethical principles, autonomy, non‐maleficence, beneficence and justice were the most frequently identified principles in the literature; the other five principles of informed consent, respect, quality of life, quality of care and collaborative participation were separately included owing to their unique and extensive applications. In particular, informed consent was conventionally categorised as an ethical principle rather than an ethical issue in domestic literature. And, prudence was defined as being careful and conscientious with ones' own behaviours either in absence or in presence of supervisions, which was viewed domestically as an essential professional quality of nurses and ultimately supplemented by team decision (Hofmeyer and Taylor 2021). Finally, regarding the ethical issues, they were largely diverse in the articles and primarily grouped as listed in Table 2, and 12 categories of ethical issues were generated and listed in Table 3.

1‐1‐1 Informing of the clients' care plans

Based on the comprehensive assessments, the nurse chose an appropriate time to inform a client b and/or his/her surrogate about the nature of dementia, his general care plan and the home care strategies that might be positively accepted.

1‐2‐2 Informing of the use of restraints

When use of restraints became a best resort for a client in the situation, the nurse informed the client and/or his/her surrogate about the rationales and the precautions of using restraints. Sufficient communications were done to assure their understandings on the use of restraints and facilitate their decision‐making.

1‐3‐3 Informing of the consent to research participation

After a client was identified as an eligible subject for a study, a nurse researcher followed the consenting procedures of research participation. She informed the client and/or his/her surrogate about the details involving in the research process and the required procedures of consenting for free decisions.

2‐1‐4 Treating a client with no biases or stigmas

When a nurse witnessed that a nursing assistant behaved in a way of disrespecting a client. Upon investigations, education sessions for the individuals and the groups of nursing assistants were arranged by the nurse to teach them the norms of respecting clients' dignity, and the ethical conducts particularly to vulnerable populations.

2‐2‐5 Protecting the confidentiality of a client with special medical issues

A nursing assistant was gossiping with a visitor about an embarrassing medical history of a client whose electric file was happened to be caught sight of. By overhearing, the nurse prohibited this event immediately, which was followed by a discussion session on the strategies not to divulge the clients' confidentiality.

2‐3‐6 Protecting the private body part of a client

A new nursing assistant was changing a diaper for the client with moderate cognitive decline without closing the bedside curtains. She was reminded of this neglect by her manager, and was reinforced with several supervisions to close the bedside curtains while changing the clients' diapers in the following days.

2‐4‐7 Respecting a client's hobby of cooking

To meet a client's preference of cooking could be challenging due to degree of cognitive decline in institutions. However, based on the assessments of the client's cognitive capacity, proper arrangements with safety measures deserved trying to make him/her happy and reduce psycho‐behaviours as well.

2‐5‐8 Helping a client to complete his/her worship

A newly admitted client had religious background with rituals of daily worshipping. As the institution had regulations regarding the time frame and location for worshipping, the nurse discussed the client's inconvenience with the manager, and efforts had been made within limit to support the client's special need for worshipping.

3‐1‐9 Treating the clients with different backgrounds equally

Some clients were sentimental as they were likely to have different family relationships, identities, education levels and working experiences. A nurse learnt to be sensitive to their clients in his/her own attitudes and behaviours, and tried best to treat the clients equally so as to avoid conflicts or envies among them.

3‐2‐10 Ensuring the clients' equal accesses to care resources

A nurse allocated care resources equally when the clients had similar care needs. However, when in shortages of care resources, she provided with care resources to the clients with the most urgent need based on their health condition rather than their authorities.

4‐1‐11 Permitting a client to withdraw from a scientific research

During a session of the research project, a client complained of intolerance and asked for withdrawal. An immediate break was arranged immediately for him/her with assessments done by the researcher. Based on the assessments, the client was reassured of her freedom of quitting from the project at any time.

4‐2‐12 Supporting a client's autonomous decision on advanced directives

A client in an institution decided to make her advanced directives with no tube‐feeding and no resuscitation. Capacity assessment was done and her/his surrogate was also informed. The nurse facilitated the client in the process of decision‐making and completion of the procedures with the presence of her surrogate.

4‐3‐13 Authorising and supporting the surrogate to make terminal care decision for a client

A client, who was confused and with a deteriorating condition, needed a decision for life suspending measures. However, he left no advanced directives. As his surrogate, his wife with his daughter at her side had difficulties in making this decision. The nurse supported them emotionally, provided them with information and facilitated them for making a final decision.

5‐1‐14 Multidisciplinary participation and collaborations in the care plan of a client

A client was a widow with poor economic status and lived in a community. He developed malnutrition due to a lack of proper care. The Initial Draft of the Criteria

The framework of the drafted behavioural criteria of ethical care with dementia consisted of three main components (indicators) which were subordinate in their relationship. The first indicator was the ethical principles, the second indicator was the ethical issues and the third indicator was a block made up of ethical care cases, scenarios and modalities of ethical care behaviours. Initially, 10 ethical principles, 40 ethical issues selected from the 12 dimensions, and correspondingly 40 clinically typical cases, scenarios and modalities of ethical care behaviours were constructed and discussed in consultation with the clinicians in the field. Hence, this initial draft of the behavioural criteria of ethical care with dementia was formed for Delphi method.

The Major Adjustments at Delphi Stage

Two rounds of expert consultation on the drafted behavioural criteria were collected at the stage of Delphi method. At Delphi round one, the experts' opinions on the first indicators were generally consistent. However, in the second indicators, the ethical issue subordinate to the principle of quality care was originally constructed as ‘client‐centred staffing’ intending to fix a specific nurse to care for a client and decrease the clients' confusion, which was rated low by the experts and had to be deleted as suggested due to a lack of universal feasibility with diverse nursing shift systems in the institutions.

Furthermore, two modifications were made regarding the third indicators. The first modification was made for the case of informing or disclosing. It was reminded by the experts to clarify the confusion in the information provided to the clients and their family members. For example, nurses are responsible for informing the clients' family members about the care strategies of dementia, while it belongs to the doctors' job descriptions to disclose the diagnosis of dementia. Moreover, due to the nature of cognitive decline, the first person to be notified at different stages of dementia should be prioritized in the following order: first the clients themselves, then their surrogates, their family members, and the interest group with a collective decision for the best interests of the clients. The second modification was made for the case of respecting the clients' religious and cultural beliefs. It was drafted to grant their friends visiting privileges and accompany the clients to pray in care institutions. However, revisions were made as suggested due to the limitations of infection controls, institutional regulations or cultural diversities.

There were two deletions made concerning the third indicators as well. The first deletion fell on the case subordinate to the ethical principle of justice, which was primarily constructed to reflect that sharing equal care resources could be threatened during busy shifts. Nevertheless, this was considered unrepresentative because extra human resources were available for exceptions or emergencies. The second deletion was made for the case of playing children's toys. This case was intentionally constructed as an example of disrespecting the dignity of the clients in relation to the principle of respect. On the contrary, it was considered acceptable as the experts believed that that some older adults maintain a youthful mindset and have a fondness for intelligent children's toys.

At Delphi round two, after all the revisions for round one were completed, a new draft of the criteria with questionnaires was presented to the experts for more opinions and additional ratings. Half of the experts put forward new suggestions for modification. One expert proposed a fine‐tuning in the naming of the individual indicators, for example, to group all the ethical issues for the cases with psycho‐behavioural symptoms into a single category in the secondary indicators rather than listing them separately; two experts provided with minor suggestions regarding the details of the case scenario of using restraints; and five experts corrected several rhetorical or editorial errors.

The Final Draft of the Criteria

Minor revisions had been made after the second round of consultation and a final review was completed by the research team. It was concluded to terminate further consultation and form a final draft of the behavioural criteria of ethical care with dementia, which included 10 ethical care principles, 37 ethical issues with their corresponding cases, scenarios, three modalities of ethical care behaviours and a column for comments or rationales that was particularly designed in the format. The Expert Correspondence

The effective responding rates of the two rounds of expert correspondence were both 100%. The Cr of the expert correspondence was 0.84 and 0.86 respectively of the two rounds, which indicated a relatively high credibility. The lowest mean of the experts' ratings on the indicators was 4.20 at round two, with the largest CV of 0.19, which met a satisfactory mean of > 0.4 and CV of < 0.25 (Wan et al. 2023). The overall Kendall's correlation coefficients of the two rounds were both 0.15, p < 0.0001, indicating that the experts' consistency was relatively low but highly significant. These results were taken comprehensively as acceptable due largely to the loose relationship among the indicators and their components.

Discussion

This study was a preliminary investigation of the case‐based narrative criteria of ethical care behaviours with dementia. The innovative format of the criteria developed via a comprehensive approach could be beneficial to clinical practice, pedagogical methodology and future research in the ethical care with dementia (Backhouse and Daly 2022; O'Donnell et al. 2022). However, both the advantages and the issues related to the behavioural criteria of ethical dementia care may deserve further discussion.

A Balance Between the Abstractness and the Concreteness

Few substantive strategies were available to diminish the gap between the ethical theories or principles and their applications despite the guidelines, frameworks and tools available (Cruz Rivera et al. 2022). However, this format of the behavioural criteria of ethical care with dementia may have bridged the gap by integrating the ethical theories or principles into typically ethical cases and scenarios. Three clinically fitted behavioural modalities including the recommended, the obscure and the false were distinctly demarcated with demonstrations. These criteria were not to stereotype nurses' ethical care behaviours, but to inform nurses and set boundaries of professional ethical conduct. The modalities of ethical care behaviours were mainly focused on three aspects which included the assessments of the ethical issues based on the clients' backgrounds and cognitive capacities, prioritisations of the clients' ethical needs and implementations of ethical care with effective communications (McKeown et al. 2020). With the demonstrations of ethical care behaviours, the criteria became user‐friendly and well balanced the levels of abstractness in epistemology and the concreteness in clinical operations. Therefore, it may truly facilitate nurses in their ethical practice in dementia care.

Solutions for Cultural Differences

Concerns may arise on the cultural diversities of the behavioural criteria of ethical care with dementia since cultural heterogeneity does exist at either international, national, organisational or even individual levels (Koh et al. 2023; Midtbust et al. 2022). This issue, nevertheless, may arguably not be an obstacle because a narrative style was adopted to describe the criteria in this study, and the advantages were apparent in assisting the nurse peers to view the landscape as illustrated by the behavioural criteria and acquire the expertise. First, this format of the criteria unfolded the ethical cases and issues by picturing the rich backgrounds of the clients and their family members, which offered a pleasant experience as if being dwelled in ethnographic studies. Second, the narrative style was beneficial for global interactions because the answers for the heated debates among cultures had been transparently described in the narratives. Third, the column for comments or rationales in this format left opportunities to clarify potential ambiguities. Thus, this narrative style of the criteria may particularly be conducive to the exchanges of transcultural propositions regarding the ethical care with dementia.

Nonetheless, a couple of suggestions may facilitate the generalisation of the behavioural criteria in order to make the best use of them. On one hand, international studies are to be inspired to decrease cultural divergences, as multi‐culturally fitted cases and scenarios could be collected globally and universal criteria of ethical behaviours in dementia care could be constructed. On the other hand, this format of the criteria could be taken as a basic framework for further exploration. Cultural differences could be identified in nurses' ethical behaviours in dementia care. It is also advocated that the behavioural criteria could be tailored to the ethical care scenarios in specific cultures, and the ethical care behaviours that are incongruent with that illustrated in this study can be substituted to fit their own cultures (Tetrault et al. 2022). Therefore, this format of the ethical criteria with dementia is not static but can be adapted to the ethical practice of various cultures.

Pedagogy for Ethical Reasoning and Decision‐Making

The common weakness of nurses in ethical care with dementia is their competencies of ethical reasoning. Due to the multiplicity of ethical issues in dementia care, the case‐based behavioural criteria as generated in this study have not been exhaustive but could be taken as references for pedagogy (Jakobsen and Maehre 2023; Moermans et al. 2022). More clinical cases and scenarios in dementia care could be added to enrich the criteria. Drafting the modalities of ethical care behaviours will prove to be an excellent practice to enhance the competency of ethical reasoning among the students or novice nurses, as it requires fundamental knowledge of ethical theory, clinical experiences and sensitivity to ethical dilemmas (Merl et al. 2022). Careful thoughts are encouraged in each step of assessment and analysis of ethical cases and scenarios, identification of ethical issues, prioritisation of ethical needs and definition of ethical conducts. Ethical theories or principles are selected along the journey of decision‐making to verify the rationales, reflections and evaluations of their ethical care behaviours (Safavi et al. 2022). Thus, this format of the criteria is highly recommended for pedagogy purpose among nurses to nurture their competencies of ethical reasoning and improve their abilities to solve ethical issues in dementia care.

Instructions for Replications or Further Developments

Due to the complexity of the behavioural criteria of ethical care with dementia, four instructions were proposed for the efforts of developments or replications in the future. First, the cognitive and decision‐making capacity must be assessed constantly and comprehensively for the clients with dementia to ensure their maximal autonomy. Meanwhile, it is the nurses' duty to facilitate the process of ethical decision‐making in order to fully protect the clients' best interests. Second, the behavioural criteria are not elusive but permeate through daily care routines (Kemp et al. 2022). Nurses must learn to ‘read’ the clients' disorganised expressions, psycho‐behavioural symptoms, daily activities and then ‘talk’ over their unmet needs. As ethical solutions exist in the mundane daily communications, building a trusting relationship with the clients is ethically essential (Gjellestad et al. 2022; Tieu 2021). Thirdly, clinical feasibility is another important element to be considered. For example, in respecting the clients' autonomy, both patience and time management could be put to test, so that nurses should be fully aware of it when they ethically cope with the muddling and hasty daily care within shifts. Lastly, frameworks, guidelines and tools relevant to ethical dementia care are to be synthesised and utilised in the construction process to enhance the quality, the credibility and the applicability of the behavioural criteria.

Limitations

This study preliminarily explored the behavioural criteria of ethical care with dementia, which might be a small innovation in its format to assist nurses' learning and clinical practice. However, three major limitations must be reminded before the criteria could be taken for references. First, this review merely included the articles with comprehensive discussions on the ethical care with dementia, which may have degraded the richness of the data. Future investigations are suggested to revise the exclusion criteria and allow a larger pool of literature to be included for analysis. Second, 15 experts from the Southeast part of China were invited in this study, which might be a weak representation either at a national or a universal level. This weakness could be further improved in future research by inviting more experts in the field from multiple regions or cultures to enhance the expert representativeness, and hence the credibility of the criteria. Third, the experts' correspondence for the criteria as indicated by Kendall's r was relatively low in spite of a significant p value. This finding fell within the expectation and was considered reasonable since the relationship among the indicators of the criteria might be genuinely slim; particularly, the expert consistency might have been diluted in the third indicators with cases, scenarios and behavioural modalities as a block. Therefore, repeated studies among multiple groups could be conducted further for more evidence to confirm this finding or to improve the experts' consistency.

Conclusions

The behavioural criteria of ethical care with dementia achieved in this study included 10 ethical principles, 37 ethical issues and their corresponding cases, scenarios and criteria of ethical care behaviours, which provided an innovative and practical format of reference for pedagogical activities, clinical practice and nursing research in the ethical care with dementia. Future investigations are encouraged to take this format as a basis to further confirm or improve the behavioural criteria of ethical care with dementia either at a universal or a cultural level. Through multiple applications and developments of the criteria, clinical nurses will be better prepared and become more critical and decisive with their ethical conduct in multiple ethical contexts of dementia care. Therefore, the behavioural criteria forwarded in this study are beneficial to promote the nurses' ethical confidence in dementia care, which will finally upgrade the nurses' professionalism.
