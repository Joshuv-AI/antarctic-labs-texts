Dynamic Weight-Bearing Lateral Radiographs to Detect Occult Nonunion After Ankle Arthrodesis: A Diagnostic Technical Tip and Case Series of 2 Patients
Authors: Ryutaro Takeda, Munetaka Ueda, Shinya Adachi, Taro Kasai, Song Ho Chang, Takumi Matsumoto, Sakae Tanaka
Published: 2026

Introduction

Ankle arthrodesis is a reliable procedure for end-stage ankle arthritis resulting from osteoarthritis, trauma, or inflammatory disease. 6 When solid union is achieved, clinical outcomes are generally favorable. However, nonunion remains a significant complication and a leading cause of failure, with reported rates of painful nonunion ranging from 9% to 41%. 7 In cases of symptomatic nonunion, revision arthrodesis or conversion to total ankle arthroplasty may be required, making accurate diagnosis essential for appropriate surgical decision making.4,8

Despite its clinical importance, the diagnosis of nonunion after ankle arthrodesis can be challenging. The fusion site is often bridged by fibrous tissue, which may obscure clear radiographic findings. Plain radiographs may be insufficient for accurately assessing fusion after hindfoot arthrodesis. Previous studies have demonstrated poor agreement between radiographs and CT, with radiographs tending to overestimate osseous fusion. 3 In addition, retained hardware may limit accurate assessment with computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). 9 Although single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) has been advocated to detect occult nonunion, these modalities are costly and not universally available. 10

We describe a simple and reproducible radiographic technique for diagnosing nonunion after ankle arthrodesis using standard plain radiographs. This method can be readily implemented in routine clinical practice without the need for advanced imaging. We further illustrate this technique with representative radiographic findings from 2 patients with clinically suspected nonunion, who had intraoperative confirmation.

Indications and Contraindications

Indications included patients with persistent pain after ankle arthrodesis in whom nonunion is suspected, and contraindications, inability to bear weight on the affected limb.

Techniques

Patients were examined using weight-bearing lateral radiographs obtained in both maximal dorsiflexion and maximal plantarflexion positions (Figure 1). All images were acquired with the patient standing, ensuring the heel remained in contact with the floor during motion, as previously described for radiographic assessment of ankle range of motion.1,2 The medial aspect of the foot was placed parallel to the image receptor to minimize rotational malalignment. Care was taken to maintain consistent foot positioning between maximal dorsiflexion and plantarflexion views.

Positioning technique for dynamic weight-bearing lateral radiographs of the ankle. (A) Maximum plantar flexion under weight-bearing. (B) Maximum dorsiflexion under weight-bearing. The patient stands with the examined foot weight-bearing, lightly resting the hands on the radiographic equipment stand for balance. The body is shifted posteriorly and anteriorly to achieve maximal plantar flexion and dorsiflexion, respectively, without lifting the heel or forefoot. Care is taken to maintain consistent foot positioning between dorsiflexion and plantarflexion imaging to minimize measurement variability.

We followed these established radiographic positioning principles. However, rather than focusing on total ankle range of motion, we carefully compared the relative deviation between the anterior and posterior edge of tibial plafond and the superior talar cortex on maximal dorsiflexion and plantarflexion images.

In cases of solid arthrodesis, no change in relative alignment was observed between the tibia and talus. In contrast, in suspected nonunion cases, subtle but definite relative motion between the distal tibia and talus could be identified when comparing the 2 extreme positions.

Case 1

A 65-year-old woman with systemic lupus erythematosus, who had been receiving oral prednisolone (4 mg/d), underwent arthroscopic tibiotalar arthrodesis. Full weight-bearing ambulation without a walking boot was initiated at 3 months postoperatively; however, anterior ankle pain during walking persisted. No clinical or laboratory findings suggestive of infection were observed. At 12 months postoperatively, clinical and radiographic evaluation was performed, including CT. According to a semiquantitative assessment method reported in previous studies, the extent of osseous bridging was classified as 50% to 75%, suggesting partial fusion. 5 However, dynamic weight-bearing lateral radiographs demonstrated subtle instability at the fusion site (Figure 2). The distance between the anterior edge of the tibial plafond and the inflection point between the talar neck and body measured 3.9 mm in maximum plantarflexion and 2.3 mm in dorsiflexion. Similarly, the distance between the posterior edge of the tibial plafond and the posterior margin of the talar dome was 16.6 and 13.6 mm, respectively. Eighteen months after the ankle arthrodesis, conversion to TAA was subsequently performed, and intraoperatively nonunion with a broken screw was confirmed.

Case 2

A 78-year-old man with a history of diabetes mellitus and a smoking history of more than 50 years underwent arthroscopic tibiotalar arthrodesis for end-stage ankle osteoarthritis. Postoperatively, poor compliance with non-weight-bearing instructions was also noted. No clinical or laboratory findings suggestive of infection were observed. Full weight-bearing ambulation without a walking boot was initiated at 3 months postoperatively; however, the patient could not walk more than 5 minutes because of the persistent pain in the anterior talocrural joint. Eleven months after the ankle arthrodesis, CT was done and the osseous bridging was evaluated as 0% to 24% according to the semi-quantitative method, suggesting absence of bone union. 5 Simultaneously, dynamic weight-bearing lateral radiographs demonstrated subtle instability at the fusion site, supporting the diagnosis of nonunion (Figure 3). The distance between the anterior edge of the tibial plafond and the inflection point between the talar neck and body measured 9.4 mm in maximum plantarflexion and 7.6 mm in dorsiflexion. Similarly, the distance between the posterior edge of the tibial plafond and the posterior margin of the talar dome was 2.0 and 5.3 mm, respectively. Fifteen months after ankle arthrodesis, conversion to TAA was performed, and intraoperative findings confirmed nonunion.

Discussion

In this article, we propose the use of dynamic weight-bearing lateral radiographs for the diagnosis of occult nonunion after ankle arthrodesis. We presented 2 patients with nonunion who had undergone arthroscopic tibiotalar arthrodesis. This technique allows visualization of subtle motion at the fusion site under physiological loading conditions, which may not be apparent on static radiographs or advanced imaging modalities.

To date, CT has been considered the gold standard for diagnosing nonunion after ankle arthrodesis because it enables detailed assessment of osseous bridging at the fusion site. However, no universally accepted CT-based criteria for union have been established. 9 A semi-quantitative method has been proposed to classify osseous bridging into 4 categories, and cases with 0% to 24% bridging have been shown to be associated with limited improvement in clinical outcomes. However, because of its semi-quantitative nature, nonunion may still be present even in cases with ≥25% osseous bridging. In fact, in case 1 of the present study, osseous bridging was evaluated as 50% to 74% on CT; nevertheless, nonunion was confirmed intraoperatively at the time of revision surgery. 5 Furthermore, CT assessment may be limited by hardware artifacts and difficulty distinguishing solid bony union from fibrous bridging. In addition, CT provides only a static evaluation and does not assess mechanical stability under load. In contrast, the present technique evaluates dynamic stability by comparing maximal dorsiflexion and plantarflexion weight-bearing lateral radiographs. Even subtle relative motion between the distal tibia and talus can be identified when the images are assessed side by side, thereby providing functional information that complements static imaging modalities.

Weight-bearing CT (WBCT) has emerged as a valuable imaging modality that enables 3-dimensional assessment of the ankle under physiological load. To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have investigated the use of WBCT for the diagnosis of nonunion after ankle arthrodesis. Building on the findings of the present study, we speculate that repeated WBCT acquisitions at different ankle positions, such as dorsiflexion and plantarflexion under load, may enable more sensitive detection of subtle mechanical instability at the fusion site. Compared with dynamic radiography, WBCT may be less affected by slight variations in limb positioning and may provide more reproducible assessments. However, higher radiation exposure and the complexity of post-processing should be considered when applying this modality in clinical practice.

SPECT has been proposed to detect occult nonunion when CT findings are inconclusive. 10 Although SPECT may reveal increased metabolic activity at the fusion site, it is costly, less accessible, and may not reliably distinguish between delayed union and true mechanical instability. MRI is generally limited by metal artifacts following arthrodesis and is not routinely used for this indication. Therefore, a simple and widely available method that evaluates dynamic stability under loading conditions may provide meaningful complementary information in daily clinical practice.

This study has several limitations. First, this report represents a small case series without formal evaluation of diagnostic accuracy or predefined cutoff values. Second, this technique has not been systematically compared with other imaging modalities or diagnostic approaches. Therefore, further studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate the diagnostic utility of this method and to establish objective diagnostic criteria.

Conclusion

The proposed method is simple, inexpensive, and broadly accessible, and may serve as an adjunctive diagnostic option in patients with suspected occult nonunion. Wider adoption of this technique may facilitate future studies to establish diagnostic criteria and determine its diagnostic accuracy.
