Low dietary fiber intake impairs small intestinal Th17 and intraepithelial T cell development over generations
Authors: Charlotte J. Royer, Naomi Rodriguez-Marino, Madelyn D. Yaceczko, Dormarie E. Rivera-Rodriguez, Thomas R. Ziegler, Luisa Cervantes-Barragan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYDietary fiber strongly impacts the microbiota. Here, we show that a low-fiber diet changes the small intestinal (SI) microbiota and impairs SI Th17, TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ and TCRαβ+CD8αα+ intraepithelial T cell development. We restore T cell development with dietary fiber supplementation, but this defect becomes persistent over generations with constant low-fiber diets. Offspring of low-fiber diet-fed mice have reduced SI T cells even after receiving a fiber-rich diet due to loss of bacteria important for T cell development. In these mice, only a microbiota transplant from a fiber-rich diet-fed mouse and a fiber-rich diet can restore T cell development. Low-fiber diets reduce segmented filamentous bacteria (SFB) abundance, impairing its vertical transmission. SFB colonization and a fiber-rich diet partially restore T cell development. Finally, we observe that low-fiber diet-induced T cell defects render mice more susceptible to Citrobacter rodentium infection. Together, these results demonstrate the importance of fiber to microbiota vertical transmission and host immune system development.

INTRODUCTION

Interactions between host and microbiota are bidirectional and dynamic. The gut microbiota shapes the host’s mucosal immune response and in turn the host shapes the microbiota via the immune system and diet.1–3 For example, in the large intestine, Clostridia clusters IV, XIVa, and XVIII contribute to the development of T regulatory cells, which help maintain homeostasis.4,5 Segmented filamentous bacteria (SFB) in the small intestine induce the development of Th17 cells.6,7 Moreover, CD4+CD8αα+ intraepithelial T cells require the presence of specific microbes, including Lactobacillus reuteri, to develop.8 While the impact of the microbiota on the development of the immune system has been extensively studied, the impact of diet on these interactions has only recently been investigated. Dietary fiber has a strong impact on the microbiota composition and function. Although it is largely unabsorbed by the host, dietary fiber is the dominant nutrient source for the microbiota, the members of which possess an estimated of 16,000 different enzymes that depolymerize and ferment fiber.9 Alterations in dietary fiber content can therefore lead to profound changes in the microbiota and the mucosal immune system. For example, a diet poor in fiber drives the microbiota to damage the colonic mucus barrier and reduces the host’s ability to control pathogens such as Citrobacter rodentium.10 In this study, we investigated how changes in the fiber content in diet affected the composition of the small intestinal (SI) microbiota and the development of small intestinal T cells. We found that a diet low in fiber reduces the development of SI lamina propria Th17 cells and SI intraepithelial lymphocytes (IEL) TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ and TCRαα+CD8αβ+ T cells, while increasing the frequency of natural TCRγδ+CD8αα+ IEL T cells. These effects on the T cell landscape were reversible in mice that were fed low-fiber diets after weaning but became persistent over generations. Pups born to dams that were fed low-fiber diets were unable to develop Th17 cells and had an impaired TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ and TCRαα+CD8αβ+ T cell development, even after introduction of a fiber-rich diet. Changes in the gut microbiota composition caused these T cell defects, since transplant of microbes from fiber-fed animals in the presence of a diet rich in fiber restored the development of SI T cell populations. We identified a specific microbe-T cell interaction affected by a low-fiber intake. Introduction of a low-fiber diet quickly reduced the abundance of SFB in the ileum. Moreover, we show that if mice born on low-fiber diet conditions received a combination of SFB and a fiber-rich diet, the development of Th17 cells as well as TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ and TCRαα+CD8αβ+ IEL T cells was restored. Finally, we demonstrate that the altered T cell development in low-fiber diet-fed mice has functional consequences. Low-fiber diet-fed mice are more sus- ceptible to C. rodentium infection, shown by an increase in bac- terial translocation to the spleen. Overall, our study shows that dietary fiber controls the development of SI T cell populations through the microbiota, and that a diet low in fiber causes the loss of microbes and impairs development of the mature T cell landscape over generations.

RESULTS

Reduced intake of dietary fiber changes SI microbiota composition and impairs T cell development in the small intestine

To determine how changes in diet affected intestinal T cell development, we fed groups of C57BL/6 littermate mice at weaning (4 weeks old) with a standard chow (SC) diet (PicoLab rodent diet 5053) that contains ground corn, soybean, wheat, beets, oat, alfalfa, and yeast as sources of fiber or an isocaloric, low fermentable fiber (LFF) diet where fermentable fiber was substituted with microcrystalline cellulose (Research Diet AIN-93G) Table S1. First, food consumption (7 days) and weight gain (28 days) were serially measured to ensure a similar caloric intake between groups and determine the need for pair-feeding experiments.11 We observed similar food intake and body weight between the two diet groups over time; therefore, mice were fed ad libitum for subsequent experiments (Figure S1). After 4 weeks on the different diets, the SI microbiome was analyzed by 16s rRNA sequencing, and T cell population frequencies in the SI IEL cell fraction and lamina propria were analyzed by flow cytometry (Figure 1A). Similar to previous observations in the large intestine,10,12 changes in the diet, particularly fiber, have a strong impact on the composition of the SI microbiota. Principal-coordinate analysis (PCoA) revealed significant differences in the community composition of the SI microbiota in mice fed LFF or SC diets (Figure 1B). On the phylum level, there were significant differences in the relative frequencies of Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes between SC and LFF diets (Figure 1C; Table S2). On the order level, Bacteroidales showed the greatest decrease and Bifidobacteriales and Erysipelotrichales showed the greatest increase in LFF mice (Figure 1D; Table S2).

Significant differences were also observed in the frequency of SI T cells as a result of diet changes. Mice on the LFF diet showed impaired Th17 development (Figures 1E, 1I, and S2) and changes in the composition of the SI IEL T cell compartment. Here, we observed impairment in the development of TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ T cells (Figures 1F, 1J, and S2) and a reduction in frequency and numbers of TCRαβ+CD8αα+ T cells (Figures 1G, 1K, and S2). In contrast, an increase in the frequency of TCRγδ+ T natural IELs was found in LFF mice; this increase in frequency (Figures 1H and S2) was a result of the defect in TCRαβ+ T cells since total numbers of TRCγδ+ T IELs remained unchanged (Figure 1L).

T cell defects resulting from a diet low in fiber are reversible in adult mice

Timing has a critical impact on the development and function of some intestinal cells, intestinal immune cells, and intestinal processes. For example, the development of goblet cell-associated antigen passages (GAPs),13 or invariant natural killer T cells induced by embryonic intestinal macrophages,14 occur in a particular time window and cannot be recovered afterward. We therefore questioned whether impaired Th17 cells, TCRαβ+CD8αα+ and TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ IEL T cell development in LFF mice was due to this diet’s administration at weaning age, and if populations could recover if fiber was introduced later in life. To test this, we weaned littermate B6 mice and fed them SC or LFF diets. After 4 weeks, the diets of these mice were switched, with SC mice switching to LFF diet and LFF mice switching to SC diet (Figure 2A). Mice remained on switched diets for another 4 weeks before analysis. As observed in Figures 2B–2D, a switch to SC diet after 4 weeks of LFF diet largely returned the composition of the SI microbiome to the SC profile. Moreover, a switch to SC diet after LFF diet largely recovered the development of Th17 cells, TCRαβ+CD8αα+ and TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ IEL T cells (Figures 2E–2L). This indicates immune cell defects caused by a fiber-poor diet at weaning can be reversed by introducing fiber later in life. A switch to LFF diet at 8 weeks in mice that received SC diet from weaning had a small effect on frequencies of TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ IEL T cells, supporting the notion that once induced, these populations can persist independent of microbial changes.15

T cell defects in mice fed a low-fiber diet become persistent over generations

We observed that LFF mice had lower relative frequencies of several bacteria. Because pups inherit microbiota from their dams, inheritance of the altered microbiota seen in LFF mice could result in a permanent change to the microbiota of these mice over generations. This would in turn affect T cell development in the offspring, leaving them with the same reduced T cell populations observed in their parents. To investigate a possible generational effect, we weaned male and female B6 mice to LFF diet. At 8 weeks, breeding pairs were created from these mice, and they remained on LFF diet for the rest of the study. The offspring of LFF dams (LFF F1 mice) were weaned at 4 weeks to either SC or LFF diets. At 8 weeks, the microbiota composition and SI T cell populations of these offspring were analyzed (Figure 3A). LFF F1 mice showed a significant reduction in Th17 cells, TCRαβ+CD8αα+ and TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ IEL T cells (Figure 3E–3L). However, in strong contrast to animals weaned to LFF from SC dams (Figure 2), offspring of LFF dams were unable to develop Th17 cells and TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ IEL T cells, even after switching to SC diet. Interestingly, PCoA of the intestinal microbiome showed that the microbiome composition of LFF F1 mice that received SC diet after weaning (LFF F1→ SC) were closer to the microbiota of SC mice (SC F1→SC) than LFF mice (LFF F1→LFF, Figures 3B–3D). These results suggest that, while the majority of microbes in the LFF F1’s microbiota can recover on SC diet, particular microbes, including those that participate in development of Th17 and IELs, can be permanently lost over generations due to parental consumption of a low-fiber diet.

Microbiota reconstitution rescues T cell development in mice born to parents fed a low-fiber diet

We hypothesized that defects in T cell populations observed in LFF F1 mice could be due to impaired transmission or colonization of microbes that promote their development. To test this, we again set up breeding pairs with LFF mice and weaned offspring to LFF or SC. This time, half the LFF F1 mice received fecal microbial transplants (FMT) from C57/BL6 mice on an SC diet (SC FT) to determine if microbial restoration could rescue T cell development (Figure 4A). We observed that LFF F1 mice that received SC diet and FMT developed Th17 cells and TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ IEL T cells and had increased numbers of TCRαβ+CD8αα+ IEL T cells (Figures 4B–4I). Interestingly, LFF F1 mice that received an FMT, but were fed an LFF diet did not develop Th17 cells or TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ IEL T cells nor had increased numbers of TCRαβ+CD8αα+ IEL T cells, indicating that fiber is required to support colonization with the microbes that induce these T cells. This demonstrates that an LFF diet alters the parental microbiota in such a way that an altered/incomplete community is transferred to the offspring, resulting in a persistent impairment of T cell development that cannot be recovered with a diet rich in fermentable fiber later in life.

A diet low in fiber reduces the numbers of SFB

Little is known about bacteria that induce TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ or TCRαβ+CD8αα+ IEL T cells. However, it is well known that the majority of SI Th17 cells in mice are induced by SFB.6,7,16 Since we observed a defect in Th17 development in LFF mice, we decided to investigate if SFB is affected by a diet low in ferment- able fiber. Quantitative PCR analysis of intestinal contents of SC and LFF mice showed a marked reduction in SFB numbers in the SI contents of LFF mice (Figure 5A). Reduction of SFB occurred rapidly after LFF diet introduction, with significant differences in SFB copy numbers observed after only 3 days (Figure 5B). To confirm that reduction of SFB numbers was a result of diet, and not differences in B or T cell responses, we performed the same experiment in rag-1 −/− mice and obtained similar results (Figure 5C). To test if an LFF diet affects SFB transmission by reducing SFB abundance, we quantified SFB numbers in the different regions of the gastrointestinal tract and in fecal pellets of LFF dams and observed reduction of SFB in all regions and in fecal pellets (Figure 5D). We also quantified SFB numbers in the LFF F1 offspring of these dams and confirmed that SFB was below the limit of detection in LFF F1 pups even after administration of the SC diet (Figure 5E) in contrast to SC F1 mice in which SFB abundance could be recovered with fiber supplementation. Finally, we quantified the abundance of SFB in the fecal pellets of SC mice used for fecal transplant (SC FT) and in the LFF F1 mice recipients. As observed in Figure 5F, SFB colonized LFF F1 in the SC diet but not LFF F1 in the LFF diet. These results confirm that SFB is highly reduced in mice fed a LFF diet causing a defect in transmission from parents to offspring.

Reconstitution of SFB recovers Th17 and TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ IEL T cell development in mice born to parents fed a low-fiber diet

Our microbiota fecal transplant experiments indicated Th17 and TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ IEL T cell development could be induced in LFF F1 mice by restoring the lost microbial members (Figures 4B–4I). Therefore, we hypothesized that SFB colonization of LFF F1 mice could recover Th17 and IEL T cell development. To test this, we set up LFF breeding pairs and weaned the offspring either on SC or LFF diets. Half of these pups received fecal transplants from mice mono-colonized with SFB (Figure 6A). Notably, mice that received SFB and an SC diet largely recovered the development of TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ IEL T cells and had increased numbers of TCRαβ+CD8αα+ IEL T cells. Moreover, mice that received SFB and an SC diet largely recovered the development of Th17 cells while mice that received the SC diet alone or SFB while on the LFF diet did not (Figures 6B–6I). Overall, these results show that fiber intake affects transmission of microbes such as SFB from parents to offspring, resulting in permanent loss of microbial members that causes SI T cell defects that cannot be recovered with fiber supplementation alone. These populations only develop when microbes are restored in combination with a diet rich in fermentable fiber.

A low-fiber diet increases susceptibility to C. rodentium infection

Finally, to test if the defects in SI T cell development due to a low-fiber diet had an impact in immune function, we tested the ability of LFF-fed mice to control an intestinal infection. We fed groups of C57BL/6 littermate mice at weaning with SC or LFF for 4 weeks and then orally infected them with C. rodentium. Seven days post infection, spleens were collected and C. rodentium colonies quantified. The presence of C. rodentium in the spleen of mice is indicative of gut bacterial translocation and therefore a more severe disease caused by the inability to locally control infection in the intestine. It has been reported that SFB-induced Th17 cells participate in the control of C. rodentium infection.6 Mice on an LFF diet had significantly higher counts of C. rodentium in the spleen while mice on an SC diet had no bacteria in the spleen (Figure 7).

DISCUSSION

In this study we discovered that low dietary fiber intake changes the small intestine microbiota composition and causes an incomplete development of intestinal T cells. We also demonstrate that these changes become persistent over generations, due to the loss of key intestinal microbes such as SFB. The beneficial physical properties of dietary fiber such as aiding in intestinal motility or creating fecal bulk have long been known.9,17 However, more recent research has revealed dietary fiber’s critical role as a food source for the microbiota.18 Previous studies show that a low-fiber diet affects the composition of the large intestinal microbiota, resulting in a loss of richness, reduction of several fiber-fermenting bacteria, and the subsequent overgrowth of mucolytic bacteria.10,19 Our initial experiments show that a diet low in fiber dramatically changes the small intestine microbiota composition in mice, similar to results found in the large intestine. For example, bacteria that are well-known fiber fermenters, such as several members of the Bacteroidales order, are remarkably reduced with low-fiber diets. Our data support the concept that the reduction of dietary fiber has further-reaching consequences, namely, impairing the development of several SI T cell populations. Mice that received an LFF diet had impaired development of Th17 cells, TCRαβ+CD8αβ+, and reduced numbers of TCRαβ+CD8αα+ T IELs. Higher frequencies of natural IELs such as TCRγδ+CD8αα+ T cells were also seen. Frequencies of these cells are reduced when acquired IEL populations (such as TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ T IELs) increase in the SI as the mouse matures. However, total numbers were not affected.20,21 Defects in SI T cells due to an LFF diet were repaired by reintroduction of fiber in the diet if mice were born from SC diet-fed dams, suggesting that microbes required for T cell development are reduced, but not eliminated, in mice that consume a short-term low-fiber diet. Nevertheless, the finding that some microbes were reduced during low-fiber diet consumption led us to investigate whether defective transmission of some key microbes from parents to offspring would produce a persistent impairment of T cell development over generations. Indeed, mice born to dams on low-fiber diet (LFF F1) were unable to develop Th17 cells and TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ IEL T cells, even after receiving an SC diet rich in fiber. Interestingly, we observed that the gut microbiome of LFF F1 mice that received an SC diet was most similar to the microbiota of SC mice, yet the defect on T cells remained. This suggested that, while several members of the microbiota were transmitted from parents to their offspring, some key members of the microbiota were not, resulting in the inability to develop T cells. To confirm this, we tested if restoring the microbiota of LFF F1 mice with a fecal transplant from SC mice recovered T cell development. We observed that the fecal transplant recovered the development of T cells, but only if mice also received a diet rich in fiber (SC). Mice that received a fecal transplant but were kept in a low-fiber diet did not develop T cells, underscoring the importance of both diet and microbes for colonization. We were able to identify SFB as one of the microbes that are lost over generations after consumption of a low-fiber diet. SFB numbers are rapidly reduced after 3 days on LFF diet, and this reduction is seen throughout the intestinal tract including fecal pellets. This suggests that SFB spores are also reduced in the feces of dams fed an LFF diet, and we hypothesize this leads to inefficient transfer of SFB from dam to offspring. Indeed, SFB is undetectable in the small intestine of LFF F1 pups (offspring of LFF-fed dams) even after they are fed SC diet. While the effects of changing the content of dietary fiber are seen after only 3–5 days on the microbiota, the impact on T cell development can only be seen after 4 weeks, likely due to the time needed for a T cell response to develop. Interestingly, it was shown recently that sugar content in the diet also affects SFB abundance in the small intestine.22

Colonization with SFB using FMT from SFB mono-colonized mice together with SC diet restored the development of Th17 cells and TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ IEL T cells, confirming that the defect was caused by inefficient colonization with maternal SFB. More- over, numbers of SFB rapidly declined in rag-1−/− mice fed an LFF diet, suggesting that the low-fiber diet effect is independent of any adaptive immune response toward SFB.

We observed that mice fed a low-fiber diet were more susceptible to C. rodentium infection, shown by increased bacterial translocation to the spleen. It is well known that Th17-derived cytokines, such as IL-22, are important for control and clearance of C. rodentium, therefore our results suggest that the defect in Th17 cells in LFF-fed mice impairs their ability to fight enteric infections.

Studies of human gut microbiome composition and complexity demonstrate that modernized societies experience loss of microbial richness, known as microbiota erosion, due to sanitation, use of antibiotics, and diets that feature heavily processed foods that are low in dietary fiber.3,18,19 This has been linked to an increase in chronic metabolic and inflammatory diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease.23 Our study reveals the far-reaching effects of this phenomenon, where changes in dietary habits lead to community-wide loss of microbes over generations, resulting in incomplete development of key immune cell populations with homeostatic and regulatory roles (such as Th17 cells or TCRαβ+CD8αβ+ IEL T cells24–29). Loss of these cells impacts the entire mucosal immune system, potentially leaving the host more vulnerable to inflammatory gut mucosal diseases. Interestingly, studies in humans have shown a link between a ketogenic diet and human microbiota species, such as Bifidobacteria and Eggertella lenta, and the development and function of Th17 cells.30–33 Our study highlights that once relevant microbes are lost, dietary interventions alone are not sufficient to reverse SI T cell development. This has been previously seen in studies focused on malnutrition, where diet interventions are not sufficient to restore nutrient intake.34 Our data suggest that a fiber-rich diet may be a simple dietary strategy to supplement the gut microbiome to improve mucosal immunity and thereby intestinal health.

Limitations of the study

Our study identified one bacteria species, segmented filamentous bacteria, that is affected by a low-fiber diet and has an impact on the development of murine intestinal T cells. However, SFB is just one of the many bacteria that are affected by a low-fiber diet and it is likely that several other bacterial species participate in the development of intestinal T cells.

METHOD DETAILS

Microbiota transplantation and segmented filamentous bacteria colonization

Donor fecal samples (C57BL/6 from Charles River laboratories on SC diet) were freshly collected and stored at −80°C. Fecal pellets of mice monocolonized with Segmented Filamentous Bacteria (SFB) were kindly provided by Dr. Andrew T. Gewirtz (Georgia State University).47 For transplantation, pellets were resuspended in PBS at a concentration of 1.66 fecal pellets/mL and filtered through a 70μm mesh to remove insolvent matter. Recipient mice were administered a single 200μL dose of fecal filtrate by oral gavage.

Intestinal content isolation

Content isolation was performed as previously described.29 Briefly, fecal pellets were freshly collected and stored immediately at −80°C. Small intestines were aseptically dissected and separated into three pieces. The first piece, just distal to the stomach, containing mostly duodenum, the second piece, distal to the ligament of Trietz, represented jejunum, and the third piece, proximal to the ileal-cecal valve, represented ileum. The cecum and distal large intestine were also dissected and processed separately from small intestinal segments to avoid cross contamination. Intestines were opened and the intestinal lumen and mucous layer obtained by gently scraping the intestine with a scalpel. The contents were immediately frozen at −80°C.

DNA isolation, 16S rRNA sequencing and analysis, and qPCR analysis

DNA from intestinal contents and fecal pellets was isolated using the QIAmp DNA Stool mini kit (QIAGEN) following manufacturer’s instructions. The V4 region of 16S rRNA gene was PCR-amplified using barcoded primers described previously48 and sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq Platform (2 × 250–bp paired-end reads, LC Sciences). Bioinformatics analysis was conducted using a QIIME2 2021.8 pipeline.38 Denoising was conducted with DADA239 and phylogenetic trees built with mafft41 and fasttree2.42 Shannon diversity index, and weighted UniFrac analyses were conducted with q2-diversity. Phylogenetic assignments were produced using q2-feature-classifier classify-sklearn,43 which assigned taxonomy using Greengenes 13_8 sequence database.44 Relative frequencies of ASVs were averaged per group, with low-frequency taxa making up to 2% total combined as other. Quantitative PCR was performed using extracted DNA from intestinal contents, SFB 736F and SFB 844R oligonucleotides37 and Sso Advanced Universal SYBR green supermix (Bio-Rad) in a CFX96 C100 real-time thermocycler (Bio-Rad). Genome copies were calculated using serial dilutions of a pUC57 plasmid expressing SFB target sequence: ATGAATAAAAAAAGAGTAGCGCTTGCTTTGGCAGCAGCACTTGGAATGAATTCTTTAATGGTTACTGTTGGTGCAGTAGGTGGACAAGCTTTTATAGCTCATGCACAAGATTCTAAATTAAGTACAGATTTATTAGATAAGTTAAAAACTTTAGAAATAGAGTCTCTAGAGACATCTGTAGATATGAGTACAGATGGTAAAGCTCAGGTTACATTTACTAAGATAAGTCCTGAAACTATAAGTAATGTTACAGGAGAG as standard. The plasmid was constructed by Genescript and purified using GeneJET Plasmid Maxiprep Kit according to manufacturer’s instructions.

Intestinal T cell isolation, flow cytometry and ex vivo T cell restimulation

Small intestine intraepithelial lymphocytes (SI IEL) and lamina propria lymphocytes (SI LPL) were isolated following a modified protocol from Lefrancois and Lycke.49 Small intestines were excised, and Peyer’s patches were removed. Small intestines were opened longitudinally, rinsed in HBSS+2% BCS to remove intestinal contents, and cut into 2 cm pieces. The pieces were gently agitated in HBSS 10% BCS and 5mM EDTA (IEL buffer) for 20 min. Supernatant was collected and the agitation in IEL buffer repeated. DTT was added to the flowthrough and incubated for 5 min (IEL fraction). The intestine pieces were washed with HBSS+2% BCS by gentle agitation for 5 min and incubated with Collagenase IV in RPMI +10% BCS+HEPES for 30 min at 37°C with gentle agitation. After incubation the supernatant and remaining intestinal pieces were passed through a 70 μM cell strainer (LP fraction). Cells were isolated from both the IEL and LP fractions using a 40–70% Percoll Gradient (850 × G, 20 min). Cells were collected from the gradient interphase, washed with HBSS-2% BCS and resuspended in PBS for following studies. For flow cytometry analysis cells were stained for 10 min at 4°C with Zombie NIR (Biolegend) for dead cell exclusion, followed by 20 min at 4°C using the antibodies listed in the Key resources table. For ex vivo T cell restimulations, cells were incubated with the Cell Activation Cocktail without brefeldin (Biolegend) for 4 h in the presence of Golgi Plug (BD Biosciences). Surface staining was performed, followed by fixation, permeabilization (BD Cytofix cytoperm Plus kit, BD Biosciences) and intracellular staining. Total number of cells was calculated using Precision Count beads (Biolegend) following manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were measured on an LSR Fortessa X-20 (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with FlowJo Software (FlowJo LLC) using the gating strategy shown in Figure S2.

Citrobacter rodentium infection

Citrobacter rodentium strain DBS100 (American Type Culture Collection) was grown in Luria Bertani (LB) broth to exponential phase. The number of bacteria was estimated by OD600 measurements and later confirmed by plating serial dilutions in LB agar plates. Experimental mice inoculated with 2 × 109 C rodentium. At day 7 post infection, spleens were excised and homogenized, and serial dilutions were plated for 24 h at 37°C onto LB agar plates for quantification of colony-forming units.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Statistical analyses were performed by Student’s t test, Mann-Whitney test, One-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-test or Kruskal Wallis with Dunn’s multiple comparison test using GraphPad Prism 9 software. Data represents the mean ± SD of three independent experiments unless otherwise indicated. p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical details for all tests performed, n, and number of experiments can be found in the figure legends.
