Why do older people not adhere to a healthy lifestyle: a qualitative exploration of perceived barriers among older people living in a low-income and middle-income country setting
Authors: Vidura Jayasinghe, Ruwan Ferdinando, Chrishantha Abeysena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore perceived barriers to adhering to a healthy lifestyle among older people living in a Sri Lankan setting.DesignQualitative thematic analysis of in-depth interviews among older people.SettingThe study was conducted in Kalutara district, Sri Lanka.ParticipantsIn total, 28 older people aged between 60 years and 74 years were interviewed.ResultsSix major themes were identified following the analysis. They were named as lack of knowledge, financial difficulties, poor health status, domestic constraints, cultural norms and changing the living environment. Lack of knowledge emerged through two subthemes, namely lack of information and lack of services. Domestic constraints were derived from family commitments and domestic conflicts, while cultural norms came via beliefs and customs. Poor health status emerged through three subthemes, namely disabilities, diseases and polypharmacy, while changing the living environment emerged via adaptation and urbanisation.ConclusionThis study provides evidence of real-life perceptions on why older people do not adhere to a healthy lifestyle. Barriers are multifaceted and complex, yet those dimensions need to be considered heavily while developing lifestyle programmes to get maximum benefit for the older people.

Introduction

Population ageing is a major public health concern worldwide, and it has already resulted in several socioeconomic consequences, particularly in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Sri Lanka.1 2 Many LMICs are facing difficulties in meeting the health and social needs of older people under conditions of resource constraints, and this situation is expected to become more severe in the near future.3 According to projections, the number of people aged over 60 years will reach 2.1 billion by 2050, of whom approximately 1.7 billion will be living in LMICs.4 Considering this situation, health-promoting programmes have been adopted as a cost-effective strategy in many low-resource settings;5 6 however, their applicability and overall benefits for older populations remain uncertain.7

Various types of lifestyle interventions have been implemented globally among older people, and evidence shows positive effects on several health outcomes.810 A few systematic reviews have evaluated the health effects of healthy lifestyle interventions, and the results have revealed both positive and negative effects among older people.1113

From a public health perspective, lifestyle interventions should extend beyond experimental settings and be implemented on a larger scale to maximise their impact.14 Despite multiple interventions, older people often remain on the periphery of these health initiatives, possibly because their concerns are not adequately considered during the development of lifestyle programmes.7 15

Lifestyle behaviours are context-specific and influenced by several sociocultural factors.16 17 Researchers have highlighted the importance of understanding the perceptions of the target population when designing health interventions.18 19 In addition, older people may face unique challenges in adhering to healthy lifestyle recommendations, which may not be identified without proper exploration.

Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of why older people do not adopt healthy lifestyles is essential prior to designing interventions and tailoring them to their specific needs. However, limited evidence is available on the barriers to engaging in healthy lifestyles among older people in LMIC settings from the perspective of end users, which may hinder the development of elderly friendly lifestyle programmes. Considering this gap, the present qualitative study was conducted to explore perceived barriers to adhering to a healthy lifestyle among older people in Sri Lanka. The findings may help inform the development of more acceptable and context-appropriate healthy lifestyle programmes for older populations living in similar settings.

Objective

To explore perceived barriers to adhering to a healthy lifestyle among older people living in a Sri Lankan setting.

Methods

A qualitative descriptive study was conducted, and the thematic analysis method was applied following in-depth interviews.

Study setting, participants and recruitment

Study participants were older people aged 60–74 years living in the Kalutara district of Sri Lanka. However, older people living in care homes, elderly houses and diagnosed with any psychiatric diseases, including cognitive impairment, were excluded from the study based on their clinical records. Participants for the study were identified purposefully during the district household survey by the Principal Investigator (PI), who was a member of the survey. The district household survey was conducted to identify health and other information among older people aged 60–74 years living in Kalutara district. It was conducted using a representative sample, which was selected using a multistage, stratified probability proportional to size cluster sampling technique. There were 2992 participants included in the survey and they were interviewed using a standard tool (online supplemental material 1). Domestic addresses of the eligible participants were noted during the survey, and the PI visited each selected household to conduct the survey. Ample information about the study was provided for the participants, and written consents were obtained before the interviews. All the interviews were conducted in person.

Data collection

In-depth interviews were conducted by the PI while ensuring privacy and confidentiality. All the interviews were conducted in a respective domestic environment for the participants. However, the PI ensured that no one was interfering and was not within hearing distance from the place of interviews. Basic sociodemographic information was collected using a data collection form. In-depth interviews were conducted using a semistructured interviewer guide (online supplemental material 2), which was validated by a five-member panel from different disciplines (Geriatrician, Health Promoting officer, Sociologist, Psychologist and Community Physician). All interviews were audio recorded using a digital audio recorder following written permission of the participants, and additional written notes were taken by the second investigator while noting non-verbal cues. Each interview was conducted for about a 1 hour period and participants were interviewed up to the theoretical saturation point.20 All the interviews were conducted in the Sinhala language, since all the participants were fully conversant with it. Transcripts were initially prepared in the Sinhala language based on audio records, which were supplemented by written notes after each interview. Then each transcript was translated into English using the forward and backward translation method by two independent translators. Discrepancies between the two translations were resolved based on the consensus between them.

Data analysis

Data analysis was done simultaneously by the PI with the support of the second investigator. Data analysis was conducted manually without any supportive software. The thematic analysis method was used for the data analysis, and it was done according to the six steps, which were described by Braun and Clarke in 2006.21

Step 1

The PI went through each transcript several times, thoroughly line by line, to be familiar with the content to be analysed. In addition, the PI listened to each audio recording again and again to ensure the accuracy of the contents of the transcripts.

Step 2

In step 2, the PI identified chunks of data, and these were highlighted and coded. These codes were labelled separately, as it was helpful to organise data to get a clear understanding of the research problem being studied. These codes were considered salient points of the collected data. These codes were further adjusted and modified during the process.

Steps 3

During this step, similar codes were identified, and they were clustered together to make categories. This was helpful to develop preliminary themes within the collected data. Themes emerged from categories of codes that gave meaning to the collected data.

Step 4

In this step, preliminary themes were further analysed and modified in a more meaningful manner to identify appropriate themes considering the sociocultural context of Sri Lanka, and similar preliminary themes were merged into major themes to get the broad picture of the problem being investigated. It was conducted iteratively till no new themes emerged. Some new themes were also identified during the process. A set of themes was also examined to see any overlap with each other.

Step 5

In this step, interrelationships between themes were identified. The content within themes was analysed, and themes were named appropriately. At the end of the process, the thematic map was developed.

Step 6

In this step, a narrative of thematic analysis was prepared, and it is presented in the results.

Patient and public involvement

The public were not involved inthe design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research. Findings of the study was not sent back to the participants for their feedback.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

During the district survey, 67 older people were identified. Out of them, 49 older people consented to the study. During the study, 28 participants were interviewed successfully up to the theoretical saturation point. There were no differences in sociodemographic characteristics between the participants who were interviewed and those who were not. The average duration of an interview was 50 min (range 43–66). Themes of perceived barriers of the participants

During the in-depth interviews, the participants exposed their barriers to complying with lifestyle modification. All those views were thematically analysed, and 67 codes were identified. Six major themes emerged during the analysis. The major themes were named as (1) Lack of knowledge, (2) Financial difficulties, (3) Poor health status, (4) Domestic constraints, (5) Cultural norms and (6) Changing the living environment. The major themes emerged through several subthemes. Figure 1 outlines the final thematic map of the study.

Thematic map of perceived barriers to adhering to a healthy lifestyle among older people. The figure illustrates the thematic structure that emerged following qualitative analysis. It consists of major themes and respective subthemes.

Lack of knowledge

Lack of knowledge emerged through two subthemes, namely lack of information and lack of services.

Lack of information

Participants expressed a lack of knowledge on how to change their lifestyle as a major barrier. According to them, the palatable information on lifestyle modification did not transmit to them despite many health educational opportunities. They were not clear about how to change their lifestyle in a healthy manner and what sort of habits to adhere to.

I like to change my daily activities to improve my health. But I wonder how I can change it and what I can do to improve my health. There is nobody to ask that sort of thing. How can I practice without knowing those. (a man in his early 60s)

Lack of services

As the interviewees pointed out, there has been no resource person in the community to provide health instructions for them. When they visited healthcare institutions like hospitals, they might get some advice, yet it was so difficult to grab that in a busy environment. According to some participants, they were not very clear about lifestyle modification advice given by healthcare workers. Also, they didn’t have opportunities to cross-question and clarify things. Also, they emphasised the importance of allocating a healthcare worker who can be accessible and clarify their issues.

I have been suffering from high blood pressure for a long time. Also, I have high cholesterol, too. I take medicine from a government hospital, but nobody told us about lifestyle. Doctors are not bad, but they don’t have time. I was eager to ask some questions, but didn’t have a chance. (a man in his early 60s)

When I went to the clinic, one nurse gave us some health instructions. But the clinic was so crowded. We could not hear what she told us properly. I was also desperate to take my medicine and come back home. So, I didn’t concentrate on what the nurse was telling. (a woman in her mid-60s)

Financial difficulties

Financial insecurity was the main barrier for the participants to adhere to a healthy lifestyle. They needed some money to fulfil their health needs. According to them, it was not easy to practise what doctors said due to financial constraints. As many participants pointed out, they had to prioritise their needs while spending the little amount of money they had. Most of the time, they cut down on nutritional intake at the expense of medical needs.

I spend most of my money on my medicine. I do not think too much about diet. Most of the time, I am satisfied with one or two things for my meal per day. (a man in his early 70s)

Sometimes older people had to work as breadwinners for their families even in the latter part of their lives. Therefore, some of them were still employed in the informal sector and looked after their families. Even though they earned, they had to spend for the family’s needs. As some of them expressed, there was hardly any money left for them to spend.

My daughter-in-law lives separately with another partner. My son is a heavy alcoholic. I work in some houses as a servant to make some money. It is necessary to look after my grandchildren. I must spend what I earn for them. I don’t think about my health at all. (a woman in her mid-60s)

Poor health status

This major theme was further derived from three subthemes: disabilities, diseases and polypharmacy.

All the participants had at least one disease, and 21 of them had multiple comorbidities. Six of them had visual impairments, while four of them had restricted movements due to joint problems. According to the views of the participants, diseases and disabilities were major limitations to adhering to a healthy lifestyle.

Disabilities

Many participants experienced difficulties in practising healthy behaviours due to disabilities. Disabilities not only limited physical activities but also community participation among the elderly. A man in his late 60s expressed his views as follows:

I was an active member in the elderly club in the village. I enjoyed participating in the activities conducted by it. But I fell down at home and had a fracture in my leg. Now I can’t travel even to the nearby places without an assistant. It is the main worry for me now.

Earlier, I used to walk to nearby places. But now it is so difficult for me to do so, as I am suffering from severe knee pain. Now I limit most of the traveling and whenever I need to go, I take a three-wheeler rather than walking. (a woman in her early 60s)

Diseases

Participants had restricted lifestyles due to disease conditions, and they expressed diseases as barriers to engaging in lifestyle modifications.

I am suffering from diabetes and on insulin. But recently I had faintish attacks several times. Now I don’t go to a nearby temple alone. I used to do religious works in many days in the month. Now I can’t do so (Crying…).

my daughter does not like me to go outside. If anything happens to me, that will lead to an additional burden to others. (a woman in late 60s)

Polypharmacy

Some participants mentioned that their dietary intake was also severely affected due to diseases and medications. Due to multiple comorbidities, some participants tend to consume several drugs per day. Participants expressed that the polypharmacy and side effects of drugs also had an impact on their lifestyle. As many describe, taking medication has become a burden for them, and it harms their lifestyle.

I consumed 7 drugs per day. Sometimes I feel a burning sensation and chest pain. I go to a nearby doctor. He said that is due to gastritis and gave me another set of drugs. This happens again and again. Now I can’t eat much, and I don’t have a desire. I can’t sleep properly due to this chest pain. (a man in his early 60s)

Domestic constraints

This emerged via two subthemes as: family commitments and domestic conflicts.

Family commitments

According to the views of some participants, older people become more responsible for some domestic work and sometimes they become exhausted with an unbearable workload. Some may not have sufficient opportunity to look after their health, too. Such a situation could be observed in many middle-class families. They become more responsible at home, yet limit the opportunity to engage with a healthy lifestyle.

My husband passed away 7 years ago. Now I live with my daughter. She works in the bank. Both (daughter and son-in-law) go early in the morning. Thereafter, I had to look after two grandchildren. I must feed them. Wash them. Play with them. I am exhausted at the end. I have arthritis too. It was very painful at night. Sometimes I miss my lunch. I don’t have time even to listen to religious chanting. (a woman in her late 60s)

Domestic conflicts

As expressed by a few, domestic conflicts are also a barrier to a healthy lifestyle. Especially, older women become victims of such conflicts, and their health is severely affected due to this. Some older people were unable to fulfil basic needs as they faced many issues following domestic violence.

I live with my son. He works in a rubber plantation. He is an alcoholic and spends most of the money on that. So hardly anything remains for our consumption. His wife lives separately with another person. Now he shouts at me too (crying). I can’t even sleep at home. I got some meals from nearby people, but some days I don’t have anything. (a woman in her early 70s)

Cultural norms

Two subthemes were identified within cultural norms, namely customs and beliefs.

Customs

Most of the time, culture and traditions influence the lifestyle of older people. Sometimes those are negatively affected among them. Elderly people are highly respected in Sri Lankan culture, but on the other hand, as some participants expressed, they are expected to behave in a certain way, especially in the rural parts of the country, which is evident in the present study. Caste is another cultural barrier in most South Asian settings. One participant mentioned that his social relationships were restricted among the few people in the village, as most of the villagers belong to the lower caste. Thereby, he was not willing to engage in many community activities organised in the village. Some participants were reluctant to engage in physical activities as they thought it was not appropriate to exercise at an older age and, more importantly, they might become alienated in their society.

I am suffering from diabetes and on drug treatment. The doctor always says that I am too fat, and I was asked to do some exercises. I feel shy to go out and do exercises (smiling). No one in our age does so, also it is not nice for an old lady to do exercises like young people. (a woman in her mid-70s).

Belief

Some participants had restricted dietary habits due to cultural beliefs. Some older people have become religiously bound, and they adhere to religious rituals rather than a healthy lifestyle. A few participants became vegetarians and restricted their day-to-day activities to the household premises. They always believed in the next birth or reincarnation and thus mostly engaged with religious work that could benefit them in the next life.

I used to eat all kinds of meat and fish earlier. But now I don’t eat those. Now I believe that it is not good to eat the meat of other animals. Now I eat vegetables only. I think it is a kind of charity work, good for the next birth. (a man in his mid-60s)

Change in the usual living environment

This happened due to many factors. As people become older, they have to adhere to the orders and instructions given by their caregivers. Two subthemes were identified within this major theme.

Urbanisation

Due to multiple socioeconomic factors, some older people have to shift their houses, yet they may lose their freedom and autonomy. It was difficult for older people to adapt to the new environment that would restrict their lifestyle. Some participants had lived in rural villages, but the existing socioeconomic context forced them to migrate to urban areas. Though it was not a pleasant experience for them, some older people did not have any other choice. That negatively impacted on their natural behaviours and healthy lifestyle too.

I lived in the village, and most of my friends and relatives are still there. I used to do paddy cultivation and was a member of the temple club. I enjoyed village life very much. Now I stay with my younger daughter here. I developed a heart problem, so my daughter pushed me to stay with her as she felt I was safer in the town. But I am restricted to this 8-perch small land. There is no one to talk to. I felt isolated during the daytime. I had enough activities in the village; now there is hardly any. She (daughter) bought me an exercise machine, but I am reluctant to use it, and it is not like walking across the paddy field. (a man in his early 70s)

Adaptation

Difficulties in adaptation to a new environment affect the lifestyle of older people. Even though they are offered better options for a healthy lifestyle, older people were not willing to accept new modifications, as shown by the following:

I have a house and other properties in the village. I have one daughter and two sons. But no one was interested in living in the village. They said that there are not enough facilities for their children. Now I also had to come with them. But I could not enjoy this life. I am not familiar with city life. We didn’t have to do exercises as we were more active in the village. We had fresh vegetables and fruits there. Now I am thinking of going back. Now we eat polluted ones in the city. I cannot even sleep here as I wish. I am thinking of going back to my village home. (a man in his early 70s)

Discussion

As is evident from the present study, the reasons for older people not adhering to healthy lifestyles are complex and multifaceted. These include a combination of physical, psychological, social, economic and environmental barriers, as well as a lack of tailored health-promoting opportunities. Such barriers may not surface without proper exploration; therefore, the present qualitative study was able to uncover underlying factors that influence why older people do not practise healthy lifestyles as expected.

Twenty-eight in-depth interviews were conducted to explore perceived barriers among participants. This method provided participants with greater freedom to express their individual views compared with focus group discussions. Furthermore, in-depth interviews are considered more appropriate for identifying sensitive issues among participants.22 In the present study, several participants expressed personal and sensitive experiences related to lifestyle changes that may not have emerged in a focus group setting. Thus, in-depth interviews, combined with the observation of non-verbal cues, added further depth to the data and contributed to a more comprehensive understanding of the research problem.23

All interviews were conducted by the PI using a semistructured interview guide, which helped minimise potential interobserver bias.24 As described by other researchers, purposive sampling25 26 was employed in the present study to select information-rich participants representing different societal strata. Thematic analysis was used to identify patterns and themes within the data, a method widely applied in qualitative research across different contexts.27 28 The findings of the study were analysed and presented according to Braun and Clarke’s Reflexive Thematic Analysis Six-phase Framework.21 In addition, to ensure transparency and rigour in reporting, the study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research 32-item checklist (online supplemental material 3).

The findings indicate that many older people do not have a clear understanding of how to modify their lifestyles to improve their health. Existing health education programmes either did not reach them or were insufficient to address their knowledge gaps. Similarly, financial insecurity emerged as another major barrier preventing older people from adopting healthy lifestyles, a finding that is consistent with other qualitative studies.29 Lack of knowledge and inadequate dissemination of relevant health information have also been identified as barriers to lifestyle modification even in high-income settings. For example, a qualitative study conducted in Washington, USA, reported that lack of professional guidance and insufficient information regarding appropriate lifestyle modification options were key barriers among older people.30

Poor health status was another major barrier to lifestyle modification among participants. Similar findings have been reported in qualitative studies conducted in other countries. Moon et al31 identified functional decline as an intrapersonal barrier to practising health-promoting behaviours among older women in Korea.31 Furthermore, evidence suggests that multiple comorbidities among older people can negatively affect various aspects of life, including functional status and quality of life.32 Comparable findings were also reported by Horne et al in a qualitative study conducted in the UK. According to their findings, both British and Asian older adults living in the UK perceived that engaging in physical activity could exacerbate existing health problems and potentially cause physical harm.33

Some participants in the present study reported domestic responsibilities as a barrier to adopting healthy lifestyle practices. Several participants mentioned that they were responsible for caring for grandchildren, while others supported their spouses in household responsibilities. Similar barriers to social participation were identified by Marsh et al, who reported that older women often engaged in domestic tasks such as cooking and caring for grandchildren, which limited their opportunities to participate in social or health-promoting activities.29 Cultural norms were also identified as a barrier to lifestyle modification among some participants. These perceptions were more common among rural older adults, as social expectations within their communities discouraged participation in activities such as exercise or sports. Similar findings have been reported in other qualitative studies, where older people expressed discomfort or embarrassment about exercising in public spaces, and cultural expectations were identified as major barriers to engaging in physical activity.34 Marsh et al also reported that Tamil widows experienced social discrimination following the death of their spouses, and in some communities, widows were considered to bring bad luck and were therefore excluded from social events.29 Such sociocultural beliefs may act as barriers to maintaining healthy lifestyles among older adults in South Asian contexts. Similar cultural and religious influences have also been identified among South Asian older adults living in the UK.33 Participants also reported several challenges related to changes in their living environments.

Many participants experienced difficulties adapting to new environments after relocating, which negatively affected their lifestyle behaviours. Some participants reported feelings of distress and social isolation due to unfamiliar surroundings, even when they had access to improved facilities. These findings highlight the importance of considering the living environment when designing lifestyle interventions for older people. The living environment plays a key role in shaping healthy behaviours among older adults, as supported by previous research findings.35 Therefore, environmental and contextual factors should be carefully considered when planning health promotion programmes aimed at improving lifestyle behaviours among older populations.

Limitations

All participants were identified during the district survey and were purposively selected to obtain in-depth information relevant to the research question. However, because the sample was not representative of the wider population, the generalisability of the findings may be limited. Additionally, transcripts were not returned to participants for member checking; therefore, participant feedback on the findings could not be obtained.

Conclusions

In conclusion, adherence to a healthy lifestyle among older people is not merely a matter of individual choice or willingness, but often reflects a range of structural, social and personal barriers. Addressing these challenges requires a comprehensive and holistic approach when designing lifestyle interventions for older populations. Future lifestyle programmes should therefore be tailored to the specific needs and contextual realities of older people in order to effectively reduce barriers and promote healthier lifestyles.
