Outcomes after laparoscopic transhiatal esophagectomy for siewert type I esophagogastric junction adenocarcinoma: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Saeed Sadreniac, Hassan Mohammadpour, Ali Yavari, Shima Nasirnia, Mahdi Mohebbi, Amirhossein Karimi, Sina Salimi Nasab, Amir Abbas Bagheri, Farzad Dehghani Mahmoudabadi, Seyed Mohamad Hossein Tabatabaei Nodoushan, Amin Esfandiari Kalajahi, Mohammad Mehdi Shadravan, Helia Ghorbani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSiewert type I esophagogastric junction (EGJ) adenocarcinoma remains surgically challenging, and the role of transhiatal esophagectomy (THE) is debated, particularly in high-incidence and resource-variable settings.MethodsThis two-center retrospective cohort study included 101 adults with Siewert type I EGJ adenocarcinoma who underwent laparoscopic THE (January 2020–December 2022). Perioperative morbidity (Clavien–Dindo), 90-day mortality, and stage-stratified overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) were assessed; neoadjuvant treatment varied in routine practice. OS/DFS were estimated using Kaplan–Meier methods and compared by log-rank tests. Prespecified time-point endpoints (OS status at 36 months; DFS status at 18 months) were modeled using multivariable logistic regression.ResultsMean age was 63.4 ± 8.9 years and 64.4% were male. Overall 90-day morbidity occurred in 47.5% and 90-day mortality was 7.9%. Pneumonia/aspiration occurred in 14.9%, delayed gastric emptying in 23.8%, and cervical anastomotic leak in 3.9% (managed non-operatively). R0 resection was achieved in 95.0% with a median lymph node yield of 11. Stage-stratified survival differed significantly (log-rank p < 0.001); three-year OS for pathologic stage III was 30%. At 18 months, DFS was 70% for stage 0–I and 54% for stage II–III. Advanced pathologic stage (II–III vs. 0–I) was independently associated with worse OS status at 36 months (OR 3.94; p < 0.001) and DFS status at 18 months (OR 3.39; p < 0.001).ConclusionsIn this single-arm cohort, laparoscopic THE was associated with perioperative outcomes and stage-dependent survival estimates. In the absence of a comparative transthoracic cohort, findings should be interpreted descriptively and support prospective comparative studies incorporating long-term and patient-centered outcomes.

Introduction

Cancer of the gastroesophageal junction (EGJ) is an anatomically and biologically heterogeneous malignancy with a substantial global disease burden. Esophageal adenocarcinoma and junctional adenocarcinomas contribute meaningfully to cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide, and their management remains challenging despite advances in multimodal therapy [1, 2]. The Siewert classification (types I–III), defined by the tumor epicenter relative to the EGJ, is widely applied to support operative planning and to contextualize expected patterns of lymphatic spread [3]. Given the distinct surgical implications of Siewert type I tumors, the present work focuses on locally advanced Siewert type I EGJ adenocarcinoma.

For Siewert type I EGJ adenocarcinoma, esophagectomy within a multidisciplinary pathway—frequently incorporating neoadjuvant therapy for locally advanced disease—remains central to curative-intent management [4, 5]. However, the operative approach must balance perioperative cardiopulmonary risk against oncologic requirements for adequate lymphadenectomy. Transthoracic esophagectomy (TTE) facilitates broader access to intrathoracic and mediastinal lymph node stations, whereas transhiatal esophagectomy (THE) avoids thoracotomy and has been associated with reduced pulmonary morbidity in selected patients [6, 7]. At the same time, mediastinal nodal involvement is clinically relevant in Siewert type I tumors and appears to be influenced by tumor characteristics, including the extent of esophageal invasion; evidence from EGJ-specific nodal mapping studies and contemporary consensus guidance supports tailoring the extent of mediastinal dissection to invasion length and other risk features [8–10]. Because THE continues to be utilized for selected high-risk patients in routine practice, transparent reporting of perioperative and survival outcomes remains clinically relevant for perioperative risk counseling and pragmatic surgical decision-making.

Postoperative morbidity is a major determinant of both short- and long-term outcomes after esophagectomy. Severe complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ III) are common, prolong recovery, increase healthcare utilization, and have been associated with inferior long-term survival in contemporary evidence syntheses [11, 12]. Accordingly, accurate characterization of perioperative morbidity—particularly severe complications—alongside stage-stratified survival outcomes is essential when evaluating a surgical strategy in clinical practice.

Iran is located within the Asian esophageal cancer belt, and population-based data from high-risk regions such as Golestan indicate a substantial burden of upper gastrointestinal malignancies [13–15]. Nevertheless, region-specific evidence describing perioperative outcomes and stage-stratified survival following standardized THE for locally advanced Siewert type I EGJ adenocarcinoma remains limited. The present retrospective cohort study was therefore conducted at two tertiary centers to (i) characterize perioperative morbidity (including severe complications and 90-day mortality) and (ii) report stage-stratified survival outcomes following transhiatal esophagectomy for locally advanced Siewert type I EGJ adenocarcinoma.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study was designed and conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational research [16]. Patients with non-metastatic Siewert type I gastroesophageal junction (EGJ) adenocarcinoma who underwent transhiatal esophagectomy (THE) between January 2020 and December 2022 at two tertiary referral centers in Iran were included. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Alborz University of Medical Sciences (IR.ABZUMS.REC.1402.300). Given the retrospective design, the requirement for informed consent was waived. Data were anonymized prior to analysis.

Participants

Adult patients (≥ 18 years) with histologically confirmed gastroesophageal junction (EGJ) adenocarcinoma were eligible if they met al.l of the following criteria: (i) Siewert type I tumor, defined as a tumor epicenter located 1–5 cm above the esophagogastric junction; (ii) non-metastatic disease at presentation (cM0) according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system [17]; and (iii) transhiatal esophagectomy during the study period.

Pre-treatment staging was determined using endoscopy with biopsy and cross-sectional imaging (contrast-enhanced thoracoabdominal computed tomography), supplemented by additional modalities such as endoscopic ultrasonography and/or PET-CT when available in routine practice, to confirm the absence of distant metastasis. Neoadjuvant treatment exposure (chemotherapy, chemoradiotherapy, or none) was recorded as a baseline variable.

Patients were excluded if they had metastatic disease at diagnosis (cM1), Siewert type II–III tumors, or a history of prior esophageal surgery. Records lacking essential information for ascertainment of key study outcomes (e.g., date of surgery and vital status or last follow-up date) were excluded.

Surgical approach selection

During the study period, laparoscopic transhiatal esophagectomy was the standard operative approach for Siewert type I EGJ adenocarcinoma at both participating centers. The surgical approach was selected within routine multidisciplinary care and informed by preoperative assessment of tumor extent and patient-related operative risk. Patients deemed unsuitable for curative-intent resection (e.g., metastatic disease or prohibitive operative risk) were not considered for esophagectomy and were therefore not included in this cohort.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed by the same high-volume esophageal surgeon (> 50 esophagectomies/year) at both participating centers using a standardized laparoscopic transhiatal esophagectomy (THE) technique, with center-specific perioperative teams.

Lymphadenectomy

Abdominal nodal dissection was performed en bloc and included lymph node stations along the cardia (station 1), lesser curvature (station 3), common hepatic artery (station 8), celiac axis (station 9), and splenic artery (station 11).

Mediastinal dissection

Posterior mediastinal dissection was carried out under direct visualization through the diaphragmatic hiatus. The descending aorta was gently retracted dorsally and mediastinal pleura were preserved bilaterally whenever feasible. Dissection included lower posterior mediastinal paraesophageal tissue and was extended cranially up to the level of the carina while maintaining anatomical orientation and avoiding pleural violation. The thoracic duct was preserved unless suspected to be involved.

Reconstruction

A gastric conduit based on the right gastroepiploic arcade was fashioned (typically ~ 4–6 cm in width) and delivered through the posterior mediastinum. A cervical end-to-end esophagogastric anastomosis was created using hand-sewn 4 − 0 PDS sutures. Intraoperative frozen-section assessment of proximal and distal margins was performed when indicated (e.g., when margin status was clinically suspected) to support R0 resection.

Postoperative management

Enteral nutrition was initiated via jejunostomy on postoperative day 1. Oral intake was commenced between postoperative days 3 and 7 after assessment of anastomotic integrity using a water-soluble contrast swallow study. Suspected anastomotic leaks were evaluated radiologically and managed according to institutional protocols. Perioperative care followed an institutional ERAS-based pathway [18] incorporating early enteral feeding, restrictive fluid management, venous thromboembolism prophylaxis, and multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia to facilitate early mobilization.

Neoadjuvant therapy

Neoadjuvant treatment was administered according to institutional protocols and recorded from electronic and physical medical records. Patients were categorized based on preoperative treatment exposure as follows: no neoadjuvant therapy, neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone, or neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy.

For patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy, platinum/fluoropyrimidine-based regimens were used according to institutional practice. For patients receiving neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, systemic therapy was delivered in combination with external beam radiotherapy (45 Gy in 25 fractions) using either three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3D-CRT) or intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), depending on local availability and treatment planning.

When documented, reasons for omission of neoadjuvant therapy included clinical contraindications to systemic therapy and/or radiotherapy, urgent need for surgery due to obstructive symptoms, and patient- or system-related factors; these were not consistently recorded and were not quantified.

Outcome measures

The co-primary outcomes were overall survival (OS) and severe postoperative morbidity. OS was defined as the time from surgery to death from any cause. Severe postoperative morbidity was defined as Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ III [19] complications occurring within 90 days after surgery.

Secondary outcomes included disease-free survival (DFS), defined as the time from surgery to the first documented recurrence (locoregional or distant) or death from any cause, whichever occurred first; lymph node yield (total number of resected lymph nodes per specimen); and additional perioperative outcomes including operative time, intraoperative blood loss, transfusion requirement, length of hospital stay, readmission, reoperation (within 90 days), and 90-day mortality.

Postoperative complications were abstracted from the medical records and graded according to the Clavien–Dindo classification by the study team; ambiguous cases were reviewed and resolved by consensus with the operating surgeon. Complications were further classified by timing (early: ≤30 days; late: 31–90 days) and by organ system (infectious, pulmonary, gastrointestinal, neurologic, cardiovascular, thromboembolic). Patients without an event were censored at the date of last follow-up. Kaplan–Meier curves were plotted as step functions directly from the survival estimates without interpolation.

Histopathological assessment

All resected specimens were reviewed independently by two gastrointestinal pathologists who were blinded to postoperative complications and survival/recurrence outcomes. Discrepancies were resolved by joint review and consensus. Pathologic staging (pT and pN) was assigned according to the AJCC 8th edition criteria. Tumor regression grade (TRG) was assessed using the Mandard system [20] in patients who received neoadjuvant therapy. Lymphovascular invasion (LVI) was recorded when tumor cells were identified within lymphatic or blood vessels.

Postoperative follow-up

Postoperative follow-up followed an institutional surveillance protocol informed by NCCN guidelines for esophagogastric cancer [21] and was abstracted retrospectively from HIS, PACS, and physical medical records. Patients were typically reviewed monthly for the first 3 months and then every 3 months up to 24 months. Contrast-enhanced thoracoabdominal CT was generally performed every 6 months during the first two years. Upper endoscopy was performed at approximately 6 and 12 months postoperatively or as clinically indicated. CEA and CA19-9 were obtained according to treating clinicians’ practice. Vital status and recurrence events were determined from follow-up documentation and medical records; for survival analyses, patients without an event were censored at the last recorded follow-up. Late postoperative sequelae (e.g., dumping syndrome, delayed gastric emptying, and malabsorption) were recorded throughout the entire follow-up period.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics, clinicopathologic characteristics, and perioperative outcomes. Continuous variables were assessed for distributional assumptions and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when approximately normally distributed, or as median (interquartile range [IQR]) when skewed. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. DFS events were defined as the first occurrence of documented recurrence or death, whichever occurred first.

For multivariable modelling of survival endpoints, event-based binary outcomes were defined at pre-specified time horizons: death by 36 months for OS, and recurrence-or-death by 18 months for DFS. These endpoints were analyzed using multivariable logistic regression and reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Patients were eligible for each time-point model if they experienced the event before the specified horizon or had follow-up of at least the specified duration, allowing ascertainment of status at that time point; patients with shorter follow-up and no event before the horizon were excluded from the corresponding time-point model due to indeterminate endpoint status.

Separate multivariable logistic regression models were used to identify predictors of early (≤ 30 days) and late (31–90 days) postoperative complications. Candidate covariates were pre-specified a priori based on clinical relevance and availability in the dataset. Operative time was modeled per 10-minute increase. Severe early complications (Clavien–Dindo grade III–IV within 30 days) were additionally evaluated as a predictor of subsequent late complications (31–90 days).

All statistical tests were two-sided, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 26.0) and R (version 4.3.1). No imputation procedures were applied and analyses were performed using complete-case data.

Results

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 101 patients were included. The mean age was 63.4 ± 8.9 years and the mean BMI was 27.05 ± 4.12 kg/m²; 65 patients (64.4%) were male. Hypoalbuminemia (serum albumin < 3.5 g/dL) was present in 71 patients (70.3%). Most patients had an ASA score of II (76/101, 75.2%). Cardiovascular comorbidities were recorded in 45 patients (44.6%), and 70 patients (69.3%) had a history of smoking. Clinical staging showed that most tumors were classified as cT2 (75/101, 74.3%), with nodal involvement (cN+) in 62 patients (61.4%). Neoadjuvant treatment exposure included chemotherapy in 49 patients (48.5%), chemoradiotherapy in 15 patients (14.9%), and no neoadjuvant therapy in 37 patients (36.6%). Postoperative outcomes

The median operative time was 200 min [180–400], and the median estimated blood loss was 250 mL [0–3000]. Blood transfusion was required in 14 patients (13.9%), and drainage for thoracic/mediastinal collections was performed in 12 patients (11.9%). The median length of hospital stay was 12.5 days [10–19]. Reoperation and hospital readmission within 90 days occurred in 11 (10.9%) and 15 (14.9%) patients, respectively.

Early postoperative complications (≤ 30 days) included pneumonia/aspiration (14.9%), prolonged intubation > 48 h (10.9%), atrial dysrhythmia (7.9%), and anastomotic leak (3.9%). Additional early events included unplanned reintubation (5.9%), recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy (4.0%), chylothorax (2.0%), pulmonary embolism (2.0%), and deep vein thrombosis (3.0%) (Table 2). Surgical site infections occurred in 11 patients (10.9%) and included superficial (5.0%), deep (2.0%), and organ-space infections (4.0%). Rates are reported per patient; patients could experience more than one complication.

Within 90 days, postoperative morbidity (Clavien–Dindo grades I–IV) occurred in 48 patients (47.5%), including grade I/II in 30 (29.7%), grade IIIa/IIIb in 14 (13.9%), and grade IV in 4 (4.0%). Ninety-day mortality (grade V) was 7.9% (8/101) (Table 4). Late postoperative sequelae recorded over the entire follow-up period are shown in Table 3.

Postoperative histopathological characteristics

Among 101 patients, an R0 resection (negative margins) was achieved in 96/101 (95.0%) cases, while 5/101 (5.0%) had microscopically positive margins (R1). The most common depth of invasion was T2 (53/101, 52.5%), followed by T1 (23/101, 22.8%); T0–Tis was reported in 16/101 (15.8%). Nodal metastases were identified in 34/101 patients (33.7%) (N1: 24.7%; N2: 8.9%). The median lymph node yield was 11 [6–26]. Among node-positive patients, the median number of positive lymph nodes was 3 [1–15]. Tumor differentiation was well in 44/101 (43.6%), moderate in 37/101 (36.6%), and poor in 18/101 (17.8%); mucinous adenocarcinoma histology was observed in 2/101 (2.0%). Overall survival by pathologic stag

Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated significant differences in overall survival across pathologic stage groups (AJCC 8th edition). One-year OS estimates were 90.9% for stage 0, 84.6% for stage I, 67.6% for stage II, and 40.6% for stage III; at 36 months, OS was 68.2%, 61.5%, 44.1%, and 31.3%, respectively. Survival differed significantly across stages (log-rank p = 0.011) (Fig. 1).

Predictors of postoperative complications

Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate factors associated with early (≤ 30 days) and late (31–90 days) postoperative complications (Table 6; Fig. 3). For early complications, low preoperative serum albumin (< 3.5 g/dL), comorbidities, smoking history, higher clinical T stage (cT2 + vs. cT1), receipt of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (CRT vs. non-CRT), and intraoperative blood transfusion were associated with increased odds of early morbidity. For late complications, comorbidities and higher clinical T stage were associated with increased odds. In addition, severe early complications (Clavien–Dindo grade III–IV within 30 days) were associated with subsequent late complications. Age, sex, BMI, ASA class, and operative time (per 10-minute increase) were not significantly associated with early or late complications.

Discussion

Esophagogastric junction (EGJ) adenocarcinoma, particularly Siewert type I tumors, represents a complex surgical entity located at the interface of thoracic and abdominal oncologic domains. Ongoing debate persists regarding the optimal operative strategy, especially in patients with locally advanced disease, where both oncologic clearance and perioperative risk must be carefully balanced [1, 2, 6, 7]. While transthoracic esophagectomy (TTE) enables extended mediastinal lymphadenectomy, it is also associated with increased pulmonary morbidity and perioperative physiological stress, particularly in older or comorbid populations [7]. Transhiatal esophagectomy (THE) has therefore been proposed as a potential alternative approach in carefully selected patients, although concerns regarding lymph node yield and long-term oncologic outcomes remain [6–9].

The present two-center retrospective cohort provides real-world estimates of perioperative morbidity and stage-stratified survival outcomes following laparoscopic THE for Siewert type I EGJ adenocarcinoma in a high-incidence region. Overall postoperative morbidity and 90-day mortality were within the range reported in contemporary esophagectomy benchmarks [11], with pulmonary complications and gastrointestinal sequelae constituting the most frequent adverse events. Cervical anastomotic leakage occurred at a relatively low rate and was managed conservatively, consistent with prior evidence suggesting that cervical leaks, while more frequent than intrathoracic leaks, are generally associated with less severe clinical consequences [6, 22]. Recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy was infrequent, reflecting careful cervical dissection and aligning with reported ranges in modern esophagectomy series [24].

From an oncologic perspective, R0 resection was achieved in the vast majority of patients, and the observed lymph node yield was comparable to that reported in other transhiatal series, albeit slightly lower than yields typically described for extended transthoracic approaches [6–9]. Importantly, survival analyses demonstrated clear stage-stratified separation of overall and disease-free survival curves, underscoring the dominant prognostic role of pathological stage. In multivariable analyses, advanced pathological stage was independently associated with worse overall survival status at 36 months and disease-free survival status at 18 months, findings that are consistent with established oncologic principles and prior large-scale analyses [12, 23]. These results should be interpreted as descriptive rather than comparative, as the study was not designed to assess oncologic equivalence between surgical approaches.

Postoperative complications emerged as clinically relevant determinants of subsequent outcomes. Severe early complications were strongly associated with late postoperative morbidity, reinforcing prior observations that postoperative adverse events exert downstream effects on recovery trajectories and long-term health status [12]. This finding highlights the importance of perioperative optimization, meticulous surgical technique, and early complication mitigation, particularly in patients with compromised nutritional or functional reserves.

Several contextual factors merit consideration. The study cohort was drawn from a high-incidence region within the so-called “Asian esophageal cancer belt,” where environmental exposures, smoking, and nutritional deficiencies are prevalent and may influence both perioperative risk and oncologic outcomes [13–15]. In such settings, surgical strategies that limit physiological insult while maintaining acceptable oncologic metrics may hold particular relevance. However, the heterogeneity of neoadjuvant treatment regimens in this cohort—including chemotherapy, chemoradiotherapy, or no preoperative therapy—introduces additional complexity in interpreting pathological stage distribution and survival outcomes.

This study has important limitations. Its retrospective, single-arm design precludes causal inference or direct comparison with transthoracic or minimally invasive transthoracic approaches. Selection bias and residual confounding cannot be excluded, despite multivariable adjustment. Lymph node yield was moderate, which may influence staging accuracy. Follow-up duration, with a mean of 18 months, limits interpretation of late survival estimates. In addition, patient-reported outcomes and quality-of-life measures were not assessed, despite their recognized importance in contemporary esophageal cancer care.

Despite these limitations, the study benefits from procedural standardization, consistent perioperative care pathways, and detailed complication reporting. The findings provide valuable real-world data from an underrepresented, high-burden region and may inform hypothesis generation and future comparative research.

In conclusion, laparoscopic transhiatal esophagectomy in selected patients with Siewert type I EGJ adenocarcinoma was associated with acceptable perioperative outcomes and stage-dependent survival in this cohort. While avoidance of thoracotomy may offer perioperative advantages in appropriately selected patients, definitive conclusions regarding comparative oncologic efficacy require prospective, controlled studies integrating long-term survival and patient-centered outcomes.
