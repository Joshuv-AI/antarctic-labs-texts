Neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin and fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio are indicators of kidney disease in sickle cell disease patients with microalbuminuria: a multicentre case-control study in Ghana
Authors: Stephen Twumasi, Enoch Odame Anto, Christian Obirikorang, Richard Kobina Dadzie Ephraim, Benedict Sackey, Vivian Paintsil, Richard Owusu Ansah, Alfred Effah, Allwell Adofo Ayirebi, Angela Opoku, Godfred Yawson Scott, Leslie Osei, Joyce Duku, Emmanuel Asafo Adjei, Lilian Antwi Boateng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) is present in secondary granules of neutrophils and it is a relatively newly recognized marker of kidney diseases. The fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio (FAR) is a marker of inflammation but its diagnostic value has not been determined in sickle cell disease patients with kidney diseases. This study investigated the diagnostic roles of serum neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (sNGAL) and FAR for kidney diseases in steady-state adult sickle cell disease (SCD) patients.MethodsThis study employed a prospective case-control design and recruited 104 SCD participants and 80 non-SCD patients. Participants’ information was thoroughly documented using a structured questionnaire and patient case records. To evaluate the hematobiochemical parameters, 5 ml of venous blood was drawn from each participant and a clean catch of midstream urine was collected from each participant. The cases and controls were further categorized into microalbuminuria and non-microalbuminuria subjects, following three consecutive urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) measurements.ResultsThe prevalence of microalbuminuria was 32.7% among adult steady-state SCD patients. Significant higher levels of sNGAL and FAR were detected in SCD patients with microalbuminuria than in SCD patients without microalbuminuria and controls (p < 0.001). A moderate positive correlation was observed between sNGAL and UACR (r = 0.45, p = 0.007). A unit increase in sNGAL (cOR: 3.25 (2.11-5.00); p < 0.001), aOR: 3.35(2.09–5.36); p < 0.0001)) and FAR (Log cOR: 12.26 (1.82–25.09); p = 0.022) were significantly associated with increased odds of kidney disease among SCD participants. sNGAL emerged as a highly early predictive marker for kidney disease in SCD patients, with a cutoff value of > 5.72 µg/L yielding a high area under the curve (AUC = 0.854, p < 0.0001). sNGAL also demonstrated an excellent sensitivity (91.2%) and moderate specificity (74.7%). The FAR at a cutoff of > 0.09 also demonstrated significant predictive value (AUC = 0.630, p = 0.009) for kidney disease in SCD patients, with a moderate sensitivity (67.6%) and specificity (61.3%).ConclusionBased on our findings, sNGAL could serve as an independent early predictor of kidney disease compared with urea and creatinine. Additionally, the fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio can be used as inflammatory marker for kidney diseases in SCD patients.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Sickle cell disease, Sickle cell nephropathy, Kidney disease, Serum neutrophil gelatinase- associated lipocalin, NGAL, Fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio, Microalbuminuria

Introduction

Renal injury is a common consequence of sickle cell disease (SCD) caused by long-term anaemia and disrupted circulation through the medullar capillaries [1]. Between 5% and 18% of sickle cell disease patients experience renal failure [2]. Renal failure affects 4–21% of adult SCD patients due to nephropathy in the United States [3]. According to a study conducted in Ghana by Ephraim et al., (2015), the prevalence of CKD on the basis of a reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in the overall SCD population was found to be 39.2%, with adults having a prevalence of 68.4% and children having a prevalence of 31.6% [4]. Microalbuminuria refers to any abnormal increase in the albumin excretion rate that falls between 30 and 299 mg of albumin/g of creatinine. A study revealed that 32.5% of paediatric SCD patients exhibited microalbuminuria [5]. Another study reported an even higher microalbuminuria prevalence of 61% among adult SCD patients, indicating a critical need for routine screening [6].

The pathophysiology of glomerular disease and kidney failure in sickle cell disease is influenced by glomerular hyperfiltration. Nitric oxide synthesis and prostaglandin synthesis, which are generated in reaction to medullary ischaemia, both promote the rise in renal blood, resulting in glomerular hyperfiltration. Also, there seems to be a favourable relationship between persistent haemolysis and glomerular hyperfiltration. Combinations of glomerular ischemia (resulting in hypoperfusion), hypertrophy, and other unknown reasons lead to progressive kidney failure, or glomerulosclerosis in SCD patients [7]. Because of this “hyperfiltration” phenomena, GFR calculations based on creatinine are erroneous [8]. The diagnosis of hyperfiltration may be challenging in SCD due to evidence of creatinine hypersecretion in the proximal tubule [9]. SCD patients frequently have glomerular hyperfiltration and extremely low creatinine values [10]. Creatinine clearance increases due to hyper flow in the kidneys and higher creatinine secretion by the proximal tubules, and can attain levels of up to 160 mL/min. Thus, creatinine clearance is not an appropriate test for evaluating kidney function in SCD [10].

The detection of increased renal impairment via conventional markers such as serum creatinine (Cr) levels, or creatinine clearance (Ccr) and urea are frequently deceptive. Serum creatinine, the primary clinical tool for measuring renal function is insensitive for detecting moderate alterations in renal function and is affected by factors unrelated to renal activity, such as age, sex, race, and lean muscle mass [11]. Conventional renal function tests such as serum creatinine, urea and the glomerular filtration rate show abnormal results in SCD only when there is extensive renal damage [12]. There is a growing need to develop more sensitive markers for the early detection of renal dysfunction in SCD patients since conventional markers provide late indications of irreversible kidney damage.

NGAL also known as lipocalin-2 is a protein with a molecular weight of 25 kDa was discovered in 1993 [13]. It is produced and stored in the secondary granules of neutrophils and released under different medical conditions [14]. In clinical settings, it acts as a marker for early diagnosis of kidney damage [15]. It contains small hydrophobic compounds and regulates innate immune responses. NGAL was suggested to be a biomarker of acute kidney injury (AKI) even in the setting of chronic kidney disease and has a strong association with albuminuria [16]. NGAL is one of the earliest substances released into the urine following ischemia and acute kidney injury. However, studies on the use of NGAL as a marker of renal tubular damage and sickle cell nephropathy in SCD are limited [17]. Multiple investigations have revealed significant increase in the concentration of NGAL in both serum and urine, which has been related to a number of renal diseases [18]. As a result, NGAL has been found to be a useful indicator for evaluating acute renal damage and renal failure [19]. NGAL was discovered to be a sensitive tool and an early biomarker for acute kidney injury in an SCD cohort, as its blood measurement appeared to predict the onset of AKI in SCD patients [20]. By comparing serum NGAL (sNGAL) with serum creatinine in hypertensive people, another study carried out in Ghana to evaluate the diagnostic performance of NGAL as an indicator for the identification of renal failure revealed that sNGAL was a superior indicator of kidney damage in the early stages of CKD [21].

An important component in the development of kidney disease in SCD patients is inflammation. The significant pathophysiological effects of SCD include haemolytic events and the formation of inflammatory processes that ultimately lead to vaso-occlusion. One biomarker for inflammation and coagulation is serum fibrinogen (FIB) [22]. Fibrinogen levels are elevated in both chronic low-grade inflammation and acute and tissue-damaging inflammation. Uadia and Gadzama [23] reported that increasing levels of urea and creatinine were linked to elevated FIB levels in patients with SCD who had renal impairment. Albumin (ALB) possesses antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [24]. In individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus and diabetic kidney disease, low albumin levels are linked to a worse prognosis for their kidneys [25]. The most abundant plasma protein, human serum albumin, is produced in the liver and discharged into the vascular space, where it travels throughout the body [26]. It is critical for maintaining homeostasis and balancing hydrostatic and colloid osmotic pressures within vessels [27]. A significant relationship between blood ALB levels and renal prognosis was discovered by Zhang et al. in 2019 [28]. Serum ALB levels are inversely correlated with glomerular lesions and proteinuria, two established risk factors for the development of diabetic nephropathy, which may account for the relationship between renal prognosis and hypoalbuminemia [25]. Participants with type 2 diabetes and diabetic renal disease had a worse prognosis when their albumin level was low [25]. According to Zhang et al. (2020), in a cancer trial, the fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio (FAR) was a more significant predictive factor of cancer than each single marker itself [29]. Combining these two inflammation markers and using their ratios would be best for detecting renal inflammation in SCD patients. The diagnostic significance of FAR in SCD patients with kidney disease has not yet been studied.

Identifying progressive renal injury with traditional parameters, including creatinine and urea, is often misleading [11, 30]. Hence, there is a need to consider markers such as sNGAL and FAR, which can predict and facilitate early diagnosis of renal impairment in SCD patients. The purpose of this study was to determine whether sNGAL and FAR could be used as indicators of kidney disease in SCD patients.

Materials and methods

Study design, duration and study setting

The present study utilized a prospective case-control design and it was conducted at Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital (KATH), Kumasi South Government Hospital and Suntreso Government Hospital from November 2023 to August 2024. The KATH is located at Bantama, Kumasi, Ashanti Region, Ghana. It is the sole tertiary healthcare facility in the Ashanti Region and the second-largest hospital in Ghana. Kumasi South Hospital is also located at Atonsu Agogo and Suntreso Government Hospital is located at Suntreso. These facilities are all situated in the Kumasi Metropolis in the Ashanti Region of Ghana. They are 24-hour working health facilities that boast services such as laboratories, radiology, obstetrics and gynecology, surgery, pediatrics, internal medicine and emergencies, among others. They also have a sickle cell clinic from which SCD participants were recruited for the study.

Ethical clearance and informed consent

This study was conducted based on the Helsinki Declaration and the study protocol, consent forms and participant information material were reviewed and approved by the Committee for Human Research Publication and Ethics of Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital (KATH IRB/AP/175/23) and Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology’s (CHRPE/AP/1037/23). Permission was sought from the research facility of Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital (KATH), the Suntreso Government Hospital and the Kumasi South Government Hospital before commencement of study. Written informed consent was obtained from participants and confidentiality was maintained. In addition, the privacy rights of human subjects were observed.

Study population and selection of participants

In this study, the cases were classified as individuals who had been medically diagnosed with SCD. Additionally, controls were defined as individuals with a confirmed hemoglobin A (Hb’A’) phenotype. The cases and controls were further categorized into microalbuminuria and non-microalbuminuria groups on the basis of their UACRs.

The aim of the study was explained to the study participants. The processes involved in the study, including blood and urine sampling, data collection and laboratory assays, were subsequently explained to the eligible participants with the help of a clinician.

We then purposively recruited a total of 104 SCD patients and 80 non-SCD individuals, of which 51 non-SCD individuals were without microalbuminuria. The SCD patients were registered with the sickle cell clinic at the Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital and Kumasi South Government Hospital. They reported at the clinic for a review as and when directed by their physician. Owing to their clinical history, physicians in both hospitals assessed the SCD patients and declared them fit for the study before they were recruited. The controls were blood donors from the blood bank units of Suntreso Government Hospital and Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital.

Eligible SCD patients for the study were those aged 18 years and above who were in stable condition for at least a month and had no history of diabetes mellitus, arterial hypertension, neoplastic, cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, lung disease, or endocrine disorders. Additionally, SCD participants who had symptoms suggestive of sickle cell pain crisis, acute sickness (fever or requiring referral to an urgent care facility), clinically suspected urinary tract infection or extensive hematuria were excluded. Individuals without a history of SCD, diabetes mellitus, arterial hypertension, or neoplastic, cardiovascular, inflammatory, renal, lung or endocrine diseases were also eligible for the control group.

Data collection

Important demographic and clinical history information about the participants were recorded using a well-structured questionnaire and patient case records. A wall-mounted ruler was used to measure height. A Zhongshan Camry Electronic Co. Ltd. bathroom scale (Guangdong, China) was used to assess weight. Blood pressure was measured with sphygmomanometer following standard principles and protocols [31]. Estimates were made for height to the nearest centimetre without shoes and weight to the nearest 0.1 kg when wearing light clothes.

Sampling and analysis

Five-milliliter venous blood samples were collected from each participant into K3 EDTA, sodium citrate, and serum separator (SST) tubes following the standard protocol outlined by the World Health Organization (2010). A total of 1.8 ml was initially collected into a sodium citrate tube, 2.2 ml was collected into a serum separator tube (SST), and finally, 1 ml was collected into a 4 ml K3 EDTA tube. Whole blood in EDTA was used for haemogram analysis. Serum in the SST was used to analyze liver function markers, i.e., creatinine, urea, electrolytes (sodium, potassium and chloride) and sNGAL. Sodium citrate anticoagulated blood was also used for fibrinogen measurement. The participants were directed to collect midstream urine in a clean, dry, capped urine container. Early morning midstream urine samples were used for urine measurements.

Laboratory assays

Blood measurements

The participants’ haemoglobin variants were confirmed via cellulose acetate electrophoresis at pH 8.9 followed by high-performance liquid chromatography (Bio-Rad variant II dual program hemoglobin testing) and results reported as HbSS, HbSC and HbAA [32]. Hemogram analysis was performed via a five-part automated hematology analyzer XN-550 (XN-550; Sysmex Corporation, Kobe Japan). Kidney function tests (electrolytes, urea, and creatinine) and liver function tests (bilirubin, AST, ALT, ALP, GGT total protein and albumin) were analyzed on a Vitros 5600 integrated system [33, 34]. The estimated GFR (eGFR) was calculated via the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine Eq. (2009) (eGFRcreat). Fibrinogen was measured with a Wonfo Fibrinogen reagent kit and an OCG-102 coagulation analyzer. Fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio calculations involved dividing fibrinogen (FIB) by serum albumin (ALB) to obtain values. A commercially available ELISA kit (Melson Shanghai Chemical Ltd., China) was used for sNGAL measurement. Reagent was used in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions to measure samples from the controls and the participants via the solid-phase ELISA technique. The commonly accepted reference value for sNGAL is 5.68 ± 1.34 µg/L.

Urine measurements and culture

Urine samples were collected and analysed to evaluate various urinary parameters, while urine culture also served as a screening tool to exclude participants with Urinary Tract Infections (UTI). The urine was poured into a 10 ml plane centrifuge tube and urine strips with 10 parameters was dipped to check proteins and blood in the urine on the basis of color reactivity [35]. Participants with protein concentrations ≥ 30 mg/dl ( ≥ + 1) were considered to have proteinuria and those with ( ≥ + 1) blood were also considered to have haematuria. The urine was examined microscopically for the presence of crystals and casts. Using a sterile 10 µl inoculation loop, a 10 µl aliquot of each participant’s urine was inoculated onto Cystine-Lactose-Electrolyte Deficient (CLED) agar and incubated under aerobic conditions for 24 to 48 h [32, 36]. The resulting cultures were then evaluated, and participants with a colony count exceeding 1000 colony-forming units per millilitre (CFU/mL) were excluded from the study, indicating a significant urinary tract infection.

Microalbuminuria measurement and definition

The urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio was analyzed on a Vitros 5600 integrated system following standard protocols [33, 34]. In accordance with the 2024 KDIGO classification [37] for establishing chronic kidney disease, the UACR was measured on three separate occasions within six months for both SCD and non-SCD participants. Participants were diagnosed with microalbuminuria if their UACRs measured three times were greater than 30 mg/g [38]. Individuals with only one or only two UACR measurements greater than 30 mg/g were considered to have probable microalbuminuria and excluded. Only individuals with three consecutive UACR measurements greater than 30 mg/g were classified as having microalbuminuria, and those with three consecutive UACR measurements less than 30 mg/g were classified as having non-microalbuminuria. The average of the three UACRs measured was used for analysis. Also, kidney damage/disease was defined as individuals with microalbuminuria (UACR > 30 mg/g measured on three occasions). UACR was used as a gold standard of which other markers were compared to.

Statistical analysis

The study’s data were entered and cleaned in Microsoft Excel (2016). The data were exported to R programming version 4.2.3, XLSTAT (2019) and IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences (version 26.0) for analysis. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentages using the chi-square and Fisher Exact tests where appropriate, while continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation or median interquatile ranges depending on the normality. Depending on the normality, One-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test was also computed to assess the mean or median differences between three or more independent variables and it was followed by pairwise comparison test to determine the significant pairs within the group using the Post Hoc test or Bonferroni correction for multiple tests respectively. To examine the correlation between urea, creatinine, sNGAL, FAR, and UACR, Spearman’s correlation was employed and results represented as heatmaps. Univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression models were also computed to assess the predictors of kidney disease in SCD. The results were expressed as the crude odds ratio (cOR) or adjusted odds ratio (aOR) and 95% CI.

Using the Receiver Operating Characteristics Curve, the area under the curves for sNGAL, Urea, Creatinine and FAR for predicting kidney diseases in SCD pateints were determined. Based on the default settings, the optimal cut-off points for the markers in the ROC curves were automatically generated using XLSTAT software. All computations of statistical significance were performed using a 95% confidence interval and a p value of 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study participants

Among the 184 participants recruited, 104(56.5) were SCD patients in their steady states as assessed by clinicians on the basis of their clinical status, whereas 80(43.5) were also individuals without SCD. Among the SCD patients, 74(71.2) were HbSS with 30(28.8) being HbSC. The age distribution significantly differed between the control and case groups (p < 0.0001) with majority 66(63.5) of younger age group (18-24) being more prevalent among the cases whereas the control group have fairly distributed age groups. Again, a significant difference between genders in the control and case groups was observed (p = 0.008). There were more males in the case group 46(44.2) compared to the control group 20(25.0). More than two-third of the cases were single 88(84.6), while the control group also had more married participants 31(38.8), p < 0.0001 compared to the cases. With respect to participants’ educational background, a significant difference (p < 0.0001) was observed between the groups. Specifically, the Control group had more participants with basic education 30(37.5), whereas the Cases group had a higher proportion of individuals with higher educational levels, senior high 59(56.7) and tertiary 34(32.7). Also, a statistically significant difference in BMI between the groups (p < 0.0001) was observed. Notably, majority of the cases had healthy weight 78(75.0), while majority of the control group were overweight 43(53.8). A significant difference in alcohol consumption was also observed between the groups (p = 0.002). Specifically, a higher proportion of cases 100(96.2) reported rare alcohol consumption, whereas a higher proportion of the control group 13(16.3) reported irregular alcohol consumption, compared to only 4(3.8) of cases (Table 1).

Haemato-biochemical profile of SCD with microalbuminuria (SCD-MA), SCD non- microalbuminuria (SCD-non-MA), controls microalbuminuria (controls-MA) and controls non- microalbuminuria (controls-non-MA)

Diastolic and systolic blood pressure were significantly higher in control groups compared to SCD groups (p < 0.0001). Electrolytes levels such as Sodium were significantly higher in both SCD-non-MA and SCD-MA groups compared to controls (p < 0.0001), while potassium levels were also significantly higher in the SCD-MA group compared to controls (p = 0.007).

Liver function markers like Aspartate transaminase (AST) levels were significantly higher in SCD-MA group (50.75 U/L, p < 0.0001). Again, Alkaline phosphatase levels were also elevated in both SCD groups, especially in SCD-non-MA (103.05 U/L) compared to controls (p < 0.0001). Both total bilirubin (p < 0.0001) and indirect bilirubin (p < 0.0001) levels were significantly higher in SCD groups, especially in the microalbuminuria (MA) group, which might reflect haemolysis or liver dysfunction. Total protein levels were also higher in SCD-MA (89.09 g/L) compared to SCD-non-MA (87.42 g/L) and controls, indicating altered protein metabolism (p < 0.0001). Globulin levels were also significantly elevated in the SCD groups compared to controls (p < 0.0001). No significant difference in albumin levels was observed between both SCD and control groups (p = 0.121).

Creatinine levels were significantly higher (p = 0.045) in SCD individuals with MA (72.00 mmol/L) compared to SCD-non-MA cases (53.50 mmol/L). eGFR was lowest in the SCD-MA group (104.78 mL/min/1.73 m²) compared to the SCD-non-MA and the control groups (p = 0.025). Again, sNGAL, a biomarker of kidney injury, was significantly elevated in the SCD-MA group, (6.66 µg/L, p < 0.0001) compared to SCD-non-MA (5.55 µg/L) and control groups. Levels of fibrinogen, an inflammation marker, were also significantly higher in the SCD groups compared to controls (p < 0.0001), indicating a heightened inflammatory state. Again, FAR was significantly higher (p < 0.0001) in SCD groups compared to their counterpart control groups.

Haemoglobin levels were significantly (p < 0.0001) lower in the SCD groups (9.22 g/dL for non-MA and 8.89 g/dL for MA) compared to the Control groups (11.99 g/dL for non-MA and 11.26 g/dL for MA). RBC count was also significantly lower in the SCD-MA (2.73 106 /mL) group compared to SCD-non-MA (3.08 106 /mL) and Control groups (p < 0.0001). WBC count was significantly higher (p < 0.0001) in the SCD groups, particularly SCD-MA (8.91 103 /mL) compared to the other groups. Lymphocyte, monocyte, neutrophil, and eosinophil counts showed significant differences (p < 0.05), with SCD groups, especially MA group exhibiting higher levels, particularly in monocyte count and neutrophil count. Basophil count also showed significant variation (p < 0.0001) across the groups. All other parameters are summarized in Table 2.

Correlation between sNGAL, creatinine, FAR, urea and UACR in SCD-MA

Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (r) was computed to assess the correlation between sNGAL, creatinine, FAR, urea, and UACR in SCD-MA. A moderate positive correlation was observed between sNGAL and UACR (r = 0.45, p = 0.007). In contrast, sNGAL showed weakly and insignificantly correlated with urea (r = -0.17, p = 0.340), creatinine (r = -0.09, p = 0.620), and FAR (r = 0.08, p = 0.664). UACR exhibited weak negative correlations with urea (r = -0.31, p = 0.077), and FAR (r = -0.01, p = 0.935), and a weak positive correlation with creatinine (r = 0.03, p = 0.883). The correlations between the FAR and the other parameters were weak and not significant (r < 0.3, p > 0.05). A strong positive correlation was observed between urea and creatinine (r = 0.75, p < 0.0001). These findings are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Renal markers as predictors of kidney disease among SCD participants

Binary logistic model was computed to determine renal markers as predictors of kidney disease among SCD participants. In a univariate logistic regression model, each unit increase in sNGAL was associated with a 3.25-fold increased likelihood of kidney disease (cOR: 3.25 (2.11-5.00); p < 0.001) among SCD participants compared to conventional kidney markers, including urea (cOR: 1.19 (1.00-1.40); p = 0.044) and serum creatinine (cOR: 1.01 (1.00-1.36); p = 0.001). Again, a unit increase in FAR (Log cOR: 12.26 (1.82–25.09); p = 0.022) was also significantly associated with increased odds of kidney disease among SCD participants.

After adjusting for potential confounders in a multivariate logistic regression prediction model, it was observed that, only sNGAL and serum creatinine retained independent significance as predictors of kidney disease among SCD participants. The adjusted odds of kidney disease rose 3.39-fold for each one-unit increase in sNGAL (aOR: 3.39 (2.12–5.42); p < 0.001), while each 1 mmol/L increase in serum creatinine conferred a 1.1% higher risk (aOR:1.01 (1.00–1.02); p = 0.037). These results underscore sNGAL and serum creatinine as predictors of kidney disease in these SCD participants (Table 3).

Diagnostic performance of sNGAL, creatinine, urea, and FAR, in predicting kidney disease among SCD patients

Using the Receiver Operating Curve (ROC) analysis for predicting kidney disease among SCD patients, sNGAL emerged as a highly predictive marker for kidney disease in SCD patients, with a cutoff value of > 5.72 (µg/L) yielding a high area under the curve (AUC = 0.854, p < 0.0001). sNGAL also demonstrated an excellent sensitivity (91.2%) and moderate specificity (74.7%), highlighting its potential as a valuable diagnostic marker in this context. The FAR at a cutoff of > 0.09 also demonstrated significant predictive value (AUC = 0.630, p = 0.009) for kidney disease in SCD patients, with a moderate sensitivity (67.6%) and specificity (61.3%). Creatinine and urea insignificantly (p > 0.05) predicted kidney disease in SCD patients with an AUC = 0.588 and AUC = 0.518 respectively with both having high specificity (90%), but lower sensitivity and positive predictive values as shown in Table 4; Fig. 2.

Discussion

Renal involvement in SCD is demonstrated by the presence of crescent RBCs in the renal medulla, which cause ischemia, microinfarcts, reduced renal medullary blood flow, and renal papillary necrosis [39]. Conventional renal markers remain normal until extensive renal damage hence the necessity for early diagnostic markers allows for implementation of preventive actions [40]. This case-control study assessed sNGAL and FAR as indicators of kidney diseases among non-hospitalized SCD patients who were unaware of their renal status.

The prevalence of microalbuminuria among adult steady-state SCD participants was 32.7%. This is above the prevalence of 17.1% microalbuminuria in SCD reported by Marouf et al. [40], in Kuwait. However, this is below a prevalence of 61% microalbuminuria among adult SCD patients reported by Odeyemi et al. [6]. in Nigeria. The differences in prevalences reported could be attributed to the overall monitoring of SCD patients in the various countries. Also, Ephraim et al. [4], found that 68.4% of adult SCD patients had CKD based on reduced eGFR in Ghana. The variation in the prevalences might be due to the fact that eGFR only explore the function of the kidney whilst microalbuminuria determines kidney damage as indicated in KDIGO classification [37].

With respect to FBC parameters, there was lower haemoglobin and RBC levels in SCD-MA participants compared to controls with or without microalbuminuria and SCD-non-MA participants. This confirms the significant association between haemoglobin, RBC and haematocrit levels and kidney disease as already indicated by various studies [41–45].

We also reported significant difference between SCD-MA, SCD-non-MA and controls with respect to total WBC levels and differentials in a decreasing order which tells the story that SCD is an inflammatory disease and get worse when there is an additional inflammatory disorder like kidney diseases which agrees with several studies [4, 46, 47]. Also, a prospective study conducted by Aneke et al. [48], in Nigeria reported high levels of platelets in SCD participants with CKD which is in agreement with this present study.

With respect to LFT parameters, ALT which is very specific to liver was apparently normal and similar in the participant groups which ruled out liver dysfunction in the study participants. The LFT markers observed in this study are in accordance with those reported by Kotila et al. [49] who reported minimal elevation of transaminases in steady-state SCD patients. With respect to kidney biomarkers, this study reported markedly increased levels of creatinine and urea in the SCD-MA group than in the other groups, which conforms with the findings of Ephraim et al. [4] in Ghana. There were minimal increases in the levels of potassium and sodium in the SCD-MA group compared with those in the control group, which is in agreement with findings from Alhwiesh et al. [39]. In a case-control study conducted by Madhuri et al. [50], potassium levels (SCD vs. controls: 3.99 ± 0.95 vs. 3.80 ± 0.32) and sodium levels (SCD vs. Controls: 134 ± 3.94 vs. 130.61 ± 3.59) were slightly higher in SCD individuals compared to healthy participants, which is in agreement with our findings. In contrast to our study, Meshram et al. [51], found that sodium levels (SCD vs. Controls: 127 ± 2.1 vs. 141.0 ± 1.21) were lower in SCD patients compared to healthy controls but potassium levels (SCD vs. controls: 4.7 ± 0.17 vs. 3.6 ± 0.17) were higher in SCD patients compared to their healthy counterparts. This study found high levels of sodium and potassium in SCD-MA compared to other groups which could be due the fact that kidney diseases contribute to electrolytes imbalance in SCD patients.

sNGAL was significantly higher in the SCD-MA group as compared to the SCD-non-MA and controls. Marouf et al. [40], and Atere et al. [20] found increased levels of sNGAL in steady-state SCD patients and its relationship with renal abnormalities as compared to healthy individuals which is consistent with this present study. Fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio was significantly higher in the SCD-MA as compared to SCD-non-MA and controls [52]. A study by Luyendyk et al. [22] and Zhang et al. [28], showed that elevated FAR is an indication of inflammation and also showed a positive correlation with renal prognosis, which is consistent with our study.

This study found a significant positive correlation between sNGAL and UACR which is in line with a study performed by Marouf et al. [40], who found a positive non-significant correlation between plasma sNGAL and UACR but a positive significant correlation between urine NGAL and UACR. This observation could be attributed to the state of patients in that, they recruited both steady-state and VOC-state SCD and they were not grouped in the correlation analysis. The correlations between FAR and the other parameters were weak and not significant. In a study conducted by Duan et al. [53], FAR correlated positively with proteinuria in phospholipase A2 receptor-associated membranous nephropathy (PMN) patients. In comparison to our results where we compared FAR to microalbuminuria, there was a negative non-significant correlation with UACR. This might be because our patients were in a steady-state with minimal inflammation. A study conducted by Atere et al. [20] observed a similar trend of correlation analysis as we reported in this current study; for example there was a strong positive relationship between creatinine and urea (r = 0.967, p < 0.001) but a weak negative non-significant relationship between sNGAL and creatinine (r= -0.027, p = 0.889) and urea and sNGAL (r= -0.113, p = 0.55). However, in a hypertensive non-SCD study conducted by Danquah et al. [21] sNGAL and creatinine (r = 0.84, p < 0.0001) had a strong positive significant relation.

Though, there are limited studies that have done regression analysis on sNGAL and FAR as predictors of kidney disease in SCD, Marouf et al. [40], found sNGAL (OR = 1.01; 95% CI = 1.00–1.02; p = 0.009) to be early predictor of kidney diseases which agrees with our findings. Also, in a non-SCD study conducted by Zou et al. [54] that utilized FAR as a prognostic marker for cardiovascular diseases in End Stage Renal Disease patients, FAR was an independent predictor of cardiovascular diseases mortality in univariate analysis (cOR: 3.44; 95% CI: 1.76–6.72; P < 0.001) and multivariate analysis (aOR: 2.31; 95% CI: 1.17–4.59, P = 0.016). Comparing to our findings, FAR could only predict kidney diseases in the univariate analysis which contradicts with the aforementioned studies. A study conducted by Yan et al. [55], revealed that, FAR was independently associated with an increased risk of first-ever cardiovascular events in patients with peritoneal dialysis. Despite the fact that this study is on inflammation-associated nephropathy, our multivariate analysis indicated opposite. This could be attributed to the different population and over all inflammation status of participants. They recruited non-SCD patients with end stage renal diseases for which their inflammation level was high as compared to our participants. sNGAL was found to be an independent early predictor of kidney diseases and good determinant of microalbuminuria in SCD patients. Also, FAR could be used as a predictor of kidney diseases in SCD patients though more findings will be needed to confirm this. In the multivariate logistic regression model, creatinine was found to be a predictor of kidney disease in relation to urea. We performed ROC analysis to determine how specific and sensitive these markers could predict kidney disease in SCD-MA participants.

In the ROC analysis, the optimal threshold at which sNGAL level indicated impairment of the of kidney was 5.72ug/l with a sensitivity of 91.2% and specificity of 74.7%. Comparing the area under the curves sNGAL significantly performed better than the conventional markers (urea and creatinine) with excellent sensitivity (91.2%) than creatinine (47.1%) and that of urea (32.4%). However, creatinine and urea were insignificantly highly specific but the AUC value was lower than that of sNGAL. This tells an interesting story that once there is kidney impairment in SCD individuals in steady-state, sNGAL levels would increase significantly and can be used as an early predictive marker of kidney diseases in SCD patients. Despite being a predictor in the multivariate logistic regression model, creatinine was not a reliable indicator of kidney disease in patients with SCD due to its low sensitivity, low AUC value, and insignificant p-value in ROC analysis. Combining the multivariate regression and ROC results of creatinine and sNGAL supports the findings of Jahaj et al. [56] who also discovered that sNGAL is a better predictor of kidney disease than creatinine. These observations are also comparable to research conducted by Atere et al. [20] in Nigeria who compared the AUC of sNGAL and conventional markers (Urea and Creatinine) with sNGAL having a superior AUC than urea and creatinine in both steady and VOC-states. However, Marouf et al. [40] indicated that urine NGAL performs better than sNGAL in predicting nephropathy in SCD in VOC-state but could not find that in SCD in steady state. Various studies have also compared sNGAL levels in non-SCD nephropathy studies indicating that sNGAL was a better marker for kidney diseases even in non-SCD patients which should be well looked at [21, 57–59]. FAR at a cutoff of > 0.09 also demonstrated significant predictive value (AUC = 0.630, p = 0.009) for kidney disease in SCD patients, with a moderate sensitivity (67.6%) and specificity (61.3%). FAR performed moderately in the ROC analysis with a significant sensitivity and specificity compared to the conventional markers (urea and creatinine) which indicates that FAR levels could be used to predict nephropathy in SCD subjects as seen in a cancer study by Hwang et al. [60] which is consistent with this study. However, based on the AUC and multivariate logistic regression value of FAR, we cannot confirm that FAR is an early predictor. But comparing to urea and creatinine, and considering the high levels of FAR in SCD-MA group compared to SCD-non-MA group in addition to significant crude odd ratio in univariate logistic regression model, FAR could be used to track inflammation in sickle cell diseases patients with or without kidney diseases.

Despite these novel findings, this study has limitations worth mentioning. The study recruited only steady-state SCD patients and thus was not a true reflection of all SCD adult patients. Most of the SCD participants were young adults and may not be reflective of old SCD patients. Moreover, some of the age groups of cases and controls were not comparable and this could affect the study findings. We recommend that further studies should recruit both steady-state and VOC-state adult SCD patients with equal age distributions. The UACR should be added to the estimated glomerular filtration rate, as suggested by the KDIGO, to confirm chronic kidney disease and thus classify sickle cell nephropathy. In addition, further studies should be performed to assess the relationship between sNGAL and urine NGAL levels and how they predict sickle cell nephropathy.

Conclusion

This study provides the first Ghanaian evidence that serum NGAL is a highly sensitive and specific early marker of sickle cell–related kidney disease, clearly outperforming traditional filtration markers in detecting microalbuminuria. Although the fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio was elevated in affected patients underscoring the role of inflammation in nephropathy, it did not retain independent predictive value once established kidney function covariates were accounted for. Taken together, these findings position sNGAL as a superior marker for early kidney disease screening in steady-state SCD, while FAR could function as an inflammatory biomarker for SCD-associated kidney disease, subject to confirmation in prospective studies.
