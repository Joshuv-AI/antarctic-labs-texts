Real-world work productivity is impaired in people with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease in the USA
Authors: Shraddha Shinde, Mary E. Rinella, Mark L. Hartman, David Schapiro, Victoria Higgins, Andrea Leith, James Pike, Quentin M. Anstee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsThe impact of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) on patient-reported outcomes is poorly understood. We assessed work productivity burden in a real-world population with suspected/confirmed MASH, comparing work productivity and symptoms across subgroups (risk status, age, BMI, and comorbidities) in the USA using secondary data from Adelphi Real World MASH Disease Specific Programmes™, cross-sectional surveys of physicians and consulting patients in 2019 and 2022.MethodsPhysicians (hepatologists, gastroenterologists, and endocrinologists) reported sociodemographic data and signs/symptoms for eight or fewer consecutive participants with MASH. Participants voluntarily completing questionnaires that assessed work productivity, health status, and quality of life/symptoms were categorized with low-, indeterminate-, or high-risk MASH using physician-stated fibrosis stage and derived risk categories. Principal components factor analysis identified factors from MASH signs and symptoms. Elastic net regression determined features associated with work productivity impairment.ResultsIn total, 87 physicians and 429 individuals with MASH provided data. The impact of MASH on activities was greater in high-versus low-risk MASH (activity impairment score: high risk, 30.8%; low risk, 17%; p <0.001 across all risk categories). Overall work impairment scores were 18.8% in low-risk and 19.9% in high-risk MASH. Of the 14 physician-reported signs/symptoms, 11 were significantly associated with physician-stated fibrosis stage and/or derived risk category. Two clusters of signs and symptoms (‘memory loss/swelling legs/abdomen’ and ‘fatigue/sleep disturbance/insomnia/general weakness’) and female sex were the strongest work impact predictors.ConclusionsActivity impairment was greater in participants with high-risk MASH, whereas overall work impairment was comparable, almost 20%, in low- and high-risk MASH. Non-specific symptoms of fatigue, sleep disturbance, and general weakness associated with MASH and other conditions predicted work impact in low-risk MASH. Thus, early detection and management of MASH could ameliorate work impairment.Impact and implicationsEven in its early stages, when otherwise asymptomatic, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) may impact the ability to work. Using the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment: Specific Health Problem (WPAI:SHP) questionnaire, we identified activity and overall work impairment in people with high- and low-risk MASH. We found a link between difficulty working and other signs, such as tiredness, sleep disturbance, and general weakness, in low-risk MASH that are not specific to MASH but can be associated with liver health. These general, non-specific signs could represent possible early warning signs preceding reduced productivity in patients with MASH.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD),1 is defined by the presence of hepatic steatosis in conjunction with one other cardiometabolic risk factor, with no other discernible cause of steatotic liver disease. The global prevalences of MASLD and metabolic-associated steatohepatitis (MASH; formerly non-alcoholic steatohepatitis [NASH]) are estimated to be 30–32% and 5% of the general population, respectively.2,3 The rising prevalence of MASLD and MASH has been associated with the global increase in obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).3 MASH is the most rapidly increasing indication for liver transplantation in the USA4 and, consequently, has increasing economic impact in terms of healthcare resource utilization, and medical, non-medical, and indirect costs.5

MASH has been associated with symptoms such as fatigue, abdominal pain, and poor sleep quality.6 Obesity and cognitive impairment are also common in individuals with MASH.7,8 MASH is commonly symptomatic only during later stages of the disease and symptoms can be highly non-specific, making diagnosis challenging for physicians.9,10 People may present with non-specific symptoms during the earlier stages of MASH, which can result in underdiagnosis of the disease.11,12 The effect of MASH and associated comorbidities on work productivity and activity is particularly relevant given the potential economic impact of reduced work effectiveness and work time missed in this predominantly working-age population.[13], [14], [15] Fatigue may result in poor performance at work and difficulty concentrating, impacting the individual’s productivity.16,17 This may be further compounded by the association of MASH with adverse effects on mental health.16,18 Medical appointments and MASH-related health issues might result in missed workdays.19,20 Patients with MASH, particularly those with more severe disease,21 report a greater burden compared with the matched general population with regard to health-related quality of life (HRQoL) outcomes, healthcare resource use (HCRU), and work productivity.20,22

The objectives of this study were to assess the work productivity burden in people with suspected/confirmed MASH in real-world clinical practice in the USA using the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment: Specific Health Problem (WPAI:SHP; hereafter referred to as WPAI) tool, and to identify factors that might predict adverse effects of MASH on work productivity. To ensure the validity of the data, assessment was performed in two temporally separated cohorts in surveys conducted in 2019 and 2022. We also assessed patient-reported outcomes (PROs) as measured by clinical assessment and the HRQoL tools NASH-CHECK and EQ-5D-5L at the 2022 time point.

Materials and methods

Study methodology

This was an analysis of secondary data extracted from two Adelphi Real World MASH Disease Specific Programmes (DSPs)™ conducted from February to May 2019 and from January to June 2022 in two independent samples. The DSPs were cross-sectional surveys with retrospective data collection of physicians (hepatologists, gastroenterologists, and endocrinologists) and their consulting patients in the USA. Specifically, patients were recruited as they consulted their physician; physicians then completed a questionnaire that could involve both assessment at the time of consultation and reference to the patient’s historical data records where required. No longitudinal data capture occurred between the two DSP time points.

Participating physicians were required to be responsible for the management of ≥10 people with clinically suspected or confirmed MASH in a typical month. Physicians provided records for up to eight consecutively consulting patients who they considered to have MASH based on their own clinical judgment, consistent with how decisions are made in real-world practice. As a result, people with suspected but unconfirmed MASH diagnosis could have been included. Participants’ inclusion criteria were: aged ≥18 years and not taking part in a MASH clinical trial at the time of data capture. Those undergoing liver biopsy as part of a clinical trial were excluded from the analysis. Real-world MASH diagnosis should exclude people who had another form of liver disease, such as alcohol-related liver disease, primary biliary cholangitis, viral hepatitis, autoimmune hepatitis, Wilson’s disease, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, or hemochromatosis. The 2019 DSP did not refer to this, leaving assessment to the physician’s discretion, whereas the 2022 DSP explicitly raised this in the exclusion criteria, citing liver disease examples while still leaving assessment to physicians’ discretion. Thus, patients with cirrhosis may have been included.

The DSP methodology has been previously described,23 validated,24 and demonstrated to be representative and consistent over time.25 The surveys were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines. Ethics exemption for the 2019 survey was obtained from the Western Institutional Review Board (sponsor/institution protocol no. AG8065) and exemption for the 2022 survey was obtained from the Pearl Institution Review Board (protocol no. 21-ADRW-129). Participants provided written informed consent for use of their anonymized and aggregated data.

Data sources

Physicians completed questionnaires that covered the participant’s demographic and clinical characteristics, including comorbidities, signs and symptoms currently being experienced (present/not), and the physician’s own assessment of the participant’s fibrosis score using the NASH Clinical Research Network scoring system.26 A liver biopsy was not mandated for this assessment.

Participants were invited to voluntarily complete a questionnaire that covered sociodemographic characteristics, disease signs and symptoms, and work productivity, including work hours lost due to MASH measured using the WPAI.27 Health status was measured using the EQ-5D-5L (scored using the US-specific tariff),28 and HRQoL, including symptoms, was measured using the NASH-CHECK.6 Further information on these questionnaires is available in the supplementary methods.

Derived MASLD risk categories

Based on available data, participants were retrospectively assigned a derived MASLD risk category in line with guidance published in 2023 by the AASLD. Participants were classified either using liver biopsy results if available, or non-invasively using liver stiffness measured by vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE), and Fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4) score.29 These risk categories were: low-risk MASH (liver biopsy fibrosis stages F0–2, VCTE <8.0 kPa; FIB-4 <1.3); indeterminate (liver biopsy n/a, VCTE >8.0–12.0 kPa; FIB-4 n/a); and high-risk MASH (liver biopsy fibrosis stages F3–4, VCTE >12 kPa; FIB-4 ≥1.3).29 This was recorded independently of the physician-reported fibrosis status.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were derived for the demographics, clinical characteristics, and measures of WPAI across subgroups in the sample. Subgroups of participants were defined by age, BMI, and the presence of specific cardiovascular and metabolic conditions. Results are presented as frequency, mean ± SD. Fisher’s exact (FE) tests and Student’s t tests were used to compare demographics, clinical characteristics, and measures of WPAI across subgroups between 2019 and 2022 cohorts. Chi-square tests were used when FE tests were not computable. ANOVA was used to compare measures of WPAI between derived MASH risk categories. FE tests were used to compare the distribution of signs and symptoms between fibrosis stages as stated by physicians and derived risk category. In all comparisons, p <0.05 was considered statistically significant, p values were not adjusted for multiple comparisons, and analysis was unadjusted for possible confounders. Where specific analysis required variables for which there were missing data, the number of observations included was reported.

Fourteen prespecified individual physician-reported features of MASH were evaluated by the survey: fatigue, sleep disturbance, insomnia, general weakness, unexplained weight loss, hair loss, changes to personality, memory loss, jaundice, aching/discomfort in upper right abdomen, itchy skin, swelling in the legs/ankles/feet, tendency to bleed and bruise more easily, and swelling of the abdomen. We used principal component factors (PCFs) to derive factors from the 14 individual physician-reported signs and symptoms of MASH. An elastic net regression (ENR) was used to determine which participant demographic and clinical characteristics were predictive of WPAI scores. Further details about the PCF and ENR methods are provided in the supplementary methods.

Both physician-stated and AASLD-derived risk categories are presented: the physician-stated categorization accounts for the real-world nature of the analysis and the fact that physicians determine treatment and management strategies based on their perception of the individual’s disease state. Use of a robust clinical classification system, as represented by the AASLD risk categories, mitigates any real-world misclassification and provides a means to further understand objective versus subjective assessment.

Analyses were performed using Stata version 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA), principally using the built-in Stata commands: table, tab, oneway, ttest, factor, rotate, and elasticnet.

Age, BMI, and CVM conditions

In general, WPAI scores tended to be lower in the 2022 cohort than in the 2019 cohort, with more pronounced differences for some comparisons (Table 2). Activity impairment and overall work impairment scores were significantly higher in the 2019 cohort vs. the 2022 cohort among younger participants, and those with a higher BMI. Activity impairment scores were significantly higher in the 2019 cohort compared with the 2022 cohort among those with no comorbid CVM conditions, and overall work impairment scores were higher in the 2019 cohort among those with CVM conditions. Presenteeism scores were significantly higher in the 2019 participants with lower BMI and no CVM conditions (Table 2). No statistically significant differences in absenteeism scores were noted between cohorts.

Health status

In general, participants who reported poor HRQoL, specifically impairment in the EQ-5D-5L mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression dimensions, had significantly higher overall work impairment compared with those who did not report having issues with the EQ-5D-5L dimensions. With the exception of the EQ-5D-5L mobility and pain/discomfort dimensions in relation to the WPAI absenteeism domain, WPAI scores were statistically significantly worse in participants with impairment in EQ-5D-5L dimensions than in those reporting no impairment (Fig. 3B).

Principal components factor analysis

In our analysis, factors derived via PCF analysis determined that four overarching factors (or composite variables) would sufficiently convey a large proportion of the variability in the original observed variables: (1) presence of fatigue/sleep disturbance/insomnia/general weakness; (2) presence of jaundice/aching, discomfort in upper right abdomen/itchy skin; (3) presence of unexplained weight loss/hair loss/changes to personality/tendency to bleed and bruise more easily; and (4) presence of memory loss/swelling in legs, ankles, feet/swelling of the abdomen.

Discussion

This analysis identified clinical characteristics associated with activity and work impairment in patients with suspected/confirmed MASH in real-world clinical practice. Whereas activity impairment was greater in participants with high-vs. low-risk MASH, overall work impairment was comparable in participants with high- and low-risk MASH, at ∼20% in both groups.

Impairment of activity and work by 15% and 20%, respectively, has been considered clinically meaningful in patients with psoriasis,30 whereas a 20% threshold for meaningful change has been proposed in caregivers of chronically ill older patients and in those with chronic or episodic migraine.[31], [32], [33] The effect of MASH severity on work impairment has not been widely studied, although Younossi and colleagues demonstrated statistically significantly greater activity impairment measured using the WPAI in people with Enhanced Liver Fibrosis (ELF) scores ≥11.3 vs. those with ELF scores <11.3.34 An ELF score ≥11.3 indicates a high probability of underlying cirrhosis and risk of decompensation. Thus, an ELF score ≥11.3 likely defines a population with more advanced disease than the high-risk population in the present analysis, that was defined by either liver biopsy (fibrosis stages F3 or F4) or non-invasive tests (VCTE >12 kPa; FIB-4 ≥1.3), although a VCTE score >10 kPa has been associated with an increased risk of adverse liver-related clinical events.35 It is well known that patients with cirrhosis can be symptomatic before hepatic decompensation.36,37 We found that patients who were at high risk of disease progression exhibited work impairment even in the absence of cirrhosis, although some patients with high-risk MASH and impaired WPAI scores might have had undiagnosed cirrhosis.

We also found that participants with low-risk MASH commonly presented with clusters of non-specific symptoms (fatigue, sleep disturbance, and general weakness), in line with findings from other studies.38,39 Previous studies reported that patients affected by MASH can experience fatigue, poor sleep quality, impaired memory, and reduced focus.6,38,40,41 In our study, two clusters of signs and symptoms were among the strongest predictors of work impairment: (1) ‘memory loss/swelling in legs, ankles, feet/swelling of abdomen’; and (2) ‘fatigue/sleep disturbance/insomnia/general weakness’. The first cluster might be an indicator of early edema, hepatic encephalopathy, and possibly ascites, which are typically sequelae of cirrhosis. The second cluster is consistent with previous findings that higher levels of fatigue and decreased work productivity occur in patients with MASLD versus those without, and that fatigue is prognostic of adverse outcomes, such as liver-related events and mortality.42 Therefore, during the early stages of MASH, when such non-specific symptoms emerge, there might be a ‘silent’ and potentially undetected pathology that impacts work productivity.

Non-specific symptoms commonly occur in patients with other metabolic disorders. For example, obesity can negatively impact sleep quality, leading to fatigue and impaired cognitive function.43 Metabolic syndrome and cardiometabolic risk factors have been linked to faster decline and poorer performance in cognitive domains, including memory, verbal fluency, and executive function.44 Non-specific symptoms, such as fatigue, sleep disturbance, and general weakness, are often early manifestations of underlying conditions, including diabetes, arthritis, and obstructive sleep apnea.[45], [46], [47] As with MASH, these conditions are frequently underdiagnosed, and the subtlety of their presenting symptoms can contribute to delays in identification and management, ultimately leading to unaddressed functional impairment and reduced work productivity. Physicians might consider MASH as part of the differential diagnosis for individuals presenting with these non-specific symptoms, which could help with earlier detection of MASH.

Although we have described WPAI assessment at two different time points and in different populations with no longitudinal follow-up, our findings appear to indicate less impact of MASH on work in the 2022 environment compared with the 2019 assessment. This might be related to lower prevalence of high-risk MASH, obesity, insulin resistance, and T2DM, and higher educational attainment in the 2022 cohort. Alternatively, this finding could be related to hybrid working principles introduced post-coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). Between 2019 and 2021, US Census data estimated a threefold increase in the percentage of patients working from home, rising from 5.7% to 17.9%.48 Similarly, the US Bureau of Labor Statistics estimated an 11.8% increase in remote workers between 2019 and 2022.49 The resulting flexibility allows for attending healthcare appointments without missing hours worked. This is particularly relevant for patients with MASLD/MASH, who have multiple comorbidities. Although hybrid and remote work remain attractive to employees, many employers might not recognize their value, preferring a return to site-based work.

Some limitations of this analysis warrant consideration in addition to those inherent in real-world surveys.23 Although not longitudinal, the two surveys provide independent cross-sectional insights with retrospective data capture, which can be used to explore patterns over time. The cross-sectional design prevents any conclusions about causal relationships, although identification of significant associations is possible. Given that physicians were not asked to attribute signs/symptoms to MASH, it is possible that these might have been related to other underlying comorbidities. We were not able to compare physician- and participant-reported signs and symptoms because data were reported differently by physicians and participants. Physician-stated fibrosis stages are subjective and not based on consistent criteria in a clinical practice setting. We have tried to address this with derived risk categories based on liver biopsy, VCTE, and FIB-4 results using the 2023 AASLD consensus guidance document.29 The 2022 survey was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have reduced in-person consultation with physicians for patients with MASH. However, this dynamic proved insightful to the findings around work impact in the 2019 and 2022 cohorts. NASH-CHECK and EQ-5D-5L were not included in the 2019 survey. Of note, differences between effects of MASH on work productivity in women vs. men were not explored, and neither was the effect of symptoms associated with the menopause. Although metabolic comorbidities were recorded in the cohorts of participants described in this study, we have not sought to disentangle their impact on work impairment from that of MASH. The study did not include a comparator group with obesity but no MASH. Such a comparison might be considered for future research. Machine learning methods in real-world data might be limited by overidentification of predictors,50 missing data, and the lack of external validation.

Conclusions

This analysis suggests that activity impairment, as measured by the WPAI, was more common in people with high-versus low-risk MASH and that overall work impairment was comparable in people with high- and low-risk MASH, with ∼20% overall work impairment in both risk categories. We identified predictors of work impairment with non-specific symptoms, such as fatigue, sleep disturbance, and general weakness, which were common in patients with low-risk MASH and may be considered potential indicators of early disease, while also considering other conditions and comorbidities that might present with similar symptoms. Early diagnosis of MASH may allow for timely interventions that treat the condition or mitigate its impact on symptoms, work, and daily activity. The latter might include adjustments including hybrid working for affected individuals, thereby reducing the impact of the condition on work productivity, which could have subsequent cost implications for patients with MASH and their employers.
