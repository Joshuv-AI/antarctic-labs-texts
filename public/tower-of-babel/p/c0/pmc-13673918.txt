Patterns of small bowel volvulus in North - Eastern Ethiopia
Authors: Yohannes Teshome Kassie, Fikru Tadelle Mengesha, Meron Tadesse Awoke, Redwan Yimer Assen, Atalel Fantahun Awedew, Assefa Sisay Ayenew, Setegn Abebaw Abera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmall bowel volvulus (SBV) is a condition where the small bowel loops coil around the axis of their own mesentery, which can lead to mechanical intestinal obstruction. It contributes a significant number of patients at surgical emergency with small bowel obstruction (SBO), especially in developing countries. The main objective of this study was to assess the clinical and epidemiological status of SBV in North-Eastern Ethiopia.MethodThe study was conducted at Dessie Comprehensive and Specialized Hospital, Dessie, Ethiopia. The study period was in November 2023. A hospital-based retrospective cross-sectional study design was used, and data were collected with a standardized structured checklist. Data were collected from medical records of adult patients with a discharge diagnosis of small bowel obstruction over a four (4)-year period from September 1, 2019, to August 31, 2023. Cross-tabulation analysis was done for factors that contributed to the mortality and morbidity of SBV.ResultA total of 162 patients with small bowel volvulus were included in the study, accounting for about 37.2% of patients with small bowel obstruction, with an average annual occurrence rate of about 40.5 patients. The mean age of patients was 47 years. The majority of them (93.8%) were males. The common seasons at presentation were September–November and March–May. All participants had abdominal pain and vomiting, but 98.1% and 88.3% had abdominal distension and constipation, respectively. 52.5% of patients with SBV presented within 24 h of symptom onset, and 61.1% had normal complete blood count results, whereas 38.9% had leukocytosis. The majority (77.1%) were managed with derotation and retrograde milking, whereas 7.4% were managed with resection and anastomosis, and 11.1% underwent band release and derotation. Primary small bowel volvulus was seen in 85.2% of patients, and the remaining 14.8% had secondary small bowel volvulus.ConclusionThe prevalence of SBV among patients with SBO was 37.2%, with 85.2% being primary and the remaining 14.8% being secondary types. The average annual occurrence rate was about 40.5 patients per year, with a mean age of 47 years.

Background

Volvulus is a mechanical intestinal obstruction resulting from abnormal twisting of a loop of bowel around the axis of its mesentery [1]. SBV is a condition where the small bowel loops coil around the axis of their own mesentery [2]. SBV can be classified as primary or secondary according to the cause. Primary SBV is defined as torsion of a segment of small bowel at the base of the mesentery without any evident underlying cause [3]. Secondary SBV occurs in the presence of acquired underlying conditions such as post-operative adhesions, tumors, pregnancy, diverticular disease, and bands [4].

The most commonly accepted explanation for the initiation of primary small bowel volvulus is abnormal bowel peristalsis generated due to the sudden overfilling of an empty bowel loop with a large portion of a meal after prolonged fasting [5]. No specific symptom, clinical sign, or abnormal laboratory finding clearly identifies both primary and secondary types. A high index of suspicion is required to reach a diagnosis [5, 6]. Abdominal pain is the most common presenting feature of small bowel volvulus [7, 8]. Urgent surgical exploration is the treatment of choice to prevent complications due to vascular compromise [5, 9, 10].

Because of its vague and nonspecific nature, it mimics irritable bowel syndrome, peptic ulcer disease, pancreatic disease, biliary disease, psychiatric disease [11, 12], and acute appendicitis [13]. Ethiopia is one of the countries where intestinal obstruction constitutes a major cause of morbidity and mortality [14]. In rural Africa, acute intestinal obstruction accounts for a great proportion of morbidity and mortality [14, 15]. CT scan is the investigation of choice, and a whirl sign is the characteristic finding of bowel volvulus [16].

Common clinical manifestations, in order of frequency, are central abdominal pain (94 to 100%), nausea or vomiting (83 to 100%), distension (55 to 100%), and constipation (14 to 26%). They may present all together or may occur in any combination [17–19]. The clinical manifestations are due to the narrowing or obliteration of the bowel lumen or cutting off of the blood supply, or both, which leads to ischemia, finally resulting in gangrene [20–22].

Outcome of the disease is mainly based on the early diagnosis and intervention [19].

Methods

Ethical considerations

Based on the Declaration of Helsinki developed by The World Medical Association (WMA), ethical clearance was obtained from the Ethical Review Committee of the College of Medicine and Health Science at Wollo University. A formal letter was written from the coordinator of the Department of Surgery to the hospital administrator’s office for cooperation. The confidentiality and privacy of patient charts were maintained during data collection.

Study area, period, and design

The study was conducted at Dessie Comprehensive and Specialized Hospital (DCSH), located in Dessie city, Amhara Region, Northeastern Ethiopia. The hospital has a catchment area of more than 10 million people. The hospital provides different inpatient and outpatient services with about 560 staff members, including 97 specialist physicians. The study period was in November 2023. A hospital-based retrospective cross-sectional study design was used.

Operational definition

Ethiopian seasons: Tsedey (September, October, November), Bega (December, January, February), Belg (March, April, May), and Kiremt (June, July, August).

Data collection methods and quality control

Data were extracted from medical records using a structured checklist, which was adopted and modified from other similar previous literature. It was prepared in English and administered by trained data collectors after a pretest was conducted. The structured checklist contains each patient’s demographic characteristics, clinical presentation, duration of illness, presence of medical comorbidity, season at presentation, history of previous surgery, procedures done, intraoperative findings, postoperative complications, and management outcomes. All adult patients above the age of 18 years with a discharge diagnosis of SBO treated at DCSH for 4 years (from September 1, 2019, to August 31, 2023) whose medical records were complete and contained adequate information were included, and charts with incomplete records were excluded from the review.

Data processing and analysis

Data were checked for possible inconsistencies, coded, and entered into SPSS version 27.1 for analysis. Before the data were run through the software for analysis, additional data cleaning was done to identify outliers, missing values, and any inconsistencies. The analysis was described in terms of frequency, percentage, mean, and median. Variables were cross-tabulated with the development of SBV and other important outcomes.

Discussion

Based on this study, SBV contributes about 37.2% of SBO. The prevalence of small bowel volvulus varies considerably in different parts of the world [4]. In the United States, Canada, and Western Europe, small bowel volvulus does not represent a health problem of the same magnitude as it does in some African and Asian states, where the disease seems to be more common. For example, a study conducted in Boston, USA, showed the prevalence of SBV among SBO to be only 4% [6]. This might be due to the commonality of a high-fiber diet and a relatively longer acquired bowel related to these bulk agents in patients from developing countries when compared to developed ones. Studies conducted in Hossana Hospital (Southern Ethiopia), Adama General Hospital (Central Ethiopia), Debre Tabor Hospital (North Central Ethiopia) and Bahir Dar Tibebe Ghion Hospital (North Western Ethiopia) show results of 51.6%, 30.3%, 59% and 25% respectively, with a mean of 41.5%, which has acceptable similarity with the current study [4, 14, 23, 24].

The mean age of occurrence is about 47 years, which has proximity to the findings mentioned in China, Ethiopia, and Iran [11, 23, 25]. The age ranges from 18 to 80 years, and occurrence is found to decrease with increasing age. The study in Boston shows a mean age of 67 years, whereas it is 34 and 37 years in Uganda and India [6, 26, 27] (Table 6). These differences are basically dependent on the type of SBV, where the secondary type is more common in older patients and in developed countries, where abdominal surgeries are more common, unlike the developing world [6].

The current study shows significant male preponderance of about 93.8%, which parallels most other local and international literature, except the study conducted in Boston, which shows slight female predominance with a 1:1.2 male-to-female ratio [6]. The exact cause of lower association in females is not clearly explained, except for some anatomic factors, such as a wider pelvis, especially in Black women, and lax abdominal wall muscles, more prominent in parous women, which are supposed to be protective for females by creating a capacious abdomen, providing adequate space for bowel movement, and allowing, if at all, spontaneous detorsion before a significant twist occurs [23].

Rural residents are significantly affected by SBV, with a rate of 94.4% in our study, which is consistent with studies conducted in Hossana and Debre Tabor, Ethiopia [4, 23]. These could be related to the heavy activities and common practice of ingesting large amounts of bulky food at a time. The common seasons at presentation are from September to November and March to May, accounting for about 33.9% and 32.7% respectively. These seasonal variations are also observed by Demissie from Southern Ethiopia, Veaz-Zadeh from Iran, Bayeh from North-Western Ethiopia, and Duke from Afghanistan [4, 25, 28, 29]. These are months of fasting for both Muslims (Ramadan) and Christians, at when large amount of fibrous diet may be taken after prolonged fasting, causing a bulky bolus of food to enter the proximal jejunum, making it fall down into the pelvis and causing the empty distal bowel to rise into the right upper quadrant. Rapid emptying of the stomach causes the distal bowel to spread across the left upper quadrant. As this distal loop is filled up again, it would fall into the left lower quadrant, completing 3600 twists [23].

The commonest complaints of patients at presentation are colicky abdominal pain with a painless interval, vomiting, constipation, and abdominal tenderness [4–6]. All (100%) of our patients had a variable degree of abdominal pain and vomiting, but abdominal distension and constipation were present in 98.1% and 88.3% of them, respectively.

We found no patient with previous abdominal surgery developing primary SBV, but about 54.2% of the 24 patients with secondary SBV had it. In our study, 52.5% of patients with SBV presented within 24 h of symptom onset. This is opposite to the finding by Demissie from Hossana, Ethiopia, which shows only 20.4% presenting within 24 h and 79.6% of them presented after 24 h [4]. This is relatively an old study (1992–1996), and the recent early presentation could be due to an increase in communal health-seeking behavior and a high index of suspicion and early detection by clinicians. Plain erect abdominal X-ray is the routine imaging technique, which usually shows dilated small bowel loops with multiple air fluid levels [4, 10, 28].

Definitive diagnosis can only be made on exploratory laparotomy [14]. The management of SBV with bowel gangrene is clear, and that is resection and primary anastomosis or stoma, but controversies arise when the bowel loop is viable [14]. Untwisting and bowel decompression are the crucial steps to avoid bowel necrosis [14, 24, 28]. 77.1% of our patients were managed with laparotomy, derotation, and retrograde milking (bowel decompression) with aspiration of air and fluid content with nasogastric tube, and 7.4% were managed with resection and anastomosis due to gangrenous change, and the remaining 11.1% undergone band release and derotation.

Based on the type of SBV (among operated patients only), the primary type accounted for about 85.2%, while the secondary SBV takes 14.8%. This has slight similarity with figures obtained from Iran (PSBV- 80% and SSBV − 20%), and India (PSBV- 70% and SSBV − 30%) [25, 27] But it is completely opposite to the same study of Boston, which shows 14% PSBV and 86% SSBV [6]. This could possibly be due to high abdominal surgery rates in the United States, predisposing patients to the development of a fibrous band around which the small bowel volvulates. In Uganda and Afghanistan, all patients with SBV have the primary type [26, 29]. This could be due to the same reason of a high fiber diet, which is more common in developing countries, predisposing to primary SBV.

Diagnosing and conducting surgery in the early stages of the disease not only enables the establishment of a working space for minimally invasive laparoscopic procedures but also offers the advantage of potentially avoiding bowel resection [30].

Wound infection with or without dehiscence, peritonitis, pneumonia, short bowel syndrome, and enterocutaneous fistula are among the commonest complications following surgery [4, 6, 24]. 77.2% of our patients improved without complication and were discharged, but 17.3% of them developed complications. Of those who developed complications, 2.5%, 1.2%, and 13.6% had SSI, anastomotic leak, and paralytic ileus, respectively. Based on single institution long-term retrospective studies conducted in the United States and China, recurrence rate was 3.9% and 3.23% associated with simple devolvulation, respectively [31, 32]. In our current study, we couldn’t find patients with recorded recurrence despite most of them being managed with simple devolvulation.

Overall mortality rate following surgical intervention varies with countries and provinces, which shows 13.3% in Southern Ethiopia, 10–30% in the United Arab Emirates, 3–13% in the USA, and 3.4% in North-Central Ethiopia [4–6, 23]. Our finding was 1.2% post operative mortality, which is lower than the previous studies.

The main limitation of this study was that there were rejected charts due to incomplete documentation, while the discharge diagnosis was still SBO secondary to SBV. The other was that the results of imaging diagnostic modalities were documented inconsistently, which led us to exclude them from description and discussion. Since our study was cross-sectional, recurrence rates are not included too.

Conclusion

Small bowel volvulus is an important and common surgical emergency in developing countries, and in this study, we found a prevalence of 37.2% among patients with SBO. The mean age at occurrence is 47 years, and it is more common among rural residents during September through November and March through May. Most of our patients had undergone operative exploration and were successfully treated with low morbidity and mortality.
