Prognostic value of lactate-to-hemoglobin ratio in nontraumatic critically ill patients presenting to the emergency department: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: İlter Ağaçkıran, Merve Ağaçkıran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNontraumatic critically ill patients constitute a substantial cohort of individuals presenting to the emergency department (ED). However, reliable parameters to predict early mortality in these patients are limited. Lactate and base deficit (BD) are commonly used markers reflecting tissue hypoxia and metabolic acidosis, respectively. This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of the lactate-to-hemoglobin ratio (LHR), a novel parameter that concurrently reflects imbalances in oxygen delivery and utilization, for predicting in-hospital mortality.MethodsThis retrospective observational study was conducted in the ED of a tertiary care hospital between January 1 and December 31, 2023. Patients aged ≥ 18 years with a lactate level ≥ 4 mmol/L and BD ≤ − 2 mmol/L at presentation were included. Patients with a history of trauma or incomplete data were excluded. Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were recorded. LHR, lactate clearance, and BD clearance were calculated. The primary endpoint was in-hospital mortality. Logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic curve analysis were used for statistical analysis.ResultsA total of 693 patients were included in the study, of whom 180 (26%) died in the hospital. The median age of the patients was 69 years, and 55.8% were aged ≥ 65 years. Compared to survivors, nonsurvivors had significantly higher lactate levels (6.47 vs. 5.24 mmol/L), higher LHR values (0.62 vs. 0.42), lower lactate clearance (13.35% vs. 51.84%), and more pronounced base deficits (− 10.55 vs. −6.10 mmol/L) (p < 0.001 for all). Multivariate analysis identified age (odds ratio [OR] = 1.043), LHR (OR = 9.033), and BD clearance as independent predictors of mortality. In ROC analysis, the predictive power of LHR (AUC = 0.749) was comparable to lactate clearance (AUC = 0.762) and superior to initial lactate level.ConclusionsThe LHR is a readily accessible and reliable predictor of in-hospital mortality in nontraumatic critically ill patients. Its prognostic performance is comparable to lactate clearance and superior to initial lactate levels. These findings warrant confirmation by prospective, multicenter studies.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Critically ill, Lactate, Lactate-to-hemoglobin ratio, Base deficit

Background

Nontraumatic critically ill patients remain a significant emergency patient group still debated in the scientific literature [1, 2]. These patients require rapid diagnosis and early initiation of treatment. In recent years, blood lactate concentration—an easily obtainable and widely used parameter—has served as a marker of tissue hypoxia and hypoperfusion. Serum lactate at emergency department (ED) presentation has been used as an important prognostic marker across various conditions [3–6]. Multiple studies have associated lactate levels > 4 mmol/L with increased mortality [7, 8]. Base deficit (BD) is a parameter used to identify metabolic acidosis in critically ill patients [9]. Numerous investigations have demonstrated that BD correlates with mortality in conditions such as sepsis, heart failure, and myocardial infarction [10–13]. Luo et al. found that patients with a BD of ≤ − 2 mmol/L experience markedly increased mortality [11].

Recently, simple-to-calculate parameters for mortality prediction have emerged as innovations in the ED. One such parameter is lactate clearance, which has been used to predict mortality in cardiogenic pulmonary edema, cardiac arrest, and septic shock [14–16]. Ratios of lactate to other blood parameters have been applied in the emergency setting to estimate mortality risk in various diagnoses [17–20]. The lactate-to-hemoglobin ratio (LHR) reflects both sides of the supply–demand imbalance at the tissue level: elevated lactate indicates hypoperfusion and anaerobic metabolism, while reduced hemoglobin indicates reduced oxygen-carrying capacity, together highlighting cellular hypoxia [21]. Although both lactate and BD are associated with mortality in acutely ill ED patients, each has limitations when applied in isolation. Lactate is sensitive but non-specific: it rises with diverse processes (global hypoperfusion, sepsis-related dysmetabolism, β-agonist use, hepatic clearance impairment), and lactate clearance requires repeat sampling, delaying early triage.

Given these considerations, combining lactate and hemoglobin parameters may yield a more meaningful prognostic index for critically ill patients presenting to the ED. This study aimed to determine whether the LHR is an accurate and reliable predictor of in-hospital mortality among this population.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective observational study was conducted in the Emergency Department of Hitit University Erol Olçok Training and Research Hospital. Prior to initiation, ethical approval (No. 2025-02) was obtained from the Hitit University Faculty of Medicine Research Ethics Committee on 7 January, 2025, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study included patients aged > 18 years who were admitted to the ED between January 1 and December 31, 2023. Two parameters deemed significant for mortality prediction were selected: a serum lactate level of ≥ 4.0 mmol/L and a BD of ≤ − 2.0 mmol/L [7, 8, 11]. Patients whose venous blood gas at presentation met these criteria were enrolled. Exclusion criteria comprised presentation in cardiac arrest, had a history of trauma, lacked a follow-up venous blood gas at 2 h, or had an indeterminate clinical outcome in the electronic health record. In-hospital mortality was selected as the primary endpoint in the study.

Data collection

For all included patients, the following variables were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical records: age, sex, lactate, hemoglobin, BD, bicarbonate, glucose values, hospital admission location, discharge status, and in-hospital mortality. Lactate clearance and BD clearance were calculated using the 2-h follow-up venous blood gas: Lactate clearance = (initial lactate − [2-h lactate])/initial lactate * 100, BD clearance = (initial BD − (2-hour BD))/initial BD * 100. The LHR was calculated by dividing the initial lactate by the initial hemoglobin concentration. Patients were categorized into the “Deceased Group” (in-hospital mortality) and the “Survivor Group” (survived to discharge).

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (for n > 50) and Shapiro–Wilk test (for n < 50) [22, 23]. Nonparametric comparisons between two groups were performed with the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared by χ² test when all expected cell counts exceeded five; otherwise, Fisher’s exact test was used [24]. Multiple comparisons of the ratios were performed using the Bonferroni-adjusted Z test. Variables associated with mortality were entered into logistic regression analysis. Diagnostic performance of lactate, lactate clearance, and LHR was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. ROC analysis was applied in the diagnostic performance evaluation, and optimal cut-off points were selected based on the Youden index [25]. Subsequently, the AUC values were compared using the DeLong test as a result of the ROC analysis. Quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (min–max), and categorical data as frequency (n) and percentage (%). A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) [26].

Results

A total of 693 patients were included in the study (Fig. 1). Of these, 25.1% presented with dyspnea, 16.5% with abdominal pain, and 14.4% with chest pain. Final diagnoses included pneumonia in 15.8%, seizure in 11.7%, urinary tract infection in 11.1%, and acute coronary syndrome in 9.4%. Detailed distributions of presenting complaints and final diagnoses are shown in Figs. 2 and 3. The median age of the patients was 69 years, and 311 patients (44.9%) were female. Overall, 55.8% of patients were older than 65 years, while 80.0% of those who died were over 65 (p < 0.001). Mean initial serum lactate was 6.47 mmol/L in the deceased group versus 5.24 mmol/L in survivors (p < 0.001). Lactate clearance was 13.35% in the deceased group and 51.84% in survivors (p < 0.001). At 2 h, mean lactate was 5.94 mmol/L in the deceased group versus 2.66 mmol/L in survivors (p < 0.001). The mean BD was − 10.55 mmol/L in the deceased group versus − 6.10 mmol/L in survivors (p < 0.001). The LHR was 0.62 in the deceased group and 0.42 in survivors (p < 0.001). In ROC analysis, the optimal cutoff for initial lactate was ≥ 5.47 mmol/L (AUC = 0.676; 95% CI: 0.631–0.721), with a sensitivity of 75.6% and a specificity of 56.1%. For lactate clearance, the optimal cutoff was ≤ 33.46% (AUC = 0.762; 95% CI: 0.719–0.806), with a sensitivity of 70.1% and a specificity of 71.0%. For LHR, the optimal cutoff was ≥ 0.50 (AUC = 0.749; 95% CI: 0.709–0.790), with a sensitivity of 73.3% and a specificity of 66.9%. The DeLong test comparison of ROC curves found lactate clearance AUC value to be significantly higher than lactate and LHR AUC values (p = 0.001, 0.004). Discussion

Nontraumatic critically ill patients admitted to the ED constitute an important patient group. With timely intervention in the emergency setting, these patients may fully recover, yet they also carry a high risk of mortality. Consequently, parameters and scoring systems for mortality prediction are frequently used in the ED. In this study, we found that the lactate-to-hemoglobin ratio (LHR) at presentation was independently associated with in-hospital mortality and demonstrated modest discriminatory performance (AUC ≈ 0.75). These results suggest that LHR may serve as an adjunctive, time-zero indicator of risk rather than a stand-alone predictor.

The parameters evaluated in this study initial lactate, lactate clearance, and LHR have significant potential as prognostic markers in critically ill patients. Elevated lactate has long been used as an indirect indicator of cellular hypoxia and hypoperfusion, and is often associated with adverse clinical outcomes [3, 4, 27]. Interpreting lactate in isolation may overlook the dynamic nature of resuscitation; thus, dynamic measurements such as lactate clearance may be more informative when assessing response to therapy and predicting mortality [14–16]. Early assessment of lactate clearance has been proposed in several studies as an actionable target for reducing mortality risk [14]. Senguldur et al. showed that lactate clearance predicted neurological survival and 48-h mortality in patients presenting in cardiac arrest [5]. Our findings indicate that LHR may serve as a prognostic indicator in nontraumatic critically ill patients. By integrating lactate, which reflects tissue hypoxia, with hemoglobin, which affects oxygen-carrying capacity, this ratio offers a more comprehensive physiopathological perspective. Although there is no directly comparable study in the literature, the recently described lactate-hematocrit ratio has shown significant associations with mortality in trauma and sepsis cohorts [28, 29]. While data on LHR remain limited, existing evidence suggests its utility as an adjunct in clinical decision-making, a conclusion supported by our results.

In this study, patients who died exhibited markedly deeper BDs. BD is an early marker of metabolic acidosis and indicates impaired tissue perfusion and inadequate oxygen utilization, particularly in trauma, sepsis, and shock. This can be considered as an indicator of poor clinical trajectories. Yuan et al. demonstrated that increasing BD in septic patients correlated with higher mortality [13]. Conversely, BD clearance was significantly lower in the deceased group. Lack of improvement in BD clearance likely reflects ongoing shock and portends a mortality-associated course.

Our analysis showed that diagnoses associated with elevated BD, such as pneumonia and acute kidney injury, predominated among the deceased group. In contrast, diagnoses not typically leading to mortality, such as seizure, nonspecific abdominal pain, and myalgia, were common in survivors. During generalized seizures, increased muscle oxygen consumption and anaerobic metabolism elevate lactate levels, which typically normalizes within 1–2 h post-ictus [30]. Elevated lactate levels and reduced base deficit in myalgia and nonspecific abdominal pain may reflect sympathetic activation secondary to pain and stress.

This study has limitations. By including only patients with lactate ≥ 4 mmol/L and BD ≤ − 2 mmol/L, we enriched for a high-risk cohort, which strengthens internal validity but limits generalizability to the broader ED population. In addition, the exclusion of patients without 2-h blood gases may have introduced survivorship bias, as very early deaths and rapid dispositions were underrepresented. In our multivariate regression analysis, blood gas pH, bicarbonate, and base deficit clearance were included in the same model. Although these parameters reflect related aspects of metabolic acidosis, they were retained together because each provides complementary information on acid–base status. Importantly, variance inflation factor (VIF) values were < 5, suggesting that significant multicollinearity was not present. Nevertheless, including physiologically interrelated variables in the same model may complicate interpretation, and this should be acknowledged as a limitation of our statistical approach. Finally, the retrospective, single-center design precludes causal inference and warrants cautious interpretation. Prospective, multicenter studies with calibration and decision-curve analyses are required to establish the external validity and clinical utility of LHR.

Conclusion

In this single-center cohort, LHR at presentation was independently associated with in-hospital mortality and showed modest discriminatory performance. At present, LHR should be viewed as an adjunctive, time-zero risk indicator; confirmation in multicenter prospective studies, including calibration and clinical utility assessments, is needed before routine use.
