Impact of COVID-19 on consumption patterns and healthcare expenditures in general dentistry and outpatient physician visits
Authors: Arman Ghafourian, Sara Emamgholipour, Ali Akbar Fazaeli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe onset of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic significantly changed the behavior of healthcare service consumers, including the utilization of physicians’ and general dentists’ services. Health system policymakers and insurance company managers can use the results of this study to implement necessary measures to mitigate changes in the provision of these services in the face of new pandemics.MethodsThis applied study employed an interrupted time-series analysis. Monthly and provincial data on the consumption and expenditures of the general dentistry visits, general practitioners, specialists, and subspecialists were collected from March 21, 2018, to the end of March 20, 2022. Two time intervals, 24 months before and 24 months after the pandemic outbreak in Iran, were analyzed. To adjust for inflation’s effect on health expenditures, real expenditures were calculated. All statistical analyses were performed using EViews 10.ResultsA declining trend in all three services occurred before COVID-19, followed by a sudden decrease in March 2020. The total number of outpatient visits to general practitioners, specialists, and subspecialists, as well as general dentistry visits, decreased by 60%, 55%, and 45%, respectively, from March 2020 to March 2021 compared with March 2019 to March 2020. These reductions were even sharper than those from March 2018 to March 2019, with decreases of 62%, 62%, and 55%, respectively. Compared with the previous year, all three services increased by 164%, 121%, and 22% in March 2021 and March 2022, respectively.ConclusionThe COVID-19 pandemic has led to a sudden decline in demand for physicians’ and general dentists’ services. Background

In 2019, the COVID-19 virus emerged in Wuhan, China, and rapidly spread across borders. This global spread eventually led the World Health Organization (WHO) to declare it a pandemic in March 2020 [1]. Healthcare systems of countries encountered major challenges due to the outbreak. Many medical and dental procedures, including routine visits and elective surgeries, were postponed, increasing the number of patients on waiting lists for care [2–6]. Furthermore, healthcare providers faced a high risk of infection during everyday patient interactions, as the virus primarily spreads through respiratory droplets and contact with saliva or other respiratory secretions from infected individuals [7]. Thus, physicians working on the front lines of patient care during the pandemic were especially vulnerable, and dentists, who had direct exposure to patients’ oral cavities, faced similar risks [8]. These infection-related hazards caused widespread disruption in the delivery of both medical and dental services [9, 10].

Like many countries, Iran also experienced these disruptions, with a sharp decline in outpatient visits and dental services [11, 12]. General dentists are the primary providers of oral healthcare. They provide minor to moderate services, such as tooth extractions and basic periodontal care, to help maintain and improve oral health. For more advanced procedures, general dentists refer patients to dental specialists [13]. General practitioners serve as the first point of contact in the healthcare system and play a key role in managing common illnesses and reducing hospital burden [14, 15]. Moreover, Specialists and subspecialists, through higher-level clinical interventions, are responsible for delivering more specialized services. As general dentists, general practitioners, and specialists/subspecialists play vital roles in healthcare, it is essential to evaluate how COVID-19 influenced their services. This study examines short- and long-term changes in utilization patterns and expenditures for outpatient visits to general practitioners, specialists/subspecialists, and general dentistry visits due to COVID-19.

The results of this study can help insurance company managers and health system policymakers determine deviations in the aforementioned services and implement appropriate interventions. By October 2021, more than 42 million individuals were covered by Iran’s Health Insurance Organization, providing a large insured population [16]. To date, no research has investigated the impact of COVID-19 on general dentistry visits, outpatient visits to general practitioners, and specialists/subspecialists at a national scale within this insurance system. Hence, this gap highlights the importance of conducting this research.

Methods

This study is a time-series analysis, and the variables examined are the numbers, nominal, and real expenditures for outpatient visits to general practitioners, specialists/subspecialists, and general dental services. Data were extracted from the Statistics Unit of the Iran Health Insurance Organization and subsequently refined for analysis. Expenditures consisted of two components, insurance payments and out-of-pocket payments. The shares of both were analyzed, and their sum represented the total expenditure.

It is important to note that only the monthly number of visits and the corresponding expenditures were available, without distinguishing the specific services provided during each appointment. For this reason, general dental services were not classified by procedure type, and the analysis is restricted to visit frequency and associated monthly expenditures. Similarly, physician visits (general practitioners, specialists/subspecialists) were not categorized by service type; only monthly outpatient visit volume and expenditures were used. Additionally, specialist and subspecialist physicians were combined into a single analytical category.

The variables for the number of visits and expenditures were initially collected at the provincial level and then aggregated to calculate national-level data. Monthly data were available for two periods: March 21, 2018–March 20, 2020 (24 months before the COVID-19 outbreak in Iran) and March 22, 2020–March 20, 2022 (24 months after). In this study, the onset of the pandemic in Iran was defined as March 21, 2020.

Nominal data, which are affected by inflation, cannot accurately represent economic changes. Therefore, to adjust for inflation and obtain real expenditures, nominal expenditures for each province were divided by the province-specific consumer price index for healthcare goods and services. The resulting real expenditures were aggregated to compute the total real expenditures for outpatient visits to general practitioners, specialists/subspecialists, and general dentistry for the Iran Health Insurance Organization at the national level. At this stage, the total real expenditures, the organization’s share, and the consumers’ share were derived. Finally, all the obtained values are divided by the US dollar exchange rate to calculate monthly expenses for all services in US dollars. The exchange rate of the US dollar was set at 42,000 Rials from March 21, 2018, to March 20, 2022[17].

To assess the impact of COVID-19, a time-series regression analysis was conducted. First, to avoid spurious regression estimates, the augmented Dickey‒Fuller (ADF) test was applied to examine data stationarity. The results indicated the presence of unit roots in the data. Since one of the objectives of this study was to analyze consumption patterns, differencing the data to remove trends would eliminate the consumption pattern variables for three services. Therefore, instead of classical detrending, the Kwiatkowski–Phillips–Schmidt–Shin (KPSS) test, which directly measures data stationarity, was employed.

The Chow structural break test was used to validate the regression results and to indicate the occurrence of COVID-19 during the study period [18]. To assess the impact of COVID-19 on service utilization and expenditures, an interrupted time-series (ITS) regression model was applied as follows:

In this model, Yt represents the value of each indicator (utilization and service expenditures) in each month; Tt denotes the elapsed time (in months) from the start of the study; Xt is a dummy variable assigned “0” before the intervention (COVID-19 pandemic) and “1” after the intervention; XtTt represents the interaction of time and intervention; and εt represents the error term. The coefficients include β0, the constant (baseline level); β1, reflecting the monthly trend in utilization or expenditures of services during the 24 months before the intervention; β2, representing the immediate change in level following the intervention; and β3, representing the change in trend (slope) during the 24-month post-COVID-19 period relative to the pre-COVID-19 trend [19].

Results

In Figs. 1 and 2, and 3, which show nominal expenditures for all three services, a relatively stable trend is observed before the onset of the pandemic. After removing inflation and converting expenditures to real values (Figs. 4 and 5, and 6), a declining trend is evident across all expenditure components during the first 24 months of the study period, similar to the pattern observed in the number of visits for each service (Figs. 7 and 8, and 9). After the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic on March 21, 2020, all three services experienced a sharp decline within the already downward trend observed prior to the pandemic. Despite a slight compensatory increase in the first couple of months, utilization remained low until the end of the first pandemic year, with the decline continuing until March 2021 for physician outpatient visits and until May 2021 for general dental services. After these dates, a compensatory upward trend emerged in both service utilization and real expenditures across all three services. During this period, consumers’ share of total expenditures, in both real and nominal terms, increased relative to that of insurance organizations, indicating higher out-of-pocket healthcare payments. Discussion

This study aimed to examine the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the number and expenditures of general dental visits and outpatient visits to general practitioners, specialists/ subspecialists covered by the Iran Health Insurance Organization nationwide. The political and economic conditions of Iran during the first half of the study should be considered to explain the discrepancy between nominal and real expenditure trends. In Iran, oil exports account for a significant share of public budget revenues. Therefore, when this source of income is restricted and national revenues decline, the financial sustainability of various sectors, particularly the health system, may be undermined [20]. In May 2018, the United States reimposed economic sanctions on Iran, which restricted the country’s oil sales [21]. These sanctions adversely affected Iran’s health system. One of the major consequences of these sanctions was a sharp increase in currency exchange rates, which raised the prices of goods that depend on foreign currency, particularly medicines and medical devices [22]. Although the majority of medicines and medical equipment are produced in Iran, domestic production remains highly dependent on the import of pharmaceutical raw materials and certain advanced medical technologies. Therefore, sharp increases in exchange rates raised the final cost of healthcare services, thereby reducing affordability and healthcare utilization [23–25]. Inflation was another consequence of the sanctions, which increased living costs and restricted households’ purchasing power for goods and services, including healthcare [26]. Since May 2018, the inflation rate also followed an upward trend, rising from 9.6% in 2017 to 30.5% in 2018, and 41.2% in 2019 [27]. The divergence between nominal and real expenditures in the first half clearly demonstrated the rise in inflation. Altogether, the decline observed in the first half reflects a combination of sanctions, inflationary pressures, currency shocks, and other widespread economic difficulties.

As illustrated in Table 1, the insurer covered the majority of costs during the first three years; however, in the fourth year, the insured accounted for more than 50% of total real expenditures. These shifts should be interpreted in light of Iran’s macroeconomic conditions. In 2021, oil sales increased by approximately 21%, partially alleviating the economic pressures of previous years [28]. Since higher per capita income is associated with greater utilization of healthcare services [29], the 49% increase in per capita income from USD 2,746 in 2020 to approximately USD 4,084 in 2021 [30] may have strengthened households’ ability to spend on healthcare. This improvement in purchasing power may have contributed to the rise in household healthcare expenditures observed from March 2021, supporting recovery in demand following the pandemic.

The smaller increase in the insured’s share compared with patients’ payments can be attributed to the government’s economic pressures, severe budget deficits, and the shift of public funds toward COVID-19 subsidies and emergency support [31, 32]. These financial difficulties may have prevented the government from maintaining its support for the insurer at previous levels. The increase in out-of-pocket payments, together with reduced insurer coverage, increases households’ risk of exposure to catastrophic health expenditures. It is important to note that this interpretation is based solely on the expenditures recorded by the Iran Health Insurance Organization and does not include payments made outside this insurance system.

As shown in Table 1, the annual number of services declined sharply during the pandemic year, from March 2020 to March 2021. In March 2020, Iran’s Ministry of Health and Medical Education implemented a national policy requiring hospitals to suspend elective services and reallocate their resources to the treatment of COVID-19 patients. As a result, hospitals had to prioritize COVID-19 care, while many routine and non-urgent services were temporarily reduced or restricted. Alongside patients’ fear of infection and pandemic-related restrictions, these emergency policies likely contributed to the sudden decline in service utilization by limiting the availability of routine services. The utilization pattern observed in this study is consistent with findings from other Iranian and international studies [33].

Rezaei et al. analyzed 72 months of data from the Social Security Organization of Iran, comparing 47 months before and 25 months after the onset of COVID-19. They found a significant and sudden decline in visits to general practitioners, specialists, and dentists during the first month of the pandemic. Although an upward trend was observed in subsequent months, service levels remained below pre-pandemic levels. Similarly, the present study showed a sudden decline in utilization followed by a gradual recovery. However, visits to general practitioners and specialists/subspecialists returned only to the levels observed in the second year, rather than the first year, while dental services remained below pre-pandemic levels [34]. A study conducted in Ontario, Canada, also reported a marked reduction in in-person general practitioner (GP) visits during the initial six months of the pandemic. This finding is consistent with the substantial decline in GP visits observed in our study during the pandemic year [35].

Another study using primary care data from England showed that referrals to musculoskeletal services were increasing before the pandemic. However, during the early COVID-19 period, from February to May 2020, referrals decreased from 4.8% to 2.4% and then recovered to 4.5% by July 2020. This pattern is consistent with the sharp decline in specialist and subspecialist outpatient visits observed during the pandemic year in the present study, followed by their gradual recovery in the fourth year [36].

Regarding dental care, Emamgholipour et al. (2023) found that Iranian households’ use of dental services declined from 4.59% one year before the COVID-19 pandemic to 2.84% in the two years following its onset, representing a 61% reduction [37]. Similarly, Akbari et al. (2024) reported that average daily dental service utilization after the onset of COVID-19 was approximately 40% lower than before the pandemic. Although a compensatory increase was observed toward the end of 2021, service levels remained below pre-pandemic levels [38]. Furthermore, Hardwick et al. reported that during the closure of primary dental services in England, hospitalizations for dental abscess drainage declined from 3.80 per 100,000 persons-year before restrictions to 0.59 during full closure, and then increased to 3.04 during limited reopening [39]. Collectively, these studies suggest that dental services experienced a sharp decline followed by a gradual recovery. In the present study, dental service utilization increased in a compensatory manner but did not return to pre-COVID levels.

The expansion of COVID-19 vaccination in Iran, which began in February 2021, may have reduced public fear of infection and contributed to the gradual rebound in healthcare utilization [40, 41]. Accordingly, the increases observed in both service volumes and related expenditures during the fourth year of the present study (March 2021–March 2022) may be partly explained by the vaccination rollout.

The sustainability of healthcare service delivery under sanctions and large-scale crises requires a multidimensional approach that encompasses both economic reforms and structural changes within the health system. In countries where a substantial share of public revenue comes from oil exports, developing other economic sectors and strengthening non-oil revenues may reduce the health system’s vulnerability to oil price fluctuations and external pressures. Furthermore, sanctions mechanisms must be revised in ways that minimize unintended consequences for the health system. Establishing transparent, sustainable channels for importing medicines, medical equipment, and raw materials can help prevent supply chain disruptions, even under banking and transportation restrictions. Furthermore, sanctions mechanisms must be redesigned so that their consequences do not affect the health system. Establishing transparent and sustainable channels for importing medicines, medical equipment, and raw materials can help prevent disruptions to their supply chains, even under banking and transportation restrictions. In addition, developing domestic capacity across the entire supply chain or key segments can reduce reliance on imports and strengthen the health system’s resilience.

Furthermore, countries should expand their strategic reserves of essential health-related items. These reserves should be planned according to consumption patterns, socioeconomic conditions, and potential supply chain risks. Expanding digital and online health services, as well as mobile and other flexible care delivery models, is also important during crises. Such measures can help maintain access to essential care during crises and prevent interruptions in its delivery.

This study has several limitations. First, the study objectives were defined according to the available data. Second, information on general dentistry visits and outpatient visits to general practitioners, specialists, and subspecialists was limited to total expenditures, the insurer’s share, the insured’s share, and the number of services. The data did not include detailed breakdowns by type of dental service or medical specialty, such as cardiology or other specialty areas. As a result, defining more specific objectives comparable to those reported in some international studies was not feasible. Third, we had no access to information on private healthcare expenditures incurred by insured individuals outside the insurance system. Therefore, our analysis and findings are based solely on data recorded by the Iran Health Insurance Organization, and out-of-pocket payments in other sectors could not be assessed. Fourth, given that healthcare utilization may exhibit strong seasonal patterns, the primary ITS model did not explicitly incorporate seasonal controls. Future research incorporating seasonal adjustment or monthly fixed effects could improve the accuracy and robustness of ITS estimates.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicated that the utilization of general dental services and outpatient visits to general practitioners, specialists, and sub-specialists showed a downward trend before the COVID-19 pandemic; a trend that may be associated with the economic consequences of sanctions and reduced household purchasing power. In this challenging economic environment, a sudden and significant decline was observed across all three service types during the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic. Following the initial shock, outpatient visits partially recovered toward pre-pandemic levels, but general dental services remained below pre-COVID-19 levels. Furthermore, the increase in out-of-pocket payments one year after the onset of the pandemic indicates intensified financial pressure on households. These findings highlight the need to strengthen insurance organizations’ financial capacity, reduce the financial burden on households, and maintain access to essential health services during economic and health crises.
