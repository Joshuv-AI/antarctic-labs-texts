Validation of Point-of-care Testing Device HemoCue® Glucose 201RT in Blood Glucose Estimation
Authors: Farhad Ahamed, Debkumar Pal, Sarika Palepu, Jeevanmuktha Somashekara, Sibasish Sahoo, Ayan Roy, Tanmay Saha, Semanti Das, Kalyan Goswami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:We aimed to conduct this study to compare the validity of the HemoCue® Glucose 201 RT System (point-of-care testing (POCT) device) in estimating blood glucose levels in patients attending a tertiary care hospital, considering the automated analyzer method (Vitros® 4600 chemistry system, QuidelOrtho Corporation, USA) as the gold standard.Methods:We conducted this study among the patients attending the outpatient department in a tertiary care hospital in Eastern India. Venous blood was collected to estimate fasting blood glucose (FBS) and postprandial blood glucose (PPBS) using the automated analyzer and the POCT device, along with capillary blood to estimate FBS and PPBS using the POCT device.Results:We enrolled 200 patients with a mean age of 49.4 (standard deviation 12.6) years. Lin’s CCC for POCT device compared to automated analyzer (venous blood) for FBS estimation was 0.95 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.94 to 0.96, P value < 0.001) for venous blood and 0.95 (95% CI: 0.94–0.97, P value < 0.001) for capillary blood. Lin’s CCC for PPBS estimation was 0.99 (95% CI: 0.98–0.99, P value < 0.001) for venous blood and 0.98 (95% CI: 0.97–0.98, P value < 0.001) for capillary blood.Conclusion:The POCT device showed substantial concordance with the gold standard method for measuring both FBS and PPBS. It also demonstrated a fair agreement with the automated analyzer in estimating blood glucose levels.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic and severe condition marked by high blood glucose levels resulting from either defects in β-cell function that affect insulin activity or insulin resistance.[1] With the global epidemiological transition, diabetes mellitus has become a significant public health problem and emerged as the eighth leading cause of death and disability combined in the world.[2] According to the International Diabetes Federation’s estimates, globally, 10.5% (537 million) of the adult population (20–79 years) has diabetes, with the Southeast Asia region contributing around 17%, and a projected 151 million cases by 2045. Further, this has caused an expenditure of USD 10 billion in 2021.[3] In India, an estimated 77 million adults over the age of 18 have type 2 diabetes, while nearly 25 million are prediabetic, putting them at a higher risk of developing the disease. Moreover, around 50% of individuals remain unaware of their diabetic status, which can lead to serious health complications if not diagnosed and treated early.[4]

Lifelong blood sugar monitoring and appropriate treatment present significant challenges. In India, frequent testing can be complex for patients, especially in primary healthcare settings with limited laboratory facilities. Hence, operator-friendly point-of-care testing (POCT) equipment with high diagnostic accuracy that provides results in short intervals is the need of the hour in India. POCT refers to diagnostic tests conducted at or near the site of patient care, using a user-friendly analyzer to facilitate quick, efficient diagnosis.[5] Currently, the revamped National Program for Non-Communicable Diseases (NP-NCD) recommends monthly blood sugar tests of all diabetic individuals at Ayushman Arogya Mandir (erstwhile Sub-Centre) using different POCT devices as part of disease management.[67]

HemoCue® Glucose 201 RT, one of the POCT machines available in the country, was validated for preliminary use in patients with diabetes mellitus almost a decade ago.[8] Validation studies of point-of-care testing (POCT) devices have reported variable diagnostic accuracy, with some demonstrating strong correlation and agreement, while others showing lower accuracy.[9101112]

Therefore, we aimed to compare the validity of the HemoCue® Glucose 201RT system in estimating blood glucose levels, considering the autoanalyzer (Vitros® 4600 chemistry system, QuidelOrtho Corporation, USA) method as the gold standard.

METHODS

Study design

We conducted a prospective validation study comparing the HemoCue® Glucose 201RT system with the auto-analyzer as the gold standard for estimating blood glucose levels.

Study setting and study participants

The study was conducted in the phlebotomy area of the outpatient department (OPD) of a tertiary care center (All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), Kalyani), located in West Bengal, with approximately 100 OPD patients undergoing daily blood glucose estimation using an automated analyzer (Ortho Vitros 4600). The mean (range) temperature and relative humidity (Rh) in the study area during the data collection period were 22° C (17 ° C–27° C) and 77% (65%–89%). The temperature and humidity were kept in this range, as enzymatic reactions and electrochemical detection may adversely impact the estimated values.[13]

We included adults aged 18 years and above who came to the laboratory for fasting blood glucose (FBS) and PPBS testing, regardless of their disease status, including normoglycemic, prediabetic, and diabetic individuals. Pregnant women, individuals with known hemoglobinopathies, and those who refused to give blood samples for PPBS estimation were excluded from the study. Both fasting and postprandial blood glucose (PPBS) were measured for participants to ensure the validity and establish the POCT device across a wider range.

Study duration

The study was conducted from December 2024 to January 2025.

Description of devices

The HemoCue® Glucose 201 RT System (HemoCue AB, Angelholm, Sweden) is a portable, battery-operated system that uses modified glucose dehydrogenase to measure the total amount of glucose photometrically at the endpoint.[14] As the machine is factory-calibrated and traceable to the isotope dilution gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (ID GC–MS) method, it requires no further calibration or coding. The device requires <4 microliter (μL) of blood, and the turnover time per sample is significantly less (within one minute) when compared to the automated analyzer (Ortho Vitros 4600) (around four hours).[14] The microcuvette contains dried reagents, and the machine can measure plasma glucose levels from 11 to 555 mg/dL. The Vitros 4600 chemistry system (QuidelOrtho Corporation, USA) was used to measure FBS and PPBS using the glucose oxidase (GOx) dry-slide method. Automated chemistry analyzers demonstrate high analytical accuracy and precision and are routinely used as reference standards in clinical studies. Their analytical validity is ensured through stringent internal quality control and participation in external quality assurance programs in accordance with standards prescribed by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO 15189) and the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute.[1516]

Sample size and sampling technique

Considering the expected mean of differences of milligram per deciliter (mg/dl), the standard deviation of differences (bias) as 9 mg/dl, the maximum allowed differences for validation of the two instruments as 25 mg/dl, alpha error of 5%, and power 80%, the minimum required sample size was 169.[1718] We recruited 200 individuals using convenience sampling. However, on a single day, the number of enrolled participants was limited to 10 to maintain data quality.

Methodology

One trained project staff member with a bachelor’s degree in laboratory technology collected sociodemographic data and collected blood samples (venous and capillary). Fasting of 8–12 hours was ensured, and two milliliters (ml) of venous blood samples were taken aseptically from the antecubital vein and stored in a sodium fluoride vial. Another 2 mL of blood was aseptically collected from the antecubital vein and stored in a fluoride vial after 2 hours of consuming 75 g of glucose for estimation of PPBS. The FBS and PPBS were estimated using the biochemistry laboratory’s automated analyzer (Vitros® 4600 chemistry system). Simultaneously, one drop of blood was collected from the vacutainer and transferred to the microcuvette of the HemoCue® 201RT to estimate FBS and PPBS using POCT. With each participant’s left hand relaxed, palms warmed, and rings removed, the middle fingertip was pricked on the side with a sterilized lancet, using aseptic precautions. In the finger-prick method, the first two drops of capillary blood were discarded using sterile gauze; after a gap of 60–90 seconds, the subsequent drop, obtained with light pressure on the fingertip, was collected into the microcuvette. This capillary blood sample was used to measure FBS and PPBS with the HemoCue® Glucose 201 RT. Capillary blood collection was performed after venous blood collection in the same setting, with a 60–90-second gap.

Quality control

Internal quality control was run daily for the Vitros® 4600 chemistry system. The biochemistry laboratory has external quality control mechanisms.

Ethical approval

We obtained ethical approval from the Institutional Ethical Committee of AIIMS, Kalyani (IEC/AIIMS/Kalyani/certificate/2024/005). Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants before data collection.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered in Excel 2013 and analyzed in R (RStudio 2024.04.0 Build 735) using the DescTools package (updated version on 20th September 2025). Mean (and confidence interval (CI)) FBS and PPBS estimated in Vitros® 4600 chemistry system and HemoCue® Glucose 201RT were compared. All values of FBS and PPBS were reported as milligrams per deciliter (mg/dl). Paired analysis was conducted using an automated analyzer (venous sample) vs. HemoCue® Glucose 201RT (venous sample) and an automated analyzer (venous sample) vs. HemoCue® Glucose 201RT (capillary sample) for both FBS and PPBS. Agreement between the point-of-care glucose measurement and the laboratory reference method was primarily assessed using Bland–Altman analysis, including estimation of mean bias and 95% limits of agreement. We conducted a subgroup analysis for the Bland–Altman analysis based on FBS ≥126 mg/dl and PPBS ≥200 mg/dl. As secondary measures, Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient (CCC) and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) were calculated to evaluate concordance and reliability between the two methods. ICC was estimated using a two-way mixed-effects model for absolute agreement. FBS and PPBS values were categorized into normal and high glucose levels (FBS ≥126 mg/dL and PPBS >200 mg/dL) to calculate the sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive values of the POCT. Weighted Kappa values were calculated to identify the agreement between HemoCue® Glucose 201RT and the automated analyzer for venous and capillary blood for FBS and PPBS values. We estimated the ICC for FBS and PPBS values obtained from venous and capillary blood samples. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the diagnostic performance of the point-of-care device in identifying clinically relevant thresholds (e.g., ≥126 mg/dL for FBS and ≥200 mg/dL for PPBS). A P value of 0.05 was used as the cutoff for statistical significance.

RESULTS

A total of 200 study participants were included in the study. The study participants’ mean (SD) age was 49.44 years. Of the 200 study participants, 113 (56.5%) were males and the rest were females. The blood glucose values ranged from 73 mg/dl to 486 mg/dl on the automated analyzer and from 59 mg/dl to 498 mg/dl on the HemoCue® Glucose 201RT device. The mean (95% CI) of FBS values using the automated analyzer was 121.87 mg/dl (116.37–127.37), HemoCue® Glucose 201RT (venous sample) was 114.59 mg/dl (109.48–119.70), and HemoCue® Glucose 201RT (capillary sample) was 116.99 mg/dl (111.90–122.08). The mean (95% CI) of PPBS values using the automated analyzer was 211.52 mg/dl (198.58–224.46), HemoCue® Glucose 201RT (venous sample) was 202.16 mg/dl (188.94–215.38), and HemoCue® Glucose 201RT (capillary sample) was 216.80 mg/dl (203.91–229.69). There was a statistically significant difference between the FBS values measured using HemoCue® Glucose 201 RT System and automated analyzer by venous sample (P < 0.001) and capillary sample (P < 0.001) (using paired-t test). There was a significant difference between the PPBS values measured using HemoCue® Glucose 201RT and the automated analyzer for venous sample (P < 0.001) and capillary sample (P < 0.001).

The Bland–Altman analysis revealed a mean difference of −7.28 mg/dL between FBS measured using venous blood with the HemoCue Glucose 201RT device and an automated analyzer, with upper and lower limits of agreement (11.36 mg/dL and −25.93 mg/dL, respectively).

Similarly, the Bland–Altman analysis revealed a mean difference of −4.89 mg/dL between FBS measured using capillary blood with the HemoCue Glucose 201RT device and venous blood analyzed using an automated analyzer, with upper and lower limits of agreement (15.62 mg/dL and −25.39 mg/dL, respectively) [Figure 1]. The subgroup analysis based on FBS ≥126 mg/dL (automated analyzer) showed a lower mean difference (-5.79 mg/dL) among individuals with lower glucose values than among those with hyperglycemia (-10.60 mg/dL) based on venous blood values. The mean difference between PPBS values measured using venous blood with the HemoCue® Glucose 201RT device and an automated analyzer was −9.37 mg/dL, with upper and lower limits of agreement (15.71 mg/dL and −34.44 mg/dL, respectively). Similarly, the Bland–Altman analysis showed a mean difference of 5.27 mg/dL between PPBS measured in capillary blood using a POCT device and venous blood using an automated analyzer, with upper and lower limits of agreement (41.25 mg/dL and −30.70 mg/dL, respectively) [See Figure 1]. The subgroup analysis based on PPBS ≥200 mg/dl (automated analyzer) reported a similar mean difference (-9.41 mg/dl) among the individuals with lower glucose values compared to individuals with hyperglycemia (-9.32 mg/dl) based on capillary blood values. However, the mean difference in capillary blood values among normoglycemic individuals was more remarkable than that among hyperglycemic individuals. [Supplementary Figure 2]

The sensitivity was 83.8%, and the specificity was 99.3% for HemoCue® Glucose 201RT using venous blood to detect hyperglycemia at a cutoff of ≥126 mg/dl, compared with the automated analyzer for FBS. DISCUSSION

We compared HemoCue® Glucose 201RT with the gold standard, resulting in better performance in terms of sensitivity, specificity, concordance, and agreement for PPBS estimation compared to FBS estimation. Validity and reliability were similar between venous and capillary blood usage in HemoCue Glucose 201 RT. Bland–Altman plots indicated a bidirectional dispersion in the differences between HemoCue® Glucose 201 RT and the automated analyzer. Our study reported a very high Lin’s CCC across all estimations. This finding was similar to the conclusions of other validation studies using POCT devices for blood glucose estimation.[19] The higher Lin’s CCC value suggested a better performance of the POCT device in measuring PPBS. This finding also implied that this machine can be used to measure any blood glucose level, varying from as low as 59 mg/dl to 498 mg/dl. Bland–Atman analysis showed a narrower bias margin than that of existing devices.[20] We also found very few values (<5%) beyond the upper and lower levels of agreement, suggesting good performance of HemoCue® Glucose 201RT (POCT). An almost perfect AUC value suggested that our test had good diagnostic accuracy. Less sensitivity of this POCT device in measuring FBS suggested a probable poor performance in screening for diabetes mellitus. However, being a continuous parameter, Lin’s CCC and Bland–Altman plots provided a better understanding of the performance of the POCT device.[2122] Subgroup Bland–Altman analysis showed that the HemoCue® Glucose 201RT system performed better at lower FBS levels, while its performance remained relatively consistent across different PPBS values. A lower sensitivity of the POCT device for FBS estimation may limit its usage for screening of diabetes mellitus in the community.

The surge in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) cases in India demands a valid and accurate POCT device for estimating blood glucose, specifically for use at the community level and at the doorstep.[23] The HemoCue® Glucose 201 RT System is valid and practical for blood glucose estimation, as it can be powered by batteries and weighs only 2 kg. This POCT device can be integrated with existing Ayushman Arogya Mandirs in India to manage T2DM patients, subject to economic considerations.[2425]

We conducted this study in accordance with standard procedures for blood collection. This ensures its generalizability across different groups of individuals with hyperglycemia and a wide range of blood glucose values. We conducted this study using capillary and venous blood, suggesting that this machine serves as an alternative to an automated analyzer in community or resource-limited settings, given its high specificity. However, our study is limited because we did not use a detailed cost-effectiveness analysis, which could improve its pragmatic value. We have used both venous and capillary blood in a POCT device, as comparing only capillary blood would not be sufficient to establish diagnostic performance.

The study was limited by the use of nonrandom sampling. Single-operator-based measurement in this study may limit the external validity and generalizability of the findings, as variability in real-world settings with multiple users was not assessed. However, it minimizes interoperator variability and enhances measurement internal consistency.

CONCLUSION

The HemoCue® Glucose 201RT POCT device was particularly sensitive for measuring FBS and PPBS with a very high AUC value, suggesting the high diagnostic validity of the device. It also demonstrated a very high Lin’s CCC value concerning automated analyzer, suggesting the high reliability of the device. The mean bias was less for both FBS and PPBS, suggesting equally good performance of the device in a wide range of blood glucose values. This POCT device can be integrated with the existing Ayushman Arogya Mandirs in India in managing T2DM patients. We also recommend for conducting further economic evaluation studies for estimating the cost-effectiveness of this POCT device compared to the existing lab-based instruments.
