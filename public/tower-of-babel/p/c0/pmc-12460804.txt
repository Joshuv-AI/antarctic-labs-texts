Urban heat island dynamics in Rawalpindi: a 30-year remote sensing analysis and future projections
Authors: Sundas Liyaqat, Muhammad Y S Dasti, Ejaz Hussain, Faisal Mumtaz, Dmitry E Kucher, Aqil Tariq
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrban Heat Islands (UHIs) pose a significant environmental challenge in rapidly urbanizing regions, influencing climate resilience, energy consumption, and public health. This study presents a comprehensive spatiotemporal analysis of UHI dynamics in Rawalpindi, Pakistan. Time-series analysis over 30 years (1990–2020) using Landsat satellite data processed through Google Earth Engine (GEE). Unlike previous studies, this research integrates a hybrid methodology. Random Forest classification for high-precision Land Use Land Cover (LULC) mapping correlates with the Statistical Mono-Window algorithm for accurate Land Surface Temperature (LST) estimation. The findings reveal a 22.2% increase in built-up areas. That impacts the past three decades, correlating with a 3.04 °C rise in LST, at an annual increment of 0.12 °C. This study applies the Cellular Automata-Artificial Neural Network (CA-ANN) model to project future UHI intensification in Rawalpindi. Results aim to forecast a 13.8% increase in built-up areas by 2030, which is expected to exacerbate thermal stress and energy demand. According to the results, the built-up area has grown highly erratic, mainly at the expense of vegetated land. A statistically significant correlation (r = 0.43) between LST and urban expansion underscores the urgency of adopting targeted mitigation strategies. Our research has some limitations; one of the important ones was that we could not assess multiple potential socio-economic drivers of urban growth because of a scarcity of spatial data. The results highlight the necessity for sustainable urban planning approaches, including integrating vegetation and heat-mitigating urban infrastructure to reduce thermal stress and enhance climate resilience.Keywords: Urban heat island, Land surface temperature, Google earth engine, Remote sensing, Random forest, CA-ANN modelingSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Urbanization, a key indicator of socio-economic development, profoundly alters natural landscapes, transforming them into urban environments with significant climatic consequences1. Among the most notable impacts of urbanization is the formation of Urban Heat Islands (UHIs), a phenomenon where urban areas experience higher temperatures than their rural surroundings2. This temperature disparity arises primarily from replacing natural vegetation with impervious surfaces, such as asphalt and concrete, which absorb and retain heat while inhibiting moisture evaporation. Furthermore, anthropogenic heat emissions exacerbate these effects, leading to localized warming and contributing to broader climate variability3,4.

The implications of UHIs extend far beyond increased temperatures, influencing energy consumption, public health, and agricultural productivity. Urban areas characterized by high-density development, narrow streets, and limited green spaces trap heat, reduce air circulation and amplify thermal effects5. These issues are intricately linked to changes in land use and land cover (LULC), as urban expansion disrupts natural ecosystems and alters the thermal properties of the land surface6,7. For example, converting green spaces to impervious surfaces diminishes moisture availability and evaporation, leading to elevated surface and air temperatures2,8. Nighttime temperature disparities in urban centers are particularly pronounced, exacerbating health risks and increasing demand for cooling energy.

UHIs are typically categorized into two types: Surface Urban Heat Islands (SUHIs), which refer to temperature differences between land surfaces, and Atmospheric UHIs, which concern air temperature variations near the ground9–12. Both types provide critical insights into urban thermal dynamics and are important indicators of the broader environmental effects of urbanization. For instance, time-series studies in Karachi have documented a marked increase in UHI intensity between 1998 and 2016, driven by urban sprawl and the loss of green spaces13. The rising nighttime temperature disparities in urban areas are particularly noticeable, highlighting the persistent and intensifying nature14,15.

On a global scale, urbanization is recognized as a major driver of climate change, with its effects most pronounced in rapidly developing regions. Recent advances in remote sensing technologies and Geographic Information Systems (GIS) have transformed how we study urban environments, providing powerful tools for monitoring and analyzing UHIs16. Remote sensing enables large-scale, consistent monitoring of land surface temperature (LST) and LULC changes over time, which is essential for assessing the extent and impact of UHIs17. Platforms such as Google Earth Engine (GEE) allow access to decades of satellite data, enabling efficient analysis of urban thermal behaviour and supporting evidence-based policy decisions18. These technologies are particularly valuable in data-scarce regions, offering high-resolution insights into urbanization’s environmental impacts and supporting evidence-based policy decisions.

Pakistan, a South Asian country of about 212 million people and the fifth most populous, is especially prone to the adverse effects of climate change since it is one of the 10 countries most severely affected by global warming. To support urban sustainability and climate change adaptation, this situation demands a grasp of LULC changes and how they pertain to LST to provide helpful standards for optimal development19. In regions like Pakistan, addressing the UHI effect is crucial on a large scale. The situation demands comprehensive studies of the metropolitan cities in the country, with a comparison of the environmental factors contributing to the high rise in temperature. In past years, many studies focused on big cities like Lahore and Karachi for studies on temperature rise. However, the limitations in advanced analysis of high-resolution data urge the research gap to be addressed by comparing major metropolitan cities’ urban sprawl and green areas. According to the recent census in 2017, Rawalpindi has been declared the third largest city of Punjab after Lahore and Faisalabad, respectively, with a population of about 2.09 million. Due to its proximity to the Capital, the city is growing and has many impervious surfaces. If it continues to increase in the same manner, it might harm the temperature in the future. Cities such as Rawalpindi are experiencing rapid urbanization, with expanded built-up areas, loss of green spaces, and conversion of agricultural land, all of which exacerbate the UHI effect and increase vulnerability to thermal stress20. The lack of coordinated urban planning and the increasing prevalence of impervious surfaces have resulted in significant environmental degradation and rising land surface temperatures. To mitigate these effects, it is essential to understand the relationship between LULC changes and UHI formation, which can inform the development of sustainable urban planning strategies.

Mitigating the UHI effect requires various context-specific strategies21. Green infrastructure solutions, such as rooftop gardens and vertical greening systems, can help offset the loss of vegetation in densely built environments. Large-scale tree plantation campaigns and converting underutilized land into green spaces can significantly reduce surface temperatures and improve urban air quality. Compact urban designs that limit horizontal sprawl while preserving natural ecosystems enhance climate resilience22. Remote sensing and GIS offer invaluable tools for monitoring LULC and LST dynamics in real-time, providing urban planners with the data needed to implement these strategies effectively23.

The study aims to develop and identify the ecological zones and affected areas to inform legislators of the intervention and sustainable plans accordingly. In the past centuries, people relied on agricultural growth for livelihood and economic dependence on natural resources, but they were affected by climate change and land degradation. Taking a sustainability approach to address ecological deterioration and socio-economic resilience is imperative.

This study investigates the dynamics of UHIs in Rawalpindi over the past 30 years (1990–2020), focusing on how LULC changes have influenced Land Surface Temperature (LST) variations. By utilizing satellite-based remote sensing and geospatial analysis, the study aims to (i) analyze 30 years of LULC data (1990–2020) using Google Earth Engine (GEE) and Landsat’s thermal bands, (ii) examine the spatial and temporal relationships between UHIs and LULC changes, and (iii) Project future LULC changes and their impact on UHIs through geospatial modeling, providing actionable insights to guide urban planning and climate mitigation efforts.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study primarily focuses on Rawalpindi, Punjab province in Pakistan, extended between 33.5651° N longitude and 73.0169° E latitude and 508 m above mean sea level. It has been declared the third largest city of Punjab after Lahore and Faisalabad, with a population of about 2.5 million (Review, 2025 #10) and a total area of approximately 458 km2. It is a humid subtropical region with hot summers and cool to cold winters24. The average temperature in summer is recorded as 38 °C. The average temperature varies from 4 to 38 °C every year and is rarely below 2 °C and above 43 °C. Pakistan Meteorological Department (PMD)(PMD, 2025 #1) recorded an average temperature of 38.11 °C in the summer (May to August) in 2020. The hottest month of the summer season was recorded as June, with an average maximum temperature of 38 °C (2020). The research focused on the urban area, an industrial, commercial, and military hub. It is adjacent to Islamabad and known as the twin city (Islamabad-Rawalpindi Metropolitan). These two cities have interactive business communities with strong economic and social links. Rapidly growing urbanization causes increasing populations and financial growth, drastically altering urban land use patterns and resulting in several environmental issues, including an escalating thermal environment, soil contamination, water shortages, and pollution25. Data preparations

This study utilized remote sensing data to analyze Urban Heat Islands (UHIs). Satellite data provided by the USGS, known for its free availability and varying spatial and spectral resolutions, formed the basis of the analysis. Given the study’s focus on a 30-year time series (1990–2020) and the launch of Sentinel in 2013, Landsat data were deemed most suitable for spatial analysis. Specifically, data from Landsat-5 and Landsat-8 were employed. Landsat-7 data were avoided due to scan line issues26. The data were accessed through Google Earth Engine (GEE), which provided significant advantages, including bypassing the need to download enormous datasets, thereby saving time and effort. Thermal bands from the Landsat series were employed to derive Land Surface Temperature (LST), while additional spectral bands were used for Land Use and Land Cover (LULC) classification. Given that the equatorial crossing time for Landsat Operational Land Imager (OLI) and Landsat Thematic Mapper (TM) differs by only 15 min, a minor discrepancy relative to Landsat’s temporal resolution, this variation was determined to have a negligible impact on the LST results26.

Data processing and spatial analysis within the GEE environment were processed to find the time-series pattern of LULC and LST and project the results for future years, such as 2030. A Cellular Automata-Artificial Neural Network (CA-ANN) model was trained using classified Land Use and Land Cover (LULC) data27. Multiple model iterations were run with accurate input data to ensure reliable predictions. For optimal LULC classification and Land Surface Temperature (LST) results, average Digital Number (DN) values for pixels with minimal cloud cover during the summer months (May and August) were filtered for each year. The data were then spatially clipped to the Rawalpindi city boundary shapefile for Spatial study area analysis.

The detailed methodology, depicted in Fig. 2, outlines the workflow adopted for the study. Data acquisition began with filtering satellite data for the specified timeframe using Landsat-5 Thematic Mapper (TM) and Landsat-8 Operational Land Imager (OLI) for LULC classification. The Thermal Infrared Sensor (TIRS) data retrieved temperature measurements. Only Tier-1 surface reflectance collections were considered for high-quality analysis. To validate the satellite-derived LST data, average air temperatures recorded for the summer months (May and August) over the 30 years were compared with calculated LST values. To ensure consistency and accuracy, a ground-truth validation is required.

Methodology

Training datasets for LULC classification

Samples for training and validation were gathered using imagery from Google Earth alongside additional datasets. These include the Copernicus Global Land Cover Layer (CGLS-100), MODIS Land Cover, high-resolution imagery from Google Earth, and various spectral indices, NDVI, NDBI, and NDWI, calculated from Tier-1 surface reflectance data of Landsat-5 and Landsat-8.

The stratified sample method took approximately 500–1500 samples over time. The time-series samples are collected and then split into a 70/30 ratio for training and validation of the model. There are 70% (∼2500) points for the training of the RF classifier, and an additional 30% (∼1000) training points are kept for the validation process. The average sample points collected for the random forest classifier are 200–500 for each class, as shown in Fig. 3. The number of samples for each class varies with the changes over time. Change detection through satellite images is essential to highlight, as it shows how surface features change spatially and statistically28. The spatial patterns of each class over time highlight the heterogeneous factors that converted from one class to another. The sample points vary for each year; for example, in 1990, there was less built-up area compared to 2000, as it increased with time.

The model plugin is installed in QGIS. 2.89 version, for the simulation analysis of LULC with multiple parameters, Table 4 has details. Validation of the model.

The model was validated with the already classified data and identified as results closely related to the LULC, with an accuracy of approximately 80% and above, which shows that the model is trained and could be used for the simulation of the year 2030. The prediction for the LULC for the year 2030 was calculated using the input data from the LULC of 2010 and 2020. Other deriving factors, such as training data, are also fetched into the model as initial data to maximize the performance and accuracy of the model. The model parameters have the LULC dataset along with multiple parameters. 30-m DEM was downloaded and cropped to the study area with a spatial resolution of 30 m and projection as WGS 1984 UTM Zone 42N. The parameters derived from DEM, the slope aspect, and the euclidean distance of the nearby roads and water features help the prediction results to maximum accuracy39.

Unlike studies focused on developing cities, which often benefit from planned urbanization and green infrastructure40,41. Our research highlights the unique challenges of unplanned urban sprawl. The projected 13.8% increase in built-up areas by 2030, accompanied by only a 3% rise in vegetation, presents a pressing case for sustainable urban planning. Our study provides actionable insights for urban planners to prioritize green infrastructure and adopt innovative strategies, such as rooftop gardens and vertical greening systems, to counterbalance rising LST. Combining these techniques with Google Earth Engine provides a scalable framework that can be adapted for other rapidly urbanizing cities with limited ground-based data. Retrieving of land surface temperature (LST)

LST was computed using the implemented Statistical Mono-Window (SMW) model developed by the Climate Monitoring Satellite Application Facility (CM-SAF)45. The SMW algorithm was developed to derive the LST from the climate data records retrieved from Meteosat First and Second Generation sensors (MFG and MSG)46. The SMW algorithm was designed with a simple structure that is easy to implement using thermal bands of Landsat data. The other datasets provided by the GEE catalogue were also included as the atmospheric data from the National Center for Atmospheric Research (NCAR)46 and surface emissivity available as ASTER Global Emissivity Database retrieved by the Advanced Spaceborne Thermal Emission and Reflection (ASTER) developed by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA).

The SMW algorithm computed the LST using the thermal bands: Band 6 from Landsat-5 and Band 10 from Landsat-8. For Landsat-8, only Band 10 was considered because B11 has recently experienced some calibration issues highlighted by the scientists of USGS47. To calculate the LST, the Top of Atmosphere image collections of Landsat-5 and Landsat-8 were retrieved through the GEE. The model’s approach was based on the empirical relationship between the LST and Top of Atmosphere (TOA) brightness temperature in the single thermal Infrared band of Landsat48, the Statistical Mono Window algorithm (SMW) process.

Discussion

This study highlights significant changes in Rawalpindi’s urban landscape over the past three decades, revealing a 22.3% increase in built-up areas, leading to a 1.2 °C rise in LST per decade. The correlation between LST and built-up areas (r = 0.43) underscores the critical role of impervious surfaces in driving UHI effects. Our findings align with these global patterns, demonstrating a 22.3% increase in built-up areas correlating with a 1.2 °C per decade rise in LST. However, Rawalpindi’s minimal vegetation increase (0.3%) suggests a greater vulnerability than cities implementing urban greening measures, such as Singapore54 and Suzhou55. This underscores the need for localized mitigation strategies tailored to the socio-environmental context of rapidly developing cities. The UHI phenomenon has been extensively studied in urban centers globally, revealing consistent patterns of increased temperatures due to urbanization. Studies in cities like Beijing and Islamabad have demonstrated a strong negative correlation between vegetation cover and LST, highlighting the critical role of green spaces in moderating UHI effects56. Impervious surfaces, such as asphalt and concrete in built-up areas, absorb and retain solar heat, significantly increasing LST values. Studies like57–59 have provided a global perspective on UHI dynamics, emphasizing the role of vegetation indices in mitigating thermal stress. The findings from Rawalpindi align with these global observations, particularly highlighting the correlation between vegetation loss and rising temperatures in the city.

Over the 30 years examined, the 3.04 °C rise in LST reflects the compounded effects of rapid urbanization and the loss of vegetative cover. This rate is comparable to other cities in developing regions, such as Dhaka, where urban sprawl led to a 2.8 °C increase in LST during a similar period36,60. However, Rawalpindi’s annual LST increase of 0.12 °C suggests an accelerating trend, particularly over the past decade, driven by unregulated urban growth.

The UHI has increased attention to urban climate and environment research due to rapid urbanization61. Researchers focused on UHI’s magnitude, structure, and mechanism and observed the changes quantitatively with analysis of heat islands and temperature in the environment and climate-related studies17,62. UHI has worse effects on heat stress and controlling ecological conditions during heat waves. So, it is essential to investigate the relationship of UHI with heat waves and other environmental variables63. Similarly, in this study, thermal bands of Landsat were used to retrieve the LST by using the SMW algorithm. The results showed the impacts of urbanization on UHI. The time-series LST maps (Fig. 7) depict the LST variations in the city with time. The transition of multiple features revealed that the increase in surface temperature was the increase in the settlements and impervious surfaces, i.e., built-up class. It has been observed that the main factor for the UHI in different places in the city was the rapid urbanization and decrease of green areas with the replacement of impervious surfaces, mostly built-up areas, parking lots, and road extensions.

The mean LST of the city increased at the rate of 1.2 °C per decade with a total increase of 22.3% built up, 0.3% vegetation, 0.23% water, and a decrease of − 22.6% barren area. The vegetation decreased by 7% between 1990 and 1999, followed by an increase of about 1.5% between 1999 and 2010 and 6.4% in 2020. Multiple new societies are being developed in the city’s surroundings that cut down the major portion of the vegetative areas, increasing the LST. A time-series analysis of LST with thermal bands of Landsat using the SMW algorithm for calculating the UHIs in the city demonstrates the role of vegetation and water in decreasing the surface temperature and increasing the built-up areas in the city. The maximum temperature was calculated for the built-up area with a range of 35–40 °C, and the minimum for the vegetation and water was 26–30 °C. Rawalpindi city has a large area of built-up and less green cover, negatively impacting the city’s environment and increasing LST. As the study area has not been developed, Rawalpindi has not followed any planned development. The continuous construction of the commercial area and rapidly growing urban sprawl in random directions have made the city more prone to rising temperatures. The leading cause of the city expansion was the people’s migration from the surrounding areas towards the city for multiple socio-economic activities. Due to this, the population has increased with the increase in urbanization.

From the above results in Fig. 5, the LST hotspot was observed in 1990 from the southeast towards the southwest in 2020, highlighting the areas affecting high-rise temperatures. The spatial shift of UHI suggests that the dynamic LST is primarily related to barren land expansion by cutting vegetation areas. As vegetation decreases, barren land reduces evapotranspiration and high surface heat retention. Similarly, the high-rise temperature shifted from the southwestern region in 2020, implying a desertification of land quality. The shift of the spatial pattern prominently to the southwestern region is due to the exhibition of the high LST zones. This highlights the urgent need for interventions addressing horizontal and vertical urban expansions contributing to surface heat intensification. Recent advancements in remote sensing, particularly the integration of Random Forest classification with CA-ANN modeling, have significantly improved the accuracy of predicting LULC changes64,65.

These findings highlight the urgency of implementing targeted mitigation measures to address rising temperatures and their impacts on urban livability. Strategies should prioritize integrating green infrastructure, such as rooftop gardens and vertical greening systems, to offset the loss of vegetative cover in densely built-up areas. Converting underutilized barren lands into green spaces through tree-planting campaigns can reduce surface heating66. For example, urban forestry initiatives in Singapore have reduced UHI effects by lowering LST by up to 2 °C in some areas67. Implementing similar measures in Rawalpindi and promoting compact urban designs that limit horizontal sprawl would enhance the city’s resilience to rising temperatures. These strategies mitigate heat and improve air quality and urban aesthetics. Our study underscores the applicability of these measures in Rawalpindi, coupled with compact urban planning, to reduce the rise of LST. RDA should seek these techniques and monitor the city development through satellite image processing, which will help identify illegal construction and UHI areas. Regular monitoring of these issues will help develop mitigation strategies.

Traditionally, UHI was measured at different meteorological ground stations. Studies focused on modern remote sensing techniques, and the results provide consistent and straightforward methods to analyze the environment using the various bands of satellites. Compared to traditional measurements, the RS and GIS methods continuously provide the historical time series and spatially large area coverage68. The Rawalpindi Development Authority (RDA) should adopt these mitigation techniques and integrate satellite-based monitoring into urban planning. Using satellite image processing to track urban growth13, the RDA can identify illegal construction and pinpoint areas most affected by UHI. Regular monitoring of these issues will help develop mitigation strategies. The government should increase the green areas in densely populated areas and along the roads. As the city has grown, and there are fewer spaces to develop green areas, an awareness campaign about the green roof concepts should be launched. Regularly monitoring the urban area through optical remote sensing data would help detect changes and areas having LST issues. The master plans of new development should be strictly monitored and implemented while ensuring the presence of approved pervious to impervious surface ratios and green areas, with the conversion of barren land to vegetation.

Moreover, citizens need to be encouraged to go to a plantation. The green roof concept should initiate the core urban area with no barren land to plant greenery. Moreover, an intelligent approach toward vertical buildings instead of horizontal growth should be preferred. Also, there is a need to maintain existing green areas in the city to mitigate such an issue in the future. While these strategies address immediate mitigation needs, the methodological advancements in this study also provide a scalable framework for future UHI research in rapidly urbanizing regions. This study contributes to the growing body of research on UHI dynamics by delivering actionable insights into urban planning in developing cities, particularly relevant to areas with limited data availability. Integrating RF classification with CA-ANN modeling offers a novel approach to spatio-temporal UHI analysis. This methodology can be adapted for other rapidly urbanizing cities, particularly in data-scarce regions, providing a scalable and efficient framework for future research. By leveraging GEE, this study also demonstrates the potential of cloud-based platforms for rapid, large-scale urban climate assessments. Unlike developed cities, where planned urbanization and green infrastructure mitigate thermal stress, Rawalpindi exemplifies the challenges of unplanned sprawl. The methodological integration of RF classification with CA-ANN modeling provides a scalable framework for other data-scarce regions facing similar challenges. By aligning these findings with global efforts, such as the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), this research underscores the importance of localized strategies to address urban heat in developing cities.

Limitations and future aspects

While this study offers valuable insights into UHI dynamics in Rawalpindi, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the reliance on satellite-derived data, though robust, lacks the granularity of ground-based measurements. Integrating ground-based data, such as temperature sensors in high-density urban areas, could enhance the precision of LST analysis and provide more localized insights. Second, the study did not incorporate socio-economic and demographic data, which are critical for understanding the drivers of urbanization and their impacts on local climates. Future research should integrate these datasets to improve predictive modeling and provide a more holistic understanding of UHI dynamics. Third, the spatial resolution of Landsat data, while suitable for large-scale analysis, may not capture finer details in highly urbanized or mixed-use areas. Incorporating higher-resolution sensors, such as Sentinel-2 or drones, could complement Landsat data and provide more detailed spatial insights.

Additionally, this study focused primarily on static temporal snapshots rather than real-time monitoring of LST. Future research should prioritize the development of real-time monitoring systems that integrate high-resolution remote sensing with socio-environmental datasets for continuous analysis and adaptive management. Lastly, the proposed mitigation strategies, including green infrastructure and rooftop gardens, were not evaluated for their long-term effectiveness in reducing UHI impacts. Future studies should assess the feasibility and performance of these interventions to provide actionable, evidence-based policy recommendations.

Conclusions

This study presents a detailed analysis of the spatiotemporal dynamics of LULC and LST in Rawalpindi over three decades (1990–2020), revealing a 22.3% increase in built-up areas and a corresponding 3.04 °C rise in LST. The strong correlation between LST and impervious surfaces underscores the role of urban expansion as a primary driver of UHI effects. Projections for 2030 suggest a further 13.8% increase in urban areas, alongside minimal vegetation growth (+ 3%), indicating that Rawalpindi’s thermal stress will intensify without immediate intervention. The findings emphasize the need for sustainable urban planning to mitigate the adverse impacts of urbanization on local climates. Key recommendations include integrating green roofs and vertical greening systems to offset vegetation loss, converting barren lands into green spaces through tree plantations, and prioritizing compact urban designs to limit horizontal sprawl. Remote sensing and geospatial technologies should be leveraged to monitor urban growth and environmental changes regularly. This research provides actionable insights for policymakers and urban planners, demonstrating the critical role of green infrastructure and adaptive management in enhancing urban resilience. This study offers a scalable framework for assessing UHI dynamics in rapidly urbanizing cities by integrating advanced remote sensing techniques with predictive modeling. Applying these strategies can help cities like Rawalpindi balance urban growth with climate resilience, ensuring sustainable development and improved livability for future generations.
