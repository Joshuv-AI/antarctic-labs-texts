Structure-guided reprogramming of DELLA turnover for a sustainable Green Revolution in rice
Authors: Chongyu Xue, Shuang Zhang, Lihao Wan, Zhihui Zhang, Chenchen Zhang, Huangqi Chen, Kai Peng, Yujie Wang, Jie Hu, Xiuhua Gao, Jiamu Du, Xiangdong Fu, Shutong Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Green Revolution of the 1960s significantly boosted cereal yields but incurred substantial environmental costs due to overreliance on chemical fertilizer inputs; thus, future agricultural sustainability demands improved nitrogen-use efficiency (NUE). The semi-dwarf Green Revolution varieties (GRVs) characterized by elevated DELLA protein accumulation introduce a fundamental trade-off, increasing lodging resistance and harvest index at the expense of diminished biomass and grain productivity per plant, and require high nitrogen fertilizer inputs to achieve maximum yield potential under high planting density. Here we show that gibberellin (GA)-bound GA-INSENSITIVE DWARF1 (GID1) induces multi-level conformational changes in DELLA protein, affecting its N-terminal DELLA and VHYN/DPT/S and C-terminal VVLV and SAW motifs, thereby facilitating recognition by the SKP1-CULLIN-F-box (SCF) ubiquitin ligase complex and subsequent proteasomal degradation. We also performed structure-guided engineering of the rice SLENDER RICE1 (SLR1)-GID1-GID2 complex and created a series of dwarf alleles exhibiting a continuous spectrum of plant heights in elite cultivars. Notably, the dominant alleles slr1Y94A and slr1Y580A achieved superior yield and enhanced NUE over conventional sd1-containing GRVs. Reprogramming DELLA turnover thus enables to break the long-standing trade-off between high yield and fertilizer dependency, offering a strategy toward a more sustainable and productive Green Revolution.

Introduction

The agricultural Green Revolution of the 1960s dramatically boosted global crop yields, primarily through the widespread adoption of semi-dwarf cereal varieties, which depended on input-intensive practices, including high inorganic nitrogen (N) fertilizer inputs, high-density planting, and high pesticide applications1–3. The semi-dwarf Green Revolution varieties (GRVs) contributed to increased grain productivity through an improved harvest index (grain-to-straw ratio) and enhanced resistance to lodging (breakage of the crop plants by wind and rain). Subsequent genetic studies identified key dwarfing alleles: gain-of-function mutations in wheat Reduced height-B1b (Rht-B1b) and Rht-D1b loci that likely resist gibberellin (GA)-mediated degradation of DELLA proteins (DELLAs)4, and loss-of-function mutation in the rice semi-dwarf1 (sd1) locus that impairs bioactive GA biosynthesis5 and increases accumulation of the rice DELLA protein SLENDER RICE1 (SLR1)6. Since these discoveries, understanding how GA signaling and DELLAs orchestrate beneficial agronomic traits has remained a central question in plant biology.

Acting as central regulatory hubs, DELLAs integrate intrinsic developmental signals and external environmental cues to modulate phenotypic plasticity7,8. The GA-DELLA module governs key processes including meristem activity, hypocotyl and root elongation, leaf expansion, and developmental transitions such as germination and flowering. It also directs morphogenesis, nutrient uptake9,10, carbon fixation, carbon-nitrogen balance6, stress and defense responses11 and symbiotic interactions (e.g., rhizobial nodulation in Medicago truncatula and arbuscule formation in Lotus japonicus)12–14. A central feature of DELLAs is their regulated stability and activity, which enables plants to balance growth with stress adaptation, helping them cope with environmental challenges like nutrient starvation15, cold and salinity16,17. Consequently, perturbations in DELLA protein homeostasis exert multifaceted effects across interconnected physiological and developmental networks.

GA regulates plant growth primarily by triggering the degradation of DELLAs18, which belong to a specialized GRAS transcription regulator subfamily, characterized by a unique N-terminal region (containing DELLA and VHYN/DP motifs and a poly-serine (S)/threonine (T) region) and a conserved C-terminal GRAS domain (containing LHRI, VHIID, LHRII, PFYRE, and SAW motifs)19,20. GA signaling is initiated when bioactive GA binds to its receptor GIBBERELLIN INSENSITIVE DWARF1 (GID1), inducing conformational changes that create a binding interface for DELLAs21,22. This GA-dependent GID1-DELLAs interaction facilitates the recruitment of DELLAs into SKP1-CULLIN-F-box (SCF) E3 ubiquitin ligase complex via F-box proteins (GID2 in rice and SLEEPY1 (SLY1) in Arabidopsis thaliana), leading to DELLAs polyubiquitylation and subsequent 26S proteasomal degradation23–26. The proteolysis of DELLA proteins acts as a core molecular switch for GA-responsive growth. In GRVs, this pathway is compromised, leading to DELLA accumulation and the desired semi-dwarf phenotype6,27. Although cryo-electron microscopy (EM) structures of the Arabidopsis thaliana DELLA protein RGA in complex with GID1A and GID1A-SLY1-ASK1/2 have recently been reported28,29, the precise mechanism by which the GA-dependent interaction between GID1 and DELLA promotes DELLA recognition by F-box proteins remains incompletely resolved.

Although DELLAs accumulation confers beneficial semi-dwarfism in high-yield GRVs, it also introduces several agronomic and genetic constraints, including reduced biomass and grain yield per plant30,31, suppressed N-responsive growth, and compromised nitrogen-use efficiency (NUE)6,9. While this input-dependent strategy has supported global food security, it has come at a significant environmental cost32. Furthermore, the widespread reliance on the recessive sd1 allele in rice GRVs has narrowed genetic diversity, exacerbating genetic bottlenecks33. The recessive nature of sd1 also complicates hybrid breeding programs: the semi-dwarf trait is masked in heterozygotes, requiring homozygosity for phenotypic selection and that also increases the risk of propagating linked deleterious alleles. Addressing the dual challenges, high N reliance and breeding inefficiency, requires novel dominant dwarfing resources. While conventional mutagenesis methods (e.g., EMS) can generate dominant DELLA mutants, these random mutations frequently result in unfavorable dwarfism that severely compromises yield26,34–37. Therefore, integrating structural insights into GA-mediated DELLA degradation with precision genome editing presents a promising alternative approach, which enables rational modulation of DELLA homeostasis and opens a new breeding strategy to develop next-generation GRVs with synergistically improved NUE and grain yield.

We here report the cryo-EM structure of M. truncatula GA3-GID1b-DELLA1 complex, along with the cryo-EM structure of the corresponding ternary complex bound to Arabidopsis SLY1-ASK1 and the crystal structure of the M. truncatula DELLA1 (MtDELLA1) GRAS domain. Mutagenesis and biochemical analyses demonstrate that GA-bound GID1 induces extensive conformational changes in DELLA, structurally rearranging it to enhance binding to GID2/SLY1, which in turn facilitates recruitment into the SCF E3 ubiquitin ligase complex and accelerates proteasomal degradation. Leveraging these conserved structural interfaces within the GA-GID1-DELLA-GID2/SLY1 complex, we rationally designed and generated a comprehensive set of rice SLR1, GID1, and GID2 variants, substantially expanding the genetic toolkit for structure-guided dwarf breeding. Notably, two dominant semi-dwarf lines (slr1Y94A and slr1Y580A) exhibited superior yield and higher NUE compared to the conventional GRVs carrying sd1 allele, highlighting their potential to drive a more productive and sustainable Green Revolution.

Results

Conformational changes in the VHYN/DPT/S motif together with evolved VVLV motif and a key aspartate residue enable multi-site interactions between GA-bound GID1 and the DELLA C-terminal region

Previous studies have suggested that the C-terminal GRAS domain of DELLAs (DELLAGRAS), in addition to the N-terminal region (DELLANTR), interacts with GA-bound GID126. To elucidate the specific role of DELLAGRAS in the assembly of the GA-GID1-DELLAs complex, we determined the cryo-EM structure of the GA3-MtGID1b bound to an MtDELLA1 variant carrying K175S/R176S/C386S (hereafter referred to as MtDELLA13M) at 3.11 Å resolution (Fig. 1a, b, Supplementary Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1). Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) and structural analysis collectively revealed that MtDELLA13M is functional as the wild type (Fig. 1a, b, Supplementary Fig. 1a, and Supplementary Fig. 3a), hence the cryo-EM structure of the engineered GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA13M complex could represent the wild-type conformation (see Supplementary Note 1 for more details). The cryo-EM structure revealed a 1:1:1 stoichiometric assembly of GA3, MtGID1b and MtDELLA13M. At Interface I, an intricate interaction network was coordinated by the SAW motif of MtDELLA1GRAS (S542, F545, and E565) in conjunction with MtGID1bN-Ex (F48 and R51) and the VHYDP motif of MtDELLA1NTR (H107, Y108, and P110) (Fig. 1b). Specifically, E565 on the β8 strand of SAW motif formed a salt bridge with R51 of MtGID1bN-Ex, which also hydrogen-bonds with T111 adjacent to the VHYDP motif (Fig. 1b). T111 in MtDELLA1 is highly conserved among land plant DELLAs, being either T or S, which align with the canonical VHYN/DP consensus (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 4). Accordingly, we redefine this motif as VHYN/DPT/S. In addition, S542 of MtDELLA1 SAW motif formed a backbone hydrogen bond with H107 of VHYDPT motif, while Y108 of VHYDPT motif occupied the N-terminal apex of the α12 helix of SAW motif, acting as a structural gatekeeper that locked the helix in position (Fig. 1b). At Interface II, residues V203-V206 of MtDELLA1 form a β-strand immediately preceding the GRAS domain. This β-strand engaged a hydrophobic surface on MtGID1bα/β core and formed a parallel β-sheet with a complementary β-strand within MtGID1bα/β core (Fig. 1d). We have therefore termed this region the VVLV motif. In addition, MtDELLA1 D207 formed a salt bridge with R71 of MtGID1bα/β core. This same R71 residue further interacted with D468 and E471, both of which were located within the PFYRE motif of MtDELLA1GRAS (Fig. 1d).

The overall structure of GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA13M complex closely resembles to that of Arabidopsis thaliana GA3-GID1A-RGA complex28,29 with a root mean square deviation (RMSD) of about 1.6 Å for 708 aligned Cα positions (Supplementary Fig. 5). Notably, in both full-length complexes, the VHYN/DPT/S motif exhibited marked conformational divergences from the GAINTR in previously reported GA3/GA4-GID1A-GAINTR complexes. The entire VHYN/DPT/S motifs in full-length complexes adopted a loop conformation, whereas the equivalent region in GAINTR, including residues V and H of its VHYN/DPT/S motif and several N-proximal residues, formed a distorted α-helix (αC). This results in an ∼180° flip of the H and Y side chains in full-length complexes relative to those of GAINTR, a critical rearrangement that prevented steric clashes between the VHYN/DPT/S and SAW motifs in full-length complexes (Supplementary Fig. 3b). To further validate this observation, we used AlphaFold3 to generate a high-confidence structural model of the GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA1NTR complex. In this predicted structure, the VHYDPT motif of MtDELLA1 adopts a conformation identical to that of GAI in the published crystal structure of the GA3-GID1A-GAINTR complex, but exhibits a dramatically different conformation compared to that observed in our cryo-EM structure of the full-length GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA13M ternary complex (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). This comparison further supports that the VHYN/DPT/S motif undergoes a significant conformational change to enable GID1N-Ex-DELLAGRAS binding.

Protein sequence alignment revealed that unlike most of the DELLAGRAS residues involved in GID1 binding (MtDELLA1 F545/RGA F540, MtDELLA1 E565/RGA E560, MtDELLA1 D468/RGA D478 and MtDELLA1 E471/RGA E481) are conserved in DELLA homologs across both non-vascular and vascular plants, the hydrophobic VVLV motif interacting with GID1α/β core is originated in gymnosperms and that is highly conserved across seed plants, indicating its fundamental role in regulating GA-GID1-mediated interaction affinity. Additionally, MtDELLA1 D207/RGA D213 is conserved only in angiosperms but is divergent in basal plant lineages, including bryophytes, lycophytes, and gymnosperms, suggesting that its salt bridge interaction with GID1 represents a later refinement in the evolutionary trajectory of GA signaling complexity (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 4). Hereafter, we refer to the DELLA C-terminal region encompassing the VVLV motif and the GRAS domain as DELLACTR.

The GID1-DELLACTR interaction stabilizes a specific DELLA conformation for GID2/SLY1 recruitment

To further dissect this, we purified truncated MtDELLA1NTR and MtDELLA1CTR fragments. In line with our structural observations, MtDELLA1CTR alone exhibited weak binding to GA3-MtGID1b, whereas this interaction was markedly enhanced upon addition of MtDELLA1NTR in GST pull-down assays (Fig. 2b). Notably, MtDELLA1NTR variants carrying the Y108A or T111L mutations at Interface I, which did not compromise their binding to MtGID1b, substantially reduced their ability to facilitate complex formation between MtDELLA1CTR and GA3-MtGID1b (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 6a). MtDELLA1CTR variants with mutations at Interface I (F545A, E565A/K) or Interface II (D207K, D468K, D468A/E471A, Δ192-208 (deleting VVLV motif and D207), and VVLV203GSGS) nearly abolished the MtDELLA1CTR-MtGID1b interaction. Similarly, MtGID1b variants carrying F48A or R51E mutation at Interface I retained the ability to bind MtDELLA1NTR but exhibited substantially impaired interaction with MtDELLA1CTR; likewise, the MtGID1b R71E mutation at Interface II also severely compromised binding to MtDELLA1CTR (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 6). Taken together, these results suggest that, rather than being essential for the formation of the full-length MtDELLA1-MtGID1b complex, GID1-DELLACTR interactions may instead function to stabilize the complex in a specific conformation.

To test this possibility, we performed molecular dynamics simulations comparing the wild-type GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA1 complex with the GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA1VVLV203GSGS mutant. Whereas the wild-type complex remained stable throughout 100 ns simulation, the mutant exhibited pronounced dynamics. Specifically, the GA3-MtGID1b module and MtDELLA1NTR rotated approximately 25° relative to MtDELLA1GRAS by 50 ns, with the angle increasing to about 35° by 100 ns (Fig. 2d). These results demonstrate that GID1-DELLACTR interactions are indispensable for maintaining the stable global conformation of the complex.

Given that a primary role of GA-GID1 binding to DELLA is to facilitate the recruitment of the F-box protein GID2/SLY1 for DELLA degradation, we next investigated whether impairing GID1-DELLACTR interactions would influence GID2/SLY1 recruitment. Mutations in MtDELLA1 or MtGID1b that disrupt MtGID1b-MtDELLA1CTR binding strongly impaired the interaction between MtDELLA1 and SLY1 in the presence of MtGID1b (Fig. 2e, f). Collectively, these results demonstrate that, although the MtDELLA1CTR-MtGID1b interaction is not required for initial complex formation, it is essential for maintaining the precise conformation necessary for efficient recruitment of the F-box protein.

GA-GID1-induced multi-level conformational rearrangement of DELLAs facilitates recruitment of GID2/SLY1

Although extensive efforts to obtain MtGID2 were unsuccessful, we purified the Arabidopsis SLY1-ASK1 complex from insect cells. Based on previous evidence that the E138K mutant of SLY1 (SLY1E138K) exhibited enhanced binding affinity for DELLAs38, we reconstituted a hybrid GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA1-SLY1E138K-ASK1 complex and determined its cryo-EM structure at 3.24 Å resolution (Fig. 3a, b, Supplementary Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 1). The binding interface between MtDELLA1GRAS and SLY1E138K was characterized by extensive hydrophobic interactions and multiple salt bridges (Fig. 3c). Similar to observations in the Arabidopsis GA3-GID1A-RGA/RGAK163Q/R164Q/K166Q-SLY1/SLY1E138K-ASK2/1 complex structures28,29, two hydrophobic residue-rich helices of SLY1E138K (involving residues L90, V93, L97, L127, L131, I134, Y137, M140) anchored into a hydrophobic groove on the VHIID motif of MtDELLA1GRAS, which was centered on residue F299. The engineered E138K mutation in SLY1 formed a salt bridge with E289 in MtDELLA1, providing a structural rationale for its enhancement of DELLA binding. Beyond these previously described features, our cryo-EM structure additionally revealed that SLY1 residues D123 and E124 formed salt bridges with K546 on the α12 helix of SAW motif in MtDELLA1GRAS. In line with these structural observations, GST pull-down assays demonstrated that the F299A and K546D mutations in MtDELLA1 significantly impaired the MtDELLA1-SLY1 interaction in the presence of GA3-MtGID1b (Fig. 3d). Furthermore, the SLY1 D123A/E124A mutant, which disrupts the stabilizing salt bridges, substantially attenuated the MtDELLA1-SLY1 interaction, whereas the D123K and E124R mutants, which introduce electrostatic repulsion with MtDELLA1 K546, completely abolished the interaction (Fig. 3e). Collectively, these data provide structural and functional validation of the critical hydrophobic core and specific salt bridges that govern MtDELLA1GRAS-SLY1 recognition within the GA-GID1-DELLA-GID2/SLY1 complex.

Consistent with earlier biochemical data and recently reported Arabidopsis GA3-GID1A-RGA/RGAK163Q/R164Q/K166Q-SLY1/SLY1E138K-ASK2/1 complex structures28,29,39, our cryo-EM structure revealed no direct contact between GA-GID1 and SLY1 (Fig. 3a, b), suggesting that GA-GID1 promotes GID2/SLY1 recruitment by inducing conformational changes in DELLAs. It was recently proposed that the disordered or transiently folded DELLANTR occludes the GID2/SLY1-binding site on DELLAGRAS, thereby weakening the DELLAGRAS-GID2/SLY1 interaction, and that GA-GID1 binding relieves this occlusion to expose the site and thus facilitate the DELLA-GID2/SLY1 binding28,29. If exposing this site is the sole consequence of GA-GID1 binding, then the DELLAGRAS fragment alone would be expected to fully recapitulate the GID2/SLY1-binding capacity of the intact GA-GID1-DELLA complex. However, while MtDELLA1CTR exhibited only a relatively weak interaction with SLY1 in the absence of MtDELLA1NTR and GA-GID1 in our GST pull-down assays, consistent with the previous size-exclusion chromatography data showing that SLY1 can be co-purified with RGAGRAS and ASK2 in the absence of RGANTR and GID1A28, the GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA1 complex bound SLY1 much more robustly than MtDELLA1CTR alone (Fig. 4a). Consistently, our yeast three-hybrid assays showed that full-length MtDELLA1 exhibited markedly stronger binding to MtGID2 in the presence of GA3-MtGID1b than did MtDELLA1GRAS alone (Fig. 4b). Crucially, this rearrangement positioned K546 on α12 helix, which is crucial for interaction with SLY1 (Fig. 3d), to form salt bridges with D123 and E124 residues of SLY1 (Fig. 3c). Corroborating these structural findings, the MtDELLA1CTR K546D mutant retained wild type-like SLY1 binding, whereas F299A severely impaired it, as with the full-length MtDELLA1 (Supplementary Fig. 8f). We further identified that the basic α7-α8 loop (containing residues R119 and K122) in SLY1 was positioned near to an acidic surface on MtDELLA1NTR (Fig. 4d), suggesting potential dynamic interactions. GST pull-down assays confirmed that R119E and K122D mutants of SLY1 exhibited reduced binding to MtDELLA1 (Fig. 4e). These results suggest that GA-GID1 binding induces multi-level conformational changes in DELLAs, encompassing N-terminal structural remodeling and C-terminal α12 helix repositioning, which collaboratively establish a high-affinity binding interface for GID2/SLY1 recruitment. Precise engineering of GID1-DELLA-GID2 interaction affinity

Leveraging the known dosage-dependent control of plant height by DELLAs40–42, we used our structural insights to rationally engineer GA-mediated GID1-DELLA-GID2 interaction affinity and systematically screen for optimized semi-dwarf traits. Sequence alignment revealed high conservation at key functional residues between rice GA signaling components (SLR1, GID1, GID2) and their Medicago and Arabidopsis orthologs (MtDELLA1, MtGID1b, SLY1), particularly at interaction interfaces critical for complex formation (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Figs. 4, 9, 10). In rice SLR1, residues Y94, D504, E507, Y580, E600, along with V229V230V231V232 motif, respectively correspond to MtDELLA1 Y108, D468, E471, F545, E565 and the VVLV motif, all of which are critical for GID1 binding. Furthermore, SLR1 F328 and K581 are functional analogs of MtDELLA1 F299 and K546, respectively, both essential for GID2/SLY1 recognition. In rice GID1, residues Y48, R51, and Q72 structurally correspond to MtGID1b F48, R51, and R71, which contributed to DELLACTR binding and complex stabilization. Similarly, in rice GID2, R171 and R174 are structural counterparts of SLY1 R119 and K122, putatively mediating DELLANTR-GID2/SLY1 interaction, while D175 and Q176 correspond to SLY1 D123 and E124, crucial for DELLAGRAS-GID2/SLY1 binding. We assessed DELLA accumulation and GA responses using sterile T0 seedlings of gid1Y48A and homozygous T2 seedlings for all other mutant lines, with non-transgenic Wuyungeng as the wild-type control. In SLR1-edited lines, phenotypic severity varied. slr1Y94A and slr1Y580A displayed mild effects, with moderately elevated SLR1 levels and partial GA insensitivity. Similar phenotypes were observed in slr1D504K and slr1E507A, which showed pronounced SLR1 accumulation and minimal GA responses. The most severe effects were seen in slr1VVVV229GSGS, slr1F328A, slr1K581D and slr1E600A, all of which exhibited markedly elevated SLR1 levels and near-complete GA insensitivity (Fig. 5b). In GID1-edited lines, gid1Y48A and gid1R51E mutant lines showed extreme SLR1 accumulation and complete GA insensitivity. In contrast, gid1Q72E displayed only slight SLR1 accumulation and GA insensitivity (Fig. 5c). All GID2-edited mutants also exhibited elevated SLR1 protein accumulation and were mostly GA insensitivity (Fig. 5d).

We next evaluated the impact of these SLR1, GID1, and GID2 mutations on plant height under field conditions using homozygous T2 plants (with the exception of the sterile gid1Y48A T0 line). Reductions of plant height correlated well with the degrees of SLR1 accumulation and GA insensitivity observed earlier. Specifically, slr1E507A mutant plants displayed mild semi-dwarfism ( ~ 10% height reduction), while slr1Y94A and slr1Y580A exhibited moderate dwarfism ( ~ 20% height reduction). More pronounced dwarfing was observed in slr1D504K and slr1E600A (~25% height reduction), whereas slr1VVVV229GSGS and slr1K581D conferred severe dwarfism ( ~ 35% height reduction) (Fig. 5e, f). Among the GID1-edited lines, the gid1Q72E mutation (slight SLR1 accumulation) resulted in minimal height reduction. In contrast, gid1R51E (moderate SLR1 accumulation) led to severe dwarfism (~40% height reduction) and reduced fertility, and gid1Y48A resulted in extreme dwarfism (~75% height reduction) and complete sterility, consistent with its near-complete GA insensitivity (Fig. 5g, h). The gid2 mutants produced the mildest phenotype, with only 5-15% height reduction (Fig. 5i, j). Notably, all slr1 mutants conferred gain-of-function dominant dwarfing phenotypes except for slr1F328A. Despite its extreme SLR1 accumulation, slr1F328A exhibited an unexpected increase in plant height (Fig. 5e, f). This phenotype resembles previously characterized loss-of-function DELLA mutants with GRAS domain substitutions26,44,45, suggesting that slr1F328A may be a loss-of-function allele, a possibility meriting future investigation. Taken together, structure-informed mutagenesis generated a continuous spectrum of rice plant heights, from mild semi-dwarfism to extreme dwarfism, wherein the extent of height reduction quantitatively correlated with SLR1 accumulation levels.

Creating semi-dwarf alleles enhances substantial Green Revolution yield

Although the mutant sd1 allele has been widely used to confer semi-dwarfism in rice breeding since the Green Revolution of the 1960s, its monoculture has led to genetic bottlenecks owing to a lack of allelic diversity. Compounding this issue, sd1-containing GRVs exhibit poor NUE and substantial yield reduction per plant. To overcome these constraints, we applied rational design of the GA-GID1-DELLA regulatory system, precisely modulating their interaction affinities to develop novel semi-dwarf lines with improved agronomic performance.

Among the engineered lines, slr1Y94A and slr1Y580A variants showed ideal semi-dwarf characteristics. Co-IP assays confirmed that while the full-length SLR1 Y94A and Y580A variants retained interaction with GID1 comparable to the wild type, their association with GID2 was attenuated in rice protoplasts (Supplementary Fig. 11d, e). Field trials using homozygous plants revealed that while the sd1 mutant plant, which was edited to mimic a strong sd1 allele from indica rice (see methods for more details)46, reduced plant height by ~25% compared to WT, slr1Y94A and slr1Y580A mutants achieved a comparable ~20% reduction, consistent with their SLR1 accumulation levels (Fig. 6a, b and Supplementary Fig. 11f). Critically, biomass production was severely impaired in sd1 but only moderately reduced in both slr1 mutants relative to WT (Fig. 6c). This improved growth was linked to a more favorable N metabolism: unlike sd1, where uptake rates of NH4⁺ and NO3− were strongly suppressed, the slr1 mutants showed only a marginal reduction (Fig. 6d, e). Furthermore, the activity of key N assimilation enzymes, such as nitrate reductase (NR, for NO3- assimilation), was significantly higher in the slr1 mutants than in sd1, reaching levels comparable to WT (Fig. 6f). Consistent with these findings, in vitro MBP pull-down assays showed that the SLR1 Y94A and Y580A mutants both modestly weakened their interaction with GRF4 (Supplementary Fig. 11g), a key transcription factor that fine-tunes N utilization6. Under paddy cultivation conditions, we further evaluated agronomic performance of the new slr1 mutants alongside the sd1 control in the Wuyungeng genetic background. Unlike sd1 plants, which exhibited characteristic d6-type dwarfism with shortening concentrated in uppermost internodes, both slr1 mutants exhibited a dn-type dwarfism pattern, characterized by uniform reduction across all internodes47, suggesting a different mechanism driven by reduced cell division and elongation (Fig. 6g). Additionally, stem diameter was significantly increased in the slr1 mutants relative to the sd1 mutants, reaching levels similar to WT (Fig. 6h). The slr1 mutants also maintained a lodging index comparable to sd1, which was significantly lower than that of the WT plants (Fig. 6i). While tiller numbers in slr1Y94A and slr1Y580A mutants were comparable to sd1 and higher than in WT (Fig. 6j), grain numbers per panicle surpassed those of sd1, though it remained lower than in WT (Fig. 6k). Notably, both slr1 mutants maintained grain yield per plant equivalent to the WT controls, which was significantly higher than that of sd1 plants (Fig. 6l). On a per-plot basis, slr1Y94A and slr1Y580A respectively outyielded sd1 by 11.8% and 18.7%, while maintaining robust lodging resistance (Fig. 6m). Thus, these results establish slr1Y94A and slr1Y580A as promising new alleles that concurrently optimize semi-dwarf architecture, grain productivity, and NUE, thereby paving the new breeding strategy to enhance sustainable green revolution yield in rice.

Discussion

While modern GRVs achieve semi-dwarfism by disrupting GA-induced DELLA degradation, the underlying mechanistic details of GA-mediated DELLAs-F-box recruitment still remain unresolved. Through determination of crystal structure of MtDELLA1GRAS and cryo-EM structures of GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA13M and GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA1-SLY1E138K-ASK1 complexes complemented by mutagenesis and biochemical analyses, we demonstrated that GA-bound GID1 engages cooperatively with multiple motifs of DELLAs: the DELLA and VHYN/DPT/S motifs within the N-terminal region, the SAW motif within the GRAS domain, and a VVLV motif identified immediately upstream of the GRAS domain. Primary binding between GID1 and the N-terminal region initiates secondary interactions with the C-terminal motifs (VVLV and SAW), inducing coordinated conformational changes throughout both the N-terminal region and GRAS domain. These structural rearrangements collectively enhance the affinity for F-box proteins GID2/SLY1, thereby accelerating ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal degradation (Fig. 4f). Recently, cryo-EM structures of GA3-GID1A-RGA, GA3-GID1A-RGA-SLY1-ASK2 and GA3-GID1A-RGAK163Q/R164Q/K166Q-SLY1E138K-ASK1 protein complexes were reported in Arabidopsis28,29, which exhibit overall architectural conservation with our Medicago complexes. While the parallel study suggested the DELLAGRAS alone may be sufficient for SLY1 binding, our integrated biochemical, structural, mutagenesis, and molecular dynamics analyses demonstrated that GA-GID1 binding orchestrates essential allosteric changes through the repositioning of α12 helix in the DELLAGRAS SAW motif and the realignment of DELLANTR, thereby enabling efficient recruitment of SCFGID2/SLY1.

Phylogenetic analysis indicates DELLAs originated monophyletically from a bacterial GRAS progenitor in the last common ancestor of land plants, followed by functional diversification: while early-diverging SLY1 orthologs display broad binding promiscuity toward DELLAs, derived GID2/SLY1 clades exhibit stringent binding specificity and affinity for GA-GID1-DELLA complex48,49. Crucially, functional GID1 GA receptors emerged exclusively in vascular plants (bryophyte GID1-like proteins lack receptor activity)50–52. Here, we identified that a hydrophobic VVLV motif and an adjacent aspartate residue that are essential for GID1-mediated recruitment of DELLA to GID2/SLY1. Protein sequence alignment further reveals that the VVLV motif in DELLAs can be traced to gymnosperms and was maintained under strong purifying selection in seed plants. In contrast, the adjacent aspartate residue is highly conserved in angiosperms yet exhibits substantial divergence in earlier lineages. These findings thus provide mechanistic insights into the progressive affinity maturation of GID2/SLY1 for specific structural states of DELLAs during plant evolution.

While semi-dwarf GRVs have enhanced food security, their cultivation has come at the costs of high environmental damage from inorganic N fertilizer inputs and reduced genetic diversity due to the pervasive sd1 allele. These issues necessitate the development of new dwarfing resources32,33. To tackle this challenge, we employed a structure-driven rational design of GA signaling components (SLR1, GID1, and GID2) combined with CRISPR-based prime editing and created a series of rice lines exhibiting a spectrum from mild to extreme dwarfism in the high-yielding, commercially utilized japonica cultivar Wuyungeng. Among these, two engineered slr1 variants (slr1Y94A and slr1Y580A) exhibited a distinct agronomic profile: their moderate height reduction was less severe than sd1, yet strikingly combined higher yield with enhanced NUE. In contrast to sd1, which distorts plant architecture at a cost to C and N assimilation, the newly designed slr1Y94A and slr1Y580A variants maintain WT-like C fixation and N metabolism. This preservation of growth-metabolic coordination confers multiple agronomic advantages: it connects plant metabolism and development, optimizes resource metabolism and allocation and ensures consistent yield, thus establishing an ideotype that breaks the dwarfism-yield trade-off. Moreover, whereas sd1 is recessive, the dominant nature of these slr1 alleles offers significant advantages for super-hybrid rice breeding. A logical and highly valuable next step is to validate whether these alleles can also confer superior yield and NUE in other commercially utilized elite varieties, especially indica varieties and F1 hybrid rice. Such validation would reveal how broadly these alleles can be applied in rice breeding. Given the high conservation of DELLAs across cereal crops, the equivalent mutant alleles to slr1Y94A and slr1Y580A hold immediate translational potential in maize and other high-yield cereal crops53. They represent a potent strategy for breeding high-yielding cultivars with reduced dependency on N fertilizers. Collectively, our work provides genetic resources to enhance breeding diversity and improve sustainable productivity in cereal crops.

Our biochemical data demonstrated that both the SLR1 Y94A and Y580A mutants modestly weakened their interaction with GRF4, a key transcription factor that fine-tunes carbon fixation and nitrogen assimilation6, thereby suggesting a potential mechanistic basis for their improved NUE and grain yield. As central hubs that integrate plant growth with environmental adaptation, DELLA proteins interact with multiple nitrogen-responsive regulators to coordinate development and metabolism54. Therefore, alternative or complementary mechanisms beyond GRF4 may also contribute to the improved NUE of these designed slr1 variants. Previous work has revealed that the GID1-DELLA-NITROGEN-MEDIATED TILLER GROWTH RESPONSE 5 (NGR5) module responds to nitrogen availability and regulates tiller outgrowth9, whereas DELLA interactions with REDUCED CULM NUMBER 2 (RCN2) and SQUAMOSA PROMOTER BINDING PROTEIN-LIKE 14 (SPL14) are closely associated with panicle development and the nitrogen-responsive regulation of grain yield55. Thus, beyond reduced GRF4 binding, it is possible that these slr1 variants also enhance NUE by modulating interactions between SLR1 and these additional regulators.

Our study further provides a structural basis of previously identified semi-dwarf SLR1 alleles from traditional genetic screens. A case in point is the rice Slr1-d6 allele: when heterozygous in the high-yielding 9311 background, it produces plants with height equivalent to homozygous sd1 controls, yet it increases grain yield under equivalent N fertilization37. The mutant Slr1-d6 allele encodes an S97L substitution in SLR1, which corresponds to T111 in MtDELLA1. Our integrated structural and biochemical analysis demonstrated that T111 in MtDELLA1 forms a stabilizing hydrogen bond with R51 in MtGID1b, which is essential for reinforcing the MtDELLA1CTR-MtGID1b interaction, thereby enabling the subsequent recruitment of GID2/SLY1. Since a leucine substitution at MtDELLA1 T111 (equivalent to SLR1 S97) disrupts the key hydrogen bond with MtGID1b R51, it destabilizes the DELLACTR-GID1 interaction required for efficient SCFGID2/SLY1 recruitment, thus explaining the semi-dwarfing phenotype of the Slr1-d6 allele. A parallel mechanism is observed in the maize dwarfing allele d9-1, which carries an E600K mutation56. E600 in maize D9 is analogous to MtDELLA1 E565, and its substitution with lysine disrupts a salt bridge with MtGID1b R51, likewise impairing DELLA1CTR-GID1 binding and attenuating SCF recruitment. Similarly, the wheat semi-dwarf alleles Rht-B1b and Rht-D1b are semidominant, which encode N-terminally truncated DELLAs lacking the DELLA motif (equivalent to MtDELLA1Δ1-87, removing the DELLA motif by deleting residues M1-E87 in MtDELLA1) and that possess altered, rather than lost, function in response to GA4,27. Different from GAI variants lacking the DELLA motif are completely GA-insensitive in Arabidopsis57, the Rht-B1b mutant, which lacks the DELLA motif but retains the VHYN/DPT/S motif, exhibited reduced protein abundance upon GA treatment in wheat27, indicating retained GA responsiveness. Here, our findings, together with a prior structure of Arabidopsis GA-GID1-GAINTR ternary complex21 demonstrate that GA-induced GID1 conformational changes enable the interactions with both N-terminal DELLA and VHYN/DPT/S motifs and C-terminal GRAS domain of DELLAs. Consequently, the absence of the DELLA motif in Rht-B1b and Rht-D1b mutants attenuates but not abolishes their binding to GA-bound GID1 (Supplementary Fig. 12), which in turn leads to less efficient recruitment of the SCFGID2/SLY1 ubiquitin ligase complex, thus explaining why these Rht-1 mutant variants exhibit relative, rather than absolute, GA insensitivity compared to the WT RHT-1 protein27,32.

Methods

Recombinant protein expression and purification

MtDELLA1 (medtr.R108.gnmHiC_1.ann1.MtrunR108HiC_012827) and MtGID1b (medtr.HM185.gnm1.ann1.g14903.t1) were cloned from Medicago truncatula R108. SLY1 (AT4G24210) and ASK1 (AT1G75950) were cloned from Arabidopsis thaliana Col-0. Rice SLR1 (LOC_Os03g49990), GID1 (LOC_Os05g33730), GID2 (LOC_Os02g36974) and SKP1 (LOC_Os11g26910.1) were cloned from Wuyungeng cultivar. The coding sequences (CDS) for full-length MtDELLA1, MtDELLA1Δ1-87 (residues M88-K593), MtDELLA1CTR (residues S192-K593), and MtDELLA1GRAS (residues Q209-K593) were individually inserted into the pGEX-6p-1 vector with a GST tag at the N terminus. The CDS of MtDELLA1NTR (residues M1-M191) was inserted into the pET28SUMO vector with a 6×His-SUMO tag at the N terminus and a 2×Strep tag at the C terminus. The CDS of full-length MtGID1b was similarly inserted into the pET28SUMO vector, generating constructs with either an N-terminal 6×His-SUMO tag or both an N-terminal 6×His-SUMO and a C-terminal 2×Strep tags. The CDS of rice SLR1NTR (residues M1-P214) was inserted into the pET28SUMO vector with a 6×His-SUMO tag at the N terminus. For baculoviral expression, the CDS of Arabidopsis SLY1 and ASK1 were co-cloned into the pFastBac Dual vector, with SLY1 containing a C-terminal 10×His tag. The CDS of rice SLR1 was inserted into the pFastBac Dual vector with a C-terminal 10×His tag. The CDS of rice GID1 was inserted into the pFastBac Dual vector with C-terminal 2×Strep and 10×His tags. The CDS of rice GID2 and SKP1 were co-cloned into the pFastBac Dual vector, with GID2 containing a C-terminal 10×His tag. All point mutants and chimeric constructs were generated using the QuickChange site-directed mutagenesis system or Gibson Assembly. All constructs were verified by DNA sequencing. Primers are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Constructs encoding GST-MtDELLA1, GST-MtDELLA1Δ1-87, GST-MtDELLA1CTR, GST-MtDELLA1GRAS, 6×His-SUMO-MtDELLA1NTR-2×Strep, 6×His-SUMO-MtGID1b, 6×His-SUMO-MtGID1b-2×Strep, and 6×His-SUMO-SLR1NTR were individually transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells. Transformed cells were grown in LB medium at 37 °C until OD600 reached 0.6–0.8. Protein expression was induced with 0.3 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG) at 16 °C for 20-24 h. For purification of individual proteins, cells were harvested and lysed using a high-pressure cell disrupter (JNBIO) in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 10% (v/v) glycerol, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), and 5 mM benzamidine). The clarified lysate was applied to affinity resins corresponding to the fusion tag: glutathione resin (Cytiva) for GST-tagged proteins, Ni-NTA resin (Qiagen) for 6×His and 10×His-tagged proteins, and Strep-Tactin resin (IBA Lifesciences) for Strep-tagged proteins. For structural studies, the purified GST-MtDELLA1GRAS was treated with HRV3C protease overnight at 4 °C to remove the GST tag. The tag-free MtDELLA1GRAS protein was further purified by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) in SEC buffer A (20 mM Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 2 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)).

To assemble the 6×His-SUMO-MtGID1b-GST-MtDELLA1, 6×His-SUMO-MtGID1b-2×Strep-GST-MtDELLA1, and 6×His-SUMO-MtGID1b-6×His-SUMO-MtDELLA1NTR-2×Strep complexes, cell pellets expressing the respective proteins were combined and co-lysed in lysis buffer supplemented with 100 μM GA3. For pull-down assays, the complexes were first captured on Ni-NTA resin and subsequently purified using either glutathione or Strep-Tactin resin. For structural studies, the purified GA3-6×His-SUMO-MtGID1b-GST-MtDELLA1 complex was treated with Ulp1 and HRV3C proteases overnight at 4 °C to remove the 6×His-SUMO and GST tags. The tag-free complex was further purified by SEC on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) in SEC buffer B (20 mM Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM benzamidine, 2 mM DTT, and 100 μM GA3).

For expression of the SLY1-10×His-ASK1, SLR1-10×His, rice GID1-2×Strep-10×His, GID1-2×Strep-10×His-SLR1-10×His, and GID2-10×His-SKP1, 35 mL of baculovirus was used to infect 1 L of High Five cells (density ≈ 2.0 × 106 cells per mL, Thermo Fischer Scientific) cultured in High Five medium (Sino Biological) at 22 °C. Specifically, for the formation of rice GA3-GID1-2×Strep-10×His-SLR1-10×His complex in insect cells, 50 μM GA3 was added to the medium to facilitate assembly. Cells were harvested 72 h post-infection. For pull-down assays, these proteins were purified by Ni-NTA affinity chromatography using a buffer containing 20 mM Tris pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 5 mM benzamidine, 100 μM GA3, and 200 mM imidazole. To assemble the GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA1-SLY1-ASK1 complex for structural studies, purified GST-MtDELLA1 and 6×His-SUMO-MtGID1b were incubated with an excess of SLY1-10×His-ASK1 in the presence of 100 μM GA3 for 4 h. The assembled complex was captured on glutathione resin and eluted with buffer containing 20 mM Tris pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 5 mM benzamidine, 100 μM GA3, and 25 mM reduced glutathione. Following cleavage of the GST and 6×His-SUMO tags with HRV3C and Ulp1 proteases, the complex was purified by SEC using the SEC buffer C (20 mM Tris pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 5 mM benzamidine, 2 mM DTT, and 100 μM GA3).

All site-directed mutants were expressed and purified using the same protocols established for their wild-type counterparts.

Cryo-EM data collection

300-mesh Au R1.2/1.3 grids (Quantifoil) were glow-discharged for 45 s using a plasma cleaner (PELCO easiGlow, TED PELLA). A 4 μL aliquot of either 1.6 mg/mL GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA13M or 1.5 mg/mL GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA1-SLY1E138K-ASK1 sample was applied to the grid. Grids were blotted for 2 s and vitrified in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 4 °C and 100% humidity. Cryo-EM data were collected on Titan Krios transmission electron microscopes (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating at 300 keV at the SUSTech Cryo-EM Center. Automated data acquisition was performed using SerialEM at a nominal magnification of 130,000× in super-resolution mode, yielding a physical pixel size of 0.92 Å. Micrographs were dose-fractionated into 32 frames with a dose rate of ~20 e−/pixel/s, resulting in a total accumulated dose of approximately 50 e−/Å². Additional datasets for both samples were collected at a 30° stage tilt using the same acquisition parameters.

Cryo-EM data processing

For the GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA13M complex, a total of 2,416 untilted and 495 tilted movies were collected. All super-resolution movies were subjected to Patch motion correction with a binning factor of 2, followed by Patch CTF estimation in CryoSPARC58. An initial set of 159,000 particles from the untilted data was picked using a blob picker and subjected to 2D classification to generate templates for subsequent automated particle picking. After template-based picking of both untilted and tilted datasets, extracted particles were binned 4x and subjected to multiple rounds of 2D classification. Selected particles (1,523,402 from untilted and 373,290 from tilted data) were merged and used for ab initio reconstruction. Particles from well-defined classes (1,129,138) were binned 2x and subjected to 3D classification without alignment and heterogeneous refinement. A subset of 732,935 particles from optimal classes was then used unbinned for further 3D classification and heterogeneous refinement. Finally, 207,930 particles were selected for non-uniform and local refinement, yielding a map at 3.1 Å resolution. Subsequent reference-based motion correction, local refinement, 3D classification without alignment, and rebalancing of orientations produced a final map at 3.11 Å resolution from 55,910 particles. All resolutions were reported based on the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (FSC) threshold of 0.143. The final map was sharpened by EMready59 for model building.

For the GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA1-SLY1E138K-ASK1 complex, 863 untilted and 2,962 tilted movies were collected and processed identically to the GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA13M complex. After template picking, particles were binned 4x and subjected to 2D classification. Selected particles (608,102 particles from untilted and 1,213,627 particles from tilted data) were merged for ab initio reconstruction. Following 3D classification without alignment, 747,551 particles from good classes were binned 2x and used for further 3D classification and heterogeneous refinement. A subset of 529,276 particles was selected, unbinned, and subjected to homogeneous and non-uniform refinement. Reference-based motion correction and 3D classification without alignment were performed, and 392,747 particles were used for non-uniform refinement, yielding a 3.4 Å resolution map. Local refinements were then performed using a mask focused on the GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA1 moiety (mask 1) and on the SLY1E138K-ASK1 moiety (mask 2), yielding a 3.2 Å and a 4.21 Å resolution map, respectively. A final round of 3D classification and orientation rebalancing with mask 1 yielded the final map at 3.24 Å resolution from 121,258 particles (FSC = 0.143). Two focused maps were sharpened using EMready and combined as the composite map using Phenix60 for model building.

Model building and refinement of cryo-EM structures

For the GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA13M complex, an AlphaFold261-predicted model of the MtGID1b-MtDELLA1 complex was used as an initial template. The models of MtDELLA1 and MtGID1b were docked into the cryo-EM density map using UCSF ChimeraX (https://www.cgl.ucsf.edu/chimerax/) and then manually adjusted and rebuilt in COOT62. The model was refined through iterative cycles of real-space refinement in Phenix and manual adjustment in COOT.

For the GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA1-SLY1E138K-ASK1 complex, an AlphaFold2- predicted model of the full complex served as the initial template and the same building and refinement procedure was followed.

Statistics for the structural models are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Crystallization and structure determination

Crystallization trials were performed at 16 °C using the sitting drop vapor diffusion method by mixing 1 μL of MtDELLA1GRAS (containing a C386S mutation) solution (10 mg/mL) with 1 μL of reservoir solution (0.1 M HEPES pH 8.1, 0.05 M MgCl2, and 30% (v/v) PEG400). Crystals were flash-cooled in liquid nitrogen using a cryoprotectant made of the reservoir solution supplemented with 5% (v/v) glycerol. X-ray diffraction data were collected at beamlines BL02U1 and BL19U1 of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility63, and processed using HKL200064. The structure was solved by the molecular replacement method using Phenix and a structure of Arabidopsis SCARECROW (SCR) in the SHORT-ROOT (SHR)-JACKDAW/IDD10-bound form as the search model. Model building and structure refinement were performed in COOT and Phenix, respectively. Stereochemistry of the final mode was assessed using Molprobity65. The PISA server66 was used for structural analysis and all structural figures were prepared with ChimeraX. Data collection and structure refinement statistics are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

In vitro pull-down assays

All the pull-down assays were performed using a binding buffer containing 20 mM Tris pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 5 mM benzamidine, 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME), and 100 μM GA3. Eluates were analyzed by SDS-PAGE, followed by Coomassie brilliant blue staining, or analyzed by western blot using the following antibodies: anti-His (1:5000, cat# AE003, ABclonal), anti-GST (1:5000, cat# 66001-2-Ig, Proteintech), anti-Strep (1:3000, cat# D191106-0050, Sangon), and anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:2000, cat# AS003, ABclonal).

To assay the interaction between MtDELLA1CTR and MtGID1b in the presence of MtDELLA1NTR, 100 μg of GST-MtDELLA1CTR was incubated with an excess of the pre-formed complex of GA3-6×His-SUMO-MtGID1b and 6×His-SUMO-MtDELLA1NTR-2×Strep for 2 h at 4 °C. The mixture was then incubated with 30 μL of glutathione resin for 1 h at 4 °C. After extensive washing with binding buffer, bound proteins were eluted with binding buffer supplemented with 30 mM reduced glutathione.

To investigate the MtDELLA1NTR-MtGID1b interaction, 60 μg of 6×His-SUMO-MtDELLA1NTR-2×Strep was incubated with 120 μg of GA3-6×His-SUMO-MtGID1b. Similarly, to investigate the MtDELLA1-MtGID1b interaction, 60 μg of GA3-MtGID1b-2×Strep was incubated with 120 μg of GST-MtDELLA1. For the rice counterparts, 60 μg of GA3-GID1-2×Strep-10×His was incubated with either 120 μg of 6×His-SUMO-SLR1NTR or SLR1-10×His. All mixtures were mixed with Strep-Tactin resin, followed by elution with binding buffer supplemented with 50 mM biotin.

To test the MtDELLA1-SLY1 interaction, 60 μg of pre-formed complex of GA3-MtGID1b-2×Strep and GST-MtDELLA1 (or GST-MtDELLA1CTR alone) was incubated with an excess of SLY1-10×His-ASK1 complex and then subjected to pull-down using glutathione resin as described above. To test the rice SLR1-GID2 interaction, 60 μg of pre-formed complex of GA3-GID1-2×Strep-10×His and SLR1-10×His was incubated with an excess of GID2-10×His-SKP1 complex and then subjected to pull-down using Strep-Tactin resin as described above.

To evaluate the interaction between MtDELLA1Δ1-87 and SLY1 in the presence of MtGID1b, 60 μg of GST-MtDELLA1Δ1-87 was incubated with GA3-MtGID1b-2×Strep for 2 h at 4 °C. The mixture was then incubated with 30 μL of glutathione resin for 1 h at 4 °C. After extensive washing with binding buffer, an excess of SLY1-10×His-ASK1 complex was added. Bound proteins were eluted with binding buffer supplemented with 30 mM reduced glutathione. GST-MtDELLA1CTR and GST-MtDELLA1 were used as controls.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using a standard protocol67. Initial systems were prepared using the PDB Reader and Manipulator tools in CHARMM-GUI68,69. The initial model of the wild-type GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA1 complex was based on our cryo-EM structure. The mutant complex (GA3-MtGID1b-MtDELLA1VVLV203GSGS) was generated by in silico mutation of the VVLV motif to GSGS. The CHARMM36m70 force field was for proteins and carbohydrates, and the TIP3P model71 was used for water molecules, along with 150 mM NaCl. Input files for GROMACS were generated in CHARMM-GUI, and simulations were run using GROMACS 2022.5. The LINCS algorithm was used to constrain bonds. The Nosé-Hoover thermostat72,73 (tau-t = 1 ps) was applied to maintain the temperature at 310.15 K, and the Parrinello-Rahman barostat (tau-p = 2 ps, compressibility = 4.5 × 10−5 bar−1) was used to maintain pressure at 1 bar. Systems were equilibrated in two steps: first under an NVT ensemble for 125 ps with a 1 fs time step, followed by an NPT ensemble for 125 ps with a 1 fs time step. Production runs for both the wild-type and mutant models were performed for 100 ns with a 2 fs time step, and each simulation was repeated three times. Molecular graphics and production movies were rendered using ChimeraX.

Yeast three-hybrid (Y3H) assay

The yeast strain AH109 was transformed with pGADT7 (containing MtDELLA1 or MtDELLA1GRAS) and pBridge harboring MtGID2 and MtGID1b using the LiAc transformation method as described previously74. The expressed proteins were then examined for interaction by dropping 2.5 µL of yeast suspension on minimal synthetic dropout (SD) agar medium containing the dropout supplement (DO) Leu-, Trp-, Ade- His-, Met-, and 100 μM GA3. Yeast growth was monitored for up to 4 days.

Rice genome editing

To precisely edit the rice SLR1, GID1, and GID2 genes, a single pegRNA was designed for each target using PlantPegDesigner. The pegRNA designs included a 20-nt protospacer sequence, a prime binding site (PBS), and a reverse transcriptase template (RTT) optimized for a GC content of 40-60%. Spacer and RTT + PBS sequences were synthesized as primes (Supplementary Table 4). An expression cassette for each pegRNA was generated by PCR and cloned into the binary vector ePPE-plus via in-fusion assembly. The resulting plasmids were used to precisely edit SLR1, GID1 and GID2, respectively, based on the prime editing system. To generate a mutant line that mimics the mutation types of strong dominant alleles in indica rice, a spacer sequence (CTGTGGTGGCGTGGGGTGCT) from the first exon of SD1 was designed for knockout, amplified with primers, and cloned into the pYLCRISPR-Cas9 vector46. Plasmids were individually introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 by electroporation and used to transform rice plants as previously described75. Transgenic plants were regenerated under selection on 50 mg/L hygromycin and validated by PCR amplification of the Cas9 transgene.

To identify edits among transgenic plantlets, genomic DNA was extracted from leaf samples using a CTAB-based method. The targeted genomic regions were amplified by PCR and subjected to Sanger sequencing. Plantlets from positive wells were sampled and tested individually to confirm the edits.

Plant materials and growth conditions

Rice japonica Wuyungeng NILs carrying various DEP1 alleles were used as the wild type. For shoot length analysis and protein extraction, seedlings were grown under hydroponic culture conditions as described76. Briefly, seeds were surface-sterilized with 20% sodium hypochlorite for 30 min, rinsed thoroughly, and soaked in water for 3 days to germinate. Uniform seedlings were selected and transferred to pots containing nutrient solution (1.25 mM NH4NO3, 0.5 mM NaH2PO4·2H2O, 0.75 mM K2SO4, 1 mM CaCl2, 1.667 mM MgSO4·7H2O, 40 mM Fe-EDTA (Na), 19 μM H3BO3, 9.1 μM MnSO4·H2O, 0.15 μM ZnSO4·7H2O, 0.16 μM CuSO4, and 0.52 μM (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O, pH 5.5). Plants were maintained in a controlled illumination incubator. T0 and T2 generation plants were cultivated in standard paddy conditions with an interplant spacing of 20 cm at the Institute of Genetics and Developmental Biology experimental station sites located in Lingshui (Hainan Province). T1 and T3 generation plants were cultivated with an interplant spacing of 20 cm at the Hefei Institutes of Physical Science, Chinese Academy of Sciences, located in Hefei (Anhui Province).

Immunoblot analysis of SLR1 protein

To compare SLR1 protein abundance in wild-type and edited lines, 2-week-old seedlings were harvested and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. To assay GA-dependent degradation, 2-week-old seedlings were pre-treated with 1 μM paclobutrazol (PAC) for 12 h to inhibit GA biosynthesis, then treated with 1 μM GA3 for 1 h before harvesting and freezing. Frozen tissue was ground to a powder and homogenized in protein extraction buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% Triton X-100, 1 mM DTT, and 1x Roche cOmpleteTM Protease Inhibitor Cocktail) at a 1:2 (w/v) ratio. The homogenate was incubated on ice for 40 min (with vortexing every 8 min) and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The cleared supernatants were mixed with SDS loading sample buffer, denatured at 100 °C for 10 min, and analyzed by western blot using the following antibodies: anti-SLR1 antibody (1:5000, cat# A16279, ABclonal), anti-HSP82 antibody (1:5000, cat# AbM51099-31-PU, BPI), and anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:2000, cat# AS003, ABclonal).

Phenotypic analysis of plant height and yield-associated agronomic traits

All phenotypic recordings and measurements were conducted at maturity (heading stage). Plant height was measured as the distance from the soil surface to the tip of the tallest panicle. Data were collected from 15 plants per line. Representative plants were selected for imaging based on the average height of each genotype. The lengths of the first to fifth internodes (counted from the top) were measured. The breaking force of the fourth internode, including the leaf sheath, was determined using a plant lodging tester (digital force gauge) with a three-point bending method. The distance between the fulcra was set to 4 cm. The center of the internode was aligned horizontally with the midpoint between the two fulcra prior to measurement. After determining the breaking force, the stem was cut at the fracture point of the fourth internode. The breaking strength (M) and lodging index (LI) were calculated using the following formulas: breaking strength (M) = breaking load (kg) × L/4 (cm) × 1000, where L is the distance between the two support pillars (cm), and lodging index (LI) = SL (cm) × FW (g)/breaking strength × 100, where SL is the stem length from the fracture point to the panicle tip and FW is the fresh weight of the segment from the fracture point to the panicle tip. The harvest index was calculated as the ratio of grain yield per plant to total biomass per plant.

Determination of nitrate reductase activity

The activity of nitrate reductase (NR) was measured using a commercial assay kit (Nitrate Reductase Kit, cat# C0080, Solarbio) strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions.

15N uptake analysis

Four-week-old rice seedlings grown hydroponically were used for 15N influx measurements. The 15N-labeled nutrient solution was prepared by replacing the 1.25 mM NH4NO3 in the standard nutrient solution with 1.25 mM (15NH4)2SO4 (cat# A110168, Aladdin) or 2.5 mM K15NO3 (atom% 15N: 99%; Aladdin, P117721), which was used as 15N tracers. Seedlings were removed from culture, and their roots were immersed in 0.1 mM CaSO4 solution for 1 min to displace apoplastic ions. After draining, roots were transferred into the 15N-labeled nutrient solution for a 5 min uptake period. Roots were then removed from the labeling solution, drained, and rinsed again in fresh 0.1 mM CaSO4 for 1 min. Roots were carefully blotted dry with gauze, placed in kraft paper bags, and oven-dried at 65 °C for approximately 3 days. The dried root samples were ground to a fine powder, and the 15N content was measured using an Isoprime 100 isotope ratio mass spectrometer (Elementar, Germany).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and rice SLR1-GRF4 pull-down assays

For Co-IP experiments, full-length SLR1, GID1, and GID2 cDNAs were amplified, then inserted into either the pUC-35S-HA-RBS, pUC-35S-GFP-RBS or the pUC-35S-Flag-RBS vector. Rice protoplasts were transfected with 100 μg of plasmid DNA and then incubated overnight in the dark. Total protein was extracted from harvested protoplasts with a lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% Triton X-100, 1 mM DTT, proteinase inhibitor cocktail (Roche LifeScience)). Lysates were incubated with magnetic beads conjugated with an anti-HA-tag antibody (MBL, M180-11) or GFP-nanoab-magnetic beads (LABLEAD, cat# GNM-25-1000) at 4 °C for 4 h, in the presence of 50 μM MG132 and 1 μM GA4. The magnetic beads were then washed 5 times with Co-IP buffer (20 mM Tris pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween 20). Immunoprecipitates were resolved by SDS-PAGE and analyzed by immunoblotting with anti-HA (1:5000, cat# M180-7, MBL), anti-DDDDK (1:5000, cat#M185-7, MBL), and anti-GFP (1:5000, cat# 11814460001, Roche) antibodies.

For SLR1-GRF4 pull-down assay, full-length rice SLR1 and GRF4 were inserted into the pGEX-4T-1 and pMAL-c2X vectors, respectively. The recombinant GST-SLR1 and MBP-GRF4 proteins were purified from E. coli BL21 (DE3) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For the assay, MBP beads pre-loaded with equal amounts of MBP-GRF4 were incubated with the same amount of GST-SLR1 for 2 h at 4 °C, washed six times with Co-IP buffer, and eluted with 50 μL elution buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 10 mM reduced glutathione, pH 8.0). Eluates were resolved by immunoblotting using anti-GST (1:5000, cat# sc-138, Santa Cruz) and anti-MBP (1:5000, cat# E8032S, NEB) antibodies.

Statistical analysis

All statistical tests were performed using R software (v4.1.2). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test. Differences were statistically significant when P value was < 0.05. The measurements shown in the column graphs display the mean values, with each individual measurement indicated by red dots.
