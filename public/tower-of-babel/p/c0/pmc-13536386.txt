From domestic science to dental science: The hidden pathway for talented Taiwanese women during the Japanese colonial period
Authors: Hung-Lun Pan, Mei-Huei Li, Feng-Chou Cheng, Chun-Pin Chiang
Published: 2026

During the Japanese colonial period (1895–1945), the educational system in Taiwan was strongly gendered and primarily intended to cultivate “good wives and wise mothers”.1 Male students were encouraged to pursue professional fields such as law, medicine, and commerce, whereas female students were largely directed to Girls’ High Schools or Vocational Schools, where the curriculum emphasized domestic skills, including sewing, cooking, and child-rearing. However, a closer examination of the “Domestic Science” (Kasei-ka) curriculum reveals an unintended consequence: it inadvertently provided a basic scientific foundation that enabled some talented women to pursue professional careers in dentistry.

Unlike the purely vocational training in sewing, the Domestic Science curriculum included a substantial component of physiology and hygiene. Textbooks from that era described not only general bodily functions but also specific oral hygiene practices, emphasizing the mother’s role in maintaining the dental health of the family. For most students, this knowledge remained confined to the domestic sphere. However, for a small group of intellectually gifted women who were often from elite families with medical backgrounds, these courses served as a cognitive bridge to the medical sciences. They recognized that the principles of “hygiene” taught for household management were grounded in the same biological foundations as those underlying professional medical practice.

During the Japanese colonial period, oral health care in girls’ high school education was not taught as an independent subject but was also incorporated into home economics, physiology and hygiene courses, as well as school-based oral health activities, such as dental examinations and internships in school dental treatment rooms. This educational arrangement reflected the policy of that era to cultivate women as “good wives and wise mothers” and as guardians of family health.2,3 At that time, a variety of textbooks, teachers’ manuals, and examination reference books related to physiology and hygiene were available in Taiwan, many of which introduced human anatomy, physiology, and health care, naturally including knowledge of oral health. The National Diet Library in Japan preserves a large collection of high school physiology and hygiene textbooks from the Meiji period, Taisho period, and early Showa period. Among these, New Physiology and Hygiene, written by Teiji Matsushita and published as a teacher’s manual in 1914, contains numerous descriptions of the structure of the oral cavity in the chapter on the digestive system (Fig. 1A). The preface indicates that the book was intended as a reference for high school teachers and could be used alongside six textbooks, including a physiology and hygiene text specifically designed for girls’ high schools. This suggests that female students in general education at that time also had opportunities to acquire relevant knowledge in this field.

This teacher’s manual provides a comprehensive overview of the structure and function of the hard and soft tissues of the oral cavity, supplemented by seven illustrations. A sagittal section of the oral cavity demonstrates the overall composition of the oral cavity and its surrounding structures (Fig. 1B). The diagrams of lip closure and opening illustrate the anatomy of the lips, the perioral region, and their relationship with the oral cavity (Fig. 1C and D). An anatomical diagram of the maxillary dentition presents the arrangement of the permanent teeth, including the incisors, canines, premolars, and molars, together with their approximate eruption ages (Fig. 1E). A cross-sectional view of a tooth depicts the structure of its layers, including enamel, dentin, and cementum, as well as the pulp chamber (Fig. 1F). A schematic illustration of the protruded tongue demonstrates the detailed structure of the tongue and the soft tissues associated with the hard and soft palates (Fig. 1G). In addition, a lateral facial anatomical diagram illustrates the distribution of the three major salivary glands (Fig. 1H). As a reference resource for teachers in the instruction of oral health, this manual clearly encompasses essential dental knowledge. If such knowledge were conveyed to female students in the classroom, it could serve as a key that opened the door for them to explore the field of dentistry and potentially pursue careers as dentists in the future.

During the Japanese colonial period, formal dental education was not available in Taiwan. Aspiring dentists, regardless of gender, were therefore required to pursue their studies in Japan. For women, the barriers were even greater, as the only local medical school was overwhelmingly male-dominated and offered virtually no opportunities for female applicants. Consequently, these determined that women had to seek educational opportunities beyond the island. Building upon the basic medical knowledge acquired from hygiene courses in girls’ high schools and supported by their families’ financial resources, they traveled to Japan to pursue dental education. Many enrolled in institutions such as Toyo Girls’ Dental College, Japan Girls’ Dental College, and Tokyo Girls’ Dental College to study dentistry.4

The transition from “household manager” to “dental professional” represented a subtle yet transformative departure from conventional gender roles. By obtaining dental licenses, these women converted the private responsibility of “caring for family members’ teeth” into a public professional role centered on the treatment of patients. After returning to Taiwan, many established private dental clinics, often attached to their homes. This distinctive “home-clinic” model enabled them to navigate the dual expectations of professional practice and domestic responsibility, a balance that was particularly challenging within the conservative social context of the colonial period.

It is noteworthy that although the colonial education system did not explicitly create a pathway for women to become dentists, the rigorous emphasis on “hygiene” within domestic science education inadvertently planted the seeds for professional aspiration. These pioneering female dentists, such as Hsiu-Yu Kuo, demonstrated that determined and talented individuals could repurpose limited educational opportunities to overcome entrenched gender barriers.5 Their legacy lies not only in the patients they treated but also in their demonstration that the intellectual potential of Taiwanese women could not be confined to the kitchen, even by a curriculum originally designed to keep them there.
