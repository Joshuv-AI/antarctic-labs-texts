Finite element analysis of the influence of surface layer on frictional wear in a follow-up screw-rod kinematic node in GGS stabilization
Authors: Klaudia Szkoda-Poliszuk, Justyna Marcula, Celina Pezowicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to numerically analyse the influence of the surface layer on the mechanical properties in a follow-up screw–rod kinematic pair in Growth Guidance Stabilization System. In addition, the polymer insert on the sliding part of the support rod, which is designed to eliminate frictional wear effects, was also analysed. Numerical simulations were carried out by loading the model with short-term repeated load tests to represent the operation of the stabilizer under near-real conditions. Analysis of the reduced stresses according to the Huber - von Mises hypothesis of the cooperating elements, i.e. the rod and the sliding screw cap, showed a point concentration of stresses. In the pairs covered with the additional coating, the maximum stress values do not exceed 4 MPa on both mentioned elements and are similar in all analysed configurations. The modification of the geometry in the form of an additional insert resulted in a different stress distribution, with the maximum values observed at the insert being around 10 MPa. The numerical simulations carried out showed areas of stress concentration at locations that may be indicative of frictional wear occurring. The design modification changed the stress distribution on the mating components, which may be preferable to point contact in standard kinematic pairs. The stress values in the coated models are similar to each other, suggesting that the choice of a particular coating does not significantly affect the mechanical performance, but nevertheless the presence of the coating is crucial.Keywords: Growth-guidance system, Sliding pedicle screws, Biomechanics, Frictional stress, Numerical simulationsSubject terms: Implants, Biomedical engineering

Introduction

Currently, Early Onset Scoliosis (EOS) management involves GGS stabilization (Growth Guidance System), which allows for deformation control at uninhibited growth1. This in an innovative approach to controlling spinal growth, used especially in cases where conservative methods fail to bring the expected results. This construction makes deformation correction possible with the application of tensile forces at the upper and lower section of the curvature using rods and sliding transpedicular screws with special screw caps2.

EOS, defined as spinal curvature exceeding 10° before the age of 10, poses significant therapeutic challenges due to ongoing growth of the spine and chest and the risk of impaired lung development and other internal organs if left untreated3. In this context, GGS-based approaches have emerged as viable surgical solutions that are friendly to the development of the young body while maintaining curvature control and allowing the spine to grow. Among the non-fusion strategies using classic implant designs, GGS allows the spine to grow along the rods without the need for repeated surgical lengthening at the screw-rod junction, which distinguishes it from traditional growing rod systems (TGR) or magnetically controlled growing rod systems (MCGR)4–6. The biomechanical concept underlying GGS is based on sliding designs that allow differential movement at the bone-implant interface, in particular through guided rods fixed by non-locking transpedicular screws at the cranial and caudal ends, while fixing the apex of the curvature6. These systems, although clinically beneficial in reducing the number of reoperations, are mechanically demanding, especially in pediatric patients undergoing rapid spinal growth. Studies have shown that the sliding mechanisms in GGS are prone to friction between the rod and screw interfaces, leading to tribological wear and the release of metallic particles5,7. This is concerning because increased levels of titanium ions have been found in paravertebral tissues and blood, particularly in children with GGS instruments, suggesting greater in vivo wear compared to other systems such as VEPTR or TGR5.

Despite the proven effectiveness of GGS stabilization, frictional wear between the surfaces in contact seems to be the major issue. Owing to the construction and frictional contact, the implants may be subject to damage, thus resulting in mechanical wear and implantation gap. The resultant release of metal particles to the adjoining tissues, especially in the case of metal-to-metal friction pair mating, may lead to so-called metallosis. This phenomenon is mainly related to implant corrosion leading to metal debris built-up in soft tissues, thus giving them a characteristic colour2. A particular threat is attributed to this phenomenon owing to the possible inflammatory reactions that may lead to multiple sequelae.

As shown by Singh et al., analysis of the wear of recovered GGS implants indicated the generation of wear particles at both the macro- and micro-levels due to the mobile connection between the rod and the screw, even in the absence of active elongation mechanisms5. Furthermore, in vitro and ex vivo studies confirmed the negative potential of stainless steel or titanium friction wear products, causing chronic inflammatory reactions, osteolysis and increased cytokine expression in peri-implant tissues5. These results emphasize the importance of detailed tribological analysis of individual components in GGS systems, especially in children during bone growth.

The literature on the subject presents research being carried out, whose main focus is to describe the effects of titanium particles release in the whole stabilizer construction, omitting what is happening at the level of particular kinematic pairs6,8,9. The authors – previously conducting experimental and numerical research – focused on the phenomena created in a single sliding kinematic pair10,11, because it is rarely mentioned in the literature. In the authors’ opinion, the analysis of a single kinematic pair allows for detailed study of tribological and biomechanical issues in GGS type constructions. A single sliding kinematic pair has six friction pairs11, yet this study focuses on two most important ones, i.e. rod–screw tulip head and rod–cap.

In medicine, particularly in dentistry, various types of coatings are applied, whose intended use is to eliminate tribology wear products. The coatings applied in implantation in medicine are characterised by high abrasion resistance and biocompatibility12,13. However, when analysing the literature, it is noted that there is a certain niche in the area of research concerning the analysis of the degree of frictional wear of coated spinal implants. Research is lacking as regards mechanical analysis and tribology analysis of the coating applied to the transpedicular stabilization elements. The most commonly applied ones are DLC coatings (Diamond-Like Carbon, namely thin protective layers containing amorphic carbon and oxygen or hydrogenated amorphous carbon doped with tungsten carbide) or ceramic coatings (titanium nitride, carbon-titanium nitride and zirconium nitride)14. Their addition significantly improves the friction wear resistance of materials. Additional coatings significantly reduce the coefficient of friction improving friction wear resistance. Coatings such as diamond-like (DLC) or ceramic have a low coefficient of friction in dry and wet conditions and act as a barrier separating two friction surfaces. The work by Madej et al.15 investigated the properties of DLC coatings deposited on titanium alloy Ti6Al4V. Deposition of the coating increased the hardness of the alloy by 75% and reduced the coefficient of friction by 80%. The volumetric wear rate of the titanium alloy was 90% higher compared to the DLC coating. Ceramic coatings have also been analysed for tribological wear in the literature. For example, the work of Mohseni et al.16 examined various ceramic coatings in terms of surface hardness or friction coefficient, among others. The hardness of the coated surfaces increased by 48% and the coefficient of friction decreased by 43%. These results confirm the effectiveness of coatings - both diamond-like (DLC) and ceramic coatings.

The research presented is a continuation and development of numerical analyses focused on the application of coatings in spinal stabilizer components. The main objective of this study was to numerically analyse the impact of the surface layer on the mechanical properties in a follow-up screw-rod kinematic pair in GGS transpedicular stabilization. Additionally, a UHMWPE (ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene) insert made of ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene on the sliding side of the load bearing rod was also analysed, and its goal is to eliminate the effects of frictional wear.

Materials and methods

Geometric model

A spatial geometric model (3D) of a single sliding kinematic screw-rod pair of the GGS spinal stabilization system was developed based on the actual dimensions of the implant by NovaSpine (Domasław, Poland). A single kinematic pair included: a transpedicular screw including the head, a locking device located in the screw head, a sliding screw cap and a rod. Screw diameter amounted to 6 mm, and the length of the bone part to 40 mm. The sliding screw cap placed in the screw head was 4.5 mm long and 11 mm in diameter. The rod mating with the cap and screw was 100 mm long and 5.5 mm in diameter. In kinematic pairs, this type of connection involves the interaction of a rod placed in the screw head, which is locked with a sliding screw cap in such a manner as to allow screw movement along the rod.

In the search for new solutions to eliminate the effects of wear, two methods were subject to analysis, i.e. modifying implant surface by applying a coating to increase frictional wear resistance and modifying the geometry of the GGS stabilizer construction by using an additional insert. Therefore, two configurations of a single sliding kinematic screw–rod pair model of the GGS spinal stabilization system were designed. The first model included a modification in the form of coatings with varying chemical composition and mechanical parameters, where the coating was 1 μm thick. The coating was modeled using the Surface Coating tool in ANSYS Workbench 2024 R2. The study analysed 5 coatings – 2 diamond-like carbon coatings (amorphous carbon with hydrogen DLC a-C: H, hydrogenated amorphous carbon doped with tungsten carbide DLC a-C: H:W), and 3 ceramic coatings (titanium nitride TiN, carbon-titanium nitride TiCN and zirconium nitride ZrN), which were expected to provide a much harder and wear-resistant surface solution and reduce the friction coefficient compared to the native material (Fig. 1a). Such coatings are widely used in medicine, in dentistry in particular. The other case included an additional UHMWPE insert on the sliding side of the load bearing rod (Fig. 1b). It has a top and bottom flange to perfectly match the geometry of the screw head. This type of construction prevents direct friction between metal-to-metal mating parts and also reduces the sliding friction force due to the increased friction surface area. Each pair was located in SYNBONE 30 PCF polyurethane foam with the density of 480.5 /3. This is a commonly applied material for mechanical testing with spinal implants and was used for experimental studies in the authors’ previous study10,11. It has biomimetic properties that imitate the construction of the vertebral bone tissue. Foam of such density corresponds to a healthy bone tissue per the ASTM F1839–08 standard. The model was developed in the Autodesk Inventor Professional 2024 programme (Autodesk, Inc., San Rafael, CA, USA).

Discrete model

The numerical simulations were performed using ANSYS Workbench 2024 R2 software (Ansys, Inc., Pennsylvania, USA), where the geometry of the discrete model was created and analysed. To determine the appropriate accuracy of the numerical calculation, a mesh convergence analysis was carried out (Fig. 2). An iterative approach was used in which the element size was reduced by 0.2 mm, analysing changes in reduced stresses according to the Huber - von Mises hypothesis. By thickening the mesh, a stabilisation of the results was observed.

Mesh convergence diagram of a cap of the DLC a-C: H model: change in von Mises stress as a function of mesh density (number of finite elements).

Based on this analysis, a finite element size of 0.4 mm was selected. A tetrahedral, ten-pair solid element of SOLID 187 type was selected, which is suitable for modelling irregular meshes due to the three degrees of freedom at each node. The kinematic pair model with a layer was divided into 8,605,081 finite elements (Fig. 3a) and the model with construction change into 8,759,849 finite elements (Fig. 3b). The mesh application made it possible to properly model the geometry of the kinematic pair.

Conditions of model load

The simulation was carried out loading the model with periodical loads (short-term repeated load tests) aimed at modelling the stabilizer’s operation under conditions close to the actual elements functioning. The rod movement was controlled in its longitudinal axis by ± 3 mm10 at the frequency of 2 Hz41. The displacement was applied to the upper surface of the rod to simulate the actuator’s effect. The model was affixed eliminating all the degrees of freedom at the upper and lower surface of the polyurethane foam (Fig. 4).

Results

The results for all models obtained from the numerical simulations were subjected to comparative analysis. The Huber – von Mises (equivalent) stress was estimated using the standard post-processing capabilities of ANSYS Workbench, as the criterion for evaluating the structural integrity of the model. The Huber - von Mises stress is based on the results of the finite element analysis, using the stress tensor components at each node. The distribution of reduced stresses according to the Huber - von Mises hypothesis of the configurations analysed was evaluated, particularly the areas with higher values of this parameter.

The distribution of interface pressure and the distribution of frictional stress was also analysed as regards all the analysed kinematic pairs at the friction point sliding screw cap - rod. In a contact analysis, when two surfaces are defined with a coefficient of friction (frictional contact), ANSYS calculates the following: normal interface pressure – the pressure acting perpendicular to the contact surface and frictional (tangential) stress – the shear stress resulting from the friction force. Frictional stress refers to the tangential interface stress generated due to friction between two surfaces, as determined using Coulomb’s law. It is derived from the product of the local interface pressure and the coefficient of friction within the contact pair.

Areas of stress concentration were determined based on the stress distribution, where they could indicate increased frictional wear, as well as the effect of using an additional insert to increase the rod–cap friction surface. What may result from the screw cap–rod mating is frictional wear visible on the surfaces of mating elements.

The analysis covered the mating elements, i.e. the rod and the cap, and in the case of kinematic pairs with a construction change, the insert was additionally analysed. Figure 5 presents the reduced stress distribution of caps in all the single kinematic pairs. The highest stress values occur in the local contact areas at the top and bottom of the contact surface with the mating component - these are the areas of direct contact between the cap and the rod (in a pair with an additional coating) or between the cap and the UHMWPE insert (in a pair with a construction modification). The stress distribution in pairs with surface modification is due to the geometry of the contact and the limited area of cooperation. The highest values were observed for the pair in which the cap and rod were additionally coated with a-C: H DLC layer and are approximately 0.42 MPa. The lowest stress values are found in the kinematic pair coated with a ZrN layer and reach approx. 0.26 MPa. The kinematic pair with an additional UHMWPE insert to increase the contact area showed a different stress distribution. The highest values reach approx. 3.48 MPa and are distributed over a much wider area, which results from the contact between the cap and the insert and may be more beneficial than the point contact present in standard kinematic pairs.

Discussion

Diamond-like carbon coatings (DLC) and ceramic coatings (i.e. titanium nitride or zirconium nitride) are commonly used in medicine to improve the mechanical and physicochemical properties of implants and surgical instruments. Research into their application focuses mainly on joint endoprostheses and dental implants, where their effects on mechanical strength, wear resistance and interaction with tissues are analysed42–44. In the context of dental implants, studies show that DLC coatings and ceramic coatings can act as an effective barrier against galvanic corrosion, improving the durability and stability of implant systems14. Similarly, in the case of surgical instruments, these coatings increase abrasion resistance, reduce friction and improve biocompatibility, resulting in a longer service life and less risk of tissue damage during procedures.

However, despite numerous studies on the use of DLC and ceramic coatings in dental implantology and orthopaedics, there is still a clear research niche for their use in spinal implants. These implants are subject to unique biomechanical conditions, characterised by high variable loads and a specific biological environment.

In the context of the observable research gap, numerical analyses may provide important information on stress distribution and potential stress concentration locations of coated implants and, consequently, the locations of potential frictional wear of these elements. This approach would allow for the optimisation of implant design, selection of appropriate materials, and increase of their durability and clinical safety. The application of protective coatings may contribute to improving the outcomes for patients with spinal diseases and injuries, which is an important area for further research in biomedical engineering.

The preliminary in vitro studies presented by Du et al.45 present the developed and modified GGS system based on Shill technique. The main innovations of this solution included the application of UHMWPE bushings preventing titanium alloy from contact with each other and polishing the rods in order to reduce the friction and improve screws sliding. The new system solves two major problems in the Shill technique: it limits the deposition of metallic particles in tissues and allows for curvature correction and supports spinal growth. Fatigue test results demonstrated that after 10 million cycles, wear on the bushings was minimal, while effectively eliminating metal-to-metal contact and the release of metallic titanium particles. Studies have also shown that polishing the rods improves the sliding motion of the screws, which may promote spinal growth.

Referring the preliminary studies by Du et al.45 to the numerical analyses conducted by the authors, it was observed that the insertion of an additional element in the form of a bushing resulted in the creation of varied stress distribution compared to a standard kinematic pair10, and the area was much larger and covered the entire length of the element. The stress concentration points are distributed randomly and are present both on the outside and inside of the insert. The maximum values amounted to approx. 10 MPa. Such an element contributes to a more even distribution of stresses, which may be more beneficial than a point contact resulting from the operation of the sliding cap and load-bearing rod. The authors presented another solution to the GGS stabilizer construction change in the previous studies10, where they analysed a model of kinematic pair with an additional insert of a different geometry. On the basis of the analyses carried out, it was observed that the results obtained in both cases (inserts and bushings) are similar and the stress distributions are characterised by a uniform distribution. Such design solutions largely eliminate local stress concentrations, which could lead to material damage and improves the durability and functionality of the stabilizer in long-term operation.

The other analysed method improving the wear resistance, applied by the authors, was the insertion of additional layers to the mating elements. The application of additional coatings reduced the stress values compared to the standard sliding kinematic pair (without coating) analysed in the previous article10. The highest values of stress in the case of a standard pair were concentrated in the upper and lower part of the cap, reaching approx. 16 MPa. Having applied additional coatings (both diamond-like carbon and ceramic ones), the stress value was reduced below 1 MPa, and the stress distribution was of a comparable nature.

Numerical studies on spinal implants are uncommon in the literature; dental or orthopaedic implants are the ones more widely discussed46,47. The numerical analyses presented by Galina et al.48 present the model of mechanical operation of materials in the frictional pair of hip joint replacement when an additional ceramic surface is applied (TiN). Simulation results proved that the application of an additional layer resulted in the creation of a varied and more beneficial stress distribution, which may help in avoiding premature endoprosthesis wear. In turn, Dhatrak et al.49, when conducting numerical analyses as regards the assessment of the impact of varied coatings applied to titanium dental implants, observed that the selection of appropriate ones would significantly reduce the stress created at the contact point of the bone and the implant. They analysed three types of coatings: titanium nitride (TiN), titanium oxide (TiO2) and zirconium nitride (ZrN).

Similar relationships were observed in the authors’ present study, where additional coatings (DLC a-C: H, DLC a-C: H:W, TiN, TiCN, ZrN) significantly impacted stress distribution in the kinematic pairs. These statements may also be confirmed with the authors’ experimental studies11, where they assessed the degree of frictional wear on the mating surfaces of components used in GGS stabilizers coated with DLC a-C: H, compared to standard stabilizer components made of Ti6Al4V titanium alloy. Cyclic tests showed that lower abrasive wear was observed in the case of elements coated with a DLC a-C: H layer than for standard elements made of Ti6Al4V titanium alloy. However, an analysis of the surface topography of the DLC a-C: H coated sliding caps demonstrated that when the structure was damaged, the damage was deeper and wider and the wear products were larger, compared to the standard caps. Therefore, it seems justified to carry out cyclic tests and analyses of the surface topography of other coatings for which numerical analyses were performed.

Numerical simulations were carried out using the Static Structural module in the Ansys Workbench environment, restricting the analysis to short-term repetitive displacement-controlled loads, with the aim of assessing the stress distribution and contact behavior within selected stabilizer components. The model was subjected to 20 displacement steps, each corresponding to an axial sliding motion of +- 3 mm at a frequency of 2 Hz. The loading protocol used reproduced the behavior of the system under time-limited conditions. The approach adopted does not allow analysis of dynamic phenomena or determination of energy dissipation per cycle. In the next stages of the study, the authors, using the Transient Structural module, will include a detailed assessment of the dynamic friction interactions, including the determination of the load-displacement hysteresis loop. This approach will enable a full evaluation of the effectiveness of the applied coatings under the long-term operating conditions of the stabilizer.

The stick-slip phenomenon was not explicitly modeled using separate static and kinetic coefficients of friction due to limitations in available experimental data. Instead, a single effective coefficient of friction was applied uniformly, representing an averaged behavior of the contact interface. This simplification may not fully capture local variations in frictional behavior and dynamic transitions between sticking and slipping states.

The performed numerical analyses have certain limitations resulting from the research method used. Numerical simulations based on the application of the finite element method represent a form of simplification of research under conditions that do not take into account the complex tissue environment. The analyses did not take into account the actual operating conditions of the stabilizer components, such as temperature, humidity or varying environmental conditions, which can have a significant impact on component operation. In addition, the simulations do not take into account the full interaction with the environment, e.g. contact at the implant–tissue interface, which also causes certain limitations. The above study included a single kinematic pair simulation, meaning that only the interaction of the rod with one transpedicular screw was taken into account. In reality, several kinematic pairs are used that work together. Our analysis takes into account only a fraction of the actual operation of the stabilizer, which affects the accuracy of the performed studies. The next stage of the authors’ study will include extending the numerical studies considering the arrangement of multiple kinematic pairs to obtain more accurate results. However, the studies conducted to date could significantly impact the development of GGS spinal stabilization systems. Many of these systems rely on the cooperation of different components, and friction between them is one of the factors leading to implant damage or inflammation in the body.
