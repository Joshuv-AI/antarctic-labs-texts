From eveningness to food addiction: exploring the roles of night eating syndrome and mindful eating
Authors: Büşra Başar Gökcen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronotype is a key biopsychosocial factor that regulates individuals’ sleep–wake cycles and daily activities while also shaping their eating behaviors. Eveningness has been associated with maladaptive eating patterns such as food addiction and night eating syndrome, with mindful eating considered a critical factor in these associations.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to examine the direct relationships among eveningness, night eating syndrome, and food addiction, and to investigate the mediating role of night eating syndrome and the moderating role of mindful eating in these associations.MethodsThis exploratory cross-sectional study was conducted with 490 adults. Participants completed validated measures including the Yale Food Addiction Scale (YFAS), Night Eating Questionnaire (NEQ), the Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire (MEQc), and the Mindful Eating Questionnaire (MEQm). To identify predictors and risk factors of food addiction and night eating syndrome were examined using binary logistic regression and hierarchical linear regression models. Mediation and moderation analyses were conducted using the PROCESS macro (Model 4 and Model 1). Model 4 tested the indirect pathways from chronotype to food addiction through night eating syndrome. Model 1 examined the moderating role of mindful eating in the associations between night eating syndrome and food addiction, as well as between chronotype and food addiction.ResultsCorrelation analyses showed that NEQ was positively associated with YFAS symptom counts (r = 0.166, p < 0.001), whereas MEQm was inversely related to both (r = -0.313 to -0.183, p < 0.001). Linear regression analyses indicated that greater night eating syndrome symptom severity predicted higher food addiction symptom counts (B = 0.026, p < 0.001), whereas mindful eating predicted fewer food addiction symptoms (B = -3.400, p < 0.001). Logistic regression further showed that night eating syndrome (OR = 1.060, p < 0.001) increased the risk of food addiction, whereas mindful eating (OR = 0.152, p < 0.001) reduced it. Path analyses indicated that night eating syndrome mediated the association between eveningness and food addiction, whereas mindful eating partially moderated the relationship between night eating syndrome and food addiction.ConclusionsThis study shows interrelations among chronotype, night eating syndrome, and mindful eating in relation to food addiction. Mindful eating was inversely associated with food addiction symptoms and was found to condition the association between night eating syndrome and food addiction.Trial registration This study did not involve a clinical intervention requiring trial registration.Keywords: Food addiction, Mindful eating, Night eating syndrome, Chronotype, Obesity

Introduction

Chronotype reflects an individual’s natural propensity for sleep-wake timing as well as personal preferences for activity and performance timing, which exist along a continuum ranging from morningness to eveningness. Morning types typically go to bed and wake up earlier, reaching peak productivity during the morning hours, whereas evening types follow later sleep–wake schedules and perform optimally in the evening [1–3].

Recent research has increasingly acknowledged that evening chronotype is closely linked to both sleep and mood disorders [4–6], as well as to eating disorders [7]. To date, most studies examining this relationship have focused primarily on binge eating disorder within the spectrum of eating disorders [2]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis provided evidence that evening chronotype is linked to suboptimal eating behaviors and confers a greater susceptibility to maladaptive eating patterns, including food addiction and night eating syndrome [8]. Accordingly, further research has been called for to gain a clearer understanding of the relationship between chronotype and these maladaptive eating behaviors [9].

Food addiction is defined as the compulsive consumption of highly palatable and energy-dense foods, characterized by intense cravings, loss of control over intake, and clinical features resembling substance dependence [10]. Highly palatable foods, particularly those rich in sugar, fat, sodium, and additives, stimulate reward-related neural pathways and encourage overeating behaviors characteristic of addiction [11]. Moreover, the modern food environment— marked by its accessibility, affordability, and convenience—further amplifies individuals’ propensity toward such foods [12]. However, many individuals struggle to suppress these impulsive tendencies and frequently experience a loss of control over eating [13]. Individuals with an evening chronotype tendency exhibit difficulty in emotion regulation, impaired cognitive reappraisal skills, greater self-blame tendencies, elevated perceived stress levels, and abnormalities in the brain reward system. These factors may help explain the link between eveningness and maladaptive eating behaviors, such as food addiction [2, 14]. Evening chronotype has increasingly been recognized as an important risk factor for food addiction [15–17]. Therefore, it is essential to examine in detail the factors that influence this strong relationship [15].

Night eating syndrome, classified as an ‘Other Specified Feeding or Eating Disorder’ in the DSM-5, represents another maladaptive eating behavior associated with the evening chronotype [9]. Night eating syndrome is a distinct eating disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of excessive food consumption after the evening meal or upon nocturnal awakenings, typically accompanied by morning anorexia, evening hyperphagia—defined as consuming at least 25% of daily caloric intake after dinner—and insomnia, resulting in significant psychological distress and impaired daily functioning [18]. The tendency of evening-type individuals to delay meal timing in accordance with circadian rhythm preferences largely explains the strong association between eveningness and night eating syndrome [19, 20]. Nevertheless, further studies are required to clarify the mechanisms underlying this relationship [21].

To date, mediation analyses examining the relationships between chronotype and night eating syndrome [20, 21], as well as between chronotype and food addiction [15, 22, 23], have primarily focused on variables such as stress, sleep disturbances, and mood disorders. However, the role of mindful eating within this framework, either as a mediator or as an associated construct, has received limited attention in the literature.

Mindful eating is a multidimensional construct that refers to the deliberate awareness of one’s thoughts, emotions, bodily sensations, and behaviors related to eating, encompassing cognitive and physiological processes such as attention, memory, recognition of hunger–satiety cues, and emotion regulation [24]. Mindful eating may act as a protective mechanism in the prevention and management of eating disorders by increasing sensitivity to hunger–satiety signals, reducing emotional eating triggers, strengthening cognitive-emotional regulation skills, and promoting more balanced attitudes toward food [25]. Recent findings also suggest that chronotype may be linked to mindful eating: adults with a morning chronotype report higher levels of mindful eating compared to other chronotypes and score higher on the recognition dimension, which reflects the ability to differentiate between hunger and satiety [19, 26]. Furthermore, mindful eating has been associated with both night eating syndrome and food addiction [26, 27]. A recent bibliometric analysis reaffirmed the significance of mindful eating in the management of eating disorders, highlighting it as a promising clinical approach, while also emphasizing the need for more robust clinical evidence [28].

Considering these interrelated mechanisms, it can be suggested that the pathways linking eveningness, night eating syndrome, and food addiction may be shaped by mindful eating. Specifically, individuals with an evening chronotype may be more vulnerable to night eating syndrome, which in turn could increase the risk of food addiction. However, the protective role of mindful eating in these associations remains uncertain. Therefore, the present study aimed to examine the direct and indirect relationships among evening chronotype, night eating syndrome, and food addiction, by testing the mediating role of night eating syndrome and the moderating role of mindful eating. In addition, all models controlled for age, gender, and BMI categories to account for sociodemographic and clinical variations.

Methods and materials

Procedure

This cross-sectional, non-preregistered study is reported within an exploratory framework. Psychometric assessments were administered directly during the interviews using standardized instruments that had previously demonstrated validity and reliability in Turkish populations. Anthropometric data (height and weight) were not measured directly by the researcher, as carrying equipment such as a stadiometer and scale was not feasible. Furthermore, standardized conditions for weight assessment (e.g., fasting in the morning) could not always be ensured, participants were asked during the face-to-face interviews to confirm their most recent and as accurate as possible body weight.

A purposive sampling method was employed to identify participants, and direct communication was established with individuals aged 18 years and older. Prior to the interviews, participants were thoroughly informed about the purpose, scope, and conditions of participation in the study. They were assured that their data would be evaluated anonymously and treated in accordance with confidentiality principles. Participation was voluntary, and no financial compensation was provided to any participant.

Overweight and obese individuals were recruited through referrals from diet counseling centers affiliated with local health institutions. The researcher contacted volunteers directly after they were informed about the study by dietitians working at these centers. Eligible participants were selected according to predefined inclusion criteria. For the recruitment of normal-weight individuals, community-based individual outreach strategies were employed to ensure representation across BMI categories.

This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Muğla Sıtkı Koçman University Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences, under protocol-decision number 240019-41, on March 6, 2024.

Exclusion criteria included having physical, cognitive, or psychological limitations that would prevent effective communication or completion of the data collection process.

A total of 490 individuals participated in the study. The mean age of the participants was 31.6 ± 11.7 years (median: 26.0), and the mean BMI was 26.9 ± 4.9 kg/m² (median: 27.0).

Most of the participants were female (68.8%) and single (59.2%). More than half had received at least 12 years of education (59.8%), and a substantial proportion reported no chronic diseases (83.1%). In addition, more than half of the participants stated that they were not following any diet (58.8%).

Measures

In this study, demographic data were first collected from participants, including age, gender, marital status, and educational attainment. At the same stage, self-reported height (cm) and body weight (kg) were obtained and used to BMI (kg/m²). Immediately afterward, participants were asked about their chronic disease status, smoking and alcohol use, and current dieting behavior to assess lifestyle and health history.

In the next step, psychometric evaluation was conducted. For this purpose, all participants completed the following standardized instruments in the same fixed order: the Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire (MEQc), the Night Eating Questionnaire (NEQ), the Mindful Eating Questionnaire (MEQm), and the Yale Food Addiction Scale (YFAS). To avoid confusion, the abbreviation MEQ-c is used for the Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire (c = chronotype) and MEQ-m for the Mindful Eating Questionnaire (m = mindful). From this point forward, these abbreviations are used in the tables and results.

Mindful eating questionnaire (MEQm)

The MEQm was originally developed by Framson et al. (2009) as a 28-item instrument designed to assess mindful eating behaviors [29]. Subsequently, five items from the original scale were retained, while the remaining items were adapted and expanded upon, resulting in a revised version consisting of 30 items. The adapted version is scored on a 5-point Likert scale and includes seven subscales: disinhibition, emotional eating, eating control, attention/focus, eating discipline, awareness, and interference. Higher scores on each subscale indicate stronger presence of the construct being assessed in that domain. In addition, the questionnaire provides a total mindful eating score. Both subscale and total scores are calculated as mean values, with higher scores reflecting greater levels of mindful eating [30–32].

The Turkish validity and reliability of the adapted version were confirmed by Köse et al. in 2016. In the validity and reliability study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the YFÖ-30 scale was reported as 0.733 [30]. In the present study, the internal consistency of the scale was re-evaluated, and Cronbach’s alpha was found to be 0.827. This result indicates that the scale demonstrated good internal consistency in our sample.

Morningness eveningness questionnaire (MEQc)

To determine the circadian rhythm preferences of participants, the MEQc, originally developed by Horne and Östberg in 1976 [33], was administered. The Turkish adaptation and validation study of the scale was conducted by Pündük et al. (2005) [34]. This self-report questionnaire consists of 19 items and is scored using a Likert-type system, with varying response scales: 11 items (S3-9 and S13-16) are scored between 1 and 4; 5 items (S1, S2, S10, S17, S18) between 1 and 5; 2 items (S11, S19) between 0 and 6; and 1 item (S12) between 0 and 5. Based on the total score, individuals are classified into chronotype categories as follows: a total score of 59–86 indicates a morning type, 42–58 indicates an intermediate type, and 16–41 indicates an evening type [35].

In the validity and reliability study, the MEQc demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.785 in the first administration and 0.812 in the second administration [34]. In the present study, the reliability analysis yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.804, indicating that the scale exhibited good reliability in our sample.

Night eating questionnaire (NEQ)

To assess the presence and severity of Night Eating Syndrome among participants, the NEQ, developed by Allison et al. (2008) was employed [36]. The Turkish validity and reliability study of this 16-item screening instrument was conducted by Atasoy et al. (2013) [37]. The first 9 items are completed by all participants. Items 10–12 are completed by individuals who report nocturnal awakenings, while items 13 and 14 are intended for those who engage in nighttime snacking. Except for item 7, all items are rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4. Item 7 assesses diurnal mood variation and is scored as 0 if no variation is reported. Items 1, 4, and 14 are reverse-coded. Item 13, which evaluates the individual’s awareness of nighttime snacking, is not included in the total score. Additionally, items 15 and 16 are supplementary and also excluded from scoring [38, 39]. The total NEQ score ranges from 0 to 52. A cut-off score of 25 or higher is used to indicate the presence of night eating syndrome. Participants scoring ≥ 25 are categorized as having night eating syndrome, whereas those scoring < 25 are considered not to have the syndrome [40].

In the validity and reliability study, the NEQ demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.69, indicating the scale’s aggregability and an acceptable level of internal consistency [37]. In the present study, the reliability analysis yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.683, confirming that the NEQ also exhibited acceptable reliability in our sample.

Yale food addiction scale (YFAS)

This instrument was originally developed by Gearhardt et al. (2009), based on the diagnostic criteria for substance dependence as outlined in the DSM-IV-TR [41]. The Turkish adaptation of the scale was conducted by Bayraktar et al. in 2012 [42], and its validity and reliability in Turkish populations were subsequently confirmed by Sevinçer et al. in 2015. In the reliability and validity study, the internal consistency of the YFAS was reported as high (Cronbach’s α = 0.93) [43]. In the present study, the reliability analysis yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.736, indicating that the scale demonstrated acceptable reliability in our sample.

YFAS is a 27-item self-report questionnaire designed to identify addiction-like eating behaviors experienced within the past 12 months. It captures key dimensions such as loss of control over eating, persistent desire or repeated unsuccessful attempts to cut down, continued overeating despite negative consequences, and clinically significant impairment or distress related to food consumption [44].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and the PROCESS macro version 4.2 developed by Andrew F. Hayes. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, visual inspections (histograms and Q–Q plots), and skewness–kurtosis values. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables were summarized as median (Q1–Q3, IQR). Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Associations between continuous variables were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient.

To examine predictors of night eating syndrome symptom severity and food addiction symptom counts linear regression analyses were performed. Unstandardized coefficients (B), standardized coefficients (β), standard errors (SE), t and p values, ΔR², F-change, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. An effect was considered significant if the 95% CI did not include zero.

In addition, binary logistic regression analyses were applied to identify risk factors for food addiction (YFAS) and night eating syndrome (NEQ). Model fit was assessed using − 2 log likelihood, Cox & Snell R², Nagelkerke R², and model chi-square (χ²) statistics. Odds ratios (OR) and their 95% CIs were calculated to evaluate the magnitude of associations.

Before regression and mediation analyses, the assumptions of multiple regression were tested. Variance inflation factor (VIF) values ranged from 1.0 to 1.5, indicating no problematic multicollinearity. As Menard (1995) noted, a tolerance value below 0.20 (corresponding to VIF >5) indicates a potential multicollinearity concern, whereas a tolerance below 0.10 (VIF >10) almost certainly signals a serious multicollinearity problem [45]. Standardized residuals fell almost entirely within the generally accepted ± 3 range, suggesting no substantial outliers; in general, approximately 99.7% of observations in the standard normal distribution are expected to fall within this range [46]. Together, these results confirmed that the assumptions required for regression analyses were satisfied.

Mediation and moderation analyses were conducted using the PROCESS macro (Models 4 and 1) developed by Hayes [78]. Model 4 tested the indirect effect of eveningness chronotype (MEQc) on food addiction symptom count (YFAS-SC) through night eating syndrome symptom severity (NEQ). Model 1 tested the moderating role of mindful eating (MEQm) on two separate associations: (a) eveningness chronotype (MEQc) → food addiction (YFAS-SC), and (b) night eating syndrome symptom severity (NEQ) → food addiction (YFAS-SC). Gender, age, and BMI were included as covariates in all analyses. Indirect effects were tested using 5000 bootstrap resamples and evaluated with bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals (CI). Conditional effects were probed at the mean and ± 1 SD values of the moderator. Effects were considered statistically significant if the 95% CI did not include zero.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that eveningness was associated with night eating syndrome, and that night eating syndrome, in turn, was related to food addiction. The analyses indicated an indirect pathway from eveningness to food addiction through night eating syndrome, whereas the direct association between eveningness and food addiction was not significant. In addition, mindful eating functioned as a moderator rather than a mediator, as the association between night eating syndrome and food addiction was stronger at lower levels of mindful eating and attenuated at higher levels.

From eveningness to night eating syndrome

Chronotype not only regulates the sleep–wake cycle but also determines the timing of food intake. A systematic review examining chronotype and eating behaviors reported that, although daily total energy and macronutrient intakes are similar, individuals with an evening chronotype tend to concentrate a large proportion of their intake during the biological night and consume meals at later hours [47]. At this point, chronotype overlaps with one of the core symptoms of night eating syndrome, namely evening hyperphagia (consumption of more than 25% of daily energy intake after dinner). This highlights a possible link between the two variables [18, 20]. Their association can be partly explained by abnormal melatonin secretion, misalignments between central and peripheral circadian clocks, and alterations in the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis (elevated cortisol levels) [20, 48].

Our findings are consistent with previous literature reporting an association between night eating syndrome and eveningness chronotype. A significant relationship was found between a stronger eveningness preference and higher severity of night eating syndrome-related symptoms. Regression analyses further indicated that, although the effect size was small, morningness preference was associated with lower levels of night eating syndrome. Overall, previous research conducted in various samples has also reported significant associations between these two variables [20, 23, 49–54]. However, a more recent study conducted in a non-clinical adult population reported an association between morningness and night eating syndrome. The authors attributed this contradictory finding to methodological artifacts, noting that the assessment tool employed (SCRAM—Sleep, Circadian Rhythms, and Mood Questionnaire) is relatively new, demonstrates low internal validity, and collapses three distinct constructs into a single score [55].

Our mediation analyses indicated evidence of a direct association between eveningness chronotype and night eating syndrome. Individuals with an eveningness preference exhibit marked impairments in emotion regulation strategies. Eveningness is characterized by dysregulated reward responsiveness, diminished cognitive reappraisal skills, a tendency toward self-blame, heightened perceived stress, and, particularly during late hours, irresistible impulsive urges and the behaviors that follow. These psychobiological features may facilitate the emergence of abnormal eating patterns such as night eating syndrome [2, 14, 20, 48]. Indeed, this syndrome is characterized by excessive evening food consumption, impulsivity, an overwhelming desire for food, and overeating behaviors aimed at compensating for negative affect [18].

Individuals with low levels of mindfulness may be unable to distinguish between hunger signals and emotional arousal, and they may lack the self-regulation strategies that involve diverting attention away from emotions toward immediate stimuli such as food. In the absence of mindfulness, maladaptive eating behaviors may therefore be adopted as a means of coping with negative states [56]. In contrast, mindful eating represents an attitude that prioritizes internal cues—including intuitive and conscious eating sensations—rather than reacting to external or environmental triggers. Mindfulness-based interventions have been reported to reduce stress-related overeating, emotional eating tendencies, and excessive food cravings. Therefore, mindful eating may be associated with more adaptive coping mechanisms, which could contribute to a lower likelihood of abnormal eating behaviors such as night eating syndrome, often observed in relation to eveningness chronotype [57, 58]. Indeed, our logistic regression analyses indicated that higher levels of mindful eating were associated with a 72.8% lower likelihood of night eating syndrome.

From night eating syndrome to food addiction: mindful eating as a moderator

Our study provided findings consistent with the existing literature on the association between night eating syndrome and food addiction. A positive correlation was observed between the severity of night eating syndrome symptoms and the number of food addiction symptoms. Logistic regression analyses further indicated that, with a small-to-moderate effect size, each additional food addiction symptom was associated with nearly a 25% higher likelihood of night eating syndrome.

In general, previous studies conducted across various samples have also reported significant associations between these two variables. These investigations consistently demonstrated a positive relationship, showing either a higher prevalence of food addiction among individuals with night eating syndrome or a higher prevalence of night eating syndrome among those with food addiction [59–66].

Foods consumed late at night are typically rich in carbohydrates, fats, and sugars. Indeed, one study demonstrated a positive association between the severity of night eating syndrome symptoms and the consumption of ultra-processed foods with these nutritional profiles, suggesting that such foods may trigger addictive-like eating behaviors [62, 67]. The link between night eating syndrome and food addiction can also be explained through a neurobiological pathway. Binge-eating behaviors associated with both conditions may induce plasticity within the orexin system, like mechanisms observed in substance dependence, leading to intensified orexin input to reward and arousal centers and heightened reactivity to food-related cues. This process fosters excessive motivation toward food, resulting in uncontrolled eating and addictive-like behaviors. Moreover, hyperactivation of the orexin system disrupts sleep and circadian rhythms, thereby further increasing the risk for night eating syndrome [68].

Our analyses revealed that the association between night eating syndrome and food addiction varied as a function of mindful eating. The relationship was significant at lower levels of mindful eating, whereas it diminished and became non-significant at higher levels. Food addiction triggers uncontrolled eating behaviors through cognitive processes such as intense food cravings, impulsivity, dysfunctional beliefs about food, and deficits in cognitive control. These cognitive factors shape maladaptive coping strategies, including emotional eating, thereby underscoring the importance of mindful eating [69]. As is well established, mindful eating enables individuals to recognize whether eating behaviors stem from physical hunger or from emotional and social triggers, thus fostering greater control over automatic behaviors such as overeating. In doing so, mindful awareness helps regulate precursors of addictive eating patterns, including emotional eating tendencies, as well as impulsive and habitual eating behaviors [27, 28, 70, 71]. Furthermore, individuals with low levels of mindful eating have been reported to be nearly five times more likely to develop food addiction [27]. Consistently, our logistic regression analyses indicated that higher levels of mindful eating were associated with an 84.8% lower likelihood of food addiction risk.

From eveningness to food addiction: mindful eating as a moderator

The circadian misalignment associated with an evening chronotype increases reward sensitivity and impulsivity, thereby elevating individuals’ likelihood of engaging in risky behaviors. This, in turn, may contribute to greater susceptibility to dependence on substances such as caffeine, nicotine, and alcohol, as well as to addictive responses to specific foods [22]. In addition to circadian disruption, the evening chronotype is also characterized by dysregulation in the secretion of hormones critical to appetite and metabolism—such as melatonin, cortisol, ghrelin, and leptin. Such hormonal imbalances can generally lead to increased appetite and cravings for high-calorie, sugary, and fatty foods. Moreover, the sleep deprivation and mood disturbances often accompanying eveningness may impair cognitive functions such as impulse control and alter the brain’s reward system, rendering high-calorie foods more appealing and harder to resist [1, 65, 72]. Supporting this, a study examining hedonic hunger from the perspective of environmental cues and psychological motives linked to palatable food intake showed that individuals with an evening chronotype reported higher motivation to consume palatable foods and beverages compared to other chronotypes [73]. This relationship can also be explained through the “Circadian–Metabolic–Hedonic Network” framework. This approach suggests that disruptions in the timing of food intake (e.g., social jetlag, shift work, night eating) may create a predisposition for addiction-like eating behaviors. Within this framework, misalignments in biological rhythms interact with metabolic processes to enhance hedonic hunger, thereby increasing individuals’ susceptibility to high-calorie, highly rewarding foods [74]. Overall, these factors appear to be linked to greater vulnerability to food addiction in individuals with an evening chronotype.

This study provides limited evidence regarding the association between chronotype and food addiction. No significant correlation was found between these two variables. Regression analyses indicated that eveningness chronotype was neither a significant factor for food addiction risk nor a significant predictor of the number of food addiction symptoms. However, mediation pathway analyses demonstrated that this association operated only indirectly through night eating syndrome; in other words, eveningness did not show a direct effect on food addiction but was indirectly associated with it through night eating syndrome. Furthermore, the analyses showed that mindful eating did not moderate the relationship between eveningness and food addiction.

A study yielding results like ours reported that the number of YFAS symptoms did not differ significantly across chronotype groups, and multivariate linear regression analyses further indicated that chronotype was not a significant predictor of food addiction symptoms. Notably, this study, like ours, recruited overweight and obese individuals who were already attending diet intervention centers [75]. In contrast, another study found that individuals with an evening chronotype exhibited higher levels of addiction-like eating behaviors compared to those with morning or intermediate chronotypes; however, that study was conducted among university students and employed a different scale to assess food addiction [19]. Moreover, in the literature, studies using the YFAS have reported mixed findings—some analyses revealed significant associations, whereas others did not, with most of these studies also conducted in student populations. One such study identified a significant association between a higher number of food addiction symptoms and a stronger eveningness preference, yet logistic regression analyses showed that circadian preference was not a significant risk factor for food addiction. In that study, eveningness was reported to show an indirect relationship with food addiction via mediators such as sleep disturbances and impulsivity [76]. Another study found that individuals with food addiction had lower MEQc scores, indicating a greater eveningness tendency, but the mediation analyses again showed that the direct effect of chronotype on food addiction was nonsignificant. In this case, indirect effects through sleep disturbances and impulsivity were also nonsignificant, while a significant indirect effect emerged through symptoms of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder [77]. Yet another study found a significant association between these two variables; however, further multivariate analyses indicated that chronotype score was not a significant predictor of the number of food addiction symptoms [22].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. By employing correlation, regression, mediation, and moderation analyses together, it comprehensively examined the interaction between circadian rhythm, night eating syndrome, mindful eating, and food addiction. The use of validated measurement tools enhanced methodological reliability, while the relatively large sample size (n = 490) provided sufficient statistical power. Moreover, the simultaneous evaluation of direct, indirect, and conditional pathways offered a novel perspective to the literature, particularly by highlighting the potential moderating role of mindful eating in the relationship with food addiction.

Nevertheless, several limitations should also be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the reliance on self-report measures may introduce recall bias and social desirability effects. Since the sample largely consisted of young adults and women, the generalizability of the findings to broader populations may be limited. Additionally, the absence of exclusion criteria for individuals with shift work, irregular sleep patterns, or other factors that could markedly disrupt circadian rhythms may have introduced potential confounding effects in the relationships among the variables. Although the mediation analyses revealed significant indirect effects, longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to confirm these relationships. Additionally, the absence of preregistration highlights the exploratory nature of some findings and underscores the need for future preregistered studies with stricter adjustments for multiple testing.

Implications for clinical and research practice

The findings of this study provide important implications for both clinical practice and future research. From a clinical perspective, it is crucial to consider individuals’ circadian rhythm characteristics and eating behavior patterns when assessing vulnerability to food addiction. Individuals with an evening chronotype appeared to exhibit greater night eating syndrome tendencies and lower levels of mindful eating, pointing to a potential behavioral risk profile. Accordingly, interventions that foster mindful eating and align dietary counseling with biological rhythms may hold promise for enhancing treatment effectiveness, particularly among populations at risk of obesity.

From a research perspective, these findings underscore the need for more rigorous study designs to clarify the observed associations. Although the present mediation analyses demonstrated significant relationships, the cross-sectional design and lack of preregistration suggest that these results should be interpreted as exploratory. Longitudinal and experimental studies are required to establish causal mechanisms and to test whether mindful eating interventions can effectively reduce the risk of food addiction.

Conclusion

The findings suggest that individuals with an evening chronotype may have higher night eating syndrome scores, which could be associated with food addiction symptoms. However, no direct association was observed between eveningness and food addiction, and mindful eating did not play a moderating role in this relationship. Nevertheless, the association between night eating syndrome and food addiction appeared to vary depending on levels of mindful eating; specifically, lower levels of mindful eating were linked to a stronger association. In addition, higher levels of mindful eating were associated with fewer food addiction symptoms.
