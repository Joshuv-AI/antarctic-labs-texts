Lithium alleviates paralysis in experimental autoimmune neuritis in Lewis rats by modulating glycogen synthase kinase-3β activity
Authors: Meejung Ahn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ImportanceGuillain-Barré syndrome (GBS)-like neuropathy mimics the leading cause of sporadic acute nontraumatic limb paralysis in individuals from developed countries. Experimental autoimmune neuritis (EAN) is an animal model of GBS and of syndromes such as acute canine polyradiculoneuritis, seen in dogs and cats.ObjectiveThe involvement of glycogen synthase kinase (GSK)-3β, a pro-inflammatory molecule, in rat EAN is not fully understood. This study evaluated the potential role of GSK-3β in EAN through its inhibition by lithium.MethodsLewis rats were injected with SP26 antigen to induce EAN. Lithium was administered from 1 day before immunization to day 14 post-immunization (PI). Then the rats were euthanized and their neural tissues were prepared for histological and Western blotting analyses.ResultsLithium, an inhibitor of GSK-3, significantly ameliorated EAN paralysis in rats, when administered from day 1 to day 14 PI. This corresponded with reduced inflammation in the sciatic nerves of EAN rats, where phosphorylation of GSK-3β was also upregulated, indicating suppression of GSK-3.Conclusions and RelevanceThese findings suggest that lithium, an inhibitor of GSK-3β, plays a significant role in ameliorating rat EAN paralysis, by suppressing GSK-3β and its related signals in EAN-affected sciatic nerves.

INTRODUCTION

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is an immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy characterized the acute onset of neurological symptoms that typically follow an infectious illness such as a gastrointestinal infection with Campylobacter jejuni [1]. A systematic review of published studies found that C. jejuni infection preceded GBS in 13%–72% of cases [2]. The associated limb weakness progressively worsens for up to 4 weeks before stabilizing [3]. A similar syndrome, acute canine polyradiculoneuritis (ACP), is observed in dogs and cats and must be differentiated from conditions such as infectious polyradiculoneuritis, tick paralysis, severe myasthenia gravis, snake envenomation, and botulism [456]. In addition, agricultural workers who handle poultry, swine, and cattle have been identified as having an increased risk of developing GBS [2].

Experimental autoimmune neuritis (EAN), an animal model of GBS, is a T-cell-mediated autoimmune disorder that affects the peripheral nervous system and leads to paralysis [7]. Symptoms in the rat model include weight loss, progressive ascending paralysis, macrophage, and T cell infiltration in the sciatic nerves, and spontaneous recovery [8]. The characteristic paralysis in EAN is mediated by pro-inflammatory mediators and the oxidative stress induced by inflammatory cells [89].

Glycogen synthase kinase-3 beta (GSK-3β) is a serine/threonine kinase involved in many cellular processes, including the inflammation and neurodegeneration that occur in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) [10] and multiple sclerosis [11]. It may also play a central role in bipolar disorders. GSK-3 signaling has been linked to the expression of adhesion molecules, including vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1), in cultured endothelial cells [12]. Lithium, an inhibitor of GSK-3, has been shown to enhance neuroprotective mechanisms and promote cell survival by acting on various molecular targets [1314]. It thus holds potential as a treatment for bipolar disorders, Alzheimer’s disease, and other neurodegenerative disorders characterized by dysregulated signaling pathways and neuronal damage. Thus far, the efficacy of lithium has been demonstrated in chronic EAE induced by myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein in mouse models [15], and spinal cord injury in a rat model [16].

This study evaluated the ability of lithium to modify the progression of EAN in Lewis rats through the involvement of GSK-3 in the sciatic nerves. Our results open up new avenues of research and potential treatments.

METHODS

Animals

Seven- to twelve-week-old female Lewis rats (n = 24) were maintained at our animal facility (Sprague-Dawley; Harlan, USA). The rodents were housed under standard laboratory conditions with a controlled environment, including a 12 h light/dark cycle, a temperature of 22°C ± 2°C, and a humidity maintained at 50% ± 10%. Food and water were provided ad libitum. All animal procedures followed the Sangji University Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (IACUC approval number 2021–0021).

Induction of EAN

EAN was induced in the rats as previously described [917]. Briefly, each rat received an injection in the hind footpad with an emulsion containing 100 μg SP-26, a neuritogenic peptide (amino acids 53–78 of bovine myelin P2 protein) (Shimadzu, Japan), and Freund’s complete adjuvant (CFA, Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37Ra, 5 mg/mL). Pertussis toxin (500 ng; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was administered on days 0 and 2 post-immunization (PI). The rats were observed for changes in body weight. In the clinical evaluation of EAN, seven stages were recognized: grade (G) 0, no signs; G1, floppy tail; G2, mild paraparesis; G3, severe paraparesis; G4, tetraparesis; G5, moribund state or death; and R0, recovery [917].

Lithium treatment

To evaluate the impact of lithium on EAN, the rats were divided into a normal control group, a vehicle-treated group, and a lithium-treated group (n = 8 per group). The lithium-treated group received an intraperitoneal injection of lithium chloride (100 mg/kg/day; Sigma-Aldrich) three times, starting 1 day prior to immunization, to rapidly elevate lithium levels. Subsequently, lithium carbonate (40 mg/kg/day; Sigma-Aldrich) was orally administered from day 2 PI until day 14 PI. This protocol is suitable for nontoxic lithium administration and is commonly employed to achieve serum levels of lithium comparable to those in human patients [15].

Tissue sampling

Sciatic nerve tissue samples were collected from the rats at the peak of EAN (G3, day 13–15 PI) and during the recovery phase (R0, day 30 PI) under CO2 inhalation (Fig. 1, arrows). Each time point included nine samples. Normal and CFA-immunized rats served as the control groups. The tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4 and then embedded in paraffin wax. Sections of the paraffin-embedded tissues, cut at a thickness of 5 μm using a rotary microtome (Leica, Germany), were stained with hematoxylin-eosin to evaluate inflammation. In addition, sciatic nerves were excised and frozen at −70°C for subsequent protein analysis by Western blotting.

Antibodies

The immunohistochemical analysis of sciatic nerves was performed using monoclonal rabbit anti-phospho-GSK-3β (Ser9) (p-GSK-3β) and monoclonal rabbit anti-GSK-3β antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, USA). PhosphoPlus Akt (Ser473) antibody kits (Cell Signaling Technology) were utilized as well. A mouse monoclonal anti-β-actin antibody (Sigma-Aldrich) was used to detect β-actin.

Western blotting analysis

The samples were prepared for protein analysis as follows. The tissues were minced, homogenized, and suspended in TNN lysis buffer (40 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.4], 120 mM NaCl, and 0.1% Nonidet P-40) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail. In all, 60 µg protein (in 20 µL) was loaded onto an 8% polyacrylamide gel and subjected to sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Immunoreactive bands were detected by Western blotting, using horseradish-peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit antibody (Vector, USA) and enhanced chemiluminescence (Amersham, USA).

After immunodetection, the bands were re-probed to ensure equal loading and the amount of protein was normalized using beta-actin as the housekeeping gene. The optical density (OD) per mm2 of each band was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

In the statistical analysis, the groups (n = 5) were compared using a post hoc Student-Newman-Keuls test for multiple comparisons. The data are presented as the mean ± standard error, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunostaining was conducted following established protocols [17]. Paraffin-embedded sections 5 µm thick were deparaffinized, blocked with hydrogen peroxide, and then incubated with primary antibodies, including monoclonal rabbit anti-p-GSK-3β (Cell Signaling Technology).

For visualization, immunoreactions were developed using an avidin–biotin–peroxidase complex Elite kit (Vector). Peroxidase activity was visualized using a diaminobenzidine substrate kit (Vector). The stained sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted for analysis.

RESULTS

Clinical observation and histopathological evaluation of EAN-affected rats

In the EAN model using SP26 peptides, rats exhibited distinct disease progression and recovery. Floppy tails (G1) developed by day 10 PI, followed by progressive hindlimb paralysis (G3) occurring between days 12 and 16 PI. Most rats demonstrated coordinated walking by day 30 PI (R0), indicating functional recovery (Fig. 1A).

Histological analysis of sciatic nerve samples revealed differences between normal controls and EAN rats. In controls, few inflammatory cells were seen in the sciatic nerves whereas in the EAN rats there was a significant infiltration of inflammatory cells (Fig. 1). By day 30 PI, the number of infiltrating cells had decreased, suggesting the resolution of inflammation during the recovery phase of EAN.

Lithium-mediated behavioral changes in EAN-affected rats

To examine whether lithium ameliorates EAN, lithium was administered to rats from 1 day prior to immunization to day 14 PI (Fig. 2A). In the treated group, the clinical severity of EAN was significantly reduced compared to vehicle treatment from day 11 PI and continued until day 21 PI (Fig. 2B). The treatment significantly reduced the incidence of paralysis in SP26-immunized rats by 25% compared to vehicle-treated EAN rats, which had a 100% incidence rate (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). The onset of EAN paralysis in lithium-treated rats was not significantly delayed compared to that in vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 2D). However, the duration of EAN paralysis was significantly shorter in lithium-treated rats (10.1 ± 0.73 days) than in the vehicle-treated controls (3.8 ± 0.85 days) (Fig. 2E). This, along with the reduced peak score of EAN paralysis in the treated group, clearly demonstrates the superiority of lithium treatment in the EAN model. Furthermore, the peak score of EAN paralysis (2.5 ± 0.15) in the treated rats was significantly lower than that of vehicle-treated controls (0.7 ± 0.17) (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2F).

Evaluation of lithium’s effects on microscopic parameters

Histopathological examination revealed distinct findings in the sciatic nerves of the different experimental groups (Fig. 3). No inflammatory cells were seen in the tissues of controls, indicating their normal histological appearance. In vehicle-treated EAN rats, the tissues had a substantial number of inflammatory cells, consistent with the pathology of EAN. In lithium-treated rats, the inflammatory lesions were significantly diminished compared to controls. This improvement correlated with the clinical signs (Fig. 2B), suggesting a positive effect of lithium on inflammation associated with EAN.

Western blotting analysis of phosphorylated Akt (p-Akt) and GSK-3 in EAN

In Western blotting, p-GSK-3β levels were lower in the EAN rats than in normal controls but were higher in the lithium-treated EAN rats. Thus, the impact of lithium treatment on GSK-3 activity in the sciatic nerves of EAN rats was evaluated in greater detail.

Specifically, GSK-3β phosphorylation and upstream Akt phosphorylation were examined by Western blotting analysis, with five rats per group (Fig. 4). At 13 days PI, the level of p-Akt in the sciatic nerves was significantly elevated in the lithium group (relative OD: 0.84 ± 0.04-fold increase vs. a 0.62 ± 0.06-fold increase in the vehicle-treated group; p < 0.05) (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, p-GSK-3β levels significantly decreased in vehicle-treated EAN rats (0.66 ± 0.002-fold increase) compared to normal controls (1.00 ± 0.01-fold increase; p < 0.05). However, lithium treatment resulted in an increase in p-GSK-3β expression (0.90 ± 0.001-fold increase; p < 0.05) compared to the vehicle-treated group (Fig. 4B).

DISCUSSION

This study is the first to demonstrate that lithium ameliorates the behavioral paralysis seen in EAN rats. The amelioration was partly associated with the activation of GSK-3 in the sciatic nerves, similar to findings in autoimmune disease models such as EAE [10] and spinal cord injury [16]. As in EAE models, our results in EAN rats suggest that lithium inhibits the migration of inflammatory cells into the sciatic nerves by suppressing the expression of adhesion molecules such as VCAM-1 [10].

In a previous study [6], 23% of canine and 59% of feline nerve biopsies submitted by veterinary neurologists showed signs of immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy. In this syndrome, which commonly affects dogs and cats, acute-onset paraparesis rapidly progresses to non-ambulatory lower motor neuron tetraparesis or tetraplegia [18]. Similar to GBS, ACP is caused by molecular mimicry [19], as an external immunogen triggers the immune system to target endogenous epitopes. The affected structures express proteins or glycolipids that are components of the myelin sheath or axonal membrane [19].

Previous studies have identified the mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) ERK [9], JNK [20], and p-38 [21] in EAN lesions. Because GSK-3 is a member of the MAPK family, it may play a significant role in the pathogenesis of EAN. Furthermore, the signal cascade of GSK-3β interacts with the PI3K-Akt pathway [22], the activity of which was reduced in the lithium-treated group.

GSK-3β plays a critical role in cellular functions such as cell proliferation, DNA repair, cell cycle regulation, signaling, and metabolic pathways [23]. It is associated with a range of diseases, including inflammation [24], neurodegenerative disorders [25], diabetes, and cancer. Given its involvement in neuroinflammation, targeting GSK-3β with inhibitors is a logical approach to the treatment of peripheral autoimmune neuritis in dogs and cats, similar to its application in GBS. In this study, a rat EAN model was employed to investigate the mechanism of GSK-3β activation and thus its potential as a therapeutic target.

GSK-3β, a key kinase in the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, is constitutively active and has been implicated in neuroinflammation and the formation of neurofibrillary tangles [26]. It is inactivated by the phosphorylation of Ser9 in its N-terminal domain, which disrupts cell signaling pathways such as the Akt and beta-catenin pathways [27]. In this study, although we did not specifically examine the beta-catenin pathway, which is downstream of GSK-3β, the lithium-induced activation of GSK-3β in EAE, an autoimmune disease of the central nervous system [16], may be alleviated through beta-catenin activation. Activated GSK-3β enhances microglial migration and inflammatory responses by promoting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines through nuclear factor kappa B [28] and nitric oxide [29] in microglia [30]. In addition, activated GSK-3β enhances the phosphorylation of tau protein [31]. GSK-3β is rendered inactive when phosphorylated at Ser9 by activated Akt. Studies of postmortem brain samples from Alzheimer’s disease patients showed reduced levels of PI3K subunits (p85 and p110) and decreased phosphorylation of Akt at Ser473 and GSK-3β at Ser9. These reductions are associated with the progression of Alzheimer’s and other neurodegenerative diseases, consistent with a crucial role of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway in these conditions [32].

Considering all factors, it is hypothesized that lithium ameliorates EAN paralysis, possibly through the suppression of GSK-3 signal cascades, as shown in the prototype autoimmune disease model EAE [1015]. Furthermore, this study demonstrated the potential of lithium as a candidate therapeutic agent for autoimmune diseases of the peripheral nervous system in dogs and cats, based on its ability to alleviate neuroinflammation through the activation of GSK-3β.
