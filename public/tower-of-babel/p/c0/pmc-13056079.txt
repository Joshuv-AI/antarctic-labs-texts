Non-ergodicity in ecology and evolution
Authors: Teemu Kuosmanen, Alexandre Minetto, Ville Mustonen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe effects of stochasticity can be averaged in two ways: over a population of individuals and over time along individual trajectories. If the encountered states and collected payoffs along any given trajectory eventually match the expected statistics of the population, the generating process satisfies the mathematical property of ergodicity, which is a key assumption for many models of complex systems. However, in reality, individuals can suffer from unrecoverable losses and have differential chances of reaching states that are statistically available, which can lead to non-ergodicity. Here, we characterize analytically how non-ergodicity arises in fundamental ecological growth processes and argue that the foundations of eco-evolutionary theory ought to be derived with non-ergodicity in mind.

Different types and levels of stochasticity invariably affect biological systems on all scales, from the molecular biology of a single cell to the ecology and evolution of populations, species, and ecosystems. Consequently, for organisms to function reliably, they must realize adaptation strategies that allow for robust regulatory control against environmental perturbations (1). Demographic and environmental stochasticity can cause extinction of populations (2, 3) as well as shape the evolution of growth strategies (4), including reversing deterministic selection (5), reducing population turnover (6), and encouraging bet-hedging (7, 8). Rapidly evolving cell populations can also leverage stochasticity to adapt to stressful environments by increasing their mutation rate (9–11), an example of antifragility (12). Indeed, it seems that stochasticity is not just “noise” that living systems must overcome, but rather an essential feature of the operating algorithm of both cellular function and evolution itself (13, 14).

The standard way to deal with stochasticity generally involves averaging over an ensemble of repeated experiments in some way. Mathematically, the most tractable approach is often to focus on expected outcomes, defined as the average over infinitely many independent stochastic realizations, a quantity that paradigmatically serves as the object of maximization in various fields of science, including statistics, machine learning, economics, and decision-theory. However, it is well known that such mean statistics need not accurately reflect the observed typical behavior in any finite sample of outcomes. Indeed, the stochasticity related to any particular trajectory can be safely “averaged out” only in the case where the underlying system satisfies the property of ergodicity (15, 16), which guarantees that all trajectories will eventually be statistically equivalent.

While the ergodic assumption has been arguably useful in the context of thermodynamics and other effective physical theories, more complex systems often exhibit ergodicity breaking where the typical behavior of individual trajectories deviates from the ensemble average. A canonical example of non-ergodicity is given by geometric Brownian motion (GBM), commonly employed for modeling asset prices (17), where the ensemble average grows exponentially indefinitely even though every trajectory is guaranteed to go ultimately extinct due to the presence of multiplicative noise (18). More recently, the idea of ergodicity breaking and its potential implications has been discussed in many other areas ranging from economics (19), human decision-making (20), medical and behavioral sciences (15, 21, 22), reinforcement learning (23), cell transport (24, 25), movement ecology (26, 27), and also evolution (28–30).

As part of the difficult task of defining the suitable fitness proxy in different types of models, eco-evolutionary theory is in principle familiar with the different types of averages, and the important distinction between arithmetic and geometric growth rates, which underlies the ergodicity breaking in GBM. However, we believe that the more general concept of non-ergodicity remains unfamiliar in the biological context even though ergodicity is commonly assumed, either explicitly (e.g., refs. 31–33) or implicitly (e.g., refs. 34–36). Here, we go beyond the much-studied GBM model and show how non-ergodicity arises in three distinct biologically motivated case-studies. By carefully distinguishing between typical versus average behavior described by the different ways of averaging, we demonstrate how ergodicity and the closely related concept of self-averaging (37, 38) are directly linked to fundamental eco-evolutionary processes.

Results

Consider a stochastic growth process x from which one can obtain sample trajectories (xi(0),...,xi(t)) as a function of time t. The asymptotic growth rate (or long-term average geometric growth rate)[1]Λ¯i=limt→∞1tlog(xi(t)xi(0))

is one of the most widely used fitness concepts for simple growth processes. We use the overbar to emphasize that it is in fact a time-average of the logarithmic growth rate. However, if the process is stochastic, Λ¯i can also become stochastic, which motivates us to define Λ¯ formally as the average over the eventually realized outcomes[2]Λ¯=〈limt→∞1tlog(x(t)x(0))〉,

where, critically, the time limit is taken first before averaging. One of its key desirable properties is that a strain (or strategy) maximizing Λ¯ can be seen as the evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS) in the sense that it will almost surely outnumber any other strategy in the long run (36). However, computing the time limit is generally mathematically untractable; it is often much simpler to compute the ensemble average first[3]Λ(t)=〈1tlog(x(t)x(0))〉=1t∫0∞log(xx0)p(x,t)dx,

where p(x,t) is the solution (probability distribution) of the process, and only then investigate convergence to a theoretical value of Λ in the limit of t→∞. For example, for GBM defined by the Itô stochastic differential equation (SDE)[4]dxt=μxtdt+σxtdWt,

where Wt is the standard Wiener process, it is straightforward to compute that Λ=μ−σ2/2, which can be recognized as the geometric growth rate of the process (18).

By exchanging the order of the limit operations, Λ can be seen as the ergodic approximation of the true fitness Λ¯: if the process is ergodic, one can safely exchange the order of the limit operations such that Λ=Λ¯, but generally, if the process is non-ergodic, this is not guaranteed. Biologically, the question of (non-)ergodicity is then actually related to estimation: what is the possible error that we make if we try to predict the true empirical fitness with the theoretical value Λ obtained by assuming ergodicity?

This motivates us to investigate the mean squared error[5]MSE(t)=E[(g(t)−Λ(t))2]

as a function of time using the estimator gi(t)=1tlog(xi(t)xi(0)) but this requires being able to compute Λ analytically. Using the variance–bias relationship, we can though decompose the MSE as[6]MSE(t)=Var(g(t))+E[g(t)−Λ(t)]2,

where the first term is the variance of the estimator and the latter the bias squared. Now, if one uses the ergodic approximation directly from Eq. 3, the bias term vanishes, and we can quantify the MSE by evaluating the variance term empirically as[7]Ω(t)=1L∑i=1L(gi(t)−⟨g(t)⟩L)2

where ⟨·⟩L denotes the ensemble average of L independent empirical realizations. We note that because g is in fact a time-average of the per capita growth rate, Eq. 7 becomes an effective metric of non-ergodicity known as the Thirumalai-Mountain (TM) metric (39) measuring the discrepancy between time and ensemble averages. Intuitively, if the process is ergodic, every trajectory i will eventually be indistinguishable from the ensemble average, and thus Ω measures the degree of self-averaging, with ergodic processes having the property that Ω(t)→0 with t→∞. It then follows that if the process is non-ergodic in this sense, even an unbiased estimator will make an error that cannot be removed even in the limit of infinite data.

In what follows, we use the TM metric to systematically quantify non-ergodicity in two analytically tractable models for demographic and environmental stochasticity. By carefully distinguishing between the ensemble average and the typical outcome, we show how the two different ways of averaging stochasticity can both be biologically meaningful and appropriate descriptors in different situations. We then consider more complicated models with ecological feedback and population structure and show how non-ergodicity and non-self-averaging can arise in eco-evolutionary dynamics.

Ergodicity Breaking from Extinction Boundary.

Although a useful starting point, GBM [4] is not directly applicable to model the growth of biological populations, because the signal-to-noise ratio in the GBM remains constant, while it is widely established that demographic stochasticity should average out in large populations. But in what sense is this precisely true?

When the population dynamics is derived explicitly from the underlying birth and death rates (6, 40, 41), the following demographic stochasticity model (DSM) is recovered[8]dxt=rxtdt+λxtdWt

where xt is the population size, r:=β−δ gives the expected per capita growth rate (difference of the birth and death rates), and the demographic stochasticity scales with the square root of the population size and the population turnover rate λ:=β+δ (see ref. 6). In the case where the birth and death rates remain constant, this equation reduces to Fellerian diffusion (42) which has a closed-form solution for the probability density p(x,t) with the absorbing boundary set at x=0 to account for stochastic extinction (see Materials and Methods for more details).

We can now compute Λ for this process from Eq. 3 and taking the limit t→∞ which yields[9]Λ=r(1−exp(−2rλx0))=rq,

where q can be interpreted as the ultimate establishment probability (Materials and Methods). Intriguingly, this result shows how the asymptotic growth rate Λ now explicitly depends on the initial condition x0, which can be considered a hallmark of non-ergodicity (see also ref. 43 where this was empirically observed). We verify this using stochastic simulations (Fig. 1B), which confirm that there exists a critical population size, which depends on the growth parameters r and λ, below which Λ is sensitive to the initial condition while above it Λ remains close to the ensemble average r. This is in sharp contrast to GBM, where the asymptotic growth rate does not depend on the initial condition at all.

We then further compared DSM with GBM by computing the bias and variance of the Λ-estimator. While the bias decays quickly for both processes, Fig. 1C shows that the variance in DSM is higher than in GBM and converges to a positive value unlike in GBM where it continues to decay. Thus, DSM is actually even more non-ergodic than GBM as measured by the TM metric [7]. If one conditions the process [8] with nonextinction, the variance decays faster than in GBM, implying that the non-ergodicity arises mainly due to the presence of the extinction boundary. However, this is a biologically important property of the model, because all mutations must arise from a single individual, which has a significant risk of stochastic extinction (44).

To characterize the apparent stochastic extinction boundary more precisely, we turn to analyze the log-transformed process [10]dlogxt=(r−λ2xt)dt−λxtdWt

which can be obtained by applying Itô’s Lemma to Eq. 8. We notice that the deterministic part of Eq. 10 explicitly depends on the current population size such that it is initially negative and switches to become positive at the critical size[11]x∗=λ2r.

We call this the extinction threshold, which allows one to better understand the early stochastic phase of growth that the ensemble average is hiding. Namely, if the population size falls below this value, its expected log-fitness becomes negative and the typical trajectory goes extinct despite having a positive deterministic growth rate. We emphasize that this threshold is not “hard” in the sense of an Allee threshold, where the deterministic growth rate becomes negative, but it only describes the fate of a typical trajectory (see also ref. 45).

We further note that if we condition the process on nonextinction, the ensemble average can be written as[12]⟨x(t)|x(t)>0⟩=∫0∞p(x,t)q(t)xdx=x01−q(t)exp(rt),

and when extrapolated backward in time, it appears as if the exponential growth had started from the initial size[13]x01−q=x01−exp(−x0/x∗)≈x∗,

that is, the extinction threshold is in fact the first-order approximation for the effective onset of the exponential growth (Fig. 1A).

This concept can be readily generalized to more complicated situations. Consider now a mutant-resident model where two types x1 and x2 are competing following their own dynamics according to Eq. 8. Kuosmanen et al. (6) show that the mutant frequency p1=x1/(x1+x2) then follows the SDE [14]dp1=(s−ϕ)p1(1−p1)dt+p1(1−p1)(λ1N−p1ϕ)dW˜t,

where s=r1−r2 is the selection coefficient (difference of the per capita growth rates), the total population size N=x1+x2 grows approximately deterministically with the mean fitness r¯=r1p1+r2p2, and ϕ=1N(λ1−λ2) is the so-called turnover flux which becomes negligible in large populations. Thus, the frequency dynamics reduces to the standard replicator equation in large populations. We can now similarly solve the mutant establishment threshold from the log-transformed equation of Eq. 14 as[15]p1∗=λ1N(2s−ϕ),

which gives the frequency above which the prevalence of the typical mutant under positive selection starts to grow and dominates genetic drift. Indeed, in the Wright–Fisher limit of constant turnover (λ1,λ2)→(1,1), the establishment threshold simplifies to p1∗=12Ns, which appears in the existing population genetics literature (46–48) as the approximate onset of the exponential growth phase where the strength of deterministic selection overcomes the stochastic forces of genetic drift.

Here we extend these observations by showing how the establishment threshold depends on the turnover rates and has a natural interpretation by describing the typical mutant dynamics (Fig. 2). If the mutant population starts to grow below the threshold, the typical mutant trajectory goes extinct since the expected log-frequency change is negative. However, if the mutant manages to temporarily cross this threshold by aid of stochastic fluctuations, this prediction is updated, and the fate of a typical mutant is to establish as part of the population, and thus avoid immediate extinction. Single trajectories can naturally cross the boundary multiple times, and each time the qualitative prediction is updated accordingly.

Fitness Fluctuations in Non-ergodic Environments.

We next consider environmental stochasticity where the birth and death rates themselves, and not just the timing of individual events, can fluctuate. Because of this, environmental stochasticity can affect also large populations (3). However, it is commonly assumed that the environmental process generating the vital rates is both stationary and ergodic (32, 33, 35).

Mathematically, we can model environmental stochasticity with a system of SDEs, where the environment also has some dynamics, which in real systems typically also depends on the organismal impact leading to eco-evolutionary feedback:[16]dxt=r(Et)xtdt+λ(Et)xtdWt1dEt=f(Et,xt)dt+h(Et,xt)dWt2.

As we want to focus specifically on the effects of environmental stochasticity, we ignore demographic stochasticity for now and model the population growth as a deterministic process[17]xi(t)=xi(0)exp(∫0tr(Esi)ds),

which simplifies the long-term growth rate [1] to[18]Λ¯i=limt→∞1t∫0tr(Esi)ds

where the growth rate still depends on the particular sequence of environmental states E0i,...,Eti of the stochastic process dEt. Now, assuming that the environmental process is ergodic, we can replace this time-average with an average over the probability space P(E), and get the following Λ-estimator:[19]Λ=E[r(E)]=∫−∞∞P(E)r(E)dE.

The essence of the above result is that it allows one to change an explicitly open time problem to an integral over the space of environments with the important consequence that the environment, if ergodic, can be treated as a confounder that can be simply averaged over to determine the fitness. For example, in the simpler case where the sequence of environments are independent and identically distributed, the law of large numbers can be applied to show that Λ¯i→Λ (36). But how good is the ergodic assumption for biological systems? In particular, how should the probability distribution P(E) be interpreted if it becomes coupled with the organismal impact (e.g., as in Eq. 16)? And how can fitness be defined for non-ergodic environments?

As a simple analytically tractable case study, let us begin by considering a family of Gaussian fitness functions of the form[20]r(E)=rmaxexp(−12(E−Eopt)2)−δ0

and assuming that the environment follows the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process (for previous modeling, see refs. 40 and 49)[21]dEt=θ(μ−Et)dt+σdWt

which is the only Markovian process admitting a Gaussian stationary distribution. Characteristic feature of the process [21] is that it is mean-reverting, but the environment becomes autocorrelated with states Es and Et having a covariance σ22θexp(−θ|t−s|) (50).

We can compute the long-term growth rate for this case analytically from Eq. 19 as (see analysis notebooks for more details)[22]Λ=rmax1+σ22θexp(−12(μ−Eopt)21+σ22θ)−δ0

where rmax is the highest achievable birth rate at the environmental optimum Eopt, 12(μ−Eopt)2 is the expected distance from the environmental optimum, σ is the diffusion constant of the environment, δ0 is the background death rate, and θ sets the characteristic timescale for the environment to mean revert and the autocorrelation time as τ=1/θ. We notice that Λ depends on the environmental parameters θ and σ only in combination v=σ22θ=τσ2/2, which can be recognized as the variance of the environmental signal v=var(Et)=cov(Et,Et).

Fig. 3 shows example trajectories and demonstrates how such autocorrelated environmental stochasticity introduces temporary non-ergodicity before the process eventually self-averages at a characteristic timescale set by the parameter θ. We note that even if the process is strictly speaking ergodic in the long run, it remains important to verify the relevant timescale after which the ergodic approximation becomes valid (51). For example, for a strongly autocorrelated process, the bias and variance of the ergodic approximation decay very slowly (Fig. 3).

In reality, however, the environmental process is likely to be much more complicated (52, 53) such that the environmental statistics along trajectories need not match the ensemble statistics. As an example of how organismal impact can cause non-ergodicity, consider two different genotypes α={1,2} which attempt to stabilize the environment to their respective fitness optima:[23]dxtα=[rmaxαexp(−12(E−Eoptα)2)−δ0α]xtαdt+λαxtαdWtαdEt=[∑αfαxtα(Eoptα−Et)+θ(μ−Et)]dt+σdWt. Fig. 4, demonstrates a case where the two genotypes drive the environment in opposite directions, and thus the environmental trajectory becomes coupled to the evolutionary dynamics. Consequently, the ensemble distribution P(E) becomes bimodal, but each trajectory will mainly sample around the other mode depending on the state of the population. As a result of this eco-evolutionary feedback, which opposes the self-averaging of the environment, we observe clear ergodicity breaking, where the TM-metric continues to grow (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, by computing Λ numerically from the empirical distribution P^(E) of simulated environmental trajectories and computing the resulting estimation error (RMSE) (see Materials and Methods for more details), we notice that the Λ-estimator can now also become biased.

Non-Self-Averaging in Eco-evolutionary Dynamics.

In previous case study, we showed how eco-evolutionary feedback can lead to non-ergodicity because the selective environment lacks the property of self-averaging. Next, we consider the broader implications of such non-self-averaging by considering evolution in a metapopulation structure where both the ensemble average and typical subpopulation average have a clear biological meaning.

A metapopulation describes independent parallel population dynamics within spatially separated subpopulations connected by a possibility of dispersal events. Metapopulation structures arise naturally at different scales ranging from habitable island patches to entire planetary ecosystems. A characteristic feature of a metapopulation is the possible extinction of local subpopulations and the presence of empty patches, which can be critical for the overall success of the metapopulation by allowing reinvasion by later dispersal (54). As we will next show, this mechanism can lead to non-self-averaging where the ensemble frequency in the entire metapopulation can diverge both quantitatively and qualitatively from the typical dynamics within any subpopulation.

Consider the well-known public good game (55), which provides a classical model for the evolution of cooperation, but now in a metapopulation context, where each of the m=1,...,M subpopulations follow their own local public good dynamics. We consider the evolutionary dynamics of two genotypes, the cooperators (C) which contribute to the production of a public good at a fitness cost, and defectors (D) which do not, but nevertheless can enjoy access to the local public good, which is distributed equally to all the members of the subpopulation. This leads to frequency-dependent growth rates:[24]rmD=bxmCnm−δ=bpmC−δrmC=rmD−c=bpmC−c−δ,

where rmD and rmC are the respective growth rates of defectors and cooperators in subpopulation m with cooperator frequency pmC=xmCnm, where xmC is the absolute population size of cooperators, nm=xmC+xmD is the total population size of patch m, b is the growth-benefit of the public good, c is the individual-level cost of producing the public good, and δ is the death rate which determines the minimum amount of public good production that allows the subpopulation to persist.

This leads to the following deterministic dynamics for the cooperator frequency pm and total population size nm of subpopulation m:[25]dpmCdt=−c(1−pmC)pmCdnmdt=((b−c)pmC−δ)nm.

Thus, without invoking any other mechanisms that could maintain cooperation, the typical cooperator frequency will decrease according to the standard replicator equation in every subpopulation, which will eventually lead to the extinction of that subpopulation. But what ultimately matters for evolution is the metapopulation frequency of cooperators pC=∑mpmCnm∑mnm, which critically must be computed as a weighted average of the subpopulation frequencies to give each individual equal weight. By simulating the public good dynamics with varying fitness costs c and metapopulation sizes M, and including the possibility for dispersal and mutation (see Materials and Methods for more details), we show how cooperation can be maintained in the metapopulation despite a significant fitness cost solely by virtue of the metapopulation structure (Fig. 5).

This result can be understood mathematically by deriving a generalized replicator dynamics for the metapopulation frequency that takes into account how the population sizes of the subpopulations, which act as weights for the subpopulation frequencies, change over time. Let us denote pα=∑mpmαnm∑mnm=∑mpmαnmN the metapopulation frequency of type α, where N is now the total population size of the metapopulation. We get that[26]dpαdt=∑mdpmαdtnmN+pmαddt(nmN)=∑m(rmα−r¯m)pmαnmN⏟“standard selection”+(r¯m−r¯)pmαnmN⏟“group selection”=(r¯α−r¯)pα

where we defined r¯=∑mr¯mnm∑mnm as the mean growth rate of the metapopulation, and r¯α=∑mrmαxmα∑mxmα as the average growth rate of type α (C or D) weighted by their subpopulation sizes xmα. This result shows how also the metapopulation frequency follows a more general form of replicator dynamics, which can be formally decomposed to standard selection acting independently at each subpopulation, and “group selection” acting at the level of absolute fitness between the groups (for full derivation including mutation and dispersal, see text in SI Appendix). Thus, even if the frequency of cooperators decreases in most subpopulations, the total metapopulation frequency of cooperators can still increase because highly cooperating subpopulations tend to outgrow defector-dominated subpopulations. This curious phenomenon represents a well-known example of Simpson’s paradox, which has also been empirically observed (56).

We note that when the more general result [26] is applied to the public good dynamics [25], the condition for positive selection of cooperators can be written equivalently as (see text in SI Appendix)[27]dpCdt>0⟺qC−pC1−pC⏟“relatedness”>cb,

where qC:=∑mpmCxmC∑mxmC can be interpreted as the conditional probability of finding another cooperator in the same subpopulation. This can readily be interpreted as Hamilton’s rule, because the left-hand side measures a type of relatedness, that is, the excess probability of sharing the cooperating genotype above the population average pC. However, in contrast to the generalized replicator dynamics from which it was derived, the Hamilton’s rule only gives the correct sign of selection and it cannot be used to integrate the full dynamics correctly. For example, one could expect based on Hamilton’s rule alone that increasing the fitness cost of public good production would decrease the level of cooperation. Remarkably, this is not what happens in large enough metapopulations, which instead show surprisingly robust cooperation despite a significant fitness cost (Fig. 5B). This is because the correct relatedness measure actually depends implicitly on both parameters b and c.

Fig. 5C shows that while the fraction of empty patches increases with c, the coarse-grained state probabilities of cooperating subpopulations remain quite robust when conditioned on nonextinction. Thus, the conditioned dynamics is less sensitive to the fitness cost of cooperation, but is rather determined by the absolute fitness effects. The evolution of cooperation appears paradoxical because, in any given subpopulation, it typically is so. However, when one appreciates that the ensemble behavior can deviate from typical outcomes, the evolution of cooperation can readily make sense in light of non-self-averaging. Fig. 5D shows that the evolutionary dynamics of cooperation becomes increasingly atypical with increasing fitness cost c. Taken together, these results suggest that large enough metapopulation structures can alone be sufficient to allow for evolution of cooperation.

Discussion

Ergodicity is a powerful mathematical concept that can greatly facilitate the analytical treatment of complex systems by allowing to freely exchange averaging of single trajectories over time with ensemble averages over repeated experiments. This implies that ergodic systems are conservative, path-independent, and eventually forget their initial state. In contrast, biological evolution is widely regarded as open-ended, cumulative, and thus path-dependent, dissipative, and operating in states that are increasingly atypical and far from the thermodynamical equilibrium. Yet, much of the existing eco-evolutionary theory makes use of the ergodic assumption either implicitly or explicitly, and with few notable exceptions (21, 28, 30, 57), the concept itself seems not to be widely established in this context.

Here we have attempted to demonstrate through concrete and biologically motivated case studies how ergodicity and self-averaging are directly linked to some of the most fundamental questions in eco-evolutionary theory and what the potential implications of non-ergodicity might be. First, we investigated two analytically tractable stochastic models that showcase the effects of demographic and environmental stochasticity. By computing the long-term growth rate Λ analytically using the ergodicity assumption, we showed how the extinction boundary and autocorrelated fitness fluctuations can lead to effectively non-ergodic dynamics, and characterized precisely in what sense demographic and environmental stochasticity can and cannot be averaged out. Then, we considered a more realistic model for the environment with explicit organismal impact and showed how this can lead to ergodicity breaking via ecological feedback which opposes the self-averaging of the fluctuating environment. Finally, we investigated evolution of cooperation in a metapopulation model, and showed how this classical problem can be reinterpreted in light of the closely related concept of non-self-averaging.

Even if the system of interest is strictly speaking non-ergodic, it remains important to quantify the ramifications of using the ergodic approximation to describe the underlying non-ergodic behavior. On the other hand, even if ergodicity is restored in the stationary limit, the temporary non-ergodicity in the nonstationary phase can have biologically important consequences as well. We approached this by systematically investigating how the bias and variance of the Λ-estimator behave as a function of time and ensemble size. In more complicated cases, where the process cannot be described analytically, ergodicity can be treated as a statistical phenomenon, which can be inferred from time series data to which end several different estimators have been developed. Here, we used the Thirumalai-Mountain effective ergodicity metric (39), which measures the degree of self-averaging over time and essentially corresponds to the (sample) variance of the estimator, which can be computed empirically even if the true mean value is not known.

Our results show that the concept of ergodicity is also closely related to typicality, which has recently been proposed as an alternative normative theory in ecology and evolution (58). By carefully distinguishing between the two ways of averaging without assuming ergodicity, new theory for the typical dynamics can be developed. We demonstrated this by developing the concept of establishment threshold, which better describes the behavior of a single typical trajectory in contrast to the ensemble average, which hides such intuitive phenomena. The existence of such a stochastically unstable equilibrium was also noticed by Lande (45) who argued that the ensemble average gives a true indication of the population’s tendency to grow only when the infinitesimal variance of the process is constant. We then showed how the typical dynamics within any given subpopulation can drastically deviate from the metapopulation dynamics where the long-term growth rate of the metapopulation can exceed the average growth rate of its constituent subpopulations. We believe that further investigations into typicality might prove to be highly useful: for example, when attempting to predict rapidly evolving pathogens, the ability to successfully establish in a typical invasion might be more predictive as opposed to anticipating the emergence of the fittest strain as the most likely adaptation (59).

Metapopulation Model.

We simulated the birth–death process [24] (with parameters b=0.085, δ=0.028) independently in each subpopulation while allowing for random dispersal events between them (with per capita rate γ=10−6 as well as mutations between the genotypes (with per capita rate μ=10−6). For numerical stability, we also implemented a local carrying capacity for each subpopulation by adding a density-dependent death rate which increases linearly with the total population size (α=10−5). Then we varied both the cost of cooperation c∈[0,0.1b] and the metapopulation size (number of subpopulations) M∈[1,25] and simulated L=100 independent realization for each parameter setting.

This produces a data tensor X with three dimensions, which can be averaged in two different ways: averaging over the entire population structure yields the metapopulation frequency as[35]pα(t)=1L∑l=1L∑m=1Mxl,mα(t)∑m=1M(xl,mC(t)+xl,mD(t))=1L∑l=1Lplα(t)

where xl,mα(t) is the absolute abundance of type α∈{C,D} at time t in subpopulation m in run l. In contrast, averaging the independently computed subpopulation frequencies yields the typical frequency as[36]p¯α(t)=1M∑m=1M1L∑l=1Lxl,mα(t)xl,mC(t)+xl,mD(t)1M∑m=1M〈p¯mα(t)〉.

Thus, for real metapopulations with M>1 and nonequal population sizes between the subpopulations (as in classical island models), the metapopulation frequency will generally differ from the average subpopulation frequency, when one is correctly computing the actual number of genotypes present in the community.
