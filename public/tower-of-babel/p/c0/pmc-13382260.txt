AVD is an effective chemotherapy backbone in first-line treatment of older patients with classical Hodgkin lymphoma
Authors: Ninja Övergaard, Kjersti Lia, Jeremia Collin, Peter H Asdahl, Per Wikman, Ingrid Glimelius, Ingemar Lagerlöf, Ann-Sofie Johansson, Lotta Hansson, Gunilla Enblad, Johan Linderoth, Christina Goldkuhl, Urban Jerlström, Alexander Fosså, Peter Kamper, Daniel Molin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•AVD in the treatment of older patients with cHL (also in elderly or frail patients) is an effective and well-tolerated regimen.•AVD seems as effective as ABVD and superior to CHOP in older patients with cHL.

Introduction

Older patients (≥60 years) diagnosed with classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL) have an inferior prognosis compared to younger. This may in part be explained by an increasing burden of comorbidity and the frailness of the aging body.1, 2, 3 It is also established that the biology of the disease takes on different characteristics compared to younger patients. The mixed cellularity subgroup is more frequent, disease is more often advanced, and to a higher degree correlated to Epstein-Barr virus.4, 5, 6

Historically, older patients with cHL were rarely included in clinical trials designed for younger individuals, presumably because of poor performance status (PS) or comorbidities. The number of prospective studies including old and frail patients with cHL have increased in recent years, but the establishment of a gold standard for treatment still remains an unmet need. A major challenge in the management of older patients is treatment-related toxicity.

Doxorubicin, bleomycin, vinblastine, and dacarbazine (ABVD) is a commonly used chemotherapy regimen in older patients with cHL, but there are pitfalls. Bleomycin-related lung toxicity (BLT) is more common in older patients, where preexisting conditions may increase the risk even further.7,8 Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor is often needed in the older patient group, and this can contribute to the development of BLT. The omission of bleomycin to mitigate the risk of BLT, especially in the older patients, has been studied and shown to be a feasible strategy.8,9 Hence, doxorubicin, vinblastine, and dacarbazine (AVD) regimen was introduced for older patients in the 2017 revised Swedish National Guidelines.

Brentuximab vedotin (BV), an antibody-drug conjugate, and programmed death 1 inhibitors have been a valuable addition to the treatment arsenal.10, 11, 12 Our aim was to evaluate the outcome in 3 Nordic countries comparing different chemotherapy backbones for older patients with cHL.

Methods

Swedish registries

It is mandatory by law to report all cancers to the Swedish National Cancer Register (NCR). Included in the NCR is diagnosis, gender, date of birth, date of diagnosis, and hospital where the diagnosis was made. On 1 January 2000, the Swedish Lymphoma Register (SLR) was launched after being commissioned by the Swedish Lymphoma Group. The SLR contains detailed information on the lymphoma diagnosis, treatment, follow-up, and recurrence. Validation of the SLR has shown a 95% agreement between patient files and registry data, and coverage compared to the NCR is reported at 89% to 95%.13 Date of death is centrally registered in the Causes of Death Register, enabling complete follow-up until December 2019. Clinical data was further collected using the LymphomaBaSe linkage, which includes data from multiple Swedish registers until 2019.

Danish registries

In Denmark, the Danish National Lymphoma Registry (LYFO) was established in 1982, initially covering only the western part of Denmark. In 1998 HL was added to the registry and on 1 January 2000, LYFO was launched nationwide. All departments of hematology in Denmark are obliged to report patients to LYFO at time of diagnosis, at the end of primary treatment, during follow-up, at recurrence and at the end of follow-up or death. The LYFO covers ∼95% of all lymphoma patients in Denmark.14 Information regarding comorbidities was collected from the Landspatientregisteret database. Date of death is centrally registered in the Danish Register of Causes of Death. It should be noted that, although patients are recorded as having received ABVD as first-line therapy, some may have been treated according to combined treatment approaches such as a response-adapted therapy in Hodgkin lymphoma (RATHL)–based strategy, involving de-escalation to AVD after the initial cycles of ABVD. As a result, some of the Danish patients may have received only limited doses of bleomycin. However, such treatment modifications are not readily identifiable in the Danish Lymphoma Registry.

Norwegian registries

The Cancer Registry of Norway was established in 1951.15 All medical doctors are obliged by law to report all cases of cancer to the register, as well as cases where cancer is suspected or cancers discovered at autopsy. Statistics Norway provides dates of death or emigration for the Norwegian population.

Patients

All Swedish patients aged ≥60 years with newly diagnosed cHL and registered in the SLR from 1 January 2000 to 30 December 2021 were included in the analysis. Danish patients aged ≥60 years, registered in LYFO with newly diagnosed cHL between 1 January 2000 and 15 April 2021 were also included. Norwegian patients aged ≥60 years diagnosed with cHL from 1 January 2000 to 30 December 2015 were identified in the Cancer Registry of Norway. Information from patient files was collected by clinicians at hospitals using a protocol with specified variables.16

Patients were not excluded due to comorbidities. All patients were observed from date of diagnosis until death, emigration, or end of follow-up. Only patients who received treatment with curative intent were included. Information on first-line therapy, age, sex, World Health Organization (WHO) PS, Ann Arbor stage, blood test results, B symptoms, International Prognostic Score (IPS), comorbidity, and recurrence were obtained. All patients with information available were categorized into 4 groups, based on the chemotherapy regimen used, ABVD, AVD, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisone (CHOP), or other.

In general, patients with cHL are treated according to national guidelines, which in Sweden were first introduced in 1985 and since then regularly updated.17,18 In Denmark and Norway national guidelines for the treatment of cHL were introduced in 1999 and 1998, respectively.

Comorbidity

In the SLR, information or grading of comorbidity is not included. This information was obtained from manual review of medical records and scored according to Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) and Cumulative Illness Rating Scale-Geriatric (CIRS-G) manuals.19, 20, 21, 22 Not all centers could provide the necessary information and, hence, comorbidity data was also collected using the LymphomaBaSe linkage where CCI was calculated. In the Danish LYFO, CCI is reported using the Landspatientregisteret. For the Norwegian patients CIRS-G was scored manually. All assessments of comorbidity only included conditions diagnosed or believed to be present prior to the onset of cHL.

For analysis purposes we chose to sort patients in 3 categories based on their CCI and/or CIRS-G score. Patients are classed as “fit” if they scored 0 to 1 on CCI or 0 to 2 on CIRS-G, “intermediate” represents a CCI-score of 2 to 3 or a CIRS-G score of 3 to 5, whereas patients categorized as “frail” scored ≥4 or ≥6 on CCI and CIRS-G, respectively. Because the cutoff between groups designated as fit or frail for these comorbidity indices vary greatly in the literature, we chose to incorporate an intermediate group and set our cutoff based on available studies using these comorbidity scales in patients with cancer.2,3,23, 24, 25, 26

Treatment for cHL in Sweden, Denmark, and Norway during 2000 to 2021

Patients aged 60 to 70 years

Swedish patients with limited stage (I-IIA) disease and no risk factor (bulky disease, erythrocyte sedimentation rate >50 mm/h or >2 involved sites) were given 2 courses of ABVD followed by radiotherapy (RT) to 30 Gy in 2 Gy fractions or, later in the period, RT to 20 Gy in 2 Gy fractions. Limited stage patients with any risk factor were treated with 4 ABVD in the beginning of the period and from 2017, 2 ABVD + 2 AVD, followed by RT to 30 Gy in 2 Gy fractions or 29.75 Gy in 1.75 Gy fractions. Patients who were at stage IB were treated according to the same principles as patients with limited stage and any risk factor, or as advanced stage (IIB-IV) disease. For patients with limited stage disease and risk factors, 4 cycles of BEACOPP baseline (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, etoposide, procarbazine, prednisone, vincristine, and bleomycin) followed by RT to 20 Gy in 2 Gy fractions was an alternative approach in the middle of the studied period.

RT was early in the period given as limited field RT (ie, modified involved field) which over time was replaced by involved site/node RT.

Patients who were at an advanced stage (IIB-IV) were recommended 6 to 8 courses of ABVD which was changed to 2 ABVD + 4 AVD from 2017.

In Denmark, ABVD-based chemotherapy has been the recommended treatment for patients with cHL aged ≥60 years during the entire period. Limited stage disease was treated with 2 to 4 ABVD and RT. Advanced stage disease was treated with a maximum of 6 (earlier in the period up to 8) ABVD. For patients with increased risk for pulmonary toxicity bleomycin could be omitted, and very frail patients were recommended CHOP or ABVD/COPP in either full or reduced dosing and/or RT that the patient would tolerate.

Norwegian patients with stage I to IIA and no risk factors received either 2 courses of ABVD or 2 courses of CHOP, depending on comorbidity and WHO PS, followed by RT to 30 Gy or, later in the period, RT to 20 Gy in 2 Gy fractions. Patients with risk factors were given 4 courses of ABVD or CHOP followed by RT to 30 Gy. Patients at stage IIB to IV were treated with 6 courses of ABVD or CHOP. AVD is mentioned as a possible alternative.

Patients aged ≥71 years

In Sweden, limited stage disease without risk factors, was in the beginning of the period treated with 2 cycles of CHOP followed by 30 Gy RT in 2 Gy fractions. This was changed to 2 AVD and RT to 20 Gy (later 30 Gy) in 2 Gy fractions in 2017. Limited stage disease with any risk factor received 4 CHOP and from 2017, 4 AVD and 30 Gy RT. Stage IB was either given 4 cycles of CHOP and 30 Gy RT or 6 cycles of CHOP and from 2017, 4 AVD with 30 Gy RT. Advanced stages were initially recommended 6 cycles of CHOP; however, some centers also used ABVD in patients aged >70 years. Since 2017, 6 AVD was recommended.

In Denmark and Norway, guidelines for patients aged ≥71 years have been identical to those stated in the paragraph concerning patients aged 60 to 70 years.

Survival analysis

Progression-free survival (PFS) was calculated from date of diagnosis to progression, relapse, or death of any cause, whichever occurred first, and overall survival (OS) from date of diagnosis to date of death of any cause. Time-to-progression was calculated from date of diagnosis to the date of relapse. Survival times were estimated using univariable Kaplan-Meier as well as univariable and multivariable Cox regression. In the univariable analysis we stratified results by different first-line treatment. In the first multivariable analysis, we adjusted for, age, sex, stage, and calendar period (model 1). In a second multivariable analysis, we also adjusted for WHO PS, and for the occurrence of B symptoms in the advanced stage group (model 2). CIRS/CCI data were incomplete, particularly in the AVD cohort, which precluded adjusting for this data in the multivariate analysis. Survival times were truncated at 5 years after diagnosis.

All calculations were performed using R 4.3.2 and the survival 3.5-7 package.

This study was approved by the Swedish National Ethics Board and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Survival analysis

For limited stage patients, OS at 3 years according to treatment regimen was 88% (95% confidence interval [CI], 84-92) for ABVD, 97% (95% CI, 91-100) for AVD, 78% (95% CI, 71-86) for CHOP, and 67% (95% CI, 54-83) in the other group (Figure 1). At 5 years, OS was 83% (95% CI, 78-87) for ABVD, 97% (95% CI, 91-100) for AVD, 69% (95% CI, 61-78) for CHOP, and 57% (95% CI, 43-74) for the other group.

Discussion

We evaluated commonly used chemotherapy regimens for first-line treatment of cHL in patients aged ≥60 years in the Nordic countries during the period 2000 to 2021, according to the recommendations in their respective national guidelines. The results indicate that AVD is an effective treatment option in this patient group with OS and PFS that are superior, or at least comparable to ABVD. CHOP tends to perform more poorly in univariate analyses, but this is not verified in multivariate analyses. Other, not recommended, regimens are less effective with a tendency toward inferior outcomes.

Patients treated with ABVD had a lower median age (66 years), better performance status and more often presented with limited stage disease (39%) compared to the other groups. Patients treated with AVD, on the other hand, had the highest median age of 74 years, and only 28% of patients presented with limited stage disease. The group treated with AVD did not differ substantially from the CHOP group or the group receiving other chemotherapy. Still, both OS and PFS for patients selected for AVD treatment was superior to those treated with ABVD and CHOP in most analyses presented and with equal outcomes in the remaining. Notably, the difference between the chemotherapy regimens became more pronounced with increasing age.

In the HD13-trial, omission of bleomycin was associated with more relapses and AVD did not reach noninferiority compared to ABVD. However in the HD13-trial, patients were predominantly younger, had more limited disease and 5-year OS was high (94%) across treatment arms.9

ABVD has, in many countries, been the gold standard for older patients with cHL. Because more knowledge emerged regarding BLT, especially in older patients, the use of ABVD has continued, but omission of bleomycin has become more common.27 The RATHL study has shown that it is feasible to exclude most of the bleomycin in a safe way for patients that respond well to first 2 cycles of ABVD and has provided information about persisting reduction of pulmonary function after omission of bleomycin.28,29 The risk of bleomycin toxicity was what prompted the Swedish Hodgkin group to introduce AVD in a structured way for the older patients.

Promising results with CHOP in older patients with cHL were demonstrated in a study from Norway with 29 patients, showing an OS and PFS at 3 years of 79% and 76%, respectively. In total, 55% of the patients in that study had presence of comorbidities.30 The outcome for the CHOP-treated patients in our study is clearly inferior to the previous Norwegian single institution experience, and due to the population-based cohort used in this investigation, we find our results reliable.

In recent years, several studies on the treatment of cHL in older patients have been published but many have included a small number of patients or have short follow-up.

AVD has been studied in combination with other agents for treatment of older patients with cHL. In the randomized ECHELON-1 trial, AVD was combined with BV (A-AVD) and 186 patients aged >60 years were included. Modified PFS at 24 months was 70.3% for A+AVD and 71.4% for ABVD. 5-year PFS was 67.1% with A+AVD and 61.6% with ABVD. A+AVD–treated patients experienced more neuropathy and neutropenia, but less pulmonary-related toxicity.10

A combination with nivolumab (N-AVD) has also been investigated. In a randomized trial this combination showed 2-year PFS for N-AVD of 92% and 83% for BV-AVD. Older patients were not analyzed separately but the oldest patient included was 83 years.11 In another study of only patients ≥60 years (n = 33 evaluable), 2 year PFS and OS were 86.2% and 96.4%, respectively.31

Combining BV with a CHOP-like backbone (B-CAP) has also been evaluated, with 1-year PFS and OS of 52.7% and 83%, respectively.26

New chemotherapy regimens without novel drugs, like PVAB (prednisone, vinblastine, doxorubicin, and bendamustine) have been introduced and could potentially serve as backbones to novel drug combinations.32 However, until these regimens are proven effective and safe in large trials or real-world cohorts, we believe our data show AVD is preferable. For patients not eligible for multiagent chemotherapy, for example BV combined with dacarbazine or nivolumab might be feasible.33

Very promising results have been seen when using BV or programmed death 1 inhibitors as a pretreatment, before AVD.34,35

The strengths of this study are its population-based data gathering, with a comparatively large number of patients included. There is relatively comprehensive information on first-line treatment and follow-up, making an extended multivariable analysis possible. The use of AVD in Sweden has been consistent since its introduction and the recommendations in general have been relatively strict in all countries, with national guidelines governing the treatment.

The retrospective nature of our study is a weakness. Furthermore, the comorbidity data is in part incomplete and not entirely consistent concerning method (CCI vs CIRS-G). The group of patients treated with AVD is relatively small and patients were mainly treated in the latest calendar period, thus this group has the shortest follow-up. To compensate for this, calendar period was included in the multivariate analysis. Furthermore, combined treatment approaches, such as RATHL-based strategies involving de-escalation from ABVD to AVD, are not thoroughly registered in available sources such as the Danish Lymphoma Registry, limiting the ability to fully capture real-world treatment adaptations.

There is a need for a standardized comorbidity assessment in studies of older patients with cHL.36,37 There is also a need for consensus concerning the cutoff for CCI and/or CIRS-G for comparability. In this study both scores were used and therefore we needed to make arbitrary cutoffs.

In conclusion, AVD is at least equal to ABVD and CHOP and superior to other chemotherapy regimens with respect to disease control but with less expected toxicity than ABVD, due to the exclusion of bleomycin. Hence, even frail older patients with cHL may tolerate treatment with curative intent. These results strongly support AVD as the preferred regimen, to continuously be explored as the backbone in treatments introducing novel agents in first line for older patients with cHL.
