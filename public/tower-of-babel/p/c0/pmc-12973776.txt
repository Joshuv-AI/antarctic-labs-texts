The experience of professionals receiving patients with manifestations of traumatic dissociation: a qualitative study
Authors: Elea Mezard, Mayssa’ El Husseini, Rahmeth Radjack, Marie Rose Moro, Sevan Minassian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Traumatic dissociation is defined as a disruption or disturbance in the integration of various cognitive functions in response to a traumatic event and has been the subject of extensive research in recent decades. For mental health professionals, exposure to patients with a history of trauma can have both negative and positive effects on their well-being. However, few studies have focused on the specific experiences of professionals encountering patients with traumatic dissociation.Method:This study analyzed the experiences of 23 professionals (psychiatrists, psychologists, and residents in psychiatry) working with patients presenting manifestations of traumatic dissociation, using Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) methodology.Results:This approach led to the identification of three key themes: (1) professionals’ reactions to patient dissociation, (2) professionals’ emotional and dissociative response, and (3) factors influencing reactions and impact on professionals’ life.Conclusion:In light of these findings, we propose several key points for training and supervision: taking into account the question of the body and physical contact; addressing the emotions experienced by professionals, particularly helplessness and loneliness, and carefully utilizing the diffusion of emotions and dissociation from the patient to the professional as a therapeutic tool to help patients self-regulate and reintegrate dissociated parts of themselves.

Introduction

Dissociation is an alteration in the integration of consciousness, memory, motor control, perception, emotion, and identity (APA, 2013). This transdiagnostic phenomenon is observed not only in dissociative disorders but also in PTSD, complex trauma, and borderline personality disorder (Lynn et al., 2019; Spiegel et al., 2011). Its prevalence varies across populations and can reach 1 to 3% in the general population (Myrick & Brand, 2016).

The link between trauma and dissociation is well-documented, with dissociation historically conceptualized by Pierre Janet as an unconscious defense mechanism against traumatic events. Research has since suggested that dissociation may be a frequent response to trauma, especially when it occurs early in life, is repeated, and involves a profound sense of powerlessness (Ogawa et al., 1997; Schimmenti, 2018). In adults, dissociation has been hypothesized to represent an ultimate adaptive strategy for enduring extreme threat by modulating pain perception and altering awareness of immediate danger, although persistent dissociation may contribute to later psychopathology and chronic dissociative disorders (Fani et al., 2024; Schauer & Elbert, 2010). In children, correlational data hypothesized that dissociation may emerge when emotional regulation is insufficient or when attachment figures are sources of danger, potentially disrupting co-regulation processes and leading children to fragment their subjective experience in order to preserve a vital connection with the aggressor (Bailey & Brand, 2017; Hébert et al., 2020; Santoro et al., 2025).

To date, psychotherapy is considered the gold standard but is often lengthy and complex due to the severity of underlying trauma and comorbid symptoms (Brand, 2012). A three-phase model is commonly recommended: stabilization and symptom management, trauma processing, and reintegration (Guidelines for Treating Dissociative Identity Disorder in Adults, 2006). When patients receive appropriate treatment, reduction in dissociative symptoms, suicide risk, and self-harming behaviours can be seen (Myrick & Brand, 2016). Pharmacological treatments remain limited and primarily target comorbid conditions (depression, anxiety, PTSD) rather than dissociation itself (Brand et al., 2009).

However, dissociation can complicate therapeutic engagement and affect the therapeutic process as patients may alternate between engagement and withdrawal, experience fluctuating states of consciousness, and prematurely discontinue treatment (Brand et al., 2009). Indeed, dissociation can disrupt both the client’s connection to their inner emotional experience and the relational connection with the therapist (Lawson et al., 2017). Additionally, emotional dysregulation, comorbid conditions (suicidal ideation, self-harm, anxiety, and depression), and vulnerability to revictimization can make their care particularly demanding and emotionally taxing for clinicians (Lynn et al., 2019 ; Salmona, 2018). Findings on dissociation’s role during sessions are mixed: some studies suggest that dissociation can be problematic, making patients less accessible and potentially hindering therapeutic progress (Kleindienst et al., 2025; Verdi et al., 2023), others do not confirm this association (Halvorsen et al., 2014; Hoeboer et al., 2020).

The lack of training among professionals may also contribute to this challenge. Insufficient knowledge in this area can hinder accurate identification of dissociative symptoms (Nester et al., 2022). Such misunderstanding may reinforce patients’ distress and impede appropriate clinical care. Dissociative disorders are often difficult to diagnose due to the diversity of their manifestations (Spiegel & Cardeña, 1991) and their overlap with other psychopathological conditions (Van Der Hart, et al., 2004). Furthermore, dissociation can alter how victims recount traumatic events, often resulting in fragmented, emotionless narratives that may discredit them in the eyes of professionals or their social circle (Salmona, 2018). Lack of understanding among both patients and professionals can foster fear, guilt, and silence, contributing to feelings of isolation and perpetuating the invisibility of dissociative phenomena (Salmona, 2013).

Literature also underscores the fundamental role of the body in both the emergence of dissociation and its therapeutic management. As a defense mechanism against trauma, dissociation can be hypothesized to produce a rupture between body and mind, preventing integration of bodily and emotional sensations necessary to tolerate overwhelming experiences (Fani et al., 2024; Schauer & Elbert, 2010; Van Der Kolk, 2014). Literature suggests that this disconnection may persist long after the traumatic event, manifesting as depersonalization or derealization phenomena, emotional numbing during trauma recall, and disruptions in the perception of bodily signals such as hunger, pain, or sleep, sometimes accompanied by somatic symptoms; these findings have fostered the development of body-integrative approaches for treating dissociative disorders which attempt to restore the body-mind connection (Fani et al., 2024; Murphy, 2023; Van Der Kolk, 2014; Woelk & Garfinkel, 2024).

In addition to the role of body, literature emphasizes the importance of implicit communication and emotional regulation within the therapeutic dyad. When patients dissociate, non-verbal cues become central to the exchange, enabling therapists to recognize the patient’s dissociative state and reestablish a sense of safety (Gill, 2010). Dissociative patients often rely on sensory experiences to regulate themselves through interaction with the therapist (Arizmendi, 2008). Moreover, dissociation can sometimes appear to extend to the therapist, a phenomenon comparable to the ‘dissociative attunement’ (Hopenwasser, 2008) which involve a shared, non-verbal communication of suffering, reflecting the bidirectional nature of the therapeutic relationship. Attending carefully to these implicit signals enables clinicians to remain attuned to the patient despite the confusion induced by dissociation and to support the patient’s regulation process (Strait, 2013).

While the impact of working with trauma survivors on professionals is well-documented, little research has specifically examined therapists’ experiences of patient dissociation in session. Some professionals report feeling emotionally numb, experiencing detachment, or even dissociating themselves when confronted with patients’ traumatic narratives (Gordon, 2013). When dissociative manifestations occur during a session, therapists may experience intense emotions such as anxiety, fear, helplessness, or guilt (Strait, 2013, 2014). These distressing feelings may persist beyond the session, influencing clinical attitudes. Literature on dissociation (Strait, 2013) and trauma (Davies & Frawley, 1992) also underlines therapists’ need to share such experiences, highlighting the emotional toll of managing dissociation in the therapeutic setting. These responses may influence the quality of the therapeutic alliance and treatment effectiveness. Understanding these phenomena is crucial for adapting clinical interventions and preventing adverse effects on therapists’ well-being. This study aims to explore therapists’ subjective experiences when faced with patient dissociation in session, identify the underlying mechanisms of these interactions, and analyze their repercussions on clinical practice, professionals’ psychological health, and therapeutic alliance. In line with Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA), the study adopted an inductive approach grounded in the phenomenon under investigation, without formulating predefined research hypotheses, to remain as open as possible to the participants lived experience.

Methods

Design

This qualitative multicentric study follows the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) and has been approved by the Ethics Committee of APHP Centre (N° IRB: IORG0010044). In accordance with French law (Law No. 2012-300 of March 5, 2012, and Decree No. 2016-1537), which governs non-interventional research (Category 3), participants received clear oral and written information about the study. Their oral and written non-opposition (that is, the absence of refusal after information) was obtained, as required by the legal framework in France.

Participants and data collection

Participants were recruited using purposive sampling to capture a wide range of professional experiences and ensure representation across key professional and demographic characteristics. Eligible participants were psychiatrists, psychologists, or psychiatry residents who had encountered at least one patient presenting dissociative manifestations in their clinical practice.

Sampling aimed to ensure diversity in terms of sex, age, years of professional experience, type of practice (hospitalization, consultation, private practice, emergency), specialty (adult psychiatry, child and adolescent psychiatry, addictology), and region of practice (Paris and Île-de-France). The detailed recruitment process and sampling strategy are described in Supplementary Material 1.

Data were collected through a single individual research interview conducted by the first author (E.M.), a female psychiatry resident who received training in qualitative research. These interviews focused on the professionals’ experiences with patient dissociation and were not clinical assessments of patients themselves. In this study, the term research interview refers exclusively to the semi-structured interviews conducted with participating professionals. To avoid confusion, we use the terms clinical interview or therapeutic session when referring to professionals’ clinical practice with patients.

To remain as close as possible to the professionals’ lived experience, no predefined definition of traumatic dissociation was provided. Instead, participants were invited to describe the manifestations they had encountered in their clinical practice. This approach was intended to minimize orientation of their discourse and to allow for the emergence of the diversity of dissociative phenomena, as experienced and understood by each professional.

Interviews were conducted face-to-face or online, lasted approximately 30–45 minutes, were audio-recorded and anonymized. A semi-structured interview guide was used to facilitate open exploration of participants’ experiences; the full guide is available in Supplementary Material. Topics included: professionals’ approaches to patients’ dissociation, the impact of dissociation on their practice, the role of dissociation for patients, professionals’ feelings and reactions during and after patients’ dissociative episodes, as well as professionals’ personal history of dissociation, either in their own lives or during the interview, including their reactions and resonance with personal events. Analysis and reflexivity

Data saturation was discussed as new interviews no longer provide new findings. As the purpose of this study was to explore professionals’ subjective experiences of dissociation, Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) was used. IPA is a qualitative research approach that seeks to understand how people make sense of their personal lived experiences (Smith et al., 2021).

Recordings were transcribed word to word. Then, the first author conducted an initial in-depth reading and annotation of transcripts to identify emerging themes. A second researcher (S.M.) reviewed annotated transcripts to enhance analytic rigor. Regular meetings were held to discuss coding decisions and interpretations, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion; in rare cases, a third researcher (M.E.H.) was consulted to reach consensus. A detailed description of the analytic steps is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Reflexivity is essential in IPA and requires self-awareness about one’s preconception. Reflexivity was discussed in research and supervision meetings where data extracts, analyses and challenges were discussed. A reflective diary was used throughout the research to elicit preconceptions and reflect on the researcher’s feelings and reactions, ensuring curiosity and reflexive awareness. To enhance credibility, preliminary findings were shared with a subset of participants (member checking), and their feedback was incorporated into the final analysis. Translation of selected interview excerpts was conducted by the first author and reviewed by a second researcher to support interpretative rigor.

Professionals’ reactions in face of dissociation

Action

When faced with dissociation, all professionals reported a need to intervene. For some, this need arose immediately in the form of an active and functional approach, directly opposing the dissociation, while for others it followed a period of observation. P2: In the moment, I think it's actually kind of the opposite of dissociation, a very functional, very active mode, in something very real, so we go for it, we just do it […] I imagine there's an active mode that kicks in, where instead of letting something come, we actively bring the patient back. P9: Initially, I do have a bit of an evaluative reaction, because unlike other consultations where I introduce myself right away, here I observe a little first.Professionals reported using different approaches when faced with patient dissociation: some focused on the senses, others on emotions and bodily sensations, and some on concrete elements (such as the weather). P15: We ask, in the room, can you see five blue objects, for example? Can you name them? Can you hear yourself? Can you describe the sensations you are feeling? It can be smells … P19: What I use most frequently is asking the patient to pay attention to their environment, to tell me again where they are, who I am, how they got here, and to talk a little about the weather.Many reported the importance of the body and movement in response to dissociation, which encouraged them to remain physically present with the patients. However, opinions on physical contact varied among the professionals in this study. Some reported avoiding any form of touch to prevent retraumatization, while others emphasized the need to use physical contact cautiously in specific contexts. P10: This could also involve exercises focused on bodily sensations and the sense of safety. P19: I don’t touch my patient, especially since we see a lot of victims of violence here. It would seem far more risky than helpful. P14: I find that physical presence is important, being there physically, providing containment, sitting next to the patient. And sometimes, maybe even touching the patient, if it's safe and if their history allows for it.

Presence and attention

In response to dissociation professionals demonstrate their presence. For some professionals, this involves reassuring the patient of their presence, even between sessions; for others, it involves working together to find meaning in the symptoms. P11: I always tell them that if anything comes up in the meantime, they can call me back. P15: We have to do something with it, try to understand it, help the patient understand it, and make sense of it together.Faced with dissociative manifestations, professionals often feel an urgent need to protect the patient and to create a sense of safety and stability. Some found themselves adopting a more nurturing and supportive stance while reflecting on the potential impact this could have on the patient. P7: I ended up adopting an overly protective stance with her. P5: I felt invited to complete, to correct, to mother, to soothe, even though it wasn’t really my place, it wasn’t where I could operate or have therapeutic effects.Professionals noted that dissociative manifestations are often subtle and difficult to detect. As a result, they focus closely on what is happening to the patient. In parallel, they also pay attention to their own internal reactions. Discussion

This study highlights the multifaceted impact of dissociation on the therapeutic relationship between patients and professionals. Professionals’ emotional and behavioural responses to patient dissociation are varied and may reflect a form of resonance with the patients’ symptoms, underscoring the importance of implicit communication and emotional regulation in the therapeutic interaction. Encounters with dissociation also bring attention to existing knowledge gaps and the need for ongoing clinical development. The themes identified in this study correspond closely to these challenges.

In this study, when a patient exhibited dissociative manifestations during a consultation, professionals reported managing intense emotions such as anxiety, fear, helplessness, loneliness, guilt, and frustration. Some professionals also experienced aggressive feelings, which they directed either toward themselves, blaming themselves for not knowing how to handle the situation, or toward the patient, attributing responsibility for these difficult emotions. These emotional responses could undermine their confidence in their own abilities and could persist after the consultation, influencing subsequent professional behaviour. Based on these results and in light of Strait’s studies (2013, 2014), one may question whether the urgent need to act in response to dissociation and to protect the patient serves as a strategy to counteract feelings of helplessness and anxiety, thereby allowing professionals to regain a sense of control. This discomfort, combined with feelings of helplessness, may partially explain the aggressive reactions observed.

During interviews, some professionals reported feeling detached, exhausted, lost, stunned, or frozen when faced with patient dissociation, consistent with existing literature. These states were primarily described by professionals with limited experience in recognizing or managing dissociation, and some of these reactions could themselves be conceptualized as forms of dissociation. In contrast, other professionals, mostly those with training and experience in psychotrauma, reported experiencing dissociation either during or outside of sessions. This raises the question of whether less experienced professionals also encounter dissociative manifestations, and whether their failure to report them reflects a lack of recognition or reluctance to discuss them due to shame or fear.

Regardless of whether the experiences involved emotions, bodily sensations, or dissociative manifestations, the professionals interviewed expressed relief and satisfaction at being able to share these experiences. This need to share experiences and emotions is consistent with the existing literature (Strait, 2013 and Davies & Frawley, 1992). Supervision and peer exchange provide opportunities to discuss clinical experiences and may allow professionals to express their feelings and emotions while counteracting the sense of isolation. However, early-career clinicians may hesitate to share their experiences due to feelings of fear or judgment, or difficulty recognizing the nature of their reactions. These results are consistent with other studies highlighting the sense of shame some professionals may experience when they feel certain emotions in response to patients’ traumatic narratives (Husseini et al., 2016). To address these challenges, it would be beneficial to include education on dissociative manifestations in clinical training, highlighting the possibility that professionals themselves may experience them to some degree. Normalizing these responses within training programs for those working with traumatized patients and providing supervision spaces that bring together both experienced and novice professionals, could help reduce isolation, enhance coping, and support professional development.

The body and its sensoriality are key elements in the therapeutic relationship (Nassif et al., 2024), as reflected in these studies where professionals often mention the use of tools focused on the body and the senses. Furthermore, in line with existing literature on the role of the body in dissociation (Van Der Kolk, 2014), professionals reported using these body-based techniques to ground patients and facilitate reconnection with their bodily sensations. Professionals differed in their views regarding the use of physical contact. Some firmly opposed it, considering that touch might be experienced as intrusive or could reactivate trauma. Others argued that, when applied cautiously and with the patient’s consent, physical contact can serve as a powerful therapeutic tool in situations where verbal communication is limited or ineffective. In such contexts, touch may help counteract the sense of discontinuity of self and the blurring of boundaries between internal and external experiences that often accompany dissociation. During these vulnerable moments, professionals may feel compelled to adopt a protective stance, using touch strategically to re-establish connection and provide reassurance to the patient.

Many professionals interviewed reported paying close attention to their own emotional responses as well as to patients’ non-verbal communication, consistent with existing literature. Research on dissociation and trauma emphasizes the importance of implicit communication and emotional regulation within the therapeutic dyad. When a dissociative patient recalls a traumatic event, they may lose the ability to verbalize and instead communicate non-verbally. Attending to this implicit communication enables the professional to identify the patient’s state and foster a sense of safety (Gill, 2010). Studies have also shown that patients with strong dissociative responses rely on sensory experiences to regulate themselves through interaction with the therapist (Arizmendi, 2008).

Interestingly, professionals’ emotional reactions in response to patient dissociation share similarities with the emotions frequently experienced by trauma victims, such as fear, loneliness, helplessness, and guilt. This raises the question of potential emotional contagion between patients and therapists. Some professionals report feeling as though they are experiencing the emotions from which the patient distances themselves when recounting trauma in a detached manner. Moreover, dissociation can sometimes appear to spread from patient to therapist, a phenomenon comparable to dissociative attunement described in the literature (Hopenwasser, 2008). Paying attention to these implicit signals allows clinicians to remain attuned to the patient despite the confusion induced by dissociation and to assist in their emotional regulation (Strait, 2013). In both this study and literature, professionals frequently report bodily sensations or emotional perceptions that are difficult to verbalize in the presence of a dissociative patient. These sensations may serve as indicators of the patient’s state or intrusive signals requiring active management. When appropriately recognized and regulated, such perceptions can become a valuable tool for understanding and supporting the patient.

In line with previous research, the present findings confirm that most professionals report insufficient training on dissociation, which limits their ability to identify and manage such manifestations effectively. Some even mentioned the risk of minimizing, misinterpreting, or doubting the legitimacy of patients’ symptoms. Considering both the literature and the study’s results, it appears essential to strengthen professional training to enhance the recognition, understanding, and treatment of dissociative phenomena. A lack of awareness not only affects clinical practice but also has serious consequences for patients, including delayed diagnosis, inadequate treatment, social isolation, and the risk of re-victimization (Salmona, 2018). Improving education on dissociation could therefore contribute to earlier identification, more appropriate care, and a reduction in the stigma and misunderstanding surrounding these experiences.

In the absence of adequate training, healthcare professionals often learn to manage dissociation through direct experience. When encountering dissociative patients for the first time, they must simultaneously provide appropriate care and develop their own strategies, relying on colleagues, research, and exchanges with patients. Several professionals reported having to adapt their practices, innovate, and be creative to help patients reconnect with the present moment. This encounter with dissociation can thus be understood as a continuous learning process in which errors are inevitable but also valuable, serving as opportunities for growth and for deepening their understanding of both their practice and their patients. Building on this and given that experiential learning is a central component of professional development in mental health training (Skovholt & Ronnestad, 1992), it seems particularly beneficial to provide targeted training in the management of dissociation, notably through role-playing exercises that allow clinicians to practice responding to dissociative manifestations in a safe and controlled setting.

Part of the therapeutic work with patient involves psychoeducation and exploring alternative coping mechanisms that align with current challenges, allowing patients to function without exclusively relying on dissociation (Schauer & Elbert, 2010). To establish a connection with their patients and make dissociation more comprehensible, some professionals draw on their own experiences of dissociation. While some professionals used their own experiences without explicitly identifying them as such, a few participants reported that, within the therapeutic relationship, they occasionally disclosed their own experiences of dissociation to patients. In therapy, self-disclosure, when used judiciously and in a limited manner, can strengthen the therapeutic alliance, normalize and validate the patient’s experience, and offer new perspectives (Knox & Hill, 2003). We wondered whether this might be related to the fact that many professionals reported that their patients were reluctant to discuss their symptoms out of fear of losing their mind or being perceived as crazy. In this context, it is plausible that professionals shared their own experiences as a way to facilitate patient disclosure, reduce the sense of unreality associated with dissociative manifestations, and reassure their patients.

The experience of working with dissociation also affects professionals, who develop strategies to manage the emotional burden, often employing tools similar to those they recommend to their patients. In the present study, differences were observed between female and male professionals in coping strategies and in the way they discuss the impact on their personal lives. This finding may be understood in the context of gender roles, which refer to socially constructed expectations regarding behaviours associated with a particular gender. One might question to what extent beliefs about women’s superior caregiving abilities and men’s responsibility for household income influence the observation that only female professionals reported impacts on family life, while male professionals focused on professional aspects (INSEE, 2017). Additionally, stereotypes that women are more ‘emotional’ and inclined to care for themselves and others may shape how female professionals verbalize difficulties and seek tools for self-care (Boquet & Lett, 2018). Previous research suggests that women tend to report greater use of emotion-focused coping strategies (including self-distraction and seeking emotional support), whereas men were more likely to employ problem-focused strategies (Graves et al., 2021; Sinha & S, 2018) and that social support had a more beneficial effect on women than on men in the context of work stress (González-Morales et al., 2006). These differences may help explain why female professionals in the present study emphasized personal impact and self-care strategies, while male professionals highlighted professional impacts and professional resources. However, no studies specifically investigating psychologists or psychiatrists could be identified and these interpretations should therefore be considered exploratory. Overall, these findings suggest that gender role may implicitly shape how professionals experience and regulate the emotional toll of working with dissociative patients. Training programs may encourage reflection on gender roles and their impact on how professionals express and deal with the difficulties that can arise when working with these patients. Future research should further explore the influence of gender roles on how mental health professionals perceive and express their difficulties, as well as on the coping strategies they employ in managing the emotional demands of working with dissociative patients.

This study presents several strengths and limitations. Among its strengths, the diversity and size of the sample (23 participants) allowed for data saturation. The sample included psychiatrists, psychologists, and psychiatry residents from different theoretical orientations and working in various settings and with various clinical populations. It also comprised both men and women of different ages with varying levels of experience in working with traumatized patients. However, certain limitations must be noted: the experiences collected were reported retrospectively, which implies a potential reconstruction of memories. Additionally, it remains difficult to distinguish which aspects of the emotions reported by professionals are specifically related to the patient’s dissociation, the recounting of traumatic events, or an interaction between the two. The study raises several implications for the management of dissociation. All participants reported a lack of training on this topic during their education. It would be relevant to include specific tools and techniques for dissociation in psychiatry and psychology training programs. Broader awareness, including among the general public, could also facilitate communication between patients and professionals. This research has highlighted the importance of the body in the treatment of dissociative disorders and the necessity of integrating body-based therapeutic approaches. It also underscores the ethical and conceptual challenges associated with physical contact in therapy, opening discussions on how professionals can adapt their stance to meet the specific needs of dissociative patients. Furthermore, professionals should be encouraged to share and analyze their emotions in supervision sessions to reduce their isolation and better manage feelings of helplessness and loneliness. Finally, the study sheds light on the phenomenon of the diffusion of emotions and dissociation from patient to professional, a valuable therapeutic lever that enables the patient to regulate and reintegrate dissociated aspects of themselves. This phenomenon should be explored in supervision to ensure that professionals can utilize it without becoming overwhelmed. Several areas for future research can be suggested. For instance, it would be interesting to examine the experience of dissociative patients in the presence of their therapist to better understand the impact of the professional’s reaction on the therapeutic relationship. Additionally, a complementary study could explore how professionals manage these emotions and the impact this may have on their personal and professional lives, particularly in relation to gender differences.
