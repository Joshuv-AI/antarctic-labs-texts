Maternal exposure to ozone during pregnancy and risk of gestational diabetes in a Beijing pregnancy cohort study
Authors: Jing Wang, Xingyan Liu, Di Wu, Bo Peng, Ya Zhang, Yuan Zhang, Yuanyuan Wang, Li Lin, Xu Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) exerts profound and lasting impacts on both maternal and offspring health, and the prevalence of GDM has been increasing. Several studies have demonstrated a close association between air pollution and GDM. Ozone, recognized as an emerging contaminant, has exhibited a rising trajectory in exposure levels over the past few years. While ozone exposure levels are rising, studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding its association with the risk of GDM. More importantly, identifying possible exposure windows is essential for informing targeted prenatal protection and population‑level preventive strategies, yet such evidence for O₃ remains limited.ObjectivesTo investigate the association between prenatal ozone exposure and the risk of GDM among pregnant women in Northern Chinese cities, and to identify critical exposure windows.MethodsA total of 8,847pregnant women who received perinatal health care services from January 2020 to June 2024 were included in this study. GDM was diagnosed by the examination of oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) between 24 and 28 gestation weeks. Two time windows were estimated as gestation0-13+ 6 weeks, and gestation14-23+ 6 weeks. Multiple logistic regression and Restricted cubic splines were used to ascertain whether the association between GDM and O3 is linear. The Generalized Linear Model (GLM) was employed to identify the potential exposure time windows.Results2,185 women were diagnosed with GDM in 8,847 pregnant women in this research. A significant positive linear association between O3 and GDM was found specifically during weeks 0–23+ 6 of gestation, with no evidence of a nonlinear relationship or a specific safety threshold. After adjusting for other pollutants, the significant association between O₃ exposure and GDM persisted across the entire exposure period (0–23⁺⁶ weeks). Gestational weeks 0–13⁺⁶ were identified as a critical window of susceptibility for GDM. Notably, significant associations between O₃ exposure and Postprandial Glucose 1 h(1hPG) and Postprandial Glucose 2 h(2hPG) levels were exclusively observed during 14–23⁺⁶ weeks of gestation.ConclusionThis study found that gestational O₃ exposure disrupts glucose homeostasis and increases the risk of GDM. Furthermore, 0–13⁺⁶ weeks of gestation were identified as a possible window for the effects of O₃ exposure on GDM. Weeks 14–23⁺⁶ of gestation were identified as a period of increased susceptibility for the effects of O₃ exposure on 1hPG and 2hPG.Keywords: Ozone, Gestation diabetes mellitus, Sensitive windows

Introduction

GDM is defined as hyperglycemia first diagnosed during pregnancy [1]. The core mechanism of GDM is insulin resistance or insufficient insulin secretion. According to the WHO criteria, also adopted in China, GDM is diagnosed via a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) at 24–28 weeks of gestation if any of the following thresholds are met: fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1-hour postprandial glucose (1hPG) ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or 2-hour postprandial glucose (2hPG) ≥ 8.5 mmol/L. It is important to note that an isolated FPG ≥ 5.1 mmol/L in early pregnancy is not sufficient for diagnosis [2, 3]. Globally, the standardized prevalence of GDM is 14.0% (95% CI: 13.97–14.04%), with significant regional variation, from 7.8% (95% CI: 7.2–8.4%) in Europe to 20.8% (95% CI: 20.2–21.4%) in Southeast Asia [4]. In China, the prevalence of GDM is 15.6% (95% CI: 14.9–16.2%), and the prevalence of GDM shows a significant upward trend [5].

As a result, GDM has emerged as a significant public health challenge, imposing substantial health and economic burdens on individuals, families, and the healthcare system. GDM has profound impacts on both maternal and offspring health. Women with a history of GDM have a 45% (OR = 1.45, 95%CI: 1.36–1.53) increased overall risk of developing cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. The adverse effects of GDM extend beyond the mother to her offspring. Epidemiological evidence has demonstrated that children born to women with GDM exhibit higher body mass index (BMI), body fat percentage, and waist circumference during growth and development [6–8]. This intrauterine hyperglycemic environment can induce fetal programming changes, thereby increasing the risk of adverse health outcomes across multiple systems [9]. Notably, GDM exposure has been closely associated with an elevated risk of neurodevelopmental disorders in offspring. The offspring exposed to GDM have a 38% increased risk of autism spectrum disorder (OR = 1.38, 95%CI: 1.20–1.59) and a 70% increased risk of intellectual disability (OR = 1.70, 95%CI: 1.52–1.90) [10]. Therefore, it is greatly important to take actions to reduce the prevalence of GDM.

Several studies have found that GDM is closely associated with both intrinsic individual factors and environmental factors [11–13]. Meanwhile, the health risks posed by gaseous pollutants cannot be ignored [14, 15]. The increase in tropospheric O3 concentrations has emerged as a new concern [16]. Owing to its strong oxidizing properties, O3 can cause adverse health effects on the respiratory and cardiovascular systems [17, 18]. Accumulating evidence has also linked O3 exposure to various pregnancy complications and adverse birth outcomes. For instance, each 10 ppb increase in O3 concentration is associated with a 6% higher risk of preterm birth (OR = 1.06, 95%CI: 1.03–1.10) [19, 20].

A Shanghai-based cohort study found that O₃ exposure during early pregnancy was significantly associated with elevated fasting glucose and increased GDM risk, with gestational weeks 5–10 identified as a critical exposure window [21]. However, other investigations have reported null findings [22, 23]. Identifying these critical windows is essential for uncovering underlying mechanisms and guiding targeted interventions and environmental policies. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to evaluate the association between gestational O₃ exposure and GDM risk, with a focus on identifying period-specific effects across pregnancy. By pinpointing susceptible windows, this study aims to provide actionable evidence for refining air quality guidelines and developing trimester-specific prevention strategies, thereby reducing the modifiable environmental burden of GDM in China.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in Peking University International Hospital. A total of 9,752 pregnant women who received perinatal health care services from January 2020 to June 2024 were enrolled after delivery, all of whom routinely underwent OGTT as part of standard prenatal care. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: [1] age outside the range of 15–49 years; [2] absence of oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) results; [1] missing residential address information. The detailed process of study population selection is illustrated in Fig. 1. Gestational age was calculated based on the last menstrual period (LMP) documented in the electronic medical records. Conception season was defined as follows: spring (January–March), summer (April–June), autumn (July–September), and winter (October–December). All participants provided informed consent before data collection. During data analysis, all personal information of the participants was anonymized to ensure data security. This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Peking University International Hospital (No: 2025-KY-0035), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The process of study population selection

Air pollutant exposure assessment

Ambient air pollutant concentrations were obtained from Tracking Air Pollution in China (TAP). The TAP team had developed a machine learning model integrating multi-source data to retrieve daily O₃ concentrations with full spatiotemporal coverage. The maximum 8-hour O3 exposure estimates at 10 km×10 km spatial resolution were used in this study. To minimize confounding effects from co-pollutants, PM2.5 and its components (including sulfate, nitrate, organic matter, fine particulate matter, black carbon, ammonium) were adjusted in the model. The exposure levels of PM2.5 and its components were estimated at spatial 10 × 10 km resolution [24–27]. Residential addresses of participants were obtained from the healthcare information system. After standardized processing, these addresses were converted into latitude-longitude coordinates using the reverse geocoding service provided by AutoNavi (Gaode Maps), followed by coordinate validity verification. Finally, 10 km×10 km grids were generated centered on each coordinate point, from which daily pollutant concentrations were extracted for each study subject.

Outcomes

All participants underwent an OGTT between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation, and we examined the association between O3 exposure during 0–23 weeks of gestation and the results of this OGTT examination. Subsequently, participants consumed 75 g of anhydrous glucose dissolved in 300 mL of warm water within 5 min. Venous blood samples were collected to determine plasma glucose levels at 1hPG and 2hPG after glucose ingestion, respectively. GDM was diagnosed based on the following criteria: ≥ 5.1 mmol/L and < 7.0 mmol/L, 1hPG ≥ 10.0 mmol/L or 2hPG ≥ 8.5 mmol/L [16, 28]. Two time-window were estimated as gestation0-13+ 6 weeks, and gestation14-23+ 6 weeks.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the study population, which was stratified by GDM status. Continuous variables were described as the median (interquartile range, IQR), and categorical variables were described as the frequency (percentage). Between-group comparisons were performed using independent-sample t-tests for continuous variables and chi-squared tests for categorical variables. Logistic regression models were used to estimate the associations between O3 exposure and the risk of GDM. Restricted Cubic Splines (RCS) were used to identify the concentration–response curve between O3 exposure and GDM and O3 exposure and the OGTT results. The number of knots in the RCS was determined by comparing Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) values across different knot placements.

To eliminate potential confounding effects, a series of confounding factors were adjusted in the models, including age, pre-pregnancy BMI, parity, and conception season. Given that previous studies have reported associations between other air pollutants and GDM, these pollutants may act as potential confounders that affect the study results. Therefore, two-pollutant models were constructed to further control for such confounding effects, with each additional air pollutant sequentially adjusted for in the models.

All analyses were conducted in R software (version 4.5.0). Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

The baseline characteristics of the study population stratified by GDM status (GDM group vs. non-GDM group) were presented in Table 1. There were 2185 GDM patients among 8,847 pregnant women. The prevalence of GDM was 24.7%. The mean age of the GDM group was 32.9 years, which was significantly higher than that of the non-GDM group. Compared with the non-GDM group, the GDM group had a significantly higher pre-pregnancy BMI. Women who conceived in summer had a higher risk of developing GDM. Significant differences in GDM prevalence were also observed across different pre-pregnancy BMI categories. There were significant differences in O3 exposure levels between GDM group and non-GDM group.

Association between O3 exposure and the risk of GDM

The RCS model was employed to investigate the relationship between O3 exposure and GDM. O3 levels demonstrated significant correlations with GDM in 0–23+ 6 weeks of gestation as well as in weeks 0–13+ 6 of gestation separately. However, the p-values for nonlinear associations were greater than 0.05 during gestational weeks 0–23⁺⁶, indicating a linear relationship between the variables (Fig. 2).

The RCS curve between ozone and GDM

In the Fig. 3, a 10 µg/m³ increase in O3 exposure during 0–23 weeks of gestation was associated with a 7.2% elevated risk of GDM diagnosis in the single-pollutant model, with an odds ratio of 1.072 (95% CI: 1.030–1.117). Meanwhile, in the two-pollutant model, the results were consistent with the single-pollutant model. GDM risk was positively associated with exposure to O3 during gestational weeks 0–13⁺⁶ (OR = 1.055, 95% CI:1.023–1.089), and in the two pollutants model, the association was still significant after adjusting PM2.5(OR = 1.052, 95%CI:1.018–1.088), SO42−(OR = 1.052, 95%CI:1.019–1.086 ), BC(OR = 1.054, 95%CI:1.020–1.080), NO3− (OR = 1.043, 95%CI:1.008–1.080), OM(OR = 1.054, 95%CI:1.021–1.089) and NH4+(OR = 1.046, 95%CI:1.011–1.081). But during gestational weeks 14–23⁺⁶, the difference was not significant both in single-pollutant model and in two-pollutant model.

Association between O3 exposure and blood glucose level

Single and double-pollutant models were used to investigate the association between O3 exposure and blood glucose level obtained from OGTT. In the Fig. 4, O3 exposure and FPG remained associated throughout 0–23⁺⁶ weeks of gestation. A 10 µg/m³ increase in O₃ concentration was associated with an elevation in FPG levels by 0.020 (95%CI: 0.012, 0.027) mmol/L. The results of the FPG indicate the body’s baseline glucose control capability. In contrast, the association with 1hPG was strongest during gestational weeks 14–23⁺⁶. With every 10 µg/m³ increase in O₃ exposure, 1hPG increased by 0.039 (95%CI: 0.016–0.061), and the 2hPG increased by 0.031 (95%CI: 0.012–0.050). Postprandial glucose levels refer to the body’s ability to respond to the increase in blood sugar levels after eating, mediated by insulin.

Discussion

The GDM prevalence observed in our study is higher than previously reported rates in Beijing general obstetric populations, which ranged from 19.7% to 24.24% [29, 30]. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in study populations, diagnostic criteria, or temporal trends. Although the association​ between O3 exposure and GDM has been confirmed by several research [31], the critical exposure window remains controversial. Our findings demonstrate that O3 exposure from 0 to 23 weeks of gestation is linearly associated with GDM risk, with 0–13⁺⁶ weeks identified as a period of increased susceptibility. The association between O3 exposure and GDM risk was significant during 0–13⁺⁶ weeks but not during 14–23⁺⁶ weeks, indicating that the period of increased susceptibility may be confined to early pregnancy. Early pregnancy is a critical window for placental development, insulin sensitivity changes, and β-cell adaptation, all of which are vulnerable to environmental disruption [32]. By the second trimester, metabolic pathways involved in GDM pathogenesis may already be programmed, diminishing the impact of later exposures [33]. The null findings during 14–23⁺⁶ weeks reinforce the specificity of the exposure window and support the biological plausibility of early-pregnancy susceptibility, informing targeted interventions during specific gestational periods. Meanwhile, O3 exposure is closely linked to FPG during the 0–13+ 6 gestational weeks 14–23⁺⁶ was identified as the potential exposure window for 1hPG and 2hPG. These findings indicate that O3 exposure may have differential effects on fasting and post-load glucose levels, with FPG affected throughout early-to-mid pregnancy and postprandial glucose showing greater susceptibility during mid-pregnancy. From a clinical perspective, elevated FPG reflects impaired basal glucose regulation, while elevated 1hPG and 2hPG indicate reduced glucose tolerance following a glucose load. Both are established risk factors for adverse pregnancy outcomes and are routinely used in GDM diagnosis. The temporal patterns observed in this study may help identify critical windows for interventions aimed at mitigating ozone-related glycemic effects.

Our results are consistent with those of several previous studies. O3 exposure (per 10 µg/m³ increase) was found to be associated with GDM, with the overall pooled OR = 1.01 (95% CI: 1.00, 1.03) [34]. Meanwhile, some studies confirmed the relationship between O3 and GDM to be linear. They found a consistent increase in the first trimester (0–13 weeks), and the first two trimesters (0–26 weeks). The risk of GDM increased with increasing O3 concentration during the first trimester (P overall = 0.034, P non−linearity = 0.090) [16]. However, other studies have reported inconsistent findings, particularly regarding the identification of critical exposure windows. Some articles have found that the key exposure time windows were three months before pregnancy [35, 36]. These studies were mainly conducted in the southern regions, and there might be differences in the exposure levels between those regions and the current study area.

Our findings support that O₃ exposure increases the risk of GDM, and this association may be mediated through oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways. O3 is a strong oxidant. After entering the lungs through the respiratory tract, it can cause oxidative stress in the lungs. Activation of Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide Phosphate (NADPH) oxidase and Toll-Like Receptor 4 (TLR4) pathways by air pollution exposure triggers the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species [37]. These mediators can enter the systemic circulation and induce systemic oxidative stress responses. These inflammatory factors (IL-6, IL-8, TNFα, IL-1β) can activate vascular endothelial cells, producing reactive oxygen species and other oxidants, thereby reducing the body’s antioxidant capacity [38]. Owing to their constitutively low antioxidant defenses, pancreatic beta cells were particularly susceptible to damage under inflammatory conditions. Such damage can exacerbate functional deficits, resulting in inadequate insulin secretion and thereby contributing to the development of GDM [39]. In addition, the oxidative stress reaction caused by O3 can also induce serine phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrates, disrupting the normal transmission of insulin signals [40]. This leads to a reduction in the peripheral tissue’s utilization of glucose, causing insulin resistance [41]. At the same time, after the oxidative stress reaction activates the inflammatory pathway, the release of the pro-inflammatory factor interleukin-6 can further exacerbate insulin resistance [42, 43]. The placenta is the organ for material exchange between the mother and the fetus. When the body’s oxidative stress level is high and causes inflammation in the placenta, the TNF-α in the placenta will increase, leading to abnormal glucose metabolism in the mother [44]. During pregnancy, there are significant hormonal changes, such as prolactin and TNF, which can further affect the mother’s carbohydrate and lipid metabolism, thereby leading to the occurrence of GDM [45]. Exposure to O3 reduced the diversity of gut microbiota, altered the relative abundance of gut microbiota, and affected the metabolism of gut microbiota [46]. An imbalanced microbiota further exacerbates insulin resistance through inflammatory responses, leads to insufficient secretion of normal short-chain fatty acids, and impairs glucose metabolism [47]. Gestational weeks 0–13 represent a critical window for placental development. Ozone-induced oxidative stress and inflammation can profoundly disrupt this process, laying the groundwork for future functional abnormalities. This may lead to increased placental increased secretion of anti-insulin hormones and sustained release of inflammatory signals during mid-to-late pregnancy [45]. Simultaneously, such exposure precedes gestational physiological inflammation, initiating a state of chronic low-grade inflammation in the maternal system. When physiological insulin resistance naturally intensifies in mid-pregnancy, the compromised metabolic buffering capacity becomes overwhelmed, ultimately significantly elevating the risk of GDM onset [48]. Elevated 1hPG and 2hPG levels reflect an inadequate adaptive response to postprandial hyperglycemia. This may stem from impaired insulin utilization, potentially attributed to defective insulin receptors on target cells that abrogate normal insulin signaling cascades. Consequently, the conversion of glucose to glycogen or lipids is disrupted, perpetuating elevated blood glucose concentrations. This condition may also be linked to beta-cell damage and insufficient insulin secretion. When blood sugar is high, the body lacks adequate insulin to lower glucose levels effectively [49]. An abnormal 2hPG suggests that the body’s beta cells have entered a state of compensatory exhaustion after long-term strain, characterized by delayed secretion speed or a postponed peak secretion. This can also be attributable to the body’s muscles or liver fail to respond properly to insulin, not only failing to lower blood glucose but also continuously producing and releasing glucose into the bloodstream [50].

From a public health perspective, our findings have several important implications. First, the identification of 0–13⁺⁶ weeks as a period of increased susceptibility to O3 exposure provides evidence-based guidance for targeted interventions. Public health campaigns could advise women planning pregnancy or in early gestation to minimize outdoor activities during high-ozone days, when the developing placenta and metabolic programming are most vulnerable. Second, the differential effects on fasting versus postprandial glucose suggest that O3 exposure may influence distinct components of glucose metabolism across gestation, informing clinical monitoring strategies. For example, enhanced glycemic screening during mid-pregnancy may be warranted for women with high O3 exposure in early gestation. Third, these results support the need for strengthened environmental monitoring and air quality regulations during critical developmental windows. Policymakers could consider seasonal O3 control measures that align with periods of heightened susceptibility among pregnant women. Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of such targeted prevention strategies in reducing the burden of GDM and its associated adverse outcomes.

This study has several notable strengths. Firstly, it was based on a retrospective cohort of 8,847 pregnant women, ensuring sufficient statistical power and enhancing the reliability and generalizability of the results. Secondly, to our knowledge, this is one of the few studies investigating the association between O₃ exposure and GDM conducted in northern China. Given that most previous relevant studies were performed in southern China, this work enables comparisons of potential differences in the O₃-GDM relationship attributed to latitude, climatic conditions, and population characteristics. Thirdly, the study adjusted for common environmental confounders, including PM2.5 and its components, which minimized the potential confounding effects of coexisting air pollutants and rendered the association between O₃ and GDM more robust. Additionally, the exploration of the relationship between O₃exposure and OGTT results provides detailed insights into the underlying mechanisms linking O₃ exposure to GDM pathogenesis.​ This study also has certain limitations that should be acknowledged.

This study has several limitations. First, due to the retrospective design, we lacked data on lifestyle factors (diet, physical activity), socioeconomic status (education, occupation), and behaviors (smoking, alcohol consumption), which may introduce residual confounding despite adjusting for BMI. Second, O3 exposure was estimated based on residential addresses using a 10 km × 10 km grid, without considering individual mobility or indoor/occupational exposure, potentially leading to exposure misclassification and biasing results toward the null. Higher-resolution exposure models are needed in future research. Third, the single-center design in Beijing limits the generalizability of findings to other populations or regions with different characteristics. Finally, the observational nature of the study precludes causal inference, despite rigorous statistical methods. Future multicenter prospective studies with comprehensive covariates and improved exposure assessment are warranted to validate our findings.

Conclusion

This study provides strong evidence that ozone exposure throughout pregnancy, particularly during the early gestational window, is an independent risk factor for GDM, with a significant impact on postprandial glucose levels. The identification of early pregnancy as a critical susceptibility window underscores the need for targeted public health interventions. These may include strengthening ozone concentration monitoring, formulating trimester-specific air quality guidelines, and implementing policies aimed at reducing population-level ozone exposure. Future research should focus on validating these findings in diverse populations and evaluating the effectiveness of intervention strategies to mitigate the health impacts of ozone exposure during pregnancy.
