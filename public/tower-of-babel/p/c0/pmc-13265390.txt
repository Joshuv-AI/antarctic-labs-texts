TTK Overexpression: A Negative Prognostic Biomarker in Anaplastic Thyroid Carcinoma
Authors: Yan Guo, Zhidong Sun, Xiao Liu, Xianni Liu, Haiwen Chen, Menglan Zhang, Weibo Mao, Yuan Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGiven threonine and tyrosine kinase (TTK)'s oncogenic role in various cancers and its understudied status in thyroid cancer, this study investigates TTK expression in thyroid lesions to assess its diagnostic and therapeutic potential.MethodsWe analyzed differential TTK expression levels using public datasets (n = 143) and clinical samples (n = 91) across various thyroid lesions. Prognostic predictions based on TTK expression were conducted using a 2 × 2 contingency table, receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC), and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis.ResultsTTK expression levels increased with the malignancy grade of thyroid lesions. In highly malignant anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC) tissue, both TTK mRNA (p = 0.003) and protein levels (p = 0.000) were significantly higher than those in less malignant papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC). TTK protein expression showed excellent discrimination between benign and malignant thyroid lesions (AUC = 0.983–0.999, p = 0.000). Notably, ATC patients with high TTK protein expression had significantly lower overall survival rates compared to those with low expression, effectively predicting prognosis (AUC = 0.833, p = 0.046).ConclusionTTK expression is markedly upregulated in highly malignant ATC tissues compared to benign lesions and PTC. The protein level of TTK emerges as a valuable diagnostic marker for distinguishing between benign and malignant thyroid conditions and serves as a crucial prognostic indicator for ATC. Further exploration and validation of its application in clinical practice are warranted.Keywords: anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, immunohistochemical staining, prognosis, threonine and tyrosine kinase, thyroid cancer

Introduction

Thyroid cancer (TC) is a prevalent endocrine malignancy, with rising global incidence, especially among females [1]. TC generally has a favorable outlook, with a five‐year survival rate over 98% [2]. However, anaplastic thyroid cancer (ATC) is notably aggressive, lacking effective treatments and showing a one‐year survival rate below 20% [3]. Advances in molecular biology and genomics have introduced new markers and targets for early diagnosis and treatment, enhancing prospects through minimally invasive surgery, radioactive iodine therapy, kinase inhibitors, and immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Threonine and tyrosine kinase (TTK) is key in spindle assembly checkpoints and chromosome segregation [4]. Overexpression of TTK is linked to poor prognosis in cancers like ovarian [5], endometrial [6], breast [7], and glioblastoma [8]. TTK inhibitors demonstrate significant anti‐cancer effects in animal studies on hepatocellular carcinoma [9] and triple‐negative breast cancer [10]. Clinical trials for TTK inhibitors in advanced cancers show promising antitumor activity, with multiple candidates advancing to phase II and III clinical evaluations (ClinicalTrials.gov ID/Drug: NCT03328494/BOS172722; NCT02138812/BAY1161909; NCT02366949/BAY1217389).

Research on TTK's expression in TC and its prognosis is rare. This study aims to analyze the expression characteristics of TTK across nodular goiter (NG), thyroid adenoma (TA), classical thyroid papillary carcinoma (CPTC), and ATC tissues through bioinformatics analysis using public data combined with immunohistochemical staining (IHC) of clinical paraffin‐embedded tissue samples. The goal is to provide laboratory evidence and insights for developing novel diagnostic methods and therapies for highly malignant tumors like ATC.

Clinical Sample Detection

Study Subjects

The study included patients diagnosed with thyroid diseases at Qinghai Provincial People's Hospital between January 1, 2015 and December 31, 2020, who underwent surgical treatment. The clinical information and remaining paraffin‐embedded tissue sections post‐pathological diagnosis were utilized as research subjects. The entire research process was conducted in vitro to avoid any additional health risks to the patients, and their personal information was strictly confidential.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion Criteria: (1) First‐time diagnosis of NG, TA, CPTC, or ATC; (2) Underwent surgery at Qinghai Provincial People's Hospital with remaining paraffin‐embedded thyroid lesion tissues; (3) Matched other types of thyroid disease cases based on age, sex, and tumor volume during ATC case surgeries; the number should be no less than twice that of ATC cases.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) History of other malignant tumors or development of other malignancies post‐surgery; (2) Presence of infectious diseases or parasitic infections such as hepatitis B, hepatitis A, HIV/AIDS, syphilis, echinococcosis, brucellosis, etc.; (3) Incomplete clinical information or follow‐up data.

Sample and Information Collection

Patient information was retrieved from the Hospital Information System (HIS), including gender, date of birth, date of surgery, clinical diagnosis, and follow‐up data up to the present. Using the patient ID number, we accessed the Laboratory Information System (LIS) to obtain the pathology number for each patient. Original HE‐stained slides and remaining paraffin blocks were located; after re‐evaluating these slides to confirm accurate diagnoses, continuous paraffin sections were prepared from any remaining thyroid tissue blocks for further analysis.

Immunohistochemical Staining and Result Interpretation

Paraffin‐embedded tissue sections underwent hematoxylin–eosin staining (HE) and IHC. HE followed the reagent kit instructions, while IHC was performed with a BenchMark ULTRA automated system, targeting markers such as TTK, Ki67, TOP2A, CD20, CD11c, CD123, CK19, TPO, Galectin3, and CD56. Antigen retrieval was done at 98°C for 30 min, with primary and secondary antibody incubations at 37°C for 40 and 20 min, respectively. The DAB reaction and hematoxylin counterstaining followed the manufacturer's guidelines.

After staining, sections were dehydrated with ethanol and cleared with xylene before mounting with neutral resin. For analysis, five images from randomly selected areas were captured at 200× magnification. Image J software calculated the average optical density (AOD) for each marker, with the mean AOD from these images representing the score for each section [18, 19]. Details about the reagents, including catalog numbers and manufacturers, are in Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative data are shown as medians with interquartile ranges, while qualitative data are presented as counts and percentages. Data analysis and visualization were done using SPSS 26.0, Python, and GraphPad Prism. For statistical analysis, the Mann–Whitney U test compared two groups of quantitative data, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for multiple group comparisons. The chi‐square test assessed differences in qualitative data. Spearman correlation analysis evaluated relationships between data sets. Predictive values for patient prognosis were analyzed using 2 × 2 contingency tables, receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC), and Kaplan–Meier curves. A p‐value < 0.05 was deemed significant.

Results

Public Data Analysis Results

Upregulation of TTK mRNA Expression in TC

The UALCAN database analysis shows that TTK mRNA expression is significantly higher in TC (n = 505) compared to adjacent non‐cancerous tissues (n = 59) (Figure 1A). There is no significant difference in TTK mRNA levels between TC with (N1, n = 58) and without lymph node metastasis (N0, n = 230) (Figure 1B). Stage II TC exhibited the lowest TTK mRNA expression compared to Stages I (n = 52, p = 0.003), III (n = 112, p = 0.028), and IV (n = 55, p = 0.035), but no significant differences were found compared to non‐cancerous tissue (Figure 1C). High‐cell variant papillary thyroid carcinoma showed significantly elevated TTK mRNA levels (n = 36, p = 0.030) compared to non‐cancerous tissue, while CPTC (n = 358) and follicular thyroid papillary carcinoma (n = 102) did not (Figure 1D). TC patients aged 21–40 years had significantly higher TTK mRNA expression than those aged 41–60 years (n = 175 vs. n = 200, p = 0.000) (Figure 1E).

Additionally, in a study of 143 cases from four GEO datasets (GSE33630, GSE53157, GSE65144, GSE85457) including 15 normal (NC), 45 adjacent to PTC (PTCAD), 56 PTC, and 27 ATC cases (Table S2), TTK mRNA was highest in ATC (vs. NC, p = 0.000; vs. PTCAD, p = 0.000; vs. PTC, p = 0.003) (Figure 1F). TTK mRNA in PTC was significantly higher than in PTCAD (p = 0.000), with no significant difference between NC and PTCAD.

These findings demonstrate that TTK mRNA is significantly upregulated in TC tissues, and its expression level correlates with tumor pathological subtype, patient age, and clinical stage, with the highest expression observed in highly aggressive ATC, suggesting TTK functions as an oncogene in thyroid carcinogenesis. Since promoter DNA methylation is the most common epigenetic mechanism driving aberrant gene expression in tumors, we next investigated whether hypomethylation underlies the observed TTK upregulation in TC.

Downregulation of TTK Methylation Levels in TC

The UALCAN database reveals that TTK methylation levels are significantly lower in TC (n = 507) compared to adjacent non‐cancerous tissues (n = 56, p = 0.000) (Figure 1G). TC with lymph node metastasis (N1, n = 226) has significantly lower TTK methylation than TC without metastasis (N0, n = 231, p = 0.000) (Figure 1H). All TC stages, except stage II, show significant methylation downregulation compared to non‐cancerous tissues, with stage IV having the lowest methylation levels (Figure 1I). Additionally, TTK methylation is lower in tumor tissues across various TC tissue types (Figure 1J) and age groups (Figure 1K) than in adjacent non‐cancerous tissues, though there are no significant differences among these subgroups. These results confirm that promoter hypomethylation drives TTK overexpression in TC, and lower methylation levels correlate with aggressive clinical features including lymph node metastasis and advanced stage. To further elucidate the oncogenic mechanisms of TTK overexpression in TC, we performed functional enrichment analysis of genes co‐expressed with TTK.

Functional Analysis and Pathways of Co‐Expressed Genes With TTK in Thyroid Cancer Tissue

Using the LinkedOmics database and criteria of correlation coefficient (r) > 0.3 or < −0.3, p < 0.05, and FDR < 0.05, 969 genes were found to significantly correlate with TTK expression in TC (Table S3). Of these, 888 genes had positive correlations with TTK expression (Figure 2A). GO and KEGG analyses showed that positively correlated genes are mainly associated with “immune system process,” “mitotic cell cycle,” and “mitotic cell cycle process” (Figure 2B), as well as “cell cycle,” “DNA replication,” and “chemokine signaling pathway” (Figure 2C). And 81 genes exhibited negative correlations (Figure 2D), and linked to functions like “cytoplasm,” “branched‐chain amino acid metabolic process,” and “flavin adenine dinucleotide binding” (Figure 2E), and pathways such as “mannose type O‐glycan biosynthesis,” “synaptic vesicle cycle,” and “metabolic pathways” (Figure 2F). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that TTK primarily regulates core processes of tumor proliferation, including cell cycle progression and DNA replication. Notably, significant enrichment in the chemokine signaling pathway suggests TTK may also modulate the tumor immune microenvironment. Consistent with these findings, we further examined the correlations between TTK expression and TC driver gene mutations, as well as immune cell infiltration.

Association of High‐Frequency Mutated Genes and Immune Infiltration in TC With TTK mRNA Expression

Using the TIMER2.0 database, it was found that patients with NRAS mutations showed significantly reduced TTK mRNA expression compared to those with wild‐type NRAS (p = 0.023) (Figure 3A). No significant differences in TTK mRNA expression were seen between TC patients with wild‐type vs. mutated BRAF (p = 0.059), HRAS (p = 0.77), or TERT genes (p = 0.8) (Figure 3B–D). Additionally, using a r > 0.3 or < −0.3 and p < 0.05, we found significant positive correlations between TTK mRNA expression and the infiltration of neutrophils (r = 0.431, p = 0.000) (Figure 3E), myeloid dendritic cells (r = 0.411, p = 0.000) (Figure 3F), CD4+ T cells (r = 0.352, p = 0.000) (Figure 3G), and B cells (r = 0.318, p = 0.000) (Figure 3H). These findings indicate that TTK expression is specifically associated with NRAS mutation status (with significantly lower expression in NRAS‐mutant tumors) and strongly correlates with the infiltration of multiple innate and adaptive immune cells—supporting its role in tumor‐immune crosstalk. To translate these molecular findings into clinical applications, we next evaluated the prognostic value of TTK mRNA expression in TC patients.

Prognostic Implications of TTK mRNA Expression in TC Patients

In the GEPIA database analysis, TC patients were stratified into high‐expression group for TTK mRNA levels above the median cut‐off value (n = 253) and low‐expression group below this threshold (n = 253). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis indicated no significant difference in overall survival (OS) between these two groups (p = 0.34) (Figure 3I). However, disease‐free survival (DFS) was significantly lower among patients with high TTK expression compared to those with low expression levels (p = 0.007) (Figure 3J). The results indicate that TTK mRNA expression is upregulated in TC, which correlates with a higher risk of recurrence or metastasis. Further investigation identified 100 genes associated with DFS in TC cases (Table S4). Although TTK expression does not affect overall survival in TC patients (consistent with their generally favorable prognosis), it is an independent risk factor for disease recurrence and metastasis. To validate TTK expression at the protein level and identify its direct interacting partners, we analyzed data from the HPA and PPI network of TTK.

Expression of TTK Protein in Thyroid Cancer Tissue and Its PPI

The HPA database results show that TTK protein is located in the cytoplasm (Figure 4A,B). In normal thyroid epithelial cells, TTK has weak positive expression (Figure 4A), while in PTC tumor cells, it has moderate diffuse expression, with some cells showing strong positive expression (Figure 4B). The HPA system also identified 40 markers linked to TC prognosis (Table S5).

Through database searches, we found 120 indicators related to TTK: (1) the top 40 genes with the highest co‐expression correlation with TTK in TC from LinkedOmics (Table S3); (2) the top 40 genes significantly correlated with DFS in TC from GEPIA (Table S4); and (3) the top 40 genes associated with TC prognosis per HPA (Table S5).

The String database analysis revealed 15 proteins in the PPI network involving TTK (Figure 4C) (Table S6), divided into three groups: the green group (5 proteins, including TTK) related to “Regulation of mitotic sister chromatid segregation and Kinesin motor domain conserved site,” the red group (6 proteins) linked to “Chromosome condensation” and “Kinetochores signal amplification and Condensin complex,” and the blue group (4 proteins) associated with “Spindle elongation” and “Outer kinetochore.” These indicators suggest close ties to cellular proliferation and division.

In summary, integrated transcriptomic, epigenomic, and proteomic database analyses consistently demonstrate that TTK is overexpressed in TC due to promoter hypomethylation. TTK drives thyroid tumorigenesis primarily by dysregulating cell cycle progression and may also modulate the tumor immune microenvironment. High TTK mRNA expression predicts poor disease‐free survival in TC patients, and its PPI network further confirms its central role in mitotic regulation. To validate these bioinformatics findings in clinical specimens and specifically explore the prognostic value of TTK in highly lethal ATC, we performed immunohistochemical staining analysis on our institutional cohort.

Patient Enrollment and IHC Testing Results

Basic Information of Enrolled Cases

A total of 13 patients with ATC were enrolled in this study, comprising 8 females and 5 males, with a median age of 65 years (56, 69), and OS of 10 months [7, 14]. Based on the gender, age, and tumor volume of ATC patients, we matched each group with 26 cases from the NG, TA, and CPTC cohorts respectively. The Kruskal–Wallis test indicated no significant differences in tumor volumes among the groups (p = 0.980). Detailed information regarding the enrolled cases can be found in Table S7.

Expression Differences of TTK Protein in Various Thyroid Disease Tissues

CK19, TPO, Galectin‐3, and CD56 are widely used immunohistochemical markers for the differential diagnosis of benign and malignant thyroid lesions, while Ki67, TOP2A, CD20, CD11c, and CD123 reflect tumor proliferation activity and immune cell infiltration status. We therefore examined the expression levels of these 10 markers in thyroid lesion tissues by IHC. The results (Figure 5A) show that TTK protein is found in the cytoplasm and membrane, with strong diffuse positive expression in ATC, moderate in CPTC, and weak or negative in TA and NG. Significant differences in TTK and nine other proteins were observed between benign lesions (BL: NG, TA) and malignant lesions (ML: CPTC, ATC) (Figure 5B). Using ROC and chi‐square tests, the effectiveness of TTK in distinguishing BL from ML was analyzed, yielding an AUC of 0.999 (p = 0.000), with a cutoff value of 0.355 (Figure 5C). The chi‐square test showed a kappa of 0.93, sensitivity of 97.4% (95% CI: 86.6%–99.6%), specificity of 96.15% (95% CI: 87.0%–98.9%), and overall accuracy of 96.7% (95% CI: 90.8%–98.9%).

When comparing TA to CPTC, all markers except TOP2A showed significant differences (Figure 5D). TTK's effectiveness here resulted in an AUC of 0.983 (p = 0.000, cutoff = 0.372) (Figure 5E), with a kappa of 0.88, sensitivity of 92.3% (95% CI: 75.9%–97.9%), specificity of 80.8% (95% CI: 81.1%–99.3%), and accuracy of 94.2% (95% CI: 84.4%–98.0%). CPTC and ATC comparisons found significant differences in all markers except TOP2A (Figure 5F), with an AUC of 0.856 for TTK (p = 0.000, cutoff = 0.505) (Figure 5G). The kappa was 0.56, sensitivity 76.9% (95% CI: 49.7%–91.8%), specificity 80.8% (95% CI: 62.1%–91.5%), and accuracy 79.5% (95% CI: 64.5%–89.2%).

In summary, TTK expression is a strong indicator for distinguishing between benign and malignant thyroid lesions but is less effective in differentiating between CPTC and ATC. Given that TTK protein expression levels increase progressively with thyroid tumor malignant grade, we further explored its correlation with clinical prognosis in both CPTC and ATC patients.

Correlation Between TTK Protein Expression and Prognosis of TC

In cases of CPTC, TTK protein expression was significantly higher in T4 compared to T2 cases (p = 0.042) (Figure 6A). Cases with lymph node metastasis (N1) showed significantly higher expression than those without lymph node metastasis (N0) (p = 0.021) (Figure 6B), and III/IV stage cases displayed significantly greater expression than I stage (p = 0.016) (Figure 6C). There was no significant correlation between TTK expression and age (r = 0.215, p = 0.292) (Figure 6D), but a significant positive correlation with tumor volume (r = 0.481, p = 0.013) (Figure 6E). Patients were categorized into TTK High (≥ 0.447) and TTK Low (< 0.447) groups based on the median TTK expression, with no significant difference in DFS outcomes between the groups (p = 0.617) (Figure 6F).

Additionally, TTK expression is not associated with patient age (D) but shows a significant positive correlation with tumor volume (E). In ATC cases, there were no significant differences in TTK protein expression among stages T2 to T4 (Figure 6G). ATC cases with OS less than 10 months had significantly higher levels of TTK expression compared to those with OS exceeding 10 months (p = 0.042) (Figure 6H). Patients were divided based on median TTK expression into TTK Low (< 0.567) and TTK High (≥ 0.567) groups. OS was significantly longer in the TTK Low group compared to the TTK High group (p = 0.031) (Figure 6I). Similar to CPTC, no correlation was found between age and TTK levels (r = 0.215, p = 0.481) (Figure 6J), but strong positive correlations were observed with tumor volume (r = 659, p = 0.014) (Figure 6K) and a negative correlation with OS (Figure 6L). Further analysis showed a predictive value of TTK expression levels for OS in ATC patients, with an AUC of 0.833 (p = 0.046) (Figure 6M). Survival curves indicated significantly lower OS in the TTK High group compared to the TTK Low group (p = 0.037) (Figure 6N), suggesting a correlation between TTK protein expression levels and prognosis in ATC where higher TTK expression is associated with poorer OS outcomes. TTK protein expression levels may serve as an effective predictor of prognosis in ATC.

Taken together, our clinical sample validation confirms the bioinformatics findings and demonstrates that TTK protein overexpression is closely associated with aggressive clinicopathological features and poor overall survival in ATC, providing direct clinical evidence for its role as a negative prognostic biomarker.

Discussion

The TTK gene, located on 6q14.1, encodes a protein kinase that phosphorylates tyrosine, serine, and threonine, playing a crucial role in chromosome alignment at the centromere during mitosis and centrosome replication. Overexpression or improper degradation of TTK can cause excessive centrosomes, leading to abnormal mitosis and tumorigenesis [20]. ATC, despite representing only 1% of thyroid cancers, is highly malignant and lacks effective treatments [21]. Our research focuses on identifying new prognostic indicators and therapeutic targets for ATC. Previous studies [22] using public single‐cell transcriptomic data and clinical samples revealed reduced cytotoxicity in CD8+ T cells, γδT cells, and NK cells, along with increased expression of immune checkpoint molecules such as LAG3, PD1, HAVCR2, and TIGIT. This study explores TTK mRNA and protein expression in thyroid lesions from public data and clinical samples to assess their association with CPTC and ATC prognosis, aiming to provide references for developing prognostic predictions and therapeutic targets for ATC.

In 2023, Ma et al. analyzed TCGA database data and found that upregulated TTK mRNA was associated with shorter disease‐free and progression‐free intervals in PTC [23]. In vitro experiments further revealed that TTK knockdown inhibits PTC cell growth, migration, and invasion, proposing TTK as a potential biomarker for PTC prognosis. Our study identified significant differences in TTK mRNA (Figure 1F) and protein levels (Figure 5B,D) between benign and malignant thyroid lesions. Additional analyses suggested that TTK protein expression can differentiate between thyroid lesions such as TA and CPTC (Figure 5C,E). However, while public data indicated a strong link between TTK mRNA expression and DFS in TC (Figure 3J), our IHC results showed no significant correlation between TTK protein levels and DFS in CPTC (Figure 6F). This discrepancy may arise because only three out of twenty‐six CPTC patients experienced recurrence.

An analysis of three GEO datasets (GSE27155, GSE29265, GSE65144) has revealed that TTK mRNA is significantly upregulated in ATC tissues compared to normal thyroid tissues, with several cell cycle‐related regulatory genes, including TTK, being enriched among the upregulated genes [24]. Our findings demonstrate that both TTK mRNA and protein levels are notably higher in ATC than in normal or benign thyroid tissues, and are further elevated compared to CPTC (Figures 1F and 5B,D,F). We also found that elevated TTK protein expression is linked to shorter OS in ATC patients, suggesting its potential role as a prognostic marker for ATC (Figure 6L–N).

Common IHC markers used in the pathological diagnosis of thyroid diseases include CK19, TPO, Galectin‐3, CD56, and Ki67 [25]. The IHC results in this study conform to expectations for marker expression in NG, TA, and CPTC [26]. Notably, all ATC cases showed negative CK19 protein expression, indicating a distinct molecular phenotype from PTC. Interestingly, more than half of the ATC cases (7/13) exhibited strong focal or diffuse positivity for CD56, a neural adhesion molecule commonly expressed in nerve cells and activated immune cells and often used as a marker for neuroendocrine tumors. Loss or reduced CD56 expression is associated with tumor metastasis and poor prognosis [27]. In solid tumors like Ewing's sarcoma, patients with fewer CD56‐positive cells have higher progression‐free survival, whereas high levels of these cells are independent risk factors for local recurrence [28]. Furthermore, CD56 correlates with perineural invasion and poor prognosis in pancreatic cancer [29] but does not significantly influence outcomes in breast invasive ductal carcinoma [30]. While research shows that 90.3% of PTC cases (28/31) lack CD56 expression, 75% (3/4) of ATC cases express it [31]. Given the rarity of reports on CD56 expression in ATC, its strong positive expression in some ATC cases, contrasting sharply with PTC, warrants further investigation.

TOP2A is a key regulatory factor in chromatin topology, essential for maintaining genomic integrity during processes like transcription, replication, and cell division. It has recently emerged as a therapeutic target in various malignancies [32]. High TOP2A mRNA expression has been linked to poor prognosis in TC [33, 34]. Our findings reveal a significant positive correlation between TOP2A and TTK mRNA levels in TC. Despite this, TOP2A protein showed strong diffuse positivity across four types of thyroid lesions without notable differences, likely due to the active proliferation of thyroid epithelium.

Using the TIMER 2.0 system, we found a significant positive correlation between TTK mRNA levels and the infiltration abundance of immune cells, such as B cells and myeloid dendritic cells, in TC (Figure 3E–H). We evaluated the protein expression of the B cell marker CD20 and myeloid dendritic cell markers CD11c and CD123. The results showed that CD20 expression at the protein level aligned with TTK levels, suggesting increased B cell infiltration in more malignant thyroid lesions. However, CD11c and CD123 proteins, which are not exclusively expressed by immune cells, were also present in varying degrees in thyroid epithelial or tumor cells across all tissue types. Notably, CD11c was most abundant in CPTC, while CD123 peaked in TA. This complicates direct comparisons between IHC results and TIMER 2.0 analysis outcomes. In future studies, we plan to use multiplex IHC techniques for more precise identification of immune cells.

Additionally, public data analysis reveals that TTK DNA methylation levels generally exhibit an inverse relationship with mRNA expression in various types of TC (i.e., higher mRNA expression correlates with lower DNA methylation) (Figure 1). Similar patterns have been observed in other tumors and genes, such as HOXD10 in PTC [35], JAK3 in renal clear cell carcinoma [36], DKK‐3 in hepatocellular carcinoma [37], and RASSF2A in endometrial cancer [38]. Studies show DNA methylation can affect mRNA expression; for example, decitabine‐induced demethylation increases TTK expression in hepatocellular carcinoma cells [39]. However, this phenomenon of TTK has not been reported in TC cells. Therefore, we plan to investigate the relationship between TTK methylation levels and mRNA expression through in vitro experiments on TC tumor cells, potentially providing new insights for ATC treatment.

While the 5‐year survival rate for TC is 98.5%, indicating a generally favorable prognosis [1], effective treatments are still lacking for highly malignant ATC and recurrent PTC. Understanding the molecular characteristics of these poor‐prognosis TCs is crucial for developing new therapies. This study, based on public data and a small clinical sample, preliminarily confirms that high TTK expression correlates with increased malignancy and poor prognosis in thyroid lesions, suggesting that IHC detection of TTK may have clinical significance. Future research will include ATC and recurrent PTC cases to further explore TTK's relationship with prognosis and assess the effects of TTK inhibitors using animal models. Given the rarity of ATC cases, single‐center studies face challenges in obtaining sufficient samples for in‐depth analysis. We hope more researchers will conduct similar studies to validate our findings.

Conclusions

The expression characteristics of TTK in ATC and its association with prognosis are seldom reported in the literature. Our study reveals that TTK expression is significantly upregulated in ATC compared to normal thyroid tissue, benign thyroid lesions, and low‐grade malignant thyroid cancer. Elevated TTK protein expression may serve as a diagnostic marker for distinguishing between benign and malignant thyroid lesions, as well as a prognostic predictor for ATC. These findings highlight the potential clinical utility of TTK as a diagnostic and prognostic tool, warranting further exploration and validation in clinical settings.
