Psychological flexibility mediates the long-term impact of adverse interpersonal experiences in childhood and adolescence
Authors: Leonardo Fazio, Cristina Semeraro, Teresa Claudia Pennacchio, Martina Grazia Asselti, Marianna Russo, Rosalinda Cassibba, Alessandro Bertolino, Antonio Rampino, Ignazio Grattagliano, Giulio Pergola, Linda Antonella Antonucci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly exposure to interpersonal adverse events, such as traumatic childhood events or exposure to bullying, abuse, or harassment, can impact an adult’s psychological functioning. The negative effects on quality of life and the increased risk of psychopathological outcomes may underscore long-lasting vulnerabilities. Psychological flexibility is defined as the ability to be present in the moment and to pursue one’s values, and it can facilitate coping with adverse events occurring in the present. An open question is whether psychological flexibility may contribute to reducing the long-term consequences of exposure to early adversities. The relevance of this research is that psychological flexibility is modifiable through intervention. The present study investigated the potential role of psychological flexibility in modulating the relationship between early exposure to interpersonal adverse events and two outcomes in young adults: quality of life and psychological symptomatology.MethodsWe recruited one hundred and nine women aged 19–21 years. Exposure to adverse interpersonal events was assessed with the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire and the Bullying Scale; quality of life and psychological symptomatology were measured with the World Health Organization Quality of Life Scale and the Symptom checklist-90, respectively; psychological flexibility was assessed with the Multidimensional Psychological Flexibility Inventory.ResultsThe frequency of adverse interpersonal events was associated with lower quality of life and greater psychological symptoms. Psychological flexibility mediated the relationship between the frequency of interpersonal adverse events and measures of quality of life, with an indirect effect explaining 18–42% of the total effect. Similarly, psychological flexibility mediated between the frequency of interpersonal adverse events and psychological symptomatology, with an indirect effect explaining 21–22% of the total effect.ConclusionThe present study evaluates the impact of exposure to interpersonal adverse events on different aspects of quality of life and psychological symptomatology. The results suggest that psychological flexibility mediates the long-term repercussions of interpersonal adversity. As psychological flexibility is a modifiable skill, interventions aimed at enhancing it in individuals with early exposure to adverse interpersonal events, such as traumatic childhood events, bullying, abuse, or harassment, could be used to reduce the long-term effects of past adversities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03297-5.

Introduction

Psychological flexibility is the ability to maintain contact with the present moment and act effectively following personal values even in the presence of interfering thoughts, emotions, and bodily sensations [1]. Psychological flexibility incorporates six core processes including acceptance, cognitive defusion, contact with the present moment, self as context, values, and committed action [1–3], which enable individuals to navigate life’s challenges with adaptability and resilience. In contrast, when these processes manifest in rigid and maladaptive forms, such as experiential avoidance, cognitive fusion, disconnection from the present moment, self as content, lack of value clarity, and uncommitted actions, they contribute to psychological inflexibility. Maintaining good psychological flexibility, as well as minimizing psychological inflexibility is essential for preserving an individual’s quality of life, well-being, health [4–6] and also for supporting the coping with life adversity [7–9]. Psychological flexibility is partly related to other forms of mental flexibility, such as cognitive flexibility, the ability to flexibly adapt one’s behavior and thoughts to changing environmental demands [10–13], which, like psychological flexibility, can help individuals respond to adverse or stressful life experiences [14]. However, unlike cognitive flexibility, psychological flexibility also recognizes the possibility that individuals may see themselves as separate from their experiences and emotions and, therefore, may sit back and accept these experiences rather than engage in behavioral change or avoidance [1, 10].

Indeed, higher psychological flexibility is associated with a greater ability to accept adverse or stressful life experiences, adapt to change, and support the individual in maintaining a meaningful and consistent direction in life [1, 15–19]. The impact that psychological flexibility can have in coping with adverse experiences can be better understood by analyzing more in depth its core processes. Cognitive defusion, for example, allows individuals to distance themselves from negative thoughts that may emerge after adverse experiences, reducing their impact on psychological well-being [20]. Consistently, this process has been shown to be effective in reducing depressive symptoms and distress following stressful conditions, traumatic experiences, or illness [21–26]. Furthermore, acceptance might support individuals in reducing experiential avoidance following traumatic or adverse experiences [27–29]. Indeed, accepting difficult thoughts and emotions facilitates trauma processing and reduces emotional reactivity [30]. Abilities in contacting with the present moment and in self-as-context processes might supporting individuals with traumatic experiences in shifting their focus away from the stressful experience and maintaining a stable perspective of self [25, 31], thus promoting consistency between personal values and behavior (values and committed action processes), even in the presence of psychological distress [29, 32, 33]. Taken together, these findings speak in favor of psychological flexibility acting as a protective and transformative factor on adverse life experiences. These studies support the notion that psychological flexibility plays an important role in mitigating the psychological impact of stressors that an individual experiences in their current life, but even adverse events from the past can psychologically impact an individual: individuals reporting early adversities report significantly lower scores in quality of life [34, 35] and more severe psychological symptoms [36–41]. This impact on adult life occurs mainly from adverse events that occur during sensitive periods such as childhood and adolescence. Traumatic childhood experiences have been consistently associated with an increased risk of physical and mental health problems in adulthood [37, 42–44] and with a poorer quality of life [34, 35]. Examples include emotional, physical, and sexual abuse or acts of emotional and physical neglect, which often occur in the context of family or caregiving [42, 45], Many studies have also investigated the relationship between social-relational adverse experiences, such as bullying victimization, and mental health. These experiences are more common in school or peer contexts [46]. They are characterized by the intentional goal of generating harm or distress through direct aggression, name-calling, or isolation from a group [47]. As with childhood traumatic experiences, social-relational adverse experiences also impact adults’ mental and physical health [48, 49] and perceived quality of life [37, 50–53], and increase the risk for psychiatric disorders [37, 54] in adulthood. In addition, social-relational adverse experiences can also occur in many contexts of adult life (e.g., workplace, family, or interpersonal contexts [55–57]), thus acting as a potential chronic stressful life event.

Past adverse events can thus severely impinge on some pivotal dimensions of an individual’s well-being and functioning. Notable among these is the impact of interpersonal adverse childhood and adolescent experiences on quality of life. Quality of life reflects the individual’s perception of their position in life regarding aims, expectations, standards, and concerns [58].

It can be considered a multidimensional construct involving several domains, including the health and physical quality of life domain, the psychological domain, the social and interpersonal domain, and the environmental context domain [58, 59]. All these domains can be impacted by exposure to adverse events in the past [35, 51–53, 60–63]. Also impacted by interpersonal adverse experiences in childhood and adolescence is psychological symptomatology: past adverse experiences have been extensively associated with depressive, anxiety, psychotic, or psychosomatic manifestations [37, 38, 44]. These symptomatologic dimensions, although different in their expressions, all have a strong impact on the individual in terms of functioning, autonomy, and the ability to act according to personal aims and expectations.

To date, no work has investigated whether psychological flexibility plays a role in the relationship between interpersonal adverse experiences in childhood and adolescence, quality of life, and psychological symptomatology in adulthood. Previous studies have reported that psychological flexibility can mitigate the impact of current stressors [7, 8, 17–19, 64–66], even during childhood and adolescence [67], while psychological inflexibility can impair individual response to stressful events [36, 68]. However, the effect of this function on the consequences of past adverse experiences in the present is not well known. As happens with currently active stressors, psychological flexibility may mitigate the effects of past adverse experiences as well.

Here, we focused on the exposure to childhood traumatic events and social-relational adverse experiences to investigate both occurrences in the context of family care and those in the context of peers. The present study hypothesized that psychological flexibility may mediate the relationship between past adverse interpersonal experiences and adults’ quality of life and psychological symptoms. More specifically, we expect that adequate levels of psychological flexibility, as well as lower levels of psychological inflexibility, may contribute to a reduction in the long-term effects of past adversities.

Materials and methods

Participants

One hundred and nine healthy female volunteers (mean age 20.1 +/- 0.6 years, range 19–21 years) were included in the study. Participants were recruited from a predominantly female university population attending the Science of Primary Education course, hence we decided to limit recruitment to women only to maintain sample homogeneity and avoid bias due to gender [69–72].

Participants were selected from an initial recruitment sample of one hundred twenty-eight participants, from which we excluded participants who had incomplete data collection. To estimate the sample size, we used a Monte Carlo simulation power analysis [73], based on previous studies [36]. The power was 0.83 in the initially recruited sample and 0.76 in the final study sample of one hundred and nine participants. Socioeconomic background status was measured for each participant using the Hollingshead Four Factor Index (mean 29.2 +/- 12.4) [74]. We obtained an estimate of intelligence quotient (IQ) using the Italian version of the Wide Reading Achievement Test (mean 110.2 +/- 3.8) [75]. Exclusion criteria were substance abuse or addiction in the previous six months and the presence of a neurological disorder (including intellectual disability or autism spectrum disorder). The present study was approved by the local Institutional Review Board, i.e., the “Comitato Etico Indipendente” of the University of Bari Aldo Moro (approval number: 7098), and we obtained written informed consent from all subjects after providing a full explanation of all procedures performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Psychological assessment

Retrospective measures of interpersonal adverse experiences in childhood and adolescence

Each participant underwent a retrospective assessment of possible interpersonal adverse experiences in childhood and adolescence. The Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (Short Form: CTQ-SF) [45, 76], in its Italian version [77] was used to assess exposure to traumatic events or maltreatment in childhood. The CTQ-SF is a standardized self-report inventory that measures childhood trauma exposure using five subscales: emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect, plus a minimization/denial scale, which assesses the likelihood of not reporting traumatic experiences. In total, the CTQ-SF includes 28 items, with answers measured on a 5-point Likert scale. The subscale scores have test-retest reliability coefficients ranging from 0.87 to 0.96 [77]. We considered the CTQ-SF total score (CTQ-T) for the study purposes.

Exposure to social-relational adverse experiences was investigated with the Bullying Scale for Adults (BSA) in its Italian version [78]. The BSA was developed to assess adults’ self-reported past experiences of bullying, harassment, and abuse in various contexts, such as school, peer, work, educational, and family settings. The entire scale is divided into three parts: part A measures the frequency of subjective social-relational adverse experiences, consisting of 13 items representing 13 types of behaviors. A five-point Likert rating is used for each item. There are specifiers regarding perpetrator, time, and duration. Part B investigates subjective distress associated with these adverse experiences, with six items measured on a five-point Likert scale. Part C includes two items investigating the experience of those who acted as bullies. The BSA showed a test-retest reliability coefficient of 0.94 for the Italian version [78]. For the study, we used the scale measuring the frequency (BSA-F) of episodes experienced as a victim of bullying, harassment, or abuse. We chose to focus on episode frequency because it is more comparable with exposure to adverse events as measured by the CTQ-T score while maintaining good reliability [79].

Measures of psychological flexibility

Psychological flexibility was measured with the Multidimensional Psychological Flexibility Inventory (MPFI) [2, 19], in its Italian version. The MPFI consists of 60 items rated on a 6-point Likert scale. There are 6 subscales related to psychological flexibility (acceptance, present-moment awareness, self-as-context, defusion, values, committed action) and six related to psychological inflexibility (experiential avoidance, lack of contact with the present moment, self-as-content, fusion, lack of contact with values, inaction) with 5 items for each subscale. In addition, global measures of flexibility and inflexibility are calculated as the average of scores in the relevant subscales. The MPFI has shown good reliability and validity in clinical and nonclinical samples, with the alpha coefficients of the Italian version ranging from 0.94 and0.85 [80–82].

In accordance with the objectives of our study, we opted to adopt a composite index that summarizes the overall balance between adaptive resources and dysfunctional processes, thus indicating the reserve of psychological flexibility available to each individual (“flexibility buffer” - MPFI-FB). We calculated this index by subtracting global psychological inflexibility scores from global psychological flexibility scores (MPFI-FB = MPFI-flexibility - MPFI-inflexibility). In our sample, the administration of the MPFI was subsequent to other psychological assessments, at an average time interval of 24 days.

Measures of quality of life and psychological symptomatology

Quality of life was investigated with the World Health Organization Quality-of-Life Scale (WHOQOL-BREF) [59, 83], a 26-item self-report instrument comprising four domains: physical health domain (WHOQOL-PH), which includes items on functional capacity, daily activities, energy, and sleep; psychological health domain (WHOQOL-PS) which contains questions on self-image, negative thoughts, positive attitudes, self-esteem, mindset, learning ability, memory concentration, religion, and mental state; social relations domain (WHOQOL-S), which investigates personal relationships, social support, and sex life; and environmental health domain (WHOQOL-E), which includes items on the safety and services of one’s living environment, financial resources, and recreational activities. Each item on the WHOQOL-BREF is scored on a five-point Likert scale. In the Italian version, the WHOQOL-BREF showed good reliability for the WHOQOL-PH, WHOQOL-PS, and WHOQOL-E dimensions, with coefficient alphas ranging from 0.8 to 0.73,

but moderate reliability (alpha 0.65) for the dimension WHOQOL-S [83]. Here we considered both the score given to each of the four domains (WHOQOL-PH, WHOQOL-PS, WHOQOL-S, and WHOQOL-E), and the total score (WHOQOL-T), obtained from the sum of the four domains.

Psychological symptomatology was instead investigated with the Symptom checklist-90 (SCL-90) [84, 85] a self-report questionnaire designed to assess psychopathological conditions in a general population. It consists of 90 items that can be answered on a 5-point Likert scale with a score from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely). Subjects respond by considering their behaviors, feelings, and thoughts from the past week. SCL-90 has good reliability with the alpha coefficients of the various indices between 0.70 and 0.96 for the Italian version [84]. The SCL-90 assesses 10 psychopathological scales and provides 3 global indices, including the Global Severity Index (SCL90-GSI), a screening index of the overall severity of psychological symptoms, which was used in the present study.

Statistical analysis

To facilitate comparison between the variables of interest, these were standardized into z-scores and then transformed into T-scores so that all values have a positive sign. Skewness and kurtosis were used to assess the normality of the variables. We adopted the conservative criterion of skewness and kurtosis < 2 to identify deviations from normality [86]. All the variables showed acceptable normality, except the CTQ-T, which showed a strongly skewed and leptokurtic distribution (CTQ-T: skewness = 2.7; kurtosis = 8). Consequently, a reciprocal transformation (Y = 1/X) was adopted for the CTQ-T variable. This transformation has been shown to be more effective in compressing high values and expanding smaller ones, thereby facilitating the smoothing of deviations from normality in the data distribution with respect to both skewness and kurtosis [87–89]. Since the reciprocal transformation reverses the direction of the source variable, the sign of the transformed variables was inverted for the sake of clarity. Again, to promote clarity, we kept the CTQ-T label even for the transformed variable.

The variables of interest underwent a specific check for the potential presence of outliers, using Grubb’s tests (p < 0.05) [90]. We identified one outlier subject for both the BSA-F and MPFI-FB variables, which was then excluded from further analysis. To assess the potential effect of confounding factors, we tested the association between sociodemographic and cognitive variables (age, sociocultural background, and IQ estimation) and the variables of interest using Pearsons’s correlations (p < 0.05). We found a significant association between background socioeconomic status, measured with the Hollingshead Four Factor Index and the WHOQOL-E (r = 0.21, p = 0.03), as well as a negative association between the estimated IQ, measured with the Wide Reading Achievement Test and the WHOQOL-T, WHOQOL-PS, and WHOQOL-S measures (r=-0.20, p=-0.035; r=-0.22, p=-0.025; r=-0.20, p=-0.036; respectively). Therefore, all analyses involving these variables were adjusted for associated confounding factors (Hollingshead Four Factor Index or Wide Reading Achievement Test).

Correlation analyses

To investigate the relationship between interpersonal adverse experiences in childhood and adolescence, quality of life, and psychological symptoms, we carried out Pearson correlation analyses (p < 0.05) between the CTQ-T and the WHOQOL-BREF measures (total score, WHOQOL-T and domain scales, WHOQOL-PH, WHOQOL-PS, WHOQOL-S, and WHOQOL-E) as well as between the CTQ-T and the SCL90-GSI. Similarly, the impact of social-relational adverse experiences was assessed by Pearson correlation (p < 0.05) between BSA-F scores and scores from the WHOQOL-BREF measures, as well as between BSA-F and SCL90-GSI scores. Finally, we investigated the relationship between MPFI-FB and the variables associated with interpersonal adverse experience exposure (CTQ-T and BSA-F), as well as the relationship between MPFI-FB and the indicators of quality of life and psychological symptoms (WHOQOL-BREF measures and SCL90-GSI), with Pearson correlation (p < 0.05) analysis. All correlations with variable WHOQOL-E were corrected for the Hollingshead Four Factor Index scores using partial correlation. The same approach was used to correct the correlations with variables WHOQOL-T, WHOQOL-PS, and WHOQOL-S for the Wide Reading Achievement Test scores. Finally, using Pearson correlation (p < 0.05), we tested the potential association between the two measures of interpersonal adverse experience exposure (CTQ-T and BSA-F). All correlations were corrected for multiple comparisons with the false discovery rate procedure (Benjamini-Hochberg method) [91]. An overview of the results of all correlation analyses was reported in Supplementary Table S1.

Mediation analyses

The relationships between interpersonal adverse experiences, quality of life, and psychological symptoms were further investigated by mediation analysis to explore the potential mediating role of psychological flexibility [92]. Specifically, to explore the potential mediating effect of psychological flexibility on quality of life, separate models were created with each measure of exposure to adverse experiences as a predictor (CTQ-T or BSA-F), psychological flexibility (MPFI-FB) as a mediator, and each dimension of quality of life (WHOQOL-T, WHOQOL-PH, WHOQOL-PS, WHOQOL-S, WHOQOL-E) as outcomes. Models including WHOQOL-E as an outcome were controlled for Hollingshead Four Factor Index scores. Similarly, models including WHOQOL-T, WHOQOL-PS, and WHOQOL-S as outcomes were controlled for Wide Reading Achievement Test scores. Finally, two other models were created to explore the potential mediating effect of psychological flexibility on psychological symptomatology, with each measure of exposure to adverse experiences as a predictor (CTQ-T and BSA-F, respectively), psychological flexibility (MPFI-FB) as a mediator, and psychological symptomatology (SCL90-GSI) as an outcome. All models were bootstrapped for 1000 repetitions, with significance set at p < 0.05 [91]. An overview of the results of all the mediation analyses was reported in Supplementary Table S2.

All statistical analyses were performed with JASP software version 0.18 (jasp-stats.org), which can perform mediation analyses based on the R package “Lavaan” (lavaan.ugent.be), in addition to standard analysis procedures.

Results

Results of correlation analyses

Correlation analyses on exposure to childhood traumatic events showed a negative association between the CTQ-T and all the WHOQOL-BREF measures (WHOQOL-T: r=-0.55, p < 0.001; WHOQOL-PH: r=-0.47, p < 0.001; WHOQOL-PS: r=-0.47, p < 0.001; WHOQOL-S: r=-0.46, p < 0.001; WHOQOL-E: r=-0.45, p < 0.001) and positive association between the CTQ-T and the SCL90-GSI (r = 0.45, p < 0.001). Similarly, correlations on the frequency of social-relational adverse experiences showed a negative association between the BSA-F and the WHOQOL-T (r=-0.28, p = 0.001), as well as between BSA-F and WHOQOL-PH/WHOQOL-PS (r=-0.35, p = 0.003 and r=-0.25, p = 0.009, respectively). Again, we found a positive association between the BSA-F and the SCL90-GSI (r = 0.38, p < 0.001). Finally, correlations on the flexibility buffer (FB) showed a positive correlation between the MPFI-FB and all the WHOQOL-BREF measures (WHOQOL-T: r=-0.50, p < 0.001; WHOQOL-PH: r=-0.42, p < 0.001; WHOQOL-PS: r=-0.48, p < 0.001; WHOQOL-S: r=-0.37, p < 0.001; WHOQOL-E: r=-0.40, p < 0.001) and a negative correlation between the MPFI-FB and the SCL90-GSI (r=-0.43, p < 0.001). Also, a negative correlation between flexibility buffer and exposure to interpersonal adverse experiences in childhood and adolescence was significant (MPFI-FB - CTQ-T: r=-0.28, p = 0.004; MPFI-FB - BSA-F: r=-0.24, p = 0.013). All the p reported are FDR-corrected. Finally, we found a positive correlation between the two measures of interpersonal adverse experience exposure (BSA-F - CTQ-T: r = 0.41, p < 0.001).

Results of mediation analyses

Mediation analyses carried out on childhood traumatic events revealed a partial mediation of the flexibility buffer on the relationship between the CTQ-T and the WHOQOL-T (direct effect Z: -5.9, p < 0.001; indirect effect Z: -2.6, p = 0.01–19% of the total effect explained; total effect Z: -6.9, p < 0.001, Fig. 1a). Considering the quality of life domains, our mediation analyses showed a partial mediation of the flexibility buffer on the relationship between the CTQ-T and the WHOQOL-PH (direct effect Z: -4.6, p < 0.001; indirect effect Z: -2.3, p = 0.02–18.3% of the total effect explained; total effect Z: -5.6, p < 0.001); the CTQ-T and the WHOQOL-PS (direct effect Z: -4.5, p < 0.001; indirect effect Z: -2.5, p = 0.01–22.5% of the total effect explained; total effect Z: -5.5, p < 0.001); CTQ-T and WHOQOL-S (direct effect Z: -4.5, p < 0.001; indirect effect Z: -2.2, p = 0.03–16.1% of the total effect explained; total effect Z: -5.4, p < 0.001) and the CTQ-T and the WHOQOL-E (direct effect Z: -4.3, p < 0.001; indirect effect Z: -2.3, p = 0.02–18.5% of the total effect explained; total effect Z: -5.2, p < 0.001). Similarly, a partial mediating role of the flexibility buffer emerged in the relationship between the CTQ-T and the SCL90-GSI (direct effect Z: 4.3, p < 0.001; indirect effect Z: 2.4, p = 0.016–20.8% of the total effect explained; total effect Z: 5.4, p < 0.001, Fig. 1b).

Analyses carried out on bullying victimization showed partial mediation of the flexibility buffer on the relationship between the BSA-F and the WHOQOL-T (direct effect Z: -2, p = 0.04; indirect effect Z: -2.3, p = 0.02–39.1% of the total effect explained; total effect Z: -3.1, p = 0.002, Fig. 1c). Considering the quality of life domains, mediation analyses showed partial mediation of the flexibility buffer on the relationship between the BSA-F and the WHOQOL-PH (direct effect Z: -3.1, p = 0.02; indirect effect Z: -2.2, p = 0.029–24.4% of the total effect explained; total effect Z: -3.8, p < 0.001) and a total mediation of the flexibility buffer on the relationship between the BSA-F and the WHOQOL-PS (direct effect Z: -1.7, p = 0.09; indirect effect Z: -2.3, p = 0.021–42.6% of the total effect explained; total effect Z: -2.3, p = 0.007). We also found an inconsistent mediation [93] of the flexibility buffer on the relationship between the BSA-F and the WHOQOL-E direct effect Z: -1.0, p = 0.3; indirect effect Z: -2.2, p = 0.028; total effect Z: -1.9, p = 0.053) as well as between the BSA-F and the WHOQOL-S (direct effect Z: -1.0, p = 0.3; indirect effect Z: -2.2, p = 0.031; total effect Z: -1.9, p = 0.06). Finally, a partial mediating role of the flexibility buffer was found in the relationship between the BSA-F and the SCL90-GSI (direct effect Z: 3.5, p < 0.001; indirect effect Z: 2.2, p = 0.025–22% of the total effect explained; total effect Z: 4.3, p < 0.001, Fig. 1d).

Discussion

In this work, we investigated the role played by psychological flexibility on the relationship between interpersonal adverse experiences in childhood and adolescence and quality of life as well as psychological symptoms in adults. Our results may support the hypothesis that psychological flexibility plays a role in relieving the effects of these past adverse events on adult life, both reducing their impact on quality of life and protecting against the outbreak of psychological symptoms.

Specifically, our results show that both childhood traumatic events and social-relational adverse experiences in childhood and adolescence are associated with different domains of quality of life, as well as with the presence of psychological symptoms. These associations are consistent with previous findings linking childhood traumatic events with impairment of quality of life [34, 35] and an increase in adult psychological symptoms [38, 40, 42, 44, 94]. Similarly, our results support the long-term effect of past social-relational adverse experiences on the quality of life [51], mental health, and psychological symptoms [61–63].

The key novelty of this study, however, concerns the results on psychological flexibility. Our results suggest that psychological flexibility has a mediating role between exposure to adverse life events and the individual’s indicators of quality of life and psychological symptoms. More specifically, psychological flexibility acts as a partial mediator of the effects of childhood traumatic events on both quality of life and psychological symptoms, with an explained effect between about 16 and 33%. Considering that psychological flexibility is negatively associated with psychological symptoms and positively with quality of life, it is conceivable that this mediation may describe a protective role of psychological flexibility from the detrimental effects of exposure to traumatic childhood events.

Similar results emerged when analyzing adverse social-relational experiences. Psychological flexibility mediated the effects of adverse social-relational experiences on quality of life, explaining between 22 and 43% of the total effect, and on psychological symptoms, explaining almost 22% of the total effect. Even for adverse social-relational experiences, the negative association of psychological flexibility with psychological symptoms and the positive association with quality of life indicators suggests a potential protective role from the detrimental effects of adverse social-relational experiences.

In our analysis, psychological flexibility constantly acts as a partial mediator: it contributes to explaining the relationship between the variables, although it is not the only mechanism at work. This result may reflect the complexity of the phenomenon under investigation. It is important to note that, in the social sciences, even a relatively low mediated proportion (around 10–20% of the total effect) can be considered significant if supported by theoretical assumptions [95]. Furthermore, the analysis of effect sizes based on the percentage of variance explained—which varies from 29 to 62% across the various models [96], (see Supplementary Table 2)—suggests that our results may be both statistically [97] and substantively relevant, despite the modest sample size.

Overall, the findings of this study lend support to the notion that psychological flexibility plays a role in protecting against the effects of past adverse events on psychological symptomatology. However, the picture becomes more complex when considering quality of life. Psychological flexibility partially mediated the relationship between exposure to childhood trauma and all dimensions of quality of life considered, with a greater effect on psychological quality of life. Similarly, when we considered the outcomes from exposure to social-relational events, psychological flexibility exerted a full mediating effect on psychological quality of life and a partial mediating effect on overall and physical quality of life. Thus, these results support the possibility that psychological flexibility processes were involved in supporting, directing, and sustaining the individual’s psychological life [1, 2] as well as maintaining overall quality of life. We also found an effect on physical quality of life, which potentially plays a critical role in supporting individuals who face illness in their lives [98–100].

The lack of association of social and environmental quality of life with exposure to social-relational events was surprising, as was the marginal mediating effect of psychological flexibility on the relationship between childhood traumatic events and the aforementioned dimensions of quality of life. Although both the dimensions of social and environmental quality of life are strongly associated with psychological flexibility [101, 102], it is possible that these dimensions are not directly implicated in processes that lead to adult-life effects of adverse events in the past. This could be due to the over-individual characteristics of these quality-of-life dimensions, i.e., the fact that they include factors that do not relate to subjective dimensions of the individual (e.g., aspects related to social support, financial resources, physical environment) [59, 83] and thus may be less influenced by personal life experiences. The possible influence of unmeasured variables on the results of our study may contribute to the clarification of the inconsistent mediation of psychological flexibility between exposure to adverse events and social and environmental quality of life due to confounders.

Very few studies have investigated the role of psychological flexibility in containing the adverse effects of exposure to childhood traumatic events [103, 104], and none have so far investigated past adverse social-relational experiences. The novelty of our work is to have extended the knowledge about the protective role of psychological flexibility even on adverse events that occurred further back in the past, or even in childhood. This may have implications for the therapeutic possibilities of improving the psychological well-being of people who were victims of childhood maltreatment or bullying.

Our findings enrich the debate on the relationship between adverse life events in critical periods such as childhood and adolescence and adult mental health [37, 42, 44, 54], suggesting that the person exposed to adverse life events may face a dual vulnerability. On the one hand, the aversive experience would reduce psychological flexibility, limiting abilities required to adapt to change while maintaining a meaningful direction in life [1–3]. Conversely, reduced psychological flexibility would make the individual more vulnerable to the stressors or critical issues they will face in adulthood, thus making coping and acceptance of adverse experiences more complex, and exposing them to greater psychopathological relapse [37, 44]. Recent studies suggest that the fallout of adverse life experiences on psychological flexibility may begin early [67, 68, 104], thus representing a chronic vulnerability factor. In addition, our data suggest broadening our vision and going beyond psychopathology, as adverse life events can also impair quality of life, which is a relevant aspect of the individual’s health and well-being [58, 59].

Several studies have highlighted how other psychological processes, such as resilience or coping style [105–108] may combine in mediating the relationship between adverse life events in childhood and adolescence and an individual’s mental health. These processes play a relevant role but both represent the reactive component in response to adversity. Coping with adversity gains functional value only as it both facilitates the pursuit of meaningful and valued aims and allows the individual to act in line with their values, all facets that are best captured within the psychological flexibility construct [1–3].

The results of this study have several clinical and practical implications. Because psychological flexibility explains a significant part of the negative effect of adverse events, it may be meaningful to develop interventions to enhance it-for example, through programs based on Acceptance and Commitment Therapy [1–3]. Through these approaches, people exposed to adverse interpersonal events could be supported in reducing cognitive fusion or experiential avoidance, in order to mitigate the distress and the behavioral impact of such events in everyday life [3, 20]. Personalized interventions aimed at promoting values and committed action can help individuals identify relevant dimensions of life and act in their direction [109, 110]. Furthermore, they could be guided to be conscious and present in the current moment, ruminating on the past or worrying about the future [110, 111]. In order to develop more targeted intervention protocols, future studies should extend our findings by identifying the contribution of each of the core processes of psychological flexibility in mitigating the long-term effects of adverse interpersonal events. To the same end, further studies are needed to investigate such specific associations in different samples, composed of individuals experiencing different adverse or traumatic events, of populations with specific vulnerabilities (such as children and adolescents, people with medical illnesses, people with social or environmental adversity), and of populations experiencing different levels of clinical symptoms subsequent to these traumatic events. Such investigations would contribute to better clarify the long-term effects of adverse interpersonal events and potential buffering mechanisms, thereby facilitating the development of personalized strategies. Furthermore, larger studies are needed to understand the generalizability and the external validity of our results.

Besides novelty points, our work also has some potential limitations, which need to be discussed. First, the selection of a study sample: our sample is composed exclusively of women of a very narrow age range (19–21 years) affects the generalizability of our results. This is a critical point, considering that there are gender differences in the occurrence of certain types of adverse social-relational experiences, such as bullying victimization in women [69–72], and also considering that the effects of exposure to adverse life events may change for different age groups [72, 112, 113]. This narrow sample composition limits generalizability to a larger population, and additional studies will need to explore whether our results persist in other groups. In addition, the possibility of extending the investigation to a larger sample should be considered. Due to the exclusion of some participants who had incomplete data collection, the final sample size of 109 individuals was marginally underpowered (power = 0.76). Although a power level marginally below 80% does not compromise the validity of the results [114], recruiting larger samples may certainly benefit the generalizability of this study.

A second study limitation may involve the use of questionnaire-based measurements for all dimensions. This is a common study design, considering that studies comparing research methods in psychology have shown that self-report questionnaire surveys are the modal class in different areas of psychology, accounting for more than 50% of the work in clinical and health psychology [115]. However, the use of this design is not unbiased [116–119]. Using only one survey method may amplify bias related to response style, covariation between constructs, or the presence of latent variables [118]. Reducing this bias is challenging. Temporal distance in the acquisition of measures (in this work, a mean of 24 days between measures of psychological flexibility and measures of exposure to adverse events, quality of life, and symptomatology) can help contain this source of bias [117, 120], but more accurate control will be provided by future studies that vary both survey methods (e.g., with behavioral tests) and sources of information [118]. Moreover, it can be a limitation to use measures of exposure to adverse events based solely on retrospective self-reports. Although this mode of investigation is common, given the retrospective nature of this information, and despite the substantial reliability and stability of such measures over time [45, 121–123], the use of retrospective self-reports can raise doubts regarding the actual occurrence of reported episodes [124]. Several studies emphasize that retrospective assessments of ACEs are susceptible to recall bias and other distortions related to subjectivity. For example, Reuben et al. [125] compared prospective and retrospective measurements of ACEs, highlighting discrepancies especially when outcomes are based on subjective self-assessments. Retrospective measurements may overestimate or underestimate such experiences depending on individual factors, such as personality traits and cognitive biases [125, 126] while remaining a valid source of data in contexts where prospective information is not available [127]. In light of the evidence presented, it is important to recognize the potential validity of our data, in line with studies that have indicated that the subjective impact of adverse events, rather than their objective occurrence, has a greater impact on symptomatology in adults [128, 129]. On the other hand, however, the limitations outlined in retrospective designs underscore the need for future longitudinal studies to validate our findings, clarify the directionality of observed associations, and enhance the generalizability of our conclusions.

The third limitation is the potential partial overlap between the measures of exposure to adverse interpersonal events. The Bullying Scale for Adults, a scale we used to measure a range of bullying, harassment, and abuse experiences in various contexts, also measures forms of victimization within families. It is therefore possible that it captures some of the variability also measured by the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire. This may have been more amplified by our choice to focus on the frequency of bullying victimization episodes, specifically in order to make the measures comparable. We do not think this is critical for this study since we did not have the primary aim to discriminate between these two modes of environmental exposure. However, it might be relevant for subsequent studies to explore the distinction between exposure to traumatic events as measured by the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire and bullying victimization as measured by the Bullying Scale for Adults, as well as to investigate the distress associated with bullying victimization that is recorded by the same scale.

The final limitation concerns the choice of statistical model used to test the relationship between interpersonal adverse events, psychological flexibility, and dimensions of psychological symptomatology and quality of life. We chose to adopt mediation models as they offer the possibility of testing the relationship between these variables more straightforwardly, with a more specific indication of what the predictors, mediators, and outcomes were [130]. We expect future studies to involve larger samples [131], allowing us to adopt more complex statistical models, such as structural equation modeling (SEM) [132], with potential advantages that should be highlighted [132]. A first advantage could be the possibility offered by SEMs to model complex relationships simultaneously. In our study, this would allow us, for example, to consider the combined effect of different types of interpersonal adverse events and to analyze the relationships between psychological symptoms and quality of life. Furthermore, SEM’s ability to include latent variables would allow us to consider the effect of multifaceted constructs, such as quality of life, within the same model. A second advantage could be the possibility of obtaining a more explicit assessment of measurement error and an estimate of the goodness of fit of the theoretical model to the observed data. This would improve the validity of the results, thereby facilitating their interpretability. Finally, the adoption of SEM models would allow us to investigate alternative models, testing competing theoretical hypotheses. This flexibility would allow us, for example, to identify any mediating or moderating effects between the outcomes considered in our investigation and determine the most parsimonious and explanatory model.

Conclusion

Overall, the results of this study suggest a potential link between adverse interpersonal events and an individual’s well-being and psychological functioning. We found that adverse events in the interpersonal context among peers may influence adult quality of life and psychological well-being. Our results also suggest that the detrimental impact of adverse interpersonal events can be associated with current psychological flexibility. This association could support intervention models that help victims of childhood maltreatment or social-relational adversity by enhancing their psychological flexibility skills. This underexplored opportunity might represent a therapeutic implication of our findings.
