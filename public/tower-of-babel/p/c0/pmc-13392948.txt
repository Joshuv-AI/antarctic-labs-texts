Partial substitution of organic trace minerals in sow diets over multiple parities and the impact on claw health, productivity, and longevity
Authors: Meghann K. Pierdon, Thomas D. Parsons
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Organic trace mineral substitution decreased the odds of dew claw lesions in sows.•Sow lactation weight loss decreased with organic trace mineral substitution.•Organic trace mineral substitution did not influence sow longevity.

Introduction

Trace minerals including copper, zinc, and manganese serve important functions in reproduction and the proper development of bones and claws. Copper plays a vital role in connective tissue formation and may improve growth rates when fed at rates above the NRC guidelines (Stein, 2024). Zinc influences horn production in multiple ways, including as a catalyst in keratinization, facilitating protein interactions that are part of the development of healthy horn tissue, and the regulation of protein kinase C, which is critical for energy during keratinocyte differentiation (Van Riet et al., 2018). Indeed, it has been shown that zinc supplementation may impact a sow’s claw health throughout life (Van Riet et al., 2018). Manganese is needed for the development of cartilage and the bone matrix (Byrne & Murphy, 2022) and may also play a role in reproductive function by increasing superoxide dismutase in the corpora lutea (Studer et al., 2021).

As trace minerals are a nutritional requirement, chelation (with an amino acid, for example) may be a way to decrease inclusion rate while seeing positive effects. Chelation of these trace minerals by combining an amino acid and a metal ion with the trace mineral to create an organic form of the mineral may improve their availability, as the trace mineral is able to utilize the absorption route of the amino acid. Diets containing the organic forms of trace minerals have been shown to influence claw health and reproduction in sows, including Varagka et al. where they collected feet from three Greek sow herds and found that partial substitution with organic sources improved total claw lesion score and resulted in beneficial microscopic changes to the lamina propria in the hoof (Varagka et al., 2016). Similarly, Lisgara et al. looked at claw lesions before and after the inclusion of organic versions of zinc, copper, and manganese in the diet and showed a decrease in claw lesion scores (Lisgara et al., 2016). Conversely, other studies do not show an impact such as Faba et al. which examined the inclusion of these compounds in gilt diets and found no impact on lameness or claw lesions (Fabà et al., 2019). The influence on reproductive performance is mixed where Skampardonis et al. compared sows before and after substitution with organic trace minerals and found an increase in liveborn piglets but no change in the number weaned or the wean-to-estrus interval (Skampardonis (Β. Σκαμπαρδωνης) et al., 2018). Peters et al., who fed diets with organic trace minerals during gilt development and subsequent parities found heavier litter weights, but no change in sow body weight, rebreeding interval, or feed intake, and individual pigs were lighter (Peters & Mahan, 2008). Most of these studies, however, are not randomized, blinded trials, but before-and-after comparisons. They often test different mineral levels but do not compare inorganic versus organic counterparts. More information is needed from field settings where longitudinal study designs with blinded observers are used to compare the substitution of inorganic trace minerals in sow diets with their organic forms.

Given the need for more studies that are randomized and longitudinal in design, the objective of this randomized controlled trial was to investigate the role of trace mineral bioavailability on claw health and consequently on lameness, reproductive performance, and longevity, by feeding sows from enrollment through multiple parities either a control diet containing inorganic trace minerals (i.e., zinc, copper, manganese) or a diet with a partial substitution of organic trace minerals. We hypothesize that this partial substitution of inorganic trace minerals with their organic forms will improve bioavailability, thereby enhancing claw health and reproductive performance, reducing lameness, and increasing the time treated sows remain in the sow herd.

Materials and methods

Animal care and use

The Institutional Care and Use Committee of the University of Pennsylvania reviewed and approved the procedures in this study (IACUC Protocol #804,656).

Animals and facilities

This randomized longitudinal study was conducted from August 2015 to July 2017 at the University of Pennsylvania Swine Teaching and Research Farm (Kennett Square, Pennsylvania, USA). Gilts (Line 241, DNA Genetics, Columbus, NE) arrived monthly (average 11.2 gilts) from September 2015 to June 2016, and a total of 10 groups were allocated to the study (n = 116 gilts). Gilts were breed eligible upon arrival and were added to the study diets as they finished their training to use electronic sow feeders (ESF) (Compident VII, Schauer Agrotronics, Prambachkirchen, Austria). Gilts were randomly allocated to treatment groups based on their ear tag number: even-numbered tags to the control and odd-numbered tags to the treatment diet. Gilts remained in the same pen from the end of ESF training throughout gestation in a group of approximately 60 gilts housed in a large dynamic pen. The gilt pen provided 2.25 m2 of space per gilt and included 6 solid concrete lying areas (2.9 × 2.2 m) separated by partitions and one ESF. After their first lactation, they entered a sow pen with approximately 130 sows. Sows that were currently pregnant and in existing breeding groups at the start of the study were housed in a large sow pen, which provided 1.86 m2 per sow, included nine 2.9 × 2.2 m concrete lying areas separated by partitions, and contained two ESF stations. These animals were randomly assigned to either the treatment (n = 117) or control (n = 111) diets as they were moved to the farrowing rooms at approximately 109 days of gestation. After farrowing, these animals returned to the sow gestation pen. Both gilt and sow pens had fully slatted concrete flooring except for the lying areas. For analysis, data for sows that had zero days on treatment at loading into farrowing (scores at enrollment) were not included until they reached their next lactation. A subset of the study sows (Control n = 36; Treatment n = 22) did not reach the next farrowing and were included only in the longevity analysis, as they had no farrowing data and lesion scores were not collected (Fig. 1).

Treatment

The treatment sows were fed a diet with increased bioavailability of trace minerals by including a commercially available product (Zinpro Availa Sow, Zinpro Corporation, Eden Prairie, Minnesota, United States) that provided partially substituted organic forms of zinc (45 of 125 ppm), copper (14 of 15 ppm), and manganese (25 of 40 ppm). The product was fed according to the manufacturer’s directions (Zinpro® Corporation, 2026) and in accordance with comparable trials (Fabà et al., 2019; Lisgara et al., 2016; Skampardonis (Β. Σκαμπαρδωνης) et al., 2018; Varagka et al., 2016). The control sows were fed only diets containing inorganic mineral sources. All diets were prepared by a commercial feed mill and developed by a swine nutritionist to meet NRC standards (National Research Council, 2012). The mineral substitution occurred in both the lactation and gestation diets. Over the course of the study 29 feed samples were tested for calcium levels to ensure proper inclusion rates of mineral sources and were on average within 10% of the expected inclusion rate. All farm staff were blinded to treatment. Diets were tagged at the feed mill with color-coded labels to ensure proper feeding on the farm, as only the feed mill personnel knew the distinction between the two colors.

Claw lesion and motion scoring

Claw lesions were scored using a modified version of the Zinpro® Feet First scoring system (Table 1) (Deen et al., 2026). Lesions were scored when the sows were lactating and thus were scored each time an animal farrowed. Every day, the same observer who was blinded to treatment would walk through the farrowing rooms looking for sows in lateral recumbency to score. All four feet were scored, and the maximum score was recorded for each lesion type. For each farrowing event, a total lesion score was calculated by adding together the scores for all the lesion types. A cracked wall score was generated by adding together cracked wall horizontal and cracked wall vertical scores. Productivity, body weight, body condition, and feed intake

Productivity measures recorded included born alive, still births, litter birth weight, number of pigs weaned, and litter weaning weight. The body weight of the sow was recorded at loading into farrowing and at weaning. Weight change was calculated by subtracting the sow’s weaning weight from her weight at loading into farrowing. Feed intake was recorded at the end of each lactation, and feed intake per day was calculated by dividing the total amount of feed fed to the sow by the number of days in farrowing. Body condition was scored on a three-point scale of 2 = thin, 3 = ideal, and 4 = heavy and recorded when sows were loaded into farrowing.

Longevity and removal reasons

A sow’s allocation date was when she was enrolled in the study. For sows, that was the first time they entered farrowing during the study period. For gilts delivered to the farm during the study period, they were allocated when the group of gilts first entered the gestation pen after training to use the ESF. The removal date and reason were recorded by farm staff and documented in the farm record-keeping system. The end of the study was the date the last enrolled sows were weaned, which was July 2017, but survival data was gathered until December of 2017. Removal for lameness was assigned to any sow that had lameness or downer recorded as the reason for her death, euthanasia, or culling.

Statistics

STATA version 15.1 was used for all statistical analyses (Stata Statistical Software: Release 15. College Station, TX: Stata Corp LLC). Parity was translated into five groups (Group 1 = P1, Group 2 = P2, Group 3 = P3 and P4, Group 4 = P5, P6 and P7, Group 5 = P8, P9, P10, and P11). Production outcomes, lactation weight loss, and feed intake were analyzed using mixed-effects generalized linear models with treatment, parity group, and litter birth weight as fixed effects, and sow identification number as a random effect to account for multiple observations. The total claw lesion score was analyzed using a mixed-effect Poisson model with treatment and parity group as fixed effects and sow identification as a random effect. Individual claw lesion scores were translated into binary variables where each lesion was either present or absent and analyzed using logistic regression models with treatment and parity group as fixed effects and sow identification as a random effect. Motion score at loading was analyzed using a mixed-effect logistic regression model with treatment and parity group as fixed effects and sow identification as a random effect. Missing values for the dependent (outcome) variable were handled via listwise deletion and for missing values for any independent variables (predictors) the entire observation was removed from the analysis.

Odds of removal for lameness were calculated using a two-by-two table to obtain the odds ratio, and the Chi-square test was applied to test for significance. Survival analysis was performed with time in the herd as the duration, and with whether the sow died or was removed before the end of the analysis period as the outcome. A log-rank test was used to assess whether survival time was associated with treatment. A significance level of α = 0.05 was used for all statistical tests. A significance level between 0.05 and 0.1 was considered a trend toward significance. The individual sow or her litter was the statistical unit. Averages and variances are reported as mean (standard deviation) or mean (standard error).

Longevity and removal reasons

Farm staff recorded removal reasons for 88% of the study sows. There was no difference in the odds of removal for lameness in the treated sows (25/153; 16.3%) compared to the untreated sows (22/149; 14.8%) (OR = 1.13; 95% CI: 0.58 to 2.22; P = 0.71). Discussion

Claw lesions

Copper, zinc, and manganese play an important role in hoof formation, and therefore, more efficient uptake of these nutrients could reasonably be expected to decrease claw lesions. In this study, there was a trend for the total claw lesion score to be greater for the control sows compared to the treated sows. The literature on the impact of feeding such supplemented diets is equivocal. Varagka et al. found improved total claw lesion score in sows after supplementation (Varagka et al., 2016), as did Lisgara et al. (Lisgara et al., 2016). However, Faba et al. found no such effects (Fabà et al., 2019). These results indicate that there may be evidence of improvements in lesion score, but perhaps the scoring method used here, assigning the most severe lesion as the score rather than scoring all lesions on all claws and summing them, captured the severity but not the overall volume of lesions. A scoring method that captures lesion volume might be more accurate for assessing the total lesion score. Of note, Gonzalez-Ramiro et al. scored claw lesions by severity (0–3), and sows were categorized accordingly. Only sows with severe lesions (score 3) showed significantly worse reproductive outcomes (fewer total piglets born, fewer piglets born alive, higher proportion of low‑weight piglets, and reduced mean litter birth weight), whereas mild and moderate lesions did not consistently impair performance. This might indicate that biological and productive consequences may be driven by lesion severity, not merely overall lesion presence or total lesion counts (Gonzalez-Ramiro et al., 2025).

The odds of having a dew claw lesion were higher in the control sows than in the treated sows. This is important because torn dew claws have been identified as a common reason for lame sow culling (Dewey et al., 1993). While the pathogenesis of increased dew claw length or torn dew claws is unclear, there are data that suggest that there is an underlying cause, other than abnormal weight bearing, since the overgrowth of the lateral toe and the lateral dew claw have been shown to be correlated (Henningsen, 2023). Interestingly, organic Zn, Cu, and Mn improved hoof horn morphology and laminar integrity, reducing laminitic-type lesions (Varagka et al., 2016). Dew claw overgrowth and tearing are linked to horn quality and keratinization, which would point to a possible role for trace minerals in the etiology and a role for mineral supplementation in the improvement of such lesions.

While there was a trend for total lesion scores to be higher in the control sows, and higher odds of long or torn dew claws, there was no significant difference in the odds of a sow having each of the other individual claw lesions, including heel overgrowth, heel erosion, heel sole crack, cracked wall, or long toes. These results showing no influence of treatment on individual claw lesions are inconsistent with several trials where lesions were examined before and after feeding substituted diets (Lisgara et al., 2016; Varagka et al., 2016) but those studies relied on samples taken at different times compared to a randomized approach that was used here. Another study did not find a difference in claw lesions, and its methods were consistent with the randomized approach used in this study (Fabà et al., 2019). Other studies also do not mention blinding the observers to treatment, which is an important consideration for future studies. Given the trend in total score and the differences in dew claw lesions in this study, it may be that the nutritional substitution makes only small differences in individual claw lesion types, which might be better detected with cumulative lesion scoring and larger sample sizes; however, such changes would also need to be evaluated for biological relevance.

Lameness

The literature is equivocal on the link between claw lesions and lameness. Many studies fail to link claw lesions with lameness (Grégoire et al., 2013; Kroneman et al., 1993), and measuring lameness directly via motion scores may more accurately assess it. There was no difference in the odds of a sow being scored as lame at loading into farrowing in the treatment group compared to the control group. This is consistent with another study looking at partial substitution of organic forms of zinc, copper, and manganese in gilt rearing diets, where no difference in lameness was found (Fabà et al., 2019). They, however, saw higher overall lameness prevalence at 20.5 to 30.8% compared to 15–16% in this study.

Productivity

There was no difference in the sow productivity between the two groups, including born alive, still births, or the number of pigs weaned. In contrast, when including organic sources of zinc, copper, and manganese in sow diets, Skampardonis et al. found that the organic sources increased piglets born alive (Skampardonis (Β. Σκαμπαρδωνης) et al., 2018) and Peters and Mahan found heavier litters (Peters & Mahan, 2008). In both studies, they found no difference in weaned pig numbers, consistent with the findings reported here. Ma et al. showed an influence on the number of pigs over one kg at birth, but similarly, no influence on other reproductive variables (Ma et al., 2020). Other studies likewise found no influence on reproduction based on feeding organic sources, leading to the conclusion that there is no consistent reproductive effect in the literature routinely associated with organic supplementation (Tsai et al., 2020; Villagómez-Estrada et al., 2021).

We found that weight loss during lactation was reduced in treatment sows, even after accounting for parity and litter birthweight, despite no change in feed intake. This points to improved feed utilization, though feed efficiency was not specifically measured. Tsai et al. found similar improvements in weight gain and efficiency during gestation in supplemented sows (Tsai et al., 2020). As in this study, Peters et al. found no influence of organic trace minerals on feed intake, but in contrast, also found no influence on body weight (Peters & Mahan, 2008). While lactation weight loss in this study was significantly higher in the control groups, it likely did not impact production. Sow weight loss here was still only about 6% of body weight on average, whereas it has been shown that upwards of 10% of body weight loss is the threshold for production impacts (Thaker & Bilkei, 2005). If similar levels of lactation weight loss are repeated over multiple reproductive cycles, cumulative depletion of body reserves could impair the sow’s ability to sustain optimal performance, resilience, and longevity.

Longevity

Longevity in sows is a major concern, given the return on investment in raising a gilt is only returned after they reach their third lactation (Stalder et al., 2003). Lameness is a common reason for early removal (Anil et al., 2009; Engblom et al., 2008; Hallowell & Pierdon, 2022) and if lameness could be prevented by use of organic trace minerals, then their use may influence the time an animal stays in the herd. Under the conditions of this study, there was no influence on lameness as measured by motion score, nor was there a difference in the odds of removal for lameness. Sow longevity is multifactorial, with many factors traceable to birthweight or selection (Patterson & Foxcroft, 2019). Thus, it is not surprising that altering diets to use organic mineral sources later in life would not influence the risk of removal, and indeed that was what was seen under the conditions of this study.

While it has been shown that lameness is associated with earlier removal from the herd (Hallowell & Pierdon, 2022), increased claw lesions are not typically associated with early removal (Enokida et al., 2011). These findings highlight the likely multi-factorial etiology of hoof lesions and lameness. Strong contributions from the sow and or environment can limit the opportunity for improvement via nutritional supplementation (Van Riet et al., 2018). In this study, the control diet may have provided sufficient mineral levels to support the available nutritional benefits, as other non-nutritional factors dominated lameness outcomes, thereby limiting the impact of additional mineral availability on lameness. Consistent with this, postmortem field investigations have identified severe claw damage, rather than minor or subclinical lesions, as a frequent underlying contributor to lameness‑related sow removal (Dewey et al., 1993; Engiles et al., 2022).

These results are unique in that they indicate that there may be a role for partial substitution of organic trace minerals in sow diets to decrease sow weight loss during lactation and improve claw health, particularly for dew claws. However, there were several variables where no association was found including lameness, productivity, and longevity. The strengths of this study include the blinded, randomized design, the duration of feeding prior to data collection, and the farm setting. There are limitations, however, including the smaller sample size, especially for animals entering the fourth lactation period. Also, while there were reasons recorded for almost 90% of the animals that were removed from the herd, some are missing reasons for their removal. The farm also uses large gestation groups, electronic sow feeders, and fully slatted concrete floors, which may limit generalizability to other farms of this type. This study suggests that more studies are needed on claw health and nutrition and that future work should use a scoring system that accounts for the volume of lesions, and not just the severity. Research should also focus on environmental contributions to lameness and possible interactions between nutritional interventions and the farm environment, especially with sows housed in group pens.
