Early-life viral infections and asthma: new cells and ideas

INTRODUCTION: ASTHMA IS A COMMON AND HETEROGENOUS PEDIATRIC DISEASE

Asthma is the most common noninfectious condition in children, as the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimates 7% of all individuals under 18 carry a diagnosis of asthma (https://www.cdc.gov/asthma/most_recent_national_asthma_data.htm). Asthma is defined by increased airway reactivity, chronic inflammation, and reversible airflow limitation, with exacerbations leading to life-threatening bronchospasm. The inflammatory pathways activated in the airways can vary, thereby making ‘asthma’ an umbrella term describing patients with differing underlying pathophysiology, referred to as endotypes [1]. Understanding these endotypes has become imperative over the past decade, as biologic therapies targeting specific immune pathways have helped revolutionize severe asthma management, including in pediatrics [2].

Asthma in childhood can be driven by type 2 (T2) inflammation, which is defined by a subset of CD4+ T cells (i.e. Th2) that produce cytokines responsible for eosinophil recruitment, goblet cell hyperplasia, and generation of IgE antibodies associated with allergic inflammation [3] (Fig. 1). T2-high asthma is often considered in young children due to presence of concomitant atopic conditions, such as eczema or food allergies [4]. However, non-T2 immune responses can drive asthma, with a recent study suggesting this endotype is also quite common in children. T2-low asthma often has a neutrophilic signature associated with different subsets of CD4+ T cells (e.g. Th17) [5▪▪]. T2-low asthma can be problematic to treat because of poor response to corticosteroids and dearth of biologics targeting non-T2 pathways [6].

Asthma exacerbations can be triggered by a variety of exposures, including viral infections, exercise, occupational irritants, aspirin, airborne pollutants, and tobacco smoke. Age of onset and association with other medication conditions (e.g. allergies, obesity, and bronchopulmonary dysplasia) are other key variables to consider when comprehensively assessing a patient's asthma history [7].

Asthma's heterogeneity in presentation, triggers, and immune signatures is illustrative when considering the origins of the disease. Asthma development in early childhood is likely multifactorial, a composite of underlying genetic susceptibilities, prenatal influences, and early-life exposures. The current review will focus on the emerging cell types found to drive asthma, as a mechanistic understanding of the contributory immune pathways is critical to advancing precision medicine treatments and potentially preventing the development of asthma (Table 1). We will then explore the epidemiologic data linking early-life viral infections and asthma, followed by discussion of the myriad immunologic mechanisms that may link these two common pathologies.

EOSINOPHILS AND NEUTROPHILS IN ASTHMA

Eosinophils are granulocytic leukocytes that play important roles in host defense against parasites and in modulating allergic inflammation [8]. Under pathological conditions such as asthma, their functions extend beyond defense to driving chronic inflammation and tissue remodeling [8]. Eosinophils play a central role in the immunopathogenesis of eosinophilic asthma, which is characterized by persistent airway inflammation and heightened disease severity [7,9]. These granulocytic leukocytes are recruited from the bone marrow into the blood stream under the influence of IL-5, IL-3, and granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factors (GM-CSF), and subsequently migrate into the airway tissues where they exert pro-inflammatory functions [8,10]. Once in the airways, activated eosinophils release a variety of cytotoxic granule proteins, such as major basic protein (MBP) and eosinophil peroxidase (EPO), which can damage airway epithelium and contribute to airway hyperresponsiveness, mucus hypersecretion, and remodeling [10]. In addition, eosinophils secrete type 2 cytokines including IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, amplifying Th2-mediated inflammation [7]. Their presence in sputum and tissue correlates with asthma severity and exacerbation frequency. Monoclonal antibodies targeting IL-5 or its receptor, such as mepolizumab and benralizumab, significantly reduce eosinophil levels and improve clinical outcomes in individuals with eosinophilic and/or oral corticosteroid-dependent asthma [11–21].

Neutrophils are the most abundant circulating leukocytes and serve as the first line of defense against infections [22]. Neutrophils have emerged as a key contributor to the pathogenesis of severe and steroid-resistant asthma [23,24]. Unlike eosinophilic asthma, neutrophilic asthma is characterized by elevated neutrophil counts in sputum and airway tissues, often in association with environmental exposure, infections, and Th1/Th17-skewed inflammation [25,26]. Activation of neutrophils release proteolytic enzymes such as neutrophil elastase and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), as well as reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can cause epithelial damage, mucus hypersecretion, and airway remodeling [27–34]. IL-8 (CXCL8) and other neutrophil chemoattractants are elevated in these patients and promote sustained neutrophilic infiltration [23]. Unlike eosinophils, neutrophils are less responsive to corticosteroids, posing a challenge for conventional treatment [35]. Targeting neutrophil-associated cytokines and signaling pathways represents a promising, albeit still experimental, therapeutic strategy [36,37▪▪,38▪▪,39,40].

TH17 CELLS IN ASTHMA

Th17 cells are a subset population of CD4 helper T cells, producing pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-17A and IL-17F [41–43]. Th17 lineage commitment is initiated through the sequential activation of STAT signaling pathways, primarily involving STAT3 downstream of cytokine receptors, in coordination with retinoic acid receptor-related orphan receptor gamma t (RORγt) [44–46]. The generation of Th17 cells is also critically regulated by transforming growth factor β (TGFβ) in combination with inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, IL-21, IL-1β, and IL-23 [47–53]. Several studies demonstrated that Th17 cell and its effector cytokines were implicated in the pathogenesis of various autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, like rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis (MS), psoriasis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and lung inflammatory diseases [54,55].

Activated Th17 cells secrete IL-17, which stimulate epithelial and stromal cells to produce neutrophil-attracting chemokines like CXCL1, CXCL2, and CXCL8 (IL-8) [56,57]. These chemokines guide neutrophils to inflamed lung tissue by binding to their CXCR1/2 receptors, driving persistent inflammation [56,58]. This pathway not only recruits neutrophils but also creates a feedback loop that sustains immune activation and airway damage [57,58]. Notably, IL-17-mediated inflammation has been shown to suppress glucocorticoid receptor function by increasing GR-β expression and altering histone deacetylase (HDAC) activity, thereby attenuating steroid responsiveness at the molecular level [59–61].

Several murine and human studies have demonstrated elevated levels of IL-17A and Th17-related cytokines in patients with severe or steroid-resistant asthma phenotypes, particularly those with neutrophil-predominant inflammation [57,62,63]. Targeting the Th17/IL-17 axis has thus emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy, although clinical trials of anti-IL-17 agents in asthma have yielded mixed results [25,64,65], likely due to heterogeneity in Th17 involvement among patients.

Regulatory T cells (Treg), characterized by Foxp3 expression and anti-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-10 and TGF-β) secretion, are a crucial subset of CD4 T cells, which preserve self-tolerance and fine-tune the immune response. Tregs suppress effector T-cell proliferation and cytokine production. In asthma, Treg frequency and function are diminished, which contributes to immune dysregulation and persistent inflammation [66–68].

Recent studies highlight the imbalance favoring Th17 over Treg cells is recognized as a key immunological hallmark of severe asthma [69–71]. In steroid-resistant asthma, decreased Treg numbers of function, along with elevated Th17 activity, contributes to a proinflammatory microenvironment [69,72]. Clinical and animal model data show increased Th17/Treg ratio in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and peripheral blood of severe asthmatics, correlating with poor steroid response [73,74▪▪,75,76]. ThisTh17/Treg imbalance skews immune regulation towards chronic inflammation and remodeling, and may impair glucocorticoid receptors signaling via IL-17-mediated pathways. Multiple mechanisms underline this imbalance, including TLR4 signaling activation by environmental stimuli, which promotes Th17 differentiation while impairing Treg development [77]. Additionally, the presence of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and IL-23 favors Th17 polarization [78], whereas deficiencies in TGF-β and IL-2 limit Treg maintenance [79,80]. Therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring Th17/Treg balance, including low-dose IL-2 therapy to selectively expand Tregs [81,82], retinoic acid supplementation [83,84], and microbiota modulation [85], are under investigation to improve immune homeostasis and treatment responsiveness.

TISSUE RESIDENT MEMORY T CELLS IN ASTHMA

Tissue resident memory cells (TRMs) are subset of long-lived T cells that reside in a specific tissue after a primary response to infection rather than recirculate through the lymph/circulatory systems [86]. As a result, TRMs express surface markers to promote tissue retention, including CD69, allowing for differentiation between circulating and resident cells [87]. With the implementation of single-cell-based transcriptomic approaches, there have been advances in our understanding of TRMs in human asthma. Patients with severe asthma were found to have a pathogenic Th2 population in the airway, which signaled to and maintained abnormal epithelial cell states [88]. Beyond Th2 cells, Herrera-De La Mata et al. [89▪] demonstrated the presence of CD4+ cytotoxic TRMs in patients with severe T2-high asthma, connecting these activated cells to tissue changes, particularly in male patients. In a noninfectious model of asthma mice, persistent antigen exposure led to TRMs with Th17 effector functions, including driving neutrophil-mediated airway damage [90▪]. TRMs in the lung are formed within the first few years of life in humans, when many children are impacted by respiratory viruses; likewise, neonatal mice are capable of forming TRMs after respiratory viral infection [91▪▪,92].

ILC2 CELLS IN ASTHMA

Innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), the most recently discovered family of innate immune cells, do not have antigen-specific receptors, but they are particularly abundant in mucosal tissues, where they help maintain immune balance and tissue homeostasis. Type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) are an important immune subset, which produce relevant type 2 cytokines, such as IL-5 and IL-13. They play a key role in allergic inflammation and tissue repair.

ILC2s are becoming increasingly recognized for their role in the immunopathogenesis of asthma, especially in type 2-high endotypes. ILC2s are triggered primarily by epithelial-derived cytokines such as IL-25, IL-33, and thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), which are released after exposure to environmental stimuli such as allergens, viruses, and pollutants [25]. Upon activation, ILC2s produce an abundance of T2 cytokines, predominantly IL-5 and IL-13. These cytokines mediate eosinophilic inflammation, mucus hypersecretion, airway hyperresponsiveness, and tissue remodeling, which are all hallmarks observed in allergic asthma [93,94]. ILC2s provide an advantage over conventional T-lymphocyte-mediated and B-lymphocyte-mediated responses, because ILC2s can be activated without antigen presentation and can initiate an immediate immune response without prior adaptive immune priming [95]. ILC2s have been found to accumulate in the lungs of patients with asthma and have also been observed in murine models of asthma following allergen challenge [96,97]. ILC2s are important not only in initiation of disease, but they can also contribute to the chronicity of the disease by inducing tissue fibrosis and promoting the survival and activation of other type 2 immune cells such as eosinophils and Th2 cells [98].

Moreover, ILC2s can also be involved in steroid-resistant asthma. Growing number of studies have shown elevated levels of IL-33 and ILC2s correlated with poor corticosteroid responsiveness, which could involve glucocorticoid-induced modulation of IL-33 signaling pathways [99]. IL-33, which is released by damaged or stressed epithelial and endothelial cells acts as a key driver of type 2 inflammation by activating various immune cells, such as ILC2s, master cells, and eosinophils via the ST2 (i.e. IL1RL1) receptor [100]. Gaurav et al. summarized 72 publications and highlighted that IL-33 levels are elevated in the serum, BALF, and lung tissues of patients with asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and other inflammatory lung diseases [101]. Mice lacking IL-33 or ST2 showed significantly reduced asthma-like symptoms, reinforcing the pathway's central role in disease progression [102]. Early stage evidence of targeting the upstream regulators of ILC2 activation – such as IL-33 and thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) – is now emerging with the clinical success of biologics (such as Tezepelumab) demonstrating promised efficacy in reducing symptoms in patients with severe asthma.

MICROBIOME

Like the gastrointestinal tract, the respiratory tract (both upper and lower) has a diverse and rich microbiome [103]. Perhaps unsurprisingly, patients with asthma have a distinct composition of the lower respiratory tract microbiome with outgrowth of several Gram-negative respiratory pathogens compared to those without asthma [104–107]. In children with severe asthma, lower respiratory microbiome and mycobiome analyses focused on bacterial and fungal species, respectively, found distinct species compared to nonasthmatic children [108]. In pediatrics, assessment of the microbiome often focuses on the upper respiratory tract due to sampling reasons. In the Childhood Origins of Asthma study, serial assessment of over the first 2 years of life found microbiome patterns (early Staphylococcus dominance and Moraxella predominance during wheezing illnesses) associated with subsequent risk of childhood asthma [109]. The dynamic ecosystem of the microbiome and how the colonizing species interact with the immune system to establish a milieu likely has an impact on asthma; associations between bacterial/fungal species and type 2 high endotypes have been drawn, for example [110,111].

EPIDEMIOLOGIC CONNECTION BETWEEN EARLY-LIFE VIRAL INFECTIONS AND ASTHMA

Pediatricians are well aware of the epidemiologic burden of respiratory viral infections early in life. Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), rhinovirus, human metapneumovirus (HMPV), and influenza are all capable of ‘going viral’, leading to surges in cases of bronchiolitis resulting in frequent hospitalizations [112▪]. In children under the age of 5, these pathogens are estimated to cause at least 50 million lower respiratory tract infections worldwide [113–116]. Viral lower respiratory tract infections have an enormous impact in pediatrics in regards to acute morbidity, hospital costs, and, unfortunately, mortality.

There are also staggering long-term ramifications of a viral lower respiratory tract infection in the first few years of life. In one prospective study, a lower respiratory tract infection before age 3 with any viral pathogen is associated with a four-fold increased risk of asthma by age 7 [117]. Similar trends in increased odds ratios of developing asthma have been shown for individual viral pathogens, including, RSV, rhinovirus (especially type C), non-COVID-19 coronavirus, and HMPV [118–122]. Early-life viral infection is not only associated with childhood asthma, as prospective studies have shown increased risk of asthma at ages 18 and 53 after a significant childhood infection [123,124].

These studies often raise the question of a direct association (e.g. a viral infection leads to or causes the development of asthma) vs. an individual's shared susceptibility to lower respiratory tract infections and asthma. Studies of twins have found some bidirectionality between RSV hospitalization and asthma development; RSV hospitalization was associated with short-term risk of wheeze, but this phenotype faded over time, suggesting a shared genetic predisposition to both severe infection and asthma [125,126]. More recently, a prospective study of any RSV infection, independent of severity, found that children without RSV in the first year of life had lower risk of developing asthma by age 5 [127▪▪]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate the difficulty in establishing bona fide causality when evaluating a complex phenotype such as asthma development in the setting of an early-life viral infection. However, one could argue that a child's increased risk of asthma development after a viral infection is so reproducible and striking that regardless of the nature of the relationship (i.e. direct or indirect), these early-life episodes could been seen as a signal capable of prompting intervention. Disruption of the pathogenesis of asthma after a risk factor like early-life viral infection is a conceptual shift, focused more on prevention than treatment. However lofty this goal may seem, designing precise interventions to mitigate asthma development requires a fundamental understanding of the immunologic responses in both scenarios (e.g. early-life viral infection and asthma) and critically how they may be linked.

CONNECTING EARLY-LIFE VIRAL INFECTION AND ASTHMA

Many of the pathways involved in asthma pathogenesis are initiated or impacted by early-life viral infections. In mouse models of early-life RSV, epithelial type-2 promoting cytokines like IL-33 and TSLP are produced following viral infection [128,129]. Downstream ramifications include expansion of ILC2 populations and establishing a type 2 poise for the adaptive immune response [128,129]. Elevated ILC2 cell abundance was associated with increased RSV severity in pediatric patients [130]. Disruption of IL-33 and TSLP signaling lead to improved asthma pathology. Loss of TSLP signaling also mitigated longstanding pulmonary structural changes after early-life RSV [131]. Other epithelial-derived cytokines, such as IL-1β, released after early-life viral infection can result in epigenetic modifications that can exacerbate asthma-like responses later in life [132].

The immune system early in life is already poised to form Th2 responses at the expense of canonical antiviral Th1s [133,134]. This Th2 skewing is likely multifactorial, with epigenetic changes unique to the neonate establishing an intrinsic predisposition to form Th2s, changes in the antigen-presenting cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs) limiting Th1 formation via TGF-β [135–138]. Taken together, early-life viral infections accentuate the Th2-dominant milieu in the lung, which can contribute to longstanding ramifications. Our work using a neonatal mouse model has demonstrated that early-life viral infection can lead to asthma-like pathology after reinfection, a process that is critically mediated by TRMs (manuscript submitted for publication). The composition of TRMs after early-life viral infection and their role in progression from early-life lower respiratory tract infection to asthma remain an active area of investigation.

The contributions of early-life viral infections on development of asthma likely extend beyond the realm of T2-high-driven asthma. Th17 immunity and the role of neutrophils in the context of early-life viral infection will need to be further evaluated. Neonatal RSV has also been shown to break the Treg-driven tolerant environment in the newborn, promoting allergic sensitization [139]. Early-life viral infections can cause a persistent destabilization of the upper respiratory tract microbiome, which may also alter immune responses to subsequent stimuli [140]. Respiratory viral infections can also impact the gut microbiome, which similarly can impact subsequent allergic inflammation [141▪].

CONCLUSION

The above studies provide an immunologic foundation for the connection between development of asthma in children with history of early-life viral infection. More studies are required to elucidate the pathways driving asthma ontogeny in children, especially as it relates to the multifactorial nature of early-life exposures. The potential of these studies is enormous though, as interventions to prevent asthma from developing would transform care for our pediatric patients.
