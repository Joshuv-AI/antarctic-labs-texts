Radiological imaging of multisystem Langerhans cell histiocytosis in a young child: A rare case report
Authors: Harry Galuh Nugraha, Astri Restuastuti Muslimah, Nur Suryawan, Hermin Aminah Usman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a rare clonal disorder of the monocyte-macrophage system. There are 3 distinct types of LCH: multisystem type (MS), single-system multi-site (SS-m), and single-system single-site (SS-s). The disease is most common in young children, especially males. In this case, we report the rare case of a young Asian girl who initially complained of dyspnea, diarrhea, rash, and fever. Upon further examination, multiple systems were implicated, including the bone, skin, lung, liver, and spleen. Multisystem LCH with risk organ involvement give poor prognosis to the patients.

Introduction

A rare disorder of the monocyte-macrophage system, Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is characterized by the uncontrolled growth and accumulation of dendritic cells (DCs) as a result of ongoing immunological activation. Numerous clinical disorders are caused by this disease, most of which are limited to children but can also impact adults. Depending on the type of histiocyte involved, the World Health Organization (WHO) divides disorders into 3 categories: malignant histiocyte disorders, dendritic cell disorders, and disorders associated with macrophages. Among dendritic cell diseases, LCH is the most prevalent. Although it can be diagnosed in any age range, children 1-3 years old are more likely to have it. The disease is most common in young children, especially males. There are 3 to 5 cases per million children. In children, higher incidence has been reported for Hispanics [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]].

LCH is classified into 3 different types: single-system single site, single-system multi-site, and multi-system type. There are 2 types of multisystem LCH: organ involvement and noninvolvement. The skin, bones, lymph nodes, and pituitary gland are low-risk organs; the lungs, bone marrow, liver, and spleen are high-risk organs. The prognosis, course, therapy, and progress of LCH can all be impacted by the particular organ involved [6].

This case involves a young Asian girl who has multisystem LCH. The role of multimodal imaging in early organ identification and disease diagnosis is emphasized.

Case presentation

A 17-month-old girl was admitted to the emergency room with complaints of shortness of breath for 12 hours before being admitted. She also had a fever, diarrhea 5 times a day, and a cough since 1 day before. The patient has failed to gain weight in the last 5 months. The patient complained of recurrent stomatitis every month since the age of 7 months. The patient also had rounded soft tissue swelling in the right temporal area measuring 3 cm in diameter that was progressively increasing for 3 months (Fig. 1). Pathologic evaluation was performed by biopsying the area in the left trunk. On macroscopic examination, a skinned tissue appears brownish-white and rubbery, measuring 0.3 × 0.3 × 0.2 cm. Microscopic examination showed that the tissue was lined with stratified squamous epithelium, and the core was normal. Subepithelially, the fibrocollagen connective tissue stroma appears in contact with inflammatory lymphocyte cells, accompanied by dilatation of blood vessels. In the dermis papillae, round-oval cells are visible that are distributed diffusely. The nuclei are reniform and eccentric, and the cytoplasm is abundant and eosinophilic. It appears that the skin adnexa consist of hair follicles, sudoriferous glands, and sebaceous glands within normal limits. The subcutis consists of mature fat cells; the nuclei are edged within normal limits (Fig. 9). The findings of the immunohistochemical tests for S100 and CD1A were positive (Fig. 10). Patient has received treatment based on protocol in multisystem LCH case such as cytosine-arabinoside, vinblastine, and prednisolone. This patient has a poor prognosis because have multisystem risk organ involvement such as liver and spleen.

Discussion

The single-system LCH affects about 65%-70% of patients, while the multisystem is seen in 10% of LCH cases and has a poor prognosis that frequently results in death. Typically, a diagnosis is made during the first 2 years of life. Multisystem LCH affecting 2 or more systems or organs [4].

The majority of bone involvement (75%) is unifocal and manifests as a soft tissue mass that may enlarge or pain. The most frequently affected site is the skull. LCH manifests radiographically as a single or many osteolytic lesions, sometimes with periosteal reaction [6,7]. After the bone, the skin is the organ system that is most often affected. Cutaneous illness is the most prevalent presentation in patients under 2 years old. About 87% to 93% of patients with cutaneous manifestations also have multisystem involvement. The most typical sign of multisystem LCH is cutaneous lesions accompanied by fever, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, bone damage, and lung damage [6,7]. In multisystem disease, pulmonary involvement is common and usually manifests as tachypnea, coughing, and chest pain. The most frequent radiological abnormalities on a chest CT scan are bilateral interstitial infiltrates with reticulonodular patterns, frequently accompanied by thickening of the peribronchi [7,8]. US of the liver show hypoechoic and abdominal CT or MR images show low signal intensity along the portal veins or biliary tracts. Hypersplenism may accompany splenomegaly [6,7]. Gastrointestinal (GI) tract involvement by LCH is a rare condition and typically noted in male patients with systemic disease and is associated with both poor prognosis and high morbidity [2,9]. However, our patient had abdominal symptoms such as diarrhea and the results of the abdominal CT scan showed thickening of the ileum wall and enhancing after post injection contrast.

In this case, the patient experiences various nonspecific symptoms and organ involvement making it very difficult to early diagnosis. In addition to clinical and radiological features, the diagnosis of LCH must always be predicated on the histological and immunophenotypic assessment of lesional tissue, which must be obtained from the lesion that is easiest to reach, like a skin lesion [7,10].

Conclusion

In our rare case of multisystem LCH, the patient presented with nonspecific complaints such as shortness of breath, cough, fever, diarrhea, lump on the head, history of recurrent mouth ulcers, jaundice of the skin and eyes, an enlarged abdomen and red rashes on the back, abdomen and legs, making it very difficult to establish an initial diagnosis. This case requires many approaches and further investigations such as further anamnesis regarding other sign and symptoms, and other radiological examinations, until finally signs are found that can point to a specific disease. A skin biopsy confirmed LCH. The liver and spleen are risk organ in cases of multisystem LCH and also GI tract involvement in young girl give a poor prognosis to the patients. Diagnostic imaging techniques such as chest and skull radiography, CT scans, and MRIs, radiology plays a critical role in early diagnosis and identifies the affected organ in our rare cases.
