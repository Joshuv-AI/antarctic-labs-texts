“Our culture doesn’t welcome seeing caring for older people as a burden”: understanding older adults’ experiences of coping with unpaid caregiving in Nigeria
Authors: Juliet Chigozie Donatus Ezulike, Shiyu Lu, Prince Chiagozie Ekoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThere is limited insight in the caregiving literature regarding how cultural factors shape the choice of coping strategies of unpaid caregivers. Although research in high-income countries reveals adoption of coping approaches based on age and caregiving circumstances, similar insights are less clear in unpaid caregiving settings in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in sub-Saharan African nations (e.g., Nigeria), where caregiving is deeply embedded in complex social, religious, and cultural contexts. This study thus aimed to investigate older Nigerian unpaid caregivers’ coping mechanisms.MethodsAligning with ages 50–55 as common definitions of older adulthood in sub-Saharan African gerontological studies, in-depth interviews were conducted with 30 community-dwelling older unpaid caregivers aged 54-88. The study was guided by Van Manen’s hermeneutic phenomenological design, while the qualitative data were managed with QSR NVivo 12 software.ResultsOur findings include themes of meaning-making, problem-focused coping, and emotional coping. The caregivers’ utilization of diverse coping strategies was rooted in their Christian religious beliefs and Afrocentric cultural norms.DiscussionSustaining the culture of family caregiving in collectivist societies such as Nigeria is critical. Our findings highlight the urgent need for the recognition of the growing population of older unpaid caregivers and the integration of their support needs into policy frameworks at the federal, state, and local government levels in Nigeria. Additionally, health care and social work interventions must be tailored to address caregivers’ individual situations, incorporating their spiritual beliefs and cultural backgrounds to ensure that they receive the required holistic support to thrive in their caring roles.

Unpaid caregivers often develop a variety of coping strategies to navigate the complexities of caring for ageing family members and friends. According to Lazarus and Folkman (1984), these strategies may be problem-focused, emotion-focused, or cognitive-based. Problem-focused coping, also known as active coping in the literature, involves actively managing stressful situations by attempting to change the person–environment relationship and modify or eliminate the source of stress through individual behavior (Schoenmakers et al., 2015). Some instances of this coping approach include confrontation, strategic problem-solving, seeking support from others, and effective communication. Some scholars (Carr & Pudrovska, 2007; Grover et al., 2015) have argued that problem-focused coping is the most effective strategy based on the idea that individuals utilize such a coping technique when they perceive themselves as having control over challenging circumstances and maintain a positive outlook while taking a self-reliant, optimistic, and proactive approach to managing life stressors.

Next is emotion-focused coping, which involves efforts to regulate and minimize the emotional impact of stressful events. According to the literature, these regulating measures encompass engaging in religious or spiritual activities, repressing negative emotions (Faronbi et al., 2019a; Kristanti et al., 2019; Kyei-Arthur & Codjoe, 2021; Yuan et al., 2021), self-distraction, avoidance, distancing, alcohol and drug use, self-controlling, denial, venting, and self-blame, among other approaches (Carr & Pudrovska, 2007; Pérez-Cruz et al., 2019). Unfortunately, most emotion-focused coping mechanisms are often passive or maladaptive and have been linked to increased burden and anxiety levels (Pérez-Cruz et al., 2019). Scholars therefore argue that people who employ emotion-focused coping strategies generally manage to cope, but not as effectively as those who utilize active coping strategies (Carr & Pudrovska, 2007).

The third category of coping mechanism – cognitive coping, also known as meaning-focused, appraisal-based coping, or meaning-making in the academic literature, involves drawing on existential goals, beliefs (religious or spiritual), and values to inspire and sustain coping (Krok, 2015; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984; Schoenmakers et al., 2015). Examples of this approach include benefit-finding, benefit reminding, positive reappraisals, adaptive goal processes, reordering priorities, infusing ordinary events with positive meaning, and revising one’s perception about the impact of a specific threat (Folkman, 2008).

The literature suggests that caregivers tend to use a combination of different coping strategies, indicating that no single group of coping mechanisms alone is most suitable (Kazemi et al., 2021; Kyei-Arthur & Codjoe, 2021; Li et al., 2021; Pérez-Cruz et al., 2019; Yuan et al., 2021). In essence, employing various coping strategies simultaneously may be more effective in helping unpaid caregivers adapt to the stress of caregiving. Yet, findings from existing studies reveal that compared to younger carers, older carers, especially spouses or adult–children of people living with dementia, tend to primarily utilize the meaning-focused coping approach, as it allows them to infuse positive meanings in their caregiving situation (Gorawara-Bhat et al., 2019; Harel et al., 2022).

This dynamic is particularly evident in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), including countries such as Nigeria where caregiving for older adults is predominantly provided through informal arrangements and is shaped by deeply embedded social, religious, and cultural norms (Ekoh et al., 2021; Ezulike et al., 2024; Okoye, 2013). These norms structure how caregivers construe their experiences and manage the strain and reward from their role. As a collectivist society, caregiving for older adults in Nigeria is understood as a moral and non-negotiable familial obligation primarily borne by children and other family members, particularly women (Ezulike et al., 2024; Okoye, 2013). Such care provision is perceived as a source of spiritual blessing, while neglect or abandonment is believed to invite misfortune (Ezulike et al., 2024, 2026).

The cultural and moral expectation of caregiving in Nigeria is coupled with a weak social security infrastructure and the limited availability of institutionalized long-term care services, leaving the family as the only source of care and support for older individuals (Agbawodikeizu et al., 2024; Ebimgbo et al., 2022). Worsening this issue is the recent demographic change wherein the previously predominant young and middle-aged caregiver cohort is increasingly transitioning into older adulthood, often while managing their own well-being challenges and a depleting social support network (Ezulike et al., 2026). This older caregiver population is a critical but frequently neglected group caught between cultural obligations and the absence of formal support, which thus makes the understanding of their specific coping strategies a crucial social and public health priority.

Despite increasing research on unpaid caregiving among collectivist cultures, the current literature has so far provided very limited insight into how cultural factors shape the choice of coping strategies of unpaid caregivers. Research conducted in high-income nations with ageing populations and a larger proportion of older unpaid caregivers has revealed evidence of caregivers adopting specific coping approaches based on age and caregiving circumstances. However, similar insights are not clear for unpaid caregiving settings in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in SSA nations such as Nigeria. This knowledge gap can be attributed to the disproportionate focus of Nigerian caregiving studies on young and middle-aged unpaid caregivers, as researchers have yet to fully recognize the extent to which these caregiver groups are transitioning into older adulthood (Ezulike et al., 2024, 2026). Considering this knowledge gap, this study sought to answer the following question: What coping strategies do older adults adopt while providing unpaid care to their peer older individuals in Nigeria? This research forms part of a broader project that explores the unpaid caregiving experiences of older adults caring for their aging loved ones in Nigeria.

Theoretical framework

This study draws on the updated Sociocultural Stress and Coping Model (SSCM) by Knight and Sayegh (2010). Aiming to understand how cultural background influences an individual’s perception of the caregiving role and its implications for physical and mental health, the updated model proposed that an individual’s ethnicity or cultural values significantly impact unpaid caregivers’ choice of coping mechanisms (Losada-Baltar, 2017). Additionally, this study incorporates the theoretical framework of Afrocentricity, a social science paradigm developed by Asante (1988) to understand the cultural behaviors of individuals of African descent. A central tenet of Afrocentricity is the conceptualization of human identity as a collective construct within African societies, emphasizing the significance placed on social connections, strong familial bonds, and shared responsibilities (Oyinlola et al., 2025; Schiele, 1996). Building upon this proposition, a key cultural norm is the reverence and care for older adults, who are regarded as representatives of ancestral lineages and custodians of cultural traditions (Pharr et al., 2014).

Our choice of both theories is deliberate, as the updated SSCM developed by Western scholars helps us understand that culture shapes coping, whereas Afrocentricity highlights the specific cultural norms and values that shape the choice of coping in the study sample. For instance, it is well established in the literature that culture shapes such caregiving behavior as the definition of good and bad care, beliefs about the normal versus abnormal aging process, perceptions of burden or stress, and family interaction patterns (Ivey et al., 2013; Nwakasi et al., 2021). Pharr et al. (2014) also observe that in collectivist cultures, forbearance is encouraged, wherein individuals refrain from voicing their challenges and instead silently accept and endure hardship to maintain social harmony. In other words, an individual’s ethnicity, in this case, the Afrocentric ways of being, potentially shapes unpaid caregivers’ perception of caregiving stress and the mechanisms they employ to cope with it.

The SSCM and the theory of Afrocentricity influenced the development of our interview guide and the interpretation of our findings. This approach enabled us to craft culturally relevant interview questions that focus on the key relational dynamics in the Nigerian caregiving context. Although these theories neither dictated the participants’ responses nor rigidly structured the data collection, they subtly shaped the research, allowing participants to reflect on the culturally significant aspects of their caregiving experiences. This leads to a more nuanced understanding of older unpaid caregivers’ coping mechanisms and how culture influences their choice of coping strategies.

Design and methods

Research design

In this study, we employed van Manen (1997) hermeneutic phenomenological design, following the six steps he outlined for hermeneutic phenomenological studies. This design differs from other qualitative methodologies, e.g., generic thematic analysis or grounded theory, in that its aim is not to categorize behavior or generate theory. In the context of phenomenology, various scholars have offered diverse perspectives and approaches to expand Heidegger’s hermeneutic philosophy, part of which van Manen’s approach is foregrounded. They include Gadamer (1975), Ricoeur (1975), and van Manen (1997, van Manen, 2014), among others. We adopted Manen’s approach because, compared to other scholars who mainly expanded on hermeneutic phenomenology’s philosophical underpinnings, his contribution was more practical, providing a methodological outline for conducting phenomenological research.van Manen’s hermeneutic phenomenology involves a reflective and interpretive process that emphasizes the importance of the lived world, dialogue, subjectivity, language, and context in interpreting people’s experiences. Acknowledging that complete bracketing is impossible, his approach recommends ongoing reflexivity alongside interpretive engagement with research participants’ accounts, ensuring that their experiences are understood within their social and cultural contexts (van Manen, 1997). With these, Manen’s six steps for conducting hermeneutic phenomenological research include: (1) turning to the nature of lived experience, (2) investigating experiences as lived, (3) engaging in hermeneutic phenomenological reflection, (4) engaging in hermeneutic phenomenological writing, (5) maintaining a robust/oriented relation, and (6) balancing the research while exploring the parts and the whole.

Following the outlined procedure, we first identified our research topic and reviewed the literature on ageing and caregiving. Second, we planned our research methodology, including research questions, participant recruitment, and data collection strategy. Third, we analyzed our data using van Manen’s three steps for theme generation. Fourth, we focused on illuminating the participants’ feelings, thoughts, and attitudes through interpretive analysis and preliminary drafting of the findings. We wrote reflective descriptions capturing the richness and depth of their experiences. Fifth, we tried balancing our voices with those of the participants by reflexively considering how our assumptions and backgrounds, and interpretations, shaped how we understood the themes. Sixth, we ensured to connect our findings with existing literature and theories.

Research setting and participants

This study was conducted in Owerri, the capital of Imo state, Nigeria. In 2020, the city’s population was approximately 872,604 (World Population Review, 2020). Residents of the city are predominantly from the Igbo ethnic group, adhering to Afrocentric cultural norms that treasure caregiving roles and respect for older people as esteemed members of society. Among the Igbos of Nigeria, a cultural expectation about caring for ageing parents within the family is placed on adult children, mainly daughters and daughters-in-law (Okoye, 2013). Within this ethnic group, informal support systems, such as extended family, community networks, and friends, are indispensable in helping caregivers meet their care recipients’ needs, following the absence of institutional support in the country (Ebimgbo et al., 2022). With an over 96% literacy level, Imo state ranks highest in terms of literacy rate in Nigeria, and its residents – predominantly Christian, demonstrate a strong work ethic across many industries (National Bureau of Statistics, 2010; UNICEF, 2019).

Ethics approval for this study was obtained from the human research ethics committee of City University of Hong Kong before conducting fieldwork. We recruited 30 participants through a snowball sampling approach based on the criteria of being: (a) older persons aged 54 years or above, (b) primary carers for other older individuals, and (c) related to their care recipients by friendship, blood, or marriage. This sample was sufficient as is recommended for qualitative studies to offer sufficient information power (Malterud et al., 2016). We adopted snowball sampling due to initial difficulties in identifying participants who met the study’s age requirement. To expand the participant pool, seven participants initially recruited from the first author’s personal/professional network were requested to refer other eligible individuals. Before interviewing each recommended individual, they were contacted by the research team through a phone call to explain the referral process and the study’s purpose. The research team included the first author, who is female and shares the same ethnic background as the research participants; the second author, who is female of Chinese ethnicity; and the third author, who is male and also shares the same ethnic background as the participants. With a median age of 17.4 and about 8% of the country’s population being aged 50 and older, age 54 was set as participants’ minimum age before inclusion in our study, in alignment with common definitions of older adulthood (usually aged 50–55) in sub-Saharan African gerontological research (Akinrolie et al., 2024; Kalu et al., 2021, 2022; United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, 2019b).

Data collection and analysis

The authors designed a semi-structured interview protocol for the study. Between July and November 2021, we conducted telephone interviews with 26 participants and face-to-face interviews with four participants, lasting approximately 50–75 min. Phone interviews were necessary due to concerns about the COVID-19 pandemic. Hence, participants were given this option, and many chose it. One interview was conducted using a combination of English and Igbo, whereas 29 others were conducted in English. The predominant use of English was not surprising, given the high literacy rate of the study location and the high education level of the study participants. The participants were mostly middle and upper class, which may impact their caregiving experiences, as they may have additional economic and family obligations.

The interviews were transcribed verbatim, with participants’ identities anonymized to facilitate data analysis. Following a parallel transcription framework, the transcript encoded in Igbo was translated into English (Nikander, 2008), while an independent researcher proficient in both languages validated it for accuracy. We conducted member checking with 22 participants who were provided with their interview transcripts and preliminary analytic interpretations to review for accuracy and completeness (Birt et al., 2016). This ensured that the analyses adequately reflected participants’ lived realities, enhancing the study’s trustworthiness and validity. We then scheduled follow-up interviews with each participant to address any concerns and to gather extra information on specific topics based on new insights gleaned from other participants’ responses, thus adding to our study’s trustworthiness.

While analyzing the data, we followed van Manen (1997)’s three steps for thematic analysis of phenomenological studies. This involved a holistic reading of a transcript to understand its overall meaning; a selective reading to identify significant excerpts; and a line-by-line approach to systematically examine the participants’ experiences, identifying patterns, connections, and overarching themes. To ensure coding reliability and facilitate initial researcher triangulation, which helped reduce bias, the first author and an experienced independent qualitative researcher analyzed the first two de-identified interview transcripts and developed relevant codes (Carter et al., 2014). The coding was inductive, allowing codes to emerge from reading the transcripts. Narratives with the same central idea were coded as independent codes, whereas narratives with similar ideas group together to form coding trees, including parent and child nodes. Subsequently, the first author conducted an independent analysis of the remaining transcripts, engaging in frequent peer debriefings and discussions with the co-authors to provide extra perspectives.

During our weekly meetings, we engaged in reflexive discussions regarding how our backgrounds, assumptions, and interpretations shaped our understanding of emerging themes. The first author’s cultural and linguistic alignment with participants fostered connection and an in-depth understanding of culturally embedded meanings. This alignment may have facilitated open discussion of coping mechanisms related to Afrocentric values and intergenerational obligations. The second author’s external perspectives served to mitigate potential bias through facilitated examination of implicit cultural assumptions. Through continuous discussion and reflexivity, the team established balanced interpretations that acknowledged both emic and etic viewpoints on the caregiving experiences of older Nigerian unpaid carers. For instance, the emic co-researchers ensured that internal cultural meaning and beliefs were maintained, while the etic co-researchers brought objectivity, comparing cultures and asking questions that ensured a more nuanced interpretation that a wider audience could understand. Meaning-making

The theme of meaning-making highlights how the participants managed their caregiving stress through positive reappraisal by drawing upon Afrocentric cultural values that emphasize reverence for older people, intergenerational reciprocity, communal obligation, and Christian beliefs about divine reward and suffering. Doing so enabled them to reframe difficult caregiving situations and identify potential benefits.

Caregiving as preparation and modeling for the future

In line with the Afrocentric norms of collective responsibility and reciprocal care, caregivers viewed their role as a form of preparation for the future and an opportunity to demonstrate appropriate caregiving behavior to their younger family members, passing down valuable caregiving skills that align with traditional African cultural expectations of younger relatives caring for older adults. In this sense, caregiving was not only just appraised as an immediate or personal obligation, but also as a strategy for sustaining moral order across generations. As a 60-year-old daughter explained:I believe that my children and grandchildren are learning on the job [caregiving]. They’re learning how to take care of me when I get old. That kind of thing… You’re modelling the right things for them. So, I try to see it [burdensome caregiving role] as a training opportunity for them.

Caregiving as a route for blessings

By maintaining the Afrocentric and Christian belief about unpaid caregiving being a pathway to divine blessings, caregivers were able to avoid excessive focus on their caregiving burden, reminding themselves that it is improper to view a source of benefits as stressful. The caregivers thus coped by perceiving it as a spiritually meaningful labor, highlighting how religious and cultural beliefs unite to discourage their articulation of burden while legitimizing endurance. A 57-year-old daughter-in-law expressed:You know, as humans, at times you will think about it [caregiving burden]. But you quickly withdraw your mind from going towards that angle… Our culture doesn’t welcome seeing caring for older people as a burden. So, because of our culture, we don’t normally see it as so much of a burden. Instead, we see it as bringing blessings.

But you quickly withdraw your mind from going towards that angle… Our culture doesn’t welcome seeing caring for older people as a burden. Reliance on God as supreme helper

Drawing on their Christian religious beliefs and engaging in spiritual practices such as prayer and spiritual surrender, the caregivers reconceptualized their caregiving stressors as a collaborative responsibility shared with a divine authority. In this process, they reallocated agency, rather than relinquishing it. This religious framing facilitated the sustenance of their moral obligation to caregiving while mitigating feelings of overwhelm, as reflected in a 54-year-old daughter’s statement: “I pray. We still pray to God, you know. The prayer always works, but it may not work immediately. Once you pray, you feel that you have handed this thing [caregiving burden] over to God and you feel better.”

Problem-focused coping mechanism

Problem-focused coping was as popular as meaning-making among the caregivers and was characterized by collective, relationally mediated approaches, rather than individualistic problem solving. This theme highlights how caregivers actively sought to address the sources of their caregiving strain by mobilizing social networks, negotiating care arrangements, and redistributing caregiving tasks in ways that are consistent with Afrocentric values of respect for social hierarchy and collective responsibility.

Negotiated problem-solving through relational authority

Where caregivers could not elicit the desired behavior from their care recipients, they often sought the intervention of respected intermediaries such as their friends, relatives, or community elders to influence their care recipients’ behavior. This approach reflects Afrocentric norms prioritizing respect, relational harmony, and social hierarchy. A 56-year-old adult-son explained:Sometimes, it even requires me complaining to people that can talk to him.… It’s not everybody that can do it. You must make sure that it is somebody he can listen to. When you complain to them, they can talk to him.

Similarly, caregivers described frequently engaging in careful dialogue with their care recipients to convey the challenges they faced without appearing disrespectful. Such negotiations allowed them to assert their needs and collaboratively identify solutions while also preserving relational balance, revealing how problem-solving was rooted in culturally appropriate communication practices. As a 56-year-old daughter explained:The domestic helpers living with her [care recipient] often complain about mummy doing this and that, especially not allowing them to take a rest. So, I try to discuss the issue with her. I will be like “Mummy, if this girl leaves, you should know that I won’t employ another person…” And once you say it, she knows you are not happy, and she is in for it if she continues with that behavior.

If there is a funeral, he could call somebody to take him there. After that, they will bring him back home. Strategic distribution of caregiving tasks

Certain caregivers expressed that they addressed some of their caregiving stress by distributing care responsibilities. The adopted measures include hiring domestic helpers and sharing tasks among family members, also reflecting the Afrocentric value of collectivism, where caregiving is regarded as a shared responsibility. As a 57-year-old daughter-in-law, some carers remarked “It [caregiving] is a lot of stress. But what is easing it is that by the time it is evening, and she [sister in-law] comes home, I will be like ‘I am off duty now, I won’t be working again.’” Another daughter-in-law, aged 63, explained:I have a paid domestic helper who comes to my house in the morning from Monday to Friday…because I go to work. In the morning, before leaving, I make sure that she [care recipient] has taken her bath before the woman [domestic helper] comes…the woman stays with her from that time till 4 pm. So, that has been helpful for me.

These problem-focused coping mechanisms were a pragmatic adaptation to sustain care provision, supported by the overarching belief that caregiving was a morally essential and spiritually approved duty. Meaning-making, therefore, served as the interpretive base legitimizing problem-focused coping, while setting culturally embedded boundaries to guide how such an approach may be performed.

Emotion-focused coping as culturally regulated emotional containment

This theme highlights the approaches carers adopted to manage the negative emotional reactions associated with burdensome unpaid caregiving roles, in ways that prioritized the Afrocentric values of collective well-being and relational harmony. Rather than openly expressing their distress, the caregivers employed culturally sanctioned mechanisms that helped them regulate emotional strain while protecting their care recipients and maintaining social cohesion.

Receiving emotional support from others

Caregivers received emotional support in the form of verbal encouragement, prayers, and shared humor from their family members, friends, and religious groups through in-person conversations or phone calls. This type of support served as a relational strategy for emotional regulation, which helped caregivers feel supported and understood. Some participants remarked: “Some people come to pray, you know. This was the kind of support we had” (Husband, 62). “My children also participated in taking care of her. Sometimes they call. They make us [caregiver and care recipient] laugh, you know; joke or something like that. That’s therapy” (Daughter-in-law, 63).

Protective emotional restraint and selective disclosure

Some caregivers reported that they frequently intentionally concealed negative emotions, such as anger or worry, from their care recipients. This restraint was done to prevent emotional harm to their care recipients, which, otherwise, could adversely impact their health or lead them to under-requesting necessary assistance, thereby disrupting relational balance. A 64-year-old son explained:Sometimes, …no matter how unhappy and troubled you are, you have to meet their [care recipients’] needs. And see, in my case, I have to make sure that she [care recipient] does not even know because that will be a bigger problem… You can imagine when she finds out, she may say “Do not do this for me,” you know. You will then go back and feel so terrible. So, I hide those ones from her. You can earn 100,000 naira a month and then you have 200,000 naira problem waiting for 100,000 naira money… So, you have to bottle up so that she would not know.

Also, few caregivers adopted self-distraction strategies such as watching television and channeling their energy to their work, to divert their attention away from their caregiving stress. Some participants stated: “I watch TV, I watch some of these series of films and other things, and some of the church programs on the television” (Wife, 56). “I channel a lot of energy into doing my work, like teaching and reading almost every day” (Daughter, 54). These practices demonstrate culturally mediated emotional regulation, through which restraint signifies a safeguarding mechanism aligning with Afrocentric values of respect, endurance, and emotional responsibility toward older individuals.

Discussion

Using a hermeneutic phenomenological approach, this study aimed to explore the coping strategies employed by older individuals providing unpaid care to their aging loved ones in Nigeria. Our analysis revealed that while the caregivers’ coping mechanisms can be broadly grouped based on Lazarus and Folkman (1984)’s classic typology of meaning-making, problem-focused coping, and emotion-focused coping, their uniqueness lies in the deep and pervasive role of Afrocentric cultural values that permeate and interconnect these strategies. These coping strategies function dynamically and interdependently, with meaning-making emerging as the foundational element shaping and enabling the two other mechanisms for coping. A holistic view of our findings reveals that while meaning-making served as the overarching framework, the spiritual and cultural perspective through which the caregivers appraised their role. This strategy, in turn, enabled their use of problem-focused strategies by seeking practical solutions to manage the stress emanating from their caring duties. Finally, they adopted emotion-focused mechanisms to manage the residual emotional distress, which problem-focused approaches did not fully address.

With meaning-making being the most prevalent mechanism utilized by the caregivers, they reappraised their caring role as a means of modeling exemplary care provision for their younger kin to learn and emulate later. This finding is somewhat different from previous studies, such as Ugargol and Bailey (2021), which reported these ideas as a motivator for assuming the caring role. Meaning-making was also facilitated when the caregivers relied on their Christian faith to facilitate coping, signifying that caregiving is a spiritually sanctioned labor within the Afrocentric-Christian perspective, where endurance portrays faithfulness instead of weakness. This finding aligns with existing research on caregiving in some African (Faronbi et al., 2019a; Kyei-Arthur & Codjoe, 2021) and Asian (Kristanti et al., 2019) contexts, where caregivers usually draw on their religious beliefs to facilitate coping. As Park (2010) explained, meaning-making involves a complex process of constructing significance from situations, events, and life experiences, which can substantially influence one’s psychological adjustment and overall well-being. Our findings uniquely expand the meaning-making framework by incorporating an emphasis on pursuing holistic well-being, encompassing both spiritual and physical dimensions, to promote human thriving.

One reason for our finding on meaning-making could be that in the absence of formal support systems such as caregiver stipends, peer support, or respite care, which are more common in high-income countries (Li et al., 2021; Pérez-Cruz et al., 2019), the caregivers may have had no other viable option but to turn to their faith to find hope and resilience in the face of their seemingly overwhelming caregiving role. This, therefore, highlights the urgent need for Nigerian policymakers to formulate welfare policies aimed at reducing unpaid caregivers’ burden. The finding also suggests the need for social service and health care providers in Nigeria to acknowledge and incorporate the role of religion and spirituality in caregiver support interventions. One instance is such service providers collaborating with religious leaders/organizations to offer faith-based counseling and support services to carers, integrating religio-cultural beliefs and rituals into carer support programs to enhance their coping capacities.

Caregivers’ predominant use of meaning-making as a coping strategy can be understood through the lens of the SSCM and the Afrocentric paradigm, which both highlight cultural values as enormously shaping caregivers’ choice of coping mechanisms (Asante, 1988; Losada-Baltar, 2017; Schiele, 1996). Although western (Gorawara-Bhat et al., 2019; Harel et al., 2022) and non-western studies (Faronbi et al., 2019a; Kristanti et al., 2019; Kyei-Arthur & Codjoe, 2021) have also reported meaning-making as a coping strategy among unpaid caregivers, the uniqueness of our study’s findings is that meaning-making was primarily shaped by Afrocentric and Christian beliefs. In contrast, meaning-making in Western contexts tended to be shaped more by individual values or situational factors such as caregivers’ personal life experiences and the nature of their relationship with care recipients, as observed in existing studies (Gorawara-Bhat et al., 2019; Harel et al., 2022). Similarly, compared to other non-Western studies, especially those in SSA, which have predominantly focused on a majority sample of young and middle-aged carers (Faronbi et al., 2019a; Kyei-Arthur & Codjoe, 2021), our findings are unique in that they focus on older carers.

The intricate interplay of religion and culture in shaping coping strategies among caregivers in Nigeria highlights the need to develop microlevel Afrocentric coping theories grounded in the distinct subthemes of meaning-making in this study. Such theorizing can deepen our understanding of how individuals of African heritage cope, particularly in the realm of caregiving. Such Afrocentric theories may also enhance our understanding of coping among caregivers in other collectivist cultural contexts, where they may hold relevant applications.

We also found that the caregivers adopted various problem-focused coping mechanisms, which were also foregrounded in Afrocentric principles, to manage their caregiving burden. These approaches allowed the caregiving dyad to understand the caregiver’s stress and navigate the sociocultural expectation of silence around caregiver hardship (Ezulike et al., 2024). Unpaid carers’ adoption of similar problem-focused coping strategies has also been documented in past studies (Kazemi et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 2021). In the context of support networks, such as the availability of instrumental support, the forms of support described in our findings are consistent with those reported in other Western and Asian studies (Arriagada, 2020; Lee, 2020; Leggett et al., 2021). However, our findings are somewhat unique in that formal support systems were not available to caregivers in Nigeria, as other studies indicate that unpaid caregivers often use both formal and informal support, with a preference for the latter (Arriagada, 2020; Leggett et al., 2021).

In our study, caregivers’ popular adoption of problem-focused coping mechanisms is somewhat of a surprise, considering that research has established that most collectivist cultures encourage forbearance, which entails keeping challenges to oneself, silently accepting and enduring hardship, as it sustains social harmony (Pharr et al., 2014). The popularity of this coping mechanism alongside meaning-making may be attributed to caregivers’ educational level, as our sample consisted mainly of individuals with higher levels of education. Hence, they had the knowledge and skills to tackle problems, albeit within the boundaries of Afrocentric values.

Furthermore, our findings highlighted caregivers’ use of emotion-focused coping as a method of culturally regulated emotional control. The receipt of emotional support from individuals, including family members, friends, and members of the local community, reflects the theory of Afrocentricity, which highlights the importance of relationships that serve as a means of conveying support (Asante, 1988; Schiele, 1996). Individuals within this cultural context are viewed as partners, sharing equal sources of strength to foster the collective well-being of the community members (Schiele, 1996). Additionally, few caregivers utilized culturally mediated emotional restraints such as self-distraction and concealing negative emotions, which can be considered passive coping mechanisms. Previous research has also reported caregivers’ use of diverse passive emotion-focused coping techniques similar to those observed in this study (Kazemi et al., 2021; Pérez-Cruz et al., 2019; Yuan et al., 2021). However, such a coping approach was the least common among the caregivers in this study, likely due to their age and experience. Given that the participants were older adults, most of whom had spent some years in the unpaid caregiving role, they may have learnt through experience that directing their efforts toward alleviating the stressor is more beneficial in the long run than employing passive means.

Based on the findings of this study, several key recommendations emerge to inform both policy and practice. First, there is a pressing need to formally recognize the role of older adults as caregivers within national and local social policy frameworks in Nigeria, as they operate without institutional support, despite bearing an enormous caregiving burden. Government and development agencies should consider introducing targeted social protection policies, such as caregiver stipends or subsidized health services and tax relief, to alleviate these pressures. Such initiatives would provide critical financial assistance to older caregivers in Nigeria, many of whom fall within the lowest socioeconomic brackets (Ekoh et al., 2020). Also, there is a need to create physical and virtual spaces where caregivers can share their experiences, access information, and offer mutual support. Structured peer support groups or caregiver forums can facilitate knowledge exchange, emotional solidarity, and collective problem-solving, enhancing caregivers’ ability to navigate the challenges they face. Additionally, time banking, an international service model, could also be adopted to provide social care services to community members, as it enables individuals to exchange services using time as a currency for various incentives (Lu et al., 2024).

Notwithstanding our study’s findings and recommendations, one main limitation is that the sample consisted primarily of highly educated caregivers, due to our initial challenge with participant recruitment and the subsequent purposive snowball sampling strategy adopted. The reliance on referrals from initially recruited, university-educated participants resulted in a disproportionate representation of literate caregivers. Subsequent research should include older unpaid carers with lower levels of formal education, to ensure equitable sample distribution. It is also worth noting that our sample was heterogenous with respect to the health conditions of care recipients and the duration of caregiving duties. Although the specific stressors linked to these diverse contexts undoubtedly differed, our phenomenological analysis centered on the essential shared aspects of the coping experience. Our findings revealed that the primary coping strategies, particularly meaning-making anchored in Christianity and Afrocentric norms, exhibited consistency across these varied caregiving scenarios. This points to the overarching Nigerian cultural framework as a potent, shared resource for coping that extends beyond the specifics of individual caregiver-care recipient dyads, thus emphasizing the profound sociocultural imprint on caregivers’ lived experiences.

Conclusion

This hermeneutic phenomenological study offers insight into older Nigerian unpaid caregivers’ coping mechanisms and how they were shaped by Christianity and various Afrocentric norms, including intergenerational respect/care for older people, collectivism, and relational identity, which served as the interpretive lens through which caregiving was understood as a moral obligation. From a social policy perspective, our findings highlight the critical gap between the central role of unpaid caregiving and the absence of formal caregiver support in Nigeria. Caregivers’ primary reliance on culturally informed coping mechanisms should be regarded as a means of adapting to the absence of institutional support services. Therefore, it is urgent for policymakers to recognize the growing population of older unpaid caregivers and integrate their support needs into policy frameworks at the federal, state, and local government levels in Nigeria. Additionally, health care and social work interventions must be tailored to address caregivers’ individual situations, spiritual beliefs, and cultural backgrounds, ensuring that they receive the required holistic support to thrive in their caring roles. Sustaining the culture of unpaid caregiving in Nigeria is critical. Yet, policymakers must take action to ensure that such roles are not too burdensome.
