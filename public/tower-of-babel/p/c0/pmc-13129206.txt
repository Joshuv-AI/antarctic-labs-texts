Estrogen Deficiency in Menopause: A Major Contributor to Cartilage Degeneration and Osteoarthritis: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Manpreet Kaur Tehalia, Shubhra Agarwal, Astha Lalwani, Sonika Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Menopause-associated estrogen deficiency has been implicated in cartilage degeneration and osteoarthritis (OA) progression. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluates evidence from human and animal studies. Five databases were searched until March 2025. Eligible studies included postmenopausal women or ovariectomized (OVX) animal models assessing cartilage degeneration, OA severity, or related biomarkers. Human and animal studies were analyzed separately. Random-effects meta-analyses were conducted when outcomes were comparable. Eleven studies met the criteria (human, n = 6; animal, n = 5). Pooled human results for C-terminal telopeptides of type II collagen and magnetic resonance imaging-based cartilage thickness showed non-significant trends toward greater cartilage loss in estrogen-deficient women. OVX animal studies demonstrated significantly greater histological cartilage degeneration compared with controls. Hormone replacement therapy showed inconsistent but directionally protective effects. Estrogen deficiency is associated with cartilage degeneration, with strong supportive evidence from OVX models. Human evidence indicates similar trends but lacks statistical significance, highlighting the need for larger and more standardized studies.

INTRODUCTION

Menopause represents a major physiological transition in women, characterized by the permanent cessation of ovarian function and a marked decline in circulating estrogen levels. Estrogen plays a critical role in maintaining joint homeostasis through its anti-inflammatory effects, regulation of chondrocyte metabolism, and protection against oxidative stress. Consequently, estrogen deficiency during menopause has been hypothesized to accelerate cartilage degeneration and increase susceptibility to osteoarthritis (OA) [12]. Clinically, menopause is confirmed retrospectively after 12 consecutive months of amenorrhea [23].

Postmenopausal women exhibit a higher prevalence and severity of OA compared with premenopausal women, suggesting a hormonal influence on disease progression. Estrogen receptors are present in chondrocytes, synovial cells, and subchondral bone, indicating that estrogen depletion may directly impact joint tissues. Preclinical studies further support this relationship: ovariectomized (OVX) animal models consistently show increased cartilage degradation, elevated inflammatory markers, and alterations in bone microarchitecture [24]. Hormonal changes significantly impact quality of life, influencing both physical and emotional well-being [56]. Systematic reviews indicate that a substantial proportion of women experience hot flashes during the menopausal transition [7]. Beyond reproductive health, estrogen deficiency contributes to decreased bone density and increased cardiovascular risk, highlighting the systemic effects of menopause [24].

Individual experiences of menopause are also shaped by cultural and ethnic factors, which influence symptom perception, awareness, and management [89]. This supports a biocultural framework for understanding menopause, emphasizing the need for individualized healthcare approaches that consider sociocultural context and lifestyle factors such as diet and physical activity [110].

While hormone replacement therapy (HRT) has been proposed as a potential intervention to mitigate menopausal OA risk, findings remain inconsistent, with variations in timing, dosage, and formulation contributing to heterogeneous outcomes [15]. Clinicians should evaluate contraindications and consider patient preferences when prescribing HRT. Non-hormonal therapies and supportive measures are increasingly employed, reflecting women’s interest in holistic approaches to symptom management [511].

OA is a chronic and degenerative disorder of the joints, characterized by progressive degradation of articular cartilage, synovial inflammation, remodeling of subchondral bone, and the development of osteophytes. The underlying disease mechanisms are multifactorial, reflecting complex interactions among mechanical stressors, biochemical mediators, and biological responses within joint tissues. In women, hormonal disturbances—particularly those occurring during the menopausal transition—have emerged as important contributors to OA susceptibility and progression. Estrogen, which declines markedly with the onset of menopause, is known to exert protective actions on cartilage by modulating chondrocyte metabolism, suppressing inflammatory pathways, and mitigating cellular senescence [1213].

Estrogen deficiency is also associated with elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), which contribute to cartilage degradation [1415]. Consequently, postmenopausal women exhibit higher rates of OA and increased susceptibility to joint degeneration [1617]. Beyond its anti-inflammatory properties, estrogen modulates the balance between matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which degrade cartilage, and their inhibitors, tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases. Loss of estrogen increases MMP activity, accelerating cartilage breakdown and pain [151819]. These changes are accompanied by subchondral bone alterations, which often precede detectable cartilage degeneration, indicating a close relationship between bone remodeling and cartilage health [2021].

Evidence suggests that HRT may mitigate some degenerative changes in OA by restoring estrogen levels, alleviating symptoms, and potentially slowing cartilage deterioration [172223]. However, the benefits remain somewhat controversial, with studies reporting variable effects on joint health. The timing of HRT initiation relative to menopause appears critical, with early administration—near menopause onset—yielding better outcomes than delayed therapy [1324].

In addition to hormonal influences, OA pathophysiology is shaped by aging and mechanical stress, both of which contribute to cartilage wear and joint dysfunction [252627]. Aging alters cartilage extracellular matrix composition, reducing its ability to withstand compressive forces [27]. Mechanical stressors such as obesity further exacerbate cartilage degeneration by increasing joint load, underscoring the importance of addressing lifestyle factors in OA management [2829].

Menopause represents a distinct risk factor for OA due to estrogen deficiency, which affects both cartilage and bone metabolism [3031]. Epidemiological studies indicate that women face a 2–4-fold increased risk of developing OA during the menopausal transition, particularly affecting the knees and hands. This heightened risk suggests a clear hormonal and biological influence [32333435]. Menopause-related changes in body composition, including increased central fat deposition, further contribute to joint stress and OA progression, highlighting the importance of weight management as part of prevention and treatment strategies [3637].

Given the limited clarity in human data and strong mechanistic evidence from animal models, a systematic and separated synthesis of human and animal evidence is essential. This review therefore evaluates the relationship between estrogen deficiency and cartilage degeneration by analyzing human and OVX animal studies independently, resolving methodological limitations in prior combined analyses.

Aim

This meta-analysis systematically examines the impact of menopause and estrogen deficiency on cartilage degeneration and the progression of OA by integrating evidence from both human and animal studies.

Data extraction and risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers worked independently to obtain study-specific information, employing a predefined and standardized data collection sheet. Extracted information included study characteristics (author[s], year, and study design), sample size, details of interventions or exposures (menopause status—natural or surgical, OVX in animal models, and HRT use if applicable), and comparator groups (premenopausal women, non-OVX animals, or hormone-treated groups). Outcome measures comprised cartilage degeneration, OA severity, and biomarkers of cartilage metabolism.

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies were evaluated independently by the reviewers. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were appraised using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool, whereas animal studies were assessed using the SYRCLE (SYstematic Review Center for Laboratory animal Experimentation) risk-of-bias framework. Any inconsistencies in data extraction or quality ratings were addressed through consultation and consensus with a third reviewer.

Statistical analysis

Human meta-analysis

Outcomes included C-terminal telopeptide (CTX), N-telopeptide of type I collagen (NTX), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-assessed cartilage thickness. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were computed and pooled using random-effects models.

Animal meta-analysis

Pooled outcomes were limited to histological cartilage degeneration due to incompatible measurement scales across studies. Random-effects models were applied.

Subgroup analysis

The subgroup analyses (Fig. 3) further highlight the role of estrogen depletion in OA pathogenesis. Biomarker studies (Nagase et al. [47]) demonstrated significant suppression of CTX-II and NTX following treatment interventions, suggesting a direct relationship between estrogen depletion and cartilage turnover.

HRT: Studies assessing the effect of HRT on OA progression, including Holinka et al. [45] and Pullerits et al. [48], reported reductions in CTX-II and osteocalcin levels, indicating potential protective effects against cartilage breakdown and bone resorption. Additionally, Pullerits et al. [48] observed improved bone mineral density (BMD) at the femoral neck and hip over 2 years, further supporting the role of estrogen in skeletal integrity.

Tibial cartilage loss and defect progression: The study by Hanna et al. [44] reported significant tibial and patellar cartilage loss in postmenopausal individuals, along with notable tibiofemoral defect progression, reinforcing the impact of estrogen deficiency on joint deterioration. MRI scans revealed a 15% greater loss of cartilage thickness in postmenopausal women than in their premenopausal counterparts.

Animal models: Findings from OVX animal studies also align with human observations. Fujita et al. [41] documented significant reductions in trabecular and cortical BMD, increased osteoclast numbers, and cartilage thickness loss in OVX models, validating the role of estrogen depletion in bone and cartilage degradation. Likewise, Gao et al. [42] demonstrated lower serum-free and total E2 levels in postmenopausal women, which correlated with increased cartilage degeneration and osteoarthritic changes, highlighting the hormonal influence on joint health. In OVX rat models (Jochems et al. [46]), histological analysis showed increased MMP expression, particularly MMP-13, which is known to drive cartilage degradation.

Alternative models: Jochems et al. [46] and Che Ahmad Tantowi et al. [49] explored alternative models, including OVX-induced CIA murine models and Ficus deltoidea treatment in OVX rats. These studies consistently reported cartilage degradation, inflammation, and alterations in bone turnover in estrogen-deficient conditions. Notably, Che Ahmad Tantowi et al. [49] demonstrated that phytoestrogen treatment significantly reduced cartilage degradation scores, suggesting potential therapeutic applications.

Publication bias assessment

Egger’s regression test suggested the presence of potential small-study effects (z = –2.31, P = 0.021). Nevertheless, the trim-and-fill procedure did not impute any additional studies, indicating that publication bias is unlikely to influence the pooled outcomes meaningfully.

The funnel plot (Fig. 4) further illustrates the distribution of study effect sizes, and post-trim-and-fill correction (Fig. 5) shows no substantial shift in the estimated effects.

Subgroup analyses were conducted by species, OA severity, and study design to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. However, the analysis did not explain the observed variability (R2 = 0.00%).

Sensitivity and meta-regression analysis

Meta-regression analysis was conducted to investigate further potential moderators, including species (human vs. rat), study type (RCT, observational, and biomarker), sample size, and OA severity. The model did not significantly explain heterogeneity (QM = 3.41, P = 0.64), and none of the tested moderators significantly influenced effect sizes. Estimates for species differences (SMD: –1.98, 95% CI: [–18.60, 14.64], P = 0.82), study type (biomarker: SMD: –4.10, 95% CI: [–12.16, 3.96], P = 0.32; observational: SMD: 0.24, 95% CI: [–6.20, 6.68], P = 0.94), sample size (SMD: 0.0019, 95% CI: [–0.11, 0.12], P = 0.97), and OA severity (SMD: –0.98, 95% CI: [–4.66, 2.71], P = 0.60) were all non-significant.

An influence analysis was conducted to identify high-leverage studies, but their removal did not substantially alter the results. Additionally, a robust variance estimation model confirmed similar effect size estimates without improving heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis using a leave-one-out approach further confirmed the robustness of the findings, as the exclusion of individual studies did not significantly alter the overall effect size. The results suggest that the conclusions of this meta-analysis are stable and not driven by any single study (Fig. 6).

Risk of bias assessment

According to the SYRCLE risk-of-bias evaluation, most animal studies demonstrated a low risk of bias in areas such as sequence generation, comparability of baseline characteristics, and management of incomplete outcome data. In contrast, domains including allocation concealment, random housing, and blinding of investigators and outcome assessors were frequently rated as unclear or high risk. These limitations were particularly evident in the studies by Christgau et al. [40] and Che Ahmad Tantowi et al. [49], leading to their classification as having a high overall risk of bias. Conversely, the investigations by Ham and Carlson [43] and Jochems et al. [46] adhered to rigorous methodological standards, resulting in an overall low risk of bias.

With respect to the Risk of Bias (RoB) 2 assessment for randomized controlled trials, most studies showed a low risk of bias in the domains related to missing outcome data and selection of reported results. Nonetheless, some concerns were identified regarding randomisation procedures, deviations from intended interventions, and the assessment of outcomes. These issues were most notable in the studies by Christgau et al. [40] and Ham and Carlson [43], which demonstrated unclear risk in the randomisation domain. Despite these limitations, none of the trials were deemed to have a high overall risk of bias (Figs. 7 and 8).

DISCUSSION

This systematic review demonstrates that estrogen deficiency contributes to cartilage degeneration, supported strongly by OVX animal models. However, human pooled results were not statistically significant, though they consistently trended toward greater degeneration in estrogen-deficient women.

Our meta-analysis demonstrates a significant association between menopause and increased OA severity, as reflected by higher levels of cartilage degradation markers and radiological progression of OA. This aligns with the findings of Ryu et al. [25], who reported that postmenopausal women, particularly those with hypertension, had a higher risk of OA compared to premenopausal women (odds ratio [OR]: 1.408, 95% CI: 1.092–1.815), supporting the detrimental impact of menopause on joint health.

Consistent with animal studies, Castañeda et al. [28] observed that estrogen deficiency in rabbits significantly increased cartilage degradation, as indicated by elevated Mankin scores. This mirrors our meta-analysis, which found a SMD indicative of substantial cartilage loss in OVX animals compared to controls. Furthermore, the inverse relationship between BMD and cartilage integrity in their study reinforces the link between estrogen deficiency, bone loss, and cartilage deterioration.

The protective effects of HRT in postmenopausal women were also evident. Jung et al. [23] reported a 30% lower prevalence of knee OA among women receiving menopausal hormone therapy (OR: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.50–0.99). Our meta-analysis corroborated these findings, showing reduced cartilage-degradation biomarkers, such as CTX-II, in HRT users, indicating that estrogen plays a key role in maintaining cartilage integrity. Similarly, Pullerits et al. [48] demonstrated that HRT significantly decreased serum soluble receptor for advanced glycation end products levels—a marker of cartilage catabolism—and improved BMD at the hip and femoral neck. Our findings support these results, with moderate effect sizes (SMD = 0.58–0.96) favoring HRT in preserving cartilage health and mitigating OA progression.

Animal studies further reinforced the role of estrogen in OA pathophysiology. It was found that OVX-induced estrogen depletion caused substantial cartilage damage, particularly when combined with additional joint stressors. These findings are consistent with our results, showing significant increases in cartilage degradation markers such as cartilage oligomeric matrix protein and CTX-II, often with SMDs exceeding 2.0. Macrini et al. [34] observed a strong correlation between reproductive status and OA severity in baboon models, mirroring human epidemiological data. Our subgroup analyses across species confirmed that estrogen depletion consistently exacerbates OA pathology, highlighting its critical role in cartilage homeostasis.

Our meta-regression identified CTX-II, osteocalcin, and MMP-1 as key biomarkers of OA progression in postmenopausal women. These findings are consistent with those of Gao et al. [42], who reported a significant reduction in total E2 levels in postmenopausal OA patients compared with non-OA controls (SMD: –1.15). Similarly, we observed a substantial decline in estrogen-related biomarkers that correlated with increased cartilage degeneration. Fujita et al. [41] reported that OVX mice exhibited a 24%–27% reduction in trabecular BMD accompanied by increased TRAP-positive osteoclast activity; our results echo these findings, confirming that estrogen deficiency drives both cartilage and bone deterioration.

Limitations and sources of heterogeneity

The included studies varied in design, population characteristics, OA severity, and interventions, resulting in I2 values ranging from moderate to high. Although subgroup and meta-regression analyses were conducted, they did not fully account for the observed heterogeneity. Potential confounders—including body mass index, physical activity, diet, and genetic predisposition—were not systematically controlled, and these factors independently influence cartilage degeneration. Some studies, particularly animal experiments, involved small sample sizes, limiting generalizability to humans. Risk of bias assessments highlighted methodological concerns, especially regarding blinding, allocation concealment, and randomization in animal and observational studies.

HRT-related findings were heterogeneous due to several factors. Timing of initiation appears critical; earlier therapy, closer to menopause onset, offers greater protective effects than delayed initiation. Dose, formulation (conjugated estrogens versus E2), and duration of treatment also influence outcomes. Individual responses to HRT may vary according to estrogen metabolism, baseline cartilage status, and comorbidities. Additionally, heterogeneity arose from differences in study design, with some studies relying on clinical outcomes (pain, function) and others on biomarkers or imaging, resulting in variability in reported effects.

CONCLUSION

Estrogen deficiency plays a significant role in cartilage degeneration, supported by strong evidence from animal studies and consistent trends in human data. Human pooled estimates did not reach statistical significance, highlighting the need for larger, standardized studies.

Early HRT initiation may provide cartilage-protective benefits, but evidence remains heterogeneous. Future research should prioritize standardized biomarkers, uniform imaging protocols, and mechanistic human studies.
