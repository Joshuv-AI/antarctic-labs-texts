Phenomenological Study of Intra-Spill Break Spots in Dose-Driven Continuous Scanning Proton Therapy

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeDose Driven Continuous Scanning proton therapy offers reduced beam delivery time compared to discrete spot scanning but requires accurate characterization of temporal beam parameters for precise dose calculation. Introduction

Proton therapy represents a significant advancement in radiotherapy, offering superior dose distribution compared to conventional photon therapy through the elimination of exit dose beyond the Bragg peak.1 This fundamental physical advantage enables enhanced sparing of healthy tissues and critical organs while maintaining target dose coverage.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Most contemporary proton therapy centers utilize pencil beam scanning delivery, where individual proton pencil beams are sequentially delivered across the target volume under precise magnetic guidance. The beam's lateral position is controlled by scanning magnets, while penetration depth is modulated through energy selection.7

Modern proton therapy systems use discrete spot scanning (DSS), where the beam is turned off during transitions between spot positions, ensuring precise dose delivery. The Hitachi synchrotron has an alternate mode of beam delivery known as dose-driven continuous scanning (DDCS), which maintains beam delivery during spot transitions using a continuous raster scanning pattern.8 This methodology enhances effective dose rate and reduces beam delivery time,9 thereby minimizing intrafraction patient motion10, 11, 12 and improving treatment efficiency.13, 14

The fundamental distinction between DDCS and DSS lies in the presence of move dose—the radiation delivered during beam transit between spots. While this continuous delivery approach offers temporal advantages, it introduces dosimetric complexities that require careful management. To maintain dosimetric equivalence with DSS, break points (BPs) are implemented when predetermined constraints are violated, temporarily interrupting beam delivery during spot transitions (setting move dose to zero). This hybrid approach balances treatment efficiency with dose accuracy requirements.

At each spot following a beam interruption, the beam current exhibits a characteristic temporal profile: an initial rise phase (trise) during which current increases from zero to plateau value (I0), followed by a plateau phase where stable dose delivery occurs.15 In DDCS, dose delivery at each spot position comprises of 2 components: Monitor units (MU) delivered during transit from the previous spot and MU delivered while stationary at the spot. The transit dose calculation is a product of the beam current and the scanning time between consecutive spots.14 Since the beam current exhibits temporal variations characterized by trise and I0, accurate knowledge of these parameters is essential for precise calculation of both transit and stop dose contributions. DDCS beam current calculation typically assumes constant I0 from the machine set values and constant trise. That may not capture the systematic variations occurring throughout a spill cycle, particularly during the rise phase following beam interruptions. The temporal beam current profile directly impacts dose calculation accuracy in DDCS planning. The MU delivered at each position depends on the actual beam current during the scanning motion and the dwell time phases. Systematic variations in I0 or trise that are not properly modeled in the planning system will propagate to errors in predicted MU delivery, potentially compromising dose distribution accuracy.

In this study, we measured the beam current using the CROSSmini detector array at the highest temporal resolution (50 μs). We quantified trise and I0 variations (designated as I0,measured hereafter) across a complete spill cycle for multiple energy levels and nominal beam current settings. We perform a systematic analysis using theoretical calculations and experimental validations to quantify the relative impact of the 2 temporal parameter variations on the average beam current within a spill. This work serves as a basis for the development of improved dose calculation algorithms that incorporate dynamic beam current modeling and enhanced beam current optimization methods in DDCS treatment planning.

Methods

Overview of the proton therapy system

All measurements were performed using a Hitachi proton therapy synchrotron. The proton energies ranged from 70.2 to 228.7 MeV, which corresponds to a 3.90 to 32.40 g/cm2 water penetration depth range. The in-air spot size at the isocenter (1σ) ranges from 2 mm at the highest energy to 5 mm at the lowest energy.16 In this work, MU is measured by the dose monitor ionization chamber located within the treatment nozzle. In DDCS delivery, the MU per spot ranges from 3 to 40 mMU, and the maximum nominal beam current is up to 20 MU/s. The nominal beam current defined per energy layer.

Irradiation conditions and instrumentations

Simple single energy layer plans irradiating at the central axis for 3 different proton energy levels (70.2, 150.2, and 228.7 MeV), 3 different nominal beam current (8, 14, and 20 MU/s), and spot MU of 40 mMU were used for measurements. Maximum spot MU was chosen to ensure high temporal sample of each spot. BPs are inserted at every alternate spot. The plan MU was chosen such that at least 1 complete spill was measured by the detector.

The beam current was measured using a CROSSmini 2D strip ionization chamber detector array (Liverage Biomedical Inc, Taiwan) positioned at the isocenter plane. The detector has a sampling rate of 20 kHz (with a minimum temporal spacing of 50 μs), pitch spacing of 2.0 mm, and 127 channels in both the X and Y directions. The instantaneous beam current at each time point is calculated from the summation of all the signals registered by the X and Y strips. The linearity range and commissioning of the CROSSmini detector have been described in our previous work.17 The dose rate linearity was within 5% from 2 to 20 MU/s up to a tested range of 10 Gy. This linearity quantification has inherently included the errors from channel summation (the linearity is relative to perfect calibration) and the detector noise. The use of CROSSmini for beam current measurements in proton therapy has also been reported by other centers.18

A non-linear least squares fit was performed using the curve_fit function in the Scipy v.1.12.0 toolbox.

The fit parameters map to the physical quantities as follows: y0 corresponds to the baseline signal level when the beam is off, y1 corresponds to the plateau beam current I0,measured, and t1 corresponds to the rise time trise. The parameter t0represents the temporal midpoint of the linear transition region. A 20-spot moving average was applied to characterize the intra-spill trends in trise and I0,measured. This window size was selected to suppress spot-to-spot stochastic variation while preserving the underlying monotonic trends and the steep terminal decline observed near the end of the spill.

Discussion

In this work, we quantified I0,measured and trise of the beam current across a spill using actual measurement with a CROSSmini detector and study the sensitivity of the average beam current of each spot to the variation of the measured I0,measured and trise. We measured the beam current during the build up by implementing break spots at every alternate spot using the CROSSmini detector at the highest temporal resolution of 50 μs for 3 energy level and 3 nominal beam current settings. Using a piecewise function fitting on the signal measured by the detector as shown in Figure 2, Figure 3 (black lines), we quantified the beam current at plateau (I0,measured) and rise time for each spot across the spill. The piecewise function fitting (Equation 4) estimates trise and I0,measured using a non-linear least square fits over multiple data points in each transition region, reducing sensitivity to individual noisy samples. Near the end of the spill, the declining beam current reduces SNR which could potentially increase uncertainty in parameter estimation. This is reflected in the larger shaded regions (1σ) in Figure 4 at the end of the spill and the reported mean and SD values encompass the full estimation uncertainty, including any potential SNR-related effects. We observed similar behavior, where the measured beam current gradually decreased across the spill.16, 19, 20 Additionally, we investigated the impact of the variation of I0,measured and trise on the average beam current Iavg.

The gradual reduction in extracted beam current throughout the spill reflects the progressive depletion of circulating particles in the synchrotron ring during extraction. Although the RF-knockout feedback system modulates the extraction rate to maintain a stable beam current, perfect compensation becomes increasingly difficult as the circulating charge diminishes, resulting in the steep terminal decline observed near the end of the spill.15, 21, 22 This behavior is inherent to synchrotron-based slow extraction, and its magnitude may vary depending on the accelerator design, extraction scheme, and feedback tuning parameters. We note that real-time ionization chamber feedback ensures that the prescribed total MU per spot is delivered accurately regardless of temporal beam current variations. The concern addressed in this work is not an error in cumulative spot MU delivery, but rather the temporal distribution of MU within each spot between the rise phase and plateau phase. Treatment planning systems typically assume an idealized beam current profile with constant I0 and trise when calculating scanning time and move dose contributions between spots. When the actual temporal structure deviates from these assumptions, the spatial distribution of dose may differ from the planned distribution even though the total MU per spot is correctly delivered.

The analytical method calculates trise from dose monitor log files by assuming a constant plateau beam current equal to the nominal value throughout the spill; however, as demonstrated in Figures 3 and 4, the actual plateau beam decreases progressively across the spill. When the analytical method assumes a higher plateau beam current than the actual value, the derived trise is correspondingly overestimated to account for the same recorded Iavg. This effect is most pronounced at 8 MU/s at 70.2 or 228.7 MeV, where the measured trise values (mean 1.43 ± 0.71 ms) were approximately 50% lower than the analytically trise calculated from the dose monitor logfiles (mean 3.5 ± 1.8 ms).19 This discrepancy highlights a limitation of analytical methods that assume constant plateau beam current through the spill. However, a detailed investigation of the dosimetric impact of incorporating dynamic trise and I0,measured modeling into the beam current optimization is beyond the scope of this work and will be addressed in future studies.

The sensitivity analysis revealed that Iavg exhibits significantly higher sensitivity to trise variation compared to I0,measured changes. The non-local sensitivity analysis from Figure 8 showed that the measured trise variations can produce up to 80% changes in Iavg across a spill, while equivalent I0,measured variations result in less than 2% change. This difference may have implication on the DDCS beam current optimization. The inverse relationship between sensitivity and nominal beam current settings indicates that treatment planning optimization should carefully consider beam current selection. Lower beam current settings will require a more dynamic rise time consideration to maintain dose delivery precision, which may influence the fraction of BPs required in clinical plans and consequently affect treatment efficiency.9

This work demonstrated the importance of including intra-spill rise time variation for accurate beam current calculation in DDCS planning, highlighting that temporal variations should be incorporated in DDCS planning optimization. However, spill characteristics vary significantly from spill to spill, as the beam spill profile extracted from a synchrotron usually varies due to small fluctuations in the power supplies for the main-ring bending magnets and extraction devices.23 While accurately modeling temporal structure during plan delivery is challenging, Burguete et al have developed stochastic models to predict the temporal structures of individual spills to address this complexity.24

A limitation of this study is that our analysis was restricted to stationary spots where delivery time exceeded rise time (T>trise). The temporal behavior spot sequences where T<trise requires further investigations, but this is currently limited by the sampling frequency of the detector. Further work should extend measurements to moving beam conditions and investigate the impact of these temporal variations on actual dose distributions.

Conclusion

This is a phenomenological study of the intra-spill break spot temporal characteristics in DDCS proton therapy. We have characterized rise time and plateau beam current reached within a spill. The measured beam current achieves only 75% of nominal beam current settings on average, and the rise time variation during beam delivery exhibits substantially higher impact on average beam current.
