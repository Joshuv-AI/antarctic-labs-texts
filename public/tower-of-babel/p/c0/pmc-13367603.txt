Effective diabetes control helps reduce cognitive decline as higher HbA1c levels were strongly associated with greater impairment severity. Ongoing research is vital to identify high-risk individuals and develop targeted interventions.

Introduction

According to the American Diabetes Association, diabetes mellitus is a collection of metabolic illnesses marked by hyperglycemia brought on by deficiencies in either insulin action or secretion or both. Long-term heart, blood vessel, kidney, eye, and nerve dysfunction has been associated with chronic hyperglycemia; caring of DM is nowadays the biggest headache of the primary care and general physicians.[1] The gradual decline in mental capacity and cognition, specifically in verbal memory, processing speed, and executive functions, is one of the most recent complications of diabetes. In contrast, visuospatial, attention, semantic, and language functions appear to be unaffected, and blood glucose control has been shown to help postpone these effects. The inability to remember, learn new things, focus, or make judgments that impact daily living is known as cognitive impairment. Because it causes functional incapacity and raises healthcare expenditures, it is a serious community concern.[2] Since patients’ concepts regarding the need for self-care, regular follow-up, and adherence to food, exercise, and medications mostly depend on an intact memory, it may negatively impact the management plan or cause substantial harm, particularly in patients with diabetes. Individuals with cognitive impairment may be susceptible to treatment-related problems, such as hypoglycemia, due to either missing meals or taking insulin or oral drugs at the incorrect time or dose.[3] Numerous research studies have connected diabetes mellitus to cognitive impairment. According to more recent research, the prevalence of cognitive impairment is greater in individuals with diabetes mellitus than in the general population and diabetes mellitus is thought to be a risk factor for cognitive impairment.[45] High blood glucose levels have been linked in a number of studies to the development and course of cognitive impairment. Some studies assessing the prevalence of cognitive impairment in the Middle East and Arabic-speaking countries demonstrated that the range of cognitive impairment in this region ranges from 4.4% to 32%.[6] A recent study assessing the prevalence of cognitive impairment among Saudi population aged 60 years and above demonstrated a prevalence of 46%. It is still controversial to determine which domain of cognition is affected the most in patient with diabetes.

As one of the primary causes of living with a disability in people 70 years of age and beyond, type 2 diabetes is known to play a significant role in the development of long-term effects such as frailty, physical impairments, and moderate cognitive impairment (MCI). The precise process by which diabetes mellitus causes cognitive impairment is complicated. According to one study, one risk factor is cerebrovascular illness. According to other research, blood glucose fluctuations, metabolic byproducts, insulin resistance, concurrent hypertension, amyloidosis, depression, and a few other physical and psychological factors may all be important.[7]

Despite the fact that people with diabetes are more likely to experience cognitive impairment, normal clinical care typically does not evaluate their cognitive performance. The prevalence of cognitive impairment in the Saudi population with diabetes is unknown. To optimize quality of life, it is essential to comprehend how diabetes mellitus and cognitive impairment are related. Improving quality of life requires an understanding of the relationship between diabetes mellitus and cognitive impairment. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to determine possible risk factors in the Aseer region and to assess the incidence of cognitive impairment in people with diabetes mellitus.

Methods

Study design

Cross-sectional study design

Study population

As per previous studies conducted in Saudi Arabia, the study size obtained was at least 246 patients, but we recruited more patients to enhance the generalization of the results and make the study more concrete and relevant in terms of sample size. The data collection duration was from August-2023 to December 2023.

Inclusion criteria

Those aged 18 years and above, diagnosed with type 1 or type 2 diabetes for at least 2 years based on laboratory tests (HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose, and 2-h post-prandial plasma glucose), and receiving any type of treatment for diabetes (diet, exercise, oral hypoglycemic medications, and/or insulin) were included.

Exclusion criteria

People who did not give their consent or have any confounding diseases like some other psychiatric issues were excluded from the study.

Ethical considerations

The study adhered to the principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration and received ethical approval from the research ethical committee of the college of Medicine, King Khalid University.

Data collection method

The data were collected by using an electronic questionnaire after the demonstration and explanation of the questionnaire by the trained data collectors.

Section 1: Sociodemographic and diabetes-related variables

The questionnaire covered sociodemographic variables (age, gender, marital status, education, job, and monthly income) and details about patients’ disease, such as duration of diabetes and the presence of diabetes-related complications like numbness and self-reported ophthalmic complications.

Section 2: Cognitive impairment

Cognitive impairment was assessed using the Arabic version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) (version 7.1 original version). The scale covered seven cognitive domains. Scores will range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating better cognitive status. Scores will be adjusted by adding one point if the years of education are ≤12 years. A cutoff of <26 will indicate cognitive impairment, while <20 will signify severe cognitive impairment.

Section 3: Diabetes-related distress (DRD)

The Arabic version of DDS-17[8] was used to measure DRD over the past month.

Statistical analyses

Data analysis was conducted using the SPSS ver 31. software. The mean ± S.D, frequencies, and percentages were obtained. To measure the degree of association between variables, univariate (Chi- square) and multivariate analyses were done at 5% level of significance. Correlation matric was established to measure the correlations between the continuous variables. To highlight the risk factors, multivariate regression analysis was conducted.

Results

The study analyzed a total of 512 participants, all diagnosed with diabetes mellitus, with an average age of 50.7 years and an S.D of 18.9 years. Cognitive assessment revealed that 25.0% had severe cognitive impairment, 33.0% had moderate impairment, and 42.0% showed no signs of cognitive decline. The sample consisted predominantly of males (67.8%), with females comprising 32.2%. Most participants were married (67.2%), followed by singles (20.1%), widows (9.4%), and divorced individuals (3.3%). In terms of income, 36.3% earned between 5000 and 10,000, 33.8% earned up to 5000, 25.0% earned between 10,000 and 20,000, and only 4.9% earned above 20,000. A majority (78.5%) were not employed in the healthcare sector, while 21.5% were. Regarding educational background, 43.2% had university degrees, 35.2% had middle-level education, 16.0% were illiterate, and 5.7% had only primary education. Over half of the participants (52.3%) had no health insurance, 32.0% were covered by government schemes, and 15.6% had private insurance. Smoking habits varied, with 68.2% nonsmokers, 16.8% current smokers, and 15.0% having quit within the past 6 months. Employment status showed 32.0% unemployed, 27.7% in the government sector, 26.4% retired, and 13.9% in the private sector. Most participants (71.7%) lived in urban areas, while 28.3% were from rural villages. Last, 71.5% reported a family history of diabetes, whereas 28.5% did not [Table 1].

Table 2 presented the Pearson correlation analysis among age, HbA1c levels, and cognitive impairment scores. The results indicated a statistically significant but weak negative correlation between age and cognitive impairment scores (r = -0.093, P = 0.035), suggesting that as age increased, cognitive function slightly declined among participants. Although the strength of the relationship was modest, it reached significance at the 0.05 level. The correlation between age and HbA1c was positive but not statistically significant (r = 0.113, P = 0.097), indicating a trend where older participants tended to have higher HbA1c levels, though this association could not be confirmed statistically. A significant positive correlation was found between HbA1c and cognitive impairment scores (r = 0.164, P = 0.015), implying that higher HbA1c levels were associated with worse cognitive performance. This result supported the hypothesis that poor glycemic control might adversely affect cognitive functioning in diabetic individuals. Overall, the analysis highlighted that both aging and poor glucose regulation were linked to cognitive impairment, though age had a slightly inverse relationship, and HbA1c showed a direct one.

The univariate analysis examined the association between various sociodemographic and health-related variables with the outcome of interest using model fitting criteria. The results showed that marital status had a statistically significant effect (Chi-square = 17.351, df = 6, P = 0.008). Similarly, smoking status demonstrated a significant association (Chi-square = 12.338, df = 4, P = 0.015). Place of living also emerged as a strong predictor, with a highly significant association (Chi-square = 20.460, df = 2, P = 0.000), indicating that whether participants lived in urban or rural areas had a notable effect. Additionally, having a family history of diabetes was significantly related to cognitive score (Chi-square = 9.092, df = 2, P = 0.011). Other variables such as gender (P = 0.078), monthly income (P = 0.108), job type (P = 0.057), and working in the healthcare field (P = 0.150) did not reach statistical significance but showed trends that may warrant further investigation in multivariate analyses. Meanwhile, education level (P = 0.790) and health insurance status (P = 0.629) showed no significant impact on the outcome in this univariate model. These findings suggested that certain factors, particularly marital status, smoking, residence, and family history of diabetes, played a more prominent role in influencing the studied outcome [Table 3].

For severe cognitive impairment, the model demonstrated that unmarried respondents had significantly higher odds of impairment compared with divorced respondents (B = 1.396, P = 0.038, Exp(B) = 4.040). This finding indicates that single individuals were approximately four times more likely to experience severe cognitive impairment than those who were divorced, suggesting that single individuals were approximately four times more likely to experience severe cognitive impairment. Although smoking (B = 0.739, P = 0.062) and urban residence (B = 0.453, P = 0.080) were not statistically significant at the 0.05 level, they showed a positive trend toward increased risk. Similarly, having a family history of diabetes approached significance (B = 0.446, P = 0.095), with an odds ratio of 1.562, indicating a possible association.

For moderate cognitive impairment, several variables showed stronger and statistically significant associations. Smoking was a significant predictor, with current smokers being 2.626 times more likely to exhibit moderate impairment than those who had quit (B = 0.966, P = 0.030), and nonsmokers had even higher odds (B = 1.263, P = 0.001, Exp (B) =3.537). Urban residence was a strong and highly significant predictor of moderate cognitive impairment (B = 1.582, P < 0.001, Exp (B) =4.865), indicating that individuals living in cities were nearly five times more likely to have moderate cognitive issues compared to those living in villages. Additionally, having a family history of diabetes was significantly associated with moderate impairment (B = 0.663, P = 0.013, Exp (B) =1.940), nearly doubling the risk. Gender and marital status (married or single) approached statistical significance, suggesting potential roles in moderate impairment, though the associations were not conclusive at the 0.05 level.

Discussion

Understanding the connection between diabetes mellitus and cognitive impairment is essential to improving quality of life. Thus, this study aims to evaluate the prevalence of cognitive impairment in individuals with diabetes mellitus and identify potential risk factors in the Aseer region. Our study suggested that significant negative relationship between age and score of cognitive impairment exists, which was evident in other studies, suggesting that in 2015, there was a new research partnership between Shanghai and the Mayo Clinic. The impact of diabetes on patients’ cognitive function was clearly visible in this study, which included a sizable patient population. Naturally, this was independent of the patients’ age, gender, and other cardiovascular risk factors.[8] In our study, we have observed that significant positive correlation was found between HbA1c and cognitive impairment scores (r = 0.164, P = 0.015), in contrast of the findings of an Iranian-based study which showed the HbA1c levels did not significantly correlate with depression or cognitive difficulties, although they did have an impact on the recent memory and depression levels of diabetes patients. We recommend routine surveys of memory and depression in these patients.[9] The findings of our study showed that marital status had a statistically significant effect (Chi-square = 17.351, df = 6, P = 0.008). Our study’s findings demonstrated that, among older adults, marital status was a major predictor of cognitive impairment, explaining over three-quarters of the variance in cognitive impairment. This result is in line with earlier research.[10] The marriage protection effect emphasizes how marriage can influence a person’s longevity and state of health by encouraging a healthy lifestyle and offering emotional and social support from one’s spouse.[1011] Our study reflected that smoking status demonstrated a significant association (Chi-square = 12.338, df = 4, P = 0.015) with cognitive impairment scores. According to our study, nicotine smokers have cognitive impairment. According to a prior study including British Civil Service participants, middle-aged smokers saw a quicker age-related deterioration in cognitive performance than nonsmokers.[1213]

Our study showed significant relationship between cognitive impairment and a family history of diabetes, which was in line with a Kenyan-based study that it is linked to mental illnesses such bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, and schizophrenia.[1415] Thus, there is a need for coordinated efforts to concentrate on elements that can be changed. In China, there are presently 15.07 million individuals suffering from dementia and 38.77 million with moderate cognitive impairment, and the number of dementia sufferers is continually increasing. Young-onset dementia is also frequently diagnosed in people at a young age. Dementia has a high mortality rate and no known cure,[16] consistent with our study’s findings. The effects of living in a rural area on cognitive health have not been well studied. Living in a rural area is linked to poorer cognitive performance throughout life. Childhood rurality is particularly associated with lower cognitive function.[17] Research suggests that there may be differences in MCI onset, progression, and prognosis with regard to the place of residence, alongside the well-established notion that the prevalence of MCI is higher in rural locations. Different environmental factors may have protective or precipitating effects on cognitive decline, according to place of residence. Future studies should address the impact of rurality on cognitive health by elucidating the mechanisms underlying cognitive decline and by investigating the risk factors that may be associated with the rural context.[16171819]

Conclusion

Diabetes is strongly linked to an increased risk of cognitive impairment. Gender, age, HBA1C, marital status, and place of residence were found to have a significant correlation with the cognitive impairment score in our study. Reducing cognitive impairment in people with diabetes is facilitated by effective diabetes management. Patients with severe cognitive impairment had significantly higher HbA1c levels, and the likelihood of both CI and severe cognitive impairment rose as HbA1c levels rose. To determine who is most at risk for cognitive decline and to customize interventions for them, research in this field is essential.
