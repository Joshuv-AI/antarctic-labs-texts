Reduced corneal toxicity following whole brain irradiation in mice
Authors: Vinoshene Pillai, Silvia Landi, Francesca Uccheddu, Fabio Di Martino, Andrea Cavalieri, Fabiola Paiar, Simone Capaccioli, Riccardo Cicchi, Mario Costa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe emergence of focal techniques such as stereotactic radiosurgery has led to a more selective use of whole brain radiotherapy (WBRT), yet it remains an important modality for treating diffuse or multiple intracranial malignancies. During WBRT, conventional dose rate radiotherapy (CONV-RT) inadvertently exposes ocular structures to radiation, potentially leading to vision-threatening complications. FLASH radiotherapy (FLASH-RT) delivers ultra-high dose rates in milliseconds, compared to CONV-RT where the dose delivery is over several minutes. This study employs 9 MeV electron beams to investigate whether FLASH-RT reduces radiation-induced corneal collagen damage relative to CONV-RT, using second-harmonic generation (SHG) imaging in a murine WBRT model. We hypothesized that FLASH-RT preferentially protects anterior ocular structures from incidental radiation damage compared to CONV-RT, potentially through preservation of extracellular matrix integrity and reduced damage to long-lived structural proteins.Materials and methodsC57BL/6 J wild type mice were divided into three experimental cohorts: untreated controls, FLASH-RT and CONV-RT. Within the radiotherapy cohorts, subgroups received a single whole-brain fraction of 10 Gy, 15 Gy or 20 Gy delivered at an average dose rate of 240 Gy/s for FLASH-RT or 0.1 Gy/s for CONV-RT. Corneal alterations were evaluated at acute (4 days) and chronic (40 days) post-irradiation timepoints. SHG microscopy quantified collagen organization using forward-to-backward (F/B) signal ratios and corneal thickness as markers of radiation-induced damage.ResultsIn the CONV-RT cohorts, we found significantly decreased F/B ratios compared to controls (p < 0.05), indicating substantial collagen disorganization, and increased corneal thickness indicative of radiation-induced tissue edema. Conversely, FLASH-RT was associated with higher F/B ratios and reduced corneal thickening compared with CONV-RT, indicating preservation of corneal extracellular matrix organization across both timepoints. However, the tissue sparing effect on corneal thickness was not sustained at the high single fraction threshold of 20 Gy.ConclusionFLASH-RT preserves corneal collagen architecture compared to CONV-RT during whole brain irradiation, demonstrating significant potential for minimizing ocular toxicity in WBRT. These findings support clinical investigation of FLASH-RT as a tissue-sparing modality for brain cancer treatment.

Introduction

Whole brain radiation therapy (WBRT) is an indispensable treatment for intracranial cancers especially when focal techniques such as radiosurgery are not applicable [1], [2]. It provides palliative benefits by slowing disease progression and increasing survival rates [3]. WBRT effectively targets diffuse leptomeningeal or multiple metastatic lesions when administered in fractioned doses of 30–40 Gy over 10–15 sessions [4], [5], [6]. However, its application inevitably subjects fragile ocular structures to ionizing radiation, posing considerable risks for long-term visual impairment [7]. Within the brain parenchyma, conventional delivery modalities frequently trigger progressive neurocognitive decline, memory performance impairment and structural microvascular damage, which severely compromises patient quality of life. Concurrently, incidental exposure of the anterior segment introduces severe long-term complications. Prominent among these is radiation-induced cataractogenesis resulting from direct lens epithelial cell damage, alongside progressive corneal keratopathy [7], [8].

Ocular complications from WBRT are mainly due to microvascular and direct cytotoxic effects on various ocular tissues [7]. Radiation retinopathy, a delayed form of microvascular occlusion, with microaneurysms, microbleeds, macular edema, and neovascularization, usually appears 6–36 months post-treatment [9]. Higher retinal doses (> 45–50 Gy) are associated with an elevated risk, with bilateral involvement occurring in over half of cases and median onset at 20.5 months around 40 Gy [10]. Anterior segment complications include dry eye syndrome due to lacrimal gland dysfunction (> 10–20 Gy), affecting 24–32% of patients, radiation cataract (lens dose >5 Gy), and optic neuropathy [9], [11], [12]. Overall, ophthalmic complications vary from 3 to 25%, depending on various dose parameters and individual factors. Notably, corneal exposure during WBRT is largely unavoidable due to treatment geometry, and incidental ocular doses can approach or exceed tolerance thresholds for several of these complications, affecting up to 25% of patients [7], [10].

Despite the considerable interest in complications involving the posterior segment and lacrimal system, WBRT's impact on corneal morphology and structure remains poorly understood. The cornea, being the outermost transparent structure responsible for refraction in the eye, is extremely vulnerable to radiation injury because of its highly ordered collagen and limited regenerative capabilities [13]. The corneal stroma, which constitutes about 90% of the thickness of the cornea, contains highly ordered collagen fibrils, primarily types I and V collagen, arranged in a regular lamellar structure [13], [14].

Conventional radiation therapy (CONV-RT) induces several corneal pathologies, including loss of epithelial cells, apoptosis of stromal keratocytes, disruption of collagen, infiltration of inflammatory cells, and neovascularization [15], [16]. In clinical settings, these changes occur as a result of keratopathy, corneal ulcers, and potentially corneal perforation [17]. Typically, the dose threshold within which these pathological changes culminate as clinically observable complications can vary from 40 to 60 Gy [18]. Within the context of WBRT, in which corneal exposure is incidental, understanding the micro-anatomical correlates of radiation-induced corneal injuries is a necessary first step toward identifying strategies that mitigate such damage. While advances in treatment planning and conformal techniques can reduce dose to some ocular structures during partial brain treatments, they cannot fully eliminate incidental corneal exposure during comprehensive whole brain fields due to geometric proximity and peripheral scatter, highlighting the need for a tissue sparing strategy [19], [20]. Preclinical evidence demonstrates that FLASH-RT can successfully spare normal, healthy brain tissues, effectively preserving neurocognitive function and memory structures while maintaining equivalent tumor control compared to CONV-RT [21], [22].

FLASH radiotherapy (FLASH-RT) is broadly characterized by the delivery of radiation at ultra-high dose rates (UHDR), in contrast to conventional dose rate radiotherapy (CONV-RT, 0.01–0.1 Gy/s). While typically defined in the literature by mean dose rates exceeding 40 Gy/s [21], [23], emerging evidence indicates that the FLASH effect is further governed by instantaneous dose rates, pulse structure, and total dose delivered within milliseconds [24], [25], with full tissue sparing effects demonstrated at dose rates exceeding 100 Gy/s in electron-based studies [26], [27]. Preclinical research demonstrates that FLASH-RT significantly reduces normal tissue damage while maintaining equivalent tumor effect, though the underlying mechanisms remain unclear [28], [29], [30]. Proposed theories include reduced production of reactive oxygen species, alterations in lipid peroxidation pathways [31], [32], [33], [34], mitochondrial metabolic responses [35], [36], changes in long-lived proteins and structural modifications to extracellular matrix proteins [37], immune-mediated mechanisms [38], [39], though the relative contribution of each remains under active investigation [31], [32], [38], [39]. While FLASH-RT has shown protective effects on brain, lung, and gastrointestinal, skin, muscle, cardiac, esophageal, and hematopoietic tissues [40], [41], [42], [43], [44], [45], [46], [47], its effects on ocular structures exposed incidentally to WBRT remain unexplored. Since incidental ocular doses during WBRT can approach or exceed tolerance limits for various complications [11], FLASH-RT's potential to spare corneal and other ocular structures while maintaining therapeutic efficacy for brain lesions would be highly valuable.

Second-harmonic generation (SHG) microscopy represents an attractive label-free technique with submicron spatial resolution for visualizing collagen organization within the cornea [48], [49]. This nonlinear optical phenomenon involves two incident photons that are up-converted in a single photon having a frequency twice that of the incident photon by molecules with high hyperpolarizability, such as ordered fibrillary structures [48]. The forward-to-backward (F/B) signal ratio quantifies collagen organization and its fibrillar orientation, with higher ratios indicating more ordered fibrillar arrangements [48], [50]. It represents an objective approach useful for revealing minute changes induced by radiation before the onset of overt disease [51], [52].

This study therefore aims to compare the effects of FLASH-RT and CONV-RT on corneal collagen structure in a murine WBRT model using quantitative SHG microscopy, with the goal of determining whether ultra-high dose rate delivery reduces radiation-induced corneal damage relative to conventional dose rate irradiation. We hypothesized that FLASH-RT would preferentially spare corneal collagen integrity compared to CONV-RT. To systematically evaluate these protective boundaries, single-fraction doses of 10, 15 and 20 Gy were selected to span the transition from sub-acute tissue changes to severe structural thresholds where persistent stromal edema is consistently induced by CONV-RT [16]. Furthermore, the acute timepoint (4 days) was designated to capture immediate, macro-structural corneal thickening resulting from early radiation induced fluid shifts, while chronic timepoint (40 days) was selected to isolate long-term extracellular matrix remodeling and collagen fibril disorganization [15]. To test our hypothesis across these established windows, we assessed: 1) dose- and time-dependent changes in corneal thickness as a primary indicator across all dose cohorts, and 2) deep-tissue microstructural alterations in collagen matrix organization quantified via depth-resolved forward-to-backward (F/B) SHG ratio profiles and polar FFT analysis at the critical intermediate injury threshold.

Materials and methods

The linear accelerator (LINAC)

All the irradiations were performed at the Centro Pisano FLASH Radiotherapy (CPFR), using a dedicated ElectronFlash linear accelerator, which was designed specifically for FLASH irradiations [53]. This linear accelerator is capable of delivering electron beams with energies of 7 or 9 MeV, though the 9 MeV beam configuration was specifically selected to optimize entrance dose parameters and ensure a highly homogeneous dose distribution across the target volume for in vivo applications. The electron beam current can be varied from 1 to 100 mA, pulse duration from 0.5 to 4 μs and pulse repetition frequency (PRF) from 1 to 245 Hz, without altering the beam energy spectrum. This flexibility allows independent modulation of both the average dose rate (ADR) and dose per pulse (DPP), enabling delivery of CONV-RT and FLASH-RT irradiations without modification of the experimental setup, making it ideal for comparative studies. Dosimetric characterization and beam calibration were performed using methodologies previously developed and validated at CPFR [54], [55], [56], [57]. The dose was prescribed at the build-up point of the percentage depth dose (PDD) in water (maximum dose). Before each experiment, the daily machine output was measured at the build-up depth using the same irradiation geometry. Dosimetry measurements were performed using a flashDiamond detector, while beam output fluctuations were monitored using an ACCT-based beam monitoring system (Bergoz Instrumentation).

Animals

C57BL/6J mice (age 8–12 weeks) were obtained from Charles River Laboratories (France) and maintained at the Istituto di Neuroscienze (CNR, Pisa) animal facility in ventilated cages under controlled conditions (12-h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 10% humidity) with food and water provided ad libitum. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with European Communities Council Directive #86/609/EEC and Italian and European institutional regulations for animal experimentation and the ARRIVE guidelines, and were approved by the Italian Ministry of Health (1204/2020-PR, prot. B4BB8.30, 02/10/2020 and addendum prot.B4BB8.30.EXT.14 del 02-07-2024) and the institutional ethical committees. To optimize animal utilization and adhere to the ethical principles of the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction and Refinement), the mice utilized in this study were part of a larger, parallel investigation evaluating the broader functional effects of cranial irradiation on behavior and the visual system. Mice exposed to WBRT were observed daily throughout the post-radiation period. Body weight, hair loss and behavior were monitored to evaluate physical pain. In accordance with humane endpoint criteria, animals exhibiting severe morbidity were humanely euthanized by decapitation following established guidelines [58]. Both male and female C57BL/6 mice were included in the study and were distributed across experimental groups to ensure balanced sex representation. Mice were randomly assigned to three experimental cohorts: untreated controls, FLASH-RT, and CONV-RT. Within the radiotherapy cohorts, subgroups received single whole-brain fraction of 10, 15 or 20 Gy. Corneas were harvested at two post-irradiation timepoints: acute (Day 4) and chronic (Day 40). Sample sizes per group were as follows: Control: n = 6; Acute FLASH-RT and CONV-RT: n = 4 (10 Gy), n = 6 (15 Gy), n = 5 (20 Gy); Chronic FLASH-RT: n = 4 (10 Gy), n = 6 (15 Gy), n = 2 (20 Gy); Chronic CONV-RT: n = 4 (10 Gy), n = 6 (15 Gy), n = 3 (20 Gy). A 100% survival rate was achieved for all animals in the 10 and 15 Gy cohorts across both the acute and chronic study windows. Conversely, the reduced sample sizes in the chronic 20 Gy groups reflect ethical attrition, where select animals exhibiting systemic radiation-induced morbidity were humanely euthanized prior to Day 40 to prevent unnecessary suffering, in strict accordance with our approved institutional protocols.

Corneal samples

Mice were deeply anesthetized with 10 mL/kg mixture of Zoletil (50 mg/kg) and Xilazine (10 mg/kg), their eyes enucleated and were humanely sacrificed via decapitation. Extracted eyes were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA)/ phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (4% PFA) for 30 min, corneas were removed and extracted corneas were fixed again in 4% PFA for 30 min. After second fixation, corneas were kept in PBS at 4 °C.

SHG microscopy of cornea

Whole intact corneas were mounted in wells formed by silicone gel on glass slides and filled with PBS. A coverslip was then carefully applied to sandwich the assembly, forming a thin imaging chamber without compressing the sample. Corneal buttons were imaged using a custom-made non-linear microscope with a 20× (0.95 numerical aperture) water-immersion objective (Olympus Corp., Tokyo, Japan). Second harmonic generation (SHG) signals were generated using an optical parametric oscillator, pumped by a frequency-doubled Yb-fiber laser Chameleon Discovery (Coherent, Santa Clara, CA, US), emitting 140 fs pulses at 840 nm. The SHG forward-scattered signals passing through the corneal buttons were collected using a 1.0 NA water-immersion condenser lens, filtered with a narrow band-pass filter (410 nm to 430 nm), and detected on a photomultiplier tube H-7422 (Hamamatsu Photonics, Hamamatsu City, Japan). Backward-scattered SHG signals were filtered with a band-pass filter (420 nm to 430 nm) and detected on a second H-7422 photomultiplier tube. Optical sectioning of the samples was performed from the posterior endothelial surface to the anterior epithelium, using a 2-μm z-axis step size.

Cornea thickness measurements

Corneal thickness was determined from the z-stack dimensions spanning from the epithelial surface to Descemet's membrane. Multiple measurements were taken across different corneal regions (central, paracentral, and peripheral) and averaged to obtain representative thickness values for each sample. Thickness measurements were performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA) with the “orthogonal views” function.

Forward-to-backward (F/B) SHG ratio analysis

F/B SHG ratios were calculated on a pixel-by-pixel basis throughout the corneal depth. Forward and backward SHG image stacks were first background-corrected and intensity-normalized. For each depth plane, the mean forward intensity (F) and mean backward intensity (B) were calculated, and the F/B ratio was computed. Depth-resolved F/B ratio profiles were generated by plotting F/B values as a function of distance from the epithelial surface. Higher F/B ratios indicate more organized collagen fibrils, while lower ratios suggest collagen disorganization or altered fibril spacing. The F/B ratio images were then processed identically to the individual F and B stacks for feature extraction, enabling depth-resolved analysis of the F/B ratio distribution.

FFT-based analysis of SHG images

SHG images were analyzed using a 2D Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)-based approach to quantify the loss of regular lamellar arrangement at specific corneal depths. The 2D FFT magnitude spectrum was computed for each image and log-transformed to enhance visualization of lower frequency components. A band-pass circular mask was applied to isolate the spatial frequencies corresponding to the lamellar periodicity while excluding DC components and high-frequency noise. The polar FFT profile was then derived by computing the mean intensity within thin elliptical sectors spanning azimuthal angles from 0° to 180° in 3° increments, yielding 60 angular bins. The coefficient of variation (CV = σ/μ) of this polar profile was used as an indicator of lamellar organization: lower CV values indicate a more isotropic FFT spectrum characteristic of organized collagen, consistent with preserved lamellar architecture, whereas higher CV values reflect a more anisotropic pattern.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using OriginPro. Corneal thickness was analyzed using two-way ANOVA with treatment modality and radiation dose as independent variables. For pooled F/B SHG ratio and FFT analyses, comparisons were performed between treatment group and timepoints. Given the small sample sizes in some groups, particularly the chronic 20 Gy cohorts, limited the reliability of normality assessment, results were additionally verified using the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn's post-hoc correction, and findings were consistent across both approaches. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Conversely, the chronic timepoint (40 days) was chosen to track the persistence of these alterations, capturing progressive changes in corneal thickness alongside permanent structural matrix remodeling. Corneal thickness changes following WBRT

Corneal edema is a potential complication of WBRT occurring due to damage or inflammation caused to the corneal epithelium barrier and endothelium (Fig. 1B). This side effect often leads to decreased visual acuity, photophobia or foreign body sensation. Therefore, to evaluate radiation-induced structural alterations in the cornea, we first quantified corneal thickness across all experimental groups (Fig. 1C). Control corneas exhibited a mean thickness of 36.5 ± 0.6 μm, consistent with normal C57BL/6 J mouse corneal morphology. CONV-RT induced dose-dependent corneal thickening at both acute and chronic timepoints. Acute CONV-RT at 20Gy, 15Gy, and 10Gy resulted in 9.3%, 7.7%, and 4.4% increases in thickness, respectively, which progressed to 21.6%, 10.7%, and 9.9% at the chronic timepoint, indicating dose-dependent radiation-induced structural changes consistent with stromal thickening and potential barrier disruption [15], [16]. FLASH-RT demonstrated relative tissue-sparing effects across radiation doses. At 20Gy, acute FLASH-RT produced comparable thickening to CONV-RT (8.5% vs 9.3%), with modest reduction at the chronic timepoint (18.1% vs 21.6%). However, at 15Gy and 10Gy, FLASH-RT showed markedly attenuated corneal thickening. Acute FLASH-RT at 15 Gy resulted in minimal increases of 1.0%, while at 10 Gy thickness was comparable to control (−0.9%), compared to increases of 7.7% and 4.4% with CONV-RT, respectively. At the chronic timepoint, 15Gy and 10Gy FLASH-RT produced 3.8% and 5.8% thickening, substantially lower than the 10.7% and 9.9% observed with CONV-RT. These findings indicate that FLASH-RT attenuates radiation-induced corneal thickening at moderate doses of 10 and 15 Gy compared to CONV-RT, suggesting a tissue-sparing effect on corneal structural integrity. Whether this reflects reduced edema, attenuated inflammation, or both remains to be confirmed through direct quantification of inflammatory markers in future studies.

SHG imaging reveals differential collagen organization

SHG enables a label-free visualization of collagen organization, revealing corneal architecture in its endogenous state and detecting disease or treatment induced changes. We used F/B analysis of SHG images to compare collagen structural alterations following FLASH-RT and CONV-RT. Representative F/B SHG images reveal distinct differences in corneal collagen architecture between treatment groups at 15Gy (Fig. 2). Control corneas exhibited highly organized collagen fibers with isotropic orientation distribution and clear regular lamellar arrangement (Fig. 2A). CONV-RT treated corneas showed substantial architectural disruption with less homogeneity in fiber distribution and greater anisotropy in orientation. This can be visually observed by fibers clustering, giving the sample a ‘striated’ appearance. Such morphology occurred across 3 irradiation doses and persisted in both acute and chronic exposure groups and is more evident in the mid-range depths (Fig. 2B). In contrast, FLASH-RT treated corneas displayed an intermediate phenotype, with relatively preserved collagen organization. FLASH-RT preserves depth-resolved corneal collagen organization

Depth-resolved F/B scattering ratio analysis of SHG images revealed modality-dependent alterations in corneal collagen organization (Fig. 3A and B). Control corneas exhibited a characteristic depth-dependent F/B profile with local minimum values in the mid-stroma, reflecting the native lamellar alignment of control corneas with well-organized, densely packed collagen fibers. CONV-RT was associated with lower F/B ratios across all stromal depths, accompanied by loss of the mid-stromal peak at both acute and chronic timepoints, indicating sustained collagen disorganization, radiation-induced collagen network disruption and reduced fiber alignment. In contrast, FLASH-RT preserved higher F/B ratios and maintained depth-dependent profiles comparable to controls at both timepoints, with values that fall between control and CONV-RT, suggesting attenuated structural damage. These F/B ratio data represent pooled metrics across all three radiation doses (10, 15 and 20 Gy) for each modality and timepoint. This pooled analysis was performed to assess overall modality-dependent effects on corneal collagen organization rather than dose-dependent differences. The pooled data are summarized in a histogram (Fig. 3C), where control, CONV-RT, and FLASH-RT are represented as violin plots for both chronic and acute timepoints. These data suggest that FLASH-RT is more effective than CONV-RT in preserving the three-dimensional corneal collagen architecture. Both irradiation modalities reduce F/B ratios compared to control, with CONV-RT demonstrating the most pronounced reduction. The results obtained with the F/B SHG analysis are further supported by the FFT-based analysis carried out on SHG images. In fact, the CV of the polar FFT profile, taken as indicator of the loss of lamellar organization, shows lower values in control than in irradiated specimens (Fig. 3D). Similar to the F/B metrics, these polar FFT-based CV analyses reflect pooled data across the 10, 15 and 20 Gy cohorts for each respective treatment group and were used to evaluate overall treatment-related changes in lamellar organization independent of dose stratification. More in detail, CONV-RT was associated with higher CV values at both chronic and acute timepoints, consistent with greater loss of lamellar organization. On the other hand, FLASH-RT corresponds to lower values that are closer to control, indicating a milder loss of lamellar organization at both chronic and acute timepoints. It's worth noting that for the irradiated specimens, the loss of lamellar organization is more severe at the chronic than at the acute timepoint.

Discussion

This study presents the first quantitative evaluation of the structural integrity of corneal collagen after WBRT administered using FLASH-RT versus CONV-RT modalities. We show that FLASH-RT attenuates corneal thickening and preserves corneal extracellular matrix architecture across clinically relevant dose ranges and temporal windows using high-resolution SHG microscopy. These findings extend our understanding of FLASH-RT's tissue-sparing mechanisms to the anterior ocular segment and have direct implications for reducing vision-threatening complications in patients undergoing WBRT.

The precise molecular arrangement of stromal collagen, with type I and type V fibrils regularly arranged in lamellae separated by uniform interfibrillar spacing, is responsible for the cornea's remarkable transparency. Following CONV-RT exposure, this architecture is partially disrupted by several processes including direct cytotoxic damage to stromal keratocytes, inflammatory cell infiltration, altered proteoglycan composition and subsequent collagen remodeling [15], [16], as measured using SHG F/B scattering ratio and FFT-based analysis. Our finding that CONV-RT substantially decreases the SHG F/B ratio suggests a widespread disorganization of this ultrastructure, while the concurrent thickening of the cornea is indicative of radiation-induced edema that is consistent with inflammatory reactions and barrier dysfunction.

When synthesized across the experimental matrix (10, 15 and 20 Gy at 4 and 40 days), our quantitative metrics reveal clear, coordinated dose- and time-dependent tissue kinetics. Following CONV-RT, the injury path is characterized by a progressive, escalating trajectory. Acutely (Day 4), CONV-RT drives initial structural swelling and localized matrix changes that scale directly with escalating radiation thresholds. Chronically (Day 40), rather than demonstrating tissue resolution, these alterations deepen into permanent collagen fibril disorganization and significant alterations in the F/B SHG ratio, peaking severely at higher doses. In contrast, FLASH-RT fundamentally alters these dose- and time-dependent pathways by isolating and compressing the initial tissue reaction, maintaining both macrostructural corneal dimensions and microstructural matrix organization near baseline thresholds across temporal windows.

Under FLASH-RT conditions, corneal architecture retains its integrity, which is consistent with new physicochemical and biological models of the FLASH effect [28], [38]. When radiation delivery is temporally compressed into millisecond timelines, achieving extreme instantaneous intra-pulse dose rates, the local spatial density of initial water radiolysis products increases dramatically. As detailed in comprehensive radiochemical and in silico kinetic modeling studies, this tight spatial clustering forces radical-radical recombination reactions to occur far more efficiently than under conventional delivery timescales [38], [59]. This rapid, localized mutual annihilation of primary free radicals (such as hydroxyl radicals and hydrated electrons) effectively suppresses the indirect action of radiation, drastically reducing the cumulative oxidative damage inflicted upon adjacent resident stromal keratocytes and long-lived extracellular matrix proteins. Concurrently, this rapid free-radical interaction drives transient radiolytic oxygen depletion at a rate that completely outpaces local microvascular reoxygenation, generating a temporary, radioprotective hypoxic state within healthy tissues [60], [61]. Emerging evidence points to a broader set of biological processes including reduced lipid peroxidation in normal tissues [33], [34], preservation of mitochondrial metabolic integrity [35], [36], and protection of long-lived structural proteins within the extracellular matrix [37], though the relative contribution of each mechanism to corneal protection specifically remains to be established. The attenuated corneal thickening seen in FLASH-RT groups compared to CONV-RT may suggest a reduced inflammatory response, though this remains a hypothesis in the absence of direct inflammatory marker quantification and warrants investigation in future studies. Our WBRT model delivered 10, 15, and 20Gy to the brain, resulting in incidental corneal exposure that mirrors clinical scenarios where ocular structures receive radiation during whole brain treatment. Particularly noteworthy is the consistency of FLASH-RT's protective effects at both acute (Day 4) and chronic (Day 40) timepoints at 10 and 15 Gy. While CONV-RT caused progressive deterioration with ongoing collagen disarray and edema, FLASH-RT attenuated initial damage and maintained protection throughout the observation period at these doses. This temporal pattern implies that FLASH-RT either improves endogenous repair mechanisms or stops damage cascades from being initiated.

Notably, at 20 Gy, FLASH-RT did not demonstrate a significant protective effect compared to CONV-RT in corneal thickness, in contrast to the clear tissue-sparing observed at 10 and 15 Gy. This finding is clinically and biologically important and should not be minimized. It suggests that the FLASH sparing effect on corneal tissue may be dose-dependent, with a potential threshold above which ultra-high dose rate delivery is insufficient to prevent radiation-induced structural damage. This is consistent with emerging observations in other tissue systems where FLASH-RT tissue sparing diminishes at very high single-fraction doses [62]. The reduced sample sizes in the 20 Gy chronic cohorts due to ethical attrition further limit the statistical power at this dose level, and conclusions regarding 20 Gy groups should therefore be interpreted with caution. Future studies with larger cohorts at high dose levels are needed to clarify whether this represents a true biological threshold.

However, there are several limitations to be considered. Despite having structural similarities to human tissue, the murine cornea differs in thickness and regenerative ability, which could affect radiation response. In addition, more precise or automated analytical approaches could further improve the accuracy of corneal thickness measurements. While the single-exposure irradiation model used here does not replicate clinically relevant fractionated delivery, it provides valuable mechanistic insight into acute and chronic tissue responses. Furthermore, although SHG microscopy reveals collagen organization with high resolution, complementary evaluations of cellular viability, inflammatory markers and functional outcomes could provide a more comprehensive understanding.

The potential clinical implications extend beyond corneal preservation. However, it must be emphasized that tumor control and global neurocognitive toxicity remain the primary metrics of success for WBRT, with anterior ocular toxicity representing a secondary, albeit significant, quality of life consideration. In modern clinical practice, conventional dose rate fields are frequently optimized using highly conformal delivery techniques like Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy (VMAT) [4], [63], which can geometrically spare the eyes during WBRT. Integrating such complex, modulated arcs with the millisecond delivery requirements of FLASH-RT is technically difficult and remains an open challenge. Nevertheless, if FLASH-RT can successfully minimize damage to non-target ocular structures while maintaining therapeutic efficacy, it could provide an important step forward in how WBRT is configured. Current treatment approaches are associated with ophthalmological complications in roughly 3–25% of patients, and FLASH-RT may reduce these risks, improve quality of life and allow for higher radiation doses that are currently limited by damage to healthy tissue. Future studies should explore FLASH-RT's effects on other ocular structures (lens, retina, optic nerve), optimize dose rates, compare fractionated versus single-dose protocols, and correlate structural changes with functional outcomes of corneal sensitivity, tear film stability, and barrier integrity.

Conclusion

This study shows that FLASH-RT better preserves corneal collagen structure and attenuates radiation-induced corneal thickening compared with CONV-RT following WBRT. These results provide quantitative support for FLASH-RT as a tissue-sparing approach that could lower the risk of vision-threatening complications in patients undergoing WBRT. The protective effects observed within lower/intermediate dose range and time points, together with preservation of three-dimensional collagen organization revealed by depth-resolved SHG analysis, support the potential for clinical translation. Further studies examining fractionated delivery, additional ocular structures, and functional outcomes will be essential to refine FLASH-RT implementation and maximize its ability to reduce ocular toxicity while maintaining intracranial tumor control.
