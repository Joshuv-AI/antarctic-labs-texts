Physicians’ perspectives on the perceived increase in respiratory infections post-COVID-19 in Pakistan: a qualitative study
Authors: Muhammad Usman Khan, Matti Ullah, Muhammad Ebad Khan, Mahnoor Ali, Uzma Nawaz, Maryam Nasir, Mahnoor Fatima, Yasir Yaqoob, Alishba Bibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic has significantly affected global respiratory health, with a notable rise in severe and recurrent respiratory infections. Irrational antibiotic use and limited public health measures during the pandemic were perceived by Physicians to have worsened post-COVID respiratory morbidity and to have contributed to the emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR).ObjectiveTo explore physicians’ experiences and perspectives on the rise in post-COVID respiratory infections in Pakistan, with a focus on contributing factors, treatment challenges, and the impact of irrational antibiotic use and antimicrobial resistance on respiratory disease management.MethodA qualitative explorative study was conducted from September to December 2025 with 20 physicians in public and private healthcare settings in Islamabad and Rawalpindi. In-depth interviews using a semi-structured guide explored post-COVID respiratory trends, contributing factors, treatment challenges, and AMR. Data were transcribed, translated, and analyzed through inductive thematic analysis until saturation, following the COREQ reporting guidelines.ResultPhysicians reported increased frequency and severity of upper and lower respiratory tract infections including asthma, COPD, and pneumonia, physicians characterized them as often severe, recurrent, and prolonged. Key contributing factors cited by physicians included perceived post-viral immune compromise, comorbidities, environmental factors, behavioral changes, and reduced adherence to preventive measures. Challenges such as irrational antibiotic prescribing, over-the-counter use, and self-medication were cited by physicians as drivers of AMR. Respondents emphasized the need for strengthened diagnostics, antimicrobial stewardship, vaccination, public awareness, and systemic interventions.ConclusionThe increase and severity of post-COVID respiratory infections in Pakistan is due to a combination of factors including lack of immune compromise, socio-environmental and behavioral factors, and irrational antibiotics prescriptions fueling the rising antimicrobial resistance (AMR) perceived by Physicians. It is crucial to integrate public health measures, vaccination, and antimicrobial stewardship to strengthen diagnostics and mitigate the long-term respiratory morbidity and potential future crises of infectious disease.Clinical trail registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13677-w.

Introduction

Coronavirus/COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2), a respiratory disease that was emerged in China during early October to mid- November and the first outbreak was noticed when it got reported from Wuhan, China in December 2019, which later caused a significant damage to the overall human health [1–4]. SARS-CoV-2 is an enveloped, positive-sense, single stranded RNA virus that contagiously infects humans and variety of animals [5]. Many patients exposed to SARS-CoV-2 encountered mild symptoms of illness, however up to 5% experienced viral manifestations and multiorgan damage which occurs in severe cases or in the patients with comorbidities like diabetes, obesity, hypertension and others can lead to systemic inflammation, acute respiratory distress syndrome, tissue damage, and cardiac damage and eventually death [6–8]. According to WHO, as of December 2025, there have been more than 778 million confirmed cases with 7.1 million mortalities throughout the world [9]. In Pakistan, the burden of drug-resistant infections, including XDR typhoid and drug-resistant tuberculosis, has been well documented. Weak respiratory disease surveillance and delayed outbreak responses have historically worsened infectious disease crises [10, 11].

Pathophysiology of this virus had a fundamental mechanism of primarily altered lung perfusion [12]. An early clinical sign of COVID-19 was Pneumonia, alongside symptoms such as fever, dry cough, and difficulty breathing, which affected the lower part of the respiratory system. Research indicates that although severe respiratory symptoms are less common in young populations, cases were observed across all age groups-including young children, adolescents, and senior citizens- contributing to increased respiratory infection burden in the post-COVID period [13–15]. An overall rise was observed in the respiratory illnesses after COVID-19 epidemic in 2021. In addition, the rates of influenza A and B viruses increased dramatically post COVID-19 e.g. in Spain, influenza A exhibited a significant elevation from 4.5 to 10.8% i.e. more than two folds shoot up in influenza A detection rates [16]. A study, conducted to discover the trends of seven respiratory diseases post COVID-19, concluded that the respiratory illnesses during the COVID-19 pandemic dopped between 2020 and 2021, however, at the end of 2021 there was a rise in the respiratory disorders as compared to 2020. Notable increase in seasonal influenza was highlighted in 2021 from 7.34 to 65.71% in the People’s Republic of China [17]. Research conducted after 8 months of COVID-19 proved that 67.5% of the tested population manifested at least one respiratory pathogen, with viral infections being more common than bacterial infections. Co-infections were significant in pediatrics and elderly populations which kept on aggravating symptoms [18]. Post-COVID studies actually proved positive perception of respiratory pathogens rose up to 56.9% in children, the most dominant pathogens diagnosed were Influenza A virus at 52.1%, with Mycoplasma pneumoniae at 21.2% [19].

However, while quantitative trends have been described, there is a lack of qualitative evidence exploring how frontline physicians in Pakistan perceive the drivers of post-COVID respiratory surges, the role of irrational antibiotic use, and the practical treatment challenges they face. This study fills that gap by capturing experiential insights from clinicians directly managing respiratory infections in a low-middle-income country setting. This study gathers practical knowledge from the clinicians in Pakistan, who have been directly involved in treating patients who had an exposure to SARS-CoV-2 during and after the pandemic on respiratory illnesses in post-COVID-19. Their valuable insights will open details about disease changing patterns, scrutinizing shift in case numbers, altered disease severity and upgrading respiratory disease surveillance systems. It aims to understand the factors that influence the alleviation of respiratory cases and grow the qualitative evidence to permeate the health policies and to prepare Pakistan for any other pandemics in the future as a united nation.

Methodology

Study design and setting

A qualitative exploratory study was conducted using semi-structured, in-depth interviews to capture physicians’ experiences and perspectives regarding the rise in respiratory infections in the post‑COVID‑19 period in Pakistan. The interviews took place across September-December 2025, in major tertiary care hospitals in Islamabad and Rawalpindi. Since these hospitals act as national referral centers, physicians can provide insights based on a wide variety of patient populations. Similar tertiary-care–based investigations have previously been used to assess system-level practices related to antibiotic use and stewardship in Pakistan [20]. Nevertheless, the hospitals are in urban northern Pakistan, which may limit the applicability of the findings to rural and more remote healthcare settings. Reporting for this study follows the established Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research COREQ checklist.

Participants

A qualitative explorative study approach was used to conduct interviews. Purposeful sampling was used to identify the relevant physicians. The physicians needed to have direct and comparative experience of the management of respiratory infections and the relative challenges before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. These specialties were included because they manage patients with respiratory comorbidities (e.g., pregnant women with respiratory infections, surgical patients with post-COVID lung complications, and histopathological assessment of lung tissue from suspected COVID-19 cases), providing complementary perspectives on respiratory infection burden. This was essential to ensure that the participants would give informed and accounts of the various changes that occurred clinically, in the manifestations of the infection, in the challenges, and in the patterns over time.

Interview guide development and data collection

The interview guide was developed after the critical study of the qualitative literature covering the Covid and post-Covid era particularly Shahil Feroz et al. [21]. Our interview guide comprised of 4 main questions, addressing the POST-COVID respiratory impact factor contributing to raised respiratory infections, suggestion or strategies to improve respiratory illnesses, COVID-Driven AMR. The Interview guide is provided in the form of Supplementary file-S1. To ensure the validity of our content the interview guide was evaluated by trained and professional Academics, with proven record of publication of qualitative studies, who provided insight to optimize its effectiveness for data collection [22]. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached, until no new themes were identified and participant responses became repetitive across several key domains [23]. This was the case after 20 interviews, beyond which additional interviews were unlikely to provide new insights. The three interviewers (MUK, MEK, MA) who interviewed participants in Urdu or English, hold pharmacy degrees without clinical practice experience. Their background in antimicrobial stewardship may have predisposed them to expect AMR-related findings. To mitigate bias, they adhered strictly to the semi-structured guide, avoided leading questions, and engaged in independent coding followed by consensus meetings. The corresponding author (MEK) audited the thematic analysis to ensure findings remained grounded in participant responses. They have received training and had interviewing experience. Their expertise ensured the interviews were conducted in a professional manner, with the necessary sensitivity and in line with the study’s objectives. The average duration of interviews is 10 to 15 min. Although interviews were brief (10–15 min), the semi-structured yet flexible guide, combined with participants’ high clinical expertise, allowed focused and data-saturated responses directly aligned with study objectives. Experienced clinicians could articulate observations concisely, and saturation was confirmed after 20 interviews when no new themes emerged. All interviews were audio-recorded with the interviewees’ verbal consent, and some brief observational notes were taken.

Data analysis

Audio records were transcribed and translated into English by bilingual staff. To maintain meaning and context, a few transcripts were translated back into Urdu. An inductive thematic analysis approach was used. Two researchers (MUK, MEK) independently coded transcripts. Disagreements were resolved through consensus meetings; if no consensus was reached, a third author (MU) made the final decision. Intercoder agreement was assessed qualitatively through discussion, and an audit trail of coding decisions was maintained. Transcription was performed verbatim by bilingual staff, with back-translation of a subset to ensure meaning fidelity, following practices outlined in this article [24]. Codes were clustered into subthemes iteratively, with two researchers (MUK, MEK) independently grouping codes. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third author (MU). Subthemes were then reviewed against raw data to ensure fit. A final coding tree was approved by all authors. Thematic coding tree is provided as supplementary file-S3. To enhance analytical rigor, representative quotations were extracted to illustrate each theme.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethical Review Committee of Hamdard University (Reference: FOP-ERC-09-11, dated 20th September 2025). Prior to the interviews, all participants (were informed of the study’s purpose. All participants gave their verbal consent which was recorded. For confidentiality, physicians were coded (P.01–P.20) and participants were free not to take part.

Post-COVID health and respiratory infection trends

Weakened immunity and increased susceptibility

Most participants reported that COVID-19 contributed to a perceived decline in one’s immunity, particularly in the respiratory system, which in their view makes people more vulnerable to infections. This perceived post–COVID immune compromise was related to a greater number of secondary respiratory infections, both viral and bacterial-according to their clinical observations. These infections were more severe than in the past, indicating a more serious immune system deficiency among the population.

Their immunity appears to be significantly compromised, making them vulnerable to immune dysfunction in the post-COVID period, which has resulted in an increased incidence of respiratory illnesses. (P.06)

Contributing factors behind the rise in respiratory illnesses

Immunity and comorbidities

The majority of participants stated that after COVID, the factors that increased the severity of respiratory infections in their judgment were perceived as an immune compromise, and post COVID comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, asthma, COPD), older or younger age, and smoking. Environmental and social factors

Most participants believed that social and environmental trends indicate that respiratory infections have increased due to the increase in low quality, polluted and smoggy air, as well as the increase in traffic and industrial emissions, the resumption of normal post COVID-19 activities, and poor ventilation. Furthermore, the individuals described the dangerous and unstable climate as a negative factor especially where nutrition was poor, where there was a lack of foods containing rare antioxidants as well as an increase in the likelihood of becoming ill with a respiratory infection.Harsh and unpredictable weather, including unexpected rainfall, can influence the incidence and severity of these infections. Dietary habits also affect immune function, and a lack of antioxidant-rich fruits, combined with the consumption of junk foods, may increase susceptibility to respiratory infections. Additionally, poor air quality contributes to a higher incidence of pneumonia, asthma, and lung damage. (P.08)

Behavioral factors and reduced precautions

The increase in post-COVID respiratory infections can be linked to a rapid decline in mask use, social distancing, and hand hygiene. In addition, a shift to poor eating habits, a sedentary lifestyle, and inactivity reduces a person’s defense mechanisms, in addition to enhancing lung capacity. Therefore, it becomes a functional and pathophysiological risk. Knowing the danger, a person’s behavior defies health norms and increases the risk of danger and transmission in public settings.The rise in respiratory infections in the post-COVID period is mainly due to increased social gatherings and reduced use of preventive measures such as masks, which increases the chances of transmission. Recommendations, treatment challenges and system needs

Diagnostic and stewardship improvements

Improving post-COVID respiratory infection management in Pakistan is directly tied to evolving diagnostics. Being able to access X-ray, HRCT, PCR, CRP and microbial culture tests more readily allow differentiated diagnoses of viral from bacterial infections, which in turn mitigate unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions and reduce antimicrobial resistance. Rapid, accurate diagnostic capabilities and well-resourced public healthcare systems are key to making informed treatment decisions to improve patient outcomesMajor treatment challenges include over-diagnosis or false diagnosis, leading to inappropriate treatment with false antimicrobial use and contributing to antimicrobial resistance. Strengthening diagnostic accuracy to clearly differentiate viral from bacterial infections is essential. Education, training, and systemic regulation

Respondents expressed the necessity for systemic improvements through education, training, and regulation for the effective handling of post COVID related respiratory infections. There was an emphasis of the need on educating the clinicians on the rational use of antibiotics, executing the antimicrobial stewardship programs, and training the general practitioners to minimize the inappropriate prescriptions. COVID-19 and AMR

Patterns of resistance and irrational use

Respondents confirmed the unnecessary and problematic adoption of antibiotics around the COVID-19 pandemic. Widely prescribed antibiotics like azithromycin, erythromycin and penicillin’s were seen by physicians as leading to more complicated infections and antibiotic resistance. Discussion

Our finding that physicians perceived increased susceptibility to respiratory infections post-COVID aligns with published evidence of post-viral immune dysregulation [25]. However, our study adds that clinicians in Pakistan specifically attribute this to a combination of immune compromise, comorbidities, and healthcare access issues – factors that may be amplified in low-resource settings. Chronic respiratory disease patients are thought to be more susceptible to reinfections because of post-viral immune dysregulation as perceived by clinicians. When considering the widespread systemic shortfalls in healthcare and increasing antimicrobial resistance, it seems to be the cause of the growing number of post-COVID respiratory infections, as noted by frontline doctors.

The majority of physicians involved in our study agreed that post-COVID immune compromise has significantly increased susceptibility to respiratory infections, particularly affecting the respiratory system. Participants described more severe and recurrent infections, prolonged recovery, and increased emergency admissions, notably among elderly patients and children. It was also observed that there was a rise in URTIs, LRTIs, and chronic conditions such as asthma, COPD, and pneumonia following the COVID outbreak. Long-term complications, including lung fibrosis, reduced lung compliance, and persistent dyspnea, suggest lasting pulmonary damage and a substantial post-COVID respiratory burden. This finding corresponds with our physicians reported understanding of immune system functioning, with some suggesting a a state of immune activation due to the persistence of interferon and low if it is enough of naïve T- and B- cells, which may lead to a higher chance of secondary infections and case system intended quoted by medical experts within the study [26]. Although COVID-19 cases can tend to be mild, there are certain individuals who are more likely to get severely ill due to the disease, these individuals include older adults, those who are unvaccinated, and those who have health conditions that are comorbidity [27].

In our study, doctors stated that after COVID respiratory infections were caused by immune system dysregulation, associated illnesses, and deficiencies in the health care system. They also noted that the poor quality of air, the degradation of the environment, and the decrease of physical activity and the adoption of risky behavior were principal factors that caused people to be more susceptible to infections and to transmit them more readily. These factors emerged inductively as primary themes from the interview data, not as author-inferred categories. In relevance to our study, it is reported that pulmonary fibrosis can be a serious long-term complication for a significant number of survivors of SARS and MERS. New data implies that the risk may be the same after SARS-CoV-2 infection as pulmonary fibrosis results in respiratory problems and decreased quality of life [28]. This is consistent with established epidemiological evidence showing a strong correlation between air pollution and the increased incidence of viral respiratory infections, including SARS, influenza, and RSV [29]. Recent studies show that children who are recovering from COVID-19 are experiencing additional symptoms like long COVID syndrome, including problems with their lungs, brain, and fatigue, while some may develop new chronic inflammatory conditions, thus decreasing their long-term health prospects [30]. While NPI’s certainly reduced the circulation of global respiratory viruses during the pandemic, the notable upturn of these infections after measures were relaxed is in concordance with our findings of elevated rates in the post-COVID period, although the complicated epidemiology indicates more factors involved [31]. This paragraph discusses immunity debt, viral rebound, increased testing, and notes study limitations in distinguishing these factors. It also clarifies that our study design cannot distinguish between these possibilities and recommends future research.

The findings of this study underline the importance of improving the ability to distinguish between viral and bacterial infections. Participants highlighted the importance of reinforcing antimicrobial stewardship programs to address the issue of unnecessary antibiotic prescription and the increasing rate of AMR. They also supported increased infection control and specific vaccinations to address respiratory complications after COVID. Similar study that implies that future diagnostic strategies focused on specific pathogens and available control measures may be more effective than prioritizing mass testing over infection containment [32]. Hospital epidemiologists and infectious disease specialists consider multiplex PCR respiratory panels as tools that improve diagnostic accuracy and outcomes for patients. However, their value depends on diagnosis stewardship, particularly the clinical decision support systems that facilitate appropriate test ordering [33]. Public awareness about personal hygiene and prevention is necessary to prevent respiratory infection, Study which is a review has provided some of the few non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPI’s) that are effective in reducing transmission of influenza including hand hygiene and oral care [34]. Another study reported that there was a spike in attack rates with no intervention, but the incorporation of social distancing NPIs significantly decreased pooled attack rates, with the greatest decrease when several NPIs were used. Although, there was variability in the results, studies suggest that NPIs are successful in reducing the amount of transmission [35]. Reducing future respiratory infections and strengthening post-COVID health system responses require preparedness measures, including effective planning, resource allocation, timely treatment, surveillance, health education, and sustained hygiene and isolation practices [36].

This study captures physicians reports that, due to the imbalanced prescribing of antibiotics, self-treatment, and no control to freely buy antibiotics over the counter, the Covid-19 pandemic was reported by clinicians to have worsened the phenomenon of antimicrobial resistance, especially for drugs such as azithromycin in their practice. In our Research study it was observed that the primary antibiotics, especially azithromycin, were perceived to have become less effective according to clinicians which attributed to AMR increase, resulting in the need for clinicians to depend on tertiary, broad-spectrum antibiotics. This makes the management of not only respiratory infections but also enteric fever more complex. The findings underscore physicians concern about a therapeutic crisis that is worsening and is more constraining and damaging to the long-term control of future infectious diseases. While our study did not measure resistance directly, physicians’ perceptions are consistent with published reports. Another study in Brazil experienced an avalanche of patients taking azithromycin during the COVID pandemic, leading to Staphylococcus resistance genes in clinical samples. This use of azithromycin demonstrates a self-imposed Health crisis that significantly degrades the public and environmental health with microbial resistance and the gut-brain axis [37]. It is consistent with the rapid review study on Covid-19 patients which concluded that Antibiotics were used empirically during COVID 19 hospitalizations despite low rates of bacterial co-infection. This shows the evidence/practice gap in relating to milder illness [38]. In comparison with other studies pooled prevalence rate of resistant bacterial co-infection in COVID-19 patients in the meta-analysis is 24% [39]. Antimicrobials have been prescribed under the assumption that they target SARS-CoV-2 directly; this can help develop resistance to co-infecting pathogens—a situation referred to as ‘bystander selection’ [40]. In a French ICU study, among 26 Covid-19 patients with acute respiratory failure due to bacterial co-infections, most frequently noted were, Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, with some even resistant to third-generation cephalosporins and amoxicillin [41]. During the pandemic, a large Sect. (76%) of respondents was involved in self-medication of antibiotics. This was propelled by non-medical factors like convenience, and medical factors like prior success which involved the use of azithromycin (34%), amoxicillin/clavulanate (22%), and metronidazole (16%) which were the antibiotics most frequently used [42]. Although our study did not generate microbiological data, the perception of worsening resistance aligns with known challenges in Pakistan, where the burden of drug-resistant infections, including drug resistant tuberculosis and XDR typhoid, may be further worsened by COVID-19-driven misuse of azithromycin and other antibiotics. This worsens drug resistant infections beyond respiratory infections which poses a greater public health threat [43]. Pakistan’s vulnerability to infectious disease outbreaks due to health system gaps has been previously highlighted [44].

Synthesizing the four major themes from this study, a conceptual model emerges from physicians’ accounts. First, perceived immune compromise following COVID-19 (Theme 1) creates a baseline vulnerability to respiratory infections. Second, environmental and behavioral factors (Theme 2) – including air pollution, reduced mask use, and sedentary lifestyles – act as immediate triggers that increase infection frequency and severity in susceptible individuals. Third, irrational antibiotic use and self-medication (Theme 4) drive antimicrobial resistance, which reduces treatment options. Fourth, diagnostic and stewardship challenges (Theme 3) perpetuate inappropriate prescribing, completing a vicious cycle: weakened immunity → more infections → more antibiotic use → more resistance → harder-to-treat infections → worse outcomes. This cycle, as described by clinicians, suggests that interventions targeting any single node (e.g., improving diagnostics, reducing pollution, or restoring immunity through vaccination) could have multiplier effects. However, our cross-sectional qualitative design cannot establish causal direction; these links represent physicians’ experiential insights rather than proven pathways. Future mixed-methods research should test this model quantitatively.

Strength and limitations of study

This qualitative study captures the first time, the views of frontline physicians on the reasons for the surges of post-COVID-19 respiratory infections and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in Pakistan. Since the interviews were semi-structured, it assisted the exploration of the context, and the complexity of the specific factors. Also, data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved. In addition, the participation of physicians of various clinical specialties and levels of experience was beneficial. The study was conducted in adherence to established reporting guidelines (COREQ) which increases the methodological transparency and replicability of the study.

Limitation of the study Limitations include: (1) urban sampling (Islamabad/Rawalpindi) limiting generalizability to rural Pakistan; (2) short interview duration (10–15 min); (3) no member checking; (4) potential social desirability bias; (5) lack of triangulation; (6) AMR claims are perceptual and not confirmed by microbiological antibiogram data; no laboratory testing was performed.(7) Transcripts were not returned to participants for validation (no member checking), and no triangulation with patient charts or administrative data was performed. These omissions may affect interpretive accuracy and should be addressed in future research.

Conclusion

This qualitative study captures physicians’ perceptions of a concerning surge in severe and recurrent respiratory infections in post-pandemic Pakistan, driven by physicians reports of post-viral immune compromise, comorbidities, environmental factors, and lapsed public health measures. Critically, pandemic-era irrational antibiotic use was perceived to have exacerbated antimicrobial resistance, by the physicians interviewed, in their view undermining treatment efficacy for common infections. To address this crisis, strengthening diagnostic stewardship, enforcing antimicrobial guidelines, and prioritizing vaccination and air quality control are imperative to potentially mitigate long-term respiratory morbidity and help prepare for for future health crises.
