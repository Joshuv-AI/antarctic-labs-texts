Vaginal cancer in the second trimester of pregnancy: Successful conservative surgery and 21-year disease-free survival
Authors: Wiebren Tjalma, Frédéric Caris alias Reynders, Christa Eelen, Justin Konje, Filip Deckers, Ines Joye, Konstantinos Papadimitriou, Anbukkani Subbian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Vaginal cancer in pregnancy is extremely rare and lacks clear guidance.•Conservative surgery during pregnancy can be feasible in early-stage disease.•Early treatment may improve maternal outcome without compromising pregnancy.•21-year disease-free survival demonstrates excellent long-term outcome.

Introduction

Primary vaginal cancer is rare, accounting for approximately 1–2% of gynecologic malignancies, and predominantly affects postmenopausal women (Tjalma et al., 2001). Vaginal cancer during pregnancy is exceptionally uncommon, with fewer than 20 cases reported in the literature (Korenaga and Tewari, 2020). In most reported cases, management involved either termination of pregnancy or early delivery to allow timely oncologic treatment.

Most vaginal cancers are of squamous origin and are associated with persistent high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, particularly HPV-16. Management is challenging due to the lack of standardized guidelines and the need to balance maternal oncologic outcomes with fetal viability. Treatment decisions are highly individualized, and delays in therapy to preserve pregnancy may adversely affect prognosis (Samejima et al., 2021).

We present a rare case of HPV-16 associated vaginal cancer diagnosed in the second trimester of pregnancy, managed conservatively with long-term disease-free survival.

Case presentation

A 38-year-old woman (G3P2) presented at 20 weeks of gestation with intermittent vaginal spotting. She had participated regularly in the national cervical cancer screening program, with three prior normal cytology results. Routine HPV testing was not yet implemented at that time. Her medical history was unremarkable, and she was a non-smoker using only vitamin supplements.

Gynecological examination revealed a 40 mm polypoid lesion on the anterior vaginal wall, clearly separate from the cervix and vulva (Fig. 1). Biopsy demonstrated an infiltrating carcinoma of Müllerian origin involving the fibromuscular stroma. Immunohistochemistry showed PAS-positive, diastase-resistant mucin, strong expression of CEA and CK7, and negativity for CK20, estrogen and progesterone receptors, and PSA. Additional staining revealed focal CA15-3 and CA19-9 positivity, weak CK19 expression, apical CA125 staining, and diffuse p16 expression. High-risk HPV testing confirmed HPV-16 positivity. PD-L1 and MMR/MSI testing were not performed, as these analyses were not routinely available at the time of diagnosis.Fig. 1Clinical image of the polypoid lesion on the anterior vaginal wall.

Staging investigations, including chest X-ray with abdominal shielding, abdominal and breast ultrasound, and colonoscopy, showed no evidence of distant disease. CT of the chest, abdomen and pelvis and PET/CT scans were not performed because of fetal exposure to ionizing radiation. Pelvic MRI demonstrated a 40 mm lesion involving the anterior and lateral vaginal wall with extension into adjacent fat, without invasion of the bladder, rectum, or parametrium, and without lymphadenopathy (Fig. 2). No invasion of the urinary bladder, rectum, or parametrial tissues was observed.

The case was discussed in a multidisciplinary tumor board involving gynecologic oncology, medical oncology, radiation oncology, obstetrics, perinatology, urology, and an ethics committee. Management options including chemotherapy with ongoing pregnancy, radical surgery following pregnancy termination, chemoradiation after termination, and conservative surgical resection with ongoing pregnancy. Immunotherapy was not considered, as immune checkpoint inhibitors were not available in clinical practice at the time of the treatment. After extensive counselling, the patient opted for surgery that allowed the pregnancy to continue.

At 23 + 6 weeks of gestation, a hemi-vaginectomy with pelvic lymphadenectomy was performed, extending from the subclitoral region to the vaginal fornix and including part of the urethra with pelvic lymphadenectomy was performed in the lithotomy position with the buttocks placed at the edge of the operating table to optimize surgical exposure. The margins of the area to be resected were carefully delineated under colposcopic guidance to achieve optimal surgical margins. The incision was made with a scalpel and started just below the clitoris. First, a transverse semicircular incision was done to define the width of the resection before extending the incision cranially. The entire resection area was outlined before starting the dissection. Care was taken to avoid both overly superficial and excessively deep dissection. The incision was made through the vaginal mucosa and lamina propria. The vaginal wall and the edges of the specimen were then placed under traction using Allis forceps. Dissection was continued cranially with scissors in a semicircular fashion while maintaining traction on both the specimen and the vaginal wall, thereby preserving the correct anatomical plane until the superior margin of the lesion was reached and the specimen was completely mobilized. In pregnancy, increased tissue hydration facilitates surgical dissection compared with postmenopausal tissue. However, vascularity is also more pronounced. During dissection, visible vessels were selectively ligated whenever possible. Minor bleeding was controlled primarily by compression.

The use of coagulation was minimized because tissue contraction caused by coagulation distorts the anatomical tissue planes, making it more difficult to maintain the correct anatomical plane. Furthermore, depth control is more difficult with coagulation.

No vaginal reconstruction using a flap was performed because of the anticipated risk of flap necrosis and infection as this could increase the risk of preterm delivery. Instead, a vaginal gauze pack impregnated with clindamycin cream was left in-situ and replaced after 48 h. Bilateral inguinofemoral lymph node dissection was not performed because no suspicious inguinal lymph nodes were identified and the expected therapeutic benefit was limited in relation to the potential morbidity of the procedure. The specimen measured 80 × 50 × 10 mm. The procedure was completed without intraoperative complications. Postoperatively, the patient was monitored in the intensive care unit for four days; transient uterine contractions controlled with indomethacin.

Histopathological examination revealed a microinvasive squamous cell carcinoma (maximum depth of invasion 0.5 mm), multifocal high-grade vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia (VAIN III), Paget-like changes, and areas of adenocarcinoma in situ. Resection margins were involved by VAIN III, while all pelvic lymph nodes were negative (pT1bN0).

The pregnancy continued without further complications. At 36 + 6 weeks of gestation, a healthy male infant was delivered by elective cesarean section (2850 g; Apgar scores 8/9/8). No intra-abdominal or pelvic tumor spread was observed, and placental histology was normal.

Three weeks postpartum, MRI showed no residual disease. However, vaginoscopy revealed a suspicious acetowhite lesion on the lower vaginal wall. Biopsies confirmed VAIN III with mucin production, consistent with adenosquamous carcinoma in situ, while endocervical curettage excluded a cervical origin. The lesion was completely excised with negative margins, and laparoscopic sterilization was performed concurrently.

Adjuvant treatment with topical imiquimod resulted in normalization of cytology within four months and clearance of HPV-16 infection.

Follow-up consisted of history and physical examination, vulvoscopy, vaginoscopy, colposcopy, cervical-vaginal cytology, and HPV testing every 6 months during the first 5 years and yearly thereafter. In case of HPV positivity, additional dual staining was performed. During long-term follow-up, intermittent low-level HPV infections were detected, with eventual persistence of only HPV-45 at low levels and negative dual staining for p16/Ki67. The patient reported preserved sexual function. At 21 years and 6 months after the initial diagnosis, she remains disease-free.

Discussion

Primary vaginal cancer during pregnancy confronts clinicians with a fundamental conflict between optimal oncologic care and continuation of pregnancy, in a setting where evidence is extremely limited and decisions must be made under considerable uncertainty. Current management is largely informed by isolated case reports, and available data suggest that postponing definitive treatment to preserve pregnancy may compromise maternal outcome (Samejima et al., 2021).

In the case reported by Samejima et al., for example initial surgical excision with ongoing pregnancy management was followed by rapid tumor progression, leading to maternal death within months after delivery (Samejima et al., 2021). A different management strategy was described by King et al. (King et al., 2023). In their case, continuation of pregnancy was considered incompatible with optimal oncologic management. After extensive counselling and shared decision-making, the patient opted for termination of pregnancy at 16 weeks, followed two weeks later by radical hysterectomy, upper vaginectomy, bilateral salpingectomy, and bilateral pelvic lymphadenectomy (King et al., 2023). Unfortunately, no follow-up data for this patient are available. In contrast, the present case demonstrates that early surgical intervention during pregnancy, without delay, may achieve excellent long-term oncologic control. These contrasting observations suggest that prognosis is primarily determined by the adequacy and timing of treatment rather than by pregnancy itself.

Two previous cases in which immediate treatment was combined with successful pregnancy-preserving management have been reported (Palmer and Biback, 1954, Fujita et al., 2005). However, the case described by Palmer et al. was treated with historical radium techniques before the modern era of imaging and oncologic surgery (Palmer and Biback, 1954). Fujita et al. reported a more limited local excision followed by additional postpartum surgery and radiotherapy (Fujita et al., 2005). In contrast, the present case demonstrates that immediate radical surgery during ongoing pregnancy may be feasible in carefully selected patients, while preserving both the pregnancy and long-term control of invasive disease.

The present case demonstrates a potential role for surgery allowing the pregnancy to continue in carefully selected early-stage disease. While radical treatment following termination of pregnancy remains standard in many reported cases, this approach may not always be necessary. In tumors confined to the vaginal wall, complete surgical excision with adequate margins can be both diagnostic and therapeutic (Tjalma et al., 2001). When oncologic safety can be ensured, pregnancy-preserving surgery may therefore be a valid alternative.

This case also highlights the limitations of initial biopsy. The discrepancy between biopsy and final pathology reflects the biological heterogeneity of vaginal neoplasia and the risk of sampling error. Complete excision is therefore essential not only for treatment but also for establishing an accurate diagnosis, with direct implications for further management.

Beyond treatment strategy, this case also highlights the role of high-risk HPV infection. HPV testing and typing should be part of both pretreatment evaluation and follow-up (Tjalma, 2026). Vaginal cancer is strongly linked to persistent HPV infection, most commonly HPV-16, as demonstrated in this case. The persistent HPV infection likely contributed to the multifocal intraepithelial disease. Detection of different HPV types during follow-up indicates new infection rather than recurrence. This highlights the importance of type-specific HPV testing to distinguish persistent infection from newly acquired HPV, to prevent misinterpretation as recurrence, thereby guiding long-term surveillance (Tjalma, 2026).

Management of cancer during pregnancy inevitably raises complex ethical dilemmas, particularly regarding the option of pregnancy termination. Termination may allow immediate standard oncologic treatment and is often recommended in advanced disease or early gestation. However, this must be balanced against maternal wishes and foetal considerations. Decision-making requires a multidisciplinary approach and must respect patient autonomy.

Importantly, these ethical considerations cannot be separated from the legal context in which care is delivered. In permissive systems such as Belgium, termination of pregnancy can be discussed as a legitimate medical option within clearly defined legal boundaries. The Belgian abortion law of 1990 marked a pivotal moment, when King Boudewijn refused to sign the legislation on moral grounds, leading to a constitutional crisis. Prime Minister Wilfried Martens temporarily declared the King “unable to reign,” allowing the law to be enacted. Subsequent reforms, including the 2018 decriminalization of abortion, further clarified access to medically indicated pregnancy termination (Belgium Law of 3 April 1990, Belgium Law of 15 October 2018, De Meyer et al., 2025). This provides legal clarity for both the patients and clinicians during shared decision-making.

In contrast, the legal landscape in the United States has become increasingly complex and heterogenous following the Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization decision in 2021 (19-1392 October term, 2021). The removal of federal protection for abortion rights resulted in substantial variation in state-level legislation, with some states adopting highly restrictive laws. Recent reports indicate that these legal constraints may delay or complicate timely indicated cancer treatment (King et al., 2023, Suran, 2022, Katana Ogongo et al., 2025). In some states, abortion is permitted only under narrowly defined “medical emergency” conditions, creating uncertainty for both patients and clinicians. In some situations, this may require medicolegal consultation before treatment decisions can be made. Rapidly evolving legislation and unclear definitions of medical necessity may create barriers to care and delay timely treatment (Barroilhet, 2023). These constraints may also complicate shared decision-making and contribute to psychological burden and moral distress among healthcare providers. Together, these observations illustrate how legal frameworks may directly influence the practical management of cancer during pregnancy despite similar underlying medical principles.

Taken together, these observations show that management of vaginal cancer during pregnancy is influenced not only by oncologic factors, but also by patient preference and the legal framework. Shared decision-making is therefore essential. Counselling should include clear discussion of maternal and fetal risks within a multidisciplinary setting.

The present case illustrates that immediate surgical treatment during ongoing pregnancy may be feasible in carefully selected patients with early-stage disease. Favorable long-term oncologic and obstetric outcomes can be achieved. However, this approach requires timely diagnosis, appropriate patient selection, close multidisciplinary collaboration and a supportive legal framework.

Primary vaginal cancer during pregnancy remains an exceptionally rare and challenging clinical condition for which evidence-based guidance is lacking.

Management should therefore be individualized and integrate oncologic, obstetric, ethical, and legal considerations.

Key clinical message

Management of vaginal cancer during pregnancy requires individualized, multidisciplinary decision-making integrating oncologic, obstetrics, ethical and legal considerations. In carefully selected early-stage cases, pregnancy-preserving treatment may be feasible without compromising long-term oncologic outcome.
