Background

Uterine leiomyomas are the most common uterine tumors. Epidemiologic studies have shown that the cumulative prevalence in women by the age of 50 years is about 70 to 80 percent [1]. However, uterine leiomyomas with abnormal growth patterns are rare and include three primary tumors: intravascular leiomyomatosis (IVL), benign metastasizing leiomyoma (BML), and disseminated peritoneal leiomyomatosis (DPL) [2].

DPL was first reported by Wilson and Peale in 1952 [3]. This rare disease is characterized by the presence of multiple leiomyomatous, fibroblastic, and myofibroblastic nodules in the peritoneum and subperitoneum [4]. To date, approximately 200 cases have been reported worldwide, most of which are benign lesions.

BML is a rare disease characterized by the formation of metastatic nodules outside the uterus with histologic similarity to uterine leiomyomas, mainly affecting middle-aged women with uterine smooth muscle tumors [5]. The most common site of metastasis is the lungs, but other sites may also be affected, including some atypical sites such as the heart or spinal cord [6]. Steiner may have been the first to describe this condition, reporting a case of a patient who died from widespread pulmonary metastases of benign leiomyomas that were histologically identical to the multiple leiomyomas in the uterus [7].

IVL, first described by Birch-Hirschfeld in 1896, is a rare smooth muscle cell tumor that primarily affects premenopausal women [8]. IVL usually does not invade the vessel wall itself, but rather extends “worm-like” along the lumen of the vein, with clear boundaries and no adhesions to the vessel wall [9]. This tumor usually grows in a “worm-like” fashion along the uterine or pelvic veins and can extend into the inferior vena cava and even the right heart [10].

Numerous case reports have documented ectopic migration of leiomyomas; however, instances involving metastasis to multiple body cavities and organs throughout the body remain exceedingly rare. Here, we present a case of smooth muscle neoplasia with multiple simultaneous pelvic, intraperitoneal, retroperitoneal, pulmonary, and inferior vena cava metastases.

Case presentation

A 45-year-old woman was admitted to the hospital with prolonged, dull lower abdominal pain and a pelvic mass of unknown origin. The patient had two previous births. She underwent laparoscopic myomectomy at an outside hospital five years ago, and a recurrence of intrauterine fibroids was detected one year postoperatively during an ultrasound examination at another hospital. We were unable to determine the size and number of fibroids at the time of the initial surgery and at the time of the initial detection of recurrence because the patient did not retain the relevant information. During physical examination, multiple solid masses can be palpated above and posterior to the uterus. The largest mass measures approximately 10 cm, exhibits good mobility, and has well-defined borders. Transvaginal ultrasound revealed multiple hypoechoic, round solid masses within the uterine wall. Computed tomography (CT) scans of the upper abdomen and chest showed: a solitary small nodule in the upper lobe of the right lung; an oval filling defect in the segment of the inferior vena cava entering the heart; and a large space-occupying lesion in the area above the right diaphragm and the right adrenal gland (Fig. 1). Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated multiple pelvic masses, with the right mass possibly invading the internal iliac vein (Fig. 1). Blood laboratory tests showed an abnormally elevated concentration of the glycan antigen CA125 of 205 U/ml. Based on the above evidence we considered the patient as a possible case of DPL, while the nature of the tumors in the lungs and retroperitoneum needs to be further confirmed. Since the patient refused to undergo puncture biopsy and requested direct surgery, we considered dealing with the pelvic as well as abdominal tumors first. After completing the preoperative preparations, the patient underwent total hysterectomy, resection of intestinal mass, and rectal repair under general anesthesia. During the procedure, we excised the serosal implant adherent to the rectum and observed that it had invaded the mucosal layer of the rectum. The final pathological results indicated colorectal leiomyomas. The patient recovered well after surgery and did not receive hormone therapy.

Six months after discharge, the patient was readmitted due to the chest nodules. At admission, she had no cough or sputum production and no significant dyspnea or chest tightness. Follow-up transvaginal ultrasound revealed a right ovarian cyst. A CT scan of the chest and abdomen showed multiple space-occupying lesions above the right diaphragm and the right adrenal region, which had increased in size compared to the previous admission; the small nodule in the right upper lobe of the lung remained unchanged (Fig. 2). After completing the relevant preoperative preparations, the patient underwent resection of the right thoracic cavity tumor and wedge resection of the right upper lung under general anesthesia. Both intraoperative frozen sections and postoperative pathological results of the pulmonary nodule and the tumor above the diaphragm indicated leiomyoma. Immunohistochemical results were not available because the lung nodule tissue was small and almost exclusively used for frozen pathology (Figs. 3 and 5). Postoperative immunohistochemical staining of the tumor above the diaphragm showed positivity for Bcl-2, VIM, SMA, and Desmin; negativity for CK, TDT, S-100, CD34, P53, and EMA, with CD99 being (±) and a Ki-67 index of < 1% (Table 1). The final diagnosis was leiomyoma of the right upper lung and right thoracic cavity.

Discussion and conclusions

Given the rarity of uterine leiomyomas with abnormal growth patterns, the pathogenesis of DPL remains unclear. The two main current theories are the mesenchymal stem cell(MSC) chemotaxis theory and the iatrogenic correlation theory. Tavassoli et al. proposed that the occurrence of DPL might be due to the abnormal susceptibility or selective sensitivity of peritoneal mesenchymal stem cells in some women, with hormonal stimulation acting as an initiator or promoter of the process [4]. This is supported by the expression of ER and PR in extrauterine smooth muscle tumors [11]. Another theory posits that iatrogenic implantation is the primary cause of DPL [12]. In recent years, numerous reports have indicated that myomectomy morcellation is a contributing factor to DPL [13]. In a recent systematic study, 13 cases with a history of laparoscopic myomectomy without morcellation were reported, with an average age of 35.6 years. The average interval between the initial laparoscopic surgery and the first diagnosis of DPL was 6.08 years [14]. In the case discussed herein, the patient had undergone laparoscopic myomectomy. Merely one year postoperatively, recurrence of uterine fibroids was detected, accompanied by symptoms of dull lower abdominal pain. Despite the relevant medical history, we cannot definitively ascertain the specific contribution of iatrogenic dissemination.

IVL is a well-differentiated smooth muscle tumor that exhibits aggressive behavior, with the ability to extend throughout the venous system. Due to the rarity of the disease, the exact mechanism of its occurrence remains unclear, and there is no consensus on the origin of IVL. However, the metastatic pathway of IVL extending along the uterine veins, internal iliac veins, and inferior vena cava to the right atrium has been confirmed in numerous publications [15]. It is worth noting that the case described herein presents with an isolated inferior vena cava tumour, which does not exhibit the typical worm-like or tubular morphology[16]. This finding is inconsistent with the staging described by Ma et al. [17]. Several case reports have documented solitary cardiac metastases from IVL, with the majority of these cases also presenting evidence of concurrent pulmonary metastases [18]. However, isolated inferior vena cava tumours have been rarely documented in the literature to date, leaving us with little more than conjecture. Isolated cardiac leiomyomas are considered to develop within the heart rather than through venous extension. Isolated cardiac smooth muscle tumours are considered to develop within the heart rather than through venous extension. Karnib M et al. propose that they are formed by BML attaching to the cardiac endothelium [19], whereas Maneyama et al. suggest they arise from spontaneous migration of IVL to the heart [20]. We hypothesise that the pathogenesis of the solitary inferior vena cava tumour in this case may be analogous to that of solitary cardiac leiomyomas. Furthermore, previous case reports have documented spontaneous regression of IVL [20], and our long-term follow-up also revealed that this solitary tumour stabilised with signs of regression. Therefore, the hypothesis that this lesion represents residual tissue following spontaneous resolution of IVL also holds. Given our conservative observation strategy and the fact that its nature as a smooth muscle tumour could only be inferred from contrast-enhanced CT, our conjecture cannot be verified or further investigated.

The etiology and pathogenesis of BML have not yet been fully elucidated. Currently, there are several main theories, including hematogenous dissemination, endometrial metaplasia, cytogenetic mutations, and metastasis from low-grade leiomyosarcoma. Among these, the theory that is widely accepted is the surgery-related hematogenous dissemination theory. In a literature review by Jautzke et al., the interval between uterine surgery and BML diagnosis was reported to be 3 to 20 years, with an average of 10 years in 74 cases of BML. They also described 5 cases of pulmonary tumors in their report, with 4 cases showing high levels of progesterone receptors and estrogen receptors, indicating that the metastatic pulmonary tumors may have originated from the uterus [21]. Some scholars believe that this type of tumor is a low-grade leiomyosarcoma [22]. Kayser et al. compared 10 cases of BML with 2 cases of uterine metastatic leiomyosarcoma and suggested that benign metastasizing leiomyoma might be a slowly growing variant of leiomyosarcoma [23]. Another possible theory is that the tumor originates from the metaplastic transformation of the endometrial epithelium, which seems to explain BML in almost any location where mesothelial metaplasia exists [24]. In a recent study, Chouchane et al. demonstrated the coexistence of ectopic coelomic mesenchymal tissue with leiomyoma tissue in two extrauterine sites in a BML patient, providing evidence for the theory of coelomic metaplasia [5]. However Awonuga et al. reported 2 patients with two recurrences of BML after hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy [25], suggesting that the hormone-related theory alone cannot fully account for BML.

The pathological features of leiomyomas include a smooth muscle phenotype, low mitotic activity, and limited vascularisation. Immunohistochemical characteristics primarily demonstrate smooth muscle actin, Desmin, and a low Ki-67 index [23]. It is noteworthy that our immunohistochemical findings revealed CD34 positivity in retroperitoneal leiomyomas, potentially attributable to their abundant blood supply. Varied reports on CD34 expression across different literature sources have led to divergent perspectives on the aetiology of these tumours. CD34 is typically expressed in vascular endothelium. Fukuyama et al. suggested that CD34 positivity in IVLs may result from tumour extrusion into vascular lumens, causing vascular walls to overlay the tumour surface [26]. Yin P et al. confirmed uterine leiomyoma CD34 +/CD49b + cells exhibit characteristics of somatic stem/progenitor cells [27]. Conversely, Miettinen M proposed that a minority of leiomyosarcomas may exhibit CD34 positivity [28]. In terms of differential diagnosis, positive staining for Desmin and SMA typically indicates a smooth muscle lineage in tumours. Key distinguishing features between benign and malignant tumours include cellular architecture, mitotic status, and the Ki-67 proliferation index, whereas the significance of CD34 remains to be further elucidated.

In terms of treatment, benign metastasizing leiomyoma (BML) is usually inert disease. Asymptomatic patients diagnosed with BML can undergo conservative follow-up without the need for treatment [29]. If progression, complications, or symptoms occur, interventions need to be considered. In previous reports, lesion removal plus oophorectomy may be the dominant intervention. A low estrogenic environment can be maintained through surgical debridement. Hormonal therapy with GnRH agonists and/or aromatase inhibitors is another therapeutic option that may reduce tumor load or may be used in patients who wish to avoid surgical intervention [30]. Owing to the rarity of such conditions, it is virtually impossible to establish standard treatment protocols through clinical trials; therapeutic approaches are typically individualised based on the patient's specific circumstances and the clinician's professional judgement.

The patient described herein underwent three surgical excisions of the lesion without undergoing castration, and no anti-hormonal medication was administered postoperatively on any of the three occasions. Results from regular outpatient follow-up examinations over four years demonstrated effective control of the leiomyomas. Considering the six-year treatment period and four-year follow-up span, during which the patient progressed from sexual maturity through perimenopause and gradually into the geriatric phase, changes in clinical presentation and prognosis may correlate with physiological transitions and hormonal fluctuations. Due to limitations in available data, we lack hormonal level measurements and relevant histochemical information, precluding definitive conclusions.

This study reviews a case of a female patient who developed multiple ectopic metastases in the thoracic cavity, abdominal cavity, pelvic cavity, retroperitoneum, and inferior vena cava following myomectomy, and who underwent follow-up after complete surgical resection. Given the diverse mechanisms of ectopic migration in leiomyomas, healthcare professionals should consider the possibility of ectopic leiomyomas in patients with a history of uterine myomatosis presenting with multiple, disseminated masses of unknown origin. Standard therapeutic strategies for this condition remain to be established; however, complete surgical resection may be considered for selected patients and may offer durable control.
