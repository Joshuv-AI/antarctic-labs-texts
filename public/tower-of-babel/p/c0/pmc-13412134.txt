The effect of remote nurse-led patient education and follow-up on glycemic control and metabolic function in patients with type 2 diabetes: a single-blind randomized controlled trial
Authors: Ali Sadeghian, Azadeh Tataei, Reza Gholikhani Darbroud, Naser Parizad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & objectiveType 2 Diabetes (T2D) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by impaired insulin secretion, insulin resistance, and progressive β-cell dysfunction that ultimately leads to serious vascular and metabolic complications. Effective diabetes management requires innovative approaches such as remote education and nurse-led interventions. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effect of remote nurse-led patient education and follow-up on glycemic control and key metabolic functions including pancreatic β-cell function (HOMA-β), insulin sensitivity (HOMA-α), and insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) in T2D patients.Methods & materialsThis is a single-blind randomized controlled trial conducted on a total of 66 patients with T2D. Participants were randomly assigned to the intervention (n = 33) and control (n = 33) groups. The intervention group received three months of daily remote education via text, audio, and video messages combined with weekly follow-ups. Baseline and post-intervention data included Fasting Blood Glucose (FBG), fasting insulin, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and HOMA indices. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA).ResultsSignificant within-group improvements were observed in the intervention group for FBG, HbA1c, HOMA-β, HOMA-α, and HOMA-IR scores (p < 0.05); however, the control group showed no significant within-group changes (p > 0.05). Between-group analysis showed greater improvements in the intervention group compared to the control group for all above outcomes (p < 0.05).ConclusionIn this randomized trial, a three-month remote nurse-led patient education and follow-up program was associated with improvements in several glycemic and metabolic markers, including FBG, HbA1c, HOMA-IR, HOMA-α, and HOMA-β, compared with usual care. These preliminary findings suggest potential metabolic benefits and highlight the need for larger, longer-term studies to confirm these effects before broader clinical application.Trial registrationThis study was prospectively registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials on 15 July 2024 (IRCT20240708062366N1, https://irct.behdasht.gov.ir/trial/77809). After trial registration, participant recruitment began on 1 August 2024 and continued until 1 October 2024.

Introduction

Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by impaired insulin secretion and resistance of body cells to insulin. Accounting for about 90% of all diabetes cases, it affected over 537 million adults in 2021 and is projected to reach 784 million by 2045 due to aging, urbanization, and unhealthy lifestyles [1, 2]. T2D results from pancreatic beta-cell dysfunction and decreased cellular sensitivity to insulin, which can lead to poor glucose uptake and utilization [3, 4]. Though initial symptoms may seem mild, long-term complications such as cardiovascular disease, kidney failure, neuropathy, and retinopathy impose major health and economic burdens [5].

Key diagnostic tools, including glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and the Homeostatic Model Assessment (HOMA), are commonly used to assess disease severity and treatment response. HbA1c reflects long-term glycemic control, whereas HOMA indices estimate β-cell function (HOMA-β), insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), and insulin sensitivity (HOMA-α) [6, 7]. These indices are cost-effective, minimally invasive, and widely applied in both clinical and research settings. Previous studies have shown that patients with T2D typically exhibit elevated HOMA-IR levels and reduced HOMA-β values, reflecting increased insulin resistance and impaired pancreatic β-cell function, respectively [8, 9]. Beyond their role in elucidating disease pathophysiology, these indices are also used to evaluate the metabolic effects of therapeutic interventions [10].

Patient education is a cornerstone of T2D management, as it enables individuals to understand their condition and adopt lifestyle changes to improve outcomes. Educational interventions covering nutrition, physical activity, and medication adherence improve glycemic control, reduce complications, and enhance quality of life [11–14]. Moreover, effective patient education fosters psychological well-being and better treatment adherence [15].

With advances in technology, traditional face-to-face education has increasingly incorporated remote and technology-assisted approaches to expand access to care and support continuity. Telemedicine, mobile applications, and web-based platforms can provide interactive, personalized, and accessible educational resources, potentially benefiting patients in rural or underserved areas [16–19]. These tools enable patients to access educational content on topics such as healthy eating, physical activity, and self-monitoring skills in formats that can be adapted to individual preferences and needs [20].

Remote patient education has demonstrated promising results, including significant reductions in HbA1c and improvements in insulin sensitivity as key indicators of T2D control [21–23]. These methods also foster continuous communication between patients and healthcare providers, ensures timely interventions, and addresses barriers to treatment adherence [24]. Beyond clinical outcomes, remote education improves patient engagement and equips individuals with the knowledge and confidence needed to make informed decisions about their health [25]. Studies on mobile and internet-based interventions further highlight the potential of remote education in T2D management. Improved HbA1c levels, enhanced insulin sensitivity, and better quality of life have been reported in various patient populations [21, 23, 24]. However, despite these positive outcomes, few studies have examined the impact of remote education on functional indices such as HOMA-β and HOMA-IR [26–28]. Addressing these parameters is crucial for understanding the physiological effects of remote interventions.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the potential association between a remote nurse-led patient education and follow-up program and changes in glycemic control, as well as selected metabolic indices (HOMA-β, HOMA-α, and HOMA-IR) in patients with T2D. In light of the limited evidence regarding effects beyond HbA1c, the study provides preliminary insights into possible underlying physiological mechanisms.

Methods & materials

Design & setting

This study was a single-blind randomized controlled trial designed to evaluate the effect of remote nurse-led patient education and follow-up on glycemic control and key metabolic functions in patients with T2D. The trial was conducted from 1 August 2024 to 1 February 2025 at the Center for Diabetes Association of Urmia, Iran. The study was reported in accordance with the CONSORT reporting guidelines [29] and the completed CONSORT checklist is provided as Supplementary File.

Participants

The study included adults aged 18 to 60 years with a confirmed clinical diagnosis of T2D. Eligibility criteria required participants to have HbA1c levels between 6.5% and 8%, use oral or injectable diabetes medications, and demonstrate the ability to read, write, and operate a smartphone. In addition, patients with comorbid medical conditions such as a history of mental illness, uncontrolled hypertension (≥ 180/110 mmHg), chronic kidney disease, or chronic pulmonary or cardiovascular disorders were excluded from the study. Participants were also excluded if they declined to provide consent, developed severe intercurrent illnesses (e.g., acute renal failure or decompensated cardiovascular disease), or required hospitalization for diabetes-related complications during the study period.

Sampling

Participant recruitment began on 1 August 2024 and continued until 1 October 2024. A total of 66 participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method and were then randomly allocated into two groups: intervention (n = 33) and control (n = 33). The sample size was determined using the formula for comparing two means as stated below. Based on the study by Nansel et al. (2012) [30], a mean difference of 0.5 and a standard deviation of 0.7 in HbA1c were assumed, with a 95% confidence level (α = 0.05) and 95% statistical power (β = 0.05). The minimum required sample size was 27 participants per group; however, this number was increased to 33 per group to account for an anticipated 20% attrition rate, thereby maintaining adequate statistical power and reliability (Fig. 1).

Randomization

Participants were randomly assigned to the intervention and control groups using a block randomization approach with a fixed block size of four to ensure balanced allocation throughout the study. Six possible block combinations (AABB, BBAA, ABBA, BAAB, ABAB, and BABA) were predefined, where the letters A and B denoted the intervention and control groups, respectively. The allocation sequence was generated objectively using dice rolling, in which a fair six-sided dice determined the order of blocks. Participants were then assigned according to the sequence of letters within each selected block.

To ensure allocation concealment, each block sequence was sealed in sequentially numbered opaque envelopes, which were opened only after participant enrollment. The randomization process was conducted entirely by an independent researcher not involved in participant recruitment, education delivery, or data analysis. No stratification factors were used during randomization.

Intervention

The intervention group received a three-month remote nurse-led diabetes self-management education and follow-up program. The educational content was delivered via daily text, audio, or video messages through a social messaging application named “Eitaa”. This platform was chosen due to its widespread use and accessibility among the study population, which ensured cultural and technological compatibility. The program emphasized self-management strategies, including blood glucose monitoring, dietary habits, physical activity, medication adherence, foot care, and smoking cessation.

Educational materials were developed based on ADA guidelines and reviewed by two endocrinologists and two diabetes nurse specialists to ensure clinical accuracy, cultural appropriateness, and consistency with evidence-based practice. Topics included glycemic monitoring, carbohydrate counting, and exercise regimens [31]. Before developing the educational content, patients’ educational needs were assessed and identified using a pre-prepared checklist. This needs assessment was conducted to ensure that the educational content would be designed and developed in accordance with patients’ actual needs, conditions, and level of knowledge, thereby improving the effectiveness of the education.

The program content was organized into 12 weekly modules (Table 1), each addressing core self-management domains. Daily educational messages (text, audio, or short video format) were sent via Eitaa to reinforce key concepts and provide practical tips for diabetes management. Messages were scripted, standardized, and derived directly from the booklet to ensure consistency. Weekly phone calls (~ 20 min each) were used to evaluate participants’ glycemic control, dietary practices, and medication adherence, while providing tailored advice to address any issues identified.

Intervention content schedule

Adherence was monitored through app-based message engagement and self-reported compliance. Participants who missed two consecutive messages were contacted via phone to identify and resolve potential barriers and to encourage continued participation. Additionally, participants were encouraged to use the messaging platform or phone to ask questions or report concerns throughout the intervention period. No objective measures of behavior change were collected and engagement metrics cannot be interpreted as evidence of lifestyle modification. The control group continued with their usual diabetes care.

Data collection

Baseline data were collected using in-person interviews and patient records. Moreover, height and weight were measured using calibrated Seca digital stadiometers and scales (0.1 cm and 0.1 kg accuracy). Blood samples were collected to measure Fasting Blood Glucose (FBG), fasting insulin, and HbA1c levels. Samples were taken between 8:00 and 9:00 am after an overnight fast of at least 8 h, drawn from the antecubital vein, stored at 2–8 °C, and analyzed within 4 h of collection. FBG was enzymatically analyzed using Pars Azmun kits (Lot. No. 1400-02, Cat. No. 13458, Karaj, Iran) with a Hitachi 902 auto-analyzer, while fasting insulin was analyzed with Demeditec ELISA kit (Lot. No. 2421960, DE2935, Germany). Primary outcomes including HOMA indices were calculated using the Oxford HOMA2 Calculator software, version 2.2.4 (University of Oxford, Diabetes Trials Unit). Secondary outcomes included changes in HbA1C and FBG levels.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was first applied to assess the normality of continuous variables. As the data were normally distributed, parametric tests were used for analysis. Descriptive statistics (mean ± standard deviation) were computed to summarize demographic and baseline characteristics. Paired-sample t-tests were used to examine within-group changes before and after the intervention, while independent-sample t-tests were used to compare differences between groups. To adjust for potential baseline imbalances, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted, with baseline values entered as covariates. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The results of the independent t-test indicated no significant differences in age, weight, height, or duration of diabetes between the intervention and control groups, suggesting that the groups were comparable in terms of these demographic characteristics (Table 2). Similarly, chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests showed no significant differences in qualitative variables such as gender, marital status, educational level, occupational status, family history, and type of treatment (Table 3). These findings indicate that the two groups were generally comparable at baseline.

Fasting blood glucose (FBG)

The baseline mean FBG differed significantly between the groups (intervention: 176.61 ± 65.36 mg/dL; control: 126.67 ± 39.94 mg/dL; t = 3.745, p = 0.000). After the intervention, the intervention group showed a within-group decrease in FBG (mean change = − 19.91 ± 40.60 mg/dL, p = 0.008), whereas the control group showed an increase (mean change = 42.57 ± 50.27 mg/dL, p = 0.000). To account for the baseline imbalance, an ANCOVA was conducted with baseline FBG included as a covariate. The adjusted mean FBG was lower in the intervention group (adjusted M = 141.14 mg/dL, 95% CI [126.05, 156.24]) than in the control group (adjusted M = 184.80 mg/dL, 95% CI [169.70, 199.89]). The adjusted mean difference was − 43.65 mg/dL (95% CI [− 66.03, − 21.27]; F (1, 63) = 15.19, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.19) (Table 4). These findings suggest that participation in the remote nurse-led education and follow-up program was related to lower post-intervention FBG levels compared with usual care.

Serum insulin level

After the intervention, mean serum insulin levels increased significantly in the intervention group (mean change = − 4.90 ± 6.21 µU/mL, p = 0.000), whereas no significant within-group change was observed in the control group (mean change = − 4.21 ± 17.63 µU/mL, p = 0.180). No significant difference was detected between the groups at baseline (t = − 1.55, p = 0.125); however, a statistically significant difference emerged after the intervention (t = − 3.166, p = 0.002). These findings may suggest that participation in the nurse-led remote education and follow-up program was related to greater increases in serum insulin levels (Table 4).

Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c)

Findings indicated a significant within-group reduction in HbA1c in the intervention group (mean change = − 0.312 ± 0.63, p = 0.008), whereas no significant change was observed in the control group (mean change = 0.17 ± 0.10, p = 0.102). Baseline HbA1c differed significantly between groups (t = 2.945, p = 0.004). To account for this baseline imbalance, ANCOVA was performed with baseline HbA1c as a covariate. After adjustment, the intervention group had a lower adjusted mean HbA1c (adjusted M = 6.44, 95% CI [5.26, 7.61]) compared to the control group (adjusted M = 7.64, 95% CI [7.46, 7.81]). The adjusted mean difference was − 0.21 (95% CI [− 0.45, − 0.04]), F (1, 63) = 12.70, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.41 (Table 4). These findings indicate potential improvement in long-term glycemic control associated with the nurse-led remote education and follow-up program after adjustment for baseline differences.

Homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR)

The intervention group demonstrated a significant within-group reduction in HOMA-IR scores (mean change = − 0.815 ± 1.25, p = 0.001), whereas the control group showed no statistically significant within-group change (mean change = − 0.272 ± 1.74, p = 0.375). No significant between-group difference was observed at baseline (t = − 0.667, p = 0.507). However, a statistically significant between-group difference was detected after the intervention (t = − 3.20, p = 0.002) (Table 5). Overall, these findings indicate that the remote nurse-led education and follow-up program could be linked to a greater reduction in HOMA-IR compared with usual care.

Homeostatic model assessment of insulin sensitivity (HOMA-α)

The mean HOMA-α score showed significant greater changes in the intervention group (mean change = − 68.05 ± 59.13, p = 0.000), whereas no significant change was observed in the control group (mean change = − 20.20 ± 78.62, p = 0.150). Although there was no significant difference in baseline HOMA-α scores between the two groups (t = 0.58, p = 0.954), a statistically significant between-group difference emerged after the intervention (t = 3.017, p = 0.004) (Table 5). These findings may suggest greater changes in HOMA-α associated with the intervention, indicating potential enhancement of insulin sensitivity among participants in the intervention group.

Homeostatic model assessment of β-Cell function (HOMA-β)

The paired t-test revealed a significant change in mean HOMA-β scores in the intervention group (mean change = − 43.64 ± 57.71, p < 0.001), whereas the control group showed no comparable change (mean change = − 7.49 ± 25.23, p = 0.098). The independent t-test identified a significant baseline difference in HOMA-β between groups (t = − 3.62, p = 0.001); therefore, ANCOVA was performed to adjust for baseline values. After adjusting for baseline HOMA-β, the intervention group demonstrated a significantly higher adjusted mean HOMA-β (adjusted M = 39.92, 95% CI [30.94, 48.90]) compared with the control group (adjusted M = 36.98, 95% CI [27.10, 45.96]). The adjusted mean difference was 2.94 (95% CI [− 10.34, 16.22]), F (1, 63) = 13.68, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.178 (Table 5). These results suggest that the intervention was linked to greater improvement in HOMA-β after accounting for baseline differences, indicating potential enhancement of β-cell function in the intervention group.

Discussion

The current study provides preliminary evidence that a remote nurse-led diabetes-centered patient education and follow-up program was associated with improvements in several metabolic outcomes among patients with T2D, including reductions in FBG, HbA1c, and HOMA-IR, alongside increases in serum insulin, HOMA-α, and HOMA-β. However, given the open-label design, small sample size, and baseline differences between groups, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. While the observed patterns suggest potential benefits of structured remote nursing support, causal inferences cannot be made.

These outcomes align with a growing body of research emphasizing the utility of remote interventions in T2D management. Our findings are broadly consistent with Gerber et al. (2023) in showing associations with reductions in HbA1c following a mHealth intervention in patients with T2D, while additionally reporting changes in selected HOMA indices that were not emphasized in their study [32]. However, unlike Gerber’s study, which found no significant differences in secondary outcomes, our research was associated with improvements in insulin sensitivity and beta-cell function, as reflected in HOMA indices. These additional physiological changes provide preliminary data on remote nurse-led education but should be interpreted cautiously​. Importantly, similar metabolic parameters have been examined in prior randomized trials. Devanbu et al. (2025) reported that a telemedicine-based intervention combined with electronic call reminders and health education significantly improved HbA1c, fasting and postprandial glucose, and HOMA-IR compared with standard care [33]. Our observations are directionally consistent with these findings, although differences in intervention structure, study design, and sample size limit direct comparability.

Our findings are in line with the systematic review by Park and Lee (2023), which found that telenursing and home-visit interventions were similarly associated with reductions in HbA1c and improvements in FBG among patients with T2D [34]. The present study extends these observations by additionally reporting changes in HOMA-derived indices of insulin sensitivity and β-cell function, although direct comparisons are limited by differences in study design and populations​. Our findings align with those of Katula et al. (2022), who reported reductions in HbA1c and weight following a digital diabetes prevention program (d-DPP) in individuals with prediabetes [35]. The present preliminary observations also suggest possible changes in HOMA indices among patients with established T2D, although the different populations (prediabetes vs. diagnosed T2D) and intervention focuses limit direct comparability.

Our findings are consistent with Jiang et al. (2022), who reported that a nurse-led smartphone-based education program was correlated with improvements in self-monitoring behaviors and reductions in HbA1c among patients with poorly controlled T2D, with efficacy comparable to standard nurse-led diabetes services. In their trial, between-group differences were not statistically significant for most outcomes [36]. The present study preliminarily observed changes in selected HOMA indices alongside glycemic measures, although baseline imbalances, the small sample size, and differences in intervention delivery limit direct comparisons and preclude firm conclusions regarding potential physiological effects.

Our observations are consistent with those of Hoyo et al. (2022) in the TELE-DD trial, which reported that a proactive, psychoeducational telephonic intervention delivered by nurses was related to improvements in treatment adherence, HbA1c, blood pressure, and diabetes-related distress among patients with T2D and comorbid depression over an 18-month follow-up period [37]. The present findings similarly suggest associations with improved glycemic markers, while additionally reporting changes in HOMA-derived indices of insulin sensitivity and β-cell function. However, differences in study populations, intervention duration, and sample characteristics limit direct comparability. Our findings are also comparable to those of Schrauben et al. (2022), who demonstrated that dietary app-supported tele-counseling was associated with reductions in sodium intake, improvements in diet quality, and better blood pressure control among patients with T2D and early chronic kidney disease [38]. Their emphasis on remote interventions aligns with the educational structure of the present program. The current study preliminarily observed concurrent associations with glycemic and selected metabolic indices (HOMA-IR, HOMA-α, and HOMA-β). However, differences in primary outcomes (dietary versus metabolic measures) and target populations (e.g., chronic kidney disease comorbidity) limit strong inferences regarding shared mechanisms.

Our findings are also consistent with those of Chan et al. (2020), who concluded in their rapid review that virtual care incorporating individualized communication was associated with glycemic outcomes similar to or better than usual care in individuals with type 1 and type 2 diabetes [39]. Although the present trial was relatively small, it similarly observed associations with HbA1c reduction and extended the analysis to HOMA-derived indices.

Our findings are broadly consistent with the narrative review by AkbariRad et al. (2023), which reported that there was a significant relationship between telenursing interventions and improvements in self-care behaviors, treatment adherence, HbA1c, plasma glucose, and quality of life in patients with T2D [16]. The present study observed similar associations with glycemic control and additionally reported changes in HOMA-derived indices. However, direct comparisons are limited by the narrative design of the review, its lack of focus on physiological indices, and the methodological limitations of the current trial. Similarly, Zamanzadeh et al. (2017) found that distance education delivered via telephone and SMS was associated with increased patient empowerment among individuals with T2D compared with usual care [40]. The present study aligns with these findings regarding the potential benefits of remote education and additionally observed changes in glycemic and HOMA-derived indices. Nevertheless, differences in primary outcomes, the modest sample size, and baseline imbalances limit direct comparability. Our findings are also comparable to those of Ye et al. (2024), who reported in a 26-week randomized controlled trial that telehealth education delivered via WeChat was linked to improvements in self-management behaviors, body weight, BMI, fasting glucose, HbA1c, blood pressure, and lipid profiles among patients with coexisting T2D and hypertension compared with conventional education [41]. While the present study similarly observed associations with glycemic and selected metabolic markers, interpretation should be cautious due to differences in study populations, intervention duration, and the small sample size and baseline imbalances in our trial.

Lastly, Rasoul et al. (2019) demonstrated that web-based self-management education positively affects quality of life and glycemic control, reinforcing the potential of remote interventions [42]. Their emphasis on patient engagement and culturally adapted educational materials aligns closely with our study’s structured approach and further supports the value of accessible, tailored programs for T2D management.

Implications for practice

These findings highlight the potential of remote nurse-led education as a scalable and cost-effective approach to supporting T2D management, particularly in resource-limited settings where specialized care may be less accessible. By providing regular follow-up, culturally tailored educational content, and ongoing professional feedback, this model may promote patient engagement, adherence, and self-management. The inclusion of HOMA-derived indices offers preliminary insight into insulin resistance, insulin sensitivity, and β-cell function, providing a broader perspective beyond glycemic endpoints.

However, these observations are based on a small sample with baseline imbalances and short-term follow-up. As such, no definitive conclusions can be drawn regarding causal mechanisms, behavioral change, or long-term clinical impact. Larger, well-powered trials with extended follow-up and careful baseline matching are needed before these findings can inform clinical practice or policy recommendations.

Study limitations

Several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a small, single-center trial with convenience sampling, which limits statistical power and restricts generalizability to broader populations. Second, despite randomization, baseline imbalances were observed in key metabolic variables, raising concerns about residual confounding, even though ANCOVA was applied to mitigate these differences. Third, the open-label design may have introduced performance bias, as participants were aware of group allocation in this behavioral intervention. Fourth, lifestyle behaviors such as diet, physical activity, and self-care practices were neither objectively measured nor systematically self-reported. Although participants were instructed to maintain their usual habits, unmeasured behavioral changes cannot be excluded, and therefore the mechanisms underlying observed metabolic changes cannot be determined. Additionally, adherence was assessed primarily through app engagement and self-reported compliance, which may be subject to reporting bias and does not provide objective evidence of behavior modification. The short follow-up period further limits assessment of durability and long-term clinical relevance. Taken together, these limitations preclude causal inference and require that findings be interpreted cautiously. The results should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Future multicenter studies with larger, more diverse samples, longer follow-up, assessor blinding where feasible, stratified randomization, and objective measurements of lifestyle behaviors and metabolic outcomes (e.g., continuous glucose monitoring) are needed to confirm and extend these observations.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that a three-month remote nurse-led education and follow-up program is associated with improvements in key glycemic markers (FBG, HbA1c) and metabolic indices (HOMA-IR, HOMA-α, HOMA-β) in patients with T2D. These findings highlight the potential of remote nurse-led interventions to support more personalized and mechanistically informed diabetes management. By demonstrating how structured remote nurse-led education can influence both glycemic control and underlying metabolic functions, this study underscores the promise of integrating telehealth-based educational programs into broader strategies for optimizing T2D care and enhancing patient engagement.
