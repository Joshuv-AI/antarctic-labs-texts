Atypical Biliary Fistula After Revisional Bariatric Surgery: A Case Report
Authors: Venkata Pavan Kumar Karanam, Aarsh Mukeshbhai Panchal, Suresh Kumar Sepuri, Lakshmi Kumar Chalamarla, Phani Krishna Ravula
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObesity affects about 39% of the global population, and bariatric surgery is a leading treatment option. As the number of primary bariatric procedures increases, so does the demand for revisional bariatric surgeries (RBSs), which are often more complex and associated with increased risks of complications. This case report details an unusual complication of RBS that surgeons should be aware of. A 41-year-old male patient with morbid obesity, after an initial sleeve gastrectomy, experienced weight regain due to gastric pouch dilatation and underwent conversion to mini-gastric bypass. Postoperatively however, he presented with recurrent left subhepatic abscess. Evaluation revealed left ductal leak from undersurface of liver as cause of recurrent liver abscess. Surgical intervention in the form of left lateral sectionectomy, resolved the symptoms. This case underscores the complexities and potential complications associated with RBS, highlighting the need for meticulous adhesiolysis between stomach and left liver during revisional gastric surgeries.Keywords: Surgical revision, Bariatric surgeries, Abdominal abscess, Biliary fistula

INTRODUCTION

Approximately 28% of individuals who undergo bariatric procedures may require revisional bariatric surgery (RBS), although the majority experience favourable outcomes without the need for additional interventions [1]. RBS is typically indicated for complications such as weight regain, gastroesophageal reflux disease, stomal ulcers, or other sequelae of the initial bariatric procedure. These revisional surgeries are inherently more complex and carry a higher risk of complications due to adhesions and altered anatomical landmarks [2]. In this report, we present a rare case of a patient who developed a recurrent intra-abdominal abscess secondary to a biliary fistula originating from a segment 3 bile duct of the left lateral hepatic segment following RBS. We discuss the intraoperative factors that may contribute to such an outcome, highlight preventive strategies, and emphasize the diagnostic approach to this atypical presentation. Given the limited literature on this rare complication, this report aims to provide meaningful insights for surgeons managing similar cases in clinical practice.

CASE REPORT

A 41-year-old male with morbid obesity (body mass index [BMI] 43.3 kg/m2) underwent laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (SG) in 2015. He achieved a sustained weight loss of 75% excess body weight over 6 years. However, during the following 3 years, he experienced significant weight regain, reaching 92 kg (BMI 33.8 kg/m2). The patient had no comorbidities and denied alcohol or high-calorie food intake.

Despite initial advice to undergo dietary and fitness counselling, the patient opted for RBS, influenced by his sibling's success with a one-anastomosis astric bypass (OAGB). Preoperative imaging revealed gastric pouch dilatation. Following multidisciplinary evaluation by a dietician, endocrinologist, and psychologist, he was scheduled for sleeve revision and conversion to OAGB with a 200 cm biliopancreatic limb.

Intraoperatively, the gastric pouch was found to be densely adherent to the undersurface of the liver. After meticulous adhesiolysis, the pouch was delineated and revised over a 42 Fr bougie. A long gastric pouch was fashioned by transecting the antrum approximately 3 cm distal to the incisura. A side-to-side gastrojejunostomy measuring 45 mm was constructed with an anti-reflux hitch. The postoperative course was uneventful. An oral contrast study on postoperative day 2 ruled out any leak, and the patient was discharged on a liquid diet by postoperative day 3.

Two weeks after surgery, the patient presented with acute epigastric pain and high-grade fever. A computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a 15-cc fluid collection in the lesser sac. Although a staple line leak was suspected, a contrast study showed no evidence of leakage. Ultrasound-guided percutaneous drainage (PCD) yielded 10 mL of seropurulent fluid. Biochemical analysis of the drainage fluid was negative for bile and amylase. Culture grew Klebsiella pneumoniae, and culture-sensitive antibiotics were initiated. The patient's symptoms improved rapidly, and the drain was removed after 1 week.

One week later, he again presented with similar symptoms. A CT scan showed a collection at the same site (Fig. 1). Contrast-enhanced CT and high-definition upper endoscopy excluded any leak, fistula, or stomal ulcer. A second ultrasonography-guided PCD was placed (Fig. 2), draining seropurulent fluid with a daily output of 5–10 mL. This time, the PCD was maintained for 4 weeks to allow the development of a mature tract for further imaging.

A tubogram performed at 4 weeks revealed a fistulous communication with segment 3 bile duct (Fig. 3). The patient subsequently underwent a left lateral sectionectomy. The postoperative course was uneventful, with complete resolution of symptoms.

DISCUSSION

SG ranks as the second most common procedure requiring revisions after adjustable gastric banding, with revision rates varying between 14–37% [3]. The primary reason for revision is insufficient weight loss, which occurs in approximately 13.1% of cases [4]. Likewise, weight regain after SG is reported at around 27.8% [5]. This is mostly due to gastric pouch dilatation over a period of years. Popular options for revision of sleeve include—redo sleeve, Roux-en-Y gastric bypass & OAGB.

RBS is more complicated than primary metabolic and bariatric surgery due to adhesions and changes in anatomy, leading to higher overall morbidity [6]. These procedures often involve longer operation times and hospital stays, increased blood loss, a greater likelihood of requiring intensive care, and more postoperative complications, readmissions, and reoperations. Frequent complications include leaks, ileus, obstructions, and gastrointestinal (GI) haemorrhage. Among these, leaks and fistulas are significant and relatively common concerns following surgery. Leakage occurs in about 0.7% to 5% of cases and can lead to various types of fistulas (gastrocutaneous, gastropleural, gastrosplenic), which are rare complications [7]. Fistulas may arise in approximately 0.2% to 2.5% of patients [8]. Managing these complications often requires a tailored approach, including drainage of any abdominal collections, endoscopic interventions, or surgical repair.

Biliary complications following bariatric surgery, although relatively uncommon, can pose significant clinical challenges. These complications may arise due to rapid weight loss, altered anatomy, or surgical manipulation near the hepatobiliary structures [9]. Common biliary issues include cholelithiasis, biliary colic, and choledocholithiasis, often precipitated by the rapid mobilization of cholesterol during weight reduction. Less frequently, more serious complications such as bile leaks, biliary strictures, or injury to peripheral bile ducts may occur, particularly during revisional procedures where adhesions and distorted anatomy increase the technical difficulty [10]. Recognition of these rare complications is critical, as delayed diagnosis can lead to recurrent infections, abscess formation, or fistulae.

In this case, the patient developed a localized intra-abdominal abscess 2 weeks following RBS. The initial differential diagnosis included a minor leak from the staple line, an infected subhepatic hematoma, or a low-grade pancreatic leak, possibly related to capsular disruption during adhesiolysis of the gastric conduit. Given the patient's rapid clinical improvement after ultrasound-guided PCD, minimal drain output, non-bilious character of the fluid, and absence of leak on oral contrast imaging, the drain was removed after 1 week.

However, the patient’s readmission with a recurrent fluid collection at the same anatomical site heightened our concern for a persistent occult leak or ductal communication. Repeat evaluation with contrast-enhanced CT and high-definition endoscopy excluded gastrojejunal suture line leakage and the presence of a penetrating stomal ulcer. Additionally, the location of the collection—well away from the gastric and anastomotic sites—further supported an alternative etiology.

At this juncture, differential diagnoses were broadened to include lymphatic leakage or a minor bile leak. Based on prior surgical experience, we considered the possibility of a peripheral segmental bile duct injury, particularly from the left lateral sector (segment 3), where subcapsular bile ducts can be vulnerable during dense adhesiolysis near the gastric pouch and liver surfaces (Fig. 5).

Operative image showing subcapsular left lobe bile ducts (black arrows).

The critical step in reaching a definitive diagnosis was the decision to retain the drain despite minimal output (2–3 mL/day). In cases of very low-output leaks, especially from the biliary or pancreatic systems, continuous drainage into a mature fibrous tract may allow for subsequent diagnostic imaging via contrast injection—commonly referred to as a tubogram. This technique has been previously described as a valuable method to detect low-output fistulous communications involving the biliary tree, pancreas, or GI tract.

In our patient, the tubogram performed at 4 weeks successfully demonstrated a fistulous connection to a segment 3 bile duct. Due to altered GI anatomy following the OAGB, conventional endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) was not a feasible option. Although laparoscopically assisted ERCP has been reported as a workaround in such scenarios, therapeutic success in sealing small, peripheral bile ducts is often limited. Therefore, a definitive surgical approach was adopted. Left lateral sectionectomy, a relatively limited hepatic resection, was performed and resulted in complete symptom resolution and recovery.

Retrospectively, the fistula was likely secondary to subcapsular bile duct injury during adhesiolysis of the gastric pouch from the left lobe of the liver. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for atypical biliary leaks and demonstrates the utility of prolonged drain placement and delayed tubography in diagnosing low-volume ductal fistulae.

This case highlights the potential for injury to superficially located segment 3 bile ducts during adhesiolysis in revisional gastric surgeries, such as RBS or redo fundoplication. Dissection close to the liver capsule—often performed to avoid injury to the gastric serosa or left gastric vessels—can inadvertently damage these peripheral ducts. Surgeons should remain vigilant for minor bile leaks originating from the undersurface of the left hepatic lobe, in addition to the more commonly suspected staple line leaks, stomal ulcers, or pancreatic duct injuries.

In patients presenting with recurrent intra-abdominal collections following RBS, the possibility of a ductal bile leak from peripheral hepatic ducts should be considered. Prolonged retention of a percutaneous drain in cases of small, atypical collections can facilitate the formation of a mature tract, enabling diagnostic tubography to identify subtle visceral communications.To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of an atypical biliary fistula from a segment 3 duct following RBS, and it underscores the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in managing such rare complications.

In conclusion, RBS presents a challenging and intricate field. Achieving a sustainable weight loss while minimizing potential risks requires a careful and delicate approach. Given the altered anatomy and challenging surgical environment, surgeons must exercise heightened caution when considering redo bariatric surgeries. Atypical collections following revisional upper GI surgeries should raise the suspicion of bile leak from superficially located ducts in left liver.
