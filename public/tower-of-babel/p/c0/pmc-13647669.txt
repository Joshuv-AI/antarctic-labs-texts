Spatial accessibility and outpatient utilization among children aged 1–5 years in South Korea: a nationwide claims-based cross-sectional study, 2023
Authors: Jae Yong Dong, Jee Hye Im, Myung Bae Park, Chang Woo Shon, Joung Hwan Back, Kwang-Soo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSouth Korea is experiencing the fastest fertility decline among Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries, with the total fertility rate reaching 0.75 in 2024, resulting in demographic changes and challenges in the distribution of the pediatric healthcare workforce. Although national policies have attempted to strengthen pediatric care through financial incentives and the expansion of Moonlight Children’s Hospitals (MCHs), no nationwide patient-level study has examined how realized travel burden is associated with pediatric outpatient utilization.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess nationwide spatial accessibility to pediatric outpatient services and to examine its association with outpatient utilization among children aged 1–5 years in South Korea.MethodsThis nationwide cross-sectional study analyzed 2023 National Health Insurance claims data for 1,116,152 children, encompassing 17,731,015 outpatient visits. Geographic accessibility was measured using network-based travel distance and travel time from township-level population centroids to healthcare providers, including clinics, hospitals, and general hospitals. Negative binomial regression and multiple linear regression models were employed to examine the associations between travel distance, travel time, outpatient visit frequency, and total medical expenditures, adjusting for age, sex, insurance type, residential region, after-hours care visits, and visits for mild diseases. Total outpatient expenditures were log-transformed prior to regression analysis.ResultsThe mean travel distance and travel time were 7.7 km and 10.5 min, respectively. Most outpatient visits occurred within 20 min of travel, with 64% occurring within 10 min. Each 1-km increase in average travel distance was associated with a 0.9% decrease in outpatient visit counts, and each 1-minute increase in travel time was associated with a 0.9% decrease in visit counts (P < 0.001). Longer travel distance and time were also associated with lower total annual outpatient expenditures. Children residing in rural counties experienced longer travel distances and times and lower outpatient utilization than those living in urban areas. Larger healthcare facilities were associated with longer travel distances and times.ConclusionsGeographic accessibility was significantly associated with pediatric outpatient care utilization in South Korea. Despite universal health coverage, disparities persist between rural and urban areas, underscoring the need for targeted policy interventions. These findings may inform geographically targeted pediatric workforce and facility planning aimed at improving equity in realized access to outpatient care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15066-0.Keywords: Pediatric, Spatial accessibility, Outpatient utilization, Travel distance, Travel time

Introduction

South Korea is experiencing the fastest fertility decline among Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries. In 2024, the national fertility rate declined to 0.75, less than half of the OECD average of 1.51 [1]. This rapid decline has led to substantial demographic changes, including population aging and a reduction in the population under 18 years of age [2, 3]. This demographic contraction has also influenced the healthcare delivery system, including the overall supply of medical institutions. Concurrently, the number of medical institutions has decreased alongside population decline [4, 5]. Interest in pediatrics as a medical specialty has also declined among medical students, resulting in growing challenges in securing an adequate pediatric workforce [6, 7]. These demographic and workforce shifts raise concerns regarding the equitable distribution and long-term sustainability of pediatric healthcare services.

In response to these challenges, the Ministry of Health and Welfare (MOHW) announced a Pediatric Healthcare System Enhancement Plan aimed at strengthening the pediatric workforce and addressing increasing demand for pediatric medical services [8]. This plan includes policy incentives for pediatric care and the expansion of Moonlight Children’s Hospitals (MCHs), which provide outpatient services during nighttime hours, weekends, and public holidays. Because pediatric patients frequently visit emergency departments after 6 PM [9, 10], the expansion of MCHs seeks to reduce unnecessary nighttime emergency department utilization. In addition, the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) introduced an after-hours care management fee for physicians working at MCHs, along with an additional reimbursement when pediatric specialists treat children aged 0–5 years [11]. However, whether these policy initiatives have translated into improved geographic accessibility and actual outpatient utilization remains unclear.

Despite these policy efforts, challenges persist in ensuring equitable geographic accessibility to pediatric care across regions in South Korea. Although the universal national health insurance system substantially reduces financial barriers, pediatric providers and healthcare facilities remain disproportionately concentrated in metropolitan areas [12, 13]. Previous studies have shown that longer travel distances and travel times are associated with lower primary care utilization and higher rates of avoidable hospitalization among pediatric patients [14, 15]. These challenges are not unique to South Korea. Similar trends have been reported in Japan, where declining child populations, shortages of physicians in rural areas, and widening geographic disparities in healthcare access have been increasingly documented [16–18]. However, Japan has implemented integrated care delivery systems linking clinics and general hospitals, along with policies designed to improve healthcare access for children living in regions with limited pediatric resources [19]. These international experiences suggest that geographic accessibility may play a critical role in shaping pediatric healthcare utilization patterns.

In contrast, evidence on how geographic accessibility affects actual outpatient utilization among pediatric patients in South Korea remains limited. Most prior studies on healthcare accessibility have estimated travel distance or travel time at the regional level or have focused primarily on emergency care, rather than examining individual-level outpatient utilization using patient-level data [20, 21]. To our knowledge, no nationwide study in South Korea has directly linked patient-level realized travel distance and travel time with observed outpatient visit frequency and expenditures among young children. To address this gap, the present study analyzed nationwide pediatric claims data to estimate individual travel distances and travel times to healthcare providers and to assess their associations with outpatient service utilization, including visit frequency and outpatient expenditures. By directly linking spatial accessibility measures with real-world utilization data, this study provides robust evidence on the role of geographic accessibility in pediatric outpatient care in South Korea.

Methods and materials

Data source and study population

This nationwide cross-sectional observational study integrated national health insurance claims data with geographic datasets to assess spatial accessibility and pediatric outpatient care utilization. Geographic datasets included the 2022 national road network and average travel speed data from the Korea Transport Institute National Transportation Database, 1-km grid population data from Statistics Korea’s Statistical Geographic Information Service, and 2022 administrative boundary shapefiles.

Healthcare utilization data were obtained from the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA) national claims database, which contains reimbursement claims submitted by healthcare providers for services delivered to National Health Insurance (NHI) beneficiaries under Korea’s universal coverage system [22]. The study included all outpatient pediatric claims submitted between January 1 and December 31, 2023, from clinics, hospitals, and general hospitals with non-zero reimbursed medical costs. In the Korean healthcare system, clinics generally refer to physician-based outpatient facilities that primarily provide primary care services, whereas hospitals and general hospitals provide inpatient care and more specialized services. General hospitals are larger institutions that meet statutory requirements regarding bed capacity and the number of clinical departments. Tertiary hospitals represent the highest level of referral care and were excluded because they primarily provide highly specialized services, making routine outpatient accessibility less relevant for this analysis.

Residential information in the claims database is available only at the township level (Eup, Myeon, or Dong). Because exact household addresses are unavailable due to privacy regulations, a population-based centroid was defined for each township to approximate residential locations. Specifically, the centroid of the 1-km grid cell with the highest population within each township was used. This approach has been used in prior spatial accessibility studies to reduce exposure misclassification when individual address data are unavailable. This population-based centroid served as the origin point for the network analysis, enabling estimation of travel distance and time for each outpatient visit.

Children were eligible for inclusion if they were aged 1–5 years and had identifiable township-level residential information. Infants younger than 1 year were excluded because individual-level patient identifiers were not consistently available in the claims data, preventing reliable linkage between residential location and healthcare utilization. Additional exclusions were applied to townships with missing residential information, fewer than 10 children, fewer than 50 annual pediatric outpatient claims, and island locations without continuous internal road network connections. The final analytic sample consisted of 1,116,152 pediatric patients and 17,731,015 outpatient claims. All data were fully anonymized prior to release, and institutional approval was obtained from the HIRA Institutional Review Board (HIRAIRB-2024-056-003).

Spatial accessibility and network analysis

Spatial accessibility was defined as the geographic relationship between patient residences and healthcare provider locations, incorporating both transportation and distance [23–25]. In this study, spatial accessibility was measured using a distance-based approach. Although distance-based measures may be derived from either Euclidean or network distances [26], a network-based method was employed to better represent real-world travel conditions.

Network analysis was conducted using ArcGIS Pro. The 2022 national road network and average travel speed layers were imported and used to construct the network dataset. Travel distance and travel time between population-based centroids and healthcare providers visited by patients were calculated using the Origin–Destination (OD) cost matrix function, which estimates distances and travel times between multiple origins and destinations based on road network data [27, 28].

For each patient, annual average travel distance and travel time were calculated across all outpatient visits during the one-year study period. Travel mode was assumed to be private automobile use. Travel times of 60 min or longer were excluded as outliers, as such values are unlikely to reflect routine outpatient care and may indicate address misclassification or non-residential travel. This threshold was determined based on the empirical distribution of travel times and was intended to minimize the influence of extreme observations on model estimates. A total of 476,171 outpatient claims (2.6%) were excluded under this criterion. A 60-minute threshold has been applied in national assessments of healthcare accessibility in Korea, and the present study adopted a comparable criterion [21].

Previous studies have frequently relied on potential accessibility measures, such as the two-step floating catchment area (2SFCA) method, which assess accessibility from a supply-to-demand perspective [25, 29]. However, such measures do not capture the actual travel burden experienced by individual patients. Accordingly, this study focused on realized accessibility based on patient-level travel distance and travel time, adopting a patient-centered approach to spatial accessibility assessment. Because realized accessibility is derived from the providers actually visited, it reflects a joint outcome of geographic proximity, provider availability, and patient choice. Therefore, the accessibility measures used in this study should be interpreted as behavioral accessibility indicators rather than purely exogenous geographic constraints.

Independent and dependent variables

The independent variables were annual average travel distance and annual average travel time, calculated for each patient based on all outpatient visits during the study period [30, 31].

The dependent variables were the total number of outpatient visits and total annual outpatient medical expenditures in 2023 [32, 33]. Total outpatient visits and expenditures were defined as the sum of all outpatient visits and reimbursed medical costs incurred by each patient during 2023. Patients with a single outpatient visit and corresponding medical expenditure were also included in the analysis.

Control variables

Based on prior research, six control variables were included: age, sex, insurance type, residential region, number of after-hours care visits, and number of visits for mild diseases [32–39]. Age was restricted to children aged 1–5 years, and sex was coded as male or female. Insurance type was categorized as National Health Insurance or Medical Aid. Residential region was classified into four administrative categories based on 2,954 townships: special city (Seoul), metropolitan cities (six large metropolitan municipalities), cities (Si), and counties (Gun).

After-hours care visits were defined as outpatient visits occurring after 6 PM on weekdays, on weekends or public holidays, or at Moonlight Children’s Hospitals (MCHs). The number of after-hours care visits was categorized into four groups (0, 1, 2, and ≥ 3 visits). Mild disease visits were defined based on 105 conditions specified in Appendix 6 (Article 6) of the Ministry of Health and Welfare Notification No. 2024 − 275 [39] (see Additional file 1). The number of mild disease visits was categorized into four groups (0, 1, 2, and ≥ 3 visits). After-hours care visits and visits for mild diseases were included as covariates to reflect clinically relevant differences in outpatient care use among young children. Young children commonly seek care for acute, relatively mild conditions, such as fever and respiratory symptoms, and may also require medical care during nighttime hours, weekends, or public holidays. Therefore, these variables were included to adjust for differences in clinical and utilization characteristics that may influence overall outpatient utilization.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using a negative binomial regression model for outpatient visit counts and multiple linear regression for total outpatient medical expenditures. Overdispersion of outpatient visit counts was assessed by comparing the mean and variance of annual outpatient visits. The mean number of outpatient visits was 15.9, whereas the variance was approximately 259.2, indicating that the variance substantially exceeded the mean. Therefore, negative binomial regression was selected rather than Poisson regression.

Total outpatient medical expenditures exhibited right-skewed distributions; therefore, a natural logarithmic transformation was applied prior to model estimation to improve the distributional properties of the residuals. Because travel distance and travel time were correlated measures of spatial accessibility, separate regression models were estimated for each accessibility variable. Model fit was assessed using deviance statistics for the count models and adjusted R² for the expenditure models. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide version 7.15. Two sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings. First, we repeated the primary models after excluding children with hospitalization history before the study period to evaluate whether the associations were influenced by children with potentially greater baseline healthcare needs. Second, although the primary models estimated population-averaged associations at the individual level, we re-estimated the primary models using township-clustered robust standard errors to account for potential within-township correlation. Multilevel or spatial models were not implemented because the primary objective was to estimate national-level population-averaged associations between realized travel burden and outpatient utilization. The results of these sensitivity analyses are presented in Additional file 3.

Impact of spatial accessibility on outpatient healthcare utilization

Because travel distance and travel time were highly correlated, separate regression models were estimated for each accessibility measure (Tables 3 and 4). In the negative binomial model, each 1-km increase in average travel distance was associated with a 0.9% decrease in outpatient visit counts (incidence rate ratio [IRR] = 0.991). Similarly, each 1-minute increase in average travel time corresponded to an approximately 0.9% reduction in visit counts (IRR = 0.991). In the expenditure model (log-transformed outcome), a 1-km increase in travel distance was associated with an approximately 0.2% decrease in total annual outpatient expenditures, and a 1-minute increase in travel time was associated with a 0.1% decrease in expenditures. All covariates were statistically significant in the visit model, and most remained significant in the expenditure model. Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the main findings. After excluding children with hospitalization history before the study period, 792,591 children remained in the analytic sample, and greater travel distance and travel time remained associated with lower outpatient visit counts (IRR = 0.9906, 95% CI: 0.9904–0.9908; IRR = 0.9911, 95% CI: 0.9909–0.9913, respectively) and lower log-transformed outpatient expenditures. Models using township-clustered robust standard errors also yielded consistent results. The detailed results are presented in Additional file 3: Tables S2–S5.

Discussion

This study examined spatial accessibility among pediatric outpatients aged 1–5 years and its association with outpatient healthcare utilization in South Korea. Utilizing national claims data, we constructed utilization-based travel distance and travel time measures to reflect realized access. In 2023, the average travel burden from patients’ residences to healthcare facilities was 7.7 km and 10.5 min. Although direct comparisons with international studies are limited due to differences in healthcare systems and measurement approaches, these findings are broadly consistent with prior evidence. Previous studies in Korea have reported that most pediatric patients access healthcare facilities within 20 min [40, 41], and similar patterns have been observed in other high-income countries, including the United States and Japan [30, 31]. Taken together, these results suggest that pediatric spatial accessibility in South Korea is generally comparable to that of other high-income settings.

Beyond overall accessibility levels, the association between geographic access and service utilization provides additional insight into how spatial factors shape pediatric care use. In the regression analysis, each 1-km increase in average travel distance was associated with a 0.9% decrease in outpatient visit counts (IRR = 0.991). Although the marginal effect per 1-km increase is small, a 10-km difference corresponds to an approximately 9% reduction in annual outpatient visits based on the estimated coefficient. While the marginal effect per unit increase appears modest, cumulative differences across larger geographic gradients may translate into meaningful population-level disparities in service utilization. These findings align with prior studies demonstrating that greater geographic accessibility is associated with higher healthcare utilization [42–44]. From a policy perspective, these results suggest that geographic proximity may be an important factor associated with outpatient utilization patterns, particularly in regions with substantial rural–urban differences in accessibility. Although the findings are associative rather than causal due to the cross-sectional design and the use of realized accessibility measures, the consistent direction of the associations across both distance- and time-based models supports the robustness of the observed relationship between travel burden and outpatient utilization. Because the realized accessibility measures used in this study were based on providers actually visited by patients, they should be interpreted as indicators of realized travel burden rather than as measures of geographic constraints alone. Realized accessibility may reflect not only physical proximity, but also provider availability, parental care-seeking preferences, perceived quality of care, and clinical need.

An important methodological contribution of this study is the application of a utilization-based measure of spatial accessibility that reflects realized, rather than potential, access. While the two-step floating catchment area (2SFCA) method has been widely used to estimate potential accessibility in contexts where utilization data are unavailable [45, 46], the network-based approach employed in this study leverages actual claims data to capture real-world patient travel behavior. By grounding accessibility estimates in observed utilization, this approach provides a more patient-centered assessment of access to care and offers a policy-relevant complement to traditional supply-based indicators.

The patterns observed for the control variables were largely consistent with existing literature. Age- and sex-related utilization patterns aligned with established trends in pediatric healthcare use [34, 35]. Medical Aid beneficiaries exhibited higher visit frequencies, whereas National Health Insurance beneficiaries incurred higher total expenditures, likely reflecting differences in cost-sharing mechanisms within the Korean health insurance system. In addition, children receiving after-hours care demonstrated higher utilization, consistent with prior evidence that expanded access to after-hours primary care is associated with increased service use [47]. The observed association between higher utilization and visits for mild conditions further supports previous findings that a substantial proportion of pediatric outpatient care involves relatively low-severity illnesses [48, 49].

Notably, children residing in rural areas exhibited lower outpatient utilization compared to their urban counterparts, even after adjusting for individual- and visit-level factors. This finding is consistent with earlier studies documenting the concentration of healthcare resources in metropolitan regions [48, 50] and highlights persistent geographic disparities in access to pediatric outpatient care. Regional differences in travel burden may partly reflect the uneven geographic distribution of pediatric healthcare resources. Children living in counties and other less urbanized regions may have fewer nearby clinics, hospitals, and healthcare providers offering pediatric outpatient care than those living in urban areas. As pediatric healthcare resources are often concentrated in more urbanized areas, rural residents may need to travel farther for outpatient care, contributing to longer realized travel distances and travel times. Importantly, these findings suggest that, despite universal health insurance coverage, geographic factors may still be associated with differences in outpatient utilization. Given the observed rural–urban disparities in travel burden and utilization, policy responses should consider geographic distribution of pediatric resources. From a policy standpoint, targeted interventions—such as incentives for pediatric providers to practice in underserved regions, expansion of public pediatric clinics, or the strategic use of telemedicine—may be considered in areas where lower utilization coincides with greater travel burden. However, given the cross-sectional design of this study, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that improving geographic accessibility alone would necessarily increase utilization.

The results of this study also have implications for how pediatric underserved areas are identified and managed in South Korea. Current designation criteria rely primarily on inpatient utilization and predefined travel time thresholds [51], with limited consideration of outpatient accessibility or primary pediatric care availability. The findings suggest that indicators based on actual patient travel distance and travel time may provide a more accurate and policy-relevant basis for identifying underserved pediatric areas. Incorporating utilization-based accessibility measures into national monitoring frameworks could support more targeted allocation of public resources, workforce planning, and decisions regarding facility designation or certification.

The robustness of these associations was supported by additional sensitivity analyses. After excluding children with hospitalization history before the study period, the associations between travel distance, travel time, and outpatient utilization remained consistent with the main findings. Analyses using township-clustered robust standard errors also yielded similar results, suggesting that the main conclusions were not materially affected by prior hospitalization history or potential within-township correlation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, patient residential locations were approximated using population-based centroids, which may have introduced spatial misclassification. Because exact household addresses were unavailable due to privacy regulations, some degree of measurement error in estimated travel distance and time is unavoidable; however, this misclassification is likely to be non-differential and may have biased the results toward the null. Second, the exclusion of sparsely populated or geographically isolated areas without continuous internal road network connections may limit the generalizability of the findings to highly remote settings. In addition, although the exclusion of visits with estimated travel times of 60 min or longer was intended to reduce the influence of potential address misclassification or non-routine travel, it may have excluded some visits reflecting genuine access barriers among children living in areas with very poor geographic access. Third, caregiver-level and household-level factors such as socioeconomic status, health literacy, and transportation availability were not available in the administrative claims data and therefore could not be incorporated into the analysis. In addition, although analyses were conducted at the individual level, unmeasured geographic clustering at the township level may have influenced variance estimates. To address this issue, we conducted sensitivity analyses using township-clustered robust standard errors, and the results remained consistent with the primary analyses. Nevertheless, future studies may further examine hierarchical or spatial dependence using multilevel or spatial regression models.

Furthermore, because the analytic sample excluded children without any outpatient visits during the study period, the findings pertain to the intensity of use among service users rather than the probability of initiating care. As a result, the estimated associations may underestimate the full impact of geographic barriers on pediatric outpatient access. The present analysis cannot determine whether lower outpatient utilization among children with greater travel burden reflects suppression of necessary care, reduced discretionary use for mild conditions, or the coexistence of both mechanisms. Because this study focused on overall outpatient utilization, the findings should not be interpreted as direct evidence of unmet healthcare need. In addition, this study did not assess downstream health outcomes such as delayed diagnosis, disease progression, emergency department utilization, hospitalization, or potentially avoidable hospitalization. Future research should examine condition-specific utilization and clinical outcomes more directly related to unmet need. Although sensitivity analyses excluding children with prior hospitalization supported the robustness of the findings, residual confounding by unmeasured disease severity, chronic conditions, and caregiver-level factors may remain. The definition of “mild diseases” used in this study was based on an administrative classification and includes a broad range of conditions; therefore, this classification was not specifically developed to identify clinically mild pediatric conditions among children aged 1–5 years. Because after-hours care visits and visits for mild diseases are closely related to healthcare use, the adjusted estimates should be interpreted as associations conditional on selected clinical and utilization characteristics rather than as total effects of spatial accessibility.

Finally, because travel distance and travel time were calculated based on the providers actually visited, residual endogeneity related to patient preference, illness severity, or provider selection cannot be fully excluded. Reverse causation where children with greater healthcare needs may selectively travel farther to access higher-level facilities remains possible. However, the consistently negative association observed between travel burden and outpatient utilization does not suggest that the findings were primarily driven by increased long-distance care-seeking among children with greater healthcare needs. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting patterns of realized access rather than causal effects of geographic barriers. Future longitudinal or quasi-experimental studies may further clarify causal pathways between spatial accessibility and pediatric healthcare utilization.

By linking nationwide claims data with network-based accessibility measures, this study provides one of the largest empirical assessments of realized pediatric spatial accessibility in an OECD country, offering a population-scale evidence base for future policy evaluation.

Conclusion

This nationwide claims-based cross-sectional study examined realized spatial accessibility to pediatric outpatient services among children aged 1–5 years in South Korea and its association with healthcare utilization. Greater realized travel distance and travel time were associated with lower outpatient visit counts and lower outpatient expenditures. These findings highlight the policy importance of incorporating geographic considerations into pediatric healthcare planning while recognizing that realized accessibility reflects not only geographic proximity but also provider availability, patient choice, and healthcare-seeking behavior. Strengthening community-based pediatric services and addressing pediatric underserved areas may contribute to improving equity in realized access to outpatient care; however, longitudinal and quasi-experimental studies are needed to clarify causal relationships and downstream clinical consequences.
