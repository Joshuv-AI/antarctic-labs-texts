Severe respiratory complications in late-diagnosed Prader–Willi syndrome: a case report
Authors: Surya Kant Tiwari, Poonam Joshi, Nihar Ranjan Mishra, Rimjhim Sonowal, Niladri Sekhar Bhunia

Background

Prader–Willi Syndrome (PWS), also known as Prader–Labhart–Willi syndrome, is a rare, complex neuroendocrine genetic disorder with an estimated global prevalence of 1 in 25,000–30,000 live births [1]. PWS results from abnormalities in the paternal chromosome 15 (15q11.2-q13), causing hypothalamic dysfunction, which leads to hyperphagia, obesity, and behavioral issues [2]. Children with PWS often exhibit developmental delays, cognitive impairment, and behavioral challenges such as anxiety and temper tantrums. Multidisciplinary management, including medical, nursing, psychological, and dietary interventions, is essential for improving outcomes. Despite guidelines for early diagnosis, late-diagnosed PWS cases still arise, posing unique management challenges and severe, often life-threatening complications, particularly respiratory and metabolic consequences stemming from uncontrolled obesity [3]. We report the case of a 17-year-old male individual diagnosed with PWS at 15 years of age to illustrate the severe complications that can develop owing to challenges in long-term management and follow-up, culminating in a fatal respiratory failure.

Case presentation

A 17-year-old male patient of Asian ethnicity was admitted to the emergency department (day 1 of current admission) with a 15-day history of generalized swelling and decreased urine output, followed by 7 days of difficulty in breathing, snoring, and profound daytime sleepiness. His presentation was the culmination of a long and complex medical history (Fig. 1).

He was born prematurely at 7 months via Caesarean section to nonconsanguineous parents. His early postnatal period was uneventful, and he had no significant health issues until the age of 4 years, when his parents noticed an insidious onset of hyperphagia and subsequent weight gain. Concurrently, the patient experienced breathing difficulties during sleep and daytime somnolence. He was always quiet and socially withdrawn, which led him to drop out of school in the 9th grade.

His medical history included a cholecystectomy for cholelithiasis at age 14 years. The diagnosis of PWS was finally confirmed via multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) at the age of 15 years, after a referral to a private tertiary care facility for congestive heart failure revealed the classic phenotypic features. The diagnosis was delayed until adolescence, likely owing to the lack of recognition of early signs (neonatal hypotonia and feeding difficulties may have been overlooked owing to prematurity) and the subsequent attribution of his obesity and developmental delays to other causes, a known challenge in resource-constrained primary care settings. He was diagnosed and treated for type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension at our facility 2 years before the current presentation, with a documented baseline blood pressure of 200/110 mmHg.

The family was from a low socioeconomic background, with the father working as a daily wage agricultural laborer and the mother as a housewife. The patient was one of two children and was affected by PWS. The immense daily burden of managing the patient’s hyperphagia, behavioral challenges, and complex medical regimen placed significant psychological and physical strain on the family caregivers, a burden exacerbated by their limited resources and lack of access to structured support services.

On admission, the patient was conscious. His blood pressure was 125/80 mmHg, but he was in clear respiratory distress with a respiratory rate of 28/minute and severe hypoxia (oxygen saturation of 86% on room air). His weight was 96 kg, with a body mass index (BMI) of 40.63 kg/m2, indicating morbid obesity. Physical examination revealed bilateral pitting pedal edema (2+), bilateral lung crepitations in the infrascapular region, and underdeveloped gonads (Tanner stage I). Neurological examination revealed normal higher mental function and intact cranial nerves. While muscle power was recorded as 5/5, functional strength was markedly poor because of severe deconditioning and obesity.

Diagnostic investigations included echocardiography, which revealed good biventricular function (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] 57%) with no pulmonary hypertension and a collapsible inferior vena cava (IVC). Hormonal assays indicated hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (luteinizing hormone [LH], 0.01 mIU/mL; follicle-stimulating hormone [FSH], 0.48 mIU/mL). The serum cortisol and thyroid profiles were normal. The fasting blood glucose level was 149 mg/dL, and the hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level was elevated at 8.5%. The urine protein level was 1140 mg/24 hours, and abdominal ultrasound confirmed grade 2 fatty liver disease. A clinical and radiological diagnosis of pneumonia was established.

The patient was immediately started on noninvasive ventilation (NIV) in the bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) mode, with initial settings of inspiratory positive airway pressure (IPAP) 15 cm H2O, expiratory positive airway pressure (EPAP) 7 cm H2O, and fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) 40%, which was titrated down to 21% as he stabilized. Empiric intravenous antibiotic therapy with ceftriaxone (2 g daily) was initiated for community-acquired pneumonia; de-escalation was not possible owing to his rapid deterioration. His medication regimen was optimized to include metformin and a glucagon-like peptide-1 agonist (GLP-1a) analog for diabetes. Despite normal blood pressure on admission, enalapril was continued for renal protection in the context of significant proteinuria and a known history of hypertension. Nebulization with salbutamol and budesonide was administered, and a strict low-calorie diet was followed. Physical and speech therapy were scheduled to address functional strength and communication issues. Growth hormone (GH) therapy was considered after his initial diagnosis at age 15 years but was deferred owing to the severity of his pre-existing obesity and uncontrolled respiratory complications. Formal polysomnography was indicated but could not be performed owing to the patient’s critical condition and lack of immediate access. Bariatric surgery was recommended but deemed exceedingly high risk because of his severe cardiorespiratory compromise.

Following initial stabilization and discharge, the patient was lost to formal follow-up over the next 3 months. According to parental reports during his final admission 3 months later, his condition at home had progressively deteriorated, with worsening somnolence and respiratory effort. Compliance with the prescribed noninvasive ventilation and medication regimen was inconsistent owing to profound socioeconomic and educational challenges, including financial constraints and difficulties in understanding complex home care instructions.

He presented to the emergency department with acute respiratory distress and cyanosis. His oxygen saturation was critically low at 46% on room air. Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis showed severe mixed respiratory and metabolic acidosis (pH 7.17, PCO2 68.5 mmHg, PO2 28.2 mmHg, HCO3 18.8 mmol/L). Despite aggressive treatment, including maximum noninvasive ventilation and antibiotics, the patient’s condition deteriorated. Chest radiography revealed bilateral lung opacity. The patient experienced sudden cardiorespiratory arrest and could not be resuscitated. The immediate cause of death was severe pneumonia with acute respiratory failure.

Discussion

This tragic case of a 17-year-old male patient with PWS diagnosed at age 15 years, culminating in fatal respiratory failure, serves as a stark illustration of the devastating consequences of delayed diagnosis. This reinforces established knowledge on PWS complications while highlighting a critical gap in real-world applications, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Our patient’s course aligns with the well-documented trajectory of PWS, where hyperphagia leads to morbid obesity, predisposing individuals to obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), obesity hypoventilation syndrome, and metabolic derangements [4, 5]. The prevalence of severe OSA in PWS is exceedingly high, reported in up to 80% of adolescents and adults, and is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, often contributing to sudden death [6–8]. Our case is a quintessential example of this fatal disease progression.

However, the profound novelty and instructional value of this case lie in the extreme delay in diagnosis and the insurmountable socioeconomic barriers that followed. While the literature extensively describes the ideal multidisciplinary management of PWS [9], few case reports detail the challenges of implementing this care when a diagnosis is made after severe complications have occurred [10, 11]. The decision to defer GH therapy in this adolescent with advanced disease was based on a risk–benefit assessment, weighing the potential benefits against the known risks of exacerbating pre-existing severe OSA and respiratory failure in the context of morbid obesity. The delay until adolescence meant that our patient missed the critical window for early GH therapy, which has been shown to improve body composition and linear growth and potentially reduce the severity of OSA in children with PWS [12, 13].

The management of our patient’s respiratory complications further underscores the challenges faced in resource-limited environments. The diagnosis of OSA was clinical and radiological, as polysomnography, the gold standard recommended in management guidelines, was unavailable [14]. Parents’ refusal of home BiPAP therapy, a decision likely rooted in financial constraints and educational barriers, represents a catastrophic failure of the support system. This contrasts with the successful management of PWS [3, 15] and emphasizes that effective multidisciplinary care must be adaptable to families’ socioeconomic realities.

The cause of death in this case was multifactorial. Although the immediate terminal event was respiratory failure due to pneumonia, this was merely the final step in a pathway paved by severe, untreated OSA, obesity hypoventilation, and metabolic syndrome. The multifactorial nature of mortality in PWS has been well described. A retrospective study of the nationwide French Reference Center for PWS found that respiratory failure was the most common cause of death in both children and adults with PWS, often in the context of obesity and cardiorespiratory compromise [16].

Ultimately, this case study moves beyond reinforcing textbook knowledge. It provides a sobering, real-world counterpoint to the optimistic outcomes presented in the guideline documents. This demonstrates that without early diagnosis, which enables timely GH therapy, strict weight management, and respiratory surveillance, the disease course can be rapidly progressive and irreversible. Furthermore, it argues that a “multidisciplinary approach” must include social workers and financial counselors to be effective. This case should serve as a powerful call to action for improved awareness of PWS signs among primary care physicians and pediatricians to facilitate diagnosis in early childhood, before the onset of life-limiting complications.

Conclusion

This case report underscores the lethal potential of late-diagnosed PWS, where severe obesity-related complications such as respiratory failure can become irreversible. This emphasizes the critical need for early genetic diagnosis to enable the timely initiation of therapies, such as growth hormone therapy, strict weight management, and respiratory support. Furthermore, this case highlights that effective management must extend beyond a hospital’s walls and requires a multidisciplinary approach that is acutely aware of and adaptable to the psychosocial and socioeconomic realities of the patient’s family. Without this holistic and early intervention, the risk of catastrophic outcomes remains unacceptably high.
