Different Patterns of Frequency, Lichen Specificity and Thallus Location Between the Yeast and Filamentous Phases of Two Lichen‐Inhabiting Basidiomycetes
Authors: Ana M Millanes, Veera Tuovinen Nogerius, Sandra Freire‐Rallo, Paul Diederich, Juan Periáñez, Martin Westberg, Sonia Merinero, Hanna Johannesson, Mats Wedin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMany fungi have a dimorphic life cycle, alternating between unicellular yeast and multicellular filamentous phases. Although dimorphism is assumed for many lichen‐associated basidiomycetes, the existence of a yeast stage has rarely been confirmed. Using taxon‐specific PCR and FISH‐CLSM, we studied Tremella hypogymniae and T. tubulosae Tremellomycetes), two presumably dimorphic species previously known only from their filamentous phase in galls on the lichens Hypogymnia physodes and H. tubulosa , respectively. We investigated their presence and frequency, lichen ranges and within‐thallus distribution of life‐cycle stages. We also explored the co‐occurrence of both species with Cystobasidiomycetes—one of the most widespread lichen‐associated yeast lineages—in the same lichen thalli. The filamentous phase of Tremella hypogymniae and T. tubulosae was confined to a single lichen species each, whereas the yeast phase occurred in several closely related lichens. Both phases co‐occurred with various Cystobasidiomycete lineages. Filamentous structures were restricted to galls, whereas gall‐free thalli contained Tremella yeasts in the cortex, soredia and medulla, and pseudohyphae in the cortex. The presence of yeasts in soredia suggests co‐dispersal with other lichen symbionts. These findings reveal narrow specificity in the filamentous phase but broader associations in the yeast phase, pointing to complex interactions within the lichen symbiosis.Keywords: Cystobasidiomycetes, dimorphic, fungi, life cycle, Tremella, Tremellales, Tremellomycetes

Introduction

Fungi are among the most ecologically diverse organisms, colonising a wide range of habitats and exhibiting a variety of lifestyles (Heitman et al. 2017). Many fungi form associations with other organisms, including bacteria, animals, plants, and other fungi (Vega & Blackwell, Vega and Blackwel 2005; Bonfante and Genre 2010; Saunders et al. 2012; Doehlemann et al. 2016; Grube and Wedin 2016; Hsueh and Blackwell 2024). Among these, dimorphic fungi—which alternate between unicellular yeast and multicellular filamentous (hyphal) stages in their life cycle—are particularly interesting in both pathogenic and symbiotic contexts.

In dimorphic fungal pathogens, the two phases typically serve distinct ecological roles (Lin 2009; Jacobsen et al. 2012; Boyce and Andrianopoulos 2015; Gauthier 2017; Kijpornyongpan and Aime 2020). In some mammalian pathogens such as Cryptococcus, virulence is associated with the yeast phase (Boyce and Andrianopoulos 2015; Xu et al. 2017; Muñoz et al. 2018; Van Dyke et al. 2019; Zhao and Lin 2021), whereas in others—such as Candida albicans —pathogenicity corresponds to hyphal growth (Bastidas and Heitman 2009; Chen et al. 2020). In fungi parasitizing plants or other fungi, infection is mostly linked to the filamentous phase, and the yeast‐to‐hypha transition is often critical to pathogenic development (Ruiz‐Herrera et al. 1995; Rodrigues and Fonseca 2003; Lin 2009; Pippola and Kytöviita 2009; Gauthier 2017; Boyce and Andrianopoulos 2015; Steinberg 2015).

The Tremellales (Tremellomycetes, Agaricomycotina) include many dimorphic species, ranging from well‐characterised jelly fungi—in which both the unicellular and filamentous phases are well‐known—to species where only one phase has been observed (Bandoni 1963, 1965, 1987; Liu et al. 2015; Wells 1994). Typically, haploid basidiospores germinate to form yeast colonies; upon mating, compatible yeast cells fuse to form a dikaryotic mycelium, which in turn produces basidiomata (Bandoni 1987; Fell et al. 2000). While the yeast phase of many Tremellales can grow in vitro under nutrient‐rich conditions, the development of the filamentous phase often requires a compatible host (Zugmaier et al. 1994; Zugmaier and Oberwinkler 1995; Bauer and Oberwinkler 2008).

A particularly intriguing group within the Tremellales is the lichen‐inhabiting species. About 130 described tremellalean fungi live in obligate association with lichens (Diederich et al. 2022). Their filamentous phase forms basidiomata—often in conspicuous galls on the lichen thallus (Figure 1)—and is highly specific towards the lichen. Species descriptions are traditionally based on characters of the filamentous phase, whereas the yeast phase has been much less studied, as it is rarely culturable. Nevertheless, basidiospores germinating by repetition—giving rise to yeast cells—have been reported and illustrated in Filobasidiales and Tremellales (Diederich 1996; Zamora et al. 2016). The scarcity of cultures and the difficulty of observing yeasts within the lichen thallus by light microscopy meant that dimorphism in lichen‐associated Tremellales was long inferred from molecular evidence and analogy with well‐studied species of jelly fungi, rather than directly demonstrated. More recently, however, fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) combined with confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) has shown that several lichen‐inhabiting species complete their entire life cycle within the thallus—producing both yeast and hyphal stages—thus confirming their dimorphism (Tuovinen et al. 2019, 2021).

All in all, a widespread occurrence of basidiomycete yeasts in lichens has been reported, sparking interest in their potential roles in the lichen symbiosis and in their lichen specificity (Prillinger et al. 1997; Spribille et al. 2016; Banchi et al. 2018; Tuovinen et al. 2019, 2021; Mark et al. 2020; Cometto et al. 2022; Freire‐Rallo 2023; Raudabaugh and Aime 2023; Pérez et al. 2024). These yeasts are either the asexual phase of described known filamentous species or independent lineages not yet linked to filamentous taxa. Lichen‐inhabiting basidiomycetes belong mainly to the Tremellomycetes (Biatoropsis, Tremella and Zyzygomyces) and Cystobasidiomycetes (Cyphobasidium) and can co‐occur within the same lichen thallus (Tagirdzhanova et al. 2021). It has been suggested that while the filamentous phases of lichen‐inhabiting Tremella and Cyphobasidium are highly lichen‐specific, their yeast stages may occupy a broader range of lichens (Fernández‐Mendoza et al. 2017; Mark et al. 2020; Tuovinen et al. 2021; Cometto et al. 2022; Freire‐Rallo 2023; Kirkhus et al. 2025). However, these patterns remain largely hypothetical, as systematic comparisons of lichen range between the two life‐cycle phases are lacking. Moreover, little is known about the extent to which different basidiomycete yeasts co‐occur or exclude one another within the same thallus.

In this work, we focus on two presumably dimorphic species, Tremella hypogymniae and T. tubulosae, whose filamentous phase is known only from galls on the thalli of Hypogymnia physodes and H. tubulosa , respectively (Diederich 1996; Diederich et al. 2020) (Figure 1A−D). While mature galls contain hyphae with clamps, basidia, and basidiospores (Figure 1E−K), the yeast stage has not been examined or confirmed, and neither of the two Tremella species has been successfully cultured.

Here, we used taxon‐specific primers—targeting Tremella hypogymniae and T. tubulosae—and screened a representative sample of lichens in the Parmeliaceae to (1) test whether the DNA of these two Tremella species—presumably from their yeast phase—can be detected in lichen samples lacking visible galls, (2) assess their occurrence across the Parmeliaceae and (3) evaluate whether Cyphobasidium and Tremella yeasts co‐occur or exclude one another within the same thallus. We used FISH‐CLSM to (4) confirm that the amplified DNA corresponds to the yeast phase of the studied species, and (5) infer whether yeast and hyphal phases occupy distinct structural niches within the lichen thallus.

By combining molecular and microscopic approaches, we aim to clarify the ecology and lichen specificity of dimorphic lichen‐inhabiting basidiomycetes, and to test the hypothesis that the yeast phase is less specific than the filamentous phase.

Materials and Methods

Lichen Selection and Sampling

We investigated the presence of hidden (i.e., not visible to the naked eye) Tremella and Cystobasidiomycetes in 439 specimens of 41 lichen species by PCR. The sampling included three groups: (1) Hypogymnia physodes and H. tubulosa, the two lichen species where the filamentous phases of Tremella hypogymniae and T. tubulosae are known to occur, respectively. These are widespread foliose lichens in the hypogymnioid clade of the Parmeliaceae, dispersing primarily through asexual soredia (Figure 1A,B). (2) A representative set of other species within the hypogymnioid clade of the Parmeliaceae (Crespo et al. 2007; Divakar et al. 2017, 2019), including Arctoparmelia centrifuga, A. incurva , Brodoa intestiniformis, Hypogymnia austerodes , H. bitteri , H. enteromorpha , H. farinacea , H. hultenii , H. cf. polycarpa, H. vittata , and Pseudevernia furfuracea. Finally (3) a selection of species representing the major non‐hypogymnioid clades of the Parmeliaceae (Crespo et al. 2007; Divakar et al. 2015, 2017; and Pizarro et al. 2018) (Tables S1 and S2). We collected lichens at 31 sites across the northern hemisphere (405 specimens). Additionally, we analysed herbarium specimens (23 sites, 26 specimens), sequences from available DNA extractions (4 sites, 5 specimens), and sequences obtained from Genbank (3 sites, 3 specimens). Altogether, the dataset comprised 439 specimens from 61 localities in 10 countries. Sampling intensity varied among sites, ranging from 1 to 125 specimens (Tables S1 and S2).

To examine the localisation of different life‐cycle phases of Tremella within the lichen thalli by FISH‐CLSM, we studied galls on Hypogymnia physodes and H. tubulosa as well as gall‐free lobes of the same thalli. We also conducted FISH on thalli without galls from H. physodes , H. farinacea , H. tubulosa and Pseudevernia furfuracea (Tables 1 and S2).

DNA Extraction, Amplification and Sequencing

DNA was extracted from specimens without galls, in many cases within one week, and in most cases within the first three months after collection, except for 26 herbarium specimens from which DNA was extracted two years after collection (Table S2). Prior to DNA extraction, specimens were carefully inspected for young thalli of overgrowing lichens. Fragments selected for DNA extraction were thoroughly cleaned with a soft brush to remove debris and potential fungal spores and soredia deposited on the surface. In initial pilot experiments, DNA was extracted separately from different parts of the thallus (i.e., fragments from central parts or near the tips, Table S2), but as no obvious differences were found in PCR amplification, we subsequently mixed different thallus parts in each DNA extraction to maximize detection probabilities of Tremella and Cystobasidiomycetes. We extracted DNA from 19 galls of Tremella hypogymniae (17) and T. tubulosae (2) (Table S2). In these cases, only the outer surface of the gall was used for DNA extraction—separated with a scalpel—to maximize the amount of Tremella hyphae included. When investigating the frequency of PCR detection, we used a homogeneous dataset consisting only of specimens (1) without galls and (2) with DNA extracted within three months of collection. This was based on preliminary observations suggesting that PCR amplification of tremellaean yeasts is more successful in specimens with galls and less successful in older collections.

We designed specific PCR primers to selectively amplify Tremella hypogymniae and T. tubulosae, avoiding other fungi. Suitable priming sites were identified by aligning available ITS and nuLSU rDNA sequences of T. hypogymniae and T. tubulosae against Parmeliaceae and other Tremella sequences, particularly lichenicolous taxa closely related to the two species of interest. Conserved fragments differing markedly—7 to 15 bp mismatches—from other tremellalean sequences were chosen in silico. However, despite 1–2 bp mismatches between T. hypogymniae and T. tubulosae in two primers, they did not discriminate between these species, and their distinction was only possible by sequencing. The Tremella‐specific primers were used in combination with general fungal primers: ITS1F (Gardes and Bruns 1993) was used with Th1R (5′ TCGGAAGCAAAAAGTATGAAGG 3′), and in a few cases with Th2R (5′ CACGTCAGGCCAGAAGGCTCGTT 3′) to amplify ITS1, the 5.8 rDNA gene, and ITS2. Occasionally the primer Th1F (5′ ATCGGCCTTCAAATGCAAATCCTTT 3′) was used in combination with BasidLSU3‐3 (Millanes et al. 2011) to amplify ITS1, the 5.8 rDNA gene, ITS2, and a fragment of approximately 400 bp of the nLSU rDNA gene. Occasionally, the primers ThLSU1F and ThLSU2F (5′ CATAACCCTTCATACTTTTTGCTTCC 3′; 5′ CCCTTCATACTTTTTGCTTCC 3′), were used in combination with LR5 (Vilgalys and Hester 1990) to amplify a fragment of approximately 900 bp in the nLSU rDNA gene. PCR amplifications were performed using Illustra Hot Start PCR beads, according to the manufacturer's instructions, with the following settings: for the primer pairs ITS1F/Th1R, ITS1F/Th2R and TLSU2F/LR5, the PCR settings consisted of initial denaturing at 95°C for 5 min, four cycles (95°C for 40 s, 53°C for 40 s and 72°C for 90 s), four cycles (95°C for 30 s, 50°C for 30 s and 72°C for 90 s), and finally 32 cycles (95°C for 30 s, 47°C for 30 s and 72°C for 90 s) with a final extension at 72°C for 480 s. For the primer pair TLSU2F/LR5, the PCR settings consisted of initial denaturing at 95°C for 5 min, four cycles (95°C for 40 s, 54°C for 40 s and 72°C for 90 s), four cycles (95°C for 30 s, 51°C for 30 s and 72°C for 90 s), and finally 32 cycles (95°C for 30 s, 48°C for 30 s and 72°C for 90 s) with a final extension at 72°C for 480 s. To detect the presence of Cystobasidiomycetes, we amplified ITS1, the 5.8 rDNA gene, and ITS2 using the primers ITS_symrho_1F and LR0_symrho_R (Spribille et al. 2016). Occasionally, we used the primers LR0_symrhoF and Cb_Sc264_R (Spribille et al. 2016) to amplify the D1/D2 domains of the nuSLU. In both cases, we followed PCR conditions described by the same authors. Before sequencing, the PCR products were purified with Exo‐sap‐ITTM (USB Corporation, Cleveland, Ohio, USA). Both strands of the purified samples were sequenced by Macrogen (Madrid, Spain, or Amsterdam, the Netherlands) using the amplification primers. Sequences were assembled and edited using Sequencher—versions 4.6 (Geneodes Corporation, Ann Arbor, Michigan) or Geneious Prime—2021.0.3 (https://www.geneious.com).We recorded the presence of a yeast species when sequences of sufficient quality (≥ 90% similarity in BLAST) were obtained.

Co‐Occurrence of Tremella and Cystobasidiomycetes

To test whether the presence of tremellalean and cystobasidiomycete yeasts was positively or negatively correlated, we estimated the degree of association between the presence of Tremella hypogymniae or T. tubulosae and the presence of Cystobasidiomycetes using φ correlations in contingency tables, implemented in the psych package (Revelle 2024) in R Statistical Software (v4.2.2; R Core Team 2021). Significance of associations was assessed using chi‐square tests. To avoid heterogeneity, we used only specimens without galls and with DNA extracted within three months of collection. Correlations were tested for all such specimens (n = 242) and separately for lichen species with sufficient sample sizes: Hypogymnia farinacea (n = 32), H. physodes (n = 84), H. tubulosa (n = 60) and Pseudevernia furfuracea (n = 39) (Table S2).

Alignments, Phylogenetic Analyses and Haplotype Networks

We checked the identity of newly generated sequences by including them in larger phylogenetic trees of the Tremellales and Cystobasidiomycetes. For these analyses, all high‐quality sequences (i.e., with clear electropherograms) were used, including those from galls and from DNA extracted more than three months after collection. Two datasets were constructed—one for Tremellales and one for Cystobasidiomycetes—combining our newly generated sequences with GenBank data. The sampling in the Tremellales included species that belonged to clade number III (Millanes et al. 2011) and closely related species, mainly Tremella species from parmelioid lichens but also from the Lecanoraceae, Pertusariaceae and Tephromelataceae. We also included sequences from Clades 1 and 2 (Tagirdzhanova et al. 2021) and closely related sequences from Alectoria sarmentosa and Abies bark by the same authors. Pseudotremella moriformis (Bulleraceae) was used as outgroup. The Cystobasidiomycetes dataset included representatives of all orders (Buckleyzymales, Cyphobasidiales, Cystobasidiales, Erythrobasidiales, Naohideales and Sakaguchiales) and two incertae sedis families (Microsporomycetaceae and Symmetrosporaceae). We also included all Cystobasidiomycetes sequences from lichens published by Spribille et al. (2016), Černajová and Škaloud (2019), Mark et al. (2020), Tagirdzhanova et al. (2021), and selected environmental sequences closely related to ours (e.g., Pitkäranta et al. 2011). Preliminary trees showed no sequences closely related to Naohideales. Therefore, to simplify alignments, we used Naohidea sebacea (Naohideales) as outgroup, as the sister group of the rest of Cystobasidiomycetes (Wang et al. 2015; Diederich et al. 2022). Sequences in both datasets were aligned using the Q‐INS‐i algorithm (Katoh and Toh 2008) of the multiple sequence alignment software MAFFT version 7 (Katoh et al. 2019). Ambiguous regions were identified and eliminated with Gblocks version 0.91b (Castresana 2000) following the relaxed conditions described by Talavera and Castresana (2007), and manually checked using Mesquite 3.7 (Maddison and Maddison 2021). Maximum likelihood analyses were carried out in IQTree (Nguyen et al. 2015). Model selection based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC) was achieved using ModelFinder in IQTree (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017). The model selected was the GTR + F + I + Γ4 both for the Tremellales and for the Cystobasidiomycetes alignments. Node support was assessed by standard bootstrap using 1000 bootstrap pseudoreplicates.

Haplotype networks were constructed using only sequences of Tremella hypogymniae and T. tubulosae. Haplotype networks were generated to depict relationships among T. hypogymniae and T. tubulosae collected from different lichen species, geographical regions, and co‐occurring with different cystobasidiomycete lineages in the same lichen thallus. Networks were reconstructed with statistical parsimony software TCS Networks (Clement et al. 2002) implemented in the software PopART (http://popart.otago.ac.nz, Leight and Bryant 2015).

Morphological Studies with Light Microscopy, Fluorescent in Situ Hybridization (FISH) and Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM)

Macromorphological traits were observed using a Leica MZ 7.5 dissecting microscope. Macroscopical photos were taken with a Canon 6D camera, Canon lenses, a Nikon BD Plan 10 objective, Stackshot (https://cognisys‐inc.com) and Helicon Focus (https://www.heliconsoft.com/heliconsoft‐products/helicon‐focus/) for increasing the depth of field. Microscopical Tremella structures were studied using hand‐cut sections stained with phloxine (1% in water) after pre‐treatment with KOH (5%), following Diederich (1996; 2022). Preparations were observed and photographed using a Leica DMLB microscope fitted with differential interference contrast (DIC), and a Leica EC3 camera.

FISH was used to visualise and discriminate between Tremella and Cyphobasidium yeasts present in lichen thalli, using probes designed by Spribille et al. (2016) and Tuovinen et al. (2019). After collection, lichen thalli were placed on wet filter paper in petri dishes and sprayed with MQ water to activate metabolism. After at least one day of wetting by spraying and natural drying, parts of the thalli and galls were hand cut, vacuum‐hydrated in 1× PBS and fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 2 h at 4°C in the dark, washed with 1×P BS and frozen at −20°C until hybridization. Permeabilization and hybridization was done as described in Tuovinen et al. (2019). Briefly, hybridization was done in a solution of 1 μM of each probe and helper probe, and hybridization buffer consisting of 10% formamide, 0.01% SDS, 0.9 M NaCl and 20 mM Tris–Hcl (1 M pH 7.2), at 46°C for 3 h. After removal of the hybridization solution, fragments were washed in buffer (0.45 M NaCl, 0.02 M TRIS–HCl, 0.01% SDS) for 20 min at 48°C, followed by a 10 min wash in 1× PBS at RT, air‐dried and mounted on microscope slides with SlowFade Diamond (Thermo Fisher). All probes were mono labelled in 5′‐end. Probes and helper probes are listed in Table S3. We included a negative control without probes for each hybridization reaction and each lichen species.

Slides were imaged with a Leica SP5 confocal laser scanning microscope with a Leica HXC PL APO 63× NA 1.4 glycerol immersion objective or a Zeiss LSM710 confocal laser scanning microscope, with a Plan‐Apochromat 63× NA 1.4 oil DIC M27 objective. Laser lines employed, and the detected emission wavelengths are listed in Table S3. Based on negative controls, we selected laser intensities and gain values that prevented interpretation of false positives in samples with probes. Probes labelled with FAM and CY5 were imaged simultaneously and the rest sequentially. We produced maximum intensity projections (MIP) of z‐stacks using Fiji, ImageJ (Schindelin et al. 2015). The z‐stack for Figure 3D and Videos S1 and S2 was deconvolved with Huygens Professional v 17.04 (Scientific Volume Imaging, the Netherlands, http://svi.nl), using the CMLE algorithm, with SNR: 5–30 and 40 iterations. Volume renderings were prepared with IMARIS v9.2 (ImarisXT, Bitplane AG, http://bitplane.com) for animation 1, Las X for animation 2 and with Zen Blue for the rest of the figures. Finally, we adjusted colour balance for the clarity of presentation post imaging.

Results

Tremella h ypogymniae and T. tubulosae Are Detected in Lichens of the Hypogymnioid Clade of the Parmeliaceae with Varying Frequencies

We generated 113 new ITS sequences of Tremella suitable for phylogenetic analyses (Tables S2 and S4), which were aligned together with sequences available in GenBank (Figure S1). The data matrix of the Tremellales included 227 terminals with 502 aligned characters (220 of which were parsimony informative). All our sequences belonged to either Tremella hypogymniae or T. tubulosae, which formed two independent clades with 100% and 88% bootstrap support, respectively. The sister group of the clade formed by the two species was T. nashii , which occurred in Usnea hirta , although this relationship was not well supported (Figure S1). None of our sequences belonged to other Tremella species associated with lichens of the Parmeliaceae, Lecanoraceae, Pertusariaceae or Tephromelataceae (i.e., Clade III in Millanes et al. 2011, and allied species), or to tremellalean yeasts sequenced from Parmeliaceae and from Abies bark by Tagirzhanova et al. (2021).

PCR screening of 390 samples without galls, and DNA extracted within three months, revealed that the presence of Tremella hypogymniae and T. tubulosae is restricted to a subset of lichens within the hypogymnioid clade and Letharia vulpina (Table 1, Figure S1). The highest detection rates were found in Hypogymnia farinacea (62.5%) and H. physodes (59.5%). T. hypogymniae was most frequently detected in H. physodes (40%). T. tubulosae was most frequently detected in H. farinacea (50%) and Pseudevernia furfuracea (28%), and only in 13% of the H. tubulosa thalli. Since the sampling was uneven, the actual incidence of T. hypogymniae and T. tubulosae is uncertain in species with fewer samples (e.g., Arctoparmelia incurva (n = 1), H. austerodes (n = 1), H. bitteri (n = 4), Table 1). Detection percentages varied among different collecting sites (Table S2). Notably, neither of the two Tremella species was detected in the other 34 Parmeliaceae species screened. These relative abundances are summarised in Table 1, which also includes corresponding data on co‐occurring Cystobasidiomycetes.

Fourteen Cystobasidiomycete Lineages Were Detected, with Mostly Low Levels of Lichen Specificity

We generated 132 new ITS sequences of Cystobasidiomycetes of sufficient quality for phylogenetic analyses (Tables S2 and S5), which were aligned together with sequences already available in GenBank (Figure S2). The data matrix of the Cystobasidiomycetes included 562 terminals with 628 aligned characters (404 of which were parsimony‐informative). Our sequences belonged to Cyphobasidium hypogymniicola, C. usneicola s. lat., Lichenozyma pisutiana, and to 11 other Cystobasidiomycete lineages not yet formally described (Figure S2).

Sequences of C. hypogymniicola were recovered from all Hypogymnia species studied and from one specimen of Platismatia glauca . Sequences obtained from Hypogymnia species by Spribille et al. (2016), Mark et al. (2020) and Tagirdzhanova et al. (2021), and a few sequences in Parmelia sulcata and Pseudevernia furfuracea by Mark et al. (2020), were also included in C. hypogymniicola. Based on ITS alone, C. usneicola was not supported as a monophyletic group. We distinguished lineage 8 (see below) as a subclade including sequences isolated from Pseudevernia furfuracea, but this lineage would probably be nested with phylogenetic support within C. usneicola using additional loci. Three other new sequences, two from Usnea sp. and one from Platismatia glauca were assigned to C. usneicola. Among the undescribed lineages of Cystobasidiomycetes, Lineage 2 was the only group lacking strong phylogenetic support (bootstrap value < 70%, data not shown) and included sequences obtained from several species of Parmeliaceae by us and by Spribille et al. (2016), Mark et al. (2020) and Tagirdzhanova et al. (2021). In addition, a few sequences obtained from Cladonia rangiferina (Černajová and Škaloud 2019) and from Lecanora spp. and Physcia spp. (Mark et al. 2020) also belonged to this lineage. Lineage 5 was a strongly supported group including some of our sequences obtained from Alectoria sp., Evernia prunastri , H. physodes , Letharia lupina and L. vulpina . Sequences from Alectoria spp., Bryoria spp., Hypogymnia spp., Lecanora pulicaris , Letharia spp., Parmelia sulcata , Pseudevernia furfuracea and one sequence from Usnea sp. obtained by Spribille et al. (2016), Mark et al. (2020) and Tagirdzhanova et al. (2021) were also included in this clade. Lineage 7 included sequences obtained from Alectoria spp. and Bryoria spp. by Spribille et al. (2016) and Tagirdzhanova et al. (2021), together with two of our sequences, one from Alectoria sp. (ALEC8) and one from Letharia vulpina (SW_81). Lineage 8 was a subclade that probably belongs in Cyphobasidium usneicola and included sequences obtained predominantly from Pseudevernia furfuracea by us and by Mark et al. (2020), but also three of our sequences obtained from H. physodes , H. tubulosa and Usnea sp. Lineage 11 was one of the few species‐specific clades, with representatives detected only from Evernia prunastri . Lineage 13 contained two of our sequences obtained from Hypogymnia farinacea and Platismatia glauca , together with one sequence from Cladonia arbuscula (Spribille et al. 2016) and one from Lecanora pulicaris (Mark et al. 2020). Lineage 14 contained sequences obtained from Pseudevernia furfuracea (this study), Hypogymnia physodes , Lecanora pulicaris (Mark et al. 2020) and dust from a floor sample (Pitkäranta et al. 2011). Lineage 16 was a small clade including two sequences obtained from Melanelixia sp. and Flavoparmelia sp. Lineage 17 included two sequences obtained from Platismatia glauca . Lineage 19 included our only sequence obtained from Protoparmelia badia , grouping with sequences obtained from Cladonia cariosa (Černajová and Škaloud 2019), Hypogymnia tubulosa and Lecanora argentata (Mark et al. 2020). Lineage 20 was a clade including sequences from a heterogeneous assemblage of lichens: Arctoparmelia centrifuga (this study), Cladonia spp. (Černajová and Škaloud 2019), Lecanora carpinea s. lat., Parmelia sulcata (Mark et al. 2020), and Tephromela atra (Cometto et al. 2022).

Detection percentages of Cystobasidiomycetes were calculated only in the genera of the Parmeliaceae in which T. hypogymniae and T. tubulosae were potentially present, viz., Arctoparmelia, Hypogymnia, Letharia and Pseudevernia. We estimated the frequency of Cystobasidiomycetes in samples without Tremella galls and with DNA extracted within 3 months of collection. We detected Cystobasidiomycetes in 54% of the 242 samples, in all genera and in all nine species with more than one specimen investigated (Table 1). Cyphobasidium hypogymniicola was most frequently identified, but at some sites, none of the Cystobasidiomycetes sequenced in Hypogymnia lichens was C. hypogymniicola, the only Cyphobasidium species previously described from Hypogymnia (Table S2; Figure S2). Our results indicate low specificity towards the lichen they associate with (Figure S2, Table S5), except for lineage 11 so far only detected from Evernia prunastri .

Two Haplotypes of Tremella hypogymniae and T. tubulosae Co‐Occur in the Same Lichen Thalli with Cystobasidiomycetes

Haplotype networks revealed two main haplotypes in Tremella hypogymniae (h1, which includes sequences from T. hypogymniae galls, and h2) and two main haplotypes within T. tubulosae (t1, which includes the type of T. tubulosae, and t2) (Figure 2, Table S2, Figure S1). The most common haplotypes were h1 and t2. The haplotypes h1, t1 and t2 occurred both in the Iberian and Scandinavian Peninsulas, while h2 was only detected in Scandinavia (Figure 2A). Most haplotypes were found in multiple lichens, but h2 is the only one restricted to Hypogymnia physodes (Figure 2B). The widespread haplotype h1 occurred in six lichens, while t1 was recovered from H. tubulosa , H. physodes , and Pseudevernia furfuracea. In contrast, t2 was absent from H. tubulosa but present in five other lichens including H. farinacea and P. furfuracea . In several cases, different haplotypes co‐occurred within the same thallus, and occasionally haplotypes of both Tremella species co‐occurred (Tables S2 and S4).

All Tremella haplotypes frequently coexisted with different Cystobasidiomycetes lineages in the same lichen thallus (Figure 2C,D). Analyses of contingency tables indicated weak and non‐significant associations between the presence of T. hypogymniae/T. tubulosae and the presence of Cystobasidiomycetes, both when considering all pooled samples (N = 242, Φ = 0.05–0.31; p > 0.05), and when analyses were separated by lichens (data not shown). The different Cystobasidiomycetes lineages were not phylogenetically close enough to construct haplotype networks (Figure S2).

Tremella Hyphae Are Detected Only in Specimens with Galls, but Tremella and Cyphobasidium Yeasts Are Detected across the Thalli and Notably in Soredia

The probes designed to selectively stain Tremella cells hybridized with hyphae and basidia of Tremella in galls (Figure 3) and with yeast cells and pseudohyphae of Tremella in specimens (or areas of the thalli) devoid of galls (Figure 4). In the galls, tremellalean hyphae occupied the cortex and the medulla, whereas basidia occurred only in the cortex (Figure 3A−C). Tremella hyphae were also observed surrounding and between dividing algal cells in the medulla under the galls (Figure 3D; Videos S1 and S2). In areas of the thallus cortex far from the galls, we found Tremella hyphae (Figure 3E−G), yeasts (Figure 3F), and spores germinating or forming conjugation tubes (Figure 3H).

In thalli without galls, we observed both Cyphobasidium and Tremella yeasts immersed in the upper cortex of Hypogymnia physodes (Figure 4A,B; Figure S3A). Yeasts of both Cyphobasidium and Tremella were also observed in soredia of H. physodes (Figure 4C–G; Figure S3A), which was confirmed by PCR in H. physodes and H. farinacea (Table S2). Other lichens than H. physodes were not that intensively investigated by FISH, but we observed yeasts of both Cyphobasidium and Tremella in the cortex of Pseudevernia furfuracea (Figure S3B,C), hyphae and yeasts of Tremella in the cortex of H. tubulosa (Figure S3D,E), and pseudohyphae and yeasts in the cortex of H. farinacea (Figure S3F).

Discussion

Although basidiomata of Tremella hypogymniae and T. tubulosae are known only from Hypogymnia physodes and H. tubulosa , respectively (Diederich 1996; Diederich et al. 2020, 2022), our results show that these Tremella species occur as yeasts and pseudohyphae in a wider range of lichens within the hypogymnioid clade of the Parmeliaceae (Arctoparmelia, Brodoa, Hypogymnia and Pseudevernia). Such shifts in lichen specialization across the different life cycle phases of T. hypogymniae and T. tubulosae align with previous observations on a limited number of other lichen‐associated basidiomycetes (Tuovinen et al. 2021; Freire‐Rallo 2023). The restricted occurrence of these two Tremella species within the hypogymnioid clade prompts hypotheses about coevolution and about chemical traits that may differentiate hypogymnioid lichens from other members of the Parmeliaceae. Recently, Kirkhus et al. (2025) showed that the presence of tremellalean fungi in Pertusaria lichens without galls was negatively correlated with the presence of thiophaninic, fumarprotocetraric and gyrophoric acids in the Pertusaria thalli, reinforcing the potential role of lichen chemistry in shaping basidiomycete occurrence (Lawrey and Diederich 2003; Werth et al. 2013; Spribille et al. 2016; Pino‐Bodas et al. 2017; Freire‐Rallo 2023; Kirkhus et al. 2025).

Since we cleaned but did not surface‐sterilise the lichens before DNA extraction it is possible that we detected some environmental or transient fungi as described in Fernández‐Mendoza et al. (2017). However, in sites where other Parmeliaceae were abundant and often overlapped physically, Tremella hypogymniae and T. tubulosae were only amplified from hypogymnioid lichens. Similar results were obtained by Freire‐Rallo (2023) in a rich population of T. parietinae, where this species was only isolated from Xanthoria parietina , but not from other lichens of different families (Candelariellaceae and Physciaceae) growing physically close on the same trees. The combination of FISH‐CLSM and molecular data suggests that the taxa detected are true lichen associates, not incidental propagules. The lichen‐associated yeast pool is seemingly still specialised to the hypogymnioid lichens, but less restrictive in lichen specificity than the filamentous phase. It could potentially act as a ‘taxon bank’ enabling host switching and colonisation of suboptimal environments before specialisation (i.e., ‘sloppy fitness’; Agosta and Klemens 2008; Freire‐Rallo et al. 2023; Kirkhus et al. 2025).

Our target species were found in many but not all suitable thalli, although the lack of detection cannot be attributed to true absences as it may result from PCR failure or uneven cell distribution rather than true absence. A metagenomic approach could potentially broaden diversity estimates but may miss yeast DNA masked by the dominant symbiont DNA (Tagirdzhanova et al. 2024). Endpoint PCR with specific primers can be more sensitive (Mark et al. 2020; Tagirdzhanova et al. 2021). Despite the possibility of false negatives, our results suggest that yeasts are not equally abundant across all sampled localities and may not be always present. Similarly, Wang et al. (2016) found that the detection of tremellalean and other lichen‐associated fungi in Hypogymnia hypotrypa varied with altitude and across collecting sites.

The haplotype networks for Tremella hypogymniae and T. tubulosae are dominated by central, widely distributed haplotypes that span multiple regions, lichen species, and co‐occurring Cystobasidiomycete lineages, indicating broad distribution and association flexibility. Both Tremella species co‐occur with (1) Cyphobasidium hypogymniicola, (2) other described Cystobasidiomycetes and (3) several still undescribed but previously reported lineages (Spribille et al. 2016; Černajová and Škaloud 2019; Mark et al. 2020; Tagirdzhanova et al. 2021). Our findings coincide with previous studies showing that tremellalean and cystobasidiomycete yeasts frequently co‐occur in parmelioid lichens (Tuovinen et al. 2019; Tagirdzhanova et al. 2021, 2024). The co‐occurrence analyses showed that the presence and potential absence of the two basidiomycete yeast classes was not correlated. However, PCR and Sanger sequencing only allowed detection of the potential mutual exclusion between Tremella and Cystobasidiomycetes. Quantitative analyses could more effectively characterise community dynamics of yeasts associated with lichen thalli. Such investigations were, however, beyond the scope of our study. Although our study focused on Tremella and only screened for Cystobasidiomycetes as potential co‐occurring yeasts, we observed a weaker lichen‐specificity in the Cyphobasidium yeast phase, whereas strong specificity is known for its filamentous phase (Diederich 1996; Millanes et al. 2016). Mark et al. (2020) interpreted the lower‐than‐expected lichen specificity of Cystobasidiomycetes yeasts as an indication of low functional specialisation. The lower specificity could also reflect functional redundancy in terms of goods and services provided to the lichen marketplace (Doolittle and Inkpen 2018; Spribille et al. 2022). Complex facilitative and competitive dynamics have been documented in other yeast communities (Bleuven et al. 2020; Agarbati et al. 2024). Diverse basidiomycete yeast communities occur not only in Parmeliaceae but also in other lichen families (Černajová and Škaloud 2019; Cometto et al. 2022, 2024; Kachalkin et al. 2019, 2024; Pérez et al. 2024; Kirkhus et al. 2025), providing an interesting avenue for further research into the factors shaping their structure, composition, and potential functions.

Our FISH‐CLSM studies reveal that in thalli of Hypogymnia physodes devoid of galls, Tremella and Cyphobasidium yeasts occur not only in the cortex but also in the medulla and soredia. In stratified macrolichens (e.g., Parmeliaceae), the basidiomycete yeast phase has been detected embedded in the lichen cortex, prompting discussions about its possible contribution to the cortex extracellular matrix (Spribille et al. 2016; Spribille 2018; Tuovinen et al. 2019; Tagirdzhanova et al. 2021). Basidiomycete yeasts may preferentially inhabit carbohydrate‐rich areas of the lichen thallus, and their presence in the medulla and soredia may rather be linked to dispersion. In non‐stratified crustose lichens (e.g., Lecanora). the Tremella yeast phase is vertically distributed within the areoles (Tuovinen et al. 2021), potentially reflecting a more homogeneous distribution of carbohydrate resources in thalli lacking a strongly differentiated cortical structure. The recurrent observation of tremellalean hyphae closely surrounding algal cells, both in this and in previous FISH‐CLSM investigations (Tuovinen et al. 2019, 2021) raises the possibility of direct nutrient transfer from the algae during the filamentous phase of lichen‐inhabiting Tremella. However, the nature of these interactions remains inconclusive without further physiological and ultrastructural studies. Both Hypogymnia physodes and H. tubulosa exhibit high specificity to their algal partners (Mark et al. 2020) and similar specificity may occur in the filamentous phase of dimorphic basidiomycetes. While lichen‐associated tremellalean fungi have traditionally been regarded as mycoparasites, it is plausible that their nutritional adaptations have varied throughout their evolutionary history and across different life‐cycle stages.

Triggers for the dimorphic switch in lichen‐inhabiting Tremella species are unknown, but may involve contact sensing, environmental stress, nutrient availability, or signals from other symbionts. In other tremellalean fungi such as Cryptococcus neoformans and Tremella fuciformis, hyphal growth is induced by temperature, nitrogen deficiency, mating pheromones, and contact sensing (Shadomy and Utz 1966; Lin 2009; Hou et al. 2011). In two populations where Tremella hypogymniae galls were exceptionally frequent (sites 22 and 52, data on gall frequency not shown), yeasts were also frequent in gall‐free thalli, possibly facilitating higher mating/contact frequency, and potentially triggering the dimorphic switch. Alternatively, the transition to sexual reproduction could be a stress response and flight reaction to high yeast abundance or another yet unknown factor within the thallus. Another possibility is that signaling from other organisms present in thalli, such as a specific photobiont lineage, could trigger the switch. Contact with the symbiotic algal species Trebouxia jamesii has been shown to trigger a yeast‐to‐pseudohyphae switch in the lichen‐forming ascomycete Umbilicaria muhlenbergii (Wang et al. 2020). We have also observed Tremella pseudohyphae in Hypogymnia farinacea . If this lichen is interpreted as providing a suboptimal environment, the pseudohyphal formation could be understood as a stress response, which has been reported in other tremellalean fungi (Neilson et al. 1978; Hou et al. 2011; Lee et al. 2012). Dimorphism could also be a plastic response to nutrient availability, which may vary across different parts of the lichen thallus. Phenotypic plasticity reflected in nutritional strategies is common in other dimorphic fungi (Alster et al. 2021). However, further studies will be needed to elucidate which factors could induce dimorphic transitions in lichen‐inhabiting Tremella.

The combination of frequent occurrence, lichen‐specificity and presence in soredia, prompts questions about dispersal. Possible scenarios include co‐dispersal with other lichen symbionts, and/or establishment of new associations. It is generally agreed that soredia constitute the main dispersal mechanism in Hypogymnia physodes and H. tubulosa (Armstrong et al., Armstrong 1990). These vegetative propagules are formed from the algal and medullary layers and accumulate in structures called soralia before dispersal (Ahmadjian 1993). The detection and visualisation of Tremella and Cyphobasidium in the soredia of H. physodes , along with the prevalence of asexual reproduction in the lichen, suggest co‐dispersal as the most plausible dispersal mechanism for the yeasts. Similar co‐dispersal has been documented for yeasts (Černajová and Škaloud 2020) and bacteria (Aschenbrenner et al. 2014) in other lichens. Whether the yeast phase of Tremella hypogymniae can survive outside the lichen thallus and form new associations with the myco‐ and photobionts is unknown. No records of T. hypogymniae or T. tubulosae exist from non‐lichen substrates, in contrast to other non‐lichen‐associated tremellalean fungi, which are common in metabarcoding studies from environmental samples (Núñez et al. 2016; Adamo et al. 2020; Rosa et al. 2020; Kadowaki et al. 2023; Monteiro et al. 2023).

Concluding Remarks

Our study expands knowledge of the frequency of occurrence of lichen‐inhabiting Tremella, the specificity of their different life cycle stages towards the lichen they associate with, and their interactions with other lichen symbionts. The yeast phase of the studied species is more generalist than the filamentous phase, yet still restricted to a limited range of lichens. Both Tremella species co‐occur with Cyphobasidium hypogymniicola and other Cystobasidiomycetes within the same lichen thalli, consistent with previous findings for other Tremella species (Tuovinen et al. 2019; Tagirdzhanova et al. 2021). C. hypogymniicola and most other detected Cystobasidiomycetes show low specificity towards the lichens they associate with, adding to the evidence presented by Mark et al. (2020). Our results also suggest that basidiomycete yeasts co‐disperse within the vegetative propagules of the lichen. Intriguing questions remain regarding the biology of lichen‐inhabiting Tremella. These include the nature of the interaction between yeast and filamentous phases with other lichen bionts, the factors shaping their presence and abundance within the lichen thalli, and the triggers underlying their life cycle transitions.
