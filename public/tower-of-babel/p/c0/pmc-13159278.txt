The progression of proliferative verrucous leukoplakia can be detected through elevated levels of salivary matrix metalloproteinases
Authors: Jan Liska, Nikoleta Molnarova, Jan Netolicky, Petra Hauerova, Marie Karlikova, Ondrej Topolcan, Ladislav Pecen, Martina Pestova, Veronika Liskova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe management of follow-up in proliferative verrucous leukoplakia (PVL) cases remains highly challenging due to the absence of established etiological factors and limited understanding of disease progression. The aim of this study was to identify optimal biomarkers of PVL behavior to facilitate decision-making in complex therapeutic management.MethodsSalivary levels of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) were evaluated in 54 confirmed PVL cases. All patients underwent follow-up at Oral Medicine Department, Dentistry Clinic at University Hospital Pilsen. Unstimulated saliva samples were collected and analyzed for MMP-1, -2, -3, -7, and − 9 concentrations. Criteria for PVL stability and progression were defined. Disease progression in individual cases was retrospectively correlated with salivary MMP levels to identify optimal prognostic markers.ResultsAssessment of PVL progression and stability was performed 6 months after salivary MMP sampling. Progression was observed in 63% of patients (34/54). Significant associations between elevated salivary MMP levels and PVL progression were confirmed for four of the five MMPs tested. MMP-1, -3, -7, and − 9 emerged as promising markers. A combination of MMP-3 and MMP-7 demonstrated the best prognostic performance, with a sensitivity of approximately 79.5% and a specificity of 89%.ConclusionsThis is the first study to evaluate salivary MMPs for PVL monitoring. Our findings confirm an association between elevated salivary MMP levels and an increased risk of PVL progression. This non-invasive approach may improve clinical management and follow-up of patients with PVL.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08211-y.Keywords: Proliferative verrucous leukoplakia, Oral potentially malignant disorders, Oral squamous cell carcinoma, Salivary markers, Matrix metalloproteinases

Introduction

Proliferative verrucous leukoplakia (PVL) is a rare and aggressive subtype of oral leukoplakia, first described in 1985 by Hansen et al. [1, 2]. The disease develops from initial homogenous leukoplakia and progresses to multifocal mucosal lesions. Over time, these lesions spread, acquire a verrucous appearance, and show marked resistance to conventional and experimental therapies such as surgery, laser ablation, and radiotherapy [3–5]. PVL is characterized by a high recurrence rate following excision [6], and a remarkably high frequency of malignant transformation (MT) (44–100%) [3–5], placing it among the most dangerous oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) [7–12]. Lesions most commonly involve the gingiva, alveolar mucosa, and tongue [9, 13, 14].

Histopathological findings of PVL include hyperplasia, hyperkeratosis, and acanthosis [13]. A subepithelial lichenoid lymphocytic infiltrate may also be observed [6, 14]. Dysplastic changes typically increase as the disease progresses, culminating in verrucous carcinoma or oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) [13, 15–20]. PVL is diagnosed using established clinical and histopathological criteria. Diagnostic guidelines have been proposed by several research groups, most notably by Cerero-Lapiedra et al. in 2010 [19, 21–24]. Several additional classification schemes were introduced in subsequent years. However, purely histopathological criteria for the reliable identification of PVL have not yet been established [13]. Currently, PVL is monitored through frequent clinical examinations, and the aggressiveness of therapy is determined primarily by visual assessment of mucosal findings. Recent advances in genetic and epigenetic research have improved the differentiation of PVL from homogeneous leukoplakia and enhanced the identification of lesions at increased risk of malignant transformation (MT). However, these approaches require biopsy-based testing [25, 26]. At present, no validated prognostic indicators, such as serum or salivary biomarkers, are available.

Treatment of PVL is challenging due to the absence of clear triggers for the onset and progression of the disease. Rigorous follow-up, with assessments at least every six months, is essential and often involves repeated re-excisions of recurrent lesions [9, 13]. Chronic gingival inflammation can destabilize lesion behavior [27]. Aggressive periodontal pathogens have been suggested as possible co-inducers in the malignant transformation of oral mucosal lesions [27–29]. Examples of PVL lesions are shown in Fig. 1.

The activity of periodontal and mucosal pathological processes can be monitored through levels of serum or salivary matrix metalloproteinases (MMP) [30, 31]. More than 20 MMPs have been identified; these zinc-dependent peptidases play diverse and sometimes contradictory roles, ranging from physiological tissue remodeling and wound healing to participation in oncogenesis and fibrosis [30–34]. Previous studies have reported associations between MMP-1, -2, -3, -7, and − 9 and OPMDs and OSCC; however, their role in the rare disease proliferative verrucous leukoplakia has not yet been described [31, 34].

The aim of this study is to explore the potential of non-invasive salivary MMP assessment for monitoring PVL development. To date, this approach has not been described in the literature. Early identification of high-risk PVL cases during follow-up is crucial for guiding appropriate treatment.

Methods

Between 2024 and 2025, 54 patients with PVL underwent salivary MMP testing at the Department of Oral Medicine, Dentistry Clinic, University Hospital Pilsen.

Inclusion criteria: Patients with PVL fulfilling the clinical-pathological criteria proposed by Cerero-Lapiedra et al. A confirmed diagnosis of PVL required the presence of at least three major criteria or a combination of two major and two minor criteria. Only PVL patients able to produce at least 0.5 milliliter of unstimulated saliva were included.

Inclusion criteria: Patients with PVL fulfilling the clinical-pathological criteria proposed by Cerero-Lapiedra et al. A confirmed diagnosis of PVL required the presence of at least three major criteria or a combination of two major and two minor criteria. Only PVL patients able to produce at least 0.5 milliliter of unstimulated saliva were included.

Exclusion criteria: Patients who did not meet the verification criteria for PVL, patients unable to produce sufficient unstimulated saliva, and those who were not available for assessment at the Department of Oral Medicine during the MMP sampling period.

Exclusion criteria: Patients who did not meet the verification criteria for PVL, patients unable to produce sufficient unstimulated saliva, and those who were not available for assessment at the Department of Oral Medicine during the MMP sampling period.

Six patients with confirmed PVL were excluded due to complete inability to produce saliva, resulting from prior head and neck radiotherapy or severe medication-related adverse effects. Figure 2 illustrates the flowchart of PVL case inclusion in this study.

All patients included in the study met at least three major criteria, with an average of 4.2 major criteria and 2.5 minor criteria per patient. As PVL has an unknown etiology, anamnestic data were limited to: gender, age, antibiotic use one month prior to testing and smoking status (internally graded as 0, 10, 20, or > 20 cigarettes per day). Smokers were asked about their nicotine consumption in pack-years (1 pack-year equals smoking 20 cigarettes per day for one year).

The clinical distribution of PVL lesions on the oral mucosa was assessed. The number of present teeth was evaluated on a scale of 0–32. In dentate patients, a round-tipped WHO probe was used to evaluate the periodontal tissues. When a true periodontal pocket (> 3.5 mm) was detected, testing for periodontal pathogens was performed. Periodontal pathogens were identified using a DNA test (VariOr-Dento Plus system, Gen-Trend Co., Czechia). Subgingival plaque samples were collected with sterile paper points inserted into periodontal pockets. The DNA test identifies 12 bacterial species associated with periodontal disease, including four species belonging to the highly aggressive “red complex” and three to the “orange complex.” The relative presence of each bacterial species was scored on a scale from 0 to 4. In addition to relative positivity, absolute bacterial concentrations (expressed in microliters) were determined. Bacterial DNA was analyzed using quantitative PCR (qPCR) with TaqMan probes. The analytical sensitivity was set to 101 and relative positivity scores of 1–4 corresponded to bacterial loads ranging from 103 to 106. Unstimulated saliva was collected over a 15-minute period using the standard spitting technique [35]. Saliva volume and pH were measured using paper indicators with a range of 4.0–7.5 (Kompava Co., Slovakia). Samples were aliquoted into sterile tubes and stored at − 80 °C in the Biobank of the Faculty of Medicine in Pilsen, Charles University, and University Hospital Pilsen. All samples were stored for no longer than 12 months prior to analysis, and each aliquot underwent only a single freeze–thaw cycle.

The selection of MMPs - Interstitial collagenase (MMP-1), Gelatinase A (MMP-2), Stromelysin-1 (MMP-3), Matrilysin (MMP-7) and Gelatinase B (MMP-9) - was based on their biological activity, documented relevance to OPMDs and OSCC, and their anticipated involvement in PVL pathogenesis. Salivary MMP levels were analyzed at the Department of Immunochemical Diagnostics, University Hospital Pilsen, using Luminex multiplex assay kits on a Bio-Plex® MAGPIX™ Multiplex Reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., USA). The assays were divided into two panels with the following dilutions: the Human MMP Premixed Kit for MMP-1 and MMP-3 (Cat. No. FCSTM07, R&D Systems; dilution 1:10) and the Milliplex Human MMP Magnetic Bead Panel 2 for MMP-2, MMP-7, and MMP-9 (Cat. No. HMMP2MAG-55 K, Millipore; dilution 1:20). Luminex assays were performed according to manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, samples were allowed to thaw at 4 °C overnight and centrifugated prior to the preparation for analysis. The reader was calibrated and verified the day before the analysis. Standard and in-house control samples were prepared in duplicate; coefficients of variation (CVs) were below 10% and 15%, respectively. Plates were washed using a Bio-Rad plate washer, which was maintained daily in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Raw data for each plate were analyzed using Bio-Plex Manager Software, exported, and further processed in Microsoft Excel. Assay results were expressed in nanograms per milliliter (ng/mL). Statistical analyses were performed using the Wilcoxon two-sample test and logistic regression with a stepwise selection method. To reduce the risk of false-positive findings, Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to the five MMP analyses, in addition to reporting unadjusted p-values. The study was exploratory in nature; therefore, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed for other statistical tests. For three biomarkers (MMP-2, MMP-7, and MMP-9), two missing values were identified. Consequently, an observed cases (OC) approach was applied. The multivariate stepwise logistic regression model included the five MMPs as potential predictors, along with sex, age, history of OSCC, current graded smoking status, smoking history expressed in pack-years, recent antibiotic use, percentage pathogen burden, and dental status expressed as a percentage.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was also performed, including calculation of sensitivity and specificity at optimal cut-off values determined using Youden index optimization, as well as at the closest cut-off yielding 95% specificity. The Chi-square test was used to assess the relationship between two categorical variables. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 M9 (TS1M9) package (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, USA).

Results

Based on criteria for stability or progression, patients were divided into a stable group (PVL0, n = 20) and a progressive group (PVL1, n = 34). Cases with a prior history of MT were included in both groups to reflect real-world clinical monitoring of PVL. Specifically, MMP-1 levels were significantly higher in the PVL1 group (p = 0.0035), and MMP-3 was also elevated in progressive cases (p = 0.0174). In addition, increased levels of MMP-7 and MMP-9 were observed in patients with disease progression (p = 0.0093 and p = 0.0275, respectively).

Discussion

Our findings highlight the potential of salivary MMP assessment as a useful, non-invasive tool for monitoring PVL progression during follow-up. To our knowledge, this is the first investigation to explore the feasibility of prognostic surveillance in the high-risk disease of PVL. Any approach that facilitates clinical management is particularly valuable, given the absence of established etiological factors and the lack of standardized guidelines for PVL [3, 22, 27, 36, 37].

Historically, several classification schemes for reporting PVL cases have been proposed [2, 13, 19, 21, 23, 24]. Some of these approaches sought to simplify diagnostic procedures by minimizing inclusion criteria [19, 23, 24]. However, from the perspective of a tertiary healthcare center, we do not support the requirement of a minimum five-year follow-up period for PVL diagnosis. In routine clinical practice, many PVL cases are initially monitored in peripheral healthcare settings with variable data quality until lesion progression becomes evident, at which point clinicians must make timely management decisions.

The development of salivary diagnostic markers is especially advantageous because saliva is easily accessible, collection is non-invasive, and patients are not subjected to additional procedural burden. Moreover, in oral pathology, the diagnostic relevance of saliva is further supported by its direct contact with affected tissues [30, 31].

Our results show that nearly two-thirds of cases (63%) progressed, requiring re-biopsy or demonstrating MT within the six-month study period. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting recurrence rates exceeding 71% [3, 4, 38]. Our data also indicate altered expression of several MMPs in PVL associated with the progression of oral mucosal disease, consistent with previously published findings on OPMD and OSCC. A significantly worse short-term prognosis was observed in women who were predominantly represented only in the progressive (PVL1) group. Although female sex exerted the strongest influence among the clinical variables in the multivariate regression model, its effect on short-term PVL behavior was less pronounced than that of the selected MMPs (MMP-1, MMP-3, and MMP-7).

On average, patients in our cohort did not exhibit hyposalivation. The oral microbiome—particularly aggressive periodontal pathogens—has been frequently implicated in carcinogenesis. We hypothesize that chronic, intense inflammation may contribute to destabilization of oral mucosal lesions [27]. In both PVL groups tested for salivary MMP, periodontal pathogens were abundant. The greater stability observed in PVL0 may have been supported by the more frequent use of antibiotics during the monitoring period.

Earlier publications considered smoking an inducer of PVL; however, our findings align with recent studies suggesting that smoking status is not a significant pathological factor in PVL development [2, 6, 9, 15]. Therapeutic approaches in our cohort included extensive surgical excision, laser ablation, and comprehensive periodontal care [6, 13], 39– [43].

During MT, neoangiogenesis is a critical process in the progression of OSCC from OPMDs [30]. The role of MMPs in oral carcinogenesis has been extensively investigated. Dysregulation of growth factor pathways and extracellular matrix degradation are considered key mechanisms in early tumorigenesis [44, 45]. MMPs are responsible for extracellular matrix degradation in physiological process, but are also active in oncology, where they regulate angiogenesis.

An association between MMP-1 and MMP-9 expression, oral epithelial dysplasia, and the risk of MT was first described in 2004 [33]. MMP-2 expression has also been detected in OPMDs [31]. The same phenomenon was confirmed in our study of proliferative verrucous leukoplakia (PVL). Levels of MMP-1, -2, -3, and − 9 are significantly elevated in OSCC compared with healthy controls [31, 34, 46, 47]. In particular, MMP-1 and MMP-3 have been proposed as biomarkers of OSCC development and poor prognosis [34]. MMP-2 is also elevated in OSCC [34]; in our study, it was the only biomarker showing a substantial, though not statistically significant, increase in progressive PVL1 cases compared with stable PVL.

MMP-7, the smallest protease in the MMP family, has been detected in OSCC and oral lichen planus, suggesting a possible role as an early indicator of MT [34]. Numerous studies have described the pathological activity of gelatinase B (MMP-9), the largest member of the MMP family, which is consistently overexpressed in cancer [30, 31, 33], 47– [49]. MMP-9 is considered a promising target for oncological drug development [48]. Its concentration is also elevated in dysplastic lesions of the oral mucosa and increases progressively during MT [30].

Oral MMP activity is influenced by pH, with levels increasing under acidic conditions [32]. However, this effect was not observed in our study, as mean salivary pH values were similar in both PVL0 and PVL1 groups. In contrast to many previous studies, we also evaluated dentition and periodontal status, including the presence of periodontal pathogens within periodontal pockets [30]. Given that inflammatory resorptive activity is associated with elevated MMP expression [32], assessment of periodontal health represents an important aspect of MMP interpretation. An additional strength of this study is the inclusion of patients with a prior history of MT in both study groups, which more accurately reflects real-world clinical follow-up of high-risk oral potentially malignant disorders.

Taken together, our findings on MMP alterations in PVL support the hypothesis that these enzymes contribute to the aggressive clinical behavior and high risk of malignant transformation characteristic of PVL. Moreover, studies evaluating salivary biomarkers, including MMPs, have yielded promising results for non-invasive early detection of oral cancer [50], underscoring the potential clinical utility of these molecules in high-risk lesions such as PVL. In conclusion, evaluating the combined expression of salivary MMP-1, -2, -3, -7, and − 9 may help identify PVL cases at increased risk of progression, given their association with proliferative, dysplastic, and neovascularization activity. In particular, combined assessment of salivary MMP-3 and MMP-7 detected approximately four out of five progressive PVL cases (sensitivity = 79.41%) while maintaining a low false-positive rate (specificity = 88.89%). This saliva-based assessment approach shows promise as part of comprehensive PVL follow-up and management. Given the usual availability of MMP diagnostics in tertiary care hospitals, salivary values could be determined during follow-up PVL twice a year, especially in cases close to the cut-off values for progression. Although recent studies have reported increased expression of several salivary proteins in PVL, evidence supporting their prognostic utility remains limited [56].

The most significant limitation of this study is its single-center design. Follow-up and management were further complicated by the rarity of PVL and the requirement for patients to provide sufficient volumes of unstimulated saliva for analysis. Future large-scale, multi-institutional studies are needed to overcome this limitation and to achieve a larger sample size. A relative limitation is the short-term predictive capability of our method which cannot determine the long-term behavior of PVL. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory. External validation in an independent cohort is required before clinical implementation.

Conclusions

Our study confirmed the association between the risk of PVL progression and the elevation of salivary MMPs. This non-invasive assessment approach has the potential to improve clinical management and follow-up of patients with PVL. Our findings support the concept that matrix metalloproteinases play a key role in the aggressive biological behavior of PVL. Beyond their mechanistic relevance, MMPs may serve as clinically useful biomarkers for risk stratification and surveillance. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate their predictive value and to define their role in routine clinical practice
