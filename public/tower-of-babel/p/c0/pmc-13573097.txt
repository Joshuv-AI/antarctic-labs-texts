Au(I) Complex With an Abnormal Carbene Ligand Shows Anti‐Kasha Dual Photoluminescence Emission
Authors: Natesan Mannangatti Rajendran, Seung Beom Cheon, Jungbin Shin, Ye Bin Jang, Taeyoon Kim, Youngmin You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report the first photoactive linear Au(I) complex with an abnormal N‐heterocyclic carbene (aNHC) ligand, Au(aIm)(Cz). The complex exhibits an anti‐Kasha dual fluorescence behavior comprised of the high‐energy, short‐lived fluorescence and low‐energy, long‐lived thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF). The anti‐Kasha emission emerges through rapid intersystem crossing (ISC) to the triplet manifold, followed by thermally activated reverse ISC to the singlet ligand‐to‐ligand charge‐transfer transition state. The aNHC ligand significantly weakens the mediation of Au in electronic transitions, thereby enabling dual fluorescence emissions. Beyond its intriguing photophysics, Au(aIm)(Cz) shows practical utility as a dual‐modal fluorescence sensor for phenyl sulfate, an important marker for diabetic kidney damage. The system exhibits a large dynamic range in both the fluorescence intensity ratio and lifetime, enabling robust detection of phenyl sulfate with high selectivity over biologically relevant interferents. These findings establish abnormal carbene coordination as a powerful design strategy for engineering unconventional excited‐state processes in photoactive linear Au(I) complexes.

Introduction

Linear, heteroleptic Au(I) complexes have emerged as a distinctive class of photofunctional molecules, bridging the fields of electroluminescence [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7], electronics [8], and photocatalysis [9, 10, 11]. A defining strength of the Au(I) complexes is in their ability to produce thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) with a short emission lifetime (τ obs) comparable to the phosphorescence emission lifetimes of well‐established Ir(III) or Pt(II) complexes [12, 13, 14, 15]. This feature, which arises from rapid and reversible intersystem crossing (ISC) between singlet and triplet excited states [16, 17, 18, 19], offers particular advantages for display applications. For example, linear Au(I) complexes function as exciton sensitizers, producing highly efficient hyperfluorescence when combined with multiresonance TADF emitters [20, 21, 22]. Moreover, the emission color of these Au(I) complexes can be widely tuned through rational ligand design, allowing access to blue‐to‐red emission while preserving favorable radiative rates [4, 5, 7, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29]. These attributes have positioned linear Au(I) complexes as promising alternatives to Ir(III) and Pt(II) emitters in next‐generation OLED architectures.

To date, the development of photoactive linear Au(I) complexes has relied predominantly on conventional carbene ligands, including cyclic (alkyl) or (aryl)(amino)carbenes (CAACs) [2, 4, 6, 18, 21, 24, 30, 31, 32, 33], mono‐ (MACs) [18, 23, 24, 34] or diamido carbenes (DACs) [35], and normal N‐heterocyclic carbenes (NHCs) [3, 5, 7, 11, 24, 25, 27, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42]. Systematic studies have shown that subtle changes in the carbene ligand can profoundly affect excited‐state ordering, radiative decay pathways, and emission efficiencies [18, 24, 26, 27, 29, 32, 41, 43]. For example, Au(I) complexes incorporating imidazol‐2‐ylidene carbene ligands display phosphorescence [44, 45], whereas those with benzimidazol‐2‐ylidene carbene ligands exhibit TADF emission exclusively. Despite these advances, however, the chemical space of carbene ligands remains narrowly defined in linear Au(I) complexes, limiting access to exotic photophysical behaviors.

An intriguing yet unexplored research avenue lies in exploring the properties of abnormal N‐heterocyclic carbene (aNHC) ligands in Au(I) complexes. Unlike normal NHCs, which bind the Au(I) center through the C2 position of the N‐heterocycle, aNHCs coordinate via the C4 position, resulting in a fundamentally different electronic structure (Figure 1a) [46, 47]. In addition, aNHCs are characterized by enhanced σ‐donor strength, reduced π‐accepting ability, and distinctive charge distribution within the carbene framework [48]. These features have been shown to stabilize unusual oxidation states and reactive intermediates in other transition‐metal systems [49, 50], but their incorporation into linear Au(I) complexes, and, critically, their impact on the excited‐state photophysics, have remained unexplored [51, 52].

Herein, we provide the first report of a photoactive Au(I) complex based on an aNHC ligand (see Figure 1a for its chemical structure). We found that the complex exhibits dual short‐lived, high‐energy prompt fluorescence (PF) and long‐lived, low‐energy TADF emissions, representing an unusual anti‐Kasha behavior within a single molecular emitter. This unique luminescence behavior distinguishes the Au(I)–aNHC complex from other dual‐emissive organic TADF molecules and metal complexes (Figure 1b). The Au(I)–aNHC complex functions as a selective sensor for the biologically relevant molecule phenyl sulfate, demonstrating its practical utility in chemical sensing. Steady‐state and ultrafast spectroscopic investigations reveal the kinetic control over excited‐state processes that gives rise to anti‐Kasha emissions. In addition, through comparative studies on structurally related Au(I) complexes bearing normal NHCs, we identify the decisive role of aNHC coordination in governing excited‐state energetics and emission pathways. These findings establish aNHC ligands as a powerful new design element for constructing photofunctional coinage metal complexes and open an unexplored direction for engineering excited‐state function.

Results and Discussion

Anti‐Kasha Dual Chromic Emission

The Au(I) complex containing carbazolide (Cz) and an imidazol‐4‐ylidene (aIm) aNHC ligand, Au(aIm)(Cz) (Figure 1a), was prepared through a five‐step synthesis, as outlined in Scheme S1. Briefly, the aIm precursor was synthesized via nucleophilic addition of 2,6‐diisopropylaniline to benzonitrile (40%), a condensation reaction between the resultant amidine and α‐bromoacetophenone to form 1‐(2,6‐diisopropylphenyl)‐2,4‐diphenyl‐1H‐imidazole (41%), and methylation of the 3‐nitrogen in the imidazole core (78%). A Au(aIm)Cl precursor was then synthesized using a two‐step protocol involving Ag2O and AuCl(SMe2) in CH2Cl2 (63%) [51]. Finally, the Cl ligand was displaced by Cz in the presence of NaOt‐Bu to afford Au(aIm)(Cz) in 75% yield. The chemical structure of Au(aIm)(Cz) was fully characterized using standard spectroscopic identification techniques, including 1H and 13C{1H} NMR spectroscopy, high‐resolution mass spectrometry, and elemental analyses. These data are compiled in the Supporting Information.

Au(aIm)(Cz) exhibits high stability in organic solvents and in the solid state under ambient conditions. Although Au(aIm)(Cz) undergoes gradual photodegradation under 365 nm irradiation, the decomposition occurs on a substantially longer timescale than that employed in the photophysical experiments (Figure S1). Therefore, the spectroscopic data reported herein predominantly reflect the intrinsic properties of Au(aIm)(Cz). Figure 1c shows the photoluminescence spectrum (λex = 370 nm) of a 10 µM Au(aIm)(Cz) in deaerated THF. Notably, dual emissions with peak wavelengths of 417 and 586 nm are observed. The dual emission is found under excitation wavelengths shorter than 400 nm, which suggests that direct excitation for the long wavelength emission is inefficient (Figure S2). The separation between the high‐ and low‐energy emissions is unusually large (6900 cm−1). Spectral comparisons with free‐base aImH and CzH ligands indicate that the vibronic 417 nm emission originates from a prompt‐fluorescent π−π* transition localized within the Au(aIm) fragment (Figure S3). In contrast, the broad emission profile of the low‐energy 586‐nm emission indicates a strong ligand‐to‐ligand charge‐transfer (LLCT) character resulting from the anionic Cz ligand to the neutral aIm ligand. These spectral assignments are supported by the short photoluminescence lifetime of the 417 nm emission (τobs, 10 ns), typical of PF, and the long τ obs of the 586 nm emission (0.42 µs), characteristic of delayed fluorescence (DF) (Figure 1d). Time‐resolved photoluminescence experiments reveal the temporal evolution from a dual PF and DF emission at short times to a single DF emission at longer times (Figure S4). The DF emission exhibits a hypsochromic shift to 528 nm and an extreme elongation of τobs to as long as 1.1 ms in a poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) matrix (5 wt% Au(aIm)(Cz)). DF emission measurements with varied doping concentrations, polymer media, viscosity, and temperature indicate that the medium‐dependence is attributed to suppression of stabilization of the DF‐emissive state in the nonpolar polymer matrix (Figures S5−S8). Au(aIm)(Cz) also retains the dual emission properties in its powder state (Figure S9).

For the low‐energy emission, the intensity decreases and τobs increases with decreasing temperature, characteristics typical of TADF (Figure S10). Kinetic analysis based on a two‐level model [53] reveals the close energetic disposition between the singlet and triplet states with an energy difference of 110 meV. In addition, aeration of the Au(aIm)(Cz) solution (10 µM in THF) quenches the low‐energy TADF emission selectively, with the high‐energy PF emission remaining relatively unperturbed (Figure S11). Both the intensity and τ obs of the low‐energy emission decrease with the solvent polarity parameter, a behavior typical of charge‐transfer emission (Figure S12). The TADF emission intensity increases with the concentration due likely to the preferential formation of the LLCT transition state, whereas the PF emission experiences concentration quenching (Figure S13). Taken together, the photoluminescence results reveal that Au(aIm)(Cz) is an anti‐Kasha dual chromic emitter, with two emissions that differ in both wavelength and lifetime. This anti‐Kasha emission behavior distinguishes Au(aIm)(Cz) from other dual‐emissive coinage metal complexes that showed Kasha‐allowed emissions−fluorescence and phosphorescence (Figure 1b) [36, 44, 45, 54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59]. In addition, the dual chromic emission of Au(aIm)(Cz) also differs from conventional TADF fluorophores that produce a single fluorescence spectrum. Photophysical data, including photoluminescence quantum yields of Au(aIm)(Cz) are listed in Table S2. UV−vis absorption spectra of Au(aIm)(Cz) and its ligands are compared in Figure S14.

Photophysical Mechanism Underlying Anti‐Kasha Emission

To elucidate the photophysical mechanism underlying the observed anti‐Kasha emission, we recorded transient absorption spectra for 2.0 mM Au(aIm)(Cz) after femtosecond (pulse duration ∼190 fs, λex = 400 nm, 1 kHz) pulsed laser excitation and for 150 µM Au(aIm)(Cz) after nanosecond (λex = 355 nm, 0.6 W) pulsed laser excitation (deaerated THF). As shown in Figures 2a and b, the femtosecond transient absorption signals contain short‐lived excited‐state absorption (ESA) bands at a peak wavelength of 675 nm and the region < 500 nm. Because the 400 nm laser photoexcites the Cz or aIm ligands predominantly, the short‐lived 675‐nm signal can be assigned to the absorption of the initially formed singlet ligand‐localized excitation (1LE) state. Rapid decay of the 1LE band (decay time (τd) = 34 ps) is accompanied with the emergence of a broad ESA band at 562 nm which persists beyond the experimental time period of > 1 ns. Global analyses reveal that this 562 nm signal exhibits a typical rise‐and‐decay profile with a rise time (τr) of 34 ps and τd > 3.0 ns (Figure 2c). The similarity of τr of the 562 nm signal and the τd of the 675 nm signal indicates a predecessor–successor relationship. Thus, this kinetics likely represents to intersystem crossing (ISC) from the 1LE state to the triplet manifold, where the triplet ligand‐to‐ligand charge‐transfer (3LLCT) state is the lowest triplet state (see Figure 2g). The upper limit of the ISC rate (k ISC) was calculated from k ISC = 1/τd(675 nm) or 1/τr(562 nm), yielding a value of 2.9 × 1010 s−1. This k ISC value is on the order of previously reported k (r)ISC values for linear Au(I) complexes with normal carbene ligands [16, 17, 23, 60], demonstrating that use of aNHC ligand rather than a normal NHC ligand does not degrade the inherent ISC kinetics driven by spin−orbit coupling of the Au center. The long‐lived ESA band of the 3LLCT state could also be observed in nanosecond transient absorption spectroscopy, although its spectra are screened by the broad TADF emission in the range 500−800 nm (Figure 2d,e). The similarity of the τ d of the ESA signals (1.2 µs) and the τ obs of the TADF emission (0.7 µs) indicates their triplet origins (Figure 2f).

Figure 2g depicts the photophysical mechanism for the dual emission. The initially populated 1LE state produces PF at a rate of 3.0 × 106 s−1 (ΦPF/τPF = 0.03/10 ns, where ΦPF and τPF are the quantum yield and lifetime of PF, respectively). However, depopulation of the 1LE state is driven by at least four orders of magnitude faster ISC to the triplet manifold (2.9 × 1010 s−1), where the 3LLCT state is the lowest triplet level. This energetic ordering is supported by our quantum chemical calculations performed at the CAM‐B3LYP level of theory (Table S3 and Figure S16). Finally, the 1LLCT state produced TADF through thermally activated rISC from the 3LLCT state. Unlike phosphorescent Au(I) complexes with normal NHC ligands [42, 61, 62], Au(aIm)(Cz) preferentially undergoes thermally activated rISC from 3LLCT to 1LLCT, resulting in delayed fluorescence rather than phosphorescence. The diminished mediation of Au in the LLCT transition state induced by the abnormal NHC ligand may contribute to this altered excited‐state decay pathway (see Effects of the abnormal carbene ligand for discussion). The key process for dual emission is ISC from the 1LE state and thermally assisted rISC to the 1LLCT state. This mechanism is reminiscent of photophysical processes in conventional TADF fluorophores or dual‐emissive single‐molecular fluorophores involving an n−π* transition or excited‐state intramolecular proton transfer (ESIPT) [63], in that the triplet state(s) mediate the emergence of the long‐lived fluorescence emission. It should be noted that this mechanism differs from the conventional mechanism for TADF as it involves two fluorescent states (i.e., the 1LE and 1LLCT states). Finally, we exclude direct internal conversion from the 1LE state to the 1LLCT state as an alternative route for dual emission because this pathway cannot account for the requirement for thermal activation. Our quantum chemical calculations indicate that the difference in geometry between the 1LLCT state and the ground state is greater than that between the 1LE state and the ground state (root‐mean‐square deviation (RMSD) = 0.365 Å for the 1LLCT state vs 0.306 Å for the 1LE state) (Figure 2h). The large geometry difference between the 1LLCT and the 1LE states suggests that direct internal conversion from the 1LE state to the 1LLCT state may be sluggish. Although single crystals of Au(aIm)(Cz) suitable for x‐ray diffraction could not be obtained despite repeated attempts, the computational protocol employed herein reproduces the experimentally determined structure of the closely related complex with high fidelity, supporting the reliability of the calculated geometries.

Effects of the Abnormal Carbene Ligand

We sought to identify the photophysical effects of the aNHC ligand by comparing linear Au(I) complexes having the aNHC ligand with those having a variety of normal NHC ligands. Four Au(I) complexes with normal NHC ligands were prepared, based on 1,3‐bis(2,6‐diisopropylphenyl)imidazol‐2‐ylidene (Au(DippIm)(Cz)) [45], 1,3‐bis(2,6‐diisopropylphenyl)benz[d]imidazol‐2‐ylidene (Au(DippBZI)(Cz)) [3], 1,3‐diphenylbenz[d]imidazol‐2‐ylidene (Au(BZI)(Cz)) [64], and 3‐bis(2,6‐diisopropylphenyl)pyrazinoimidazol‐2‐ylidene (Au(DippPZI)(Cz)) [65]. We used the Cz ligand throughout the series of Au(I) complexes in order to limit our structural control to the NHC ligand. As shown in Figure 3a, the five Au(I) complexes exhibit different emission colors under photoexcitation at 365 nm, indicating that the NHC ligand structure significantly affects the photophysical properties of complexes.

The aNHC ligand notably affects the absorption band of the 1LLCT state. As shown in Figure 3b, the UV−vis absorption spectra of the Au(I) complexes with normal NHC ligands contain vibronically well‐resolved absorption bands resulting from the ligand‐centered transition at < 360 nm and broad, low‐energy LLCT absorption bands (marked with an asterisk). The LLCT transition band is most apparent for Au(DippPZI)(Cz) (λabs = 442 nm (molar absorbance (ε) = 5.0 × 103 M−1 cm−1)), whereas the spectra of the other normal NHC complexes feature the LLCT transition as a shoulder band. For Au(DippIm)(Cz), the LLCT transition band appears at relatively short wavelength (λabs = 328 nm (ε = 1.6 × 104 M−1 cm−1)). In contrast to the spectra of the normal NHC complexes, the absorption spectrum of Au(aIm)(Cz) contains a barely discernable LLCT absorption band. The molar absorbance of the LLCT transition (εLLCT) of Au(aIm)(Cz) is < 103 M−1 cm−1, which is approximately one order of magnitude lower than the εLLCT of the Au(I) complexes with normal NHC ligands (0.36−1.6 × 104 M−1 cm−1) (Figure S17). Given that the same Cz ligand is present in all of the Au(I) complexes, this result indicates that the aNHC ligand is inefficient at promoting the LLCT transition.

We hypothesize the emergence of dual emissions might be related to the weak LLCT transition. As shown in Figure 3c, those of the 10 µM Au(I) complexes with normal NHC ligands, specifically Au(DippBZI)(Cz), Au(BZI)(Cz), and Au(DippPZI)(Cz), show broad LLCT emissions with long τobs values (0.18−1.2 µs; Table S2 and Figure S18) in deaerated THF. By contrast, Au(DippIm)(Cz) (10 µM in deaerated THF) exhibits a dual photoluminescence spectrum consisting of PF (λems = 393 nm and τobs = 3 ns) and phosphorescence (λems = 429 nm and τobs = 0.40 µs) emissions; both of these emissions result from the Cz ligand following Kasha's rule, as we reported recently [45]. The dual emission of Au(aIm)(Cz) is thus, unique because it emerges in an anti‐Kasha manner from the singlet ligand‐localized state (λ ems = 417 nm and τobs = 10 ns) and the singlet LLCT state (λems = 586 nm and τobs = 0.42 µs).

The photoluminescence excitation spectra exhibiting the PF (λobs = 417 nm) and TADF (λobs = 586 nm) fluorescence of Au(aIm)(Cz) are virtually identical (Figure S19). This high similarity suggests that direct photoexcitation of the LLCT state for TADF emission is inefficient, and is consistent with the indistinct LLCT band in the absorption spectrum of Au(aIm)(Cz). This suggestion is further supported by the results of our quantum chemical calculations at the CAM‐B3LYP level of theory (see Tables S3−S10 for the full results of calculations). Figure 3d shows the overlaps between the density‐based hole and electron distributions in the LLCT transition (S r, total(r)) for the five Au(I) complexes. S r, total(r) is directly proportional to the square of the matrix term , where and ϕS0 are the wavefunctions for the 1LLCT and ground states, respectively, and μ is the transition dipole moment, thereby quantifying the oscillator strength for the LLCT transition. Notably, in contrast to the delocalized isosurfaces for S r, total(r) observed over the Au 6s and C 2p orbitals in the Au−NHC fragment in normal NHC complexes, the S r, total(r) of Au(aIm)(Cz) shows a negligible contribution of Au. This feature is further supported by an insignificant contribution of Au in the intra‐fragment charge‐transfer analysis for the 1LLCT transition (Figure S20). The weak mediation of Au can be attributed to the presence of a nodal plane dissecting the CaNHC atom in Au(aIm)(Cz). The destructive effect is ascribed to the intrinsic lowest‐unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) patterns having a nodal plane in the aIm ligand, as shown in Figure 3e. Collectively, our spectroscopic and theoretical findings establish that the aNHC ligand weakens the mediation of Au in the LLCT transition state. This electronic character may suppress internal conversion from the 1LE to the 1LLCT state, where both of these states have identical LUMOs in the aIm ligand, enabling PF emission from the 1LE state. The claim that the aNHC ligand weakens the mediation of Au in the LLCT transition is corroborated by our electronic isolation analyses (see Tables S9 and S10 for the details). It is also found that the mediation of Au varies with the dihedral angle between the aIm and Cz ligand planes, pointing to strong conformation dependence (Figure S21). Future studies are required to establish effects of conformers.

The aNHC ligand also provides other electronic effects. The aNHC ligand is a strong σ‐donor, as demonstrated by the up‐field shift of the 13C{1H} NMR (150 MHz, CD2Cl2) peak of the CaNHC atom in Au(aIm)(Cz) (156.53 ppm) with respect to CNHC atoms in normal NHC complexes (179.24−192.33 ppm) (Figure 3f). This σ‐donation induces a slight cathodic shift of the Cz‐centered oxidation potential of Au(aIm)(Cz) (0.65 V vs. saturated calomel electrode (SCE)) relative to that of Au(DippIm)(Cz) (0.73 V vs. SCE) (Figure 3g). The reduction potential of Au(aIm)(Cz) is in the significantly positive region (−2.06 V vs. SCE) with respect to those of three of the four normal NHC complexes, the exception being Au(DippPZI)(Cz) (−1.58 V vs. SCE). As a result, the electrochemical bandgap energy of Au(aIm)(Cz) (2.71 eV) becomes comparable to that of the Au(DippPZI)(Cz) complex with a narrow bandgap PZI ligand (2.44 eV) (Figure 3h). The relatively narrow bandgap of Au(aIm)(Cz) is notable, given that the nominal π‐conjugation length in aIm is extremely short. The narrow bandgap of Au(aIm)(Cz) despite the short π‐conjugation length may be attributed to the mesoionic character of aIm. Collectively, our investigations reveal an electrochemical effect of the aNHC ligand: in particular the substantial decrease in the electrochemical bandgap energy. The large energetic separation between the PF and TADF emissions is ascribed to the profound stabilization of the 1LLCT state due to the decreased electrochemical bandgap energy.

Dual‐Modal Fluorescence Detection of Phenyl Sulfate

Having identified the anti‐Kasha fluorescence, we sought to demonstrate the utility of Au(aIm)(Cz) in a practical application, specifically the detection of phenyl sulfate, a marker for kidney disease [66]. The phenyl sulfate sensing experiments were conducted as a proof‐of‐concept application to demonstrate how the anti‐Kasha dual emission behavior can be translated into a dual‐modal photonic response based on both emission color and lifetime. Because phenyl sulfate is a biologically relevant analyte, the measurements were performed in THF containing 5 vol% aqueous 0.10 M 4‐(2‐hydroxyethyl)piperazine‐1‐ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) solution (5 vol%) buffered to pH 7.4. We found that the photoluminescence intensity ratio, quantified as the PF versus TADF intensity (I 405 nm/I 650 nm), of the Au(aIm)(Cz) solution increases markedly from 1.8 to 133 with exposure to potassium phenyl sulfate (KOSO3Ph) as shown in Figure 4a. This increase in the photoluminescence intensity ratio demonstrates a dynamic range (DR) of an almost two orders of magnitude. In addition, the ratiometric response to the presence of 1.0 mM KOSO3Ph is accompanied by a selective change in the τobs of the 650 nm TADF from 304 to 5.8 ns (Figure 4b).

The dual response to KOSO3Ph in both the photoluminescence intensity ratio and lifetime can be attributed to abrogation of the Cz ligand‐to‐aIm ligand charge‐transfer transition due to ligand substitution. This mechanism is supported by the observation that the LLCT absorption band characteristic of the anionic Cz ligand disappears on addition of 1.0 mM KOSO3Ph into 100 µM Au(aIm)(Cz) (Figure S22). Additionally, in electrospray mass spectra, addition of 1.0 mM KOSO3Ph into 100 µM Au(aIm)(Cz) elicits the emergence of the peak corresponding to an adduct of Au(aIm) and phenolate (i.e., Au(aIm)(OPh)) that is derived from hydrolysis of −OSO3Ph (Figure S23). These spectroscopic and spectrometric signatures point to the liberation of Cz, and are consistent with the selective response of the TADF emission to phenyl sulfate. Note that the photoluminescence response does not arise from conventional dynamic quenching processes. Instead, the observed sensing behaviors reflect the dosimetric transformation of Au(aIm)(Cz) into a new emissive species.

To test whether the large‐DR dual fluorescence responses (i.e., changes in the PF/TADF intensity ratio and the TADF lifetime) are selective for KOSO3Ph, we conducted experiments using a variety of biologically relevant Lewis bases, including spermine, spermidine, biotin, xanthosine, D‐ribose, thymine, uracil, citrate, lactic acid, glucose, fructose, and cortisol. None of these compounds elicited the dual photoluminescence response found for KOSO3Ph (Figures 4c and S24). Importantly, KOSO3Ph induced a pronounced shortening of τobs of the 650 nm TADF from 304 to 5.8 ns, whereas the other biologically relevant analytes caused negligible changes in τobs of the 650 nm TADF. Thus, the low τobs value observed for phenyl sulfate in Figure 4c represents the strong lifetime response of the sensor. In addition, we showed that the dual‐modal sensing capability is compatible with conventional biosensing techniques. Phenyl sulfate could be readily identified in microsolutions in a conventional 96‐well plate by recording dual‐wavelength photoluminescence intensities at 400 and 600 nm (Figures 4d and S25). Fluorescence lifetime‐imaging (FLIM) techniques could also be used to visualize the phenyl sulfate by detecting the decrease in the lifetime of the 650‐nm emission of Au(aIm)(Cz) (Figures 4e and S26).

Finally, to test whether the large‐DR dual responses can be used for the high‐fidelity quantification of KOSO3Ph, we constructed two calibration curves, one for the I 405 nm/I 650 nm ratio and another for the τ obs at 650 nm (Figure 4f). A KOSO3Ph solution was prepared by an unbiased student. Then, the I 405 nm/I 650 nm and τ obs values were determined by a researcher blinded to the KOSO3Ph solution. This blind experiment was repeated three times. The measured values (I 405 nm/I 650 nm = 2.85 ± 0.0712 and τ obs = 288 ± 5.3 ns) are inserted into the two calibration curves to yield the KOSO3Ph level. The two quantification methods enabled reliable quantification of a phenyl sulfate concentration of 49 µM. Note that this value is within the phenyl sulfate diagnostic window observed in patients with advanced diabetic kidney disease (20−50 µM) [66]. Because phenyl sulfate can serve as a useful marker for diabetic kidney disease, the capability of Au(aIm)(Cz) for large‐DR and high‐selectivity sensing should be useful for disease diagnosis.

Conclusion

In summary, we have reported the synthesis, photophysics, and sensing capability of the first linear Au(I) complex with an abnormal carbene ligand. The Au(I) complex containing the abnormal imidazol‐4‐ylidene (aIm) NHC ligand exhibits unique dual‐chromic emissions. Spectroscopic measurements revealed that the two emissions originate from PF from the high‐lying singlet LE state and TADF from the low‐lying singlet LLCT state. These two fluorescence emissions, which differ in color and emission lifetime, are shown to exhibit a true anti‐Kasha behavior. Ultrafast spectroscopic investigations, based on femto‐ and nanosecond transient absorption spectroscopy, suggest that the system follows a triplet‐detouring pathway mediated by intersystem crossing. This unique mechanism is promoted by the substantially diminished contribution of Au in the LLCT transition, an effect that is associated with the emergence of a node in the molecular orbital topology along the Au‐coordinating C atom in aIm. In addition, the abnormal NHC ligand substantially stabilizes the TADF emission energy, due to combined effects of lowering the electrochemical bandgap energy. Finally, the photonic utility of the anti‐Kasha single emitter was validated by demonstrating highly reliable detection of phenyl sulfate over biologically relevant Lewis basic species, based on the PF/TADF intensity ratio and TADF lifetime.

Although the present study focuses on Au(I), the mechanistic origin of the anti‐Kasha emission appears to be closely associated with the unique electronic topology of the aNHC ligand, particularly its ability to diminish metal mediation in the LLCT transition state. This observation raises the intriguing possibility that aNHC ligands may provide a general design strategy for engineering unconventional excited‐state processes in other coinage‐metal complexes, including Cu(I) and Ag(I), although the extent to which such behavior can be realized will depend on metal‐specific excited‐state dynamics. Exploration of these systems is currently underway in our laboratory.
