Assessment of the metabolome across the glucose tolerance spectrum in adults with cystic fibrosis hospitalized for pulmonary exacerbations
Authors: Chin-An Yang, Jonathon Batross, Lucia A Gonzalez Ramirez, Joshua Chandler, Douglas Walker, ViLinh Tran, Dean P Jones, Gabriel Olveira, Thomas R Ziegler, Vin Tangpricha, Jessica A Alvarez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Cystic fibrosis-related diabetes (CFRD) is one of the most common co-morbidities among adults with cystic fibrosis (CF), yet the mechanisms determining its development remain unclear. This study explored the underlying biological mechanisms associated with CFRD development using plasma high-resolution metabolomics (HRM).Methods:This cross-sectional study included 52 adults with CF hospitalized for a pulmonary exacerbation. Participants were categorized into three glucose tolerance groups: normal glucose tolerance (n = 26), pre-diabetes (n = 9), and CFRD (n = 17), based on data abstraction from the electronic medical record. Untargeted metabolomics were performed on fasted plasma using dual column liquid chromatography and high-resolution mass spectrometry. Analysis of covariates and pathway enrichment analysis were used to define the metabolites and pathways that differentiated between glucose tolerance groups. A sub-set analysis was performed using highest confidence metabolites quantified by reference standardization.Results:Among the 2134 features in HILIC and 1909 in C18 modes, glucose tolerance status was significantly associated with 94 and 107 features (p < 0.05), which were significantly enriched within three and eleven pathways, respectively. These pathways included redox-related processes, such as glutathione metabolism and the tricarboxylic acid cycle, as well as pentose phosphate pathway. In the sub-set analysis, two per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances were identified as decreasing with worsening glucose intolerance group (FDR q = 0.07 and 0.11, respectively).Discussion:Untargeted metabolomics demonstrated several redox-related pathways differentiating between glucose tolerance categories among adults with CF during a pulmonary exacerbation. Future targeted research is warranted to determine biological mechanisms of CFRD pathophysiology, including the role of the environment.Keywords: Cystic fibrosis, Cystic fibrosis-related diabetes, Metabolomics, Redox, Pollutants

Background

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a recessive genetic disease caused by mutations in the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene, leading to disruptions in the transport of chloride across epithelial surfaces. Consequently, the formation of abnormally thick mucus irregularly affects multiple organs and systems, including pulmonary, digestive, and endocrine systems [1]. Various complications arise during the progression of CF, with cystic fibrosis-related diabetes (CFRD) being one of the most prevalent extrapulmonary comorbidities among them [1]. Approximately half of adults with CF eventually develop CFRD, negatively impacting the quality of life and survival. Distinct from type 1 and type 2 diabetes, the factors contributing to CFRD onset and progression are little understood [2]. As the CF population ages, prevention and management of glucose intolerance in individuals with CF becomes a critical issue for maintaining life quality and survival [1].

The exact mechanisms driving the development of CFRD remain incompletely understood, with studies often concentrating on individual pathological pathways such as insulin deficiency and pancreatic islet impairment [3]. However, previous studies have revealed broader metabolic derangements in both diabetes and CF [1]. High-resolution metabolomics (HRM), which profiles thousands of metabolites via liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS), offers a powerful tool to unravel these complexities. By mapping metabolic pathways that connect environmental exposures and endogenous metabolic responses to health outcomes, HRM enables a more comprehensive exploration of glucose tolerance mechanisms in individuals with CF, potentially guiding the development of targeted diagnostics and therapies [4].

Taking advantage of HRM to interrogate the metabolome, the objective of this exploratory study was to determine the differences in plasma metabolites and correlated metabolic pathways between adults with CF in different glucose homeostasis levels. We hypothesized that plasma metabolic signatures and pathways in adults with CF would be altered in a stepwise manner as glucose intolerance worsens.

Materials and methods

Research design and participant

Participants were those enrolled in the Vitamin D for Enhancing the Immune System in Cystic Fibrosis (DISC) trial [5], a multi-center, double-blinded, randomized, placebo-controlled, intent-to-treat clinical trial designed to study the recurrence of pulmonary exacerbations of adults with CF after taking a bolus dose of high-dose vitamin D3 compared to placebo (clinicalTrials.gov NCT00788138). Study sites included Emory University, Case Western Reserve University, University of Iowa, University of Alabama at Birmingham, and University of Cincinnati. Briefly, individuals with CF who were hospitalized for a pulmonary exacerbation of CF were screened and enrolled within 48 h of hospital admission. Inclusion criteria were age greater than 18 years old, admission for acute pulmonary exacerbation of CF, having a serum 25 (OH)D level below 30 ng/mL in the previous year, and currently taking vitamin D supplements at a dosage of less than 2000 IU per day. Exclusion criteria were a history of conditions that could affect vitamin D, calcium, or phosphorus metabolism; a history of organ transplantation; or current pregnancy or plans to conceive during the trial [5]. The study was approved by each site’s Institutional Review Board, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

This sub-study used de-identified demographic data, clinical data, and stored plasma samples as research materials from the baseline visit only (before intervention). Of 91 adults with CF, five were excluded for unknown diabetes status information, 17 without fasting blood samples, and 17 without metabolomics data. After exclusion, a total of 52 CF adults were used for this sub-study (Fig. 1). Participants were categorized into three glucose homeostasis level groups: normal glucose tolerance, pre-diabetes, and diabetes, based on the diagnosis of CFRD in the medical record or fasting glucose values in the electronic medical record.

Participant metabolomics analysis workflow in HILIC and C18 modes.

High-Resolution Metabolomics (HRM)

Blood samples were collected using EDTA-containing tubes (BD Vacutainer®, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and centrifuged at 1300 RCF for 10 min. Once the separation process was complete, the resulting plasma was carefully preserved at −80 °C until the time of analysis. The untargeted HRM assessment of plasma was performed at the Emory University Clinical Biomarkers Laboratory, employing established protocols [6]. Briefly, samples were treated with acetonitrile in a 2:1 ratio (v/v), followed by the addition of internal standards. The mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C, employing established methodologies [7]. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate using a Thermo Q-Exactive HF high-resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) with hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) in positive electrospray ionization (ESI) mode and C18 chromatography in negative ESI mode. Both HILIC and C18 chromatography columns were used to maximize the detection of metabolites [8]. To ensure quality control and assurance, every batch of samples was preceded and succeeded by analyzing pooled reference samples from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (Standard Reference Material® 1950) and Equitech-Bio, Inc (SHP45, Kerville, TX). Two R packages, apLCMS [9] and xMSanalyzer [10], were used to extract the raw intensity data. The data underwent filtering to eliminate metabolic features (i.e., chemical compounds with distinct mass-to-charge ratios (m/z) and retention times) that exhibited weak correlations among technical replicates, a criterion assessed using Pearson correlation analysis. Subsequently, triplicate values were summarized using the median value.

Data processing

xmsPANDA (available at https://github.com/kuppal2/xmsPANDA), an R-based analytical program focused on -omics studies, was used for data filtering, missing values imputation, and normalization. For metabolite feature filtering, those with over 50 % missing values in all samples were removed. Half of the minimum value per feature was used for imputation. Following imputation, the feature intensity data were log2 transformed and underwent quantile normalization to mitigate the influence of variations in the bioinformatic analysis. Lastly, the R package, xMSannotator, was used to select only the features annotated with moderate-to-high confidence [11] for subsequent statistical analyses.

Statistical analysis and bioinformatics

Characteristic comparisons, data visualization, and metabolomewide association studies (MWAS) were performed using R. Demographic and clinical characteristic comparisons between the three groups were performed using Fisher exact test for categorical variables and one-way ANOVA for continuous variables. Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) and partial least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were used to visualize the metabolomic patterns between the three different glucose tolerance groups. In a MWAS, ANCOVA adjusted for sex was used to assess the significance of features between groups. The false discovery rate (FDR) by the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure was calculated. Utilizing the raw p-values derived from ANCOVA, pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using mummichog, a python-based tool designed to integrate data from untargeted metabolomics [12]. Mummichog employs permutation testing (100 permutations in this analysis) to generate an empirical null distribution, thereby estimating the statistical significance of pathway enrichment while accounting for correlations among features. Presented pathways were selected based on the criteria of 1) a p-value of pathway analysis less than 0.05, and 2) the presence of more than three metabolites enriched in the pathway.

A sub-set analysis was performed using only metabolites within an in-house library of metabolites with identities that have been confirmed via ion dissociation mass spectrometry (MS/MS) and co-elution with authentic standards. These confirmed metabolites fulfill the Level 1 identification standards established by the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) [13]. Reference standardization was performed by calibrating samples against the pooled reference standards to provide quantitative estimates based on m/z and ion intensity [14].

Metabolome-wide associations with glucose tolerance

HRM identified 13,768 and 10,309 features in the HILIC ESI positive mode and C18 ESI negative mode, respectively. After filtering out features with high missing rates and those not annotated by xMSannotator with high-to-moderate confidence, 2134 and 1909 features were retained for MWAS analysis. There were 94 and 107 features significantly different between the three groups by conducting ANCOVA (p-value <0.05) (Fig. 1). One feature (m/z 251.2005, retention time 235.8 in HILIC mode) met the FDR threshold of q = 0.20, although it did not match any previously confirmed metabolite identities.

Visual comparison of metabolite patterns between three glucose tolerance groups

The partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), utilizing filtered features with high-to-moderate confidence, demonstrated a clear separation of metabolite patterns between the normal glucose tolerance group and the CFRD group on the score plots in two different modes (Fig. 2A & B). The prediabetes group exhibited a transitional pattern overlapping with the other two groups. Similarly, the unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA), which employed significant filtered features by MWAS (p-value <0.05 by ANCOVA), also revealed a distinct separation between the normal glucose tolerance group and the CFRD group on the heatmaps (Fig. 2C & D). The heatmap results mirrored the score plots, with the pre-diabetes group located between the other two groups.

Pathway enrichment analysis

Metabolic pathway enrichment analyses unveiled a significant correlation between glucose tolerance status and metabolites enriched across a total of 13 pathways in both HILIC and C18 modes. Specifically, within the HILIC mode, we observed significant associations with pathways encompassing tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, carnitine shuttle, as well as androgen and estrogen biosynthesis and metabolism. In the C18 mode, multiple pathways displayed significant associations, including glutathione metabolism, glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, pentose phosphate pathway, fructose and mannose metabolism, androgen and estrogen biosynthesis and metabolism, and hyaluronan metabolism (Fig. 3).

Confirmed metabolite analysis

In the subset analysis of in-house MSI level 1confirmed metabolites, three metabolites were significantly associated with glucose homeostasis (VIP score >2.5 by PLS-DA, p-value <0.05 by ANCOVA, and FDR q < 0.20) (Table S1 and S2). Specifically, 6-deoxy-L-galactose (Lfucose) was significantly lower in the CFRD group compared to both the prediabetes (mean difference = 0.94 log2-abundance, 95 % CI: 0.38–1.51, p < 0.001) and normal glucose tolerance groups (mean difference = 1.00 log2-abundance, 95 % CI: 0.29–1.71, p = 0.003) (Fig. 4A). In addition, both perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS) were significantly lower in the CFRD group compared with the normal glucose tolerance group (Fig. 4B and C). For PFOA, the mean difference was 0.44 log2-abundance (95 % CI: 0.81–2.82, p = 0.02), and for PFOS, the mean difference was 1.84 log2-abundance (95 % CI: 0.23–3.45, p = 0.02).

Discussion

The mechanisms underlying the development of CFRD remain largely unclear. Using multivariate analysis approaches on the metabolome, we observed considerable variations across the spectrum of glucose tolerance in hospitalized adults with CF, along with transitional metabolome patterns in the impaired glucose tolerance group.

An increasing volume of research indicates that the pathophysiology of CFRD is connected to oxidative stress, possibly due to mutations in the CFTR gene. Hyperglycemia may worsen the clinical progression of CF by affecting immune responses, damaging pancreatic β-cells and disrupting glucose regulation in CF [15]. Hunt et al. found that pronounced systemic oxidative stress was associated with decreased insulin secretion in people with CF [16]. Our current investigation, through pathway enrichment analysis, revealed that glutathione metabolism, TCA cycle, and carnitine shuttle pathways – each with connections to redox metabolism – differentiated between glucose tolerance groups (Fig. 3). Glutathione is a critical antioxidant that helps mitigate oxidative stress, particularly in cells, linking to aging and various diseases [17]. In individuals with CF and CF animal models, studies found that disrupted glutathione metabolism and increased oxidative stress have been observed not only in lung, but also in the pancreas, suggesting that oxidative stress contributes to glucose tolerance impairment in people with CF, with glutathione metabolism being an important factor [18, 19].

The TCA cycle and carnitine shuttle pathways play a central role in mitochondrial energy production and metabolic homeostasis. Alterations in the TCA cycle reflect impaired mitochondrial function, which can lead to disruptions in cellular redox balance due to altered NADH/NAD+ and FADH2/FAD ratios [20]. Additionally, dysregulation of the carnitine shuttle, which facilitates the transport of long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria for β-oxidation, suggests a shift in substrate utilization and energy metabolism [21]. This shift may exacerbate mitochondrial stress and lead to an overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS). These findings support a role of redox imbalance and oxidative stress in CFRD pathophysiology.

Glucose-related pathways, including glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and fructose and mannose metabolism, were significantly associated with glucose tolerance. As a key source of NADPH for maintaining cellular redox balance, the pentose phosphate pathway has been shown to have reduced metabolite levels in CF human airway epithelial cells, suggesting increased cellular sensitivity to oxidative stress [22]. Similarly, dysregulated fructose metabolism has been linked to glucose intolerance [23]. Within the fructose and mannose metabolism pathway, we identified L-fucose, a metabolite confirmed at MSI level 1, to be lower among those with CFRD. L-fucose has been reported to alleviate insulin resistance by activating protein kinase B (Akt) and promoting glucose uptake [24].

Our analysis also revealed perturbation in the androgen and estrogen biosynthesis and metabolism in adults with CF with different levels of glucose intolerance. Prior studies have reported sex differences in both mortality and CFRD prevalence among adults with CF [25], with evidence indicating that estrogens and androgens play key roles in regulating insulin sensitivity and systemic inflammation [26]. These findings suggest that disruptions in sex hormone metabolism may contribute to sex disparities in CFRD progression.

Another notable finding through the pathway enrichment analysis was the involvement of hyaluronan metabolism. Hyaluronan (HA), a major glycosaminoglycan component of the extracellular matrix (ECM), plays critical roles in tissue hydration, inflammation, and cellular signaling [27]. There are studies focused on the potential effects of HA on glucose homeostasis and diabetes progression, although results are inconsistent [28]. In people with CF, reports have demonstrated that nebulized HA can improve clinical outcomes, as well as reverse CF lung chronic inflammation [29]. Further studies are needed to support a role for HA metabolism in the mechanisms of glucose homeostasis among those with CF.

In the subset analysis focusing on MSI level 1 confirmed metabolites, we identified PFOA and PFOS—two common per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs)—as being associated with impaired glucose tolerance in adults with CF. Widely used in industrial and consumer products, PFASs are persistent environmental pollutants detectable in most individuals. Prior studies have linked PFAS exposure to insulin resistance, lipid abnormalities, and organ dysfunction, including effects on the liver and kidneys [30]. Plasma levels of both PFOA and PFOS were significantly lower in the CFRD group compared to the normal glucose tolerance group, following a decreasing trend with worsening glucose tolerance. This is in contrast to previous studies showing increased circulating PFASs with impaired glucose tolerance and type 2 diabetes [30] among those without CF. Reasons for differential findings from type 2 diabetes are unclear and can only be considered speculative. However, CFRD is a distinct entity with CF-specific pathophysiology compared to type 2 diabetes [31]. In people with CF, dysfunctional CFTR impairs epithelial barrier integrity [32]. In experimental non-CF models, PFOS have been shown to disrupt tight junction proteins, enabling enhanced PFOS transport into cells [33]. Likewise, in CF cell models, hyperglycemia weakens epithelial barrier function [34]. Thus, lower PFOS concentrations among people with CF with worsening glucose tolerance may be a reflection of enhanced translocation of PFOS into cells. Detailed, direct mechanistic studies on the role of PFAS in CF and CFRD development are warranted.

It is important to note that all subjects were examined during pulmonary exacerbations, which may influence the metabolome through biological/pathophysiologic mechanisms distinct from those in stable conditions. Acute exacerbations in CF are accompanied by increased systemic inflammation and stress responses, which transiently worsen insulin resistance and glucose control [1]. Thus, our findings should be interpreted within the context of acute disease states. Future studies comparing stable versus exacerbation conditions will be important to better distinguish the chronic versus acute effects on glucose metabolism.

This untargeted metabolomics study, while innovative, has some limitations. As a secondary analysis of an existing clinical trial dataset, we were limited in sample size within specific groups, including CFRD. This may have limited the ability to detect differentiating metabolites between glucose tolerance groups that passed the FDR threshold in the MWAS. However, CFRD progression is multifactorial and heterogeneous in nature [1], and, thus, unlikely to be driven by a few metabolites with large effect sizes, but rather subtle shifts across multiple metabolic pathways. Focusing on results of pathway enrichment analyses captures broader, biologically meaningful patterns and avoids over-interpretation of individual metabolites. Further, the cross-sectional design prevents confirmation of causal relationships between CFRD and metabolome alterations. While the groups were generally demographically similar, there may have been unmeasured confounders that were not accounted for. Given that this was an exploratory study with small sample size, findings should be considered hypothesis generating. Although we statistically adjusted for sex, residual sex differences may have played a role, as reflected by differences in sex steroid pathways. Future, prospective studies should consider matched study designs with balanced sex distributions across groups, larger group sample sizes, and sex-stratified analyses to better characterize sex-specific metabolic features. Such approaches would help clarify whether the observed differences are attributable to disease progression, intrinsic sex-related biology, or their interaction.

Despite these limitations, our study serves as an innovative exploratory investigation, utilizing cutting-edge metabolomics technology that illuminates specific physiological pathways potentially involved in CFRD pathology. In summary, this study provides a comprehensive overview of the metabolome and pathways related to CFRD during acute pulmonary exacerbation. Results emphasized the potential importance of redox balance mechanisms and environmental compounds, among other pathways. Further research is needed to confirm and refine these findings, with a focus on specific pathways to better understand the links between glucose tolerance and CFRD progression.
