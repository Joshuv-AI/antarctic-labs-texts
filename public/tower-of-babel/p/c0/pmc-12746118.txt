The clinical utility of elevated monocyte count in patients with fibrotic interstitial lung disease
Authors: Bohyung Min, Daniel-Costin Marinescu, Deborah Assayag, Jolene Fisher, Nasreen Khalil, Martin Kolb, Hélène Manganas, Veronica Marcoux, William A. Fahy, Simon R. Johnson, Gauri Saini, Christopher J. Ryerson, R. Gisli Jenkins, Iain Stewart, Kerri A. Johannson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
RationaleHigher peripheral blood monocyte count has been associated with disease progression and mortality in patients with fibrotic interstitial lung disease (fILD), but with uncertainty regarding the strength of this association and the potential impact of confounding. This study aimed to characterise the associations of clinically ascertained peripheral blood monocyte count with survival and lung function decline in patients with fILD.MethodsPatients with fILD enrolled in the prospective Canadian Registry for Pulmonary Fibrosis (CARE-PF) with baseline complete blood count were included. Monocyte counts were analysed continuously and dichotomised ≥0.6 versus <0.6×109 cells·L−1 and ≥0.95 versus <0.95×109 cells·L−1. Cox proportional hazards models, unadjusted and adjusted for age, sex, lung function, smoking and treatment, evaluated associations of monocytes with transplant-free survival. Survival analysis was repeated using the prospective PROFILE cohort. Unadjusted and adjusted linear mixed models evaluated association of monocyte count with annual decline in forced vital capacity (FVC) % predicted.ResultsIn 1489 patients with fILD, higher monocyte count was associated with reduced transplant-free survival in unadjusted models, but not after adjustment for relevant confounders (continuous model, HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.54–1.17; p=0.24; dichotomised at 0.6 cells·L−1, HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.72–1.10; p=0.29; and dichotomised at 0.95 cells·L−1, HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.68–1.26; p=0.62). Findings were consistent in the PROFILE external replication cohort. Monocyte count was not associated with FVC % decline in the full cohort or within fILD subtypes.ConclusionsPeripheral blood monocyte count was not associated with transplant-free survival or lung function decline in this multicentre cohort study, indicating that it is not a reliable biomarker in fILD.

Introduction

Fibrotic interstitial lung diseases (fILDs) are a group of disorders characterised by inflammation and fibrosis of the lung parenchyma. While these conditions are heterogenous, they are often progressive and are associated with worsening physiological impairment, symptomatic morbidity and early mortality. Diagnosis and management of fILD is challenging, but several biomarkers have been tested and proposed for diagnosis or prognosis. Prognostic and theranostic biomarkers would allow for baseline risk stratification and inform cohort enrichment for clinical trials. To date there are few prognostic biomarkers for disease progression and response to treatment, and none is validated using readily available clinical tools.

Several studies have suggested a potential role for elevated monocyte counts as an informative biomarker in patients with fILD. In an analysis of patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), those with an elevated monocyte count (≥0.95×109·L−1) had an increased risk of age- and FVC-adjusted mortality [1–6]. A recent systematic review demonstrated that in five studies with IPF, there was an increased risk of mortality associated with elevated monocyte count, but there were insufficient data to support a meta-analysis of non-IPF interstitial lung disease (ILD) [7]. However, small single centre retrospective analyses have shown similar associations between higher monocyte count and disease progression in patients with indeterminate usual interstitial pneumonia on high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) pattern when dichotomised at 0.9×109·L−1 [5] and when dichotomised at the population mean in patients with RA-ILD [8]. However, these reports were heterogeneous in their study populations and definitions of progression, and did not consistently account for potential confounding.

It remains unknown whether the adverse prognosis associated with higher monocyte counts are generalisable to other fILD subtypes, or are affected by residual confounding, such as by treatment at time of blood sampling. Several variables can affect peripheral blood monocyte count but have not been fully accounted for in most prior studies, which were largely single centre and focused on specific ILD subtypes. The objective of this study was to determine whether elevated peripheral blood monocyte counts were associated with transplant-free survival and lung function decline in a large multicentre cohort of patients with a variety of fILD subtypes. We then sought to replicate our survival findings in an independent external cohort.

Methods

Study population

Patients with fILD enrolled in the multicentre Canadian Registry for Pulmonary Fibrosis (CARE-PF) [9] were included if they had a complete blood count (CBC) within 90 days of the date of diagnosis. Eligible fILDs included IPF, non-IPF idiopathic interstitial pneumonia, fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis (fHP), connective tissue disease (CTD)-ILD and unclassifiable ILD (uILD). The date of diagnosis was defined as date of first definitive evidence of ILD on chest HRCT. Study protocols were approved by the institutional review board/ethics committee at each centre (coordinating site, University of British Columbia Research Ethics Board approval no. H22-00780). The Prospective Observation of Fibrosis in the Lung Clinical Endpoints (PROFILE) study is a multicentre cohort study of incident treatment-naive cases of IPF and fibrotic idiopathic nonspecific interstitial pneumonia from two coordinating centres in the United Kingdom: Nottingham University Hospitals and Royal Brompton Hospital (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT01134822) [10]. The PROFILE dataset was analysed as a replication cohort for the survival analysis conducted in CARE-PF.

Sample collection and measurements

Peripheral blood monocyte counts were obtained from the CBC drawn closest to the date of diagnosis, and all were obtained within 90 days (figure 1). Clinical data ascertained from CARE-PF included age, sex, smoking status, diagnosis, baseline and longitudinal FVC % predicted and diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO ) % predicted, and dates of death or lung transplantation up to the time of censoring. Lung function data were recorded from within 1 year before and up to 2 years after the CBC. Chronic use and typical daily dose of immunosuppression was recorded, including mycophenolate, azathioprine, cyclosporine, cyclophosphamide, tacrolimus, tocilizumab, rituximab, methotrexate and oral prednisone. Antifibrotic (nintedanib and pirfenidone) use and dosage were also recorded. “Baseline treatment” was defined as any immunosuppression received for greater than 90 days prior to the date of CBC, and “ever treatment” was defined as antifibrotic therapy or immunosuppression received for greater than 90 days after the date of CBC. Patients with at least two FVC % measurements within 1 year prior to and up to 2 years after the date of CBC were evaluated for annual FVC % change. Clinical data from the PROFILE cohort included age, sex, smoking status, baseline FVC % predicted, DLCO % predicted, dates of death and peripheral blood monocyte counts from the CBC drawn up to 1 year from baseline.

Statistical analysis

Data are shown as mean±sd, median (interquartile range) or number (%). Monocyte counts were analysed as a continuous variable and by dichotomised thresholds used in prior studies, including at the population mean (0.6×109 cells·L−1) and at the upper limit of the normal range (0.95×109 cells·L−1) [1, 2]. Cox proportional hazards models assessed the relationship between monocyte count and time to event. In the CARE-PF cohort, the event was death or lung transplantation, unadjusted and in models adjusting for age, sex, baseline FVC %, smoking status and both baseline and ever treatment. Time-to-event analysis (as competing risks) was performed for the overall cohort and in subgroups of IPF and non-IPF ILD. For the replication cohort, time to event was death only, as there were no lung transplant events in this cohort during the follow-up period. In the PROFILE cohort, models were adjusted for age, sex, baseline FVC %, smoking status and ever treatment with antifibrotics. Survival was visualised using Kaplan–Meier curves and the log-rank test. Linear mixed models were used to estimate the annual change in FVC % over time as a function of monocyte count, both unadjusted and adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, baseline treatment, ever treatment, and both baseline and ever treatment. Models were also tested that excluded patients who received baseline treatment and adjusted for age, sex, smoking status and ever treatment.

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

A total of 1489 patients were included from CARE-PF (table 1). The diagnostic subgroups were CTD-ILD (36%), IPF (32%), uILD (21%) and fHP (11%). The median duration between date of diagnosis and date of monocyte count measurement was 23 (7–49) days. The mean monocyte count for all patients was 0.66±0.26×109 cells·L−1. Most patients (89%) had monocyte counts within the normal range <0.95×109 cells·L−1, and a minority (11%) had elevated monocyte counts ≥0.95×109 cells·L−1. Patients with elevated monocyte counts had slightly lower FVC % and DLCO %, were more often male and of older age. There was no clear difference in use of immunosuppression or antifibrotics at baseline across monocyte count. Follow-up time was 5 (3–7) years, during which 30% (n=424 died) and 8% (n=73) underwent lung transplantation. The PROFILE cohort included 197 patients. Like the CARE-PF cohort, 83% had monocyte counts within the normal range and 17% had elevated monocyte counts (table 2). The median duration between date of diagnosis and date of monocyte count measurement was 6 (0–12) months.

Transplant-free survival

Considered as a continuous variable, higher monocyte counts were associated with worse transplant-free survival (HR 1.58 per 0.1×109 cells·L−1, 95% CI 1.14–2.20; p<0.01) in unadjusted models for the overall cohort (figure 2), but this did not persist in models adjusted for age, sex, smoking, FVC % and treatment (figure 3a). Similarly, monocyte count ≥0.6×109·L−1 or ≥0.95×109·L−1 was associated with worse transplant-free survival in unadjusted models (figures 2 and 3a). However, neither of these associations persisted in the adjusted models (figure 3a).

Change in FVC %

The mean annual FVC % change for the overall cohort was −0.92% (−1.06 to −0.78), −3.09% (−3.36 to −2.82) for IPF, and −0.28% (−0.44 to −0.12) for non-IPF. FVC % change was not associated with monocyte count, either as a continuous variable or using a threshold of 0.6×109 cells·L−1, or with unadjusted or adjusted analysis. This lack of association was consistent in the overall cohort and when stratified by ILD subtype (table 4). In the overall cohort, patients with elevated monocyte counts (≥0.95×109 cells·L−1) had lower annual FVC % decline compared with those with normal monocyte counts in both unadjusted (β=0.88; p=0.057) and adjusted models (β=0.88; p=0.04). This association was not seen in IPF and seemed to be driven by the non-IPF subgroup, whereas this finding persisted in adjusted models (table 4).

Discussion

In this multicentre study of patients with fILD, we found that baseline peripheral blood monocyte count was only associated with greater risk of death or lung transplant in the unadjusted analysis. However, following adjustment for potential confounders, including disease severity and treatment, the association was lost. Similarly, higher monocyte count was not consistently associated with accelerated lung function decline. Together, these findings suggest that monocyte counts are not reliable prognostic biomarkers in patients with fibrotic ILD and more likely reflect ILD severity.

Our findings contrast with some prior studies showing that peripheral blood monocyte count was associated with worse clinical outcomes in patients with IPF [1–3]. Monocyte counts were associated with worse outcomes in univariate analyses; however, this relationship may be driven by their association with disease severity. When adjusted for established predictors, monocyte count added little additional prognostic value. This suggests that while monocyte levels may reflect disease burden, they offer limited utility as an independent biomarker beyond existing clinical tools. Heterogeneity in prior analyses complicates the association between monocyte count and outcome, with inconsistent adjusted variables, including age, sex, GAP index, FVC %, DLCO %, 6-min walk distance, smoking and other comorbidities [1–3]. Furthermore, variable timepoints for monocyte counts were also used, with peripheral blood monocyte counts often obtained from CBCs prior to initiating antifibrotic therapy [1–3, 11]. Furthermore, the study populations were also different. The CARE-PF cohort included a majority of non-IPF diagnoses (68%) compared with prior studies that included mostly IPF [1–3] and scleroderma-related ILD [6]. Specifically, our study involved a greater proportion of patients with CTD-ILD (32%), which may further explain the lack of a relationship between elevated monocytes and transplant-free survival. Patients with CTD-ILD may demonstrate lower peripheral monocyte counts, the majority (n=424, 80%) receiving immunosuppressive therapy, and the prognosis of CTD-ILD is better than in IPF [12]. Patients with CTD-ILD had the lowest monocyte counts (0.63×109 cells·L−1) compared with other fibrotic ILDs, indicating heterogeneity across fILDs and need for disease-specific approaches to prognostication and biomarker use. Finally, the effect of treatment including antifibrotic therapy and immunomodulatory treatment is unclear.

In our study, high peripheral blood monocyte counts were not associated with disease progression in IPF. Previous studies showed inconsistent associations and used variable definitions of progression [2, 3]. Our finding that high monocyte counts were associated with slowed disease progression in patients with non-IPF may reflect more inflammatory pathways in these patients, potentially responsive to immunomodulatory medications. A high proportion of patients were also on prednisone (n=299, 28.7%), which may preserve lung function in the non-IPF subgroup. This is possibly an aberrant finding but given its consistency in both adjusted and unadjusted models, it warrants further study in the non-IPF population to understand how immunosuppressive medications impact monocyte counts and potentially mediate response to therapy.

While our findings suggest that peripheral blood monocytes may not drive disease progression in fILD, further work is needed to clarify the effects of specific cell subsets. Recent single-cell RNA sequencing of peripheral blood monocytes suggests that classical and intermediate monocytes are increased in progressive IPF, whereas total lymphocytes are decreased [5]. Monocytes exhibit complex roles in tissue repair, regeneration and fibrosis [13]. Peripheral blood monocytes can migrate to lung tissue during injury and inflammation [14–16] and are thought to be the origin of SPP1/osteopontin-expressing fibrotic macrophages [17]; osteopontin levels are associated with poor outcomes in IPF [18]. Measuring different subpopulations of peripheral blood monocytes may clarify their relationship with clinical outcomes in fILD. Prior in vitro and in vivo studies showed that CD14+ monocyte precursors give rise to fibrocytes, which could support a role for elevated monocytes in the pathogenesis of fILD [16, 19–21]. Fibrocytes are CD45+, Col1+ monocytes and using a threshold of 5% high levels of fibrocytes have been associated with increased mortality in IPF including the PROFILE study and fILD studies [22–24]. Recently, through single-cell RNA sequencing, a gene-signature derived from the transcriptome of a specific monocyte subtype (CD14+CD163+HLADRlow) was shown to be associated with lower transplant-free survival in IPF patients [25]. A previous study demonstrated that increases in the CD14+ CD16+ subset of monocytes measured at baseline correlated with disease progression in patients with IPF; however, when measured longitudinally, these monocytes did not consistently remain elevated in IPF cases with progressive disease [26]. Similarly, a change in monocyte count from baseline over 90 days was not associated with disease progression or increased mortality [2]. These results underscore the need to measure peripheral blood monocytes over time in longitudinal cohorts to determine their role as a prognostic or theranostic biomarker in determining response to treatment. Peripheral blood monocytes may be impacted by several external factors (i.e. treatment, diagnosis, age and infection), thus being unreliable as a biomarker using clinically ascertained samples.

Our study has several limitations. Residual confounding due to therapy was mitigated by adjustment for baseline treatment, but it was not possible to fully adjust for the impact of changes in treatment or for comorbidities that could potentially impact monocyte counts. Both CARE-PF and PROFILE are enrolled at specialised ILD centres and may not represent the general ILD population. In our study, the CBC was measured within 90 days of the date of diagnosis, which was defined as the date of first radiographic evidence of ILD. This captured disease in various states of severity, which was accounted for by adjusting for FVC % and DLCO. We adjusted for treatment as a potential confounding variable by including both immunosuppression and antifibrotic therapy, which was not consistently done in prior studies. Importantly, the limitations inherent to secondary analyses of registry data were mitigated by replication of the survival analyses and findings in two independent cohorts. Last, acute exacerbations were not consistently documented in either cohort, limiting our ability to determine relationships between monocyte counts and acute events across ILD subtypes. Bronchoscopic alveolar lavage was also infrequently performed and not systematically collected, preventing comparison between peripheral and alveolar monocyte counts. While the use of monocyte counts as a biomarker is appealing due to ease and cost of measurement, prospective implementation studies to determine the role of monocyte count in the management of fILD are needed.

Conclusions

Peripheral blood monocyte counts do not seem to add value as a biomarker of disease progression and mortality in patients with fILD, and monocyte counts overall are not likely the driver of clinical outcomes. Conflicting results compared with other published reports highlight the importance of adjustment for confounding variables, and external replication of findings. Further studies are needed to robustly characterise the pathobiology and effects of specific monocyte subsets in patients with fILD.
