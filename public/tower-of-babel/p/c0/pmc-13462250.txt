Early venetoclax dose escalation at minimal residual disease progression may benefit selected acute myeloid leukaemia patients
Authors: Sari Kytölä, Anu Partanen, Milla E. L. Kuusisto, Sirpa Koskela, Annasofia Holopainen, Ida Vänttinen, Tanja Ruokoranta, Pia Ettala, Johanna Rimpiläinen, Timo Siitonen, Marja Pyörälä, Heikki Kuusanmäki, Mika Kontro
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

The combination of venetoclax and hypomethylating agent (HMA) has improved outcomes for patients with newly diagnosed acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) who are ineligible for intensive chemotherapy while the efficacy in secondary and relapsed or refractory (R/R) setting remains more limited. 1 , 2 Achieving minimal residual disease (MRD) negativity is associated with more durable remissions and prolonged survival. Nonetheless, the regimen is frequently complicated by severe or prolonged cytopenia and infections, which can necessitate dose modifications and interrupt treatment continuity. In current practice, dosing of both venetoclax and HMA is reduced in responding patients—venetoclax is typically decreased from 21 to 14 days per cycle. 3 Ultimately, disease progression occurs in most patients, either during or after treatment.

Therapeutic options following disease progression remain limited. Targeted agents may be used in patients with actionable mutations, such as IDH1/2 or FLT3‐ITD; however, responses are often poor. 4 For patients relapsing after venetoclax‐HMA–induced remission and treatment discontinuation due to adverse events, re‐challenge with the same regimen may be beneficial. 5 For others, valid options are currently lacking. Given that a subset of patients responds to re‐challenge, venetoclax dose escalation may be a rational strategy in cases of on‐treatment progression when cytopenia is not a limiting factor. However, clinical data supporting this approach are limited. To investigate this further, we analysed the outcome of AML patients who underwent venetoclax dose escalation due to either MRD progression or morphological relapse during azacitidine and venetoclax therapy within our recent prospective VenEx trial.

The VenEx trial (NCT04267081) enrolled 104 eligible participants between 12 February 2020 and 13 April 2022, including 48 patients with newly diagnosed and 56 with secondary AML (sAML) or (R/R) AML. 6 , 7 Of these, 92 patients received study therapy. The treatment regimen consisted of 28‐day cycles of venetoclax and azacitidine, with the option to extend up to 42 days for responders to recover from cytopenia. If haematological recovery was not observed by day 42, an additional 14‐day recovery period was allowed after at least two therapy cycles. Venetoclax was initiated with a ramp‐up dosing schedule of 100–200–400 mg over 3 days, followed by a daily dose of 400 mg for up to 28 days per cycle until morphological remission was achieved. Azacitidine was administered subcutaneously for 7 days per cycle, with a reduction to 5 days after six cycles in responding patients. For responders, the duration of venetoclax was reduced to 21 days, and further to 14 days after three cycles if the response was sustained. The study protocol permitted continuation of therapy and re‐escalation of venetoclax duration after AML progression, at the investigator's discretion.

Morphological response was evaluated from bone marrow smears after the first and third cycles. For MRD assessment, the most appropriate marker was selected from immunophenotyping (IFT), fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), digital droplet polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) or allele‐specific oligonucleotide PCR (ASO‐PCR). 8 After achieving morphological remission, subsequent evaluations of both morphological response and MRD were performed every three cycles, as well as whenever disease progression was suspected. Morphological relapse was defined by either appearance of blasts in peripheral blood or excess blasts (>5%) in the bone marrow. MRD progression was defined by reappearing MRD after initial negativity or any increase in MRD percentage based on the patient‐specific method.

Among the 92 participants who received study therapy, 18 (19.6%) were primary refractory and 2 (2.2%) died early during the first cycle before response evaluation. Complete remission (CR) or complete remission with incomplete recovery (CRi) was achieved by 59 (64.1%) patients and morphological leukaemia‐free state (MLFS) by 13 (14.1%). Overall, of the responding 72 patients, 30 (41.7%) achieved MRD negativity, while 31 (43.0%) remained MRD positive. For 11 (15.3%), MRD evaluation was not available due to either the absence of a suitable marker or lack of evaluation (Figure 1A). At the time of the analysis, 12 patients had ended the therapy due to toxicity, one had proceeded to allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation and 19 continued to respond to therapy.

At the time of progression, venetoclax was escalated, as defined per protocol, from 14 to 21 days in 25/40 (63%) of eligible patients. Reasons to not pursue escalation included rapid progression of leukaemia, declining clinical condition and severe cytopenias and for one patient extra‐medullary relapse. Two patients were excluded from the analysis: one who proceeded to allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation and another who discontinued therapy due to suboptimal adherence; both received only one cycle of escalated therapy. Thus, our analysis included 23 participants with venetoclax dose escalation from 14 to 21 days for at least one cycle due to either MRD progression (N = 11) or morphological relapse (N = 12) (Figure 1A). The data cut‐off for this analysis was January 31, 2025. At the time of the analysis, 12 patients had ended therapy due to toxicity.

Among de novo AML patients, the median overall survival (OS) was significantly longer when escalation was initiated due to MRD progression compared to morphological relapse (13.6 months [95% confidence interval (CI), 7.4–NA] vs. 3.1 months [95% CI, 1.5–not applicable (NA)]; p = 0.001). In patients with secondary or relapsed/refractory (s/R/R) AML, the median OS following venetoclax escalation was poor, with no significant difference observed between those escalated for MRD progression and those escalated for morphological relapse (1.9 months [95% CI, 1.5–NA] vs. 2.9 months [95% CI, 1.9–NA]; p = 0.11) (Figures 1B and 2A).

In this cohort, when prompted by morphological relapse, the efficacy of the venetoclax dose escalation was notably poor for all participants. For patients with de novo AML, especially those harbouring IDH2 mutation, OS benefit was observed when venetoclax escalation was initiated early, after MRD progression. However, no patients converted to MRD negativity following dose escalation. Notably, two patients who appeared to derive the greatest benefit from dose escalation had newly diagnosed AML with co‐occurring IDH2 and DNMT3A mutations (Figure 1B).

This analysis suggests that a subset of newly diagnosed AML patients, especially those with IDH2 mutations, may benefit from venetoclax dose escalation when initiated soon after the detection of MRD progression. However, the analysis has several inherent limitations to consider. The limited sample size precludes definitive conclusions. Additional data would be beneficial to compare whether re‐escalation of venetoclax results in significantly longer OS in newly diagnosed AML patients in MRD progression compared to continued 14‐day venetoclax dosing. In our study, all eligible patients underwent dose escalation when AML progression was detected, and no equivalent control group was available for analysis. Furthermore, four patients subsequently enrolled in another clinical trial, which may have shortened the duration of venetoclax dose escalation and influenced survival outcomes. Notably, enasidenib or ivosidenib were not available during the study period due to lack of EMA approval or ongoing compassionate use programs. Response assessments every three cycles led to variable progression detection, ranging from early progression to morphological relapse, with heterogeneous MRD markers further complicating interpretation. Additionally, often the rapid pace of progression precluded repeated confirmatory testing.

As dose escalation generally has little impact on the outcome, an alternative strategy in selected patients with deep responses may be to discontinue venetoclax and HMA and re‐challenge at progression. Although this remains controversial and requires further validation, re‐initiation following a treatment pause is potentially preferable to dose escalation during ongoing therapy, as continuous exposure may increase the risk of resistance development.

Our findings suggest that the benefits of venetoclax dose escalation are minimal in s/R/R AML and in all cases of morphological relapse during venetoclax and azacitidine therapy. In contrast, a subset of previously untreated patients may derive benefit when dose escalation is implemented early, at the time of MRD progression. This highlights the potential value of MRD monitoring in newly diagnosed AML patients lacking treatment options at progression, particularly when their clinical status and cytopenia levels allow the dose escalation.
