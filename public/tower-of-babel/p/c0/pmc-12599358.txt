Epidemiological and clinical impact of pneumococcal disease in Spain in 2023: A nationwide retrospective analysis
Authors: Rafael Garcia-Carretero, Ruth Gil-Prieto, Valentin Hernandez-Barrera, Angel Gil-de-Miguel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStreptococcus pneumoniae remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality in Spain, particularly among older adults and individuals with comorbidities. This nationwide retrospective study analyzed hospitalizations in 2023 due to pneumococcal pneumonia, sepsis, and meningitis using data from the Spanish Minimum Basic Data Set at Hospitalization (MBDS-H), covering 98% of public hospitals. A total of 66,391 hospitalizations were recorded, with an overall rate of 140.94 per 100,000 population. Pneumonia was the most common presentation, followed by sepsis and meningitis. Hospitalization and mortality rates increased with age, peaking in individuals aged ≥90 years. Men consistently showed higher rates than women. The overall in-hospital mortality rate was 13.27 per 100,000, with sepsis showing the highest case fatality. Comorbidities such as cerebrovascular disease, malignancy, and chronic kidney disease were strongly associated with increased mortality risk. Despite the introduction of broader-spectrum vaccines like PCV15 and PCV20, adult vaccination coverage remains suboptimal. The emergence of non-PCV13 serotypes highlights the need for improved immunization strategies targeting high-risk adult populations. Pneumococcal disease continues to pose a major public health challenge in Spain. Strengthening adult vaccination programs and prioritizing vulnerable groups are essential to reduce hospitalizations and mortality.Keywords: Streptococcus pneumoniae, pneumococcal disease, epidemiology, comorbidities, hospitalizations, Spain

Introduction

Streptococcus pneumoniae is a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, particularly among young children, older adults, and individuals with underlying health conditions.1 It is responsible for millions of cases of pneumonia and hundreds of thousands of deaths annually despite the availability of effective antibiotics and vaccines.1,2 It is the cause of several severe conditions, such as pneumonia, meningitis, and sepsis. All of these diseases may require hospitalization. Beyond its clinical severity, pneumococcal disease imposes a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems due to costs associated with hospitalization, and long-term sequelae.2

Despite the implementation of national immunization strategies and the availability of pneumococcal conjugate vaccines, such as PCV13 and PCV20, S. pneumoniae has a significant health impact in Spain.3 The effectiveness of vaccination programs in reducing invasive pneumococcal disease among children has been well-documented.4,5 However, hospital admissions due to pneumococcal infections have a significant health burden, particularly in the elderly and in patients with comorbidities.6,7

The burden of pneumococcal disease is not distributed equally across populations. It disproportionately affects individuals with specific comorbidities, such as chronic cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, and liver diseases, as well as diabetes, and immunocompromising conditions, which are known to significantly increase the risk of invasive infection and mortality.8,9 Furthermore, socioeconomic factors, including lower education levels, income inequality, and barriers to healthcare access, can influence both vaccination uptake and disease susceptibility, creating significant health disparities.10 Despite the recognized efficacy of vaccines, adult vaccination coverage in Spain and many other countries remains suboptimal, often hindered by complex recommendation schedules, lack of provider reinforcement, and low public awareness of risk.10,11 This composite of clinical and social factors highlights the need for ongoing, detailed epidemiological surveillance to identify at-risk groups and evaluate the real-world impact of vaccination programs.

Hospital discharge data provide a valuable source of epidemiological information for monitoring the burden of pneumococcal disease.8,10 Recent analyses covering from 2016 up to 2022 in Spain highlighted high hospitalization and mortality rates, particularly among the elderly.1 However, there is a need to monitor and update these data using the more recent registry to plan vaccination strategies and public health interventions. Given the rapidly changing epidemiology of pneumococcal disease, and vaccination efforts, current and detailed national-level data are key for public health decision-making.

This brief report aims to provide updated, population-based data for 2023 on the burden of S. pneumoniae-related hospitalizations in Spain, focusing on pneumococcal pneumonia, meningitis, and sepsis due to S. pneumoniae. Our objective is to describe the incidence, mortality, and age-related trends to support evidence-based public health policy and vaccination strategy.

Methods

We performed a retrospective study utilized administrative data provided and validated by the Spanish Ministry of Health for epidemiological research. This nationwide registry is the Minimum Basic Data Set at Hospitalization (MBDS-H), covering approximately 98% of public hospitals. All data are publicly available through the Spanish National Health System upon reasonable request.12 The data utilized herein were anonymized, and no identifying information was included in the manuscript. Because the authors used historical data and do not contain personal identifying information, the Ethical Board of Universidad Rey Juan Carlos considered informed consent was not necessary. So, the need for informed consent to participate was waived by our Ethical Board.

The MBDS-H uses administrative and diagnosis-related data from discharge reports and is coded using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10-CM). The codes used to extract data were the following: J13 (pneumococcal pneumonia), A40.3 (pneumococcal sepsis), and G00.1 (pneumococcal meningitis). All age groups were analyzed, with stratification by sex, age, and comorbidities. i.e., diabetes, malignancy, etc. We calculated hospitalization/mortality rates (per 100,000 population), and mortality-related odds ratios (ORs) for comorbidities, using logistic regression. To account for multiple comparisons across comorbidity subgroups, p-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni method, since it is a simple and conservative method. It reduces the chance of false positives but it is very strict and can increase the chance of missing real effects (false negatives). Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4 and SPSS 27, and significance was set at p < .05.

Results

In 2023, a total of 66,391 hospitalizations were recorded in Spain due to diseases caused by S. pneumoniae. The overall hospitalization rate was 140.94 cases per 100,000 population (95% CI: 139.88–142.00), with pneumococcal pneumonia being the most frequent condition (44.31/100,000), followed by sepsis (1.34/100,000) and meningitis (0.84/100,000). Supplementary Table S1 and Figure 1 show the results for hospitalization incidence. Hospitalization rates increased with age, particularly in individuals aged ≥90 years (1,046.53/100,000), with rates significantly higher in men compared to women across all categories.

Hospitalization rate per 100,000 for pneumococcal disease in Spain (year 2023).

The overall in-hospital mortality rate for pneumococcal disease was 13.27 deaths per 100,000 population (95% CI: 13.01–13.53), with the highest mortality observed in patients aged ≥90 years (20.74/100,000). Sepsis was associated with the highest case fatality, with an in-hospital mortality rate of 22.83/100,000, followed by pneumonia (8.37/100,000) and meningitis (7.20/100,000). Men showed higher mortality rates than women, particularly for pneumonia and sepsis. Supplementary Table S2 shows the results for case-fatality rates.

Pneumonia

Pneumonia accounted for the majority ofS. pneumoniae-related hospitalizations in Spain, with a total hospitalization rate of 44.31 per 100,000 population (95% CI: 43.72–44.90). The rate increased progressively with age, reaching its highest value in patients over 90 years (416.02/100,000), followed by the 85–89 (266.49/100,000) and 80–84-year groups (184.35/100,000). Hospitalization rates were consistently higher in men than in women across nearly all age groups.

The in-hospital mortality rate due to pneumococcal pneumonia was 8.37 deaths per 100,000 population (95% CI: 8.00–8.74), and increased notably with age (Figure 2). The highest mortality rate was observed among patients aged ≥80. Case fatality rates were also higher among men than women, although sex-related differences were less marked than those observed for hospitalization rates.

Sepsis

Sepsis represented a hospitalization rate of 1.34 per 100,000 population (95% CI: 1.24–1.44). The rate showed a marked age-related increase, with a peak of 9.40/100,000 in patients aged ≥90 years. Men had higher hospitalization rates than women across all age groups, with statistically significant differences in the elderly population.

Among all three pneumococcal conditions analyzed, sepsis was associated with the highest in-hospital mortality rate, reaching 22.83 deaths per 100,000 population (95% CI: 19.59–26.07). Mortality rates increased significantly with age, particularly in individuals over 85–89 (Figure 2). The overall case fatality rate for pneumococcal sepsis is worth mentioning, which likely reflects the severity of this clinical presentation.

Patients with comorbidities had significantly greater odds of in-hospital death from sepsis compared to those without (OR: 3.1, 95% CI: 2.0–4.9, p < .01; mortality rate: 20.06% vs. 7.39%). Among the underlying predictors analyzed, cerebrovascular disease (OR: 8.0, 95% CI: 4.6–14.1, p < .01), malignancy (OR: 4.8, 95% CI: 2.9–8.0, p < .01), chronic kidney disease (OR: 3.9, 95% CI: 2.5–6.3, p < .01), and chronic liver disease (OR: 3.5, 95% CI: 1.8–6.6, p < .05) were strongly associated with increased mortality. Diabetes mellitus (OR: 3.5, 95% CI: 2.2–5.7, p < .05) and cardiopulmonary disease (OR: 3.3, 95% CI: 2.1–5.3, p < .05) also showed as significant risk factors for death in patients with pneumococcal sepsis.

Meningitis

The hospitalization rate for pneumococcal meningitis was 0.84 per 100,000 population (95% CI: 0.76–0.92), the lowest among the three pneumococcal disease groups studied. Incidence was highest among infants (5.76/100,000), but declined with age, with a second peak observed in older adults. Differences by sex were not statistically significant across most age groups.

The in-hospital mortality rate due to meningitis was 7.20 per 100,000 population (95% CI: 4.68–9.72). Mortality was frequent in the elderly, with patients over 85 years showing the most elevated rates (Figure 2). However, it is worth mentioning the low number of meningitis cases, which may result in wider confidence intervals and some variability across age groups. It is important to consider that when the number of events is extremely small, the calculation method for confidence intervals may result in a negative value for the lower confidence limit. This occurrence is often attributed to the limited number of events. Consequently, these results should be interpreted with care.

The presence of comorbidities significantly increased the risk of death from pneumococcal meningitis (OR: 4.4, 95%; mortality rate: 13.57% vs. 3.45% in patients without comorbidities). It is worth noting that patients with malignancy had the highest risk of death (OR: 13.2), followed by patients with chronic kidney disease (OR: 9.8) and chronic liver disease (OR: 9.3). HIV infection (OR: 8.0, 22.22%) and diabetes mellitus (OR: 5.9, 17.53%) were also associated with risk of mortality.

Discussion

Our nationwide study provides an updated overview of the burden of pneumococcal disease in Spain in 2023, focusing on hospitalizations due to pneumococcal pneumonia, sepsis, and meningitis. We described the burden of Streptococcus pneumoniae-related hospitalizations, with over 66,000 cases recorded. Our findings confirm that Streptococcus pneumoniae continues to represent a significant cause of severe disease and in-hospital mortality, particularly among the elderly and patients with underlying comorbidities. Pneumococcal pneumonia was the most frequent clinical presentation, while sepsis, though less frequent, had the highest mortality. This pattern is in line other authors and global data.1,2 As mentioned, our findings highlights the role of age, sex, and comorbidities in both hospitalization and mortality.

Compared to a previous report in which hospitalization rate was 108.4 per 100,000 population,1 we noticed a rising trend over the years, with 140.94 cases per 100,000 population in 2023. Also, we observed an increase in hospitalization rates with age, up to a peak at 1,046.53 per 100,000 population in adults ≥90 years. This finding is in line with other authors concerning immunosenescence and comorbid vulnerability.7 The decrease in the hospitalization rates shown in 2020 may have been due to the COVID-19 pandemic, as pointed out in a recent research.1 SARS-CoV-2, as a respiratory virus, can increase susceptibility to pneumococcal pneumonia or invasive pneumococcal diseases. However, coinfection of SARS-CoV-2 and S. pneumoniae were infrequent.1 There is no clear hypothesis to explain this phenomenon. Men exhibited higher hospitalization and mortality rates across all pneumococcal diseases, although we cannot explain such differences. These differences were reported in a previous research, although we cannot hypothesize any explanation because it would be merely speculative.6

It is worth noting the fivefold increase in mortality among patients with comorbidities (OR = 6.6). This finding highlights the correlation between S. pneumoniae and underlying chronic conditions. As mentioned, cerebrovascular disease (OR = 10.5), malignancies (OR = 11.5), and chronic kidney failure (OR = 9.8) were the strongest predictors of death, in line with other studies reporting the association of these conditions with immune dysfunction and poorer outcomes.10 The more severe clinical presentation, pneumococcal sepsis, showed a mortality of 22.83 per 100,000 population, which reflects the great impact of this condition. This should urge physicians to an early recognition of S. pneumoniae-related diseases.

Pediatric PCV13 inclusion in Spain’s immunization program began in 2016 with high vaccination coverage rates. New vaccines have been incorporated recently, PCV15 was approved in children in a 2 + 1 regimen in 2023 and PCV20 in 2024 in a 3 + 1 schedule. However, adult coverage rates remains suboptimal in may regions, due to factors such as complex and varied vaccination recommendations, limited provider knowledge, insufficient patient awareness, difficulties in accessing and tracking vaccination history, racial and ethnic disparities, and varying national guideline adoption. These issues contribute to gaps in vaccine administration, particularly among younger adults, those with risk conditions, and certain racial/ethnic groups.10 Epidemiological context and data at national level cannot be extrapolated from other populations or countries because of the different burden of disease, circulating serotypes or vaccine calendars. However, while pneumococcal vaccination coverage in Spanish adults has improved in recent years, it remains around 20–25% for key groups like the elderly and those with chronic conditions, which is well below public health targets.11 The resurgence of invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) caused by non-PCV13 serotypes such as 24F, 11A, and 9N is well-documented in global surveillance analyses, reflecting serotype replacement following widespread use of PCV13 and PCV10 in national immunization programs. These non-PCV13 serotypes now account for a substantial proportion of residual IPD, particularly in both pediatric and adult populations, and are associated with increased antimicrobial resistance and disease potential. Wider-spectrum vaccines such as pneumococcal conjugate vaccine 20 (PCV20) and pneumococcal conjugate vaccine 21 (PCV21) are specifically designed to address this gap.13,14 The persistent burden observed in 2023 and the different serotype distribution observed in children and adults suggests room for improvement in adult vaccine coverage and targeted immunization programs. So, our data suggest the need for new strategies with broader spectrum specifically for adult population over 60 years old, with risk factors or immunocompromised, where higher risk of hospitalizations and higher mortality rates exist due to pneumococcal disease.15 We consider the importance of surveillance to monitor the impact of newer vaccines and evolving serotype distribution, especially given the rising life expectancy and the increasing number of immunocompromised individuals in Spain.

Regarding other European studies, our findings are consistent with data reported by Italian authors for Liguria (2012–2018), with hospitalization rate up to 500 per 100,000 population in patients over 85 years.4 A nationwide study in France showed the benefits of vaccine implementation concerning a major reduction in the incidence of invasive pneumococcal disease, but also showed the high mortality among the elderly and patients with underlying comorbidities.16 In Germany, recent studies showed a case-fatality rate over 22% in patients >70 years,17 consistent with our findings. In the United Kingdom, despite high coverage of vaccination, the overall hospitalization rate due to invasive pneumococcal disease among the elderly and immunocompromised remained high (years 2016–2017).18,19

Limitations

This study has some limitations. The analysis is based on administrative data and ICD-10 coding, which may lead to misclassification or underreporting. Furthermore, we lacked data on important potential confounders such as socioeconomic status, smoking history (beyond a basic diagnosis code), and regional variations in healthcare access, which could influence both hospitalization rates and outcomes. Residual confounding due to these unmeasured factors may affect the accuracy of our odds ratio estimates. Additionally, serotype information or vaccination status data were not available. However, the large sample size and nationwide coverage provide robust epidemiological insights and robust estimates for policy-making.

Conclusions

Pneumococcal disease remains a significant public health challenge in Spain. Age and comorbidities remain key determinants of hospitalization and mortality. These findings support the need to strengthen adult immunization strategies and to prioritize vulnerable populations in future vaccine policy planning.

Biography

Ruth Gil-Prieto is a Preventive Medicine and Public Health professor at Rey Juan Carlos University in Madrid. Trained as a biochemist, she completed her PhD in epidemiology and public health. Because of her interest in humanitarian medicine and development, she quickly turned her attention to infectious disease epidemiology, particularly vaccines. After serving as an adjunct professor, she completed research stays in the pharmaceutical industry, closely following the clinical development of several vaccines. In 2010, she obtained her Tenure at the University Rey Juan Carlos (URJC) Madrid, where she taught epidemiology and served 7 years as the director of the International Cooperation and Volunteering Program. She leads the consolidated research group in epidemiology and the diagnosis of infection. She was also a visiting associate professor at the Department of Population Medicine, Harvard Medical School, in Boston, where her research focused on vaccine safety.
