Early safety and efficacy outcomes of the PAUL glaucoma implant in a Spanish population: A retrospective study
Authors: Sara Labay-Tejado, Néstor Ventura-Abreu, Sofía Porto-Castro, Ainhoa D. Castellarnau, M. Jesús Muniesa, Elena Millá, Marta Pazos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PURPOSE:The purpose of the study was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of the PAUL glaucoma implant (PGI) in refractory glaucoma patients, during the early postoperative period.METHODS:We conducted a retrospective, cohort study of patients that had received a PGI between February 2022 and December 2023. An intraluminal polypropylene suture was placed to prevent early hypotony in all cases. Demographics, intraocular pressure (IOP), and medication variation were evaluated for up to 6 months. The success rate was stratified into <18 mmHg and 30% from baseline or <15 mmHg and 40% IOP reduction. The safety outcomes were also analyzed.RESULTS:Sixteen eyes from 16 patients were included in the analysis. Fifteen of them had had a previous surgical glaucoma procedure. At 6 months, IOP decreased from 26.6 (±6.2) to 15.3 (±4.6) mmHg, with fewer glaucoma medications, from a median (interquartile range) of 3 (2–3) – 1.5 (0–2) (P < 0.005). Twelve (75%) and seven (43.8%) eyes met the qualified and complete success definition for the first category, and eight (50%) and four (25%) met the second criterion. One case with self-limiting choroidal detachments, but >6 mmHg IOP was found in our cohort.CONCLUSION:The PGI seems to be safe and effective from the early postoperative period. The IOP reduction rate could make it suitable for patients with refractory moderate-to-advanced glaucoma.

INTRODUCTION

Glaucoma is a chronic and irreversible neuropathy that represents one of the leading causes of irreversible blindness. Because the disease is asymptomatic in its earlier phases, the patients are unaware of the progression, leading to a high prevalence of an advanced-stage diagnosis at first presentation even across different populations.[1] The presence of advanced glaucoma at presentation is identified as the primary risk factor for progression to blindness, although several other factors may also exert influence.[23]

Reducing intraocular pressure (IOP) is still the primary controllable risk factor to slow the disease progression.[4] The best option to manage these advanced cases is not completely clear, but primary surgery (mainly trabeculectomy) could be more effective at preventing disease progression without compromising safety.[5] Glaucoma drainage devices (GDDs) are another effective surgical option.

When performed as the initial procedure, trabeculectomy still achieves better results compared to tubes;[6] however, in cases of advanced glaucoma or high-risk situations, there is a greater likelihood of requiring a subsequent glaucoma surgery, in which tubes (specifically the Baerveldt glaucoma implant [BGI]) have shown to be more effective than a repeated filtering (trabeculectomy, nonpenetrating deep sclerectomy) procedure.[7] Various GDDs differ in design features including plate size and material, biocompatibility, rigidity, and tube size and diameter.[8] These distinctions are also reflected in their effectiveness and safety profiles. The BGI is more effective in lowering the IOP and reducing the need for medications, but it also carries a higher risk of sight-threatening hypotony compared to the Ahmed glaucoma valve (AGV) implantation.[910]

The recent development of the PAUL glaucoma implant (PGI) aims to achieve a balance between efficacy and safety.[11] This nonvalved aqueous shunt is constructed from flexible and biocompatible silicone, featuring a large plate surface area similar to the BGI (342 mm2 vs. 350 mm2) but with smaller internal (0.127 mm vs. 0.305 mm) and external (0.467 mm vs. 0.60 mm) tube diameters, to prevent early hypotony. However, there are various techniques reported to reduce hypotony in the initial months (e.g., intraluminal stenting). The goal of this study is to present our initial experience with the PGI in terms of safety and efficacy.

METHODS

This was a single-center, noncomparative retrospective cohort study involving patients who had undergone PGI implantation in the glaucoma department of our institution. Data were extracted from the electronic medical records system of the hospital (SAP® Hana 2.0, Walldorf, Walldorf, Baden-Württemberg, Germany). The study received approval from the local Ethics Committee, ensuring adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki principles. Due to the retrospective, noninterventional nature of the study, a waiver of written informed consent was approved; however, all patients provided written informed consent before undergoing surgery. All patients were subjected to a documented full ophthalmological first examination, including assessment of best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) using Snellen chart (converted to logarithm of minimum angle resolution [logMAR]), IOP measurement with Goldmann applanation tonometry, slit lamp and fundus examination, optical coherence tomography using the Cirrus® device, and visual field testing (VF) using the Humphrey® 24-2 (Carl Zeiss Meditec Inc., Dublin, CA, USA).

The study included patients who had a minimum follow-up period of 6 months (and interim data from at least the 1st-month postoperative visit) and had received a PGI. Patients who did not meet the required follow-up were excluded from the study, and only one eye per patient was considered if both eyes were eligible.

The patient medical records were analyzed to include the following preoperative and postoperative information: Medication, average and inferior retinal nerve fiber layer values, mean deviation (MD) value of the VF, BCVA (starting from 3 months postsurgery), and IOP (1 day; 1 and 2 weeks; 1, 3, and 6 months). In addition, data on intraluminal stent removal time, timing, and duration of postoperative complications were recorded. Demographic details such as ethnicity, race, sex, age, systemic and ocular comorbidities, type and severity of glaucoma, and previous ocular surgeries were also documented. Glaucoma severity was determined based on the MD values using the Hodapp-Parrish-Anderson criteria,[12] whereas the type of glaucoma was classified according to the ICD-10 glaucoma reference guide.

Surgical technique

All cases were performed by two glaucoma surgeons (MP, NV-A). The surgery was typically done under peribulbar or retrobulbar anesthesia. Two corneal traction sutures using 5/0 polyglactin sutures were placed to fully expose the conjunctival area where the PGI was going to be located, normally in the superotemporal (first option) or superonasal quadrants (when superotemporal quadrant was not available) depending on the previous glaucoma surgeries on each eye. Before the tube insertion, a 6/0 polypropylene suture was placed in the lumen of the tube to prevent early hypotony. The tube was then inserted in the anterior chamber or the sulcus using a 25G needle; before closing the conjunctiva, the suture was pulled (leaving the distal end usually 1 mm before the tip) until adequate flow was ensured; then, the other end of the polypropylene suture was buried under the nasal or temporal conjunctiva, to be fully accessible and removable in case of need during the postoperative period. We did not use mitomycin C (MMC) during the surgery in our cohort; the rest of the surgical technique is fully described elsewhere.[13]

The postoperative treatment consisted of topical ofloxacin q.i.d. for 7 days, dexamethasone ointment overnight for one month; and dexamethasone 1 mg/ml eye drops 7×day 1–2 months, tapered until complete cease at 6 months. All glaucoma medications were ceased immediately postoperative; however, aqueous suppressant eyedrops were reintroduced if an early postoperative raised pressure was detected (>18 mmHg). When additional IOP reduction was required, the intraluminal polypropylene was gently moved through the conjunctiva at the slit lamp until it became less noticeable in the tube tip, thus enhancing flow. If insufficient IOP control still existed, the intraluminal suture was entirely removed by pulling it away from the conjunctiva following a minor incision, but not earlier than the first postoperative month.

Primary outcomes

Primary outcomes included the mean pre and postoperative IOP and the number of postoperative glaucoma medications. In line with the guidelines for reporting surgical trials,[14] the primary outcome was failure, defined as (a) sight-threatening complications; (b) removal of the implant due to postoperative complications; (c) additional glaucoma procedures (other tube, cyclodestructive procedures), and (d) IOP out of range (6–18 mmHg) AND <30% IOP reduction on two consecutive visits, after the first postoperative week (criterion A). The eyes that met the success criteria without antiglaucomatous drops were deemed a complete success, whereas those that met the definition with medications were considered a qualified success. We also included the failure and complete/qualified success rate but with stricter criteria: IOP in range (6–15 mmHg) and a 40% reduction of IOP (criterion B).

Secondary outcomes

Clinical postoperative maneuvers such as intraluminal suture removal or needling were also recorded but not considered complications. Surgical complications were included in the secondary analysis, further subclassified as intraoperative or early, late, and early/late, again following the EGS guidelines.[14] Visual acuity (VA) was converted to the logMAR. Numerical transformation of low vision values/categories was performed following Lange et al. recommendations.[15]

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical data were summarized as mean ± standard deviation for continuous quantitative data, median (interquartile range [IQR]) for discrete quantitative data, and frequencies and percentages for categorical values. The lack of a normal distribution was assumed due to the sample size, and Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare IOP and medication values between baseline and postoperative visits. The Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to assess the probability of survival for each criterion described above. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the proportion of failure/success patients who had removed the polypropylene stent. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was deemed as statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata® version 14.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

RESULTS

Twenty-four eyes of 24 patients underwent PGI surgery between February 2022 and December 2023, but only 16 were included in the analysis due to insufficient postoperative follow-up. The baseline characteristics, as shown in Table 1, revealed a mean age of 67.8 years (range: 22–85). The youngest patient was a 22-year-old with congenital glaucoma, whereas pseudoexfoliative glaucoma was the most frequent diagnosis (43.6%). The PGI was the initial glaucoma surgery in two uveitic glaucoma cases (one with a previous vitrectomy, the other with the conjunctiva in a poor condition); eleven (68.8%) cases had one previous filtering surgery, and in three (18.7%), the PGI was the third glaucoma procedure. The surgical technique was the same for all cases; in four surgeries, the PGI was placed in the superonasal quadrant since these patients had an Ahmed valve located superotemporally.

Primary outcomes

The IOP and the number of medications were significantly reduced at all postoperative time points [Figure 1]. The most significant decrease was observed 1 week after the surgery, followed by a slight but sustained increase in the first (17.1 ± 6.7) and third (17.0 ± 6.3) months after surgery, resulting in an increase in the use of glaucoma medications. By the 6-month mark, the IOP had decreased from 26.6 (±6.2) to 15.3 (±4.6) mmHg, along with a decrease in the number of hypotensive medications from a median (interquartile range) of 3 (2–3) to 1.5 (0–2) medications at 6 months (P < 0.005, Wilcoxon signed-rank test).

At 6 months, four eyes (25%) were considered a surgical failure, because they did not meet the criteria of achieving <18 mmHg IOP and a 30% reduction. No instances of additional glaucoma procedures or <6 mmHg on two consecutive visits were reported. Seven patients (43.8%) were deemed as complete success at 6 months as they did not require medications to achieve the <18 mmHg and 30% IOP reduction. The survival analysis is shown in Figure 2a.

The analysis also included a more stringent threshold of 40% IOP reduction and an upper limit of 15 mmHg. Under these conditions, eight (50%) of the cases were considered qualified success, whereas only four (25%) achieved the reduction without any drops. Figure 2b displays the results of the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis.

Secondary outcomes

Needling and polypropylene suture removal were the two types of postoperative maneuvers performed during the follow-up. In two cases, 5-FU needling was performed. On the other hand, suture removal was done in 6 eyes with a median IQR of 3 (1–6) months: One patient had the stent removed at the 1st month, three at the 3-month visit and had their intraluminal suture removed during the 6th-month visit. In five of these cases, IOP was reduced after removal (from 25.6 [5.2] to 15.6 [3.6] mmHg), and in the remaining case (removed at the 6-month visit), IOP increased from 22 to 24 mmHg even after reintroducing glaucoma medication. As shown in Table 2, the stent was not removed in ten cases: In fact, the proportion of patients with the stent and deemed successful (for both crit, A and B) was significantly higher compared to those where the stent was removed (P < 0.05, Fisher’s exact test).

No cases of the shallow anterior chamber or transient hypotony were detected, although one patient experienced minor choroidal detachments (with IOP over 6 mmHg) that spontaneously resolved. Only two patients exhibited a postoperative IOP spike at the first postoperative visit, which resolved within the 1st week; one patient had a hyphema that resolved within the 1st week, whereas the other had an unidentifiable cause. DISCUSSION

The current study aimed to assess the efficacy and safety of the recently developed PGI during the early postoperative period. Despite the quick adoption of this new technique, there remains a lack of literature detailing the outcomes (and a standardized technique) for the PAUL implant surgery. In our retrospective series, we found a significant decrease in IOP and medication usage without an increased incidence of complications in the first 6 months following the operation.

The findings from our retrospective series showed an IOP reduction from 26.6 ± 6.2 to 17.1 ± 6.7, 17.0 ± 6.3, and 15.3 ± 4.6 at the 1-, 3-, and 6-month visits, respectively. Analysis of data from various PGI reports shows different IOP and medication figures during the postoperative period;[111316] and even though our IOP readings are slightly higher, they consistently fall within mid-teens during the follow-up period. The postoperative medications administered in our study are also in line with those reported by other authors.

Different success rates can also be found in the literature regarding PGI. The first report by the authors who designed PGI indicated a success rate of 93.2% at 12 months;[11] this rate remained high at 85.4% even after 3 years of follow-up.[17] The success rates cannot be directly compared due to considerable differences in postoperative monitoring; however, achieving such high success rates may not be feasible at all times. Patients requiring a GDD surgery typically have undergone at least one prior glaucoma surgery and have moderate-to-advanced glaucomatous damage, as evidenced by our patients (mean DM of −14.5 dB). EGS guidelines[14] recommend stricter IOP targets and reductions for more severe glaucoma cases (<18 mmHg and 30% reduction for moderate [a] and <15 mmHg and 40% reduction for advanced glaucoma cases [b]). Our study revealed a 75% and 50% success rate for each target category (A and B, respectively). These findings align with the success rate reported by José et al.,[16] who also utilized similar criteria (criteria A) to assess success rates after 1 year. Despite differences in follow-up periods, the data suggest comparable outcomes across different surgeons and glaucoma cases.

Following the Tube Versus Trabeculectomy study, the utilization of GDD, particularly the BGI, has become the standard in managing refractory glaucoma surgically.[7] Despite achieving positive outcomes, the increased rate of postoperative complications and eye integrity concerns have shifted toward a more cautious approach in real-world settings, leading to higher usage of AGVs.[18] The PGI was developed with a smaller inner lumen diameter to lower the outflow rate and minimize hypotony risk. However, the absence of a restrictive mechanism along with other potential factors contributing to early hypotony such as peritubular filtration, has resulted in various restrictive measures being implemented to prevent this complication, similar to those used during BGI implantation. By employing an intraluminal 6-0 polypropylene suture technique as outlined by Vallabh et al.;[13] we were able to achieve comparable IOP and medication results, as well as a similar pattern of behavior over time (a slight IOP increase at the 1-and 3-month visits followed by a reduction at 6 months). Berteloot et al. also replicated this IOP behavior using the same stenting technique, noting that patients who received a PGI instead of a BGI had better IOP control, fewer stenting maneuvers, and fewer postoperative complications.[19] There are two potential reasons for the slightly elevated IOP values. The first reason could be due to maintaining the intraluminal stent beyond the 1st month if the target IOP was achieved after reintroducing hypotensive medications. The logic behind this approach is twofold: In the BGI, the dissolution of the absorbable suture at the fifth postoperative week can still lead to a marked hypotony.[20] Despite the smaller inner diameter, the absence of a restrictive mechanism in the 1st month might lead to a higher rate of hypotony-related complications[16] compared to maintaining the stent for a longer period.[19] Furthermore, there is increasing evidence that modulating the bleb exposure to aqueous humor with either aqueous suppressants and/or maintaining an intraluminal stent could improve the long-term results.[2122]

The second reason for the subtle higher IOP values in the early postoperative period could be the absence of intraoperative MMC usage in our cohort. Currently, there is insufficient evidence to support the widespread use of antimetabolites (such as MMC, 5-Fluorouracil) in tube shunts; although in AGVs, MMC has been proposed to help lower IOP in the first postoperative weeks, its clinical significance and long-term efficacy remain uncertain.[23]

In terms of safety, we observed minimal complications in the first postoperative months. The most concerning issues were associated with low IOP in the 1st weeks, and we did not find any case of numerical hypotony, choroidal folds, or shallow anterior chamber. This low incidence of hypotony-related complications is consistent with findings from previous studies,[111317] and a recent comparison between BGI and PGI showed a higher indicated a slightly higher rate of low-IOP-related complications in BGI patients, although not statistically significant.[19] A comprehensive evaluation of the safety profile requires an extended follow-up period, with detailed information on these complications as described in the Ahmed Baerveldt comparison study.[10] A longer follow-up of our patients’ cohort will provide a more accurate assessment of the overall complication rate, including those inherent to tube surgeries.

The present study is not exempt from limitations that are worth mentioning. The retrospective design and the lack of a comparative group hinder the ability to guarantee superior efficacy and safety compared to other GDDs. Another significant limitation includes the small sample size and short follow-up period. Given that glaucoma is a progressive, chronic condition, long-term data are essential when considering a new device or technique in place of current standards of care. Nevertheless, the findings may shed light on two key points: First, the percentage of patients meeting both success criteria definitions (30% and 40%) appears more realistic for individuals with advanced glaucoma requiring a substantial reduction in IOP, particularly in the early postoperative phase. Second, the efforts to minimize postoperative complications, specifically those related to hypotony, did not result in sustained IOP spikes in the initial weeks. Effective early postoperative management is crucial to prevent further harm in patients with already compromised optic nerves.[24] One more limitation is the aforementioned approach of reintroducing glaucoma medications before stent removal even after the first postoperative month. However, this translated only in a few more cases not considered a complete success. Moreover, most of the patients were deemed as successful despite having the stent, and in the remaining cases, the planned target IOP was achieved with both IOP medications and stent removal, which suggests that those were more prone to failure due to other factors. Finally, as this study was conducted in a real-world setting, we did not monitor endothelial cell counts, which are also important for patients undergoing any tube implantation.

CONCLUSION

Despite our limited experience with nonvalved tubes initially (potentially leading to a learning curve), PGI shows a good safety and efficacy profile in our moderate-to-severe, refractory glaucoma population, using a standardized technique. The outcomes observed prompted a shift to PGI as a frequent GDD surgical choice, but future comparison studies with other GDDs will determine its position in the surgical algorithm.
