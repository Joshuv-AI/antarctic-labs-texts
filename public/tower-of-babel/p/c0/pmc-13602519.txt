Serotypes distribution characteristic of enterovirus in sewage in Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province from January 2023 to December 2024
Authors: Lingxuan Su, Qiong Ge, Yi Sun, Jian Gao, Changping Xu, Zhen Li, Liming Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnteroviruses (EV) represent a significant public health concern due to their widespread prevalent and ability to cause disease in humans. Due to the limitations of routine surveillance, which primarily only detect clinically symptomatic cases, environment surveillance of urban sewage has emerged as a valuable and complementary strategy for EV surveillance.MethodsA total of 48 sewage samples were collected monthly from two wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) in Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province from January 2023 to December 2024. Virus isolation and direct next-generation sequencing (NGS) were performed to characterize the EV serotypes profiles in sewage. Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was used to amplify VP1 gene sequences for molecular epidemiological analysis of EV serotypes.ResultsTen distinct serotypes were isolated and identified from WWTP-QT and sixteen from WWTP-XS via virus isolation. EV-B was the dominant group in both treatment plants, with E3 and E11 representing the most frequently isolated serotypes. Phylogenetic analysis showed that the predominant genotypes in E3 was C6, whereas all E11 included phylogenetic analysis were classified into genotype D5. On the other hand, direct NGS uncovered as many as 108 distinct EV serotypes covering four human-infective species across both WWTPs. CVA4 and CVA6 were the dominant serotypes identified via NGS. Additionally, multiple uncommon serotypes as EV-A76, A89, A90, A92, A114, B74, B79, B98, B101, B106, B107, C96, C99, C113, C116, D70, D94, D111, D120 were detected in sewage.ConclusionEnvironment surveillance is an important tool for monitoring EV circulation in the population. Furthermore, direct NGS can serve as a supplementary tool to the routine virus isolation method, particularly in detecting EV serotype diversity and evolution, and identifying uncommon serotypes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12985-026-03240-z.

Background

Enteroviruses (EV) belong to the genus Enterovirus, family Picornaviridae, and are classified into 12 species, among which Enterovirus alphacoxsackie (EV-A), Enterovirus betacoxsackie (EV-B), Enterovirus coxsackiepol (EV-C) and Enterovirus deconjuncti (EV-D) have been reported to infect humans [1]. Based on the VP1 sequences, there are a total of 116 serotypes across EV-A to EV-D, with 25, 63, 23 and 5 serotypes in each respective species [2]. EVs virions are icosahedral, non-enveloped particles approximately 30 nm in diameter, with a positive-sense, single-stranded RNA genome of about 7.4 kb in length [3, 4]. The EV genome has a single large open reading frame that only encodes a single polyprotein, which is then processed into four capsid proteins (VP1, VP2, VP3 and VP4) and seven nonstructural proteins (2A, 2B, 2C, 3A 3B, 3C and 3D) within the host [1].

EVs are the causative agents for a wide spectrum of diseases, including respiratory tract disease, hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD), and central nervous system disease, and are primarily transmitted via the fecal–oral route [1].

Multiple studies have reported that EVs can be detected in healthy individuals, indicating that most EV infections remain subclinical [5, 6]. Therefore, monitoring efforts that solely on clinically symptomatic cases is insufficient. Environmental surveillance (ES) based on urban sewage represents a more sensitive approach for monitoring the diversity and prevalence of agents excreted via feces, urine and bodily fluids [7]. At present, ES is applied for epidemiology monitoring of various microbes, including SARS-CoV-2 and gastroenteritis viruses [8, 9].

ES has long underpinned global poliovirus (PV) eradication initiatives, particularly facilitating early detection of wild poliovirus, vaccine-derived poliovirus (VDPV) and oral poliovirus vaccine related virus [10]. In 2016, WHO announced the withdrawal of PV2 from oral poliovirus vaccine. However, VDPV2 were subsequently detected in sewage and clinical cases in several instance across different countries. For example, from January 2020 to December 2021, VDPV2 was repeated detected in multiple provinces in Egypt [11]. In 2022, a VDPV2 case was reported in Rockland County, New York. Subsequently, VDPV2 isolated from sewage samples from other counties near the Rockland which was related to this clinical case, indicating the community transmission [12, 13]. Furthermore, in a separate incident, another VDPV2 strain was again detected in New York City sewage in 2022 [13]. More recently, a circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus type 2 (cVDPV2) emerged in five European countries, Finland, Germany, Poland, Spain and the United Kingdom in 2024, with all cVDPV2 strains isolated from these countries exhibiting high genetic similarity [14]. The above examples illustrate that VDPV has been found in many continents, posing a widespread threat to global public health. Therefore, ES as an invaluable and highly sensitive measure that plays an important role in early detection of importation and silent circulation of wild polioviruses and vaccine-derived polioviruses, especially in regions implementing a full-course inactivated poliovirus vaccine immunization strategy.

Importantly, ES also plays a crucial role in monitoring non-polio enteroviruses (NPEV), especially uncommon NPEV serotypes, which may otherwise go unnoticed [15]. Moreover, ES can detect a greater number of NPEV serotypes, compared to hospital-based clinical EV surveillance [16, 17]. Previous studies from China, Japan, Tunisia, Italy and Spain, have collectively demonstrated rich NPEV diversity within sewage samples [15, 17–21]. A study in Shandong Province, China, detected five uncommon types (EV-A76, EV-A89, EV-A90 and EV-C113) in sewage, which were rarely reported in clinical surveillance [22]. In Italy, a rarely reported serotype EV-C99 was also frequently detected in sewage [15]. Moreover, in Nigeria, four uncommon serotypes of NPEV namely EV-A119, EV-B87, EV-C116, and EV-D111 were detected in the sewage [16].

Therefore, this study aims to characterize the prevalence, serotypes diversity, and molecular epidemiology of EVs in urban sewage in Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, eastern China, using both virus isolation and direct next-generation sequencing (NGS) between January 2023 to December 2024.

Methods and materials

Urban sewage sample collection

A total of 48 sewage samples were collected from two wastewater treatment plants (WWTP) in Hangzhou, the capital city of Zhejiang Province, namely Qiantang WWTP (WWTP-QT) and Xiaoshan WWTP (WWTP-XS). WWTP-QT receives approximately 600,000 m3 of untreated wastewater daily from a population of 2.35 million accounting for 20.18% of total population in main urban area of Hangzhou. WWTP-XS receives approximately 400,000 m3 of untreated wastewater daily from a population of 730,000 accounting for 6.40% of total population in main urban area of Hangzhou. From January 2023 to December 2024, one liter sewage sample was collected monthly from the inlet of the primary sedimentation tank at each WWTP and transported to the laboratory under low-temperature condition.

Virus concentration

Viruses were concentrated from sewage samples using an anion membrane adsorption elution method. Briefly, 800 mL sewage samples were centrifuged at 4 °C and 1000 xg for 30 min to obtain the supernatant. MgCl2 was subsequently added to the supernatant at a final concentration of 0.05 M. The pH was then adjusted to 3.5 with 0.5 M HCl solution. Subsequently, the filter paper and anion membrane were sequentially placed in the filtration equipment, and a peristaltic pump was used to filtrate the supernatant to adsorb viruses on the anion membrane. Following this, the virus-loaded anion membrane was cut into small pieces and placed in a 50 mL centrifuge tube with 10 mL of 3% beef extract solution (pH = 9.0), followed by shaking for 5 min. Finally, the pH of beef extract elution was adjusted to 7.0 for subsequent detection.

Cell culture virus isolation

The virus elution was filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter to remove bacteria and fungi prior to isolation. L20B, RD and Hep-2 cell lines were used for virus isolation. A 200 μL aliquot of virus elution was inoculated into 8 tubes each of L20B, RD and Hep-2 cells. Cells were cultured in an incubator at 36 °C and 5% CO2 and monitored for the appearance of specific cytopathic effect (CPE) for 7 days. RD and Hep-2 cultures exhibiting CPE were passaged onto L20B cells to identify potential PV. The cell cultures with CPE were harvested and stored at -80 °C for further analysis.

Viral RNA extraction

Viral RNA was extracted from 200 μL EV isolates and sewage concentrates using QIAamp Viral RNA mini kit (QIAGEN, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA elution was stored at -80 °C for further analysis.

VP1 gene amplification and sanger sequencing

The complete sequence of VP1 gene was amplified by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) using the PrimeScript™ One-Step RT-PCR Kit Ver.2 (TaKaRa 055RA). The reaction mixture was prepared in a total of 50 μL, consisting of 25 μL of 2 × buffer, 2 μL of Enzyme mix, 1 μL of forward primer, 1 μL of reverse primer, 13 μL of RNase-free water, and 8 μL of template RNA. The primer sequences were adopted from a previous study [23]. The amplification program of one-step RT-PCR was as follows: reverse transcription at 42 °C for 30 min, initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 50 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 2 min. A final extension step was performed at 72 °C for 10 min. Finally, amplified RT-PCR products were sequenced using the Sanger sequencing method (YouKang Biological Technology). Serotypes were determined by BLAST analysis using the Nucleotide BLAT tool (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi).

Direct NGS-based P1 region amplification and sequencing

Semi-nested RT-PCR targeting the P1 genomic region was performed directly on sewage concentrate extracts for subsequent high-throughput sequencing [24]. In the first round, two independent RT-PCR reactions were performed using the PrimeScript™ One-Step RT-PCR Kit Ver.2 (TaKaRa R055RA). The volume of each reaction mixture was 25 μL, containing 12.5 μL of 2 × buffer, 1 μL of Enzyme mix, 0.5 μL of forward primer Pan F1, 0.5 μL of reverse primer AC-R, 0.5 μL of reverse primer BD-R, 5 μL of RNase-free water, and5 μL of template RNA. The amplification program of one-step RT-PCR was as follows: reverse transcription at 50 °C for 30 min and initial denaturation at 98 ℃ for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 63 °C for 10 s, 72 °C for 5 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min.

In the second round, PCR was performed using TaKaRa Taq™ Hot Start Version (TaKaRa R007RA). The volume of each reaction mixture was 50 μL, containing 5 μL of 10 × buffer, 0.25 μL of Taq enzyme mix, 1 μL of forward primer Pan F2, 1 μL of reverse primer AC-R, 1 μL of reverse primer BD-R, 40.75 μL of RNase-free water, and1 μL of first round products. The amplification program of PCR was as follows: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 63 °C for 10 s, 72 °C for 5 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min.

The final products were purified by Agencourt® AMPure® XP beads (Beckman, USA). Libraries were prepared using the MATRIDX Metagenomic DNA Library Construction Kit (MATRIDX, Hangzhou, China) and purified using Agencourt® AMPure® XP beads. Library concentrations were quantified using the KAPA Library Quantification Kit for the Illumina® platform (Roche, USA). The library was sequenced using the Illumina NextSeq 2000 Reagent Kit P2 (100 cycles and single end) on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform. The raw data was processed using Microbiome & Virus Analysis Platform (MVP) [25]. Using MVP to do the de novo assembly and local BLAST to analyse the composition of EV serotype in sewage samples. Contigs covering entire VP1 with no insert and gap were reversed for further phylogenetic analysis.

Phylogenetic analysis

DNA sequences were aligned using the Clustal W method in MEGA 7.0 [26]. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Maximum Likelihood algorithm and Kimura 2-parameter model with 1000 bootstraps. The tree was visualized and annotated using the ITOL online tool (https://itol.embl.de/).

The sequences and contigs included into phylogenetic analysis cover complete VP1 gene with no insert and gaps. All reference sequences were retrieved from the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GenBank database. All sequences obtained in this study were submitted to NCBI GenBank (GenBank accession numbers: PZ044459-PZ044500, PZ056942-PZ056986, PZ495538-PZ495554).

Statistical analysis

Graphpad Prism 10.4.2 was used for data processing and statistical analysis. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to assess the correlation between virus isolation and NGS detection for both E11 and E3. P value < 0.05 was considered significant. The r < 0 was considered negative correlation, while r = 0 was considered no correlation, and r > 0 was considered positive correlation.

The distribution of EV serotypes by virus isolation method from WWTP-QT and WWTP-XS

The 102 EV isolates from WWTP-QT were classified into 3 species (EV-A, EV-B and EV-C) and 10 serotypes. EV-B was overwhelmingly dominant, accounting for 90.20% (n = 92), followed by EV-C (6.86%, n = 7) and EV-A (2.94%, n = 3), including 6, 2 and 2 types respectively (Table 1). E3 and E11 were the most prevalent serotypes, accounting for 42.16% (n = 43) and 34.31% (n = 35) of all isolates, respectively (Table 1). For WWTP-XS, 141 EV isolates consisted of 16 serotypes across EV-A/B/C. Consistent with findings from WWTP-QT, EV-B was the dominant species, accounting for 94.33% (n = 133), followed by EV-C (4.26%, n = 6) and EV-A (1.42%, n = 2), composing 13, 1 and 2 types respectively (Table 1). E11 (37.59%, n = 53) and E3 (33.33%, n = 47) were the most frequently detected serotypes as well (Table 1).

Moreover, two uncommon serotypes EV-A76 and EV-A89 were isolated from sewage samples (Table 1). One EV-A89 isolate originated from WWTP-QT, and one EV-A76 isolate was obtained from WWTP-XS. All PV3 and PV1 isolates were identified as Sabin-like strains with VP1 nucleic acid mutations within 1 to 4.

Prevalence characteristic of CVA4 and CVA6

Direct NGS analysis revealed that the predominant serotypes varied monthly (Supplementary Table 1 & 2). Nonetheless, CVA4 and CVA6 were consistently identified as the most prevalent serotypes. CVA4 was detected in 100% (24/24) of samples from both WWTP-QT and WWTP-XS. Similarly, CVA6 was detected in 100% (24/24) of samples from WWTP-QT and 95.83% (23/24) for WWTP-XS. Notably, CVA6 was not recovered by virus isolation from either WWTP despite its high detection frequency by direct NGS. Likewise, no CVA4 isolates were recovered from WWTP-XS, while only two isolates were obtained from a single sewage sample in WWTP-QT with a detection rate of 4.17% (1/24).

Discussion

To better understand the prevalence, serotype diversity and molecular epidemiology of EVs in the human population, virus isolation and direct NGS were used to characterize EV serotypes in sewage from Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province from January 2023 to December 2024. Overall, both methods revealed serotypes diversity, however, direct NGS approach detected a broader range of serotypes than the virus isolation method, demonstrating its value as a complementary approach for EVs surveillance. These findings further highlight the importance of ES based on urban sewage as an effective strategy for monitoring EVs circulation in human populations.

Virus isolation identified E3 and E11 as the predominance serotypes in Hangzhou. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting that E11 is among the most frequently detected EVs serotypes in sewage. In Guangzhou, E11 was the predominant serotype detected in sewage samples collected between 2009 to 2021 [19, 29]. A similar observation has been reported in Shandong Province in sewage as well from 2008 to 2012 and from 2015 to 2017 [18, 30, 31]. Similarly, E3 was also consistently detected in Shandong Province during 2008–2012 and 2015–2017 [18, 31]. In contrast, E3 was only detected sporadically in Guangzhou sewage from 2009 to 2021, where E11 and E6 were the dominant serotypes from 2009 to 2012, and E11 and CVB5 were the dominant serotypes from 2013 to 2021 [19, 29]. These findings indicate that the distribution of dominant EV serotypes varies geographically when assessed by virus isolation method.

Notably, E3 and E11 are clinically important human pathogens with viral encephalitis, aseptic meningitis and acute flaccid myelitis [1]. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that genotypes C6 was the majorE3 genotypes circulating in Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province. This is consistent with previous reports indicating that genotype C and genotype D are the dominant E3 genotypes circulating in China [28]. The phylogenetic analysis further revealed that E11 strains included in this study belonged to genotype D5 consistent with previous studies reporting that D5 has been the dominant E11 genotype circulating in mainland China since 2016 [27, 32]. Beyond China, genotype D5 has also been reported as the dominant lineage in Italy, Spain and Poland [33–35].

Direct NGS identified CVA4 and CVA6 as the predominant serotypes in this study. Similar findings were reported in a sewage-based ES conducted in Spain between 2020 to 2024 [17]. However, the serotypes distribution observed in Hangzhou differed from those reported in other regions. In Shandong Province, E11, CVA10, E18, CVB4, and CVB5 were the most abundant serotypes from 2019 to 2021, whereas E11, CVB5, CVA9 and PV3 dominated from 2018 to 2019 [22, 30]. In Italy, E11 and CVB5 were the most frequently detected serotypes from 2022 to 2023 [15]. Similarly, the United States identified CVB3, CVA6 and E9 as the dominant serotypes [36], while CVB5 and CVA16 predominated in France, and E11 and E6 were most prevalent in Scotland [37, 38]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate considerable geographical variation in EVs serotype distribution.

It is well established that CVA4 and CVA6 are important etiological agents for HFMD and acute flaccid myelitis [1]. Consistently, CVA6 and CVA4 were the most prevalent EV serotypes in patients with influenza-like illness in Hangzhou in 2023 [39]. CVA4 was identified as the leading cause of herpangina in Zhejiang Province in 2024 [40]. CVA6 became the predominant HFMD pathogen in Shandong Province in 2023 [41]. In Zhejiang Province, CVA6 has been reported to account for 64.4–77.8% of HFMD [42, 43]. Together, these findings indicate that CVA4 and CVA6 are currently circulating widely in human populations, and are likely major contributors to the EV burden in eastern China.

Comparison between virus isolation and direct NGS revealed distinct differences in serotype diversity, with direct NGS detecting a broader range of serotypes. The observed bias toward EV-B isolation may be attributed to the specific cell lines employed. In this study, three cell lines (RD, L20B and Hep-2) were used for virus isolation. Previous research has demonstrated that RD cells are highly sensitive to EV-B (especially echoviruses), L20B cells are more sensitive to PV, and Hep-2 cells are more sensitive to coxsackievirus B [44, 45]. In addition, certain EV serotypes require multiple passages to manifest CPE, while some EV-A serotypes are known to be difficult to propagate in cell culture [22, 30]. This selective sensitivity may explain why EV-B accounted for the vast majority of our isolates, while EV-A to EV-C were detected at much lower rates.

In contrast, direct NGS method bypasses this culture bias, enabling the detection of a comprehensive range of serotypes spanning EV-A to EV-D. This approach proved particularly effective for EV-A serotypes and other fastidious, non-CPE-producing serotypes.

Although uncommon serotypes can occasionally be recovered via virus isolation, direct NGS offers a clear advantage in their detection [46]. In this study, while virus isolation identified only two uncommon serotypes (EV-A76 and EV-A89), direct NGS successfully detected these serotypes across nearly all sewage samples alongside several other uncommon serotypes. These findings align with global ES data, for instance, reports from Shandong Province, China found EV-A76, EV-A89, EV-A90 EV-B88, EV-C99 and EV-C113 in sewage using NGS [22, 47]. Similarly, NGS-based ES in Cameroon detected EV-A76, EV-A119, EV-B74 and EV-B111 [46]. EV-A119, EV-A76, EV-A89, EV-B78 and EV-C116 were detected in sewage in the United States [37]. Furthermore, extensive NGS monitoring of sewage in Spain revealed a high diverse profile of uncommon serotypes including EV-A76, EV-A89, EV-A90, EV-C116, EV-C105 and EV-C109. Collectively, these results underscore the pivotal role of direct NGS in ES for monitoring EVs, particularly for capturing the less common serotypes.

We also identified several EV-D serotypes including EV-D68, EV-D70, EV-D94, EV-D111 and EV-D120. EV-D68 is a well-documented pathogen associated with respiratory tract disease and acute flaccid myelitis [48] Numerous studies have confirmed its frequent detected in sewage [17, 46, 47]. Conversely, other four EV-D serotypes are rarely reported in environmental or clinical settings. EV-D94 was originally discovered in Egyptian sewage and acute flaccid paralysis (AFP) cases in the Democratic Republic of the Congo [49]. Moreover, a seroprevalence study in Finland revealed high levels of anti-EV-D94 antibodies within the population [49]. In addition, EV-D111 has previously been detected in sewage in Nigeria [16]. Taken together, these findings highlight the necessity of monitoring these uncommon serotypes, which continue to circulate in human populations.

Notwithstanding, there were some limitations in this study that should be acknowledged. The monthly collection frequency may not accurately or fully reflect the temporal distribution of EV serotypes in sewage. Additionally, virus concentration was performed exclusively on sewage supernatants, which may inadvertently introduced composition bias. Previous studies have demonstrated that the isolation efficiency of certain EV serotypes differs significantly between sewage supernatants and sediment particles, leading to biased serotype composition when only the supernatant is used [50]. Finally, the PCR amplification procedure may have also introduced preferential amplification, potentially skewing the perceived proportional distribution and composition of the detected serotypes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, EVs remain important human pathogens associated with a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations ranging from asymptomatic infections to severe neurological disease, particularly in vulnerable population like children and the elderly. As routine clinical surveillance often misses asymptomatic or mild cases, ES of urban sewage serves as a powerful tool for monitoring population-level EV prevalence.

This study demonstrated that while virus isolation remains a classical mode of surveillance, direct NGS offers superior capability in resolving serotype diversity and identifying uncommon strains and acts as an invaluable complementary approach. Ultimately, integrating virus isolation with direct NGS approach provides a more robust and comprehensive strategy for monitoring EV circulation in human populations.
