Flavonoid Oxime Carbamate Derivatives: Reversal of Multidrug Resistance in Cancer Cells
Authors: Filipa Barbosa, Gabriella Spengler, Noémi Bózsity, Nikoletta Szemerédi, István Zupkó, Maria‐José U Ferreira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe effectiveness of cancer treatment has been seriously hindered by the development of multidrug resistance (MDR), mainly mediated by efflux transporters such as P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp/ABCB1). Aiming at obtaining new compounds for overcoming MDR in cancer, tangeretin (1), a natural polymethoxyflavonoid, was derivatized. After obtaining the corresponding oxime (2), a set of 23 novel oxime carbamates (3–26) was prepared via carbonyldiimidazole‐mediated reaction with various amines or by reaction with isocyanates. Their structures were assigned mainly by 1D and 2D NMR experiments. The compounds (1–26) were evaluated for their MDR reversal potential, using the rhodamine‐123 accumulation assay and chemosensitivity experiments, in human ABCB1‐gene transfected L5178Y mouse lymphoma cells. A significant increase in P‐gp inhibitory activity was observed for most of the derivatives at noncytotoxic concentrations. Notably, compounds 19, 20, and 22, bearing an aliphatic substituent, were the most active, exhibiting a strong MDR reversal effect at 2 μM. Moreover, drug combination assays revealed that most of the derivatives were able to synergize doxorubicin. Selected compounds were also tested in the ATPase assay, where most of them acted as inhibitors.Keywords: multidrug resistance, oxime carbamate, P‐glycoprotein, polymethoxyflavonoid, tangeretin

Introduction

The efficacy of successful cancer therapies has been hindered by the development of resistance to chemotherapeutic agents. Multidrug resistance (MDR) can be broadly defined as a phenomenon by which tumor cells show cross‐resistance to a wide range of anticancer drugs with different structures and mechanisms of action. Consequently, MDR represents a significant public health concern, requiring an urgent solution [1]. Several factors may contribute to tumor cell resistance to anticancer drugs, including downregulation of drug uptake, induction of efflux systems, inhibition of cell death, enhanced DNA repair, altered drug metabolism, and changes in molecular targets. A key mechanism of MDR is the overexpression of ABC transporter proteins, which act by pumping the anticancer drugs out of the cell through an ATP‐dependent mechanism, leading to a subsequent reduction in the intracellular accumulation of the drug necessary for effective therapy. Three ABC transporters have been investigated in more detail due to their involvement in MDR, namely P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp/ABCB1), MDR protein 1 (MRP1/ABCC1), and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP/ABCG2), with P‐gp being the most important [1, 2]. The development of P‐gp inhibitors, able to restore the cytotoxic action of anticancer agents, has been considered a good strategy to circumvent drug resistance; however, no compound has successfully passed clinical trials due to several issues, namely high toxicity or poor in vivo efficacy [3, 4].

The importance of natural products in drug discovery and development has long been recognized. Around 65% of anticancer drugs currently used in clinical practice are either natural products, their derivatives, or synthetic compounds inspired by natural products [1, 5]. The remarkable structural diversity and complexity of natural products make them a promising source of compounds to overcome the limitations of previous generations of P‐gp inhibitors [6, 7].

Flavonoids have been shown to act as efflux modulators of the main contributors to MDR [8, 9]. Specifically, they are able to inhibit P‐gp activity through various mechanisms, including blocking the drug binding site, interfering with ATP hydrolysis, and reducing P‐gp expression. However, despite the potential of natural flavonoids as anti‐MDR agents, they require optimization to possess the desired potency, selectivity, and pharmacokinetic characteristics for clinical application [9].

Aiming at reversing MDR, we have investigated several plant‐derived compounds as potential ABC‐transporter inhibitors [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Among them, the flavanone naringenin, isolated in large amounts from the Portuguese plant Euphorbia pedroi, was chemically modified at the carbonyl group, yielding several derivatives such as hydrazones, azines, and carbohydrazides. Promising results were obtained for hydrazone and azine derivatives, which exhibited high MDR reversal activity toward BCRP, while carbohydrazide derivatives were selective efflux modulators of MRP1 [10].

Tangeretin (1) is a natural polymethoxyflavonoid found in citrus peels that has attracted considerable interest due to its multiple biological activities, namely antiproliferative, anti‐invasive, antimetastatic, and antioxidant [19, 20]. Moreover, tangeretin was also able to reverse P‐gp‐mediated MDR, by inhibiting P‐gp efflux activity [20] and modulating key signaling pathways associated with drug resistance, including the Nrf2 pathway [21].

The carbamate group, structurally related to amide and urea functions, is found in many chemotherapeutic agents. In effect, this structural feature is a key motif in medicinal chemistry, frequently used in drug design to enhance metabolic stability, bioactivity, membrane permeability and ability to participate in hydrogen bonding [22]. In our recent studies, carbamate‐containing derivatives of the natural Amaryllidaceae‐type alkaloid lycorine were shown to effectively reverse P‐gp‐mediated MDR on resistant colon adenocarcinoma cells, highlighting the potential of this structural moiety [16]. Similar to classic carbamates, oxime carbamates have also been considered as an important moiety in drug design [23, 24, 25].

In this work, we have generated a library of tangeretin oxime carbamate derivatives to evaluate the effect of the oxime carbamate structural feature on the ability of tangeretin to reverse MDR. Thus, the newly prepared compounds were investigated as P‐gp inhibitors, through the rhodamine‐123 accumulation assay, in human ABCB1‐transfected mouse T‐lymphoma cell model, overexpressing this ABC‐transporter. Furthermore, chemo‐sensitization assays, with the anticancer drug doxorubicin, were also carried out. To obtain additional insights into the type of interaction between selected compounds and P‐gp, an ATPase assay was also performed.

Results and Discussion

Chemistry

In the present study, aiming at generating a small library of bioactive flavonoid analogs as MDR‐reversers, the polymethoxyflavonoid tangeretin (1) was derivatized, yielding 23 oxime carbamates (3–26). As outlined in Scheme 1, for the preparation of these compounds, was adopted a two‐step synthetic protocol. Initially, tangeretin (1) was condensed with hydroxylamine hydrochloride to give rise to tangeretin oxime intermediate 2 (Scheme 1, i). The formation of the oxime 2 was evidenced in the 13C NMR spectrum, where the signal of the carbonyl group of the starting compound (1) (δC 177.4, C‐4) was replaced by a resonance showing a strong diamagnetic effect, at δc 154.1, due to the new oxime function(−C=N−OH). Thereafter, the oxime carbamate derivatives 3–12 were prepared by reaction of the compound 2 with 1,10‐carbonyldiimidazole (CDI), in the presence of sodium hydride, followed by reaction with primary or secondary amines (Scheme 1, ii). In turn, the reaction of compound 2 with aliphatic (furfuryl, pentyl, tert‐butyl, cyclohexyl) or aromatic (phenyl, phenylpropyl, benzyl) isocyanates yielded derivatives 13–26 (Scheme 1, iii).

Preparation of tangeretin oxime (2) and derivatives 3–26. Reagents and conditions: (i) hydroxylamine hydrochloride (3 equiv.) in MeOH, reflux, overnight. (ii) (3–12) NaH (1.5 equiv.), CDI (1.5 equiv.), amine (1.5 equiv.), in THF, 0ºC, 30–120 min. (iii) (13–26) isocyanate (1 equiv.) in THF, 70°C, 1–5 h.

The structures of all these derivatives were established mainly by comparing their 1H and 13C NMR data with those of tangeretin oxime (2), coupled with 2D‐NMR experiments (HSQC and HMBC). In this way, when comparing the NMR data of compounds 3–27 with those of compound 2, the main differences were additional carbon and proton signals owing to the new substituent. The presence of the oxime carbamate moiety (−C=N—O—CO—NR) was evidenced in the 13C NMR spectra by the presence of a shielded carbonyl signal at δC 155.1–156.8 (C‐1″) due to mesomeric effects of the nitrogen and the oxygen atoms.

Most of the oxime carbamate derivatives (3–26) displayed moderate antiproliferative effect (IC50 > 10), in both sensitive and corresponding resistant cells, when compared with doxorubicin, while exhibiting higher antiproliferative activity than tangeretin (1), which showed no significant antiproliferative activity (1, IC50 = 38.84 and 77.26 µM in sensitive and resistant cells, respectively). The oxime intermediary (2) was inactive, thus substantiating the importance of the carbamate function for the activity. Remarkably, compound 4, with an oxime carbamate moiety having an unsubstituted benzyl group, showed selectivity toward the multidrug‐resistant subline, displaying IC50 values of 8.60 and 6.02 µM against the sensitive and resistant cells, respectively. Compound 7, an oxime diethylcarbamate derivative, and compound 25, bearing a para‐dimethylamino‐phenyl moiety, also showed a significant increase in the antiproliferative effect (7, IC50 = 8.82 and 12.35 µM; 25, IC50 = 6.49 and 12.68 µM in sensitive and resistant cells, respectively) when compared with tangeretin (1). The remaining derivatives exhibited IC50 values ranging from 11.53 to 30.97 µM in the sensitive cell line and from 16.16 to 47.47 µM in resistant cells.

Inhibition of P‐Glycoprotein Efflux Activity

The ability of the compounds 1–26 as P‐gp inhibitors was evaluated on L5178Y‐MDR cell line, which overexpresses this transporter protein, in the rhodamine‐123 functional assay, by flow cytometry. The intracellular accumulation of this fluorescent dye, a P‐gp substrate, serves as an indicator of efflux pump inhibition. In the untreated L5178Y‐MDR cell line, where P‐gp is active, low fluorescence is detected, whereas P‐gp inhibition leads to increased rhodamine‐123 accumulation and high fluorescence. Thus, the ability of compounds to modulate the P‐gp efflux activity was evaluated by comparing the fluorescence in the treated resistant cells to the untreated L5178Y‐MDR cells (Figures S1–S3, supporting information). Results were expressed as fold increase, where higher values indicate stronger P‐gp inhibition and greater intracellular accumulation. The effectiveness of the compounds was compared with that of reference P‐gp inhibitors verapamil (20 µM) and tariquidar (0.2 µM). All compounds (1 – 26) were tested at concentrations of 2 and 20 µM.

As can be observed (Figure 1), all oxime carbamates (3–26) displayed a significant increase in efflux inhibition compared with tangeretin (1) (fold increase = 1.1 at 2 µM and 1.4 at 20 µM), which showed weak activity at both tested concentrations. The strongest efflux inhibition effect was found for compounds 19, 20, and 22. Compound 22 (fold increase = 143 at 2 µM and 247at 20 µM) was the most active at both concentrations. At 2 µM, it showed 57‐fold greater activity than the reference inhibitor verapamil (fold increase = 2.2, at a 10‐fold higher concentration) and comparable activity to the strong inhibitor tariquidar (fold increase = 79.46 at 0.2 µM) (Figure 2). Overall, except for compound 17, which was already in the saturation zone at 2 µM, the inhibitory activity of the compounds was concentration‐dependent. This set of derivatives includes compounds with aliphatic and aromatic substituents, all exhibiting variable degrees of P‐gp modulatory activities, suggesting that the presence of the oxime carbamate moiety, which increases the hydrogen bonding potential, was an important structural feature for the activity. The weak activity observed for the parental compound 1 and the oxime intermediary (2) (fold increase = 1.0 at 2 µM and 1.5 at 20 µM) supports this finding. When analyzing the results, the most active compounds at the lowest concentration (19, 20, and 22) share an aliphatic substituent and not an extra aromatic ring as expected, which could increase π–π stacking interactions with P‐gp aromatic residues. The five‐carbon aliphatic substituent in compound 22 and the cyclohexyl ring in compounds 19 and 20 confer greater conformational flexibility than the aromatic benzene ring. The results suggest that the increased flexibility of these compounds enables more effective interactions with P‐gp, probably by adopting more favorable binding conformations, which appear to be more effective, in this structural context, than the π–π stacking interactions in the benzyl/phenyl‐containing compounds.

When analyzing the predicted physicochemical properties of compounds 1–26 (Table S1, supporting information), most of the compounds are more lipophilic than the parent compound, tangeretin (1), thus corroborating lipophilicity as an important parameter in the development of P‐gp inhibitors [26]. It is interesting to note that compounds 5 (log p = 2.97) and 8 (log p = 2.99), the least lipophilic in this set of compounds, are also less active (fold increase = 22 and 25, at 2 µM, respectively). On the other hand, compound 16, one of the most lipophilic (log p = 5.19), is the least active, substantiating that lipophilicity influences the inhibitory activity but is not linearly correlated. When comparing the activity (at 2 µM) of compound 13, which has an unsubstituted phenyl group, with compounds 16 and 23, bearing a phenyl moiety with electron‐withdrawing substituents, and compounds 14, 15, and 25, having electron‐donor groups, it may be deduced a detrimental effect of the electron‐withdrawing substituents and a positive effect of the electron‐donor ones. Conversely, in compounds 6, 11, and 12, sharing a more flexible benzyl moiety with chlorine in different positions, when compared with the unsubstituted benzyl group of 4, the effect of the halogen seems to be related to its position. It might cause steric hindrance when it is in ortho‐position (12), influencing the conformational flexibility of the benzylic moiety. However, it has a beneficial effect in para or meta‐positions.

Chemosensitivity Assays

The in vitro interaction of tangeretin (1) and derivatives 3–26 in combination with the chemotherapeutic drug doxorubicin, a recognized P‐gp substrate, was performed. In this assay, human ABCB1‐transfected mouse T‐lymphoma cells (L5178Y‐MDR) were simultaneously exposed to both compounds and doxorubicin, in different ratios as described previously [27, 28]. The type of drug–drug interaction was calculated by using the Chou and Talalay method for drug combination, by determining the combination index (CI), and therefore evaluated as synergism (CI < 1), additive effect (CI = 1), or antagonism (CI > 1). As can be seen in Figure 3, except for compounds 14 and 16, which showed an additive effect (CI≅1), all derivatives exhibited a synergistic behavior (CI < 1) when coadministered with doxorubicin, thus substantiating the results obtained in the transport assay. The most active were compounds 3, 6, and 11 that showed very strong synergism, with a CI lower than 0.3.

P‐gp ATPase Activity

Since adenosine triphosphate (ATP) hydrolysis is directly linked to the P‐gp efflux activity [29], to access the type of interaction between some representative compounds (3, 5, 8, 11, 13, 17, 19, 21, 23, and 24) and this transporter protein, namely to determine if they interacted as stimulators or inhibitors, a P‐gp ATPase activity assay (P‐gp Glo, Promega) was conducted on recombinant human P‐gp membranes. This ATPase activity assay allows direct measurement of P‐gp‐mediated drug transport by assessing ATP hydrolysis. The assay quantifies unmetabolized ATP using a firefly luciferin‐luciferase reaction, in which the luminescent signal generated is directly proportional to ATP concentration. After incubating P‐gp with ATP, the reaction is stopped and the amount of ATP consumed is determined by the intensity of the luciferase‐generated luminescence in response to the remaining unmetabolized ATP. Pretreatment of samples with sodium orthovanadate (vanadate, Na3VO4), a known inhibitor of P‐gp ATPase activity, results in no P‐gp‐dependent ATP consumption. Therefore, the P‐gp basal activity (ΔRLUbasal) is defined as the difference in luminescent signals of Na3VO4‐treated samples (RLUNa3VO4) with those of untreated (NT) samples (RLUNT). On the other hand, the compound‐related P‐gp ATPase activity (ΔRLUTC) is given by comparing the luminescent signals between Na3VO4‐treated (RLUNa3VO4) and treated (TC) samples (RLUTC). By comparing the P‐gp ATPase activity of the tested compounds (ΔRLUTC) with the basal activity (ΔRLUbasal), the compounds can be classified as stimulators (ΔRLUTC>ΔRLUbasal), inhibitors (ΔRLUTC<ΔRLUbasal), or as having no effect on P‐gp ATPase activity (ΔRLUTC=ΔRLUbasal). Verapamil (0.5 mM), a P‐gp substrate and stimulator of the ATPase activity, serves as a control [29].

The results, shown in Figure 4, indicate that compounds 3, 8, 13, 23, and 24 exhibited lower P‐gp ATPase activity compared with the basal activity, suggesting that these compounds are inhibitors of P‐gp ATPase activity. This inhibitory effect may result from direct blocking of ATP hydrolysis, either through noncompetitive binding at the P‐gp ATP‐binding site, or through binding to a P‐gp allosteric residue, thereby reducing its efflux activity and consequently inhibiting the ATPase activity. Conversely, compounds 17 and 19 slightly increased ATP consumption by stimulating the P‐gp activity, suggesting that at the tested concentration, they likely act as P‐gp substrates and may competitively inhibit the efflux of other P‐gp substrates.

Prediction of Pharmacokinetic Properties of Compounds

It is important to consider the pharmacokinetic properties of compounds to ensure a suitable absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) profile. Therefore, pharmacokinetic parameters for compounds 1–26 and the reference inhibitors verapamil and tariquidar were predicted using the pkCSM descriptor algorithm protocol, enabling assessment of their ADMET profiles), (Table S2, supporting information). Derivatives 3–26 were predicted to exhibit poor water solubility, with log S values ranging from −6.59 to −4.77, comparable to controls verapamil (log S = −5.23) and tariquidar (log S = −3.70). In addition, several compounds (4, 6, 9, 11–14, 16, 17, 19, 20, 23, 25, 26) were predicted to have good intestinal absorption, ranging from 94.1% to 100%, in line with the verapamil (97.1%) and tariquidar (98.7%) controls. Also, all derivatives were indicated to have reduced ability to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), suggesting the absence of central nervous system (CNS) side effects. Regarding metabolic liability, all compounds was predicted to inhibit the cytochrome P3A4. Furthermore, none of the derivatives were predicted to exhibit Ames toxicity, suggesting the absence of mutagenic potential.

Conclusions

The main goal of this work was to identify new anticancer compounds with a focus on targeting MDR cancer cells. To achieve this goal, a set of 23 new oxime carbamate derivatives (3–26) was prepared by modifying the C‐4 position of the tangeretin (1) scaffold. These compounds were then evaluated for their antiproliferative activity against both sensitive and P‐gp overexpressing resistant mouse T‐lymphoma cells. While most compounds exhibited moderate antiproliferative activity against both sensitive and resistant cells, compounds 4, 7, and 25 demonstrated relevant improvement of the antiproliferative effect when compared with tangeretin (1), which showed no significant activity. Furthermore, compound 4 was more effective against the resistant cells, exhibiting IC50 values of 8.60 and 6.02 µM against sensitive and resistant cells, respectively. All compounds showed considerably enhanced P‐gp inhibitory activity compared with tangeretin (1). Among them, compound 22 displayed the strongest inhibitory effect, comparable to the activity of tariquidar. Regarding the drug combination assay, all compounds but 14 and 16 exhibited synergistic interactions with doxorubicin, with compounds 3, 6, and 11 showing the most pronounced effects. In the ATPase activity assay, compounds 3, 8, 13, 23, and 24 acted as inhibitors, while compounds 17 and 19 slightly stimulated the ATPase activity, suggesting a possible role as competitive substrates. Overall, these findings highlight the potential of the structural modification of the tangeretin scaffold through the introduction of the oxime carbamate moiety. It allows additional hydrogen‐bonding formation, which seems to be a good approach for developing effective MDR reversal agents.

Experimental Section

General Considerations

Deuterated chloroform used for NMR analysis was purchased from Merck with a degree of purity higher than 95%. Reagent grade chemicals were acquired from Alfa Aesar, Sigma–Aldrich or Merck. Column chromatography was carried out on silica gel (Merck, 230–400 mesh ATM). Thin‐layer chromatography (TLC) was performed on precoated silica gel 60 F254 (Merck) plates, with visualization under UV light (λ 254 and 366 nm) and by spraying with a solution of H2SO4‐MeOH (1:1), followed by heating. All the reactions were monitored by TLC. Preparative thin‐layer chromatography was performed with silica gel 60 GF254 (Merck, 0.040–0.063 mm). NMR spectra (1D and 2D) were recorded on a Bruker Fourier 300 Ultra‐Shield instrument (1H 300 MHz, 13C 75 MHz). Chemical shifts, δ, are expressed in ppm (parts per million), referenced to CDCl3 solvent, and coupling constants, J, are expressed in Hertz (Hz). Multiplicities are given as: s (singlet), d (duplet), t (triplet), q (quartet), and m (multiplet). Spectra were assigned using appropriate APT, HSQC, and HMBC sequences. High‐resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) analysis was performed by direct injection on a Q Exactive Plus Hybrid Quadrupole‐Orbitrap mass spectrometer using electrospray ionization (ESI) in positive mode.

General Preparation of Derivatives 3–12

Tangeretin oxime (2) (50 mg, 0.129 mmol) was dissolved in tetrahydrofuran (THF, 7 ml) at 50ºC. To the stirred solution was added sodium hydride (5 mg, 0.194 mmol) at 0ºC and stirred for 10 min under a nitrogen atmosphere. Then, 1,1′‐carbonyldiimidazole (CDI, 32 mg, 0.194 mmol) was added and stirred for 10 min at 0ºC. After that, the appropriate amine (0.194 mmol) was added to the reaction mixture. The reaction mixture was stirred at 0ºC for 30–120 min under nitrogen. After evaporating the solvent, under vacuum at 40°C, the residue was submitted to flash chromatography and further purified by preparative TLC.

Cell Lines and Cultures

L5178Y mouse T‐lymphoma cells (parental, PAR) (ECACC catalog no. 87 111 908, US FDA, Silver Spring, MD, USA) were transfected with pHa MDR1/A retrovirus, as previously reported [30]. The ABCB1‐gene transfected cells (MDR) were selected by culturing the infected cells with 60 ng mL−1 of colchicine (Sigma–Aldrich Chemie GmbH, Steinheim, Germany) to preserve the MDR phenotype. Both cell lines were cultured in McCoy's 5A supplemented with 10% heat‐inactivated horse serum, 100 U L−1 L‐glutamine, and 100 mg L−1 penicillin–streptomycin mixture, all obtained from Sigma–Aldrich. Cell lines were kept at 37°C and the passaging of cultures was performed every 2–3 days.

Antiproliferative Assay

The antiproliferative effects of tangeretin (1) and its derivatives (2–26) on parental (PAR) and multidrug‐resistant (MDR) mouse T‐lymphoma cells were assessed using the thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide assay. In 96‐well flat‐bottomed microtiter plates, 100 μL of medium was pipetted to each well, except for the medium control wells, where 200 μL of medium was pipetted. The compounds were diluted in 100 µL of medium through a twofold serial dilution starting at 100 µM. Doxorubicin was used as a positive control and was also diluted by a twofold serial dilution (starting with 8.6 μM). Then, the cells were prepared at a density of 6 × 103 cells in 100 µL of medium per well, except for the medium control wells. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 72 h. After the incubation period, 20 µL of MTT (thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide; Sigma–Aldrich Chemie GmbH, Steinheim) solution of 5 mg mL−1 in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 4 h at 37°C. Drug Combination Assay

Doxorubicin (2 mg mL−1, Teva Pharmaceuticals, Budapest, Hungary) was serially diluted horizontally in 100 µL, starting at a concentration of 8.6 µM, following the previously described protocol. The resistance modifier was then serially diluted vertically in 50 µL; the starting concentration was determined based on the IC50 value. Cells were resuspended in culture medium and distributed into each well in 50 µL containing 6 × 103 cells, except for the medium control wells, to a final volume of 200 µL per well. The checkerboard plates were incubated for 72 h at 37°C in a CO2 incubator. At the end of the incubation period, cell growth was assessed using the MTT staining method, as described earlier. Drug interactions were analyzed using Calcusyn software [27, 28]. Each dose–response curve (for both individual agents and combinations) was fitted to a linear model using the median effect equation, which provided the median effect value (corresponding to the IC50) and the slope (m). The goodness‐of‐fit was evaluated using the linear correlation coefficient (r), and only data with r > 0.90 were considered. The extent of drug interaction was expressed using the CI. A CI value close to 1 was indicative of additivity, a CI < 1 represented synergy, and a CI > 1 was indicative of antagonism.

P‐gp ATPase Activity Assay

P‐glycoprotein ATPase activity was assessed using the Pgp‐Glo Assay Systems Promega kit according to the manufacturer's instructions [12]. Briefly, 20 µL of recombinant human P‐gp membranes (1.25 mg mL−1), which express high levels of human P‐gp, were mixed with 20 µL of Pgp‐Glo assay buffer and incubated for 5 min at 37°C. The compounds were tested at a concentration of 25 µM. Verapamil (0.5 mM) was used as a substrate control, sodium orthovanadate (Na3VO4, 25 µM) as an inhibitor control, and DMSO (2%) as a solvent control. The reaction was initiated by adding 10 µL of 25 mM MgATP, followed by incubation at 37°C for 40 min. After the ATPase reaction was stopped, 50 µL of ATP Detection Reagent was added, and the samples were incubated at room temperature for 20 min. The luciferase‐generated luminescent signal, corresponding to the remaining unmetabolized ATP, was measured at 580 nm using a CLARIOstar Plus plate reader (BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany), and the signal was expressed in relative light units (RLU). Basal P‐gp ATPase activity (ΔRLUbasal) was determined by calculating the difference in luminescent signals between the Na3VO4‐treated sample (RLUNa3VO4) and the untreated (NT) sample (RLUNT). ΔRLUTC>ΔRLUbasal: the compound is stimulator of P‐gp ATPase activity; ΔRLUTC=ΔRLUbasal: the compound has no effect on P‐gp ATPase activity; ΔRLUTC<ΔRLUbasal: the compound is inhibitor of P‐gp ATPase activity.
