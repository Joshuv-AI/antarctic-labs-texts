Reduced Clinical and Economic Burden Through Evidence‐Based Dressing Selection for Wound Management: Findings From a Systematic Literature Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Catherine Milne, Kelly McFee, Ben Costa, Neil Askew, Devon Allen, Lloyd Atkinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe growing burden of wounds is placing pressure on clinicians and healthcare providers globally. This study aimed to determine the impact of using a hydrocellular polyurethane foam dressing (HPFD) on weekly dressing changes in mixed aetiology wounds. A systematic literature review was performed to identify studies reporting HPFD use versus other/previous dressings via PubMed, EMBASE and the Cochrane Library. A total of four studies were found reporting the use of HPFD in comparison to other dressings (386 patients), with 3 of these studies specifically providing data for HPFD use versus other (non‐HPFD) foam dressings (136 patients). Meta‐analysis revealed mean differences of −1.66 [95% CI −1.87;−1.48] and −1.23 [95% CI −1.65;−0.83] dressing changes per week in favour of HPFD versus other dressings and other foam dressings, respectively. A cost calculator model assessed the economic impact of this reduction of dressing change frequency from US and UK payer perspectives and found a 41% and 36% relative reduction in dressing change‐related costs in hospital/clinic and community settings for both perspectives. These findings were stable under sensitivity analyses, which identified clinical time as being the predominant driver of costs in all settings. These findings highlight the importance of appropriate evidence‐based dressing selection in enabling the extension of dressing wear times.

Introduction

Recent data has reconfirmed that wound burden is increasing year‐on‐year [1, 2]. This burden extends beyond patients and places substantial strain on caregivers, healthcare systems and, critically, the workforce. For patients, the impact of their wounds on formal quality of life (QoL) measures has been reported heavily using several wound‐specific and general QoL tools [3]. The immediate effects, such as pain, immobility and odour, can precipitate further burdens which weigh heavily on patients. Social isolation and loss of autonomy are widely reported among patients with ongoing wound care requirements [4, 5]; while wound burden is primarily driven by physical sequelae, the need for frequent wound management both in primary and community settings requires that patients be closely tied to their healthcare providers, further disrupting their lives.

The economic burden of wounds is significant. From a global perspective, the cost of wounds is estimated to be over $300 billion for the top 10 wound care spending nations as of 2022 [6]. In the US alone, wounds are estimated to cost the Medicare system $22.5 billion annually, with a growing proportion of these costs moving away from hospital and clinic settings to community‐based care [2]. Similarly, in the UK, the cost of wound care has been estimated to have risen 48% between the 2012/2013 and 2017/2018 period to over £8.3billion [1]. Therefore, strategies must be implemented, both large scale and small, to address these spiralling costs.

With increasing numbers of wounds to care for, more complex patient needs, a reduced relative workforce and heavier emphasis on documentation, nursing staff are also feeling the burden of wound care [7, 8]. These pressures are further compounded by limited wound‐specific training among non‐specialist nurses and other healthcare professionals, contributing to a workforce that is both stretched and insufficiently prepared to deliver optimal wound care [9, 10, 11]. This lack of training not only relates to wound care practices but also precludes evidence‐based dressing selection. Non‐specialised nursing staff are often unable to differentiate dressings between or within categories [12], resulting in further barriers to change and propagating clinical inertia. The negative impact of poor work environment and education on wound‐related outcomes has recently been reported by Turi et al. [13] who observed significantly improved outcomes in centres with higher levels of staff wellbeing and education than centres with poorer work environments. Clearly, there is a need for improved education and new efficiencies in the care of wounds to help reduce case‐load burden on staff.

One such change is the practice of undisturbed wound healing, where dressings are left in place to allow for the body's healing response to occur and limit potential trauma to the wound during changes. This practice also benefits clinical resources, with fewer changes required during a treatment period and has been shown to result in tangible economic savings compared to ritualistic dressing change practices [14]. To enable this practice, wound dressings require physical characteristics such as the ability to absorb and retain exudate/bacteria, prevent leakage and odour release and effectively mask exudate. An evolving feature of advanced dressings is the ability to signify when the absorbent layer is approaching capacity; this can help facilitate appropriate dressing changes and undisturbed wound healing practices through extending wear times [7, 15]. Furthermore, such technologies could be vital in shared wound care approaches by taking the guesswork out of dressing changes, and this uncertainty can result in regular contact with supervising clinical staff. The increasing decentralisation of wound care away from clinics and into the community leads to more nurse time spent travelling and a growing reliance on telemedicine for patient management [16]. Having insight into the fullness of a dressing can enable patients to flag to their care team when a change appointment is needed or allow them, or their carers, to change the dressing when clinically appropriate. This can lead to increased wear times for such dressings and could reduce dressing change‐related travel and telemedicine enquiries for nursing staff, of which 24% of telehealth calls were reported to be related to wound dressing enquiries from patients [16].

The time taken to change a dressing is often misreported when in the context of estimating resource cost (i.e., cost of clinical time). While the physical act of applying and removing a dressing may take only up to 5 min, time spent during the dressing change appointment needs to be accounted for, which then allows for more accurate accounting of time for the episode of care. Factors such as the patient consultation, gathering supplies, donning of personal protective equipment, preparing the environment, wound cleansing/debridement, completion of documentation and coordination of further care should be accounted for. It has been recently reported that a dressing change appointment takes approximately 30 min and up to an hour for some patients with complex needs in UK settings, not accounting for travel between appointments within the community [8]. When accounting for travel, the time taken to change dressings has been reported to take on average 60 min [17]. It is therefore unsurprising that 50% of nurse time in the community has been estimated for the delivery of wound care, which could be substantially reduced with the combination of shared wound care practices and use of advanced dressings that enable longer wear time practices [18].

The aim of this study was to investigate the clinical and economic impact of adopting a five‐layer hydrocellular foam dressing (HPFD; ALLEVYN Five‐Layer Foam Dressings) on weekly dressing change frequencies in mixed aetiology wounds compared to other dressings.

Materials and Methods

This systematic literature review and meta‐analysis was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Literature Reviews and Meta‐analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [19]. The review protocol was not prospectively registered.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

A search was conducted in PubMed, EMBASE and the Cochrane Library on 10th October 2023 for articles reporting on the duration of clinical wear times with a five‐layer hydrocellular polyurethane foam dressing (HPFD; ALLEVYN Life Dressing, Smith+Nephew, Hull, UK) in mixed aetiology wounds. Search terms included ‘ALLEVYN Life’, ‘five‐layer’, ‘multilayer’ and ‘foam dressing’. Limits for English and human‐only studies were applied where appropriate.

Exclusions

Studies were excluded if they were non‐comparative in relation to dressing change frequency and did not report dressing usage of the target dressing versus other passive dressings, or if substantial transformation of the source data was needed to estimate the mean weekly wear times, which could introduce substantial variability into the data. Studies were also excluded if they were meta‐analytical in nature.

Data Extraction and Assessment

Mean weekly dressing usage was extracted from all relevant studies. When variability was reported using metrics other than standard deviation (SD) (e.g., standard error of the mean), these were converted to SDs. Where no variability data were reported, study authors were contacted to request this information. Studies for which no measure of variability could be obtained were excluded from the analysis. When available from publications or provided by study authors, data were used to perform sub‐analyses comparing the HPFD with previous foam dressings as the comparator. In instances where the number of wounds per patient was not specified, it was assumed that each patient had one wound.

Two authors independently assessed the literature arising from the searches against the inclusion and exclusion criteria and discussed any discrepancies until agreement was reached. Extracted data included: the location of the study, dressings used (exact dressing(s) or categories), weekly dressing usage and the types of wounds investigated. Due to the nature of the data and mixed experimental designs of the included studies, risk of bias assessment was not performed.

Statistical Analysis

Analysis of the target dressing and comparators was performed via pair‐wise meta‐analysis using the inverse‐variance method (DerSimonian‐Laird) in the R package (R 4.2.1) and represented as mean difference (MD), with a 95% confidence interval (CI) as the summary statistic, with significance defined as p < 0.05. Heterogeneity of included studies was assessed using the I2 statistic. A random effects model was used for all analyses due to heterogeneity in study types and practices prior to implementing an education programme. Resulting data were assessed for robustness using sensitivity (leave‐one‐out) analysis.

Economic Analysis

A cost calculation model was developed using the meta‐analytical dressing change findings for hospital/clinic and community settings, from both US and UK payer perspectives. Time required for the hospital/clinic settings accounted for the patient consultation, time to physically change/remove the dressing, need for wound cleansing, the sourcing and disposal of materials and completion of documentation. Additional time was included for travel for community settings. Nursing costs were obtained from the National Bureau of Labor Statistics and the NHS PSSRU, for US and UK model inputs, respectively [20, 21]. A one‐way sensitivity analysis was conducted for each setting/perspective to assess robustness of findings by varying input parameters of nursing costs/time per dressing change appointment and the HPFD and other dressing costs independently by ±20%. Confidence intervals derived from the meta‐analysis were converted to SD as outlined in the Cochrane handbook and imputed into the model to account for change variability [22].

Results

The searches resulted in a total of 785 studies, which after assessing against the inclusion and exclusion criteria, resulted in four studies eligible for analysis (Figure 1) [23, 24, 25, 26]. Of note, there was one additional study that might appear to meet the inclusion criteria, but was excluded after closer analysis. This study was a crossover RCT reported by Alvarez et al. [27], which reported strikethrough and edge seal loss for investigated dressings at days 3 and 7. As this publication did not report dressing change frequencies, and the transformation of these data would rely on multiple assumptions on dressing change triggers, this study was not included.

Results of Individual Studies

All identified studies reported improvements in the frequency of dressing changes with HPFD (with or without an accompanying education regime) compared with all previous dressings (Table 2). Tiscar‐Gonzalez et al. reported a 47.1% reduction in mean weekly dressing changes when compared to previously used dressings (1.66 ± 0.87 vs. 3.14 ± 1.77, respectively). Joy et al. similarly reported a 50% reduction in mean weekly dressing changes in a UK community care trust, with a pre‐implementation frequency of 3.57 ± 2.39 compared to 1.78 ± 0.85 post‐implementation. A 27% increase in dressing wear was reported by Krönert et al. based on wounds treated in two German ambulatory wound care sites (4.59 ± 2.19 previous dressings vs. 3.34 ± 0.75 HPFD). Scalise et al. reported the impact of the dressing on wear times in mixed aetiology wounds among multiple primary and community care providers across Spain, Italy and Portugal, and found a 50% reduction in weekly dressing changes (3.60 ± 1.53 previous dressing vs. 1.80 ± 0.73 HPFD).

Study authors were contacted to obtain additional information on dressing types to allow comparative analyses with foam dressings. Authors from three studies provided foam‐specific data; however, Scalise et al. did not record data for types of foam dressings used. Analysis of data from the three contributing studies demonstrated that the observed reduction in weekly dressing changes seen with HPFD persisted when restricted to patients who had previously received a different, non‐HPFD foam dressing.

Meta‐Analysis

Meta‐analysis of the four studies for mean weekly dressing change frequency versus previous dressing use resulted in a mean difference in favour of HPFD of −1.66 changes per week [95% CI −1.87;−1.45] (p < 0.0001, Figure 2). Analysis of dressing change frequency for HPFD versus other previously used foam dressings resulted in a mean reduction in weekly dressing changes of −1.23 [95% CI −1.63;−0.83] (p < 0.0001, Figure 3). Taken together, these results represent a 45% and 38% relative reduction in mean weekly change frequency versus all previous dressings and foam dressings, respectively.

Economic Analysis

The use of HPFD in these settings for the treatment of mixed aetiology wounds was found to be cost saving when compared to other dressings and specifically foam dressings, with relative cost reductions of 41% and 36% for community and hospital/clinic settings, respectively. Calculated wound care costs and relative differences between HPFD and other dressings are provided in Table 3 and present cost calculations for a cohort of 1000 patients receiving dressings for a four‐week period. One‐way sensitivity analysis was performed by changing individual cost inputs (Table S1) and demonstrated that the model was robust and remained cost‐saving when varying inputs to account for variability in real world practice. Primary cost drivers in the model were: dressing change frequencies in either the HPFD or comparator groups, followed by nursing time/cost (Figure S1). Importantly, cost of dressings in either the HPFD or comparator groups were the lowest contributors of cost in the model for all settings, therefore validating the cost equity approach.

Discussion

To meet the increasing clinical and economic demands of wounds, efficiencies need to be realised across the continuum of care. Against the backdrop of high levels of staff burnout and rising caseloads, any proposed changes must be both easy to implement for clinical staff and at least cost‐neutral for healthcare systems. The findings presented in this study indicate that dressing selection has the potential to reduce wound care burden by reducing the number of dressing changes. The health economic analyses provided highlight the potential monetary benefits for healthcare providers in using advanced foam dressings which can facilitate extended wear times. These findings agree with the resource savings reported in the identified studies and highlight the importance of assessing the cost of a dressing change beyond the unit price of the dressing itself [23, 26].

Identifying the exact mechanisms through which extended dressing wear time is enabled was beyond the scope of this study. Nevertheless, several dressing characteristics may contribute, including effective exudate masking, reliable absorption and retention of exudate and the ability to indicate when the dressing needs changing. Others have suggested that the masking of strikethrough in combination with the novel change indicator may instil confidence in clinical staff to leave the dressing on for longer [26]. This confidence is appreciable in shared wound care settings and could help patients, under clinical oversight, to assess the necessity of changing their dressing, which may help to reduce dressing‐related enquiries in community settings. With the ongoing drive to move wound care away from hospital settings into community and to involve patients and family in the delivery of their care, the use of dressings which help to determine change needs could help widen the pool of patients deemed suitable for share care.

The benefits of dressing wear time extension are not a new concept and are one of the foundational requirements to enable successful undisturbed wound healing practices. An often‐unrecognised determinant of this success, however, is the performance of the dressings being used. Substantial variations exist in dressing characteristics, even within the same category, as evidenced through independent testing reported by the NHS Clinical Review Team [28]. While not a novel observation for wound care specialists, limited education on appropriate dressing selection persists among non‐specialist clinicians. With an increasing reliance on non‐specialists for the delivery of wound care globally, there is a risk of dressings being increasingly selected ritualistically rather than in an evidence‐based manner. Increasing efficiencies in the delivery of care is a key priority in NHS England's 2025/2026 priorities and operational planning guidance [29]. Variation in practice has been identified as a primary cause of inefficiencies in wound care as reported via a cross‐sectional multiservice survey by Gray et al. [30], who attributed these, in part, to a lack of evidence‐based dressing use. Our findings echo this sentiment and data (such as provided in this current study) can be a valuable tool enabling clinicians and procurement teams to source appropriate dressings best suited to their clinical and financial needs.

Other, less apparent benefits could be realised when extending dressing wear times. For changes undertaken by clinicians in the US, community care providers are reimbursed a flat fee for an episode of care under the Medicare Patient‐Driven Groupings Model, meaning that additional visits incur cost to third party care providers [31]. As a result, agencies are often reluctant to accept patients with high weekly dressing change needs into home‐based care, which can result in delayed discharge from hospital and leave the patient in a ‘wound care desert’, with a hospital keen to discharge and an agency unwilling to transition them into community care. Through enabling the reduction of weekly dressing changes, such patients could be more likely to transition unimpeded into home‐based care, while also helping to protect reimbursement for home care providers.

While this study provides valuable insight into the value of evidence‐based dressing selection and its impact on wear times when using HPFDs, some inherent limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Firstly, the number of studies identified was limited and characterised by heterogeneity in study design. This is in part due to a lack of standardised wear time data being reported in the literature and the limited scope of inclusion for dressings from one manufacturer, meaning findings should not be interpreted as being representative of multilayer foam dressings as a category. The present analysis is therefore intended, in part, to serve as a catalyst for improved and more consistent wear time data reporting to facilitate evidence‐based decision making. Secondly, the diversity in study methodologies and the operational nature of the outcome measured in these studies precluded the application of a robust risk and conventional risk‐of‐bias assessment. As a result, the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution, particularly when extrapolating across settings or care models. However, the consistency and magnitude of effect size observed between these limited numbers of heterogeneous studies suggests that the positive effect on wear times may be achievable across varied healthcare settings, but further data obtained from high level clinical studies should be sought to confirm this. Finally, it was not possible to disentangle the independent effects of training or educational interventions on dressing change practices from the observed outcomes. This likely underscores the importance of pairing appropriate dressing selection and staff education in driving operational efficiencies and supporting best practice implementation.

Conclusion

This systematic literature review and meta‐analysis demonstrated reductions in mean weekly dressing changes with HPFD compared to both all previous dressings and alternative (non‐HPFD) foam dressings. In both comparisons, HPFD was cost saving, suggesting incorporation of these dressings can be clinically and economically effective in wound care. Habitual dressing change practices contribute to inefficiencies in wound management and suboptimal use of staff resources, driven in part by limited wound care training, an increasing number of wound cases and trends to managing wounds in post‐acute care settings. The capacity of dressings to address key clinical challenges, such as exudate management and change indication, is often overlooked, despite their positive impact on patient experience, staff stress, and economic realities.
