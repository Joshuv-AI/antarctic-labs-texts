ATE1 promotes breast cancer progression via arginylation-dependent regulation of MAPK-MYC signaling
Authors: Laxman Nawale, Shinyeong Ju, Jung Gi Kim, Nak Kyun Soung, Bo Yeon Kim, Cheolju Lee, Hyunjoo Cha-Molstad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArginyl-tRNA–protein transferase (ATE1) catalyzes N-terminal arginylation, a regulatory protein modification implicated in various cellular processes, including proliferation, apoptosis, and migration. Although ATE1 has context-dependent roles in cancer, its specific function in breast cancer remains unclear. This study investigates the oncogenic role of ATE1 across multiple breast cancer subtypes and its underlying molecular mechanisms.MethodsATE1 expression in breast cancer was evaluated using TCGA data and immunoblotting across breast cancer cell lines and normal mammary epithelial cells (HMEC). Functional studies using siRNA- and shRNA-mediated knockdown assessed ATE1’s role in cell viability, clonogenic growth, migration, and tumorigenesis in vitro and xenograft models. Quantitative proteomics, R-catcher–based N-terminomics, and pathway analyses were employed to identify ATE1-dependent signaling networks, with a focus on MAPK-MYC axis regulation. Flow cytometry and immunoblotting were used to assess cell cycle progression, apoptosis, and MYC stability.ResultsATE1 was significantly upregulated in breast cancer cells and associated with poor prognosis in early-stage patients. ATE1 depletion selectively impaired viability, proliferation, and migration in breast cancer cells, but not in HMECs. In vivo, ATE1 silencing suppressed tumor growth in xenograft models. Proteomic profiling revealed that ATE1 regulates the cell cycle and survival pathways in a subtype-specific manner, particularly through modulation of the MAPK–MYC–CDK6 axis in luminal T-47D cells. ATE1 stabilized MYC protein via ERK-mediated phosphorylation at Ser62, promoting cell cycle progression and suppressing apoptosis. Rescue experiments confirmed that ATE1’s tumor-promoting activity depends on its arginyltransferase function.ConclusionsATE1 promotes breast cancer progression by enhancing cell proliferation, survival, and migration through MAPK-dependent stabilization of MYC in a lineage-specific context. These findings identify ATE1 as a potential therapeutic target and highlight the relevance of protein arginylation in the molecular heterogeneity of breast cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02376-9.Keywords: ATE1 (arginyltransferase 1), Breast cancer, MYC signaling, Cell proliferation, Cell survival, Tumor progression, N-terminal arginylation, N-degron pathway

Introduction

The arginyl-tRNA-protein transferase 1 (ATE1) enzyme is evolutionarily conserved from yeast to humans and mediates post-translational protein arginylation, which plays a crucial role in ubiquitin-dependent N-degron-mediated proteolysis (The N-degron pathway). Protein arginylation traditionally occurs at the N-terminal residues of proteins whose neo-N termini are either N-terminal aspartate (Nt-Asp), glutamate (Nt-Glu), or oxidized cysteine (Nt-oxidized Cys). The neo-N-termini Nt-Asn and Nt-Gln can undergo deamidation to generate Nt-Asp and -Glu, respectively (Fig. 1a) [1]. ATE1 transfers Arg to these Nt-residues from Arg-tRNA in a ribosome-independent manner. In addition to the N-termini mentioned above, ATE1-catalyzed midchain arginylation of proteins has also been reported [2]. Typically, Nt-Arg acts as a canonical N-degron of substrates, leading to proteasomal or autophagic degradation via the Arg/N-degron pathway [3–5]. However, the arginylation of some proteins, such as β-actin and calreticulin, does not lead to metabolic instability but is involved in other functions, such as cell motility and apoptosis [6–8]. Through these molecular mechanisms, ATE1 regulates a variety of developmental and physiological processes, including mammalian embryogenesis; cardiovascular development and angiogenesis [9]; sensing of heme, nitric oxide, and oxygen [10–12]; regulation of G proteins [13]; inhibition of apoptosis [14]; protein quality control [4, 8]; cell migration and adhesion [6, 7]; cell division [15]; cell proliferation [16] and cancer [17–20].

The number of studies investigating the role of ATE1 in cancer is increasing. Most studies have described ATE1 as a tumor suppressor and a potential prognostic indicator that inversely correlates with metastatic progression and survival in certain types of cancers, specifically prostate and liver cancer [17–19]. In contrast, the oncogenic effect of ATE1 on melanoma cell growth and migration has recently been reported [20]. These conflicting ATE1 effects on cancer development and progression indicate that its function in cancer may depend on the cancer type-specific ATE1 substrate, the arginylome of cancer. The varying effects of this enzyme on different types of cancer are not unique to ATE1. N-terminal acetyltransferases (NATs) have also been reported to be oncogenic and tumor-suppressive [21]. In certain types of breast cancer, proteolysis of the C-terminal fragment of BRCA1, a tumor suppressor in breast cancer cells, has been shown to require ATE1 [22], suggesting a potential pro-tumorigenic role of ATE1 in breast cancer. However, the role and detailed mechanisms of ATE1 in breast cancer development and progression remain poorly understood.

Breast cancer (11.6%) is the second most common cancer after lung cancer (12.4%), with 2.3 million new cases annually across 185 countries. It ranks fourth among cancer-related deaths owing to tumor heterogeneity, rapid progression, and poor prognosis [23]. Treatment primarily involves surgical removal followed by radiation therapy. Olaparib and talazoparib are FDA-approved treatments for metastatic breast cancer with BRCA mutations [24], with the OlympiA trial showing improved outcomes in early-stage cases [25]. Although tamoxifen has been used for ER-positive breast cancer, its efficacy is limited [26, 27]. Despite progress in some breast cancer treatments, certain types, such as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), lack therapeutic targets and FDA-approved drugs [28]. Therefore, identifying more effective therapeutic targets and strategies is essential to improve treatment across all subtypes [29].

Oncogenic MYC, particularly c-Myc, is a master regulator of malignant growth and is deregulated in over 70% of human cancers [30–32]. As a master regulator, c-Myc orchestrates various aspects of oncogenesis, including proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation, and metabolism [33, 34]. MYC is highly expressed in breast cancer, and its deregulation contributes significantly to breast cancer development and progression, and is associated with poor prognosis [35, 36]. Mechanisms causing MYC deregulation include gene amplification, transcriptional regulation, and mRNA and protein stabilization, which are associated with oncogenic pathway activation and loss of tumor suppressors [35, 37]. Previous studies have shown that the post-translational modification of MYC is essential for tumorigenesis [38]. Specifically, Ras activates the MAPK pathway, leading to ERK-mediated phosphorylation of MYC at Ser62, which enhances MYC stability and transcriptional activity [39]. In contrast, subsequent phosphorylation at Thr58 by glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK-3β) destabilizes MYC through Fbw7 E3 ligase-mediated ubiquitylation of MYC p-Thr58 [40, 41]. This phosphorylation-dependent regulation of MYC stability represents a crucial intersection between the MAPK pathway and cell cycle control, as MYC drives the expression of cyclins and CDKs, while suppressing cell cycle inhibitors. In addition to Ser62 and Thr58, 11 additional phosphorylation sites have been identified that affect MYC stability [31]. UBR5, an E3 ligase, can mediate MYC proteolysis in breast cancer cells [42]. More recent studies have advanced our understanding of how MYC stabilization and degradation are regulated in cancer. Kotani et al. demonstrated that SUMOylation and ubiquitination pathways compete to control MYC levels in cancer cells [43]. The E3 ubiquitin ligase FBXW7 remains critical for promoting MYC polyubiquitination and degradation [44, 45], while β-TrCP has been shown to ubiquitinate MYC with context-dependent effects on its stability [46]. Alternative degradation mechanisms independent of ubiquitination have also been identified. Gu et al. described the midnolin-proteasome pathway as a contributor to MYC turnover [47], and Toure et al. reported that the degradation of transcriptional partners like CDK9 can indirectly affect MYC stability [48]. External cues such as metabolic shifts and stress responses further influence MYC degradation. Gao et al. found that the SET protein can prevent MYC degradation by modulating E3 ligase interactions [49], whereas Zhu et al. showed that squamocin-induced endoplasmic reticulum stress enhances MYC degradation [50]. Additionally, Liu et al. identified FNDC3B-267aa as a protein that facilitates MYC proteasomal degradation, emphasizing the role of tumor cell–intrinsic factors in modulating MYC levels and cancer progression [51]. This study demonstrates that ATE1-mediated post-translational arginylation exerts proliferative effects across multiple breast cancer subtypes, primarily through cell cycle modulation. Our mechanistic analyses reveal a complex, context-dependent regulatory network, as evidenced by ATE1’s differential impact on MAPK pathway activation between breast cancer cell lines. Specifically, ATE1 activates the MAPK signaling cascade in T-47D cells, leading to enhanced phosphorylation of MYC at Ser62 and subsequent metabolic stabilization of this oncoprotein. However, this MAPK-dependent mechanism is notably absent in MDA-MB-157 cells, suggesting alternative pathways through which ATE1 influences cell cycle progression in different breast cancer contexts. This heterogeneity in ATE1’s molecular interactions highlights the complexity of post-translational arginylation in oncogenic signaling. Despite these mechanistic differences, ATE1 consistently promotes multiple hallmarks of cancer, including sustained proliferative signaling, resistance to apoptosis, and enhanced migratory capacity across diverse breast cancer cells. The elucidation of these cell-type-specific regulatory mechanisms identifies ATE1 as a potential therapeutic target that could disrupt oncogenic signaling in various breast cancer subtypes, albeit potentially requiring tailored approaches based on the molecular context of individual tumors.

Methods

Antibodies and chemical reagents

The following primary antibodies were used for immunoblotting: ATE1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc-271220), β-actin (Sigma #A1978), cyclin D (Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc-8396), Cdk4 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc-23896), Cdk6 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc-7961), phospho-Rb (Ser795) (Cell Signaling Technology #9301), Rb (Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc-50), cyclin E (Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc-25303), cyclin A (Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc-751), Cdk2 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc-6248), cyclin B (Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc-166152), c-Myc (D84C12) (Cell Signaling Technology #5605), c-Myc (9E10) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc-40), p27 Kip1 (Cell Signaling Technology #3686), p21 Waf1/Cip1 (Cell Signaling Technology #2946), p16 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc-81613), GAPDH (Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc-47724), phospho-c-Myc (Ser62) (abcam #ab78318), phospho-p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) (Thr202/Tyr204) (Cell Signaling Technology #9106), p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) (Cell Signaling Technology #9102), phospho-Akt (Ser473) (Cell Signaling Technology #9271), phospho-Akt (Thr308) (Cell Signaling Technology #9275), Akt1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc-5298), PARP-1 (H-250) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc-7150), Caspase-3 (Pro and Active) (IMGENEX #IMG-144 A), E-cadherin (Cell Signaling Technology #3195), and vimentin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc-6260). Bortezomib (A2614) was purchased from APExBIO Technology (Houston, TX, USA).

Cell culture

The human breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-157, BT-549, HCC1143, Hs 578T, MCF7, SK-BR-3, T-47D, and BT-474 were cultured in RPMI (Hyclone, SH30027.01) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Gibco,16000.044). For control purposes, an immortalized normal human mammary epithelial cell line (HMEC, ATCC PCS-600-010) was cultured in HuMEC Basal Serum-Free Medium, which included HuMEC Supplement and bovine pituitary extract (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, 12752-010). The HEK 293T cell line, derived from human embryonic kidney cells, was grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco, SH30243.01) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS). All cells described earlier were cultured for less than 30 passages and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 95% air and 5% CO2. They were routinely monitored for cross-contamination and screened for mycoplasma contamination using an e-Myco™ Mycoplasma PCR Detection Kit (iNtRON Biotechnology, Inc., Korea, 25235).

Lentivirus production and transduction

To produce VSV-G-pseudo-typed lentivirus particles, HEK 293T cells at 90–95% confluence were co-transfected with 10 µg of the pCMV-dR8.2 dvpr transfer plasmid, 5 µg of the pCMV-VSV-G coat protein vector, 10 µg of the vector plasmid (shC, shATE1#69 and #71, Wt-ATE1, Mut-ATE1), and 62.5 µl of HP DNA transfection reagent (06366236001, Sigma). Twelve hours after transfection, the medium used for transfection was replaced with a complete growth medium (DMEM containing 10% FBS). Forty-eight hours after transfection, the viral supernatant was collected and passed through a 0.45- μm surfactant-free cellulose acetate syringe filter. Lentiviral particles were stored at −80 °C in 1 ml aliquots before titration/use. The physical titer was determined using the guidelines of the Invitrogen ViraPower Lentiviral Expression Kit. Briefly, BT-474 cells were infected with a 2 × dilution series of the lentivirus. To select cells, puromycin was applied at a concentration of 2 µg/ml. Following–10–12 day period of puromycin selection, cells were stained with 0.5% crystal violet solution for 30 min at room temperature. The blue-stained colonies were counted to determine the titer. The efficiency of knockdown or overexpression was confirmed by immunoblotting.

To transduce the targeted cells, 1 × 105 cells in a 6-well plate were infected with titered amounts of virus particles for 24 h. The next day, the virus-containing medium was removed and refreshed with 500 µl complete growth media, and the cells were further cultured for the indicated time points. We do not recommend using polybrene at the time of virus transduction because it alone is cytotoxic to breast cancer cells at concentrations as low as 5 µg/ml. For xenograft studies, 5 × 105 MDA-MB-231 cells per 10-cm dish were transfected with 2 × dilutions of lentivirus for 24 h. The cells were replenished with fresh complete growth medium and incubated for 12 h. Cells transduced with lentivirus were subjected to selection with 2 µg/ml puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich) for 12 h before their application in xenograft assays.

Western blotting

The specified cultured cells were washed twice with cold PBS before being immediately lysed in 2X Laemmli protein sample buffer (2ME +) for 10 min at 100 °C. For immunoblotting, equal protein quantities were separated using 9% SDS-PAGE. The separated proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes overnight (Bio-Rad, 1620177) and blocked with 5% skimmed milk dissolved in 1X TBS with 0.1% Tween-20. The membranes were incubated with the appropriate primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C with gentle rocking (FinePCR, CR3000). Subsequently, the samples were incubated for 1 h with a secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology). After incubation, the blot was processed using the ECL Plus western blotting detection solution from Thermo Scientific (34577, 1:1). Visualization of the blot was achieved on an X-ray film using an image processor, and analysis was conducted using FiJi (ImageJ software 3.0). The density of the bands was quantified by normalizing to β-actin.

Cell viability and proliferation assays

Cell viability and proliferation were assessed using an EZ-Cytox cell viability assay kit (LPS SOLUTION, Daejeon, South Korea, CYT3000), which employs water-soluble tetrazolium salts (WSTs), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. To evaluate cell viability, 2 × 104 cells were seeded into 96-well clear flat-bottom plates (Corning, 3595) and incubated overnight. After overnight culture, the cells were transfected with ATE1-targeting siRNAs or treated with tannic acid at the indicated concentrations for 72 h, as described in the transfection section. Subsequently, 10% WST solution was added to each well, and the plate was incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The conversion of WSTs to chromogenic formazan by mitochondrial dehydrogenases is a marker of cell viability. The percentage of viable cells was calculated by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm compared to the control values.

To evaluate cell proliferation, 24 h post-infection with the respective lentivirus particles (shATE1#69 and #71 or shC), 850–1000 cells were placed in 96-well plates containing a complete growth medium. The time of cell seeding was designated as day 0. The cells were then incubated at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator for a specified duration (0–7 days). The WST solution (10% WST) was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Metabolic activity was assessed by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer. Wells containing medium with WST, but no cells, served as blanks. Each condition was tested in three separate experiments, with a minimum of four replicates.

Colony formation assay

In the clonogenic assay, cells in the exponential growth phase were collected and replated into 6-well plates, with each well containing 3.5 × 104 cells. Lentiviral particle-mediated knockdown of ATE1 was performed by infecting overnight cultured cells with lentivirus particles (shATE1#69, #71, or shC) for 24 h. For siRNA-mediated knockdown of ATE1, cells cultured overnight were transfected with 5 nM siRNA (siATE1#1 or siC) using Lipofectamine 2000. Every three days, the cells were transfected with 5 nM siRNA. The culture medium was changed every 2–3 days, and colonies were allowed to develop for a period–4–14 days at 37 °C. The colonies were then fixed using 6.0% glutaraldehyde and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 30 min at room temperature. Following a 30-min incubation at room temperature, the stained cells were rinsed five times with PBS.

Tumor xenograft model

Female BALB/c nude mice were obtained from DBL (Daejeon, Republic of Korea). The Ethics Committee of the Korea Research Institute of Bioscience and Biotechnology approved all mouse-related experiments (Approval No. KRIBB-AEC-14124; Daejeon, Korea). Under temperature-controlled, specific pathogen-free conditions, mice had sufficient access to water and standard mouse food. In the MDA-MB-231 tumor xenograft model, BALB/c mice were randomly assigned to the specified groups, with six mice per group: shC, shATE1#69, shATE1#69 + Wt-ATE1, and shATE1#69 + Mut-ATE1. The cells were mixed at a 1:1 ratio with PBS and Matrigel (Corning, 354234). A subcutaneous injection of 100 µl Matrigel containing 1 x 106 cells was administered to the female BALB/c mice. Every three days, body weight and tumor dimensions were measured using an electronic caliper. Tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: volume (mm3) = length × width2 × 0.52.

Scratch wound healing assay

In the wound-healing migration assay, monolayers of MDA-MB-231 cells, both control (shC) and ATE1-depleted (shATE1#69 and #71), were grown to 90–95% confluence in a six-well plate. On the day of the scratch test, the cells were exposed to 10 µg/ml Mitomycin-C for 2 h before creating a scratch. A sterile 200 µl pipette tip was used to make a straight, unidirectional scratch on the monolayer. The cells were then gently washed with DPBS to remove debris and replenished with the RPMI medium containing 10% FBS. Immediately following the scratch, phase-contrast images of the wound area were obtained at specific intervals (0, 6, 12, and 24 h) using an Evos M5000 inverted microscope. The wound closure rate was determined by measuring the distance between migrating cell borders at these time points in three separate wounds across two independent experiments.

Invasion assay

Invasion assays were performed using Corning BioCoat Matrigel Invasion Chambers (354480, Corning). MDA-MB-231 cells, transfected with either control (shC) or ATE1 knockdown lentiviral particles, were serum-starved overnight. On the day of seeding, the invasion chambers with inserts were equilibrated by adding 500 µl of bicarbonate-based culture medium to both the inserts and the wells. The chambers were then rehydrated for 2 h at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. A total of 5 × 10⁴ cells per well, suspended in 350 µl of starvation medium, was seeded into each insert, bringing the final volume to 500 µl. Medium containing 10% FBS (750 µl) was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After a 24-hour incubation at 37 °C, the medium in the inserts was aspirated, and the membranes were gently cleaned with cotton swabs and washed twice with 1× PBS. Invading cells on the underside of the membranes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, washed twice with 1× PBS, and the inserts were cut out and mounted membrane side up on microscopy slides using 8.0 µl of mounting medium (17985-10, Electron Microscopy Sciences). Images were captured using an EVOS M5000 microscope, and invading cells were counted using Fiji (ImageJ software 3.0). Three technical replicates were performed for each experimental group.

Immunofluorescence staining

For immunofluorescence microscopy, 2 × 104 shC- and ATE1-depleted cells were cultured on coverslips treated with 0.01% poly-L-lysine. After 48 h of viral transduction, BrdU (10 µM) was introduced directly into the culture medium. The cells were incubated for 30 min in a CO2 incubator at 37 °C. Subsequently, the coverslips were washed once with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (v/v), and permeabilized with ice-cold 100% methanol (v/v) for 2.5 min. The cells were neutralized for 2 min with 0.07 N NaOH. After neutralization, the cells were treated with 50 µl of anti-BrdU FITC (BD Biosciences, Cat# 347583) for 30 min at room temperature. Finally, the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (1 µmol/l) for 10 min, and the coverslips were mounted on glass slides using 8.0 µl of mounting medium (17985-10, Electron Microscopy Sciences). The samples were examined by immunofluorescence microscopy (Zeiss) and analyzed using the Zeiss ZEN 2009 software.

To identify proliferating cells with Ki-67, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (v/v) 48 h post-viral transduction and permeabilized using ice-cold 100% methanol (v/v) for 2.5 min. Following a wash with staining buffer (PBS containing 1% FBS and 0.09% NaN3), the cells were exposed to 10 µM FITC-conjugated anti-Ki-67 antibody (BD Biosciences Cat# 556026) for 30 min in the dark. Subsequently, the DNA was counterstained with DAPI, and coverslips were placed on glass slides for 16–18 h. To determine the percentage of BrdU+/Ki-67 + cells per field, the total number of BrdU/Ki-67 positive nuclei and DAPI-positive nuclei was counted using FiJi software (ImageJ software 3.0).

Cell-cycle and apoptosis analysis

Cell cycle progression was analyzed using the bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) incorporation assay and quantified using CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter, Inc.) flow cytometry (FCM). Briefly, on the third day following virus transduction, MDA-MB-157 and T-47D cells were exposed to 10 µM BrdU for 1 h. After BrdU labeling, cells were harvested and passed through a 50-µm filter to obtain single cells. Collected cells were fixed using ice-cold 100% (v/v) methanol. The fixed cells were then stained with anti-BrdU-FITC antibody (BD Biosciences, 347583) and 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD, Sigma, A9400). De novo synthesized DNA content was measured using CytoFLEX and analyzed using the CytoExpert software. Cell cycle analysis was performed by gating the debris and dead cells.

To synchronize cells at the G1/S boundary, T-47D and MDA-MB-157 cells were cultured overnight until they reached 40% confluence. The cells were then exposed to a specific amount of viral particles and simultaneously treated with thymidine (2 mM) for 16 h. Following this period, the cells were rinsed three times with DPBS and incubated in fresh growth medium for an additional 8 h. Subsequently, a second thymidine treatment was administered for 16 h. Once the double thymidine block was completed, the cells were released from the S-phase block by washing with DPBS and replacing it with a fresh growth medium. The cells were subsequently incubated for a period of 8–17 h to be harvested during the G1 phase and examined by western blotting [52].

The apoptotic rate of the cells was measured using an Annexin V-FITC staining kit (BD Biosciences Cat# 556547). The cells were harvested on day 3 of virus transduction and passed through a 50-µm filter to obtain single cells. Cells that were collected were resuspended in 1X Annexin V binding buffer and then stained with FITC annexin V and 0.5 mg/ml propidium iodide (PI) for 15 min at room temperature, and kept in the dark. The apoptotic rate was analyzed using CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter, Inc.). using the CytoExpert software (Beckman Coulter, Inc., version 2.3).

N-terminome enrichment and proteome analysis

N-terminal proteomic analysis consists of four significant experimental steps: Enrichment of N-terminal peptides from protein extracts, followed by subfractionation using high-pH reversed-phase liquid chromatography (RPLC), analysis of the fractionated peptides using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS), and deduction of peptide sequence information from the mass spectral data.

According to experimental setups, whole proteins or the R-catcher purified proteins were resuspended in lysis buffer containing 0.2 M EEPS (pH 8.0), 6 M guanidine, 10 mM TCEP (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 40 mM 2-chloroacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich). N-terminal peptides were enriched from 1 mg of protein lysate using the iNrich method described in our previous paper [53]. For whole-proteome analysis, we used 100 µg of protein lysate. The peptides were reconstituted in 10 mM ammonium formate (pH 10), injected into an XBridge Peptide BEH C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 3.5 μm, 130 Å; Waters) on an Agilent 1290 ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography system (UHPLC), and separated at a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min. The solvent setup consisted of 10 mM ammonium formate at pH 10 (solvent A) and 10 mM ammonium formate with 90% (v/v) acetonitrile (ACN) at pH 10 (solvent B). The gradient started from 0% solvent B and was increased to 5% B in 10 min, to 40% B in 38.5 min, to 70% B in 14 min, held at 70% B for 10 min, and then decreased to 5% B in 10 min. Fractions were collected every 1.6 min from 10 to 77.2 min. The fractions were concatenated into 12 fractions by combining every 12th fraction and dried in a vacuum concentrator.

One microgram of the subfractionated N-terminal peptides and 100 micrograms of subfractionated whole-protein peptides was injected injected into an Ultimate 3000 RSLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an EASY-spray nano-electrospray ion source (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Trapping and desalting were performed using 0.1% formic acid (FA) (flow rate 5 µl/min, 5 min) on a pre-column (Acclaim PepMap 100, 2 cm × 75 μm ID nanoViper column, 5 μm C18 beads). The peptides were separated on a 50 cm C18 analytical column (PepMap RSLC, 50 cm × 75 μm i.d. EASY-spray column, 2 μm beads) during a biphasic ACN gradient with a flow rate of 300 nl/min. Solvent A (0.1% FA in water) and solvent B (0.1% FA in 80% ACN) were used with the following gradient composition: 2% B for 20 min, 2–40% B for 110 min, 40–90% B for 17 min, 90% B for 30 min, 90–2% B for 7 min, 2% B for 5 min, and 2% B for 10 min. The mass spectrometer was operated in a data-dependent mode to automatically switch between full-scan MS and MS/MS acquisition. The MS spectra (m/z 400–1800) were acquired at a resolution of 60,000. The 12 most intense peptides were sequentially isolated and fragmented at a normalized collision energy of 27%. Fragments were detected in Orbitrap at a resolution of 15,000. Dynamic exclusion was utilized with an exclusion time of 25 s and ‘exclude isotopes’ enabled. A lock-mass internal calibration (m/z 445.12003) was performed.

Raw spectral files were processed with FragPipe (v20.0) and searched with MSFragger (v3.8) [54] against a database of UniProt human reference proteome (release 2023_01) with the additional common contaminant database. Identification was filtered using a 1% false discovery rate (FDR). For modifications, carbamidomethyl (+ 56.021464 Da) on Cys and D3-acetyl (+ 45.029395 Da) on Lys were set as fixed, oxidation (+ 15.994915 Da) on Met, D3-acetyl, acetyl (+ 42.010565 Da), pyroglutamation of Glu (−18.010565 Da), of Gln and Cys (−17.026549 Da), D3-acetyl-arginylation (+ 201.1305 Da) at N-terminal Asp and Glu, and D3-acetyl-arginylation-deamidation (+ 202.1145 Da) at N-terminal Asn and Gln were set as variable.

Bioinformatic analysis of proteomics data

All statistical analyses were performed using a custom script implemented in R (version 4.5.0). For the N-terminomics analysis of HMEC and T-47D cells, we focused on canonical N-terminal peptides originating from established translational initiation sites, specifically, residues at positions 1 or 2 of annotated protein sequences, to best capture proteomic alterations induced by ATE1 knockdown. Through this, we aimed to measure relative protein expression on intact proteins across HMEC and T-47D. For R-catcher-enriched proteins, to accurately identify interacting partner proteins of the Nt-arginylated proteins while minimizing background noise, we applied the SAINT algorithm to spectral count data obtained from RA and AR dipeptide experiments [55]. The spectral count of RA dipeptide experiment was used as negative control. FDR cut-off was set at 0.05.

Over-representation analysis (ORA) using MSigDB hallmark gene sets (v2024.1.Hs) and GOBP collection (ver. 2025-Feb22) was performed by the R package clusterProfiler (v4.16.0) [56]. Protein-protein physical interaction data was acquired using STRING database (v12.0) and combined score cut-off was set as > 400. The network graph object generated by R package OmnipathR (v3.4.0) [57] using function finds all paths with the argument of maxlen; 3 and illustrates the network with CytoScape (v3.9.1) with the Omics Visualizer plugin.

Survival analysis

Expression data for breast cancer samples from 1075 patients were sourced from The Human Protein Atlas [58] (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). The dataset comprises clinical details and gene expression levels measured as Fragments Per Kilobase of transcripts per million mapped reads (FPKM), sourced from The Cancer Genome Atlas Breast Invasive Carcinoma (TCGA-BRCA) [59]. Using the R package, hazard ratios were calculated using the Cox proportional hazards regression model, while overall survival (OS) curves were derived using the Kaplan-Meier method.

A sample with ATE1 gene expression higher than the upper outlier boundary (FPKM ≥ 13.45) was set as an ATE1-over-expressed sample and a sample with ATE1 gene expression below the first quartile (FPKM < 4.7) was designated as an ATE1-low-expressed sample.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 6 and Excel. For data that followed a normal distribution, an unpaired Student’s t-test was employed to compare two groups, while one-way and two-way ANOVA were used for comparisons involving multiple groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant in all cases. Statistical differences were denoted as follows: p > 0.05 = n.s., *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. Each experiment is represented as the mean ± standard deviation from at least three independent experiments, yielding consistent results.

Results

High expression of ATE1 is associated with poor prognosis in early-stage breast cancer

The reported biological roles of ATE1 in the maintenance of protein quality control [4, 8], response to cellular stresses [60–62], inhibition of apoptosis [14], regulation of G proteins [13], angiogenesis [9], cell proliferation [16] and cell migration [63], are favorable for cancer formation and progression. However, recent findings indicate that ATE1 plays a dual role in cancer because it can be both oncogenic and tumor-suppressing, depending on the type of cancer. For instance, ATE1 acts as a tumor suppressor in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [18] and prostate cancer [19], whereas its function is oncogenic in melanoma [20], indicating that cancer-specific arginylome patterns likely determine the role of ATE1 as well as its expression, and activity. In this study, we investigated the oncogenic role of ATE1 in breast cancer (Fig 1).

Since ATE1’s role in breast cancer remains poorly understood, we examined the effect of ATE1 expression on breast cancer patient survival using TCGA-BRCA data from The Human Protein Atlas. Previous studies have shown that liver cancer patients expressing lower levels of ATE1 have a poor prognosis for overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival [18]. In contrast, breast cancer patients with low ATE1 levels (n = 541) had a better prognosis for OS in breast cancer (Fig. 1b). These results indicate that the role of ATE1 in cancer may differ among cancer types, similar to that of human N-terminal acetyltransferases (NATs), which can act as both oncogenes and tumor suppressors in human cancers [21]. As a result of HR analysis, breast cancer samples with tumor stage level iii or higher were significantly associated with poor prognosis regardless of ATE1 expression (piii<4.57E-4, piv<1.70E-9, see details in Supplementary Table S1). Therefore, we repeated the survival analysis using only samples below tumor stage iii (n = 800) to examine the potential impact of ATE1 on the survival of patients with breast cancer. In early-stage breast cancer patients, ATE1-overexpressed samples (n = 18, average FPKM of 49.67) were significantly associated with poorer OS than other samples (n = 782, average FPKM of 6.47, p < 0.018; Fig. 1c, left), and ATE1-low-expressed samples (n = 180, average FPKM of 3.48, p < 0.026; Fig. 1c, right). Groups divided by mean (7.4 FPKM), median (6.4 FPKM), and quantiles (Q1 = 4.7, Q3 = 8.2 FPKM) did not show a significant difference in OS. These results suggest that high ATE1 expression plays an oncogenic role in early-stage breast cancer.

To determine whether ATE1 expression serves as a differential marker between malignant and non-malignant mammary cells, we conducted immunoblot analysis across multiple breast cancer subtypes, including four luminal (MCF7, SK-BR-3, T-47D, and BT-474) and five basal (MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-157, BT-549, HCC1143, and Hs 578T) breast cancer cell lines, with human primary mammary epithelial cells (HMEC) serving as a non-transformed control. As depicted in Fig. 1d, ATE1 protein expression was significantly upregulated in the majority of breast cancer cell lines compared to HMEC controls, with T-47D, MCF7, and HCC1143 cells exhibiting the most pronounced ATE1 overexpression. These findings suggest that ATE1 overexpression may represent a critical dependency in breast carcinogenesis.

ATE1 exhibits diverse oncogenic functions in breast cancer

Several types of constitutive stresses related to cancer cells include mitotic, DNA damage, proteotoxic, ER, metabolic, oxidative, and logistic stresses [64]. In our previous study, ATE1 promoted HeLa cell survival in response to various cancer-related cellular stresses [4], hinting that ATE1 may play a positive role in human cancer. To determine whether this is the case in breast cancer, we treated T-47D, a luminal-type human breast cancer cell line with a variety of cancer-related stressors, including bortezomib (proteasome inhibitor), thapsigargin (ER stress inducer), H2O2 (oxidative stress inducer), 5-fluorouracil (DNA damage inducer), bafilomycin A1 (an autophagy inhibitor), and 2-deoxy glucose (a glycolysis inhibitor), and assessed the effect of ATE1 knockdown on cell viability. The results demonstrated that ATE1 depletion significantly attenuated cell viability following exposure to proteotoxic, endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and oxidative stress conditions (Fig. 2a), whereas ATE1 silencing exhibited minimal impact on cellular viability in response to DNA damage, autophagy inhibition, or glycolytic inhibition (Supplementary Fig. S1a). These results diverged from those previously observed in HeLa cells, wherein ATE1 was essential for cellular survival following autophagy inhibition and proteasome inhibition, whereas ATE1 expression demonstrated negligible effects in response to oxidative stress. This discrepancy suggests that the molecular mechanisms through which ATE1 promotes cancer cell survival may exhibit both stress- and cell-type-specific dependencies.

Next, we investigated the impact of ATE1 depletion or inhibition on cellular viability across diverse breast cancer cell lines. To inhibit ATE1, we either silenced its expression using three distinct ATE1-targeting siRNAs (siATE1#1, siATE1#2, and siATE1#3) (Supplementary Fig. S1b) or treated cells with tannic acid, a small molecule known to reduce ATE1 activity [65] (Supplementary Fig. S1c). As shown in Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. S1c, both ATE1 knockdown and tannic acid treatment for 72 h significantly diminished the viability of all examined breast cancer cell lines, regardless of their molecular subtypes. These findings suggest that breast cancer cells may generally depend on ATE1 for survival.

One hallmark of malignant transformation is the capacity to form anchorage-independent colonies. Therefore, we conducted clonogenic assays to evaluate ATE1’s role in the proliferative capacity of individual breast cancer cells. To investigate this phenomenon, we suppressed ATE1 expression utilizing two distinct lentiviral-based short hairpin RNAs (shATE1 #69 and #71) (Supplementary Fig. S1d). Crystal violet visualization of breast cancer cells cultured for 7–14 days post-lentiviral transduction with shATE1 constructs or transfection with ATE1 siRNA demonstrated that clonogenic potential was significantly attenuated following ATE1 depletion across all breast cancer cell lines examined (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. S1e). Conversely, ATE1 silencing exhibited negligible effects on the colony formation efficiency of HMECs (Fig. 2c, right panel). These findings indicate that ATE1 is essential for clonogenic growth in breast cancer cells, with minimal impact on non-tumorigenic HMECs, suggesting a tumor-selective role in supporting anchorage-independent proliferation.

As in vitro clonogenic assays do not fully recapitulate tumor microenvironmental conditions, we employed xenograft models to assess ATE1’s function in breast cancer tumorigenesis in vivo. For this purpose, we established ATE1-depleted cell lines using shATE1#69 (Fig. 2d, right panel). While body mass measurements of tumor-bearing nude mice remained comparable across experimental conditions (Supplementary Fig. S1f), tumor volume (Fig. 2d, left panel) and tumor mass (Fig. 2e) were significantly reduced in the shATE1#69 group compared to the shControl group. These in vivo findings demonstrate that ATE1 depletion significantly impairs tumor growth in breast cancer xenograft models, highlighting its important role in promoting tumorigenesis.

Cancer cell migration and invasion are key hallmarks of metastatic progression, a complex cascade initiating with localized cancer cell motility, invasion of proximal tissues, and subsequent dissemination to distant anatomical sites via hematogenous and lymphatic circulation. To investigate the potential role of ATE1 in breast cancer cell migration, we conducted in vitro scratch wound-healing assays utilizing MDA-MB-231 cells transduced with either control lentiviral vector (shC) or ATE1-targeting shRNAs (#69 and #71). Our findings demonstrated that ATE1 depletion (Fig. 2g, right panel) significantly attenuated the migratory capacity of mitomycin C-treated MDA-MB-231 cells in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 2f). Notably, control cells exhibited near-complete wound resolution at 24 h post-scratching, whereas ATE1-depleted cells displayed approximately 50% wound closure at the equivalent time point (Fig. 2f, g), indicating ATE1’s potential role in regulating breast cancer cell migration. To further investigate ATE1’s involvement in breast cancer cell invasion, we examined its effect on single-cell invasive capacity through the extracellular matrix (ECM) using transwell invasion assays. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S1g and h, MDA-MB-231 cells with ATE1 knockdown exhibited a significant reduction in their ability to invade the ECM. To validate these findings, we analyzed the expression of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers by western blotting. ATE1 knockdown led to increased expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin, while levels of the mesenchymal marker vimentin remained unchanged or were only minimally affected (Supplementary Fig. S1i). This differential response suggests that ATE1 may influence breast cancer cell invasion primarily by modulating epithelial characteristics rather than by broadly altering the EMT program.

Taken together, these results suggest that ATE1 contributes to breast cancer tumorigenesis and progression by promoting cell survival, sustaining proliferation, and enhancing migration and invasion, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target in breast cancer.

ATE1 promotes breast cancer cell proliferation through its arginyltransferase activity

As a first test to determine the impact of ATE1 expression on breast cancer cell proliferation, we used 5-bromo-2’-deoxyuridine (BrdU) immunofluorescence to quantify newly synthesized DNA as an indicator of cell proliferation. As expected, the number of T-47D and MDA-MB-157 cells positive for BrdU was significantly diminished in response to ATE1 silencing, whereas no such effect was observed in HMEC. These results indicate that ATE1 silencing selectively impairs proliferation in breast cancer cells but not in HMECs, suggesting that ATE1 promotes proliferation in a tumor-specific context (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. S2a). To further verify these results, we performed Ki-67 staining of the breast cancer cells. Ki-67 is produced only in actively dividing cells, making it an ideal marker of cell proliferation. Similar results to those in Fig. 3a were observed in that shATE1 RNAs #69 and #71 significantly reduced the number of T-47D and MDA-MB-157 cells positive for Ki-67 staining (Supplementary Fig. S2b, c). These results prompted us to investigate the role of ATE1 in breast cancer cell proliferation by performing proliferation assays using two basal (MDA-MB-157 and Hs 578T) and two luminal (MCF7 and T-47D) breast cancer cell lines. Consistent with our previous results, ATE1 silencing (Supplementary Fig. S2d) completely abolished breast cancer cell proliferation, whereas lentiviral shC displayed a growth spurt over eight days (Fig. 3b).

We then investigated whether the ability of ATE1 to enhance breast cancer cell proliferation is dependent on its arginyltransferase activity. To achieve this, MDA-MB-231 cells, in which ATE1 was initially silenced (Supplementary Fig. S2e, lane 2) and subsequently restored by ectopic expression of either wild-type (Wt) or enzymatically inactive mutant (Mut) ATE1 [62] (Supplementary Fig. S2e, lanes 3 and 4) was introduced into the mammary fat pad. The body weights of xenografted nude mice were comparable among the different conditions (Supplementary Fig. S2f). As expected, tumor formation was significantly inhibited by shATE1#69, as indicated by a considerable reduction in tumor volume and weight upon silencing ATE1. Notably, ectopic expression of Wt-hATE1-1, but not the mutant, effectively rescued tumor volume (Fig. 3c) and weight (Fig. 3d), indicating that the arginyltransferase activity of ATE1 is likely responsible for its ability to promote tumorigenesis in breast cancer xenografts.

The Nt-arginylation interactome identifies ATE1 as a modulator of cell cycle progression and proliferation in breast cancer

To investigate ATE1’s role in breast cancer, we conducted N-terminal proteome analyses in HMEC and T-47D cells following ATE1 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Quantitative profiling of the canonical N-terminal peptides revealed substantial changes upon knockdown. In HMEC, N-terminal peptide levels were decreased in 523 proteins and increased in 529 proteins (|fold change| >1.5). In contrast, T-47D cells exhibited reductions in N-terminal peptides for 697 proteins and increases for 187 proteins (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Pathway analysis indicated that ATE1 depletion in T-47D cells led to significant suppression of proliferation-related signaling, including MYC targets and E2F-regulated genes (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Network analysis identified significant perturbation of the MAPK-MYC-CDK6 signaling axis, implicating ATE1 as a critical modulator of cell cycle progression (Supplementary Fig. 3d).

To further elucidate the mechanism, R-catcher pulldown was used to enrich N-terminally arginylated proteins and their interacting partners [66], followed by N-terminomics and whole-proteome profiling (Fig. 4a). Control samples were treated with an RA dipeptide competitor, which blocks R-catcher binding to arginylated proteins; proteins captured under these conditions are considered non-specific background. After background subtraction, we identified 6 enriched candidates in HMEC and 25 in T-47D, with broader protein interactomes of 541 and 1,967 enriched proteins, respectively (Fig. 4b, c). Given the limited number of confidently enriched Nt-arginylated candidates, we focused on functional analysis of the associated interactome.

Over-representation analysis (ORA) of the T-47D interactome revealed significant enrichment of hallmark gene sets related to cell cycle regulation, including MYC targets v1 (q = 4.28e-51), E2F targets, and the G2/M checkpoint. In contrast, the HMEC interactome was enriched for MYC targets v1 (q = 5.10e-23), oxidative phosphorylation, and reactive oxygen species pathways. Gene Ontology analysis further confirmed “cell cycle” (GO:0007049) as the most enriched biological process in T-47D cells (Supplementary Fig. 3e).

Among the proteins enriched in T-47D R-catcher samples, 130 mapped to the MYC targets v1 gene set. STRING-based network analysis showed that 62 of these proteins (48%) directly interacted with enriched candidate proteins, while 114 (88%) were connected via first-degree interactions (Fig. 4e). In contrast, these proportions were substantially lower in HMECs, where only 6 out of 58 MYC targets (10%) showed direct interactions, and 30 (51%) were linked through broader interaction networks (Supplementary Fig. 3f). Notably, CDK2, MCM7, and HDAC2, key components of the KEGG cell cycle pathway (hsa04110), were identified as direct binding partners of Nt-arginylated proteins captured in the T-47D R-catcher sample (Fig. 4e). MAPK3, an upstream regulator in the MAPK signaling cascade, was also detected in the same sample and found to interact with Nt-arginylated proteins (STRING physical interaction score > 0.4) (Additional file 1, Table S1). These findings support a potential signaling pathway from ATE1 through MAPK3-mediated stabilization of MYC and subsequent activation of CDK6, thereby promoting cell cycle progression.

Together, these results reveal that ATE1 plays a critical role in regulating cell cycle and proliferative signaling in breast cancer cells, particularly in T-47D. ATE1 knockdown altered global protein expression profiles and disrupted key regulatory pathways, including the MAPK-MYC-CDK6 axis. R-catcher-based proteomics further uncovered a T-47D-specific protein interaction network enriched in MYC- and E2F-associated functions, with a large subset of MYC target proteins linked to potential Nt-arginylated proteins. These findings suggest that ATE1 may influence breast cancer cell proliferation not only through direct arginylation but also by reshaping associated signaling networks critical to cell cycle control.

ATE1 controls cell cycle progression and apoptosis in breast cancer

Cell proliferation, tightly governed by cell cycle regulation and apoptosis, is frequently dysregulated in cancer [67]. Our proteomic data suggest that ATE1 plays a key role in promoting breast cancer cell cycle progression, as indicated by Nt-arginylation–based affinity profiling (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 3a). We assessed DNA synthesis by comparing BrdU incorporation between cells with varying ATE1 expression levels using flow cytometry (FCM). Flow cytometric analysis of BrdU incorporation revealed that ATE1 silencing with two distinct lentiviral shRNAs (#69 and #71) increased the proportion of cells in G1 phase while reducing S-phase entry in both T-47D and MDA-MB-157 cells, consistent with G1 cell cycle arrest (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. S4a). To further evaluate the role of ATE1 in S-phase entry, we synchronized T-47D and MDA-MB-157 cells at the G1/S boundary using a double thymidine block and assessed their ability to re-enter the cell cycle upon release. Progression through the G1 phase and transition into S phase is regulated by key cell cycle proteins, including Cyclin D-Cdk4/6 and Cyclin E-Cdk2 complexes, which sequentially phosphorylate and inactivate the retinoblastoma protein (Rb), leading to the release of E2F transcription factors and the initiation of S-phase entry [68]. As illustrated in Fig. 5b, key cell cycle genes were significantly downregulated following ATE1 knockdown in T-47D and MDA-MB-157 cells, as evidenced by reduced expression levels after thymidine release. These effects were not observed in HMECs, suggesting a cancer-specific role for ATE1 in regulating the breast cancer cell cycle (Supplementary Fig. S4b). To further investigate the consequences of ATE1-induced cell cycle arrest, we assessed apoptosis in the presence of shATE1 RNAs (#69 and #71) using Annexin V-FITC staining followed by flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 5c, the proportion of Annexin V-positive cells increased significantly in ATE1-depleted T-47D and MDA-MB-157 cells, indicating that ATE1 silencing induces both early and late apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. S4c). PARP-1 cleavage mediated by caspase-3 is often associated with apoptosis [69]. As illustrated in Fig. 5d, ATE1 inhibition using shATE1 RNAs #69 and #71 led to the cleavage of PARP and caspase-3 in T-47D and MDA-MB-157 cells (Fig. 5d), but not in HMEC (Supplementary Fig. S4d). Apicidin, a known inducer of apoptosis, triggered PARP and caspase-3 cleavage in all three cell lines (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Fig. S4d). Collectively, these results suggested that ATE1 contributes to breast cancer cell survival by facilitating cell cycle progression and suppressing apoptosis.

ATE1 regulates MYC phosphorylation in a breast cancer subtype-specific manner

Proteomic analysis of T-47D breast cancer cells revealed that several N-terminal arginylated proteins physically interact with known MYC target proteins. Given MYC’s central role in regulating cell proliferation and apoptosis, this finding suggests a potential link between ATE1-mediated arginylation and MYC-driven oncogenic signaling. These interactions provide a mechanistic link for our functional data, showing that ATE1 promotes breast cancer cell survival by facilitating cell cycle progression and suppressing apoptosis. To confirm these findings, we examined the effect of ATE1 silencing on MYC expression in G1/S-synchronized T-47D and MDA-MB-157 cells. Interestingly, ATE1 knockdown led to a significant reduction in MYC protein levels in T-47D cells, whereas MYC expression remained unchanged in MDA-MB-157 cells (Fig. 6a). Consistent with this MYC downregulation, we observed increased expression of the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors p27, p21, and p16, well-known transcriptional targets repressed by MYC, in T-47D cells (Fig. 6b).

To investigate the mechanism by which ATE1 regulates MYC expression, we assessed MYC mRNA levels in T-47D cells by RT-qPCR. ATE1 knockdown did not affect MYC transcript levels, indicating that ATE1 controls MYC expression post-transcriptionally (Supplementary Fig. S5b). MYC protein stability is known to be regulated by the MAPK and AKT signaling pathways, both of which are frequently dysregulated in cancer [35, 70]. Notably, ATE1 silencing significantly inhibited the MAPK pathway in T-47D cells, as evidenced by reduced phosphorylation of ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204), a key upstream regulator of MYC stabilization via phosphorylation at Ser62. Accordingly, phosphorylation of MYC at Ser62 was markedly decreased in ATE1-depleted T-47D cells, while no such effect was observed in MDA-MB-157 cells (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Fig. S5c). This reduction in phosphorylation was further supported by cycloheximide chase (CHX) assays, which revealed a significant decrease in MYC half-life upon ATE1 knockdown in T-47D cells (Fig. 6c, d). In contrast, ATE1 knockdown had no inhibitory effect on AKT phosphorylation levels in either cell line, indicating that ATE1-mediated MYC stabilization is MAPK-specific and independent of the AKT pathway (Fig. 6a).

To assess the specificity of these effects, we examined ATE1 knockdown in HMECs and found minimal changes in the expression of MAPK-MYC axis-related proteins (Supplementary Fig. S5a, S5c), further supporting a breast cancer subtype–specific function for ATE1. These findings demonstrate that ATE1 promotes MYC stability in T-47D cells through ERK1/2-mediated phosphorylation, thereby facilitating cell cycle progression and survival via a MAPK-MYC axis that appears to function in a lineage-specific manner.

While ATE1 exhibits conserved roles in regulating the cell cycle across different breast cancer subtypes, the molecular mechanisms involved are context-dependent. These results align with our proteomic observations (Fig. 4d) and further demonstrate that ATE1 differentially regulates the MAPK-MYC axis in malignant versus non-transformed breast epithelial cells. Collectively, these findings highlight the relevance of considering lineage-specific molecular contexts when investigating oncogenic signaling, as mechanistic heterogeneity may influence the development of targeted therapies.

Discussion

This study characterizes the role of arginyl-tRNA-protein transferase (ATE1) in breast cancer and examines its impact on key signaling pathways associated with tumor progression. Through integrative analysis of gene expression, proteomic, and functional data, we demonstrate that ATE1 is selectively upregulated in breast cancer cells and that its expression is significantly associated with poor prognosis in early-stage breast cancer patients.

Our findings suggest that ATE1 promotes multiple hallmarks of cancer, including cell survival, proliferation, migration, and tumorigenic potential in both luminal and basal breast cancer subtypes. These effects were not observed in non-tumorigenic human mammary epithelial cells (HMECs), indicating a possible tumor-specific dependency on ATE1. Moreover, our data suggest that the tumor-promoting functions of ATE1 depend on its arginyltransferase activity, as only the wild-type enzyme and not the catalytically inactive mutant was able to rescue tumor growth in xenograft models.

Using N-terminal proteomics and R-catcher–based interactome profiling, we identified distinct Nt-arginylation-associated protein networks in T-47D breast cancer cells and HMECs. These networks were enriched for MYC and E2F target genes, as well as cell cycle regulators, particularly in luminal-type T-47D cells compared to HMECs. ATE1 knockdown disrupted several of these networks, including pathways involving MAPK, MYC, and CDK6, leading to cell cycle arrest, reduced DNA synthesis, and apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 3f).

Notably, ATE1 regulated MYC at the post-transcriptional level via ERK1/2-mediated phosphorylation at Ser62, a modification known to stabilize MYC and enhance its oncogenic activity. This regulatory effect was subtype-specific, with significant MYC destabilization occurring in T-47D but not in MDA-MB-157 cells or HMECs, indicating the context-specific role of ATE1 in breast cancer.

While our study highlights the involvement of ATE1 in modulating oncogenic signaling networks, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although we observed downstream effects on MAPK–MYC signaling, we did not directly examine whether MAPK pathway components (e.g., Ras, RAF) are arginylated by ATE1. The transient and low-affinity nature of enzyme-substrate interactions, common among post-translational modifiers, presents technical challenges for co-immunoprecipitation or mass spectrometry-based identification of ATE1 substrates. Consequently, future studies employing targeted substrate-trapping approaches or advanced proteomic techniques will be required to elucidate direct molecular links between ATE1 and components of the MAPK signaling pathway.

Second, while our TCGA-based analysis supports an association between ATE1 mRNA expression and clinical outcomes, we did not evaluate ATE1 or phosphorylated MYC protein levels in patient tissues. Access to clinical specimens limited our ability to validate these findings at the protein level or correlate them with clinical staging and molecular subtypes. Prospective studies involving tissue microarrays and immunohistochemical analyses will be critical to confirm these associations and strengthen translational relevance.

Third, although we demonstrated that tannic acid, an ATE1 inhibitor, suppresses breast cancer cell viability in vitro, we did not assess its efficacy in vivo using patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models. Such studies would be essential for evaluating the therapeutic potential of ATE1 inhibition in breast cancer.

Lastly, while our data implicate ATE1 in the modulation of MYC stability, the direct arginylated substrates responsible for this regulatory effect remain unidentified. Future studies employing site-specific arginylation antibodies or high-resolution mass spectrometry will be necessary to identify and characterize direct substrates of ATE1.

ATE1’s functional roles appear to differ across cancer types. While some reports suggest that ATE1 acts as a tumor suppressor in liver and prostate cancers [18, 19], it has been implicated in promoting tumor progression in breast cancer and melanoma [20]. This context-dependent behavior may reflect differences in substrate availability and specificity, cell-type-specific signaling pathways, or differential expression of ATE1 isoforms and components of the protein degradation machinery. Further investigation into the cell-type-specific interactome of ATE1 will be necessary to fully elucidate its dual roles in cancer biology.

Taken together, our findings position ATE1 as a context-dependent regulator of oncogenic signaling pathways in breast cancer. By modulating key networks such as MAPK–MYC–CDK6, ATE1 may promote breast cancer cell proliferation and survival (Fig. 7). However, these conclusions are based on correlative and functional analyses, and further mechanistic validation is needed to confirm direct molecular interactions.

Model illustrating ATE1’s function as a new MYC regulator in breast cancer development

In our study, we demonstrated that ATE1 initiates the non-canonical ERK/AKT/MYC phosphorylation pathway, which in turn stabilizes MYC oncoprotein. This stabilized MYC influences breast cancer tumorigenesis by promoting cell cycle progression and enhancing cell proliferation.

Conclusions

Our study identifies ATE1 as a potential modulator of breast cancer progression, with elevated expression associated with poor prognosis in early-stage patients. ATE1 enhances cancer cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and survival, with these effects appearing to depend on its enzymatic arginyltransferase activity.

While these findings suggest that ATE1 may have therapeutic potential in breast cancer subtypes with active MYC and MAPK signaling, further research is needed to establish its clinical and therapeutic relevance. Future studies should focus on identifying direct arginylation targets of ATE1, validating its role in patient samples, and evaluating the efficacy of ATE1 inhibitors in in vivo models.

Our work advances the understanding of ATE1’s role in breast cancer biology and highlights the importance of context-dependent regulatory mechanisms in cancer progression.
