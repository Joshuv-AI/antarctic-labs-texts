Systolic Blood Pressure Variability and Outcomes in Atrial Fibrillation: A Nationwide Population Study in Korea
Authors: Jaechan Park, So-Ryoung Lee, Jungmin Choi, Kyung-Yeon Lee, Hyo-Jeong Ahn, Soonil Kwon, Bongsung Kim, Kyung-Do Han, Seil Oh, Gregory Y H Lip, Eue-Keun Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationship between systolic blood pressure variability (SBP-V) and clinical outcomes in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) remains unclear.MethodsUsing the Korean National Health Insurance database, we included patients with prevalent AF who received health checkups in 2013–2014, with at least three checkups in total (n = 235,091). SBP-V was defined as the standard deviation of SBP. The outcomes, including stroke, major bleeding, and all-cause death, were assessed during follow-up.ResultsDuring the follow-up period (median, 4.9 years; interquartile range, 4.3–5.3), 5,339 strokes (0.5/100 person-years [PYs]), 3,432 major bleeding incidents (0.3/100 PYs), and 16,755 all-cause mortality (1.5/100 PYs) occurred. Compared to patients with SBP 120 to < 130 mmHg at the index health checkup, those with SBP ≥ 130 mmHg had a significantly higher risks of stroke and major bleeding. All-cause mortality was elevated at both high (≥ 150 mmHg) and low (< 120 mmHg) SBP levels. Compared to the lowest SBP-V group, the highest SBP-V group had significantly higher risks of stroke (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.211; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.125–1.304), major bleeding (aHR, 1.125; 95% CI, 1.025–1.234), and all-cause death (aHR, 1.189; 95% CI, 1.141–1.239). No significant interactions were observed between oral anticoagulation use and SBP-V for any of the clinical outcomes.ConclusionSBP and SBP-V were independently associated with higher risks of stroke, major bleeding, and all-cause death in patients with AF. These findings highlight the importance of not only achieving target blood pressure levels but also minimizing SBP variability through optimal hypertension management to improve outcomes in this population.Keywords: Blood Pressure, Blood Pressure Variability, Hypertension, Atrial Fibrillation, Stroke, Major Bleeding, Korea

INTRODUCTION

Systolic blood pressure variability (SBP-V) serves as a marker of long-term blood pressure (BP) fluctuations and has been linked to adverse cardiovascular outcomes in the general population. In the Antihypertensive and Lipid-Lowering Treatment to Prevent Heart Attack Trial, individuals in the highest quartile of SBP-V had a 30% higher risk of coronary heart disease compared to those in the lowest quartile.1 Similar associations have been reported in individuals with established cardiovascular conditions such as coronary artery disease2,3 and heart failure.4,5

In patients with atrial fibrillation (AF), the presence of hypertension significantly elevates the risk of ischemic stroke, major bleeding, and overall mortality.6 Recent guidelines globally underscore the importance of achieving optimal BP control as part of an integrated care approach to AF care.7,8,9 Despite this, the prognostic significance of SBP levels and their variability in the AF population remains incompletely understood. Although several studies have investigated the relationship between SBP-V and clinical outcomes in patients with AF,10,11,12,13,14 previous findings remain inconsistent due to a limited sample size or insufficient adjustment for confounding variables. Furthermore, previous research has rarely explored whether the prognostic implications of SBP-V differ according to oral anticoagulation (OAC) use in a long-term real-world setting—an important consideration given its potential to modify outcome risk.

To address these gaps, the present study aimed to examine the associations of SBP and SBP-V with the risks of ischemic stroke, major bleeding, and all-cause mortality in patients with AF, using data from a large-scale nationwide population-based cohort.

METHODS

Data source

We utilized data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) database for this study. It encompasses comprehensive health-related information for the entire Korean population. The database includes sociodemographic data, healthcare utilization records, diagnostic codes defined according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM), and prescription data. The NHIS–National Health Screening Cohort derives from Korea’s nationwide health checkup program conducted every two years for all NHIS enrollees.15,16 These health examinations consist of physical assessments, laboratory testing, and self-reported questionnaires on lifestyle and medical history. De-identified cohort data are publicly available through the National Health Insurance Sharing Service for researchers with Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval.

Study population

The patient selection process is summarized in Supplementary Fig. 1. From a total of 1,177,048 individuals diagnosed with AF between January 1, 2002 and December 31, 2014, we excluded those with valvular AF (n = 31,134). Among the remaining patients, individuals aged ≥ 20 years who underwent a national health screening in 2013 or 2014 after AF diagnosis were included (n = 390,661). To assess SBP-V, we further excluded individuals with fewer than three health screenings, including the index exam (n = 155,570), yielding a final study population of 235,091.

BP measurement and visit-to-visit variability

BP was measured at certified health screening centers under the supervision of trained personnel following the Joint National Committee 7 guidelines.17 Measurements were performed using mercury sphygmomanometers or validated oscillometric devices after at least five minutes of seated rest. SBP-V was calculated based on three or more measurements per patient. The primary measure of variability was the standard deviation (SD) of SBP. Additional indices included the coefficient of variation (CV; SD divided by mean) and variability independent of the mean (VIM), calculated as 100 × meanβ, where β is the regression coefficient from the log-log relationship between SD and mean SBP.18,19

Covariates

Covariates included demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, laboratory results, comorbidities, and a history of medications. Obesity was defined as a body mass index ≥ 25 kg/m2, and abdominal obesity as a waist circumference ≥ 90 cm for men or ≥ 85 cm for women.20 Lifestyle variables, including smoking, alcohol intake, and physical activity, were derived from self-reported questionnaires. Heavy drinking was defined as alcohol intake ≥ 30 g/day, and regular physical activity was defined as engaging in moderate exercise ≥ 5 times/week or vigorous activity ≥ 3 times/week for ≥ 20 minutes. Laboratory test results included fasting glucose, total cholesterol, and estimated glomerular filtration rate. Low-income status was defined as the lowest 20% of the income distribution. Comorbidities included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, heart failure, prior myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, and major bleeding (e.g., intracranial or gastrointestinal hemorrhage). Medication history included use of antiplatelet agents, OACs, and antihypertensive drugs. Full definitions of comorbidities are available in Supplementary Table 1.

Study outcome and follow-up

The clinical outcomes assessed were ischemic stroke, major bleeding, and all-cause mortality. Definitions for each outcome are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Follow-up commenced at the index health screening and continued until the first occurrence of each outcome, death, or October 31, 2018, whichever came first.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means ± SD, and comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square test. Participants were stratified into quartiles based on SBP-V values.

Incidence rates (IRs) for outcomes were calculated per 100 person-years (PYs). Hazard ratios were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. The association between SBP at the index health checkup and clinical outcomes was assessed first, followed by the association between SBP-V and outcomes. Multivariable models were adjusted as follows: model 1: unadjusted; model 2: adjusted for age and sex; model 3: adjusted for CHA2DS2-VASc score; model 4: adjusted for demographics, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors; model 5: further adjusted for use of antiplatelet agents and OACs; model 6: additionally adjusted for CHA2DS2-VASc score; and model 7: included SBP at index health screening. Adjusted cubic spline regression was performed (model 6 for SBP, model 7 for SBP-V) to evaluate the continuous association of SBP and SBP-V with outcomes.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate effect modification by OAC use. Sensitivity analyses using CV and VIM were performed to confirm the consistency of associations across different variability indices. Statistical significance for interaction was defined as P for interaction < 0.5, and a two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) was used to conduct all analyses.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the IRB of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. E-2504-041-1628), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the use of anonymized data.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics of groups according to SBP

A total of 235,091 patients were included in the final analysis (mean age 62.8 ± 12.3 years; 56.0% male). The baseline characteristics by SBP category are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. The SBP categories were: < 110 mmHg (n = 26,953; 11%), ≥ 110 and < 120 mmHg (n = 50,208; 21%), ≥ 120 and < 130 mmHg (n = 60,724; 25%), ≥ 130 and < 140 mmHg (n = 59,357; 25%), 140–<150 mmHg (n = 21,895; 9%), ≥ 150 and < 160 mmHg (n = 10,310; 4%), and ≥ 160 mmHg (n = 5,644; 2%). Patients with higher SBP were generally older, had a lower proportion of men, and more frequently had obesity, comorbid conditions, and elevated CHA2DS2-VASc scores.

Association between SBP and clinical outcomes

Over a median follow-up of 4.9 years (interquartile range: 4.3–5.3 years), 5,339 ischemic strokes (0.5 per 100 PYs), 3,432 major bleeding events (0.3 per 100 PYs), and 16,755 all-cause deaths (1.5 per 100 PYs) were documented. The crude IRs and adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) for each SBP group are shown in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 3.

Using SBP ≥ 120 and < 130 mmHg as the reference, a higher SBP was associated with increased risk of stroke (from ≥ 130 mmHg), major bleeding (from ≥ 130 mmHg), and all-cause death (from ≥ 150 mmHg). SBP < 120 mmHg was also associated with increased mortality risk. Kaplan–Meier curves and cubic spline analyses for each outcome are provided in Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3. A significant interaction between SBP and OAC use was observed for all-cause death (P for interaction < 0.001), with markedly different hazard ratios at SBP < 110 mmHg and ≥ 160 mmHg depending on OAC use (Supplementary Table 4).

Associations between SBP-V and clinical outcomes

The crude IRs, aHRs, and Kaplan–Meier curves for clinical outcomes according to SBP-V are shown in Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 4, and Supplementary Table 5. Compared with the lowest variability group (Q1), patients in Q3 and Q4 had significantly higher risks of stroke and all-cause death. For major bleeding, increased risk was observed only in Q4. Specifically, Q4 was associated with a 21% higher risk of stroke, 13% higher risk of major bleeding, and 19% higher risk of all-cause death compared to Q1.

DISCUSSION

This large nationwide cohort study demonstrated the following: 1) Compared to AF patients with SBP 120 to < 130 mmHg, higher SBP was associated with an increased risk of stroke (from SBP ≥ 130 mmHg), major bleeding (from SBP ≥ 130 mmHg), and all-cause death (from SBP ≥ 150 mmHg); and 2) AF patients with the highest SBP-V had significantly increased risks of stroke, major bleeding, and all-cause death compared to those with the lowest SBP-V, even after adjusting for the baseline SBP at the index health checkup.

The associations between SBP level and clinical outcomes in our study are consistent with findings from a previous Korean nationwide cohort study of OAC-naïve AF patients.21 That study also demonstrated an increased risk of stroke from SBP ≥ 130 mmHg, intracranial hemorrhage from SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, and a U-shaped association with all-cause death. On the other hand, a post-hoc analysis of the RE-LY trial,22 which focused on OAC-treated patients, showed a similar association for stroke and death; however, contrary to our findings, major bleeding risk was only elevated at SBP < 110 mmHg. These discrepancies highlight that the relationship between SBP and bleeding risk may vary depending on treatment status or population characteristics. Still, our findings align with the 2024 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for AF, which recommend a target SBP of 120–129 mmHg.7 Although the latest hypertension guidelines from the American College of Cardiology and the American Heart Association suggest a more intensive SBP target of < 120 mmHg when clinically feasible,23 our findings, along with other Korean nationwide data, demonstrate a paradoxical increase in all-cause mortality at SBP levels below 120 mmHg. This suggests that intensive SBP control may exacerbate vascular fragility or increase the risk of hypotension-related adverse events in patients with AF. Consequently, the safety and efficacy of an SBP target below 120 mmHg in the AF population require further validation through dedicated randomized controlled trials (RCTs) before such intensive strategies can be routinely recommended. Regarding SBP-V, our study supports the growing body of evidence linking higher BP variability to adverse outcomes. Our study represents the largest cohort to date examining SBP-V in patients with AF and strengthens the importance of managing both SBP levels and variability to improve long-term outcomes. In a post-hoc analysis of the AFFIRM trial,14 elevated SBP-V (from Q3 onward) was significantly associated with stroke, major bleeding, and all-cause mortality. The J-RHYTHM registry12 also demonstrated consistent associations. In contrast, the COOL-AF registry13 did not find a significant association between SBP-V and stroke, though bleeding and death were linked to higher variability. This inconsistency may stem from differences in population characteristics, such as OAC usage and CHA2DS2-VASc scores. Notably, the stroke incidence in our study (0.5 per 100 PYs) was markedly lower than in the COOL-AF registry (1.42) and also lower than in the AFFIRM and J-RHYTHM studies (1.02 and 0.8, respectively), which could have contributed to the variability in observed associations.

The ESC guideline for AF emphasizes the management of hypertension as a cornerstone of comorbidity and risk factor control, represented by the “C” in “AF-CARE” framework. Our study provides critical clinical implications by demonstrating that maintaining stable SBP control is as vital as achieving the target mean SBP for optimizing outcomes in AF patients. Specifically, our findings reveal that even when mean SBP remains within the controlled range, patients with high SBP-V face a significantly higher risk of adverse events compared to those with stable SBP. This aligns with recent evidence from the general population, which underscores that both high SBP-V at a single time point and temporal increases in variability are associated with worsened cardiovascular outcomes.24 Consequently, clinicians should proactively monitor visit-to-visit SBP-V as a marker of residual cardiovascular risk, even in patients who appear to have reached their BP targets. Managing this variability requires a multifaceted approach, including the preferential use of long-acting antihypertensive agents—such as calcium channel blockers or thiazide-like diuretics—which have been shown to stabilize BP fluctuations more effectively than other classes.25,26,27 Furthermore, strengthening medication adherence and promoting healthy lifestyle modifications, such as ensuring high-quality sleep and consistent physical activity, should be emphasized to further mitigate SBP-V.25,26 These strategies may provide a more comprehensive pathway to reducing the risk of stroke, major bleeding, and mortality in the AF population.

Our analysis showed that OAC use did not significantly modify the association between SBP-V and clinical outcomes. This lack of effect modification aligns with the post-hoc ENGAGE AF-TIMI 48 study.27 However, that study may have been underpowered due to its limited follow-up, as noted by the authors in the discussion section. These findings underscore the importance of achieving well-controlled BP control to minimize variability, regardless of anticoagulation status. Our data also argue against a mechanism driven solely by medication non-adherence. Evidence linking SBP-V to anticoagulation quality is inconsistent. The AFFIRM trial14 reported an inverse correlation between time in therapeutic range (TTR) and SBP-V, suggesting that suboptimal anticoagulation control might contribute to excess adverse events. In contrast, the J-RHYTHM registry12 detected no TTR gradient across SBP-V quartiles, and the ALLHAT trial1 indicated that non-adherence did not account for the prognostic impact of BP variability. Taken together, these data also suggest that hemodynamic and vascular pathophysiology, rather than adherence alone, underpins the association between SBP-V and clinical outcomes in AF patients.

A plausible mechanism for the increased risk of stroke, especially in AF patients with high SBP-V, involves left atrial (LA) remodeling. Indeed, BP variability is positively correlated to LA enlargement and LA fibrosis.28,29 Such remodeling of LA in AF patients was associated with a higher risk of stroke,30,31 as it impairs LA contraction function and induces stasis of blood flow in LA and the LA appendage.32 The association between increased SBP-V and major bleeding, including intracranial bleeding and gastrointestinal bleeding, can be explained through the framework of systemic microvascular damage.33 High SBP-V induced repetitive shear stress and increased flow pulsatility, leading to systemic endothelial dysfunction and reduced arterial elasticity.34,35 This chronic pulsatile strain can cause microvascular fragility not only in the cerebral vasculature36 but also in other high-flow, low-resistance beds, such as the gastrointestinal mucosa, thereby increasing overall bleeding susceptibility.37,38,39 In patients with AF, these vulnerable vessels are more prone to clinical bleeding events when sudden BP spikes exceed the structural threshold of the microvasculature. This risk is particularly heightened when such hemodynamic instability coincides with the pharmacological effects of OAC therapy, which weakens the hemostatic seal and exacerbates the severity of bleeding events. Consequently, our findings suggest that SBP-V serves as a critical marker of overall vascular frailty and a significant predictor of systemic major bleeding in the AF population. The associations between SBP-V and all-cause mortality suggest that BP variability may also be linked to other disease states. Previous studies have demonstrated independent relationships between SBP variability and a range of adverse outcomes, including coronary heart disease, chronic kidney disease, and dementia.24 Potential mediators—such as systemic inflammation40 and endothelial dysfunction41—have been proposed, but their roles warrant further investigation, especially in patients with AF.

Although several studies have investigated the relationship between SBP-V and clinical outcomes in patients with AF, our study provides several distinct strengths. First, it is based on the largest nationwide sample to date (n = 235,091), substantially exceeding prior studies, including the largest comparable cohort of 19,680 patients reported by Proietti et al.27 Previous investigations have often demonstrated conflicting results for individual outcomes. For instance, one study found no significant association between SBP-V and stroke, cardiovascular mortality, or all-cause mortality,27 while another study13 similarly reported no association with stroke. In contrast, the large sample size of our study allowed robust evaluation of the association between SBP-V and multiple clinical outcomes, including stroke, bleeding, and all-cause mortality, in a nationwide AF population, thereby resolving inconsistencies observed in smaller previous studies. In addition to the large sample size, our nationwide study population more accurately reflects real-world clinical practice. It might reduce potential selection bias inherent to RCTs, on which some previous studies were based.13,14 Second, very few studies have explored whether OAC exposure modifies the association between SBP-V and clinical outcomes. To our knowledge, only one study performed such an interaction analysis, reporting no significant interaction.27 However, as acknowledged by the authors, the short follow-up duration of their study (1 year) may have been insufficient to allow complete vascular remodeling related to SBP-V or for OAC-related stroke-risk modification to manifest. Our study, with a substantially more extended follow-up period, was better positioned to evaluate the potential interaction between SBP-V and OAC use over a clinically meaningful timeframe.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective observational study based on claims data, which may be subject to misclassification and cannot confirm causality. Second, although we used multiple validated SBP-V indices in sensitivity analyses (SD, CV, VIM), these indices do not capture directional trends in SBP over time, which may confound interpretation. Also, in real-world practice, managing BP within the optimal range through adherence to anti-hypertensive medication is highly associated with a reduction in SBP-V. Therefore, whether SBP variability represents an independent modifiable risk factor beyond SBP control remains uncertain. Further studies are warranted to clarify its clinical relevance in patients with AF. Third, SBP measurements may have varied across hospitals or over time. However, all participating institutions were NHIS-certified, and previous studies using the same database have demonstrated reliability.19 Fourth, while stroke and death showed relatively clear dose-response associations with increasing SBP-V, the pattern for bleeding risk was attenuated, which may reflect differences in bleeding etiology and warrants further investigation. Fifth, information on the types and duration of AF, as well as echocardiographic parameters such as left atrial volume and the E/e′ ratio, was not available in our study. Future studies incorporating imaging data are needed to clarify the potential pathophysiological mechanism linking SBP-V to clinical outcomes. Finally, our cohort was Asian, and additional data from different ethnicities and healthcare systems are warranted, given the recognized differences in AF-related clinical epidemiology and complications (e.g., stroke, bleeding)42 between Asians and non-Asians.

In patients with AF, elevated SBP-V was independently associated with increased risks of stroke, major bleeding, and all-cause mortality. These findings underscore the importance of not only achieving target BP levels but also minimizing SBP variability through optimized BP management to improve long-term clinical outcomes in this population.
