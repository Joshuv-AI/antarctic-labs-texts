Multidisciplinary therapy targeting histological heterogeneity in prostate cancer with bone marrow carcinomatosis: A case report
Authors: Naoya Nagaya, Kento Hokota, Kaho Hirose, Junro Yanai, Junki Morino, Yuki Nagashima, Akiko Nakajima, Kazuhiko Fujita, Shigeo Horie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProstate cancer with bone marrow carcinomatosis is rare and has a poor prognosis. A 68-year-old man with severe thrombocytopenia was diagnosed with prostate cancer based on MRI and bone marrow biopsy findings. Initial treatment with a GnRH antagonist and apalutamide showed limited efficacy for thrombocytopenia despite PSA response, prompting the addition of carboplatin and etoposide (CE) chemotherapy. Thrombocytopenia resolved following CE chemotherapy. After four CE cycles, ProGRP stabilized and PSA decreased significantly. Tumor control was maintained for a year with hormonal therapy. This case underscores the need for early, multidisciplinary treatment in histologically heterogeneous prostate cancer.

Introduction

Bone marrow metastasis (BMM) occurs when malignant tumors from non-hematopoietic tissues spread to bone marrow, causing hematopoietic dysfunction and life-threatening conditions.1,2 Common primary cancers with BMM include breast, prostate, neuroblastoma, lung, and gastrointestinal tumors.3 BMM typically presents with anemia and thrombocytopenia.1,4 Diagnosis uses imaging and bone marrow biopsy, with immunohistochemistry useful for identifying the primary tumor.3,5

BMM has a poor prognosis, particularly worse in patients with thrombocytopenia.7,4,6 However, aggressive treatments such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy have been shown to improve prognosis compared to no treatment.1

Here, we present a case of metastatic prostate cancer with bone marrow metastasis, highlighting the clinical course, diagnostic challenges, and treatment strategies employed in managing this complex condition.

Case presentation

A 68-year-old man presented with dyspnea. CT revealed diffuse alveolar hemorrhage, and labs showed severe thrombocytopenia (platelet count: 7.0 × 109/L) (Fig. 1). After platelet transfusion, bone marrow biopsy revealed small-cell carcinoma, confirmed by chromogranin A and synaptophysin positivity and PSA negativity (Fig. 2). Serum ProGRP (157.6 pg/mL) were elevated. Serum NSE was within the normal range at the time of diagnosis and continued to remain normal during follow-up. Although pulmonary hemorrhage was observed, there were no findings suggestive of a primary lung tumor. Additionally, the bone marrow biopsy did not provide sufficient information to determine the primary site of the small-cell carcinoma. Therefore, a panel of tumor markers was assessed, which revealed a markedly elevated serum PSA (735 ng/mL) level. Prostate MRI revealed a lesion extending from the left peripheral zone to the seminal vesicle (PI-RADS v2.1 Category 5), with multiple pelvic lymph nodes and bone metastases (Fig. 1). Despite continued platelet transfusions, thrombocytopenia persisted. A summary figure of the patient's hematological data, treatment interventions, imaging tests, and transfusion timelines during the initial treatment phase is presented to provide a clear overview of the clinical course (Fig. 3). Based on imaging findings, metastatic prostate cancer was diagnosed, and the patient received treatment with a GnRH antagonist and apalutamide. PSA decreased to 250 ng/mL by day 15 of treatment, but ProGRP increased to 176.1 pg/mL. Apalutamide was temporarily discontinued due to a grade 1 skin rash. Despite initiating treatment, thrombocytopenia did not improve. Based on the pathological findings from a bone marrow biopsy, the treatment strategy was redirected toward targeting small cell carcinoma. Although both EP (etoposide plus cisplatin) and CE (etoposide plus carboplatin) regimens were considered, CE therapy was selected in this case. While CE is also associated with myelosuppression, it is generally regarded as more manageable with supportive care and dose adjustments compared to EP, making it a more suitable option for this patient. By day 22, carboplatin (AUC 4) and reduced-dose etoposide (80 mg/m2 at 77 %) combination chemotherapy was initiated. Following CE initiation, thrombocytopenia resolved, eliminating the need for further transfusions, and prostate biopsy was performed. Histopathology revealed adenocarcinoma (Gleason score 5 + 5) with no evidence of small-cell carcinoma (Fig. 2). The biopsy was obtained shortly after the initiation of hormonal therapy. As the sampled tissue included regions without apparent treatment-related histologic alterations, Gleason scoring was considered valid and was performed based on these unaffected areas. After one cycle of CE, ProGRP decreased to 23.6 pg/mL, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) normalized, and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) transiently increased before returning to normal levels. A bone scintigraphy scan on day 38 showed a bone scan index (BSI) of 10.7 and Hs (Hot spot) of 101 (Fig. 4). PET-CT on day 70 revealed diffuse bone uptake (SUV max early phase: 4.2; delayed phase: 5.1). With tolerability confirmed, etoposide was administered at 100 % dose from the third cycle, and carboplatin was increased to AUC 5 from the fourth cycle. After four cycles of CE, PSA decreased to 0.71 ng/mL, and ProGRP remained stable at 42.6 pg/mL. Apalutamide was resumed, as biopsy findings supported its efficacy. The rate of PSA decline during the discontinuation of apalutamide was lower compared to the rate observed during apalutamide administration. Neutropenia (CTCAE (Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events) Grade 1–2) persisted, leading to CE discontinuation after four cycles. By day 343, PSA reached its nadir (0.10 ng/mL), and ProGRP remained stable at 44.1 pg/mL. On day 499, imaging showed diffuse bone uptake and reduced bone scan indices, indicating stable disease (Fig. 4). Contrast-enhanced CT at the same time showed reduced lymph node metastases. However, rising PSA levels led to a diagnosis of castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) on day 508, and apalutamide was discontinued. Docetaxel was initiated but failed to control PSA progression. Cabazitaxel was also ineffective, and worsening bone metastases were observed. On day 652, multiple liver metastases appeared. Because of stable ProGRP levels, CE therapy was not resumed. The patient succumbed to the disease on day 699.

Discussion

Prostate cancer is well-known for its histological heterogeneity, with diverse subtypes that exhibit variable responses to androgen deprivation therapy (ADT).8 This case emphasizes recognizing such heterogeneity, as small cell carcinoma's presence9 required cytotoxic chemotherapy integration. While ADT remains fundamental for prostate cancer treatment, histologically diverse cases benefit from multimodal approaches to achieve optimal outcomes.

BMM introduces additional challenges in managing advanced prostate cancer. Although the efficacy of chemotherapy or ADT in treating bone marrow metastases from prostate cancer has been reported in some cases,10,11 bone marrow metastases remain rare, and there is no established evidence-based treatment protocol for these patients. BMM often results in severe thrombocytopenia, which not only complicates the administration of systemic treatments, particularly cytotoxic chemotherapy, but also increases the risk of bleeding and infections. In this case, the patient exhibited persistent and severe thrombocytopenia requiring frequent platelet transfusions. However, the administration of a CE regimen resulted in the resolution of thrombocytopenia, indicating that effective control of the underlying malignancy is crucial to alleviating marrow suppression.

Although thrombocytopenia and other complications of BMM are associated with a poor prognosis in cancer patients,7,4,6 this case underscores that precise diagnosis through biopsy and timely initiation of appropriate treatment can extend survival, even in critical situations. The histological examination revealed the coexistence of adenocarcinoma and small cell carcinoma, guiding the choice of chemotherapy and influencing subsequent management decisions. The combined effects of CE and hormonal therapy alleviated thrombocytopenia and contributed to tumor burden reduction.

This case further supports the argument for integrating cytotoxic chemotherapy early in the treatment of select prostate cancer patients with aggressive features or histological subtypes less responsive to hormonal therapy.12,13 The successful management of thrombocytopenia in this patient highlights the importance of a multidisciplinary approach, incorporating histological diagnosis, careful treatment planning, and vigilant monitoring to overcome the challenges posed by advanced and aggressive prostate cancer.

Finally, this case reinforces the need for continued research to optimize therapeutic strategies for histologically heterogeneous prostate cancer. Tailored approaches that consider biological characteristics are essential to improve outcomes in this challenging population.

Conclusion

This case highlights challenges in managing prostate cancer with bone marrow metastasis, a rare condition lacking standardized protocols. Accurate diagnosis with timely, personalized treatment were crucial for improved outcomes.
