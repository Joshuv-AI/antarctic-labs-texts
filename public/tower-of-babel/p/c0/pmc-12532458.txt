Impact of nutrition and health interventions on undernutrition: an overview of systematic reviews
Authors: Mireya Vilar-Compte, Natalia Rovelo-Velázquez, Hoa Thi Mai Nguyen, Michelle Ashwin Mehta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nearly two decades after adopting SDG 2 to end malnutrition by 2030, significant challenges persist, including high anemia rates and stalled progress in reducing stunting and low birth weight. This paper provides an overview of the latest systematic reviews and meta-analyses on nutrition and health interventions assessing their effects on undernutrition and breastfeeding indicators. A systematic review was conducted following PRISMA guidelines, searching PubMed for relevant systematic reviews and meta-analyses published between 2018-2023. The search yielded 881 reports, with 46 systematic reviews and meta-analyses included after screening. These reviews spanned multiple regions and assessed various nutrition interventions. The findings underscore robust evidence for effective nutrition interventions but highlight that a large proportion of this evidence emerges from trials, posing significant challenges for translating this evidence into actual scale up, implementation, and sustainability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-025-02525-z.

Introduction

Nearly two decades since the adoption of Sustainable Development Goal 2 (SDG 2) and its Target 2.2 to end all forms of malnutrition by 2030, the world is facing significant challenges in achieving nutrition goals. The Sustainable Development Goal 2.2 (SDG 2.2) [1] aims to incentivize key stakeholders to prevent malnutrition throughout a person’s life. The targets focus on improving anemia among women of reproductive age (15–49 years), promoting optimal breastfeeding practices (particularly exclusive breastfeeding during the first 6 months), and reducing low birth weight, wasting, stunting, and childhood overweight/obesity. The nutrition benefits linked to these indicators can have long-lasting impacts on human capital and productivity.

Globally, approximately 571 million (29.9%) women of reproductive age and 269 million (39.8%) children aged 6 to 29 months are affected by anemia [2]. Since 2012, the decline in anemia rates has stalled among pregnant women (37% in 2012, 36.5% in 2019) and children (40.8% in 2012, 39.8% in 2019). Conversely, the prevalence of anemia among non-pregnant women has increased (28.1% in 2012, 29.6% in 2019) [2]. The global prevalence of exclusive breastfeeding has seen a significant rise over the past decade, increasing from 35.4% in 2000 to 48.6% in 2019. However, this rate varies considerably across different income groups: 51.2% in low-income, 46.7% in lower-middle-income, and 37.0% in upper-middle-income countries in 2019, highlighting the need for improvement, particularly in upper-middle-income countries [3, 4]. Despite notable progress in reducing stunting globally over the past two decades, the trend has recently plateaued. As of 2022, 148 million (22.3%) children under five years of age remained stunted [2]. Additionally, wasting affected 45 million (6.8%) children under five worldwide in 2022 [2]. In 2020, 19.8 million (14.7%) children were born with low birth weight globally, reflecting a stagnant trend since 2012 (15.0%) after a decade of decline from 16.6% in 2000 [5].

Despite these great challenges, evidence-based interventions are available. The three Lancet Series on maternal and child undernutrition established global evidence-based interventions for reducing stunting, micronutrient deficiencies, and emphasized the importance of nutrition interventions during the first 1,000 days of life [6–8]. The 2021 Series also highlighted that program delivery has lagged the science, placing the operational considerations as a substantive aspect. Accelerating improvements in the nutritional status of children and mothers requires a holistic approach, with actions across multiple sectors and levels of intervention. To this end, we conducted an overview of the latest published systematic reviews and meta-analyses assessing nutrition and health interventions with effects in undernutrition (stunting, wasting, low birthweight, anemia) and breastfeeding indicators. While many of this interventions are nutrition-specific, a few are health interventions with indirect nutrition impacts. A common feature among all the interventions included in the review is that they tend to be delivered through clinics, primary health care, or community health workers. This review informed the Investment Framework for Nutrition 2024 recently launched by the World Bank [9].

Methods

The protocol for this overview of systematic reviews was registered in PROSPERO (the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) in the early stages of the literature search (#CRD42024529609). This systematic review followed the guidance of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [10].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Peer reviewed systematic reviews with meta-analyses (or modelling of prior meta-analyses) published in English or Spanish were included if they addressed the exposures summarized in Table 1. In addition, reviews were only included if they focused on pregnant individuals and infant and children aged 0–59 months, living in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The outcomes of interest included stunting, wasting, breastfeeding, anemia, low birthweight, as well as other related birth outcomes such as stillbirth, preterm, and infants born small for their gestational age (SGA), Supplementary Table S1 offers specific information regarding the operationalization of the main outcomes. Studies were excluded if focusing solely on high-income countries (HICs, as categorized by the World Bank), for which more than 50 percent of the study population lived in HICs, or that included pregnant individuals, infants or children with serious medical conditions affecting independently their nutrition status. Studies were also excluded if a potential conflict of interest could be identified. Search strategy

PubMed database was searched electronically for meta-analysis and systematic reviews published between 2018–2023. The search strategy used controlled vocabulary and was tested against a validation set of relevant articles previously selected. To try to include as much reviews as possible, the search terms incorporated broader design approaches. Additionally, references in included studies were revised to determine if additional reviews could be considered (i.e. forward citation). We also considered specific studies recommended by members of the Technical Advisory Group (TAG) of the Investment Framework for Nutrition, some of which were published out of the specified time range but that were dimmed fundamental due to lack of newer or higher-quality studies. The tracking of the search was conducted in Excel and the results of the search are presented using the PRISMA flow diagram.

Study selection

To identify the final list of reviews, the records identified in PubMed were exported to Excel. Two authors (MVC and HTMN) reviewed the titles and abstracts of each identified review and excluded those that did not meet the inclusion criteria. Prior to this, reviewers independently assessed the first 20 titles and abstracts for purpose of harmonization. Following a similar procedure, the same two authors reviewed the full text of manuscripts still considered for inclusion and made a final decision on which ones would be included in the overview of reviews. Throughout the process, any differences or questions were discussed with members of the TAG of the Investment Framework for Nutrition 2024 (based on their area of expertise).

Quality assessment and strength of evidence (SOE) assessment

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist for systematic reviews was used to assess the quality of manuscripts [11]. The JBI checklist provides a thorough framework for evaluating the methodological quality of systematic reviews, ensuring that all critical aspects are considered. The checklist can be adapted to various types of systematic reviews. It uses a binary scoring process (i.e., yes/no) to assess quality. Two of the authors (HTMN and NRV) independently assessed the quality of the included systematic reviews. Through the binary scores of different domains, the quality assessment of the manuscripts was displayed graphically to highlight their methodological strengths and weaknesses. In addition, a strength of evidence (SOE) assessment was conducted based on the tool summarized in the supplementary data (Table S2), which was designed based on prior synthesis analyses. One of the authors (HTMN) independently assessed the reviews based on the SOE tool, and around one third of the studies were also assessed by a second author (MVC).

Data synthesis

Two of the authors (HTMN and NRV) extracted the key information from each of the reviews using a form that included: authors and year; aim of the review; review type; countries or regions including number of studies; participants and sample size; interventions and outcomes; tools to assess quality; and methodology. For a narrative synthesis of the studies, we followed the conceptual strategy set in the Investment framework for Nutrition [9], summarizing the findings by population subgroup and outcomes.

Results

Search outcomes

The search generated 881 reports of different nutrition and health interventions with effects on undernutrition and breastfeeding indicators, with 3 additional records recommended by the TAG of the Investment Framework for Nutrition 2024 (see Fig. 1, PRISMA flow chart). After removing 47 duplicates, 834 titles and abstracts were reviewed. Study characteristics

Out of the 46 selected systematic reviews and meta-analyses, 36 included studies from at least one country in two or more regions, 3 focused solely on studies in Sub-Saharan Africa, and 7 did not explicitly state the countries involved (see Table S3). On average, the reviews included information from 38 countries (median = 20, maximum = 197, minimum = 5). The maps in Fig. 2 provide a more detailed geographic description per outcome. Shaded regions indicate countries included in the reviews, highlighting the wide variation in geographic coverage. These maps underscore that evidence-based interventions are informed by studies conducted across the globe, with contributions from every region. Notably, East, West, and Central Africa consistently provide evidence for all interventions. Additionally, all interventions are supported by at least one study from the Latin America and the Caribbean region, while every intervention benefits from evidence generated in Southeast Asia and the East Asia and Pacific region.Fig. 2Global maps highlighting nutrition evidence by outcome

Among the 15 studied interventions, delayed cord clamping had the highest number of reviews with 7, while therapeutic zinc supplementation and BEP supported with 1 review had the fewest. Most of the reviews addressed multiple nutrition-related outcomes. Approximately half of the reviews (22 or 48%) had sample sizes ranging from 0 to 15,000. Ten reviews (22%) had sample sizes between 15,000 and 50,000, and another ten reviews (22%) had sample sizes larger than 50,000. Four reviews did not specify the sample size (9%). Most of the studies used well-known quality assessment tools, including Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) (n = 14), Cochrane risk of bias tools (n = 10), Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for Randomized Trials (RoB2) (n = 1), Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) (n = 2), AMSTAR (n = 1), and the Newcastle Ottawa scale (n = 1). Additionally, 16 reviews used more than one tool, with all cases including either GRADE or Cochrane plus another tool (e.g., JBI, EPOC, RoB2, etc.). These reviews generally performed well in most dimensions assessed by the JBI tool, including clear search strategies, inclusion criteria, and review questions. However, there are areas with room for improvement as approximately 25% of the reviews either lacked or had unclear assessments of publication bias. Prenatal interventions

As highlighted in Table 2, five prenatal interventions are assessed. Iron/iron folic acid (IFA) supplementation, an intervention recommended by the WHO for over a decade, has consistently been associated with a reduced risk of maternal anemia and low birth weight (LBW) when compared to supplements without iron or a placebo. Recent reviews [12, 13] align with prior findings, showing moderately strong evidence of a significant reduction in maternal anemia by approximately 49% [14] and a 12% reduced risk of LBW when comparing IFA with folic acid alone [13]. Additionally, there is evidence suggesting a beneficial effect on reducing the incidence of babies born SGA [14], although further research is needed. Given the association of iron deficiency with other micronutrient deficiencies, there has been a shift towards recommending multiple micronutrient supplements (MMS). Meta-analyses consistently show strong evidence that MMS, compared to IFA supplementation, significantly reduces the risk of LBW by 12–17% [15–17] and SGA infants by 7–12% [13, 17, 18]. Additionally, there is strong evidence of a significant 9% decrease in stillbirths, defined by the death of a baby in utero at 28 weeks or more of gestation. [13, 16]. MMS performs as well as IFA in reducing maternal anemia.

For healthy pregnant women with low calcium intake, calcium supplementation leads to better pregnancy and birth outcomes. Analyses from LMICs show strong evidence that calcium supplements reduce the risk of preeclampsia by 48% compared to placebo [17]. Similar associations are found when pooling data from HICs and LMICs [19, 20]. Evidence also confirms a strong association between calcium supplementation and a reduction in the risk of preterm births, with recent estimates showing a 47% reduction [16, 17, 20]. There is some evidence of a reduced risk of LBW, likely associated with the reduction in preterm births, with a significant 16% risk reduction reported in recent meta-analyses [17] although some contradictions exist between studies.

For balanced energy protein (BEP) supplementation, a macronutrient supplement for pregnant women in which the proteins account for less than 25 percent of the total caloric content, only one review was found, showing a significant decrease in risk of LBW by 40%, SGA babies by 29%, stillbirths by 61%, and perinatal mortality by 50% when compared to no supplementation [21]. Despite these findings, the review is based primarily on outdated studies that do not account for the current standard of care, which generally includes IFA and increasingly MMS. Additionally, its SOE is determined as insufficient due to lack of sufficient published research.

Intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp), a public health intervention recommended by the WHO for pregnant women in malaria-endemic areas, shows strong evidence of significant reductions in maternal anemia by at least 10% [22] and LBW [23] by 21%. Concerns about resistance to sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine (SP), the recommended antimalarial medication, have led to assessing other antimalarials. Findings suggest that IPTp-SP remains effective [24]. Using azithromycin as part of IPTp has also shown positive impacts on LBW, preterm births, and neonatal deaths [25].

Perinatal and neonatal interventions

Two interventions during this window of transition period shortly after birth are considered. The first is delayed cord clamping, for which there is strong and significant evidence from both HICs and LMICs indicating that, as opposed to immediate or early clamping, it reduces the risk of neonatal death by 27% and intraventricular hemorrhage by 17% among preterm babies [26]. Additionally, delayed cord clamping is significantly associated with a reduced risk of anemia in both full-term and preterm babies [27, 28], as well as increased total hemoglobin levels during the initial days after birth (ranging from 1.6 g/dL for full-term babies to 2.4 g/dL for preterm babies) [22, 29].

The second intervention, Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC), is a method of care for infants that involves skin-to-skin contact with a parent to promote infant health and bonding. It is particularly beneficial for preterm and low birth weight (LBW) newborns, as it promotes bonding, regulates body temperature, and supports breastfeeding. Evidence from LMICs indicates that KMC reduces neonatal mortality by 32% and all-cause mortality during the first year [30, 31]. Additionally, KMC is associated with a 15% reduction in severe infection and sepsis [30]. Other significant benefits of KMC include improvements in exclusive breastfeeding at discharge or at 28 days (52%) and in children aged 1 to 6 months (51%) [30]. Furthermore, KMC is linked to gains in height (0.21 cm/week) and weight (4.08 g/day) [30], as well as increased growth velocity [32].

Interventions for children younger than 5 years

Table 2 summarizes the evidence of six interventions linked to children. Vitamin A is essential for children’s healthy growth and development, and there is strong and significant evidence that vitamin A supplementation in children aged 6 to 59 months reduces all-cause mortality by 12%, diarrhea-specific mortality by 12%, and diarrhea-specific incidence by 15% [33]. For zinc, another essential micronutrient that supports immune function, earlier reviews documented that preventive zinc supplementation in healthy children aged 1 to 59 months in LMICs was associated with an 11% reduction in the incidence of diarrhea, but showed no effect on mortality, anemia, stunting, or wasting [34]. Updated reviews confirm these findings [33, 35] and report a significant 9% reduction in the risk of diarrhea incidence [36]. Furthermore, zinc supplementation for the treatment of diarrhea in children older than 6 months has been associated with a shorter average duration of diarrhea and a significant 27% reduction in the risk of diarrhea persisting until day seven [37].

The preventive use of small-quantity lipid-based nutrient supplements (SQ-LNS) has been extensively evaluated over the past two decades as a supplement for infants and young children aged 6 to 24 months who are at risk of undernutrition. Previous studies involving populations in LMICs have shown that providing SQ-LNS during complementary feeding for children aged 6 to 24 months, compared to no intervention, significantly reduces the prevalence of severe stunting, moderate stunting, and moderate wasting [38]. More recent reviews confirm these findings, showing a significant reduction in the prevalence of stunting (12%), severe stunting (17%), wasting (14%), and severe wasting (31%) [39, 40]. Additionally, evidence has linked SQ-LNS to a reduced prevalence of anemia (16%) and iron deficiency anemia (64%) [41]. Furthermore, other studies suggest that for children aged 6 to 24 months, SQ-LNS can reduce the risk of all-cause mortality [42].

Micronutrient powders (MNPs), which are a dry blend of iron and other essential micronutrients, have been linked to a 24% reduction in the risk of anemia among children aged one to 59 months [34]. Recent robust evidence confirm this finding, and find that MNPs reduce anemia by 18% to 31% [22, 43]. However, MNPs have not shown significant effects on stunting or wasting, potentially due to factors such as the nutritional status of the population and the presence of infections and inflammation.

Management of severe acute malnutrition (SAM) in children under five years old involves both stabilization and rehabilitation. Stabilization includes interventions such as treating dehydration and potential infections. Rehabilitation for children with uncomplicated SAM focuses on promoting catch-up growth ready-to-use therapeutic food (RUTF), which is provided at the community level and through outpatient services to aid recovery. Previous evidence has shown that standard RUTF, compared to alternative dietary approaches, is associated with a 33% improvement in weight recovery and an increase in the mean rate of weight gain by approximately 1.12 g/kg/day [44]. A more recent review found that, compared to energy-dense home food, RUTFs likely improve the mean rate of height gain by about 0.7 mm/day [45], and also supported the concurrent use of broad-spectrum oral amoxicillin for children with uncomplicated SAM. Compared to no antibiotic, amoxicillin increased recovery, possibly enhanced weight gain by 0.67 g/kg/day, and reduced all-cause mortality by 26%.

Infant and young child nutrition education and counseling

Although the timing, frequency, delivery platforms, and settings of counseling and education during infancy can vary, there is strong evidence from LMICs that breastfeeding counseling and education are associated with increased rates of early initiation and exclusive breastfeeding [21, 35]. Previous studies have shown a two-fold improvement in exclusive breastfeeding rates at less than 1 month and at 1–5 months when interventions were delivered at the home or community level [46]. Notably the study also suggested that continued breastfeeding after 6 months showed a slightly lower increase when education and counseling strategies were delivered in combined settings (i.e., home or community and health services). Interventions to improve complementary feeding practices often consist of education and counseling aimed at informing and shaping caregivers'decisions about proper feeding practices and may also include food supplements. Evidence reviews assessing education and counseling interventions generally find no significant effects on growth (i.e., no impact on stunting or wasting) but do find significant associations with feeding practices, including the age at introduction of semi-solid foods, hygiene practices, and duration of breastfeeding [47]. Some studies have examined whether the effects of complementary feeding education and counseling are modified by food security [35], but more research is needed in this area.

Discussion

This review of maternal and child nutrition and health interventions aligns closely with the findings of the 2021 Lancet Series in terms of recommended evidence-based interventions to address undernutrition. This alignment is significant as it includes newly published evidence and utilizes a different SOE assessment approach. The differences identified are in the assessment of calcium supplementation, which was rated more favorably in our review, and RUTF, which received a lower SOE rating. Additionally, our assessment concluded that the evidence for BEP is outdated, which is concerning since the comparison group is no intervention, a scenario that does not reflect the current context. Therefore, newer evidence is warranted [48–50].

In addition, these high-impact interventions need to be adequately contextualized for their correct use. For instance, SQ-LNS must be adequately integrated within broader efforts to improve the diets of infants and young children [2]. As highlighted in the latest WHO report [51], SQ-LNS can be considered for the prevention of wasting for a limited duration, while ensuring active screening for malnutrition, access to adequate home diets, and providing infant and young child feeding counselling. Scaling up SQ-LNS will require the design and implementation of systems to correctly identify and follow-up at-risk children and link the provision of this commodity with further actions to improve diets [52].

Furthermore, there needs to be careful consideration of the context into which some of these interventions will be scaled. For example, nutrition counselling and education interventions can significantly improve caregivers’ knowledge of breastfeeding and complementary feeding recommendations. However, high-quality social and behavior change communication interventions, people-centered counseling, along with an adequately trained workforce, are necessary to translate that knowledge into actual improvements in practices. But, it is also crucial to acknowledge the challenges posed by the environments where nutrition counseling and education will happen, which may imply challenges linked to food insecurity, as well as the negative impact of commercial determinants on decisions regarding infant and young child feeding [53].

In this sense, one of the key values of this review, is in underscoring the robust body of literature on the “what” works but acknowledging that it mainly emerges form controlled studies. This leads to the fundamental need to focus on the remaining challenge of the “how” to scale, implement, and sustain these interventions. This requires detailed understanding of the challenges, gaps, and needs for prioritization to maximize returns on investments. It is a moral imperative to utilize resources in the right nutrition interventions, but making sure that their implementation is feasible, with fidelity, and with the required contextual adaptations to optimize its impact and sustainability in the long run [54–60].

While this overview of reviews had some limitations, the most notable was the use of only one search engine. However, sensitivity searches conducted in other engines showed little added value. This is likely because the study methodologies and outcomes included are commonly indexed in PubMed. The second limitation is that the review did not include the estimations to be a full umbrella review, but the diversity of interventions and outcomes considered would have made this approach difficult in its execution and reporting. It is relevant to keep in mind, that future studies could do this for specific outcomes and/or interventions.

Conclusions

Despite some advances, global nutrition challenges remain highly relevant. Although there is robust evidence supporting high-impact nutrition and health interventions, a more critical discussion and pragmatic knowledge are needed on how to effectively scale and integrate these interventions within different existing platforms. This integration is a crucial next step for which a different type of research is needed.
