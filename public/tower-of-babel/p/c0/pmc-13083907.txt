Experimental study on settling, transport, and packing mechanisms of proppants with different shapes in a physical model
Authors: Jun Li, Siyuan He, Mingyi Wu, Kewen Tang, Changyin Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydraulic fracturing, as a critical technology for reservoir stimulation, has functioned as a central stimulation technique in the large-scale development of shale gas and coalbed methane. During hydraulic fracturing operations, the movement and distribution of proppants within fractures serve as the key determinant of both fracture conductivity created by fracturing and long-term sustained production performance. Meanwhile, the settling and packing mechanisms of proppants greatly affects proppant transportation efficiency and distribution within fractures, which in turn determines the level of oil and gas production. However, current research on settling and packing mechanisms of proppants has primarily focused on spherical proppants, while some aspects of the non-spherical shaped proppants with different shapes and structures have not yet been fully considered. In this study, 3D printing technology was utilized to fabricate various non-spherical proppants, including cube, rhombohedron, tetrahedron, cuboid, and cylinder shapes. Compared to spherical proppants, these non-spherical counterparts were examined in terms of their settling, transport, and packing mechanisms using visualized proppant settling and transport setup. The result show that proppants with irregular shapes, such as rhombohedrons and tetrahedrons, experienced greater turbulent effects in the fluid, while proppants with regular shapes, such as cuboids, cubes, cylinders, and spheres, generated much less turbulence. For instance, under a low viscosity of 1 mPa·s, the settling velocity of spherical proppants was measured at 0.086 m/s, significantly higher than the 0.046 m/s for rhombohedron and 0.052 m/s for tetrahedron proppants. More importantly, the angle variation during the settlement process was much larger for the irregular-shaped proppants than for the regular ones. Moreover, the results also indicate that under low-viscosity conditions (< 3 mPa·s), non-spherical shape has a more significant effect on settling velocity. However, this effect becomes less apparent when the viscosity increases to the range of 6–9 mPa·s. Porosity measurements further revealed that rhombohedron and tetrahedron proppants achieved higher porosities (approximately 40–45%) compared to spheres and cubes (~ 35%), suggesting potential for enhanced fracture permeability. Finally, six new mathematical models were developed to predict the terminal settling velocity of different types of non-spherical shape proppants. The study of non-spherical proppants might provide a new idea for proppants application with new structures in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40890-z.

Introduction

Unconventional oil and gas play a crucial role in ensuring energy supply security and mitigating energy scarcity. However, achieving economically viable development of these resources remains the fundamental prerequisite for their industrial-scale commercialization1–3. Hydraulic fracturing is an effective technique for enhancing production in unconventional reservoirs4,5. The primary objective of fracturing treatment is to inject fluid with a high pressure to break the reservoir rock and create a fracture channel, and simultaneously placing proppants to maintain long-term fracture conductivity. Subsequently, effective proppant placement within the hydraulic fracture system is crucial for maintaining the fracture conductivity, directly impacting the extent to which oil and gas resources can be efficiently extracted. Finally, the fracturing fluid flows back, and oil/gas is produced6,7. However, during the hydraulic fracturing process, the proppant’s settlement, transportation, and packing directly affect the oil and gas’s permeability and flow capacity within the fractures. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms governing proppant settlement, transportation, and packing is essential for optimizing fracture performance and enhancing oil and gas recovery.

Natural sand has been widely used as a proppant due to its predominantly spherical shape. Natural sand tends to be closer to a spherical shape, and research has shown that spherical proppants within a foam fracturing fluid can be transported more efficiently. In other words, this enables spherical proppants to support the largest fracture area, optimizing fracture conductivity8. Consequently, many studies have focused on spherical proppants’ settling, transport, and packing behaviors.

In the early stage, McMechan and Shah9 used the rectangular shape slot to visually observe the relationship between spherical shape proppant settling velocity with the proppant concentration and different types of fracturing fluid. Following this study, by using the static model system to measure the phenomenon of the proppant settling process10 and using a simulation method to ascertain what factors influence spherical shape proppant settling, Luo and Tomac11 conducted the experimental method to test the relationship between slurry settling velocity and particle concentration and particle agglomeration. They found that the width of the fracture greatly affected proppant settling. Zhang, et al.12 also used the experimental method to test spherical shape proppants falling in slick water, and the settling velocity of the proppant was measured. Similarly, in the same year, Zhu, et al.13 carried out many experiments to observe the settling behaviors of spherical shaped particles in parallel plates. They also focused on factors involving effective viscosity, dimensionless diameter, and wall width. Okesanya and Kuru14, Ba Geri, et al.15, Yuan, et al.16, and Zhu, et al.17 also utilized the experimental method to measure the settling process of spherical shaped proppants with some key factors such as fluid rheology (viscosity and elasticity), proppant size, and fluid type. In recent years, Malhotra and Sharma18 used the visualization model to test key factors, such as the fracturing fluid effect on the proppant settling process. Huang, et al.19 further employed a transparent glass cylinder to investigate the effects of proppant size and different types of fracturing fluids on the static settling velocity of spherical proppants.

Indeed, much work has been done on the settlement process of spherical shape proppants in fracturing fluid. The influence of laws of related key factors and some studies also prove that proppant settling has a great effect on proppant placement into fractures and increases the fracture conductivity and propped fracture length20,21. The effective control of the settling velocity of proppant directly determines the above factors22,23. Not only does the settling velocity affect the aforementioned factors, but proppant transport and accumulation are also key factors influencing fracture conductivity and the effective propped fracture length24.

Thus, to fully understand the spherical-shape proppant transport process and packing mechanism in fractures, a lot of studies also concentrate on spherical shaped proppant transport processes with different key factors such as proppant properties, fluid properties, and injection type11,25–41. It is worth noting that Mollanouri-Shamsi, et al.42 investigated the effects of proppant angularity on the dynamic conductivity of a fracture filled with a proppant. They found that different angular proppants would lead to different proppant packing permeability.

Although the relevant research on spherical shape proppants has been fully studied, little attention has been paid to the effects of non-spherical proppants on settling velocity, transport, and packing mechanisms. In recent years, non-spherical proppants have attracted increasing attention due to their advantages, including reduced settling velocity, improved flowback control, and mitigation of sand production. Yao, et al.43 pointed out that, unlike spherical shape proppants, due to the effect of shape, non-spherical proppants might greatly affect hydraulic fracturing treatments. Therefore, the non-spherical shape proppants are likely to become more widely utilized in the oil and gas industry in the future.

In a simulation study, Osiptsov44 used the lattice-Boltzmann method to demonstrate that cylindrical proppant packing provides the highest permeability among ellipsoidal and cylindrical proppant packing. Later on, Zhu, et al.45 mentioned that due to the different flow state and flow resistance, compared to spherical shaped proppants, the non-spherical shaped proppants received increasing attention because they could greatly improve the performance of hydraulic fracturing. Xu, et al.46, Leonardi, et al.47 and Al Balushi, et al.48 also illustrated that with the change in the sphericity of the proppants, the permeability and conductivity of the fractures would be different. In other words, studying the settlement of non-spherical proppants and their filling performance in fractures was necessary. Based on Hertz’s theory of elasticity, Jia, et al.49 proved that compared with spherical proppants, rod-shaped proppants theoretically provided a better fracture conductivity than spherical shape proppants. Liu, et al.50 and Lv, et al.3 also concluded that the non-spherical shaped proppants settling or packing behavior had a deep relationship with the proppant backflow and fracture conductivity. Their results suggested that it was necessary to reveal the settling behavior of non-spherical shaped proppants and consider the migration characteristics of non-spherical shape proppants with the dynamic flow of fracturing fluid in fractures. Thus, in the last year, Li, et al.51 conducted an experimental method to observe the transport and deposition of non-spherical proppant particles that concluded that ovals and cylinders in complex fractures differed from spherical proppants. In other words, the non-spherical proppant might have a different settling mechanism in the fracture model. Thus, they suggest focusing on different types of spherical proppants settling in the fracture model. In contrast to spherical proppants, the non-spherical shaped proppants settling behavior, transportation, and packing process had a deep relationship with the fracture conductivity and propped fracture length. Previous attempts in this respect have generated useful results for illustration. However, certain aspects of non-spherical proppants with different geometries and structures have not yet been fully explored, such as cylindrical and ellipsoidal proppants, on their settling and transport behaviors within fractures. Some proppants with more complex structures were not considered. To the best of our knowledge, very limited experimental data was available to show the effects of non-spherical shaped proppants with complex structures on both settling behavior, transportation process, and packing porosity. These were especially crucial for understanding the new structure of non-spherical shaped proppants fundamentals. Such a study is presented in this paper. Given the importance of non-spherical geometries highlighted in previous studies, the associated limitations can significantly influence proppant transport and packing behaviors and therefore need to be systematically addressed. This study explored the settlement, movement, and packing behavior of different types of non-spherical shape proppants. First, 3D printing technology was used to create the non-spherical shape proppants, which included cubes, rhombohedrons, tetrahedron, cuboids, and cylinder shapes. More importantly, the 3D printing technology could ensure the consistency of their volume and apparent density. The visual container fracture models were reproduced, and they could be used to observe the non-spherical shaped proppant settling process, transportation process, and packing porosity. The rest of this paper is structured as follows. A detailed description of the experimental system and the fundamentals of PIV and PTV are presented in Section "Materials and methods", followed by the results and discussion in Section "Results and Discussion". In Section "Conclusions", the major findings of this study are presented.

Materials and methods

In this work, six different proppant shapes (cuboid, sphere, rhombohedron, tetrahedron, cube, cylinder) were used to investigate the influence of the proppant shape on the settlement, transportation, and accumulation behavior of proppants. All six different shapes of proppants were manufactured using 3D printing technology by Sichuan BaiYue 3D Technology Co., Ltd, which could ensure particle size uniformity. The apparent densities were based on the density of the 3D printing materials at 1.25 g/cm³. The volume densities of the cuboid, sphere, rhombohedron, tetrahedron, cube, and cylinder proppants were 0.665, 0.693, 0.59, 0.523, 0.728, and 0.68 g/cm³, respectively. It is worth mentioning that the volume density, also known as bulk density, refers to the mass of proppant material per unit bulk volume. This parameter directly reflects the packing capability of proppant. The bulk volume primarily consists of two components: the solid volume of the proppant material itself and the void volume between proppants. However, when designing the experiment, we pay more attention to the apparent density (about 1.25 g/cm³) of proppant (Ratio of proppant mass to its volume).In this study, the apparent density of proppant particles with different shapes remains identical, and what we need to ensure in our experiment is that the apparent density is consistent. The apparent densities were based on the density of the 3D printing materials at 1.25 g/cm³. The cuboid, sphere, tetrahedron, cube, and cylinder proppants possessed an equivalent spherical diameter (ESD) of 3 mm (Fig. 1). However, due to the complex structure of the rhombohedron, the printing difficulty of 3D printing technology increased, and the 3D printing accuracy decreased. Even though the ESD of the rhombohedron proppant was 2.83 mm, with a deviation of 5.6%, it can still be said that it remains within an acceptable experimental tolerance. Notably, due to precision limitations in 3D printing, the size of fabricated proppants is still larger than that of common proppant. However, we have minimized the dimensions of fabricated proppants to the greatest extent while maintaining precision. In addition, slickwater with viscosities of 1, 3, 6, and 9 mPa·s were prepared to analyze the effect of fracture fluid viscosity. At the same time, a type of tracer particle with an extremely small particle size and density similar to that of the fracturing fluid were used in this study to characterize the movement of the fracturing fluid. The tracer particle was purchased directly from Oriental Fluid Measurement Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd, with a particle size of 20 μm.

To systematically investigate the settling, transport, and packing characteristics of proppants with different shapes, three specialized experimental setups were constructed. The visual proppant settling experimental system (Fig. 2) consists of a vertical visualized fracture model (self-made, 20 cm in length, 50 cm in height, and 0.9 cm in width), a high-speed camera (Shenzhen Huagu Power Technology Co., Ltd, resolution 1920 × 1200, 2. 3 million pixels, 150 FPS), a laser light source (Zhimai Laser Co., Ltd., power 1000mW, wavelength 532 nm), and a Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) analysis system, used to observe the settling process of proppants with different shapes in fracturing fluids of varying viscosities. The placement and transportation experiment setup (Fig. 3) includes a pre-pad fluid tank, a slurry tank (self-made, 20 cm in diameter, 30 cm in height), a screw pump (Shanghai Hongdong pump manufacturing Co., Ltd, 960 rad/min, 0.75 kW, 0.8 m3/h maximum pump rate), a fracture model (self-made, 20 cm in length, 10 cm in height, and 2 cm in width), a high-speed camera (1920 × 1200 pixel resolution), and LED light sources (220 V, 200 W, 400 lamp beads) to simulate the movement and placement behavior of non-spherical or spherical proppant within the fracture model. Additionally, a porosity measurement system (Fig. 4) was established, which consists of one graduated syringe (200 mL), one experimental shelf, and one measuring cylinder (150 mL). Notably, the parameters of the fracture model in the placement and transportation experiment setup were acquired by the parameter conversion of the actual formation fracture based on the principle of similarity to maintain the similarity between the experimental and field conditions30,31,36. A comparison of the experimental parameters with field operations for both field and laboratory fractures are provided in Table 1, and a detailed description of the corresponding methods is provided in “Appendix A”.

Based on those experimental apparatuses described above, standardized procedures were designed and implemented for each investigation. The settling experiment involved filling the vertical visualized fracture model with fracturing fluid, adding 0.1 g of tracer particles, releasing a non-spherical or spherical proppant freely at the surface of the fracturing fluid with zero initial velocity, and recording its descent with the high-speed camera until it reached the bottom. The movement and transportation experiment required preparing the pre-pad fluid and slurry fluid first, then filling the whole fracture model with pre-pad fluid; then pumping the slurry into the fracture model until the entire slurry has been fully injected into the fracture model, and recording the non-spherical or spherical proppant movement process by the high-speed camera. For porosity measurement, a calibrated syringe was used to pack a specific volume of proppants, after which water was added to determine the total volume and calculate porosity.

A comprehensive experimental scheme was designed to investigate the behavior of non-spherical or spherical proppant. Twenty-four settling tests (Table 2) were conducted by varying proppant shape and fluid viscosity individually to evaluate their effects on settling dynamics of non-spherical or spherical proppant and PIV (particle image velocimetry) method that could analyze of flow field also used in this part. Meanwhile, six proppant experiments (Table 3) were designed to study the transportation mechanisms of the non-spherical or spherical proppants in the fracture models. Simultaneously, another twenty-four testing cases were designed (Table 4) to study the porosity of the non-spherical or spherical proppants. It is worth mentioning that each experiment was repeated multiple times to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the results.

Results and discussion

Settling characteristics of non-spherical or spherical proppants

Proppant shape is an important parameter that directly corresponds to the settling velocity of the proppant. Figure 6 compares the settling velocity of non-spherical or spherical proppants in the fracturing fluid with different viscosities, which was obtained through the method described in the Appendix A (cases 1–24 in Table 2). With the increase in fluid viscosity, the settling velocity of non-spherical proppants (cuboid, sphere, rhombohedron, tetrahedron, cube, cylinder) decreased. The settling velocity of spherical proppants was significantly higher than that of other non-spherical proppants in the fracturing fluid with different viscosities. Under a low viscosity of 1 mPa·s, the settling velocity of spherical proppants was measured at 0.086 m/s, significantly higher than the 0.046 m/s for rhombohedron and 0.052 m/s for tetrahedron proppants. Among the non-spherical proppants, the tetrahedron and rhombohedron shapes exhibited the lowest settling velocities, resulting in longer suspension times.

Fig. 6Comparison of the settling velocity of different non-spherical proppants under different fluid viscosity.

Comparison of the settling velocity of different non-spherical proppants under different fluid viscosity.

The main reasons for this phenomenon are twofold: First, in the case of low-viscosity fluids, the spherical proppants experience less disturbance from the fluid due to their shape, leading to less obstructions in their settling process, resulting in a higher settling velocity (Fig. 7b). In contrast, rhombohedron and tetrahedron proppants, due to their angular shapes, experienced greater fluid disturbances, which led to more hindrance during settling and thus resulted in a lower settling velocity (Fig. 7c, d). The relatively regular shape of the cylinder, cuboid, and cube proppants also resulted in some fluid disturbance affecting their settling, but not as significantly as proppant with other shapes (Fig. 7a, e, f). This causes their settling velocity to fall between that of the spherical and angular (rhombohedron and tetrahedron) proppants. Second, besides shape-induced turbulence, the proppant deflection angle change in the flow process was greatest for the tetrahedron and rhombohedron proppants. The larger the angle of deflection, the greater the turbulence generated, further reducing the settling velocity of the tetrahedron and rhombohedron proppants (Fig. 8). In comparison, cube, cuboid, and cylinder proppants also experienced some angle changes, but to a lesser extent than the tetrahedron and rhombohedron proppants. This explains why their turbulence effects were weaker than those of the rhombohedron and tetrahedron proppants. Since there was no angle variation, the turbulence effect was the weakest for the spherical proppants, and so their settling velocity was the highest (Fig. 8).

The deflection angle of different non-spherical proppants.

In addition, it was also observed that the viscosity range limits had distinct boundaries for different particle shapes, meaning that the settling velocities of proppants with different shapes showed significant differences. However, as the viscosity increased, the velocity differences between different shapes gradually decreased. The main reason for this is that at lower viscosities, the drag force was smaller, allowing the influence of shape to play a dominant role, which led to clear differences in the settling velocities of proppants with different shapes (Fig. 6a). However, as the viscosity increased, the drag force exerted by the fracturing fluid on the proppants also increased. The effect of shape becomes less significant, reducing the settling velocity differences between different shapes (Fig. 6b). Additionally, through a comparison of flow lines at higher viscosities, it can be seen that at higher viscosities, the viscoelasticity of the fracturing fluid played a decisive role in the settling process of the proppants (Fig. 7). As a result, the turbulence effects on proppants of different shapes (spherical, cylinder, cuboid, cube, rhombohedron, and tetrahedron) were all reduced, significantly lowering the differences in their settling velocities.

Piling-up process of the non-spherical or spherical proppants in the fracture

The piling-up process of the non-spherical proppants was mostly consistent with that of the spherical proppant25, which can also be divided into three stages (Fig. 9).

Fig. 9Piling-up process of different non-spherical proppants.

Piling-up process of different non-spherical proppants.

In the first stage, as the fluid carries proppants of different shapes into the fracture (0–5 s), the proppants quickly move to the fracture’s front end and accumulate at the bottom. In the second stage, as time progresses (5–15 s), the accumulation height of the proppants continued to increase, and the proppants moved further along the fracture until they reached the end. In the last stage of fluid carrying proppants into the fracture (15–25 s), the accumulation height of the proppants reached a dynamic equilibrium state, and a stable proppant placement pattern was finally achieved.

In addition, it was also observed that the motion mechanisms of proppants with different shapes were generally consistent with the motion type of spherical proppants. The primary motion types are still suspension, saltation, and creeping. In other words, different morphological structures of non-spherical or spherical proppant do not significantly change the accumulation and movement mechanism of non-spherical or spherical proppant.

Final packing pattern of the non-spherical or spherical proppants in the fracture

Figure 10 shows the final packing pattern of the non-spherical proppants in the fracturing model (cases 1–6 in Table 3). The final packing pattern of the non-spherical proppants was mainly in the form of pentagons, and a depression was formed in the middle of the proppant packing pattern due to the flush of the non-spherical proppants. This phenomenon has also been commonly reported in spherical proppant research33,53. In addition, due to the different flushing rates of non-spherical proppants on the surface of the proppant dune, the depression height and pinchout angle (measurement method described in Appendix A) of the final packing pattern also varied. Among them, rhombohedron proppants had the shallowest depression height (about 1 cm, Fig. 11). The smallest pinchout angle is about 16°. Tetrahedron and cube proppants had the deepest depression height (about 3 cm, Fig. 11). The largest pinchout was angle (about 33°, Fig. 11). The velocity distribution of different non-spherical or spherical proppants (Fig. 12) indicates that the proppant velocity distribution of tetrahedron and cube proppants was concentrated in the middle of the fracture, resulting in a deeper depression height. Meanwhile, the particle velocity distribution of rhombohedron proppants was relatively dispersed and wide, which resulted in a shallow depression height in the middle of the proppant packing pattern and the smallest pinchout angle, indicating that the transport performance of the rhombohedron-shaped proppants is better than that of other non-spherical proppants.

Porosity of the non-spherical or spherical proppants

Figure 13 shows the non-spherical or spherical proppants’ porosity (measurement method is described in the Appendix A) (cases 1–24 in Table 4). In earlier research, due to the limited variety of non-spherical shapes, cylindrical or rod-shaped proppants were generally considered to exhibit higher permeability44,47. While, through porosity measurement experiments, the porosity of the rhombohedron and tetrahedron proppants was found to be relatively large. The porosity of rhombohedron and tetrahedron proppants achieved higher porosities (approximately 40–45%) compared to spheres and cubes (~ 35%). In other words, the permeability of rhombohedron and tetrahedron should be highest when different media (water or gas) pass through their sandbank. The main reason for this is that the packing structure of rhombohedron and tetrahedron proppants primarily exhibit more pronounced edges and greater geometric irregularity, which result in looser packing structures and higher porosity. In contrast, if the packing structure of the dunes involves face-to-face contact, the resulting porosity is smaller. Therefore, it is generally recommended that rhombohedron and tetrahedron proppants are more favorable for providing higher accumulation porosity to enhance the permeability of oil and gas.

Fitting of experimental settling velocity

The particle shape and viscosity of the fracture fluid influence the settling velocity of proppants. To address this, five mathematical models were developed to predict the settling velocity of proppants with different shapes. Notably, our prediction models were based on empirical formulas. We selected shape factor (k) to characterize particles of different shapes in these models as training data. Conclusions

In existing research on the movement of non-spherical particles, the focus has been primarily on cylindrical and rod-shaped proppants. Therefore, based on the non-spherical and spherical proppant fabricated via 3D printing technology, this study systematically investigated the settling, transport, and packing characteristics of proppants with different shapes through settling, transport, and porosity measurement experiments. The results showed that unlike spherical proppants, non-spherical ones, including cubes, rhombohedrons, tetrahedrons, cuboids, and cylinders, significantly impact proppant settling, transportation, and packing. By adopting PIV and PTV methods, the movement mechanisms of non-spherical or spherical proppant during settling and transport processes were analysed. Furthermore, several new mathematical models were developed to predict the settling velocity of non-spherical proppants, thereby revealing their settling behavior. The key findings are as follows: This study used 3D printing technology to create spherical and non-spherical proppants, including sphere, cube, rhombohedron, tetrahedron, cuboid, and cylinder shapes.Under low viscosity conditions (< 3 mPa·s), non-spherical shapes had a greater effect on the settling velocity. However, as the viscosity increased to a certain range (6–9 mPa·s), the effect of non-spherical shapes diminished significantly.Proppants with irregular shapes, such as rhombohedrons and tetrahedrons, experienced greater turbulent effects in the fluid, in comparison to proppants with regular shapes, such as cuboids, cubes, cylinders, and spheres, which generated much smaller turbulence. Additionally, we observed that the angle change during the settlement process was much larger for irregular-shaped proppants than for regular ones.Porosity measurements revealed that the porosity of rhombohedrons and tetrahedrons proppants was higher than that of other non-spherical shapes. The transportation experiments showed that the packing height of cylindrical and cubic proppants was the largest, while rhombohedron proppants demonstrated a better horizontal transportation efficiency than the others.Six new mathematical models were designed and used, accurately predicting the terminal settling velocity of different types of non-spherical shape proppants.djjd
