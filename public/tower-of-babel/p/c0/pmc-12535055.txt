Deep learning models for ICU readmission prediction: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Emanuele Koumantakis, Konstantina Remoundou, Nicoletta Colombi, Carmen Fava, Ioanna Roussaki, Alessia Visconti, Paola Berchialla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntensive Care Unit (ICU) readmissions are associated with increased morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs. Therefore, determining an appropriate timing of ICU discharge is critical. In this context, deep learning (DL) approaches have attracted significant attention.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review of studies developing or validating DL models for ICU readmission prediction, published up to March 4th, 2025, and indexed in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science. We summarised them along multiple dimensions, including outcome and population definition, DL architecture, reproducibility, generalizability, and explainability, and provided a meta-analytic estimate of model performance.ResultsWe included 24 studies encompassing 49 DL models, predominantly trained on US-based datasets, and rarely subjected to external validation. There was considerable variability across study settings, including the definition and timeframe of the ICU readmission outcome, as well as DL architecture used, alongside a substantial risk of bias. Technical reproducibility and model interpretation were rare. A meta-analysis of AUROC values from 11 studies yielded a mean of 0.78 (95% CI = 0.72–0.84), with very high heterogeneity (I2 = 99.9%). Models targeting disease-specific ICU subpopulations achieved significantly higher performance (mean AUROC = 0.92, 95% CI = 0.89–0.95, p = 0.002), and substantially lower heterogeneity (I2 = 17.1%).ConclusionsDL models showed promising performances in predicting ICU readmissions, but exhibited several shortcomings, including low reproducibility, over-reliance on a few US-based datasets, and limited explainability. Additionally, the high heterogeneity and risk of bias limited our ability to assess their pooled performance through meta-analysis. Taken together, our observations suggest that the quality of the evidence regarding the application of DL approaches to ICU readmission prediction is poor, thus hindering their clinical applicability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-025-05642-x.

Background

The decision to discharge a patient from the intensive care unit (ICU) is a complex process. Discharging a patient too early increases their risk of adverse outcomes, particularly ICU readmission and mortality, whereas a delayed discharge contributes to higher healthcare costs [1]. Given that ICU readmissions are themselves associated with a more than double mortality risk [2–4], extended hospital stays [2, 4], greater morbidity [2, 4], and the use of more resources [1, 3], identifying patients at high risk of ICU readmission is crucial [1, 3]. Numerous attempts have been made to develop models for risk of ICU readmission [5]. One of the earliest is the Stability and Workload Index for Transfer (SWIFT), a multivariable logistic regression model estimating readmission risk based on the original source of ICU admission, ICU length of stay, and patient stability [6]. Others are the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II and III scores [7, 8], and the Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS) II [9]. However, the clinical utility of these models is limited by insufficient evidence regarding their performance, potential biases, and overall lack of effectiveness in real-world settings [5]. Additionally, they depend on strict statistical assumptions, such as specific error distributions, additive effects in linear models, and proportional hazards, which are often violated in real-life scenarios [10], and are unsuited for high-dimensional multimodal datasets [11].

Machine learning (ML) methods are better equipped to handle complex interactions and to uncover intricate relationships between numerous risk factors [12]. Among those, deep learning (DL) models have gained significant attention in healthcare, especially in the analysis of medical images [13–16], and in the prediction of prolonged hospital stay and readmissions [17]. However, there is a significant gap in the literature regarding the application and validation of DL models for ICU readmission prediction. Published systematic reviews primarily focus on traditional statistical models or broader ML techniques, and typically overlook the unique capabilities of DL approaches [5, 18, 19].

Here, we perform a systematic review and comprehensive meta-analysis on the current state-of-the-art DL-based methods. We assess their predictive performance, present their associated challenges, and discuss current and future prospects. Our work synthesizes the current status of the literature and critically appraises the quality of existing studies, thus aiding clinicians in making informed decision regarding the application of such approaches in critical-care settings.

Methods

Data sources, search strategy, and study selection

We performed a systematic review of studies describing DL models for the prediction of ICU readmission risk, according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [20]. We registered this systematic review in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under ID: CRD420251000813.

We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science for articles using MeSH terms and free words (Supplementary Table 1, date of systematic search: March 4th, 2025). We also used reviews as an additional search method through citation tracking.

Inclusion criteria were: P (patients): patients aged 15 years or older hospitalized in the ICU; I (index prognostic model): development or validation of DL prognostic models; C (comparative model): none; O (outcomes): ICU readmission; T (timing of the prediction horizon): no restriction; S (setting): ICU, studies published in English. We excluded conference abstracts, editorials, preprints, and non-peer-reviewed articles.

After article retrieval, we performed article screening using the Rayyan online platform [21], which we used to remove duplicates, and to assist with the independent blind screening. Two independent reviewers (EK and PB) manually screened the title and abstract of each article, and a third independent reviewer (AV) resolved all disagreements. EK and KR independently performed full-text screening, with disagreements resolved by AV and PB.

Data extraction

We used the Critical Appraisal and Data Extraction for Systematic Reviews of Prediction Modelling Studies (CHARMS) to create the data extraction form [22]. This included general article information, data source, study setting, inclusion and exclusion criteria, sample size, number of events, predictors, outcomes, modelling techniques, model validation, and model performance. One reviewer (EK) extracted data from all included studies, while a second reviewer (KR) independently reviewed them. Disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Assessment of risk of bias and applicability

Two reviewers (EK and KR) independently assessed the risk of bias and applicability of the studies using the Prediction model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST, version 2019-05-15) [23]. We used the “robvis” R package (version 0.3.0) for graphical visualization [24].

Data synthesis and reporting

Throughout this study, we preferred a qualitative synthesis of the evidence. However, when evaluating model performance, we pursued a meta-analytic approach to average the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC) metric when reported, along with a measure of variability, by at least two studies. If variability was not directly provided, we estimated it, whenever possible, using the available information, e.g., by calculating the standard error from the reported confidence interval. To perform the meta-analyses, we used the “rma” function from the “metafor” R package (version 4.8.0) [25], after applying a logit transformation [26]. We used the I2 statistic to measure heterogeneity (function “rma”, “metafor” R package, version 4.8.0). Finally, we conducted subgroup analyses based on whether disease-specific subpopulations were used, the outcome type, the time window for readmission observation, the specific architecture of the DL model, and the data source (MIMIC-III and IV datasets). In the last two subgroup analyses, to control for heterogeneity, we focused on a specific definition of the outcome and timeframe, i.e., 30-day ICU readmission not considering mortality. We also excluded studies using a disease-specific subpopulation. If a study presented more than one model, we selected the best-performing one, except from the specific subgroup analysis on the model architecture, where we selected the best-performing model for each architecture used in the study. We tested subgroup differences through meta-regression, considering p-values less than 0.05 as significant.

When a study also reported a comparison with a traditional statistical (e.g., SWIFT, APACHE-II) or ML approach, we calculated the AUROC improvement as the difference in AUROCs between the best-performing DL model and the best-performing traditional model, divided by the AUROC of the best-performing traditional model.

Results

Included studies

After the systematic search and screening (Fig. 1), we included 23 articles. The main reasons for exclusion during full-text screening were incorrect publication type (n = 14), general in-hospital readmission outcome (n = 3), no DL models used (n = 3), and having a paediatric population (n = 2). An additional article was identified through citation tracking, for a total of 24 articles (Supplementary Table 2).

No studies published before 2019 met our eligibility criteria. This aligns with the paradigm shift towards the use of DL in the analysis of medical records which started in 2016 [27], when large datasets regarding ICU readmission were also made publicly available [28].

Data sources

Most of the studies [29–46] (n = 18, 75%; Supplementary Table 2) used the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) III for training and validation [28]. MIMIC-III includes 53,423 ICU admissions recorded in a single US-based hospital between 2001 and 2012. Deng et al. [47] used MIMIC-IV, which includes admissions up to 2019, counting over 65,000 patients [48]. As single-centre data sources, both MIMIC-III and IV increase the risk of overfitting and may introduce database-specific bias. This will likely reduce generalizability to novel datasets, particularly when applied to distinct populations [49].

Three studies [31, 50, 51] (13%, Supplementary Table 2) employed the multicenter eICU Collaborative Research Database, which includes more than 200,000 ICU stays from 208 US hospitals [52]. This potentially mitigates overfitting issues, but it does not address the problem of generalizability to non-US populations.

The remaining three studies used hospital-specific ICU data sources [53–55]. El Hassouny et al. [53] used 69,540 patients’ records collected from 130 US hospitals between 1999 and 2008 [56]. Lim et al. used data from 70,842 patients admitted to the ICUs of the Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH) between 2007 and 2021 [54]. Shickel et al. [55] used 73,190 ICU admission from the University of Florida Integrated Data Repository, which were collected between 2012 and 2019 [57].

Definition of ICU readmission outcome

The optimal time interval for defining ICU readmissions remains a matter of debate. Early readmissions are thought to reflect ICU care quality rather than patient characteristics, leading to suggestions that a 48-hour window could serve as an appropriate ICU quality indicator [1], as supported by empirical evidence [58]. However, only one study [54] used this definition, while another study [35] also included patients discharged from the hospital, thus, not limiting their analysis to ICU readmission within the same hospital stay (Supplementary Table 2).

Most of the studies [29, 30, 32, 33, 36–39, 41–47, 53] defined the outcome as readmission within 30 days of ICU discharge (n = 16, 67%, Supplementary Table 2), followed by readmission during the same hospital stay, regardless of the time window (n = 5, 21%) [31, 50, 51, 54, 55].

Two studies [34, 40] (8%) explored multiple timeframes, ranging from 5 to 30 days. Additionally, six studies [29, 36, 39, 43, 45, 46] (25%) also explored mortality risk within 30 days of discharge or transfer to general wards.

Patient inclusion and exclusion criteria

Common patient exclusion criteria were: (i) death during ICU stay or within 30 days of discharge (even without ICU readmission, n = 10, 42%) [30, 32, 36–38, 42, 44–46, 54], (ii) ICU length of stay shorter than one day, or exceeding 30 days (n = 6, 25%) [32, 35, 39, 44, 47, 50], with one study [55] excluding patients with length of stay shorter than one hour, and (iii) insufficient patient data (n = 5, 21%) [32, 35, 44, 51, 54], e.g., Chiu et al. [32]. excluded patients missing more than 30% of their data. Additionally, Deng et al. [47] excluded patients aged 90 or over, and Lim et al. [54] removed patients who refused life-sustaining treatments. The removal of patients can improve model performance by reducing population heterogeneity [19], but is generally discouraged as it can introduce selection bias, and prevent the resulting model from generalizing to the excluded patients’ subpopulations.

Additionally, three studies focused on specific patient populations: patients with heart failure [42], with pneumonia or chronic kidney disease [34], and those admitted to the cardiac surgery and coronary care unit [35], potentially improving model performance and applicability, as already observed in the literature [19].

Deep learning architectures

The 24 studies included in this systematic review described 49 models (Supplementary Table 3). Ten studies [30–32, 34–36, 39, 40, 47, 54] (42%) developed multiple DL models. Six studies [31, 34, 35, 39, 40, 54] (25%) trained variants of the same architecture (e.g., using different patient subgroups or subset of data, such as those collected in the last 48 h), while four [30, 32, 36, 47] (17%) experimented with different architectures.

The most commonly used DL architecture was Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) [59], a variant of recurrent neural networks (RNN) [60] that can capture long-term dependencies in sequential data. This preference likely reflects the ability of LSTM to handle the interdependence of events as well as the temporal irregularity and sequential nature of ICU patient data, which evolve throughout an ICU stay [61]. The LSTM architecture was either used alone (6 models [32, 35, 39, 47]) or in combination with traditional ML approaches, i.e., LightGBM (3 models [54]) and gradient boosting trees (2 models [34]), or with other DL architectures. Additional DL architectures were convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [62, 63], optimized for grid-structured data, such as images, but also suitable for longitudinal electronic health records’ (EHR) data (3 models [36, 46]) [62–64]; transformers [65], which learn contextual relationships, and handle long-term dependencies (2 models [29, 32]); and Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) [66], which learn from graph-structured data, such as diagnosis, treatment, and lab codes (1 model [38]). Hybrid architectures have the potential to enhance performance, for instance by taking advantage of multiple types of predictors. Chiu et al. [32] improved the performance of their LSTM model by adding a transformer, BERTopic [67], to extract important features from patient discharge summaries (AUROC without BERTopic = 0.75, 95% CI = 0.75–0.76; AUROC with BERTopic = 0.80, 95% CI = 0.80–0.80).

RNNs alone were another commonly used DL architecture (17 models [30, 40, 41, 44, 47]), but only in studies published up to 2023. Other architectures no longer used after 2023 included transformer-based models, i.e., Longformers [68], which are able to manage longer text sequences (2 models [55]), and Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) [45, 69]; and Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs) [70] (2 models [44, 47]), a variant of RNNs that includes update and reset gates to improve the overall model performance while reducing computational costs [71]. These were also combined in one model [33].

CNNs alone were also used, albeit less frequently (2 models [36, 43]).

Several architectures were used in only one study (Supplementary Table 3). The Multiview Adversarially Regularized Graph Autoencoder (MRGAE) [37], used by Lu et al. (2020) [37], takes advantage of two parallel GCNs to capture relationships between patients (topological view) and their clinical similarities (semantic view). The Decay Replay Mining (DREAM) algorithm [72], used by Pishgar et al. [42], generates patient clinical trajectories from timestamped events, which, together with demographic data and severity scores, are processed by three independent deep neural networks [42], thus combining process mining with DL and increasing model performance. Deep Forest [73], used by Khodadadi et al. [50], is a decision tree-based ensemble approach which requires fewer hyperparameters than traditional deep neural networks, making them more accessible [50]. The Multi-Gate Mixture of Multi-view Graph Contrastive Learning (MMMGCL) [31], used by Cao et al. [31], combines hierarchical graph modelling of patients’ data, multiview contrastive learning [74] (to maximize agreement between different types of patients’ data), and multitask learning [75] (for performing multiple clinical prediction tasks) to generate more powerful patient representations. The three-stage enhanced predictive readmission to the ICU (EPRIC) algorithm [53], El Hassouny et al. [53], increases DL model accuracy while preserving clinical reliability by identifying and correcting uncertain, error-prone cases using statistical modelling, and optimal stopping theory. The Code-Text cross-modal Contrastive Learning (CTCL) [51] used by Sun et al. [51], employs a multiview GCN and a transformer (BioClinicalBERT [76]) to model structured and unstructured data, respectively, which are then fused using Cross-Modal Fusion [77], while contrastive learning [78] ensures agreement between these two sources of information, building a richer and more robust patient representation.

Technical reproducibility

To be reliable, results from DL models should at least be technically reproducible, i.e., an independent research team should obtain identical results using the data and the code provided by the original work [49].

MIMIC-III, MIMIC-IV, and eICU are publicly available upon a motivated request to Physionet [79–81], following completion of the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) “Data or Specimens Only Research” course. The dataset used by El Hassouny et al. [53] was previously published [56], and is currently publicly available. The dataset used by Lim et al. [54]. and Shickel et al. [55] are not publicly available. However, the former is reported to be available upon reasonable request to the corresponding author, while the latter is subject to managed access due to ethical constraints, and may require additional approvals from the University of Florida Institutional Review Board.

Although the data sources are, at least theoretically, all available, code for the data preprocessing and model training was provided in only four studies [29, 30, 36], with an additional study [44] sharing only the code for data preprocessing, and another [37] providing only the code to train the model. Notably, only one study [30] made the pretrained model publicly available. This lack of code and/or pretrained models not only makes technical reproducibility impossible but also hinders their deployment in clinical settings.

Predictors and model explainability

The predictors were typically classified as: (i) static variables, e.g., non-time-varying demographic and anamnestic data, general hospitalization or ICU admission characteristics like length of stay, and/or admission source, (ii) dynamic variables, e.g., time-dependent vital signs, laboratory test results, diagnosis codes, and/or medications, and (iii) unstructured text, such as clinical notes from discharge summaries.

Most studies [29–31, 34–37, 39–43, 45–47, 53–55] combined static and dynamic variables (n = 18, 75%, Supplementary Table 2), with an additional three [32, 33, 44] (12.5%) also incorporating specialized modules to extract features from unstructured medical text. Khodadadi et al. [50] used only static variables, excluding those, such as lab results, that can change during the ICU stay. Lu et al. (2021) [38] used unstructured text only, and Sun et al. [51]. used both unstructured text and dynamic variables.

DL models are considered particularly well-suited to handling unstructured data, such as text or images, due to their ability to extract relevant features directly from the data without the need for manual feature engineering [82]. However, models using only clinical notes achieved widely varying performances, ranging from only marginally better than chance (AUROC = 0.56 [33]) to very good (AUROC = 0.83 [38]). We also observed in the ablation studies (i.e., a feature selection technique that evaluates predictor contributions by removing categories of them, and assessing performance changes) performed by Darabi et al. [33] and Chiu et al. [32], that the AUROC improvement from including clinical notes was marginal (4.5% and 6.5%, respectively).

Ten studies [32, 35, 36, 39–42, 46, 47, 54] (42%) explicitly stated their predictor selection rationale, i.e., literature review, expert opinion, existing scores (e.g., SWIFT and APACHE), greedy backward elimination approach (i.e., a feature selection technique that starts with all potential predictors and iteratively removes the least significant), or ablation study.

Notably, ablation studies showed no significant improvement in model performance (AUROC difference < 0.02) when using data from the last 48 h of ICU stays compared to the first 48 h [36] or the whole ICU stay [39]. This contradicts previous observations regarding the predictive relevance of more recent trajectories [58]. Additionally, another ablation study [36] highlighted that chronic diseases, as indicated in EHRs using International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision (ICD-9) codes, were among the most important features associated with ICU readmissions. This is in line with previous observations showing that chronic conditions, such as respiratory disease, dialysis dependence, and immunosuppression, were strongly linked to higher odds of readmission [58].

Despite these attempts at quantifying the importance of the predictors, the explainability of DL models remains a critical challenge and an active area of research. Only two studies [42, 54] used the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) [83], which quantifies the contribution of individual predictors, to provide a model interpretation. Lim et al. [54] reported that, for the 48-hour readmission prediction task, the most important time-dependent variables were peripheral oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, and heart rate. Pishgar et al. [42] reported that, for the 30-day readmission task in patients with heart failure, the Charlson and Elixhauser severity scores were the most important features, while demographic information, admission, comorbidity, and lab measurement were the least relevant.

Risk of bias and applicability

Based on the PROBAST assessment, we found a low risk of bias in three studies, and a high risk in 16, while the risk for the remaining five studies was unclear (Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 4). Reasons for high risk included composite outcome involving death events (n = 8, 33%), specific ICUs or disease-based patient selection (n = 4, 17%), listwise data deletions (n = 3, 13%), varying outcome timeframes to improve results (n = 2, 8%), and unreported performance metrics (n = 1, 4%). Unclear risk typically arose from insufficient reporting of predictor handling (n = 5, 21%), inclusion/exclusion criteria (n = 3, 13%), missing data handling (n = 3, 13%), number of events (n = 3, 13%), sample size (n = 2, 8%), and internal validation (n = 1, 4%).

We found low applicability concerns in 12 studies, and high applicability concerns in nine, while these were unclear in the remaining three studies (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 4). High applicability concerns primarily stemmed from composite outcomes that included mortality (n = 8, 33%), thus reducing their relevance for ICU readmission. Ibrahim et al. [34] averaged the performance of the model over different readmission timeframes, contributing to applicability concerns. Unclear applicability concerns were due to incomplete reporting of inclusion and exclusion criteria.

The overall high risk of bias observed, along with several applicability concerns, suggests that the reported results may not reliably reflect the true performance of the models, thus increasing the risk of suboptimal performance when applied in clinical practice.

Performance metrics, model comparison, and external validation

To evaluate the performance of their model, the selected studies used standard approaches, such as the division into training and testing set, the former used for training and/or tuning the model (e.g., to perform hyperparameters optimisation), and the latter for evaluating the model performance (n = 10, 42; Supplementary Table 2) [29, 30, 32, 35, 39, 47, 50, 53–55]. In two of these studies [54, 55], data were split into training and testing sets using a chronological cutoff, with the earliest observations used for training and the latest for testing. Often, a validation set, used specifically for model tuning, was also present (n = 9, 38; Supplementary Table 2) [36–38, 41–43, 45, 46, 51]. Another standard approach for evaluating ML and DL models is k-fold cross-validation, which partitions the dataset into k subsets (or folds), then trains the model k times, using k-1 folds as the training set, and the remaining fold as the testing set. This approach was used by three studies [33, 34, 44] (13%), which used three, five, and seven folds (Supplementary Table 2). Niu et al. [40] and Cao et al. [31] did not clearly explain their validation approach.

AUROC was reported in all articles apart from Cao et al. [31] and El Hassouny et al. [53], and was generally promising (AUROC > 0.62). Other common performance metrics were precision, F1 score, PRAUC, and recall, while accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity were used less frequently (Supplementary Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 3). Notably, El Hassouny et al. [53] did not report any performance metrics.

Only two studies performed external validation, i.e., evaluated their model performance on independent datasets that were not used for training and/or tuning the model (Supplementary Table 2). Lim et al. [54] trained their model on South Korean patients admitted to the ICU of the SNUH, reaching an AUROC of 0.82 (95% CI = 0.81–0.83) on a testing set derived from the same Korean dataset. However, performance decreased to 0.77 (95% CI = 0.75–0.79) and 0.73 (95% CI = 0.71–0.74) on the US-based MIMIC-III and eICU datasets, respectively. Moazemi et al. [39] achieved an AUROC of 0.80 on the US-based MIMIC-III dataset, which greatly decreased to 0.55 when using data from the German Universitätsklinikum Düsseldorf ICU. The observed lack of external validation is extremely concerning, as it hinders the assessment of the broader applicability and robustness of these models [84], as indicated by the decreased performance reported in the two studies presented above, likely due to overfitting. This may limit the usefulness of these models when applied even in slightly different clinical contexts, and was already suggested by our analysis on risk of bias and applicability concerns.

Fourteen studies (58%, Supplementary Table 5) included a comparison with traditional ML and/or statistical approaches, achieving a median AUROC improvement of 11% (IQR = 3.8% − 19.6%). Notably, the LSTM-based model proposed by Kessler et al. [35] performed as well as the simpler extra trees [85] and random forests [86] models when predicting 2-day ICU readmission in patients in the cardiosurgery recovery and coronary care units from MIMIC-III (AUROCs = 0.777). The highest improvement was observed by the Deep Forest-based model proposed by Khodadadi et al. [50], which showed an improvement of 87.8% over traditional ML approaches such as random forests, support vector machines [87], and XGBoost [88]. However, the outcome definition included all readmissions during the same hospital stay, regardless of the time window. Particularly interesting are the improvements observed by Lim et al. [54]. Their coupled LSTM and LightGBM model achieved a modest AUROC improvement of 4.3% on the dataset used for internal validation, compared to traditional statistical approaches (i.e., SWIFT, APACHE-II, MEWS, and NEWS). However, the improvement increased to 23.3% and 14% when external validation was performed on the MIMIC-III and eICU datasets, respectively. This suggests greater generalization potential of DL approaches compared to more traditional ones.

Comparing performance with previous DL-based models was uncommon, with only five studies [29, 35, 37, 38, 42] (21%) evaluating their models against those developed earlier by Lin et al. [36], making it harder to assess actual improvements between models.

Meta-analysis results

Eleven studies were suitable for meta-analysis (Table 1). We obtained a meta-analytic mean AUROC across all models of 0.79 (95% CI = 0.73–0.85), but observed an extreme heterogeneity (I2 = 99.9%, Fig. 2). This was likely due to the already described high variability in data source, definition of the ICU readmission outcome and timeframe, and deep learning architecture used. To control for these possible sources of heterogeneity, we performed several subgroup meta-analyses.

We observed similar performances in models predicting 30-day readmission (mean AUROC = 0.73; 95% CI = 0.67–0.78, I2 = 99.9%). Whether or not mortality was included in the 30-day readmission definition led to comparable mean AUROC: 0.79, when mortality was included (95% CI = 0.78–0.80) and 0.70 when it was excluded (95% CI = 0.63–0.77, p = 0.09; Supplementary Fig. 4), but considerably different heterogeneity (I2 = 0% and 99.9%, respectively). This may be due to the composite outcome including more cases, thus resulting in a more balanced dataset that is better suited to DL approaches [82], leading to similarly high performance in the two studies using this definition.

When focusing on 30-day readmission and excluding mortality, LSTM achieved the highest mean AUROC (0.72, 95% CI = 0.54–0.86, I2 = 99.8%), followed by BERT- (0.71, 95% CI = 0.59–0.80, I2 = 99.9%), RNN- (0.67, 95% CI = 0.58–0.75, I2 = 99.8%), and GRU-based models (0.64, 95% CI = 0.61–0.67, I2 = 97.5%; Supplementary Fig. 5). This suggests that successfully representing patients’ trajectories may be a critical factor that should be considered when developing prediction models in this field. However, the very high heterogeneity (> 98%), small subgroup sizes (with a maximum of three studies per architecture), and overlapping confidence intervals prevented us from drawing definitive conclusions about architectural superiority. Even when the models were trained on the same data source (i.e., MIMIC-III and/or IV) the heterogeneity remained high (I2 = 99.9%, Supplementary Fig. 6), highlighting a high sensitivity to modelling choice. The mean AUROC remained similar to the overall estimates: 0.72 (95% CI = 0.63–0.79) for MIMIC-III alone, and 0.70 (95% CI = 0.63–0.77) for both MIMIC-III and IV. This is in line with the fact that most of the selected studies used these two data sources.

Models developed on a homogeneous patient population (i.e., same disease and specific ICU type) showed a statistically significant higher AUROC of 0.92 (95% CI = 0.88–0.94, p = 2 × 10−3), and consistently reduced heterogeneity (I2 = 17.1%) (Fig. 2), as previously observed [19].

Discussion

Accurately predicting ICU readmissions is crucial for improving healthcare quality and preventing adverse patient outcomes, including increased morbidity, mortality as well as personal and healthcare costs [2–4].

DL approaches have enabled the development of innovative predictive models that have the potential to outperform traditional prognostic models. We observed here a median AUROC increase of 11% compared to statistical or traditional ML approaches, which could be clinically meaningful. Additionally, we observed that DL models may generalize better to external datasets than traditional statistical approaches.

However, healthcare professionals applying these models as a support for their clinical decision-making process should be aware of a few shortcomings.

First, the lack of reproducibility hinders clinical applicability, and may undermine trust [49]. Technical reproducibility, defined as the ability to fully replicate results, relies on dataset availability and shared code for preprocessing and model training. Although virtually all studies used accessible datasets, only three provided the necessary code. Moreover, even when data and code are available, training the model require substantial computational resources, which may be inaccessible to most clinicians or come with a high price tag. Additionally, DL-based models are computationally demanding to operate, which may further limit their applicability for real-time prediction in resource-constrained settings. Taken together, these limitations can make model deployment difficult, if not impossible.

Second, conceptual reproducibility, reflecting model validity across diverse clinical settings, remains underexplored. Most studies relied on single-centre US-based ICU datasets, exacerbating database-specific bias. They also showed highly variable performance, suggesting a high sensitivity to modelling choice. Only two studies used non-US (South Korean and German) populations, and, to date, none has explored how these US-derived models perform outside the US. Additionally, while recent evidence suggests that integrating data from different sources and geographic regions could reduce overfitting [89], no study exploited multiple datasets. This is despite the availability of pipelines to harmonize the MIMICs and eICU datasets [90], and to merge them with two European ICU data sources [91], i.e., the Amsterdam University Medical Center database (AmsterdamUMCdb) [92], and the High time-Resolution ICU Dataset (HiRID) [93]. Moreover, only two studies performed external validation, and both showed reduced performance, therefore questioning the generalizability of their results. This may stem from a lack of data, and is particularly true for DL models which take advantage of unstructured text, which is not available in datasets such as eICU or AmsterdamUMCdb. In line with recent evidence emphasizing the need for multi-institutional datasets to improve generalizability [49, 89], clinical practitioners should publicly share properly deidentified data from their patients admitted to ICUs. Another explanation may be the complexity of these models, which have a large number of hyperparameters that are heavily optimized on the training data. As a result, they are difficult to deploy and may perform poorly on different datasets [94]. However, even statistical reproducibility, which refers to the stability of results under resampling or minor variations within the same dataset, was often not properly addressed. Half of the studies did not report variance metrics, which led to their exclusion from our meta-analyses.

Third, the high heterogeneity across study setting, including differences in data source, definition of ICU readmission outcome and timeframe, and DL architecture used, combined with the high risk of bias observed, severely hampered our assessment of their pooled performance through meta-analysis. This is further supported by the higher pooled performance achieved by studies focusing on specific ICUs or diagnoses, likely due to reduced heterogeneity in patient characteristics and disease trajectories. This, together with the lack of comparison between DL approaches, contributes to the difficulty in selecting the best-performing model from those available. Clinicians should prioritize models trained on their specific ICU subpopulation, and use more general models only when a tailored option is not available. Novel models capable of effectively handling population heterogeneity, improving predictive accuracy across diverse patient cohorts, are still lacking.

Fourth, the explainability of DL models remains an important challenge, as their “black-box” nature can limit trustworthiness, and hinder clinical adoption [95, 96]. Approaches aimed at making DL methods explainable, such as SHAP [83] and LIME [97], can provide transparent explanations of model outputs, enabling clinicians to better understand and trust DL-based decisions [35]. However, only two studies used SHAP to provide a model explanation. Access to global (SHAP) and/or local (LIME) feature importance would enable clinicians to understand which variables (e.g., static, dynamic, or unstructured data) are the most important when deciding whether a patient should be discharged from the ICU.

This study has some limitations. We included only articles written in English, which may have led to the exclusion of relevant studies during the systematic search. For example, Hu et al. [5] included articles published in Chinese, which we were unable to access. Furthermore, we included only peer-reviewed journal publications, and may have excluded emerging research. Finally, the heterogeneity in study setting limits the strength of the conclusions drawn from the meta-analyses, which should be interpreted with caution. Attempts were made to reduce this heterogeneity through subgroup analyses. However, these efforts resulted in meta-analyses being performed on a reduced subset of studies, thereby undermining the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusions

This systematic review provides the first comprehensive synthesis of the literature on the application of DL models to the task of predicting ICU readmission, offering insights into current approaches and their open challenges, helping healthcare professionals in assessing their clinical applicability. We observed a high risk of bias, considerable variability across study settings, and highly heterogeneous performance. Furthermore, reproducibility was poor, and most approaches relied on US-based datasets, raising concerns about their generalizability, Model explainability was also limited. Taken together, these observations suggest that the quality of the existing studies is currently inadequate, hindering both the evaluation and clinical applicability of DL approaches to this important area.
