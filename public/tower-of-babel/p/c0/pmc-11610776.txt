Exploring Recidivism in Cardiac Surgical ICU: Can this Understanding Translate to Enhanced Patient Outcomes?
Authors: Raj Sahajanandan, A V Varsha, Vinay M. Rao, Ben B. Kurien, Korah Kuruvilla, Roy Thankachen, Madhu A. Philip
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:The need for reinstitution of intensive care unit (ICU) care (“recidivism”) in post-cardiac surgery patients is associated with increased morbidity, mortality, resource use, and healthcare costs. Recidivism is propounded as a quality indicator of ICU care. There is a paucity of studies from India regarding cardiac surgical ICU readmissions, their outcomes, and risk factors.Methods:Nested case-control study including 1,711 consecutive adult patients who underwent cardiac surgery over a two-year period at a tertiary care institute. The patients were grouped into recidival (R) and control (C) groups. The reasons for readmission, outcomes, and predictive risk factors were analyzed.Results:Fifty-four of 1,711 (3.1%) patients were readmitted to ICU, main reasons being cardiac arrhythmias (24, 41%), pericardial effusion (9, 15.2%), and infection (8, 13.5%). Readmission was significantly higher for valvular interventions (39 patients, 24.3%, mitral valve 25 patients) than coronary artery bypass grafting (13, 10.6%), P value 0.003*. On multivariate analysis, EuroSCORE 2 (>5), age, surgical reexploration, postoperative pulmonary complications, and infections were independently associated with a need for ICU readmission. The mortality rate among the readmitted patients was 7.4% compared to 1.4% overall mortality. The mean total postoperative length of stay was significantly longer for recidival patients (17.6 ± 14 days vs 7.6 ± 2.4 days; P < 0.0001).Conclusions:Recidivism is associated with longer hospital stay, suboptimal outcomes as well higher risk of mortality. Postoperative cardiac dysrhythmia was the most common cause of recidivism in our cohort. Early identification of patients at risk for recidivism and timely management of cardiopulmonary complications can translate to better outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

All patients undergoing cardiac surgery invariably have a period of care in the cardiac surgical intensive care unit (CSICU). Since the turn of the century, surgeries of intricate complexities have been performed on the heart and great vessels on patients with various comorbidities with resultant longer postoperative stays in the CSICU. In this era of cost awareness, efforts are made to utilize precious resources like ICU care optimally with fast-tracking protocols and efforts to minimize the ICU stay to expedite availability of ICU beds. However, there is a lack of universal discharge protocols from cardiac ICUs and there is diversity in the step-down care. Both prolonged initial ICU stay and readmission to the CSICU due to deterioration in the clinical condition during the same hospitalization or within 30 days of surgery are linked to a heavy burden of cost as well as increased morbidity and mortality.[12] Recidivism is considered a quality indicator for an ICU care.[3] It is important to identify the predictors of readmission to CSICU so that patient care is not compromised.[4]

After getting the approval of the Institutional Review Board (IRB number 12118/19), we performed a retrospective study to determine the incidence of recidivism, causes, and its predictors at our institution. Secondary objectives were to determine whether recidivism was associated with an increased mortality and/or total length of hospital stay. We hope that the prompt recognition of these predictive factors could contribute to the early identification of the patients at risk for recidivism and prioritize escalation of care, which may result in the effective allocation of limited healthcare resources.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A nested case-control study including 1,711 consecutive adult (>18 years) patients who underwent cardiac surgery followed by an index admission to a 15-bed CSICU in a tertiary care hospital over a two-year period was performed. Twenty patients who died during the index ICU admission were excluded from the study. Those patients who were readmitted to the CSICU within a single hospitalization or within 30 days of the index surgery for a myriad of reasons formed the recidivism group (R). The control group (C) consisted of patients who did not require any readmission to CSICU as the rest of their hospital stay was uneventful. From the control group of 1,637 patients, 241 age- and gender-matched controls were taken for comparative analysis. Cardiac surgeries for diverse etiologies conducted with or without cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), including adult congenital heart disease and redo operations were included. All the cardiac surgeries were performed by the surgical team headed by experienced cardiac surgeons. The surgical approach was median sternotomy in all cases. Standard anesthesia protocols were followed in all cases. All operations were performed under varying hypothermic conditions as mandated by the surgical procedure using antegrade cold crystalloid cardioplegia (del Nido solution). Postoperatively, patients were transferred to the CSICU, staffed round the clock by cardiothoracic residents, cardiac intensivists, and intensive care nurses. The decision to discharge the patient after the initial ICU stay was based on clinical parameters and was taken by the intensivist. Transfer to the ward after the initial ICU stay was based on hemodynamic stability and not protocol driven or time based. Patients with stable vital signs (heart rate between 60 and 100 beats/min and blood pressure systolic blood pressure >90 mm of Hg, mean arterial pressure MAP >65 mm of Hg) without any intravenous ionotropic support were considered hemodynamically stable. The primary discharge from CSICU was always done during the daytime.

The decision for readmission to CSICU was taken after assessment by a consultant surgeon and intensivist. The criteria for readmission to the cardiac surgical ICU included: (a) hemodynamic instability (hypotension defined as systolic BP <90 mm of Hg, MAP <65 mm of Hg, presence of shock and cardiac arrhythmias), (b) need for respiratory support (escalating oxygen requirement, de novo respiratory failure requiring non-invasive ventilation or invasive mechanical ventilation), (c) new onset altered level of consciousness, and/or (d) new onset metabolic derangements such as metabolic or lactic acidosis (pH <7.3, lactate >3 mmol/L).

Data were collected from medical, nursing, and perfusion records and from our hospital electronic database. The preoperative variables included hemoglobin, creatinine, HbA1c values, and comorbidities (obesity, systemic hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, renal impairment, neurological impairment, and peripheral vascular disease). EuroSCORE II for preoperative risk stratification was computed.[56] The NYHA class, primary cardiac pathology, prior need for any ventilatory support, presence of congestive cardiac failure, left ventricular ejection fraction, right ventricular tricuspid plane annular excursion (RV TAPSE), cardiac rhythm, and history of smoking were noted. The nature of the timing of surgery (elective, emergency, urgent), the occurrence of sepsis, and any hemodynamic instability requiring inotropic support were documented. The intraoperative factors noted included aortic cross-clamp and total CPB time. The usage of hemofilters and the lactate levels immediately after bypass were noted. Postoperatively, the kidney disease improving global outcomes (KDIGO) staging was determined based on the creatinine level at 48 hour after surgery.[7] Details of usage of blood and blood products such as leukoreduced red blood cells, platelet concentrates, cryoprecipitate, and fresh frozen plasma (FFP) were collected. Postoperative data such as usage of these blood products, mechanical ventilation days, duration of index ICU stay, need for inotropic support >48 hours after primary surgery, pulmonary complications, tracheostomy, deep sternal wound infection (DSWI), gastrointestinal, neurological, vascular complications, sepsis, rhythm abnormalities, reexploration for any cause, use of IABP, and final surgical outcomes were noted.

“Readmission” (recidivism) was defined as any admission into the ICU for definitive management of postoperative problems during the index admission or a requirement of repeat hospitalization and ICU care within 30 days of index admission for surgery. If there were multiple admissions into the ICU, they were noted as separate instances for the same patient. To ensure reliability and validity of the data, all the data were collected by a single researcher.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 21.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY). Continuous variables were represented as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were represented as number (percentage). Inter-group comparisons were performed using t-test, Mann–Whitney U test, and Chi-square test as appropriate. Logistic regression analysis was performed to determine the predictive factors for readmission to CSICU. A P value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Among the 1,711 adult patients who underwent cardiac surgery, 20 patients expired in the index admission and were excluded from the study. Of the 1,691 patients who were discharged from our ICU during the period of study, 54 (3.1%) patients were readmitted to ICU. Of the readmitted patients, 34 (62.9%) were males and 20 females (37.1%). The mean age of the readmitted patients was 50.7 ± 13.6 years (range 31–74 years) which was comparable to the control group (no readmission 48.3 ± 13 years, range 14 to 69) (P = 0.218). Readmission was significantly higher for valvular interventions (39 patients, 24.3%) compared to coronary artery bypass grafting (13, 10.6%), P value 0.003*.

The mean initial length of ICU stay was higher among the R group (2.8 ± 2.2 days) than the C group (2.2 ± 0.7) patients (P = 0.008). The median interval from primary CSICU discharge to readmission was 6 days (IQR 3–11 days).

There were 59 events of recidivism [Figure 1] among the 54 patients (2 requiring multiple readmissions). The most common cause of recidivism to ICU was cardiac arrhythmias (24 events, 40.6%), with cardiovascular instability. Pericardial effusion leading to low cardiac output, requiring subxiphoid pericardiostomy/reexploration was the second most common reason for readmission (9 events, 15.2% patients). Other reasons for readmission were postoperative sepsis (13.5%), respiratory complications (10.2%), DSWI, neurological complications, uncontrolled diabetes mellitus needing intravenous insulin, and acute kidney injury. All the arrhythmias were managed with electrical/chemical cardioversion and appropriate rate control measures. Fifteen patients had a return to the operating room for remedy of the recidival cause.

The median secondary length of stay (LOS, after readmission) in CSICU was 2 days (IQR 1-4 days). Nineteen patients (35.2%) had a length of stay more than 48 hours during the readmission period. The mean total postoperative LOS was significantly longer for recidival patients (17.6 ± 14 days vs 7.6 ± 2.4 days; P < 0.0001).

Four patients (7.4%) expired during the recidival stay, of which two patients had intractable arrhythmias, one patient had septic shock secondary to DSWI, and one patient had an intracerebral hemorrhage. There were no deaths in the control group.

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first Indian study on predictive factors of recidivism in the cardiac surgical ICU. The demographic profile of our population and hospital-ICU resources available differs considerably from the West. Moreover, in the present era of cost awareness and practice of fast-track protocols and short initial length of ICU stay becoming the norm, the number of ICU readmissions may increase. In this study, we proposed to determine the incidence, profile, outcomes, and predictors (risk factors) for readmission to ICU after cardiac surgery. In our cohort, we report an ICU recidivism rate of 3.1% which is comparable to other studies where it ranges from 2.2% to 3.7%.[89101112] while also being lesser than rates reported in other major studies.[131415]

The most common indication for readmissions in our study was cardiac arrhythmias (40.6%) which was marginally higher than previous studies reporting cardiac complications as the chief cause of readmission to ICU.[1617] Of note, previous few studies had observed pulmonary complications as the primary cause of recidivism in their cohort.[131819] Moreover, in a systematic review involving 25 studies of recidivism, Kimani et al.[20] reported pulmonary complications as the one of the most frequent causes of readmission to ICU. In our cohort, 10.1% of readmissions were due to pulmonary complications. The higher proportion of patients with cardiac arrhythmias compared to pulmonary complications in our cohort as opposed to previously reported studies could be attributed to the high number of patients with rheumatic heart disease operated in our institution which could be different from the profile in other countries. The hesitancy in preemptive usage of amiodarone in management of arrhythmias, due to its interference with oral anticoagulants may be another factor which deserves mention in this context. Moreover, the demographic factors such as younger age and lesser pulmonary comorbidities compared to the other cohorts may also account for the lesser pulmonary complications in our cohort. Effective analgesia and intense respiratory physiotherapy for patients with respiratory complications have been associated with decreased morbidity and hospital mortality and may help considerably to avoid subsequent ICU recidivism.[21] We practice regular radiological as well as sonologic screening of our patients and active early intervention which could also be a reason for the decreased pulmonary complications compared to other studies.

It must be emphasized that premature discharge is often cited as a reason for readmissions.[22] Mean length of initial ICU stay in recidivism group was 2.8 ± 2.2 days while it was 2.2 ± 0.7 days in those not requiring readmission, with trends toward significance. This is in agreement with Cohn et al.[13] whose central finding was that patients most likely to return to ICU were those with the longest period of initial ICU stay. However, Vohra et al.[3] did not identify length of primary hospital stay as a risk factor for readmission. These differences in the observations could be attributable to changes in sample size, individual hospital protocols, the age, comorbidity profile, and primary indications for cardiac surgery in the patients. Early identification based on predictive risk factors and cardiopulmonary comorbidities could help identify patients at a higher risk of ICU readmission. These patients may potentially benefit from a longer period of observation before primary discharge after the cardiac surgery.

Most of the risk factors associated with recidivism in our study are consistent with observations from previously reported studies. Preoperative anemia (Hb <11 g/dL) was a risk factor as most of our patients are small built with reduced RBC mass which is a risk factor for transfusion.[23] Our analysis identified a history of congestive cardiac failure and NYHA class III and IV clinical status as risk factors similar, to those reported by Cohn et al.[13]

Prolonged CPB time and cross-clamp time were identified as significant predictors of readmission. Other studies have shown similar results and found that a CPB time >103 min as predictive factor for recidivism.[24]

Homologous transfusion on pump or in ICU was a significant risk factor. Among the blood products, transfusion of FFP was a significant risk factor. Many of our patients had preoperative arrhythmia such as atrial fibrillation and were on low molecular weight heparin (LWMH) which was stopped 12 hours prior to surgery. LWMH even when stopped 12 hours prior can increase bleeding risk compared to those not on any anticoagulants and this may be the reason for higher FFP transfusions.[25]

Our study also found an association between high preoperative creatinine and recidivism which corresponds to studies done by Giakoumidakis et al.[8] and Bardell et al.[11]

A high preoperative EuroSCORE (>6) was found to be a significant independent risk factor for recidivism which was also identified by other studies.[81218] Therefore, a preoperative evaluation of EuroSCORE for all patients can predict the risk of readmission after initial discharge from the ICU.

Through multivariate analysis, it was also noted that age was an independent significant predictor of recidivism. This is consistent with a few previous studies which showed age as a significant predictor for readmission to ICU.[41924]

Reexploration was found to be a significant risk factor for readmission to ICU similar to the observations by Vohra et al.[3]

Mortality in our recidivism group was 7.4% which was much lesser than in other studies which varied from 17% to 30%.[31824] The mortality rate among patients who required readmission was higher than the overall mortality of 1.4% (24/1711). Our results are in agreement with the available literature that recidivism is an important risk factor for mortality and prolonged ICU stay.

van Diepen et al.[26] had derived and validated a comprehensive clinical prediction model named Alberta Provincial Project for outcome assessments in coronary heart disease (APPROACH) incorporating several perioperative variables such as age, type of cardiac surgery, presence of lung disease, and postoperative complications. The prediction model can be used to identify patients at higher risk for ICU readmissions and provide opportunities for targeted therapeutic interventions in this subset of patients. Our results were quite similar to those of Diepen et al. where the mean CVICU length of stay (19.9 vs 3.3 days, P < 0.001) and in-hospital mortality (14.4% vs 2.2%, P < 0.001) were higher among patients readmitted to the CVICU. However, this model needs revalidation in an external cohort. This calls for a large multicentric trial in India to develop a predictive model for the subcontinent.

The main limitation of the study can be attributed to its retrospective nature and the study being conducted at a single center. The relatively small sample size could account for some of the variables in the multivariate analysis having wide confidence intervals. Selection of controls using a propensity score matching taking into account the other covariates might have improved the strength of the study.

CONCLUSIONS

Given the complexities of cardiac surgeries performed across all age groups, complications may not be entirely preventable. Recidivism is clearly associated with higher mortality and morbidity after cardiac surgery. Identification of high-risk patients and using this information to modify the fast-track protocols may reduce the readmission rates and may improve the overall outcomes. However large multicentric studies or incorporating recidivism as a part of risk prediction scores may help the clinicians to rationalize the resources yet optimizing outcomes.
