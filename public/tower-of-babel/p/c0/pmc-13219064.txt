The erythropoietin gene polymorphism (rs1617640) is associated with retinopathy in type 2 diabetes patients
Authors: Jolanta Szeliga-Krol, Agata Betlejewska, Monika Buraczynska, Wojciech Zaluska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsOur study aimed to evaluate the association between the erythropoietin gene rs1617640 polymorphism and diabetic retinopathy (DR) in diabetes patients.MethodsIn this preliminary retrospective study the genotyping was performed on 860 DNA samples from Caucasian patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). For analyzing the effect of the polymorphism, patients were assigned into three phenotypic subgroups: non-DR (without retinopathy), NPDR (with non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy) and PDR (with proliferative diabetic retinopathy). The rs1617640 polymorphism was analyzed using polymerase chain reaction (PCR)–restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) and direct DNA sequencing procedures.ResultsA statistically significant difference in the polymorphism distribution was observed between T2DM patients with DR (both NPDR and PDR) and those without DR. The minor G allele was associated with the increased risk of DR. In the NPDR subgroup subjects carrying the G allele had 1.53-fold higher risk of developing retinopathy. Similarly, in the PDR subgroup patients carrying the G allele showed almost twofold increased risk of PDR in a dominant model of inheritance.ConclusionOur results demonstrate that in T2DM patients the EPO rs1617460 polymorphism is associated with significantly increased risk of developing DR. This finding can provide a new insight into the role of EPO gene in the pathophysiology of microvascular complications of diabetes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00592-026-02658-9.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is the main microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus and the leading cause of visual impairment and blindness in developed countries [1]. This neurodegenerative complication involves both vascular and neuronal units of the retina. It is characterized by increased vascular permeability, hemostatic abnormalities, increased tissue ischemia and neoangiogenesis [2]. The phenotype of DR is heterogenous. It is classified into two major categories based on severity, less severe non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) and severe proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) [3]. NPDR is an early stage of DR associated with an increase in vascular permeability and capillary occlusion. The earliest clinical signs are microaneurysms, resulting from hypoxia and venous bleeding by the cells of vascular endothelium, and followed by dot and blot intraretinal hemorrhages. A diabetic macular edema (DME) develops in some NPDR patients. PDR is a more advanced form of DR that follows NPDR after longer duration of diabetes. Its main feature induced by severe ischemia is retinal neovascularization with new blood vessels forming in the retina and posterior surface of the vitreous [2]. The major risk factors contributing to the development of DR are diabetes duration, poor glycemic control and hypertension [4, 5]. Several studies reported significant genetic susceptibility to the development of DR and numerous candidate genes have been implicated in the pathogenesis of DR [6–8].

Current research on the mechanisms in DR pathogenesis suggests that retinal blood vessels are damaged by long-lasting hyperglycemia that leads to retinal hypoxia. This in turn stimulates DNA-binding activity of hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF). HIF upregulates the hypoxia-inducible genes including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and erythropoietin (EPO) [9]. Under physiological conditions, EPO is expressed in low levels but it was found to be elevated in the vitreous fluid, aqueous humour and postmortem retinal tissue from the eyes of patients with PDR and DM [10–12]. EPO has an angiogenic potential and could be an important factor involved in stimulating retinal angiogenesis in the course of proliferative retinopathy [13].

Erythropoietin is a 30.4 kDa pleiotropic glycoprotein with a 165 amino acids polypeptide chain. It plays a major role in stimulation of bone marrow stem cells and erythropoiesis [9]. The human EPO gene is located on chromosome 7q22.15 [14]. The most studied polymorphism in the EPO gene is the rs1617640 SNP in the promoter region. It is already known to be associated with development of diabetic retinopathy and nephropathy [15]. The risk allele (T allele) of this SNP is associated with an elevated level of EPO in the vitreous body of human eye [10].

In this study we aimed to investigate the relationship between erythropoietin gene polymorphism (rs1617640) and diabetic retinopathy in subjects with T2DM. To this end, we performed a case–control study in a cohort of 860 T2DM patients.

Methods

Study subjects

Our retrospective case–control study involved 860 unrelated patients with T2DM lasting more than 10 years (mean age 59.4 ± 9.3 years). All patients recruited from the Medical University of Lublin Hospital were Caucasian of Polish origin. Before inclusion into the study, a written informed consent was provided by all subjects in accordance with standards of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki (version 2013). The detailed protocol of the study was approved by the institutional ethics committee. The same patient cohort with a well defined disease phenotype was involved in our earlier study [16].

Type 2 diabetes was diagnosed on the basis of American Diabetes Association criteria (ADA 2014). One or more of the following conditions were required for diagnosis: the classic symptoms of hyperglycemia (polyuria, polydipsia, loss of weight), fasting plasma glucose level > 7 mmol/l or random level > 11 mmol/l and the use of insulin or oral hypoglycemic medication. All subjects underwent complete physical examination that included fasting plasma glucose, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), full lipid profile, albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR), albumin excretion rate (AER) and body mass index (BMI). This was a cross-sectional study, thus the patients were not matched for demographic or medical characteristics.

Upon enrollment in the study all patients were evaluated for renal function and ophthalmological status. Their medical records were reviewed, additional tests were performed where needed and diagnoses verified.

Diabetic retinopathy was initially diagnosed by independent ophthalmologists in 328 patients who underwent a comprehensive eye examination including corrected visual acuity, slit-lamp biomicroscopic examination and dilated color fundus photography (Topcon TRC50-DX, Tokyo, Japan). After enrollment in the study, findings from fundoscopic examination were assessed and verified by a retinal specialist. The Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) classification system was used for diagnosis and determination of the stage of DR (ETDRS 1991). The exclusion criteria for DR during the first ophthalmological evaluation were other ocular disorders e.g. branch retinal venous occlusion, glaucoma, cancer.

For analyzing the effect of EPO gene polymorphism, patients were assigned into three phenotypic subgroups:non-DR: patients without retinopathy (n = 532): no clinical signs of retinopathy for at least 10 years of DM duration;NPDR: patients with non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (n = 194): microaneurysms and at least one of the following: retinal hemorrhages, soft or hard exudates, cotton spots;PDR: patients with proliferative diabetic retinopathy (n = 134): at least one of the following signs: neovascularization, vitreous hemorrhage, fibrovascular proliferation in at least one eye.

Determination of rs1617640 genotype

Genomic DNA was extracted from venous blood using a Qiagen DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen Poland, Warsaw, Poland). DNA purity and concentration were determined in Nano Drop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA. USA). The EPO rs1617640 was determined by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) followed by restriction fragment length polymorphism procedure (PCR–RFLP). The following primers were used in this assay: forward 5′-GTCTTGGTCTCCTGCTCTGG-3′ and reverse 5′-GTCCATTGTGCAGGACACAC-3′. Genomic DNA (200 ng) was amplified in a 30 μl reaction. The initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min preceded 35 cycles consisting of denaturation at 95 °C, annealing at 60 °C and extension at 72 °C (1 min each) and the final extension step at 72 °C for 7 min. The 207 bp amplicon (10 μl) was digested with 5 units of BseRI restriction endonuclease (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C for 12 h. Resulting DNA fragments were resolved by electrophoresis in 3% agarose gel. The observed fragments were 207 bp for a wild type allele (T) and 187 bp and 20 bp for the mutant allele (G). The genotyping results were validated by using blind DNA duplicates (96 samples). Additionally, 20 random samples for each genotype were analyzed by automated sequencing in CEQ 8000 Genetic Analysis System (Beckman Coulter). The rate of concordance between assays was 100%.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of collected data was carried out using a statistical software SPSS version 18.0 for Windows (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL. USA). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used for normality. The values of normally distributed variables for the baseline characteristics are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or numbers and percentages where appropriate. Differences in discrete and continuous variables between groups were evaluated by an unpaired Student’s t-test and Mann–Whitney test. The genotype and allele frequencies were attained by direct count. Potential deviation from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium in the genotyping results was assessed in a Fisher exact test. The EPO rs1617640 polymorphism distribution in patients with and without DR was compared using a Pearson chi-square test of independence. The strength of the observed associations of studied SNP with clinical phenotype (non-DR, NPDR, PDR) was estimated by computing the odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI), with a non-risk allele/genotype considered a reference. Variables showing significant associations were analyzed in multivariate logistic regression model used to find independent predictors of DR risk in T2DM patients. ORs were adjusted based on possible confounders such as duration of diabetes, BMI, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol and HbA1c levels. The level of statistical significance for all tests was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The patient cohort used in this study consisted of a total of 860 subjects with T2DM with diabetic retinopathy (n = 328) and without it (n = 532). Discussion

The reports concerning a role of genetic variants in the EPO gene in diabetic retinopathy in T2DM are scarce, especially in Caucasians. In the present case–control study we have investigated the possible association of the rs1617640 SNP in the EPO gene with susceptibility to diabetic retinopathy in T2DM patients. This common variant is known to have functional consequences [10, 19].

In this preliminary study we demonstrated that rs1617640 SNP in the EPO gene is significantly associated with diabetic retinopathy in type 2 diabetes. The association analysis revealed that the minor G allele had a 1.3-fold increased risk of developing NPDR. In the PDR group, the G allele had a 1.6-fold increased risk of retinopathy and the GG genotype was associated with a 1.9-fold increased risk of developing PDR, when a dominant mode of inheritance was assumed. The similar association of the minor G allele with DR, also in a dominant model, was observed in the study from Northern India [20]. In a case–control study of 217 Slovenian subjects with PDR the patients with GG genotype had a 1.6-fold increased risk of developing PDR when compared to subjects with the major T allele [21]. Other studies of the rs1617640 variant in type 2 diabetes also revealed higher risk of DR in the carriers of the G allele. In one study of T2DM patients, the GG genotype of rs1617640 variant was significantly associated with the risk of DR [9]. In both studies the rs1617640 variant showed the effect on susceptibility to PDR only when the recessive mode of inheritance was considered. In a study of 397 Chinese diabetic retinopathy patients, those with the G allele had the highest risk of diabetic retinopathy [22].

There are also some contradictory results reported concerning the effect of rs1617640 polymorphism in the EPO gene on diabetic retinopathy. In a study of European-American population the authors implicated not the minor, but major (T) allele as the risk allele for PDR in combination with end-stage renal disease [10]. Similarly, in a group of 320 subjects with DR, the GT genotype of rs1617640 had a significant protective association with DR and the T allele had a significant risk association with DR [23]. In a Chinese cohort of 792 individuals with T2DM, no association was found of rs1617640 polymorphism with diabetic retinopathy (either NPDR or PDR) [24]. It can be suggested that the same allele confers risk in one population while it has a protective effect in another [9]. Thus, different ethnic groups might show opposite results with the same polymorphism, due to differences in the genetic background and environment.

The mechanism by which the rs1617640 SNP in the EPO gene confers susceptibility to diabetic retinopathy is not entirelly clear and needs to be explored. Also, it should be clarified whether the studied EPO gene polymorphism plays a protective or detrimental role. The EPO gene encodes a potent angiogenic factor expressed in the retina and kidney. Ramus et al. stated that while VEGF alone does not complete angiogenesis, the binding of EPO/EPOR has a potential to induce new vessel formation under physiological and pathological conditions. In addition to angiogenic activity, EPO immunoreactivity was observed in endothelial cells forming microvascular cavities and stromal cells of fibrovascular membranes in Slovenian T2DM patients. The number of EPO positive cells was most pronounced in the carriers of GG and GT genotypes [21]. In humans high concentrations of EPO in vitreous body are strongly associated with PDR [25]. Also, the prevalence and severity of PDR increase with increasing levels of EPO. In the study of Tong et al. the EPO rs1617640-T allele was associated with a 25-fold higher promoter activity when compared to the G allele, thus suggesting that this variant plays a significant functional role in the EPO expression. The high levels of erythropoietin may have detrimental effect in patients with T2DM. They stimulate migration, proliferation and angiogenesis in vascular endothelial cells exposed to hypoxia [10]. It was reported that increased erythropoietin concentrations were associated with PDR characterized by excessive vascular growth [26].

Our results have to be interpreted with caution, since the study has some limitations. This preliminary study was restricted to one EPO-related SNP, therefore the effect of other polymorphisms in the EPO gene on the development of DR cannot be excluded. Also, the determinations of EPO levels or its enzymatic activity were not performed in the studied T2DM subjects. The numbers of NPDR and PDR patients were relatively small and a larger study should be performed to confirm the observed associations. To explore the potential role of EPO in the development and progression of DR, comprehensive studies with other variants of EPO gene, in large groups from different populations are needed. Also, the EPO gene polymorphisms, involved in retinopathy should be analyzed at the functional level.

In conclusion, our study is the first to demonstrate that the rs1617640 SNP in the EPO gene is significantly associated with increased risk of diabetic retinopathy in Polish population of T2DM patients. A novel observation of this association as well as its potential clinical significance need to be confirmed in further, larger studies.
