Targeting sialic acid metabolism: a therapeutic strategy against gastric cancer driven by WZ35
Authors: Boxiang Hong, Ying Wang, Xinran Feng, Lihan Wei, Yichen Huang, Xiawei Zhang, Longyuchen Xu, Pinyan Jin, Liyuan Huang, Rui Li, Xinyu Wu, Zhi Li, Hangyu Ye, Tongke Chen, Xiaoyan Wang, Hao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlycolytic reprogramming is closely associated with the occurrence and progression of gastric cancer. Specifically, the energy derived from glucose metabolism and the cellular proteins by its intermediate products influence gastric cancer development. However, as an important branch of glucose metabolism, sialic acid metabolism and its mediated sialylation modifications remain insufficiently studied in gastric cancer, and their specific relationship with malignant tumor progression requires further exploration. This study employed a multi‑omics approach, integrating metabolomics, single‑cell RNA sequencing, and bulk RNA sequencing analyses, to investigate the metabolic landscape of gastric cancer and its associated alterations. The results indicated that sialic acid is a characteristic metabolite in malignant gastric cancer tissues. It modulates biological functions such as immune response, proliferative activity, and metabolic remodeling within gastric cancer tissues by influencing sialylation modifications. Furthermore, we identified the drug WZ35, which can inhibit the malignant proliferation of gastric cancer by targeting both sialic acid metabolism and sialylated protein modifications. We put forward a conjecture that the metabolism and modification of sialic acid promote the malignant development of gastric cancer, and we discovered that the drug WZ35 has an inhibitory effect on the sialic acid metabolism of gastric cancer.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Gastric cancer, Sialic acid, Tumor metabolism, Multi-omics analysis, Glycometabolism, Glycosylation, WZ35

Introduction

Gastric cancer ranks among the most prevalent malignant neoplasms worldwide, characterized by elevated incidence and mortality rates [1]. Its pathogenesis is linked to a multitude of factors, including alterations in molecular subtypes, modifications within the immune microenvironment, and the functional dynamics of gastric cancer stem cells [2–4]. Recent research has increasingly underscored the significance of metabolic alterations in facilitating the progression of gastric cancer [5, 6]. Nonetheless, a comprehensive understanding of the metabolic profile associated with gastric cancer remains insufficiently elucidated. This study seeks to employ metabolomics to analyze the metabolic landscape of gastric cancer and to identify critical metabolic pathways that contribute to its malignant progression.

Glucose metabolism constitutes a fundamental energy supply pathway within the body, intricately associated with various organs and tissues [7–9]. Moreover, it serves as a pivotal component of cellular metabolic networks, influencing the reprogramming of fatty acid metabolism and amino acid metabolism to facilitate cancer progression [10]. For instance, intermediates of glucose metabolism, such as citrate and α-ketoglutarate, act as precursors for de novo fatty acid synthesis [11], while key products of amino acid metabolism, such as serine, can modulate glucose metabolism by influencing PKM2 protein levels. Additionally, intermediates in the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway participate in N-glycosylation modifications, which are integral to the activation and transduction of signaling pathways, including Jak-Stat, PI3K-Akt-mTOR, and RAS-MEK-ERK [12].

Glycosylation constitutes a significant post-translational modification that governs cellular homeostasis and the microenvironment. The two primary forms of glycosylation, N-glycosylation and O-glycosylation, are recognized for their impact on protein folding, intracellular transport, and intercellular signaling [13]. Among these modifications, sialic acid is particularly noteworthy for its involvement in tumor signal transduction, immune suppression by adjacent immune cells, and tumor cell metastasis [14–16]. It is evident that sialic acid metabolism is pivotal in various stages of cancer progression. Nonetheless, the precise regulatory mechanisms of sialic acid metabolism within tumor cells require further elucidation.

The sialic acid metabolic pathway initiates with the synthesis of sialic acid precursors in the cytoplasm, followed by their activation to CMP-sialic acid in the nucleus. CMP-sialic acid is then transported to the Golgi apparatus, where specific sialyltransferases catalyze its attachment to the terminal ends of glycan chains on proteins or lipids, forming cell surface sialylation modifications. These sialylated structures can ultimately be hydrolyzed and recycled by sialidases. In tumors, sialic acid metabolism is frequently hyperactive, leading to a significant increase in cell surface sialylation levels. This elevated sialylation directly promotes tumor cell invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance. Concurrently, it forms a dense “glycan shield” that interacts with inhibitory receptors on immune cells, thereby facilitating tumor immune evasion.

WZ35 is a compound derived from the natural product curcumin, specifically 1-(4-hydroxy-3-methoxyphenyl)-5-(2-nitrophenyl)-5–1,4-diene-3-one. It has been demonstrated to influence glucose metabolism in gastric cancer cells via the ROS-YAP-JNK signaling pathway [17]. Furthermore, WZ35 exhibits inhibitory effects on liver cancer, colorectal cancer, breast cancer, and various other cancer types [18–23].

This study aims to employ a combination of metabolomics, single-cell and bulk RNA sequencing analyses to elucidate the metabolic landscape of gastric cancer. We will investigate the role of sialylation within the glycosylation pathway in the progression of gastric cancer malignancy. Our findings suggest that WZ35 may impede gastric cancer progression by diminishing tumor cell glycosylation levels and sialic acid metabolism.

Materials and methods

Human tissue collection

Gastric adenocarcinoma specimens were collected by qualified medical staff at the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University. A total of 10 gastric adenocarcinoma patients were included in the study. All patients were non-pregnant, and there was no age or gender bias in the selection.

Metabolomics analysis

Samples were processed in a 50 Hz centrifuge at 4 °C, using 300 μL of cold extraction solvent (acetonitrile/methanol/H2O = 2:2:1) with 0.5 μg/mL L-phenylalanine (Beyotime ST1469) as an internal standard. Protein was precipitated by centrifugation, and 250 μL of the supernatant was collected. The metabolites were dried in a vacuum concentrator and re-suspended in 10% methanol. Equal amounts of the extracted samples were mixed to prepare quality control (QC) samples. The liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis was performed using an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system combined with an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Chromatographic separation was carried out on an Acquity HSS T3 column (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) (1.8 μm × 2.1 mm × 100 mm) with a 20-minute gradient. The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min, and the column temperature was 35 °C. For positive ion mode, mobile phase A was H2O with 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B was methanol with 0.1% formic acid. For negative ion mode, mobile phase A was 6.5 mM NH4HCO3 solution, and mobile phase B was methanol. The mass spectrometer operated in both positive and negative ion modes using electrospray ionization (ESI). Full scans (m/z 70–1050) were performed at a resolution of 120,000, with an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 4 × 105 ions and a maximum ion injection time of 50 ms. Data-dependent MS/MS data were collected in the highest-speed mode with a cycle time of 0.8 seconds. The resolution was 30,000, the AGC target was 5 × 104 ions, and the maximum injection time was 54 ms. Normalized collision energy for stepped high-energy collision dissociation (HCD) was set at 15, 30, and 50%. Compound Discoverer 3.0 was used to extract peaks. The peak tables and mgf-formatted MS2 files were uploaded to mzCloud, HMDB, and MassBank servers for metabolite identification. According to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) guidelines, identified metabolites were classified into levels 1–3 and unknown categories. Non-novel metabolites (level 2 and above) were extracted for statistical analysis, enrichment analysis, pathway analysis, and causal analysis using Metaboanalyst6.0 [24]. Data normalization was performed on both intra-group metabolites and inter-group samples as per the standardization method provided by the website.

GEO database

The GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), maintained by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), is one of the largest gene chip repositories in the world. Transcriptomic data for normal and tumor tissues are available on this platform. We collected RNA-seq data from 26 primary gastric cancer samples and 26 normal control tissue samples from the GSE51575 dataset, along with matched clinical pathology information. Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis was conducted, and volcano and heat maps were visualized using the built-in R packages ggplot and pheatmap in R Studio. The GEO database is publicly accessible, so local ethical committee approval is not required.

Bulk RNA-Sequencing

Raw RNA-seq data were processed and normalized as described in the relevant sections. Differential expression analysis was performed using limma R package. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change ≥ 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Volcano plots were generated using the ggplot2 R package to visualize the distribution of DEGs based on log2 fold change and -log10 (FDR). Heatmaps of the top DEGs were created with the pheatmap R package using normalized expression values, with clustering applied to both genes and samples.

To explore the biological functions and pathways of the DEGs, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were performed using the clusterProfiler R package. Enriched terms with a p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted using GSEA software (version 4.x) or the clusterProfiler R package, with genes pre-ranked based on log2 fold change or signal-to-noise ratio. The Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) was used as the reference gene set collection. Gene sets with an absolute normalized enrichment score (NES) >1 and a nominal p-value < 0.05 (or FDR < 0.25) were considered significantly enriched.

KEGG, GSEA, and GO pathway enrichment analysis

To identify significantly enriched pathways, biological processes, and molecular mechanisms, we performed pathway enrichment analysis on the DEG expression matrix using the clusterProfiler package with a p-value cutoff of 0.05. Gene and protein functions were annotated and described using the KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) and GO (Gene Ontology) databases, and pathway enrichment analysis was further conducted using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA).

scRNA-seq data quality control:

RNA-seq data from the GSE183904 project were obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), selecting 26 gastric cancer samples. The Seurat package in R was used to integrate the samples using anchor-based methods, and low-quality cells were filtered out. Excluded cells included those with fewer than 500 detected genes, those with fewer than three detected genes in three or fewer cells, or those with mitochondrial content greater than 20%, ribosomal content lower than 3%, or hemoglobin content lower than 0.1%. The core cells for further analysis were selected. Data were reduced using the Harmony algorithm, and principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on the first 20 principal components. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was used for overall dimensionality reduction of the samples.

Cell-Cell Communication

Cell-cell communication in gastric cancer cells and other cell types was analyzed using the CellChat package (https://github.com/jinworks/CellChat) [25].

scMetabolism analysis

The scRNA-seq data for epithelial cells from the gastric cancer samples were extracted for further analysis. KEGG pathway analysis was performed using the R package scMetabolism (metabolism.type = “KEGG”).

Gastric cancer cell lines

The human gastric cancer cell line BGC-823 was obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, GIBCO C11875500BT) or Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium (RPMI-1640, GIBCO C11995500BT), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (SIGMA F7524) and 1 mM L-glutamine (Beyotime C0212). The cells were maintained in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator, with media changes every two days. When cells reached 80–90% confluence, they were digested using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution (GIBCO, 25,200–072) and passaged at a 1:3 ratio.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) proliferation assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in a 96-well plate. Once the cells reached 50–60% confluence, the original medium was replaced with fresh medium containing different concentrations of the drug. In the CCK-8 assay, the following concentrations were used: sialic acid at 6.25 nM, 16.25 nM, and 31.25 nM, and WZ35 at 2.5 μM, 7.5 μM, and 12.5 μM. After 24 hours of treatment, the medium was replaced with 100 μL of medium containing 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO₂ for 1.5 hours. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured every 30 minutes using a SpectraMax 190 microplate reader (Molecular Devices).

Cell apoptosis assay

In the experimental design, the concentration of WZ35 was 2.5 μM. Cell apoptosis was detected using the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The original medium was collected, and cells were digested with trypsin without EDTA and collected into a centrifuge tube. After centrifugation, cells were washed twice with PBS and centrifuged again (2000 rpm for 5 minutes at 4°C). Cells were resuspended in 195 μL binding buffer and stained with 5–10 μL of Annexin V-FITC. After 5 minutes of incubation at room temperature in the dark, 10 μL of PI was added, and cells were incubated for an additional 10 minutes in the dark. Apoptosis was analyzed by flow cytometry using the FITC channel for Annexin V-FITC and the PI channel for PI staining. The gate was set using unstained cells, and compensation was adjusted using single-staining controls.

Protein mass spectrometry

Analysis was performed using LC-MS/MS to compare the gastric cancer WZ35 treatment group with the control group. Mass spectrometry coupled with an electrospray ionization (ESI) interface was operated in positive ion mode. Chromatographic separation was achieved using an Agilent 1200 series HPLC system with a column (50 mm × 2.0 mm) under isocratic elution with a mobile phase consisting of 0.1% formic acid in water and methanol (50:50, v/v), at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min and a column temperature of 40 °C. The analysis was conducted in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode with an ion spray voltage of + 5500 V and a temperature of 550 °C.

The SMPDB and RaMP-DB databases

SMPDB is a highly specialized visualization knowledge base for pathways, focusing on providing static yet interactive pathway maps enriched with biochemical details such as metabolite structures and subcellular localizations. It is primarily designed for in-depth study and presentation of specific pathways. RaMP-DB (https://rampdb.nih.gov/) [26] is a powerful integrated relational database that does not generate pathway diagrams itself. Instead, it consolidates and standardizes data from multiple sources—including KEGG, Reactome, WikiPathways, and SMPDB (https://smpdb.ca/) [27, 28]—and is tailored for high‑throughput omics research. Its core value lies in offering robust computational biology tools such as gene‑metabolite identifier mapping and pathway enrichment analysis to support batch processing and interpretation of large‑scale datasets.

Cell labeling method for zebrafish model studies

A plasmid expressing the green fluorescent protein (GFP) gene was transiently transfected into the cells. Following transfection, 10 nL of a cell suspension at a concentration of 3 × 107 cells/mL was injected to establish the labeled zebrafish model. The fluorescent construct used was the Cas9-GFP lentiviral plasmid from Sigma-Aldrich (Sigma-Aldrich CAS9GFPV). This plasmid utilizes the EF1α promoter to drive the expression of a Cas9-GFP fusion protein, with the two moieties linked by a 2A self-cleaving peptide. This design ensures the co-expression of Cas9 and GFP as separate proteins after translation, facilitating the monitoring of transduction efficiency and Cas9 expression levels via fluorescence. The vector is built on a lentiviral backbone and carries an ampicillin resistance gene, enabling the production of high-titer lentiviral particles for gene editing applications requiring stable Cas9 expression in mammalian cells.

Zebrafish model studies

The labeled cells were counted and adjusted to a concentration of 3 × 107 cells/mL. Using a microinjection system, 10 nL of the cell suspension was injected into the yolk sac of zebrafish at 2 days post-fertilization (dpf), resulting in approximately 300 cells per zebrafish. After injection, the zebrafish were maintained in a biochemical incubator at 35 °C. In the experimental design, the concentration of sialic acid was 0.05 mM. After 24 hours, zebrafish with consistent fluorescence intensity were screened under a stereoscopic fluorescence microscope and grouped according to the experimental design, with 10 zebrafish per group. A negative control (NC) group and a sialic acid treatment group were established, with each treatment administered via water immersion, which corresponds to the clinical oral administration route in the zebrafish model. Fluorescence imaging was performed from 3 to 5 (dpf), and the differences in fluorescence intensity among the different groups were analyzed. The observation equipment used was a Zeiss microscope from Germany.

Measurement of Oxygen Consumption Rate (OCR) and Extracellular Acidification Rate (ECAR)

Cells (BGC-823) were seeded at a density of 15,000 cells per well and cultured at 37 °C for 16 hours. The sensor cartridge was hydrated in XF assay medium for 16 hours. For the mitochondrial respiration assay (Oxygen Consumption Rate, OCR), a mitochondrial stress test was performed by sequential injection of 1 μM oligomycin, 1 μM carbonyl cyanide p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP), and a mixture of 0.5 μM rotenone and 0.5 μM antimycin A. For the glycolytic function assay (Extracellular Acidification Rate, ECAR), 10 mM glucose, 1 μM oligomycin, and 100 mM 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) were injected sequentially, and measurements were taken using a Seahorse extracellular flux analyzer. The OCR measurement parameter was the oxygen consumption rate, and the ECAR measurement parameter was the extracellular acidification rate. The instrument used was the Seahorse XF Pro Analyzer from Agilent Technologies.

Quantitative real-time PCR

In the experimental design, the concentrations of WZ35 was 2.5 μM. Total RNA was extracted from tumor tissues or cell lines using AG RNAex Pro Reagent(Accurate AG21102), and RNA concentration was measured by determining the A260/A280 ratio. Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized using 5X Evo M-MLV RT Master Mix (Accurate AG11706). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed on a StepOnePlus PCR Systemusing (Thermo Fisher A28574) 2X SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix. The relative transcript levels of target genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with β-actin serving as the endogenous control. Transwell

Cell migration and invasion assays were performed using Transwell chambers (Beyotime FTW001-6Ins). In the experimental design, the concentrations of WZ35 and sialic acid were 2.5 μM and 6.25 nM, respectively. For the migration assay, 1 × 105 cells suspended in serum-free medium were seeded into the uncoated upper chamber. For the invasion assay, 7 × 104 cells in serum-free medium were plated into the upper chamber pre-coated with Matrigel. The lower chamber was filled with 600 μL of culture medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) as a chemoattractant. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 hours, cells that had migrated or invaded to the lower surface of the membrane were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 minutes. Cells in five randomly selected fields (100× magnification) were counted under a light microscope using ImageJ software. Optical microscope information for Transwell chamber imaging: Nikon Ti2 Inverted Microscope.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.1) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.0). Differences in categorical variables among subgroups were analyzed by the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Bulk RNA sequencing analyses reveals the role of glycosylation in the malignancy progression of gastric cancer

To investigate the specific factors influencing the progression of malignancy in gastric cancer, we utilized the dataset GSE51575 from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (accessible at https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE51575). This dataset comprises gastric cancer tissues and adjacent normal tissues from 26 patients diagnosed with gastric cancer. Following initial data preprocessing, we employed volcano plots and heatmaps to visualize the differentially expressed genes between the two groups (Fig. 1A–B). In gastric cancer tissues, the expression levels of genes such as osteopontin (SPP1), hyaluronan-degrading protein (CEMIP), cysteine protease inhibitor (CST1), signal transduction molecule (LY6E), and extracellular adhesion factor (CEACAM6) are elevated. These genes are implicated in tumor proliferation, migration, immune regulation, and other biological functions [29–37].

The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses demonstrated that, relative to the control group, the gastric cancer patient cohort exhibited significant enrichment in functional pathways, including protein O-linked glycosylation and O-glycan processing. Additionally, we noted that the biological function of glycosylation modification was repeatedly enriched in the transcriptomic data (Fig. 1C–F). Results from Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further indicated elevated levels of glycosylation activity, glycosyltransferase activity, and sialic acid synthesis in gastric cancer patients (Fig. 1G1–4).

Based on a comprehensive analysis of these findings, we propose that there is an upregulation of glycosylation levels in gastric cancer tissues, contributing to tumor malignancy progression.

Sialic acid metabolism as a key factor influencing gastric cancer

To elucidate the metabolic profile of gastric cancer patients and identify metabolic pathways implicated in the progression of gastric cancer malignancy, we performed a non-targeted metabolomics analysis on gastric cancer tissues and corresponding adjacent non-cancerous tissues from 10 matched gastric cancer patients. A heatmap was generated to display the top 50 differential metabolites (Fig. 2A). Based on the results of the volcano plot of the differential metabolites (Fig. 2A)，we found that gastric cancer tissues exhibited elevated levels of acetylated metabolites, such as Acetylhomoserine, S-Acetyldihydrolipoamide, and N-Acetylglutamine, in comparison to adjacent non-cancerous tissues (Fig. 2B-D).

Sialic acid most common modification is the O-acetyl­ation of carbons 4, 7, 8 and 9 [38]. The difference in acetylation modifications also confers a certain level of biological functional diversity. Combining this with the bioinformatics analysis mentioned earlier, we hypothesize that high levels of acetylated metabolites, may indicate an upregulation of sialic acid metabolism within gastric cancer cells.

To undertake a comprehensive analysis of the characteristic metabolic pathways within the gastric cancer microenvironment, we conducted enrichment analyses on the differential metabolites using the KEGG, Small Molecule Pathway Database (SMPDB), and Relational Database of Metabolomics Pathways (RaMP-DB) databases. The KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the pathways of amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism were significantly upregulated. (Fig. 2E). And the metabolism of sialic acid is an important part of the overall metabolic pathway of aminoglycosides and ribonucleosides. The enrichment results of SMPDB and RaMP-DB further demonstrate the Amino Suger Metabolism, which also supports the findings of KEGG. At the same time, we also noticed the diversity of amino acid metabolism in the metabolic microenvironment, which still requires further in-depth exploration by other studies. (Fig. 2F-G).

In gastric cancer cells, glycosylation modification serves as a crucial intermediary, connecting metabolic traits to functional attributes. Notably, common glycosylation modifications encompass increased branching of N-glycans and truncation of O-glycans [39]. This observation prompted us to concentrate on a specific aspect of glycosylation: sialic acid metabolism. Traditionally, sialic acids are perceived as a composite of 2-keto acids, deoxy sugars, and amino sugars [40]. Their distinctive biological properties facilitate roles such as intracellular protein transport, mucin function, and cation transport. Various sialyltransferases are capable of mediating the sialylation of both N- and O-glycans [41]. In the context of gastric cancer, sialic acid metabolism may be attributed to the synergistic effects of multiple metabolic pathways.

Consequently, we note that the metabolomic KEGG enrichment analysis is centered on amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, whereas the bulk RNA-sequencing enrichment results highlight O-glycosylation modifications. The amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism encompasses both sialic acid metabolism and sialylation, which involve both O-glycosylation and N-glycosylation. Thus, the two sets of analytical findings are not contradictory; rather, they provide complementary and deeper insights into each other.

In our analysis of the metabolic pathways involving aminoglycosides and nucleosides, we identified an upregulation of N-Acetylneuraminic acid, concomitant with a downregulation of N-Acetyl-D-mannosamine 6-phosphate and Cytidine 5’-monophosphate-N-acetylneuraminic acid (Fig. 2H–J). N-Acetylneuraminic acid, a pivotal metabolite in the sialylation processes of glycosylation pathways, plays a significant role in cell signaling, immune regulation, and the progression of malignancy in cancer cells [14, 39, 42]. Furthermore, it has been documented that elevated levels of N-Acetyl-D-mannosamine 6-phosphate can inhibit the synthesis of N-Acetylneuraminic acid [43]. This also provides indirect evidence that the metabolism of sialic acid is upregulated in gastric cancer tissues.

Our study revealed that glutamine metabolism underwent significant changes in the metabolic microenvironment of gastric cancer. (Fig.S 1A-C). As a subset of carbohydrate metabolism, glutathione metabolism plays a crucial role in mediating various redox pathways within gastric cancer cells. The potential metabolic relationship and interaction between these two pathways warrant further investigation.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis of communication mechanisms between subpopulations in gastric cancer

We conducted a further analysis of the data utilizing the single-cell RNA sequencing dataset GSE150290 (accessible at https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE150290). During the preprocessing phase, red blood cells, as well as mitochondrial and ribosomal genes, were excluded from the dataset. Dimensionality reduction was achieved through the application of Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), leading to the identification of 18 distinct cell clusters (Fig. 3A). Through the examination of differential gene expression among these clusters (Fig. 3B), we categorized them into five principal cell subpopulations: Endothelial cells (characterized by VWF, ENG, CLDN5, CDH5), Epithelial cells (characterized by KRT18, EPCAM, KRT19, PROM1, ALDH1A1), Fibroblasts (characterized by DCN, FBLN1, TAGLN, COL3A1, COL1A1, COL6A1), Lymphocytes (characterized by CD3D, CD27, CD79A, MS4A1, CD52, CD37, BLNK, MZB1, SDC1), and Monocytes and Macrophages (CD68, CD14) (Fig. 3C).

In the existing literature, it is widely believed that gastric cancer cells are differentiated from epithelial cells [44, 45]. Subsequently, we conducted a CellChat analysis to evaluate the frequency and intensity of intercellular communication among various cell subpopulations (Fig. 3D). In the resulting diagram, the thickness of the arrows between cell populations indicates the strength of communication. We observed strong communication between epithelial cell subpopulations and lymphocyte subpopulations. However, the specific mode of communication remains to be further investigated. Through examining the signaling interactions mediated by ligands and receptors across distinct cell types (Fig. 3E), we identified a robust reciprocal interaction between epithelial cell subpopulations and lymphocyte subpopulations, within the MIF signaling pathway (Fig. 3F). Here, although epithelial cells were observed to interact with lymphocytes via MIF signaling, the lymphocytes did not appear to reciprocate this interaction. This phenomenon warrants further discussion. The MIF signaling pathway is implicated in several critical functions, including shielding tumor cells from macrophage-mediated cytotoxicity, facilitating tumor cell metastasis, and promoting macrophage differentiation [46–48].

The findings suggest that within the immune microenvironment of gastric cancer, cancer cells impose immunosuppressive effects on adjacent immune cells to sustain their proliferative and metastatic capabilities. However, the specific underlying mechanisms of intervention and effect remain to be further explored.

Upregulation of the sialic acid metabolism pathway contributes to distinct biological functional differences in gastric cancer

As also mentioned above, we regarded epithelial cells as gastric cancer cells for further analysis. We focused on the epithelial cell subgroup for further analysis to investigate the metabolic and biological functional characteristics. Using the UMAP algorithm for nonlinear dimensionality reduction with a resolution parameter set at 0.5, we identified 10 epithelial cell subsets (Fig. 4A). Differential gene expression among these clusters was analyzed using heatmaps and volcano plots (Fig. 4B–C). Our findings revealed an upregulation in the expression levels of several genes associated with tumor malignancy progression, including REG3A, FAM3D, MUC6, and S100P [49–52]. Based on the differential gene expression profiles, we categorized the ten cell clusters into high malignancy (clusters 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6) and low malignancy groups (clusters 0, 7, 8, 9) (Fig. 4D).

Utilizing the scmetabolism package, we conducted a comparative analysis of metabolic differences between subgroups characterized by high and low malignancy (Fig. 4E) (red indicates high expression, and blue indicates low expression). The analysis revealed that the high malignancy subgroup demonstrated elevated levels of amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism relative to the low malignancy group. Additionally, there was an upregulation in the amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism metabolism pathways (Fig. 4F), corroborating previous findings in metabolomic studies.

We conducted an enrichment analysis of gastric cancer cell subgroups with varying malignancy levels using the KEGG database. The subgroups characterized by high malignancy exhibited elevated activity in pathways associated with N-Glycan biosynthesis and diverse types of N-glycan biosynthesis. Additionally, these subgroups impacted the immune microenvironment via IL-17 signaling and the regulation of Th17 cell differentiation (Figs. 4G–1). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that high malignancy gastric cancer cells demonstrated increased activity in MHC protein complex regulation (Figs. 4G–2), N-terminal glycosylation modification (Figs. 4G–3), Golgi vesicle transport, and COP I and COP II vesicle transport (Figs. 4G–3, 4). These findings further underscore the critical role of sialylation modification in the progression of gastric cancer. In the single-cell transcriptomic analysis, we primarily enriched for N-glycosylation modifications, whereas the previous findings predominantly reflected O-glycosylation (Fig. 1C–F). Given that sialylation encompasses both N-glycosylation and O-glycosylation modifications, these two distinct enrichment results are not contradictory but rather complementary, collectively revealing the functional alterations of sialic acid metabolism and sialylation in gastric cancer cells.

Enrichment analysis confirmed that glycosylation modifications play a key role in promoting malignant progression of gastric cancer. Furthermore, comparative analysis revealed that high-grade malignant gastric cancer tissues exhibited heightened biological activity across multiple functional domains compared to their low-grade counterparts, particularly manifesting enhanced energy metabolic reprogramming, dysregulated immune regulation, and intensified intracellular protein transport processes.

To enhance the credibility of our findings, we conducted Transwell experiments and CCK-8 assays using gastric cancer cells (BGC-823) to investigate the effect of sialic acid (6.25 nM) on the migration and viability of gastric cancer cells. The analysis of the results revealed that the application of exogenous sialic acid led to a statistically significant increase in the number of migrating gastric cancer cells (Fig. 5E). Additionally, we observed that sialic acid promotes the proliferation of gastric cancer cells, an effect that intensifies with increasing concentrations of sialic acid (Fig. 5F). Finally, the zebrafish fluorescence detection method was used to compare the average fluorescence intensity differences between the control group and the exogenous sialic acid treatment group (Fig. 5G-1). The fluorescence intensity indicated the number of gastric cancer cells. We found that after adding sialic acid (0.05 mM), the fluorescence intensity in the zebrafish increased, and the number of gastric cancer cells rose, showing a statistical difference (Fig. 5G-2). This further confirmed the promoting effect of sialic acid on the proliferation of gastric cancer cells.

In conclusion, by analyzing key gene nodes in the sialic acid metabolism pathway, we found that genes related to sialic acid synthesis, as well as the sialyltransferase family associated with sialic acid modification, were upregulated. In contrast, protein families involved in the hydrolysis of sialylated glycoproteins were expressed in higher levels in high-malignancy gastric cancer subgroups. The combined effects of these factors led to a high level of sialylation in gastric cancer cells.

WZ35 Inhibits biological functions in gastric cancer

In our prior analysis, we identified elevated levels of sialylation modification as a distinctive metabolic characteristic of gastric cancer cells. This observation prompts the inquiry as to whether any pharmacological agent can modulate the sialylation levels in gastric cancer cells to impede their progression. Consequently, we selected the curcumin derivative WZ35 to evaluate its impact on gastric cancer cells.

CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that WZ35 inhibited the proliferation of gastric cancer cells (BGC-823) (Fig. 6A), and its inhibitory effect increased with the concentration of the drug (0 μM, 2.5 μM, 7.5 μM, 12.5 μM) (Fig. 6B). Cell apoptosis experiments showed that WZ35 also had a cytotoxic effect on gastric cancer cells (Fig. 6C).

To further investigate the relationship underlying the drug action of WZ35, we conducted the following experimental investigations. Based on the results from CCK-8 and Transwell assays, we found that WZ35 exerted inhibitory effects on the proliferation and migration of gastric cancer cells, and this inhibition could not be fully reversed even with the addition of exogenous sialic acid (Fig. 6D, E). To further investigate the effects of WZ35 and sialic acid on energy metabolism in gastric cancer cells, we conducted a Seahorse XF analysis. Four groups were set up: NC group, sialic acid group (8.25 nM), WZ35 group (12.5 μM), and combination group (WZ35 12.5 μM + sialic acid 8.25 nM). The OCR and ECAR results showed that treatment with sialic acid alone slightly upregulated mitochondrial respiration and moderately increased the glycolytic rate (ECAR). In contrast, WZ35 treatment markedly suppressed both mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis. Notably, this inhibitory effect of WZ35 could not be reversed by co-treatment with sialic acid (Fig. 6F, G). We also performed proteomic analysis comparing gastric cancer cells treated with WZ35 to control cells that were untreated (Fig. 6H). The results revealed that, after treatment with the drug, the expression of pathways related to the cell cytoskeleton and cell junctions were downregulated.

Through our experimental validation, we have demonstrated that WZ35 exhibits significant inhibitory effects on gastric cancer cell proliferation. Furthermore, we identified its suppressive activity against pan-glycosylation modifications within gastric cancer cells. Meanwhile, qPCR results demonstrated that WZ35 also exhibited inhibitory effects on key proteins involved in the previously mentioned sialylation modification pathway (Fig. 6I), the mechanism of action of WZ35 was further elucidated in this study.

Discussion

Glycosylation represents a critical post-translational modification of proteins that modulates their functional properties. Numerous studies have demonstrated that mannose metabolism, glycolysis, and glutamine metabolism can influence intracellular glycosylation levels [53–55], thereby affecting a range of cellular functions. In this study, we analyzed the metabolic characteristics of gastric cancer cells through three approaches: transcriptomics, single-cell transcriptomics, and metabolomics.

In the context of gastric cancer, sialic acid metabolism may be attributed to the synergistic effects of multiple metabolic pathways. We conducted an analysis of the expression levels of key metabolites involved in the glycolysis, glutathione, aminoglycoside, and nucleoside metabolism pathways in gastric cancer tissues. Within the glycolysis pathway, we observed an upregulation of metabolites such as thiamine pyrophosphate, phosphoenolpyruvic acid, 2-phospho-D-glyceric acid, and (S)-lactate in gastric cancer tissues compared to adjacent non-cancerous tissues (SFig. 1D–G). Conversely, the expression of pyruvic acid was found to be downregulated (SFig. 1H). In glycolysis, 2-phospho-D-glyceric acid is enzymatically converted to phosphoenolpyruvic acid by enolase, which in turn is subsequently converted to pyruvate by pyruvate kinase. This irreversible step is critical for maintaining proper metabolic flux. In the gluconeogenesis pathway, pyruvate is carboxylated to oxaloacetate, which is then transformed into phosphoenolpyruvic acid by phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase. Despite the observed upregulation of phosphoenolpyruvic acid and 2-phospho-D-glyceric acid in gastric cancer tissues, pyruvate, the terminal product of glycolysis, exhibits a downward trend.

We hypothesize that multiple factors could account for this observation: (1) pyruvate may undergo conversion via gluconeogenesis; (2) it may be transformed into lactate through alternative pathways; and (3) pyruvate might serve as a substrate in other metabolic processes. The precise mechanisms underlying these conversions warrant further investigation.

Here, we hypothesize that the observed changes in pyruvate metabolism could be related to the regulatory mechanisms of the sialic acid biosynthesis pathway. The specific mechanisms underlying this interaction warrant further in-depth investigation.

In our study, multiple omics enrichment analyses were employed to characterize the metabolic functions of cells. The results, while seemingly contradictory, were inherently interconnected. Enrichment analysis of bulk RNA-Sequencing data indicated O-glycosylation, whereas subsequent single-cell transcriptomic enrichment analysis revealed N-glycosylation modifications. However, these findings are not contradictory; instead, they represent different manifestations of sialic acid modification. In N-glycosylation, sialic acid is typically linked to the terminal galactose of the N-glycan chain, and sometimes to N-acetylglucosamine [56, 57]. In O-glycosylation, sialic acid can be directly attached to the GalNAc of the core structure [56]. Both findings can be viewed as different manifestations of the same biological process.

In our comprehensive analysis of sialic acid metabolism and its modification, we identified the involvement of various enzymes in the sialylation process. We propose that distinct modification enzymes may exert differential effects on the functionality of gastric cancer cells. Our study revealed that gastric cancer subgroups exhibiting elevated expression of the ST6GALNAC1 gene were more inclined to enhance energy metabolism pathways, such as the tricarboxylic acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation. Meanwhile, other studies have primarily indicated that ST6GALNAC1 promotes tumor proliferation and migration [58–60]. However, the mechanisms through which ST6GALNAC1 exerts these functions have not been extensively investigated. In this study, we propose new functional hypotheses for ST6GALNAC1 based on two approaches—its gene expression levels in single-cell transcriptomics and functional enrichment analysis—thereby serving as a supplement to the existing research on the functional roles of ST6GALNAC1. These findings also warrant further validation and deeper exploration in our future analyses. Conversely, subgroups with heightened expression of ST6GALNAC6, ST6GAL1, and ST3GAL6 were predominantly associated with immune regulation and suppression in gastric cancer cells. This gene cluster mediates a diverse range of functions. For instance, ST6GALNAC6 regulates immune function through the Sialic acid/Siglec axis [61], while ST6GAL1 is involved in mediating immune homeostasis, promoting cancer progression, and contributing to drug resistance in cancer cells [62–64] and ST3GAL6 participates in mediating cancer invasion [65]. These findings align with our hypothesis, and our analytical inferences further enrich the understanding of the functional roles of these genes. Collectively, the modification processes catalyzed by sialyltransferases mediate diverse functional outcomes in gastric cancer cells. This observation leads us to further hypothesize that the reprogramming of sialic acid metabolism in gastric cancer cells may drive intratumoral functional diversification. Targeting these enzymes could offer a selective strategy for modulating gastric cancer metabolism and remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment.

Although sialic acid metabolism is a known marker in cancer progression [39], its regulatory determinants in gastric cancer (GC) are poorly defined. Our multi-omics analysis maps this pathway within the GC microenvironment. While prior research, such as the study by Junjie Jiang’s team, has valuably connected sialic acid metabolism genes (e.g., ST3GAL1) to patient stratification and prognosis [66], our work diverges by investigating its functional remodeling. We elucidate how sialic acid reprogramming in GC cells influences glycosylation, energy metabolism, and immune crosstalk. Crucially, we translate this mechanistic understanding into a therapeutic discovery, identifying WZ35 as a promising agent that effectively inhibits sialic acid-driven oncogenic functions. This study not only deepens the mechanistic knowledge but also advances the field toward targeted intervention.

Within the context of sialic acid metabolism, numerous enzymes associated with sialylation have been implicated in various studies. Among these, ST3GAL1 appears to be a central regulator, playing significant roles in tumor proliferation, metastasis, and immune regulation [67–69]. However, the sialylation-modified protein family is vast. We propose that distinct sialylation molecules may exert different biological functions for gastric cancer cells under varying biological contexts. Our research has identified selective utilization of specific sialylated proteins among different gastric cancer cell subpopulations, a selectivity that correlates with the unique biological functions of each subpopulation. This observation may provide a novel conceptual framework and perspective for future investigations into the functional diversity of sialylation in cancer.

In the preceding findings, we observed a unidirectional MIF-mediated communication from epithelial cells to lymphocytes (Fig. 3E). This unidirectional communication is highly likely to be associated with the regulation of the immune microenvironment in gastric cancer cells [70]. MIF is recognized as one of the key signaling pathways in tumor immunomodulation. However, what factors contribute to this unidirectional signaling from epithelial cells to lymphocytes in our study, and whether this communication is modulated by sialic acid metabolism or the pharmacological effects of WZ35, are questions that warrant in-depth investigation in our future research.

Furthermore, when analyzing the intercellular communication among the distinct epithelial subpopulations G1, G2, and G3, we also observed differences in the MIF signaling pathway, which is involved in immunoregulation. Although investigating the differences in immune function is not the primary focus of this study, we aim to further explore the relationship between alterations in sialic acid metabolism and these three functional subgroups in future research.

WZ35 has demonstrated the capability to reverse the metabolic reprogramming associated with glycolysis and sialic acid metabolism in gastric cancer cells. Based on proteomic analyses and cellular experiments, it is hypothesized that WZ35 may influence sialylation modifications and modulate cytoskeletal remodeling, thereby inhibiting the proliferation and metastasis of gastric cancer cells.

In our study, we also employed transcriptomic and proteomic analyses to compare functional changes in cells following WZ35 treatment. A comparative analysis revealed that prior to drug treatment, the enriched pathways were predominantly associated with glycosylation modifications, vesicular transport between the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus, and Golgi structure. However, after drug treatment, these pathways were no longer enriched; instead, the enrichment shifted toward the regulation of the cytoskeleton. The cytoskeleton is one of the research hotspots in cancer therapy, and inhibiting cytoskeleton remodeling can effectively suppress cancer metastasis. This finding is also consistent with our results [71]. We further speculate here whether the remodeling effect of WZ35 on the gastric cancer cytoskeleton is related to its regulatory role in sialic acid metabolism. This also represents one of the starting points for our future research.

Furthermore, we note that existing studies have demonstrated the inhibitory effects of compounds such as 3-fluorosialic acid derivatives on sialic acid [72]. This naturally leads us to consider whether the drug WZ35 could achieve more effective inhibition when used in combination with such sialic acid inhibitors. We plan to further investigate this avenue of research and analysis in our future studies.

In conclusion, this study presents several novel contributions to the field. Firstly, we have identified the metabolic reprogramming of sialylation in gastric cancer, thereby advancing the current understanding of the metabolic landscape associated with this malignancy. Secondly, our research delineates the functional roles of sialylation enzymes in gastric cancer, highlighting that distinct modification enzymes are linked to diverse functions such as immune regulation, metabolic processes, and protein translation, which collectively contribute to the malignancy’s progression. Lastly, we have demonstrated the targeted inhibition of sialic acid metabolism by WZ35 in gastric cancer, which effectively suppresses key biological functions, including sialic acid modification, cytoskeleton remodeling, and cell proliferation. This finding not only enhances the mechanistic understanding of WZ35 but also suggests potential clinical applications.

However, our study still has certain limitations. On the one hand, it remains unclear through which proteins the sialylation modification exerts its effects on immune and metabolic reprogramming. On the other hand, the exact mechanism through which WZ35 reshapes gastric cancer sialic acid metabolism still requires further investigation.

Conclusions

Our research findings indicate that sialic acid metabolism and sialylation modification are important factors influencing the malignant progression of gastric cancer. The WZ35 drug can inhibit gastric cancer by inducing metabolic reprogramming in gastric cancer cells and reducing the level of sialylation.
