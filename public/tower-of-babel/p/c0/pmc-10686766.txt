A case of cold agglutinin disease in a bottlenose dolphin (Tursiops truncatus)
Authors: Kenichiro TAKUBO, Chika SHIRAKATA
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Cold agglutinin disease, one of the serological classifications of immune-mediated hemolytic anemia, is caused by the production of autoantibodies that react with erythrocytes at low temperatures. A captive bottlenose dolphin presented with regenerative and hemolytic anemia. Anticoagulated whole blood was agglutinated at room temperature (approximately 18°C), with reversal of agglutination on warming to 37°C, indicating the presence of cold agglutinin. Based on these findings, this animal was diagnosed with cold agglutinin disease. Clindamycin, doxycycline, and prednisolone were administered orally to treat the infection and immune-mediated hemolytic anemia. Anemia gradually improved after initiation of pharmacotherapy, and erythrogenesis slowed as erythroblasts disappeared and reticulocyte count decreased in peripheral blood. This represents the first report of cold agglutinin disease in a cetacean.

Immune-mediated hemolytic anemia (IMHA) is a disease that causes anemia due to the production of autoantibodies that react to antigens on erythrocyte membranes, resulting in a shortening of the erythrocyte life span. Cold agglutinin disease (CAD) is one of the serological classifications of IMHA. Contrasting with warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia, which causes erythrocyte aggregation at near-body temperatures, CAD is caused by the production of autoantibodies that react with erythrocytes at below-normal body temperatures. Cooling of peripheral parts of the body then leads to the activation of autoantibodies that binds to the erythrocyte membrane, followed by erythrocyte agglutination. Activation of the complement cascade caused by the reaction of cold agglutinins with erythrocytes leads to intra- and extravascular hemolysis. Symptoms primarily comprise anemia and peripheral circulatory disturbances, with acute or chronic anemia and skin necrosis in peripheral areas such as the auricles and extremities [13]. CAD is known to be idiopathic in the absence of a confirmed primary disease, or secondary to infection or lymphoproliferative disease [13]. The bottlenose dolphin (Tursiops truncatus) is a small cetacean species that lives primarily in temperate to tropical coastal waters, and is one of the most common species of cetacean under human care worldwide. In cetaceans, acute hemorrhagic diseases (trauma, hemorrhagic gastroenteritis, etc.) [4] and Bartonella infection are known to cause regenerative anemia [10], but CAD and other types of IMHA have not previously been reported as causes. Here, we offer the first description of CAD in a captive bottlenose dolphin.

The subject animal was a female bottlenose dolphin (Tursiops truncatus) captured in the wild and kept for 1 year, with an estimated age of 3 years. The dolphin weighed 182.0 kg on the first day of illness (Day 1). The animal was kept in an open-air pool containing 1,350 m3 of natural seawater with a semi-closed life support system. The dolphin had been fed thawed fish (e.g., mackerel, saury, herring, sand lance, sardine, and capelin) with sea mammal vitamin supplement (MAZURI® Marine Mammal Supplement 5M26 [3 days/week] and 5TLA [4 days/week], 1 tablet per 2.27 kg of food; Land O’Lakes Inc., Arden Hills, MN, USA). During the disease period, mean air temperature was 7.8°C, mean maximum air temperature was 12.3°C and mean minimum temperature was 4.1°C. Water temperature was controlled to be 19.5°C, with measured values within the range of 19.1–20.1°C. The animal was kept in a divided area with another female bottlenose dolphin. On Day 1, the animal was restrained on land for intramuscular injection of inactivated erysipelas vaccine (Porcilis® ERY; MSD Animal Health, Tokyo, Japan) as well as sampling of blood from a fluke vein for medical screening. Although body temperature was slightly high at 37.4°C, the animal was vaccinated as scheduled because this animal showed no symptoms until the day before Day 1. After release back into the pool, the dolphin showed normal appetite. Body surface and visible mucous membrane color were normal.

Blood analysis was performed in a human medical laboratory (Showa Medical Science, Tokyo, Japan). Samples were refrigerated at 4°C, sent to the laboratory and analyzed the same day. Abdominal ultrasonography on Day 2 revealed no evidence of ascites or intra-abdominal bleeding. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy on Day 3 revealed no bleeding in the esophagus or forestomach. Erythrocyte ghosts, representing remnant membranes of hemolyzed erythrocytes, were observed on a blood smear collected on Day 2 (Fig. 1Fig. 1.An erythrocyte ghost (arrowhead) on the blood smear collected on Day 2 (Giemsa stain, ×1,000).). A urine sample collected by abdominal compression on Day 3 and tested with qualitative urine test paper (Uropiece S 9L; Minaris Medical Co., Tokyo, Japan) showed bilirubin 2+, hemoglobin 3+.

On Day 3, the following medications were started to treat both infectious hemolytic anemia and autoimmune anemia: clindamycin hydrochloride (CLDM) (Darasin capsule; Pfizer, Tokyo, Japan) at 5 mg/kg, orally (per os; PO), twice a day (bis in die; BID) (Days 3–23), doxycycline hydrochloride (DOXY) (Vibramycin tablet; Pfizer) at 2.75 mg/kg PO, BID (Days 3–37), and prednisolone (Predonine tablet; Shionogi, Osaka, Japan) at 0.5 mg/kg, PO, once a day (semel in die; SID) (Days 3–16). Prednisolone dose was gradually reduced to prevent steroid withdrawal syndrome, to 0.4 mg/kg, PO, SID (Days 17–30), 0.25 mg/kg, PO, SID (Days 31–44), 0.1 mg/kg, PO, SID (Days 45–58), and 0.05 mg/kg, PO, SID (Days 59–70), with the dosage completed on Day 70. Dosages of prednisolone were determined based on a previous study that suggested a long-term dose ≥0.45 mg/kg/day might result in immunosuppression in bottlenose dolphins [24]. In addition, the following medications were administered to promote hemogenesis: methylcobalamin (Mecobalamin tablet [SW]; Sawai Pharmaceutical, Osaka, Japan) at 10 μg/kg, PO, three times a day (ter in die; TID) (Days 3–72) and folic acid (Foliamine tablet; Nippon Pharmaceutical, Tokyo, Japan) at 0.2 mg/kg, PO, BID (Days 17–72). Aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures were performed using blood collected on Day 2, yielding negative results. We applied Bartonella-specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR) targeting gltA and rpoB genes to whole-blood samples taken before administration of medications on Day 3. In addition, the same freeze-thawed whole blood sample was inoculated on heart infusion agar with 5% rabbit blood. Both examinations yielded negative results. Tests for Toxoplasma immunoglobulin IgM and IgG yielded negative results by the Chemiluminescent immunoassay (CLEIA) method in a human laboratory (Showa Medical Science, Tokyo, Japan).

The dolphin fought with a cohabiting female on Day 22, resulting in a cut approximately 5 cm in length on the right side of the caudal fin. Three cross-sections of blood vessels were exposed on the wound surface, and massive bleeding was observed. Astriction was applied with gauze soaked with 0.1% adrenaline saline while the animal was voluntarily restrained its caudal fin, but this did not stop the bleeding. The animal was then restrained on land and astriction was completed. Despite this event, comparison of blood test results from shortly before and after this accident showed no impact of the bleeding (Day 20: Ht 40.1%, RBC 297 × 104/μL, Hb 13.2 g/dL; Day 23: Ht 40.3%, RBC 303 × 104/μL, Hb 13.5 g/dL) (Table 1). On Day 72, all medications were completed. Blood tests on Day 123 showed that all blood test values had normalized.

To analyze the change in ALP values, ALP isozyme fractions were analyzed by electrophoresis with the frozen serum collected on Day 3 (before initiation of medication), Day 22 (ALP peak during medication), and Day 123 (blood test values were recovered). These analyses were also performed in a human medical laboratory (Showa Medical Science, Tokyo, Japan). Table. 2 shows the total ALP measurements, ALP 1 to 3 isozyme fraction ratios, and values calculated by multiplying each fraction ratio by the measured total ALP values. Total ALP value and calculated values of each fraction decreased on Day 3 before medication, fraction ratios and calculated values of liver-derived ALP 1 and 2 increased during medication on Day 22, and the fraction ratio of bone-derived ALP 3 decreased compared to Day 3, with no change in calculated values. After recovery, the total ALP and fractional calculated values of Day 123 increased to approximately double those of Day 3, and the fractional ratios were similar to those of Day 3.

According to the consensus statement of the American College of Veterinary Internal Medicine, signs of autoimmune hemolytic anemia in dogs and cats include: signs of immune-mediated destruction (spherocytes [only in dogs], positive saline agglutination test, and positive direct Coombs test); and signs of hemolysis (hyperbilirubinemia, bilirubinuria, icterus without other cause, hemoglobinuria, hemoglobinemia, erythrocyte ghosts) [8]. However, confirming signs of immune-mediated destruction was difficult in this case. The presence of spherocytes can be a diagnostic finding in dogs, but was not evident in this case. The saline agglutination test was not performed on blood from Day 1. The direct Coombs test could not be performed because no laboratory system applicable to dolphins has yet been devised.

It is known that the aggregation of erythrocytes by cold agglutinin can be reversibly detach by warming up to 37°C in human [13]. Also in the present case, as seen in Fig. 2, agglutination occurred at low temperature and reversed when the sample was warmed to around body temperature.

To determine the presence of erythrocyte agglutinating factors during disease period, the following examination was applied: after the animal recovered to a good and hematologically healthy condition, blood samples were collected. To prepare an erythrocyte suspension, the sample was processed with EDTA-2K and centrifuged (3,000 rpm, 5 min). Plasma was removed and 50 μL of sediment was added to 1 mL of saline. The sample was then diluted up to 10 mL with saline and centrifuged (3,000 rpm, 1 min). The supernatant was removed and 1 mL of saline was added to the sediment. The erythrocyte suspension was examined speculatively to confirm the absence of aggregation between erythrocytes. We identified hyperbilirubinemia and bilirubinuria as findings suggestive of hemolysis. Declining α2 fraction in serum protein electrophoresis is also suggestive of hemolytic disease in human medicine [2]. The α2 fraction contains haptoglobin, an acute-phase reactant protein known to be elevated during inflammation. Haptoglobin also works to prevent kidney damage by binding to free hemoglobin in plasma. Under hemolytic disease conditions, haptoglobin is consumed and the α2 fraction thus decreases [2]. The α2 fraction in protein electrophoresis and serum haptoglobin is known to elevate in cetaceans under inflammatory disease conditions [9, 15]. Even though an elevated fibrinogen value is used as an indicator of inflammation in cetaceans [17], the α2 fraction was lower than the average value for this animal. In the present case, we suspect that the α2 fraction had declined, as haptoglobin was consumed in binding free hemoglobin released by hemolysis. Haptoglobin was not examined in this case.

In excluding diagnoses, the increase in Ret and the appearance of EBL in peripheral blood indicated regenerative anemia and ruled out anemia caused by decreased erythrogenesis. Furthermore, intra-abdominal and gastrointestinal tract examinations with ultrasonography and endoscopy ruled out hemorrhage. No evidence indicated ingestion of drugs or poisons that would have caused hemolytic anemia. Toxoplasmosis is known to cause hemolytic anemia in humans. Although hemolytic anemia has not been reported with Toxoplasma infection in cetaceans, this infection is common among both wild and captive cetaceans [14]. Kamio et al. [11] suggested that some Toxoplasma infection test kits are applicable to dolphins, but those kits are not currently commercially available in Japan. The dolphin in this case tested negative on human Toxoplasma IgM/IgG tests using CLEIA, but whether this test is able to detect dolphin anti-Toxoplasma antigen was unclear. Harms et al. [10] reported that a captive bottlenose dolphin showing regenerative anemia tested positive for Bartonella PCR. Bartonella spp. are known to be transmitted via arthropod vectors such as lice, or by bites or scratches from infected animals. In the present case, no opportunities for Bartonella transmission were apparent in the absence of vector parasitism or contact with animals such as dogs or cats. Indeed, Bartonella genes and cultures tested negative in this case.

In the present case, although there are findings of hemolytic anemia (hyperbilirubinemia, bilirubinuria, and decline of α2 fraction), there is no evidence of immune-mediated erythrocyte destruction (not applicable in saline agglutination test and not available in direct Coombs test) as described in the ACVIM statement [8]. However, erythrocyte aggregation reversible by warming is one of the features of CAD [13], and it was shown that cold agglutinin had been present only during the disease period. In addition, the anemia improved in response to prednisolone medication. Based on these findings, we concluded that the present case had CAD, a serotype of IMHA. CAD is known to occur secondary to some infectious diseases. In general, idiopathic and lymphoproliferative CAD are known to cause chronic symptoms, whereas CAD caused by infection is acute and resolves within a few weeks. In humans, Mycoplasma, parvovirus B19, Epstein-Barr virus, adenovirus, influenza virus, and varicella zoster virus infections and syphilis can cause CAD [13]. Mycoplasma infection is also known to cause CAD in non-human animals, including chimpanzees, horses, dogs, chickens, and swine [3, 12, 18, 19, 22]. Mycoplasma spp. have been cultured from lung, spleen, liver, kidney, and blood samples of stranded harbor porpoises (Phocoena phocoena) and a Sowerby’s beaked whale (Mesoplodon bidens) [7]. Although not performed in this case, Mycoplasma-targeted culture and genetic detection should be attempted in such cases with CAD symptoms. In this case, the subject animal recovered within a short period of time, suggesting that the disease may have been related to infection, although the specific pathogen involved was unable to be identified. In this case, CLDM and DOXY were administered in anticipation of Toxoplasma, Bartonella, or Mycoplasma infection. CLDM has been shown to be effective in the treatment of Toxoplasma infection in cetaceans [5]. In this case, toxoplasmosis was ruled out once CAD was identified, so CLDM was terminated prior to DOXY, which is used as a treatment for Bartonella infections, as well as for Mycoplasma pneumonia in humans [1].

Prednisolone was started at a lower dose of 0.5 mg/kg/day, compared to the 2–3 mg/kg/day recommended in the ACVIM Consensus Statement for the initial treatment of IMHA in dogs [23] and the dose of 1 mg/kg/day used for the initial treatment of IMHA in humans [13]. In the present case, 0.5 mg/kg/day of prednisolone was sufficient for immunosuppression, so anemia recovered gradually after administration. In addition, no withdrawal symptoms were observed as the prednisolone dose was gradually tapered. The prednisolone dosing schedule used in this case was considered appropriate. In human medicine, CAD is controlled by avoiding cold conditions [13]. However, in cases such as this one, where animals are kept in an open-air tank and the environmental temperature is difficult to control, prednisolone medication may represent a viable option. Terasawa et al. [24] reported a case with long-term administration of prednisolone for steroid-responsive enterocolitis in a bottlenose dolphin, suggesting that doses even less than 0.45 mg/kg/day may cause immunosuppression. Alterations in hepatic deviation enzymes are thought to be influenced by the hepatic damage from prednisolone and antibiotics, so these parameters decreased along with reductions in doses of the responsible agents. A decrease in ALP and increase in fibrinogen level are observed in inflammatory diseases in cetaceans [17]. However, in this case, ALP value was low at the beginning of the disease period, rose to slightly below average during medication, fell again with dose reduction, and then rose again to average after medication. As serum ALP levels in cetaceans are known to derived from several organs [20], isozymes were analyzed to identify the organs that caused relative increase in ALP values during medication. Before medication, calculated values of each fraction decreased, then liver-derived fractional ratio and calculated values increased during medication, while bone-derived fractional ratio decreased. Based on the above, administration of prednisolone at 0.5 mg/kg/day is recommended as initial treatment for CAD in bottlenose dolphin. However, prolonged administration of this dose may have caused serious side effects such as other infections due to immunosuppression or hepatic failure. The dolphin in this case showed no clinical symptoms other than findings on blood tests during the entire disease period. Peripheral skin necrosis has been reported in CAD in dogs and humans [13, 16, 18, 21], but was not present in this case. This may have been because the anemia did not progress to the level of clinical symptoms or because treatment was initiated before the anemia progressed. This represents the first report of CAD in a cetacean. Little is known regarding hematological disease in cetaceans, so further accumulation of clinical information in this field remains important.
