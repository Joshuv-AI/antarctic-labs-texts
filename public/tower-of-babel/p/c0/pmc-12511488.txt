Analysis of Adverse Drug Reactions of Clofazimine Reported in the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System from 2004 to 2025 Q1
Authors: Ruoyu Zhang, Yunwen Tao, Ziwei Bao, Jianping Zhang, Lingwu Zeng, Chen Fang, Meiying Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionClofazimine (CFZ) is an antimycobacterial agent used primarily for leprosy and multidrug-resistant tuberculosis. Despite its long clinical history, comprehensive pharmacovigilance data remain limited. This study aimed to analyze CFZ-associated adverse events (AEs) reported in the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS), identifying and pharmacovigilance signals.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective pharmacovigilance analysis of the FAERS database from 2004 to 2025 Q1. ASCII-format data were imported into R 4.4.2 and deduplicated using FDA guidelines. Reports Listing CFZ as the primary suspect drug were identified using generic and brand names. AEs were coded using MedDRA 27.1. Disproportionality analyses, including reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN), and empirical Bayesian geometric mean (EBGM), identified signals of disproportionate reporting. Subgroup analyses examined sex differences, while time-to-onset (TTO) analyses characterized latency patterns.ResultsA total of 1287 CFZ-related AE reports were identified, with 995 (77.3%) classified as serious. Death (11.6%) and hospitalization (18.1%) were the most frequent serious outcomes. The majority of reports originated from the United States (59.4%). Demographic analysis showed higher reporting among females (49.6%) and patients aged 18–64 years (46.5%). Disproportionality analyses identified 135 preferred terms with positive safety signals. The most prominent signals included QT prolongation (ROR ~ 37.61), drug resistance (ROR ~ 17.31), skin hyperpigmentation (ROR ~ 13.07), and respiratory failure (ROR ~ 7.46), ranging from moderate to strong signal intensity. Subgroup analyses revealed significant sex differences in specific AE signals. TTO analysis indicated varied latency distributions across System Organ Class (SOC) and preferred term levels.ConclusionOur pharmacovigilance assessment of FAERS data from 2004 to 2025 not only identified multiple serious and consistent safety signals associated with clofazimine such as prolonged QT intervals but also revealed a life-threatening AE respiratory failure. Although the analysis of these AEs cannot directly reflect causal relationships due to the nature of the FAERS data from spontaneous reporting, our findings highlight the critical importance of continuous pharmacovigilance, targeted clinical monitoring, and consideration of sex-based risk differences to ensure the safe use of clofazimine in clinical practice.

Introduction

The development of drug-resistant tuberculosis (TB) represents a major global health threat. According to the World Health Organization [1], an estimated 410,000 cases of multidrug- or rifampicin-resistant TB (MDR/RR-TB) occurred globally in 2023, with nearly 17% among previously treated patients.

Clofazimine (CFZ), first described in the mid-1950s as a riminophenazine antibiotic [2], demonstrated antimycobacterial activity comparable to isoniazid in early animal models. By the early 1960s, clinical trials confirmed its efficacy against leprosy [3], establishing CFZ as a cornerstone of leprosy treatment. Interest in CFZ’s anti-TB activity resurfaced in the 1990s in response to rising MDR-TB prevalence [4, 5]. Historically, treatment for resistant TB relied on highly toxic agents with limited efficacy. The inclusion of CFZ in MDR-TB regimens improved treatment outcomes, and, over the past decade, the treatment landscape has evolved significantly with the development of shorter-course regimens. Although CFZ is not included in standardized BPAL/M regimens, it remains an important option in individualized MDR-TB treatment strategies [6]. Clofazimine is also used for treating non-tuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) pulmonary infections, especially in some refractory nontuberculous mycobacterial lung diseases [7].

However, its widespread and prolonged use has raised important concerns about adverse events (AEs), particularly given its unique pharmacokinetic properties. CFZ is highly Lipophilic, distributes extensively in adipose tissue, and accumulates in fatty tissue, macrophages, and reticuloendothelial organs. This large peripheral volume of distribution results in steady-state concentrations being reached only after approximately 5 months of therapy [8]. Additionally, elimination is extremely slow, with a half-life that increases with duration of administration (ranging from 45 h to several weeks) [9] and can even reach a terminal half-life of around 30 days in humans [10]. Reported AEs have increasingly focused on cardiac toxicity—particularly QT interval prolongation—as well as skin pigmentation changes and gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea and vomiting [8, 11, 12]. CFZ exerts cardiac toxicity by inhibiting the human hERG channel [12], and it carries an additive risk of QT prolongation and arrhythmia when combined with other common DR-TB agents such as bedaquiline and fluoroquinolones [13–15].

Although randomized controlled trials (RCTs) conducted under highly controlled conditions provide valuable information on drug efficacy and selected adverse reactions, real-world evidence quantifying the frequency and distribution of these AEs remains Limited. Notably, rare but severe AEs reported in real-world settings also warrant attention. For example, a 2017 nationwide non-RCT cohort study in Brazil covered 1446 MDR-TB patients treated with CFZ. It not only confirmed gastrointestinal side effects typical of RCTs but also quantified QTc prolongation (2.8%), severe skin pigmentation (18.7%), and three deaths due to respiratory failure for the first time in a real-world population. The authors suggested that strict inclusion criteria and limited follow-up in RCTs may underestimate the true risk of cardiac and respiratory toxicity associated with CFZ in real clinical practice [16].

RCTs’ strict inclusion criteria, standardized interventions, and limited follow-up often fail to capture the heterogeneity of real-world comorbidities, polypharmacy, and prolonged treatment durations. In contrast, spontaneous reporting systems and administrative claims databases can more comprehensively capture patient diversity and long-term safety signals, supporting risk–benefit assessments and pharmacovigilance strategies [17]. This is particularly important for CFZ, which is typically used for many months in complex MDR-TB regimens.

The FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) is an open-access database widely used for post-marketing safety surveillance [18]. As a spontaneous reporting system containing millions of AE reports along with drug usage information [19], FAERS enables individual-level safety signal analysis [20], making it valuable for identifying previously unrecognized risks that can inform clinical practice and regulatory decision-making [21]. Prior FAERS-based studies have applied signal detection methods to analyze adverse event profiles for anti-TB agents such as bedaquiline [22].

Previous safety evaluations of CFZ have largely relied on small case series, local pharmacovigilance efforts, or narrow analyses focused on individual AE types [7, 23, 24]. There is still a lack in comprehensive, large-scale, multi-country assessment of CFZ-associated AE patterns. Here, we present the first large-scale, real-world pharmacovigilance analysis of CFZ-associated AEs using over 20 years of FAERS data (from 2004 to 2025 Q1). Our study aims to characterize the demographic and geographic distribution of reports, quantify the frequency of serious outcomes, detect signals of disproportionate reporting across AE categories, and explore subgroup differences, including sex-specific risk signals and time-to-onset (TTO) patterns. By providing this comprehensive assessment, we seek to inform clinical practice, support patient safety efforts, and guide future research into the optimal use of CFZ in MDR-TB and leprosy treatment.

Methods

Data Source and Collection

FAERS is a publicly accessible database maintained by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, characterized by voluntary reporting, underreporting bias, and the absence of denominator data. We extracted FAERS reports spanning 2004 Q1 to March 2025 Q1. Data cleaning followed FDA guidelines, removing duplicate records by retaining the most recent entry per CASEID. Reports listing CFZ as the primary suspect drug (role code “PS”) were included in the final dataset. We excluded records with misspelled drug entries, duplicates, and non-informative or irrelevant AE descriptions. To further mitigate potential confounding, we reviewed available case-level data where possible, including patient comorbidities and concomitant medications, to better contextualize the reported AEs. AEs were standardized using MedDRA version 27.1 coding. All AEs were mapped to preferred terms (PTs) and grouped by system organ class (SOC) to ensure uniform classification across reports.

Ethical Approval

The data used in this study were obtained from the publicly available FDA FAERS database, which contains de-identified data. Therefore, the data are non-identifiable, and no personal identifying information was included in the dataset. As the data are non-identifiable, there was no need for additional ethical review for individual consent.

Signal Detection Analysis

Signal detection relied on disproportionality analysis, comparing the reporting proportion of specific AEs for CFZ against the entire database. Four methods were employed: (1) reporting odds ratio (ROR): signal defined as lower 95% confidence interval (CI) > 1 with a ≥ 3 reports; (2) proportional reporting ratio (PRR): signal defined as PRR ≥ 2, χ2 ≥ 4, and a ≥ 3; (3) Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN): signal defined as IC025 > 0; and (4) empirical Bayesian geometric mean (EBGM): signal defined as EBGM05 > 2. These complementary methods capture both frequent and rare signals. Detailed formulae, calculation procedures, and signal thresholds are provided in Table 1. To interpret the strength of positive disproportionality signals, we followed the classification proposed by van Puijenbroek et al. [25], which has been widely used in FAERS-based signal detection studies. Subgroup Analyses

We conducted subgroup analyses stratified by sex and seriousness to explore differences in AE patterns. For all the PTs, sex or seriousness-specific ROR values with χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact test were calculated. PTs with P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. A total of nine PTs demonstrating significant sex-related differences were selected and visualized in bar plots to compare RORs between male and female subgroups. For seriousness, the top nine PTs with the highest frequency of serious reports among those showing significant disproportionality were selected, and bar plots were generated to compare RORs between serious and non-serious subgroups.

Time-to-Onset (TTO) Analysis

TTO was defined as the number of days between treatment start date and event onset date. Records with inconsistent or missing dates were excluded. TTO distributions were summarized by median, interquartile range (IQR), and range. We compared TTO across SOC and PT levels using boxplots and cumulative incidence curves. In addition, TTO data for serious cases were extracted separately, and a lollipop plot was generated to visually present the TTO distribution for each PT within the serious case subset.

Statistical Analysis

All data preprocessing and analysis were performed in R version 4.4.2. Visualization was conducted using ggplot2 and other tidyverse packages. Two-tailed P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Figures have been produced to illustrate global distribution, reporting trends over time, signal overlap, volcano plots for signal strength and significance, and TTO distributions.

Results

Basic Characteristics of CFZ-Related Adverse Event Reports

A total of 22,775,812 AE reports were retrieved from the FAERS database between Q1 2004 and Q1 2025. Reports mentioning clofazimine under drug name or prod_ai fields were extracted and filtered for primary suspect role. FDA-recommended deduplication was applied by CASEID and PRIMARYID fields. Discussion

This study provides the first large-scale, real-world pharmacovigilance assessment of CFZ-related AEs using over two decades of FAERS data. Among 1287 deduplicated reports (including 995 serious cases), tuberculosis—especially pulmonary forms—was the most common indication for CFZ use, consistent with clinical guidelines [26]. A total of 311 cases related to AEs associated with CFZ involved patients diagnosed with TB, which is the primary indication for CFZ. Meanwhile, there were also 88 cases and 444 cases indicating its use in patients diagnosed with leprosy and NTM, respectively

A notable consideration in our analysis was the decision not to conduct a direct subgroup comparison of AEs between patients with TB and leprosy. This decision was based on several key factors. First, given that clofazimine’s pharmacological action targets mycobacteria [6, 7], its mechanism and resulting AEs profile are expected to be similar across these two conditions [4]. Second, and more importantly, our dataset exhibited significant diagnostic heterogeneity. Beyond TB and leprosy, a substantial number of reports were associated with other conditions such as NTM, osteomyelitis, various other infections and immunodeficiencies. This diversity, combined with the complexity of co-prescribed medications in these patient populations, would introduce significant confounding variables, making a direct and meaningful subgroup comparison between TB and leprosy challenging and potentially misleading. Therefore, we focused on analyzing the overall safety profile of CFZ across all reported indications.

Our findings confirm expected safety concerns such as QT interval prolongation, skin hyperpigmentation, gastrointestinal intolerance, and dizziness—all commonly observed in clinical practice. We also identified less frequently emphasized events like dyspnoea and hemoptysis, which may indicate under-recognized pulmonary toxicities deserving further investigation.

CFZ showed strong disproportionality signals for QT prolongation (ROR ~ 37.61), in line with its known hERG channel blockade and cardiotoxic risk [12]. This underscores the importance of ECG monitoring, especially when used with other QT-prolonging drugs such as bedaquiline and fluoroquinolones [13–15, 27, 28], Bedaquiline and fluoroquinolones could influence the attribution of QT prolongation [29, 30], co-administration of CFZ with other QT-prolonging drugs may complicate the attribution of QT prolongation. Skin hyperpigmentation (ROR ~ 13.07) remains a major tolerability issue, significantly impacting adherence during prolonged MDR-TB regimens [8, 11]. While not life threatening, this cosmetic effect can deter patients from completing therapy. The signal for drug resistance (ROR ~ 17.31) is particularly concerning. Resistance may involve mutations in genes like Rv0678, pepQ, and Rv1979c [31, 32]. One Chinese cohort reported an 8.3% overall CFZ resistance rate among MDR-TB patients, with acquired resistance significantly higher than primary resistance [33]. However, it is important to note that, in the FAERS database, “drug resistance” is coded as a PT under the MedDRA system, as the identification of “drug resistance” in these reports is likely based on reporter impressions, clinical observations of poor response, or patient narratives, rather than standardized laboratory confirmation. Nonetheless, the strength and consistency of the association suggest a potential real-world concern, particularly in the context of MDR-TB treatment.

Respiratory failure (ROR ~ 7.46) emerged as another critical signal. While causality cannot be proven from spontaneous reports, real-world cases have described severe pulmonary toxicity linked to long-term CFZ use. For example, Schreiber et al. [34] reported a patient developing progressive dyspnoea, hemoptysis, and type I respiratory failure after 27 years of CFZ therapy, with biopsy confirming crystal deposition. Another case described CFZ crystals in BAL fluid from a patient with persistent cough and nodular shadows, underscoring the potential for insidious pulmonary injury [35]. These data highlight the need for vigilance, particularly in patients with pre-existing lung disease.

Our analysis also documented a substantial burden of severe outcomes, with 11.6% of reports describing death and 18.1% requiring hospitalization. While spontaneous reporting cannot establish causality, such rates suggest that CFZ-associated AEs can be clinically serious and warrant proactive risk mitigation strategies.

Sex-stratified analyses revealed notable differences in AE reporting odds between male and female patients, suggesting possible pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic variation [8]. Given CFZ’s lipophilicity and extensive tissue distribution, these differences merit further investigation to inform individualized dosing and monitoring strategies.

TTO analysis offered valuable clinical insights. Early-onset events like QT prolongation and gastrointestinal intolerance emphasize the need for close monitoring at treatment initiation. In contrast, delayed-onset events such as skin hyperpigmentation align with CFZ’s slow tissue clearance and long half-life [9, 10]. Clinicians should tailor surveillance accordingly, combining early ECG monitoring with ongoing assessment for late-appearing toxicity.

Geographic analysis revealed a significant geographic skew, with over 59% of adverse event reports coming from the United States, while high-TB-burden countries such as India, Indonesia, and China showed under-reporting, despite CFZ’s inclusion in WHO-endorsed MDR-TB regimens [36, 37]. For example, in China, MDR-TB represents 2.9% of new TB cases and 19% of retreatment [1], yet only seven CFZ-AE reports were identified. Such discrepancies likely reflect differences in pharmacovigilance infrastructure, healthcare resources, and clinician awareness [38]. Strengthening AE reporting systems in these settings is critical for global drug safety surveillance and ensuring equitable patient protection. Additionally, ethnicity can play a critical role in how individuals respond to medications, including differences in drug-metabolizing enzymes, transporters, and immune-related genes [39]. Therefore, the lack of ethnicity data in FAERS may limit the ability to clarify ethnic variations in adverse drug reactions.

In interpreting the findings of this study, it is essential to consider several key limitations associated with the FAERS database. First, FAERS is a spontaneous reporting system prone to underreporting, duplicate entries, and inconsistent data quality. The lack of denominator data (e.g., total CFZ exposure) precludes true incidence estimates or direct comparisons to other treatments [40]. Confounding by disease severity, comorbidities, polypharmacy, and variable dosing cannot be fully adjusted for, and differences in coding practices may introduce misclassification bias. A large portion of the reports in this study came from consumers and non-health professionals. Reports from non-professional journalists may be more prone to inaccuracy, misunderstanding or misclassification, and these reports may need to be carefully evaluated when assessing the severity of AEs. Second, although we have employed multiple disproportionality methods to ensure the robustness of our signals and examined case-level data to assess potential confounders, we cannot entirely account for factors such as disease severity, other medical conditions, or the interactions from co-administration of multiple medications, Like other QT-prolonging drugs such as bedaquiline. Third, our analysis is challenged by the common issue of missing data prevalent in real-world research. A significant number of reports lack complete demographic details, with sex unspecified in 96 instances (7.5%), age in 328 cases (25.5%), and weight in 627 reports (48.7%). These data gaps primarily affect the descriptive characterization of the study population. However, we believe they have a Limited impact on the overall applicability of our pharmacovigilance analysis, as our signal detection methods rely on disproportionality by comparing reporting frequencies, which generally remain robust despite missing demographic information. It should be noted that excluding 96 cases with unknown sex from sex-stratified analysis might slightly reduce statistical power, although the overall interpretation is likely minimally affected.

Furthermore, due to the inherent limitations of the FAERS dataset, definitive causal inference cannot be established. Detected signals should be seen as hypotheses for further evaluation rather than definitive evidence of risk. Prospective cohort studies, electronic health record analyses, and pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic modeling are essential to confirm these signals, define patient-level risk factors, and develop evidence-based monitoring guidelines. Such research is urgently needed given CFZ’s critical role in MDR-TB treatment and global TB control strategies.

Conclusions

Our large-scale FAERS analysis identified consistent and clinically significant safety signals for CFZ, including QT interval prolongation, skin hyperpigmentation, drug resistance, and respiratory failure. These findings underscore the need for vigilant pharmacovigilance, individualized patient monitoring, and awareness of sex-based risk differences. Integrating real-world safety data into clinical decision-making will be essential to optimize CFZ use while ensuring patient safety. Future research should prioritize validating these signals, understanding mechanisms, and establishing robust monitoring strategies to support effective and safe MDR-TB treatment.
