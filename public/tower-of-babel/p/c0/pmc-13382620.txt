Interaction of red-emitting gold nanoclusters with human serum albumin: insights into cell imaging and in vitro applications
Authors: Shanmugavel Chinnathambi, Redhalfi Fadhila, Manoharan Rupavarshini, Karthikeyan Subramani, Ganesh N. Pandian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) are emerging as promising fluorescent nanomaterials for bioimaging and nanomedicine due to their ultrasmall size, high photostability, and favourable biocompatibility. Despite their potential, the interactions between AuNCs and serum proteins, which are critical for determining biological behaviour, are not yet fully understood. In this study, glutathione-stabilized, red-emitting AuNCs with crystalline cores smaller than 3 nm were synthesized and thoroughly characterized using UV–Vis spectroscopy, fluorescence spectroscopy, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), dynamic light scattering (DLS), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). The interactions between AuNCs and human serum albumin (HSA) were systematically examined using steady-state and time-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy, Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET), synchronous fluorescence, three-dimensional fluorescence, circular dichroism (CD), Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, molecular docking, density functional theory (DFT), and in vitro cellular assays. Results from steady-state fluorescence, UV-Vis absorption, and Stern–Volmer analyses confirmed the formation of a stable AuNC-HSA ground-state complex with strong binding affinity. Thermodynamic analysis indicated that the interaction is spontaneous and primarily driven by hydrophobic forces. Time-resolved fluorescence measurements revealed a slight reduction in the average fluorescence lifetime (from 39.9 to 38.2 ns), supporting static quenching as the main mechanism, while FRET analysis determined a donor–acceptor distance of 3.1 nm, suggesting non-radiative energy transfer as a secondary relaxation pathway. Spectroscopic studies indicated only minor conformational changes in HSA upon AuNC binding, and molecular docking and DFT calculations further supported the stability and specificity of the interaction. Additionally, the AuNCs demonstrated high biocompatibility, efficient cellular uptake, and strong intracellular fluorescence in fibroblast, HeLa and A549 cells. Collectively, these results offer detailed molecular-level insights into the nano-bio interface of glutathione-stabilized AuNCs and provide a mechanistic basis for their interactions with serum proteins, facilitating their advancement as biocompatible platforms for bioimaging, biosensing, drug delivery, and nanomedicine.

Introduction

Gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) represent a highly promising class of ultrasmall nanomaterials due to their exceptional photoluminescent, catalytic, and therapeutic properties. Their sub-2 nm size, discrete electronic states, and strong fluorescence distinguish them from larger plasmonic gold nanoparticles, facilitating diverse applications in sensing, targeted cancer imaging, and nanomedicine.1,2 A major advancement in this field is the discovery of aggregation-induced emission enhancement, in which the controlled assembly of AuNCs results in significantly improved photoluminescence.3–6 Hybrid AuNC-based assemblies not only amplify intrinsic optical properties but also introduce advanced functionalities, including stimulus responsiveness, programmable self-organization, and targeted biological interactions. To achieve these features, strategies such as ligand-mediated aggregation, metal-ion coordination, supramolecular assembly, and polymer templating have been developed, enabling precise structural control and tunable optical behavior. These engineered systems typically exhibit enhanced quantum yields, superior photostability, and improved biocompatibility, establishing AuNCs as versatile platforms for next-generation diagnostics and therapeutics.

Despite recent advances, a significant challenge in translating AuNCs into clinical and biological applications is understanding their interactions with biomolecular environments, particularly plasma proteins.7,8 When exposed to physiological media, nanomaterials rapidly adsorb proteins onto their surfaces, resulting in the formation of a “protein corona” that determines their biological identity and influences biodistribution, cellular uptake, and immune recognition.9 Among plasma proteins, HSA is the most abundant and frequently the first to associate with AuNC surfaces. These interactions are primarily governed by electrostatic forces, hydrophobic interactions, and surface chemistry, which significantly affect nanocluster stability, aggregation behavior, and biological fate.10 Consequently, elucidating the binding mechanisms and structural consequences of HSA–AuNC interactions is critical for the rational design of safe and effective nanomaterial-based systems.

Beyond its established role in biological interactions, HSA acts as a stabilizing and templating agent during gold nanocluster (AuNC) synthesis, which enhances both luminescence and biocompatibility. Chan et al. demonstrated that HSA-stabilized AuNCs serve as selective luminescent probes for Staphylococcus aureus and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, highlighting their potential for biosensing applications.11 Additionally, Russell et al. found that encapsulating AuNCs within HSA induces significant conformational and physicochemical changes in the protein, suggesting strong binding affinity and structural adaptability.12 Huang et al. showed that HSA-mediated aggregation can markedly increase emission intensity, underscoring the importance of protein-driven assembly in modulating photophysical properties. Collectively, these studies indicate that HSA not only stabilizes AuNCs but also actively influences their optical characteristics, interaction dynamics, and biological functionality.13

Red-emissive AuNCs have attracted significant attention due to their emission in the visible-to-near-infrared (NIR) region, which reduces background autofluorescence, improves tissue penetration, and limits photodamage.14–16 These nanoclusters are commonly stabilized by biomolecules such as proteins, peptides, or thiolated ligands, including glutathione. Such stabilizers influence their size, structure, and emission properties, while also enhancing colloidal stability and biocompatibility. The red emission typically arises from ligand-to-metal charge transfer or metal-centered transitions and is highly dependent on core size and surface chemistry. Due to their low toxicity, photobleaching resistance, and efficient renal clearance, red-emissive AuNCs are well-suited for bioimaging, biosensing, and theranostic applications. Recent research emphasizes improving quantum yield, tuning emission characteristics, and achieving targeted delivery through advanced surface functionalization strategies.17

This study reports the synthesis of red-emitting glutathione-stabilized AuNCs and provides a comprehensive analysis of their interactions with HSA using integrated spectroscopic and biological approaches. Steady-state and time-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy, ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption, thermodynamic analyses, Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET), and circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy were employed to elucidate the binding mechanisms, fluorescence-quenching behaviour, energy-transfer processes, and structural modifications in HSA induced by AuNC binding. FRET analysis demonstrated that AuNCs are located in close proximity to the Trp214 residue within the primary drug-binding site of HSA. CD and thermodynamic studies identified significant conformational and energetic changes associated with complex formation. Furthermore, cellular uptake studies in fibroblast, A549, and HeLa cells evaluated the influence of nanoparticle surface properties and protein adsorption on cellular internalization. Although protein-nanomaterial interactions have been widely investigated, the novelty of this research lies in integrating molecular-level binding interactions, Trp214-associated energy transfer, protein conformational changes, thermodynamic parameters, and cellular uptake behaviour into a single study. These findings enhance understanding of protein corona formation and the biological fate of AuNCs, supporting the rational design of AuNC-based platforms for bioimaging, diagnostics, and nanomedicine applications.

Materials and methods

Gold nanocluster synthesis

Preparation of GSH-stabilized AuNCs was carried out using a procedure adapted from previous research.18,19 In a typical synthesis, 50 mL of an 8 mM aqueous HAuCl4 solution was combined with 50 mL of 6 mM reduced glutathione (GSH) under gentle stirring. The mixture initially turned dark orange, which gradually faded to colorless. The reaction solution was then incubated at 90 °C for 24 hours with continuous mild agitation (500 rpm). Upon completion, the final product was yellow, indicating the successful formation of GSH-capped gold nanoclusters. Additionally, to concentrate the AuNCs, we removed 50 mL of water using a rotary evaporator and then diluted the residue with 10 mL of fresh water. The final product was centrifuged to remove aggregation and stored at 4 °C.

Gold nanocluster-loaded liposome preparation (Lipo-AuNCs)

AuNC-loaded liposomes were prepared using a thin-film hydration method followed by sonication-assisted nanoparticle coating. DSPC, cholesterol, and DSPE-PEG2000 (32, 27, and 5.6 mg, respectively; molar ratio 3 : 2 : 0.15) were dissolved in chloroform and evaporated using a rotary evaporator at 40–45 °C to form a uniform thin lipid film. The film was dried under vacuum for 1–2 hours to remove residual solvent. The lipid film was hydrated with 5 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), vortexed to form multilamellar vesicles, and then incubated overnight at 4 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere to minimize oxidation. For lipid coating, 4 mg of nanoparticles were added to the hydrated lipid suspension and subjected to probe sonication (15 minutes, 15 seconds on/off cycles, 20% amplitude) under ice-bath conditions. The resulting dispersion was centrifuged at low speed to remove aggregates, and the supernatant containing lipid-coated AuNCs was collected. Samples were further purified by centrifugation and washed with PBS to remove excess free lipids, then redispersed in PBS for subsequent characterization.

Characterization

UV-visible absorption spectra were recorded using a JASCO V-650 spectrophotometer. The crystalline lattice structure of the AuNCs was examined using high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) on a G2 F30 instrument operated at 300 kV. Prior to imaging, AuNC samples were deposited onto ultrathin copper grids. The crystal structure of the glutathione-stabilized gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) was characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD). The purified aqueous AuNC dispersion was drop-cast onto a clean glass sample holder and dried at room temperature under ambient conditions to obtain a thin solid film for analysis. XRD measurements were performed using a Rigaku MiniFlex 600 X-ray diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) and operated at 40 kV and 15 mA. Diffraction patterns were recorded over a 2θ range of 10–80° at room temperature. A dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Malvern Zetasizer (Malvern Instruments, UK) measured particle size in aqueous suspension. A JASCO spectrofluorometer recorded fluorescence excitation and emission spectra of AuNCs and HSA–AuNCs complexes. Time-resolved fluorescence measurements were performed using a FluoroCube system (Horiba Jobin Yvon, USA), with the AuNCs excited at 370 nm using a pulsed spectral light-emitting diode (LED). Experimental decay curves were fitted, and the goodness of fit was evaluated by ensuring the chi-squared (χ2) value was close to 1. CD spectra were measured using a JASCO J-725 spectropolarimeter with a 1 mm quartz cuvette at a scanning rate of 100 nm min−1. We performed 3 scans per sample (n = 3), and the average spectrum was used for secondary structure calculations. FTIR spectra, particularly focusing on HSA amide bands, were obtained using a JASCO FTIR-6600 spectrometer. Spectra were recorded in the 400–4000 cm−1 range with a resolution of 0.4 cm−1 and a signal-to-noise ratio of 45 000 : 1. Each spectrum represented an average of 64 scans collected in attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode at room temperature. The photostability of AuNCs was assessed under intense 254 nm UV irradiation for 30 min. The UV radiation intensity was measured at 610 µW cm−2 from a distance of 50 mm.

Cell culture and cytotoxicity of AuNCs

Normal human skin fibroblast cell line (JCRB0527), HeLa (JCRB9004), and A549 (JCRB0076) were obtained from the JCRB Cell Bank, Japan. Fibroblast, HeLa, and A549 cell lines were cultured in 75 cm2 flasks at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The cells were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma-Aldrich), 5000 U mL−1 penicillin, and 50 µg mL−1 streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich). All other tissue culture reagents were obtained from Fisher Scientific. For cytotoxicity assessment, cells were seeded at 5000 cells per well in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Following this preincubation period, the cells were treated with various concentrations of AuNCs and incubated for an additional 24 and 48 h. Cell viability was then evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo Laboratories), and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite F50 Plus, Japan). All experiments were performed in triplicate. Cell viability data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation, and cytotoxicity is calculated as the percentage relative to untreated control cells.

Live cell imaging of AuNCs and Lipo-AuNCs in different cellular models

To visualize the cellular uptake of AuNCs, confocal laser-scanning fluorescence microscopy was performed using a FluoView FV10i. Fibroblasts, HeLa, and A549 cells were seeded in 35 mm glass-bottom dishes and allowed to adhere for 24 hours. AuNCs were added at final concentrations of 1–5 mg mL−1. After a 24 hour incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS. The cells were then imaged using the confocal microscope under appropriate fluorescence settings.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking simulations were performed to understand the binding interactions of GSH and HSA. The 3D chemical structure of GSH was extracted from PubChem with ID CID 124886, and the crystal structure of human serum albumin was obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank with PDB ID 1N5U. Initially, the protein structure was preprocessed using PyMOL to remove crystal waters and native ligands. Then, the GSH ligand was subjected to force-field optimization using the Avogadro software package.20–22 Molecular docking was performed using the Autodock4 and AutoDock software packages.23,24 Initially, the protein structure was loaded into the working environment, and hydrogen atoms were added. Non-polar hydrogens were then merged. Kollman charges were added, and the residual charges were distributed to the entire protein structure. Then, the AD4 force field type was assigned and saved in .pdbqt format. Subsequently, the optimized ligand molecule was introduced into the working space, and the ligand torsions were set and saved in .pdbqt format. Here, the protein was kept rigid, while the ligand molecule was allowed to be flexible. The grid parameters were set to the native ligand coordinates (X = 32.059288, Y = 7.786726, and Z = 32.745959) and saved as a .gpf file, after which autogrid was executed. Following that, the genetic algorithm parameters were set to 100 runs and 25 000 000 evaluations. Then it was saved as a Lamarckian genetic algorithm .dpf file, and AutoDock4 was executed. Based on the docking score, the best-docked pose was extracted. The results were analyzed using Biovia Discovery Studio.

Density functional theory

DFT calculations were carried out using the Gaussian 16 software package and analyzed with Gauss-View.25 The B3LYP functional was used to optimize GSH with the 6-311G++ basis set.26 Geometry optimization was carried out for the ligand before docking, and a single-point energy calculation was conducted for the ligand after docking. The gold cluster was approximately 6 Å in diameter. To comprehend the interaction between gold nanoclusters and GSH in both its neutral and deprotonated states, geometry optimization was performed by maintaining a neutral charge for gold and GSH, while assigning a charge of −1 to deprotonated glutathione thiolate. For the deprotonated glutathione thiolate, the same basis set was used, and for the gold cluster, the LANL2DZ basis set was used.

Results and discussion

Physical characterization of luminescent AuNCs

The morphology and size distribution of the synthesized AuNCs were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and dynamic light scattering (DLS). Fig. 1a and b illustrate that the TEM images of AuNCs are uniformly dispersed, exhibiting ultra-small sizes as well-defined dark dots without observable aggregation. High-magnification images further verify the monodispersity of the nanoclusters, with sizes confined to the sub-5 nm range. Elemental mapping (Fig. 1c) demonstrates a homogeneous distribution of Au, C, N, O, and S, indicating effective stabilization of AuNCs by organic ligands. The energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) spectrum (Fig. 1d) confirms the presence of characteristic Au signals alongside other constituent elements. Furthermore, EDX analysis confirms the presence of Au as the metallic component of the nanoclusters, while the detection of C, N, O, and S is consistent with the glutathione capping ligand (Fig. S1). The elemental mapping further demonstrates a uniform spatial distribution of Au and the ligand-associated elements throughout the sample. DLS analysis (Fig. 1e) reveals an average hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 3.4 nm with a narrow size distribution (PDI = 0.187), supporting the conclusion of good colloidal stability. TEM imaging following lipid coating (Fig. 1f) shows that the AuNCs remain well-dispersed within the lipid matrix, confirming successful encapsulation or coating without significant aggregation or structural change. The XRD pattern (Fig. S2) of the glutathione (GSH)-protected Au nanoclusters exhibits a broad diffraction halo centred at approximately 2θ = 21.7°, which is attributed to the predominantly amorphous GSH ligand shell surrounding the metallic Au cores. In addition, weak diffraction peaks corresponding to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) crystallographic planes of face-centred cubic (fcc) gold (JCPDS PDF no. 04-0784) are observed. The broad, low-intensity diffraction peaks indicate that the metallic Au domains are ultrasmall and exhibit limited long-range crystallinity, characteristic of ligand-protected fluorescent gold nanoclusters. The weak diffraction intensity arises from the extremely small crystalline core size together with the dominant scattering contribution of the surrounding amorphous GSH shell. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the formation of ultrasmall, stable, and uniformly distributed AuNCs appropriate for subsequent biomedical applications. Fig. S3 shows the AuNCs-loaded liposome, the lipids and cholesterol used, and the fluorescence confocal images of the AuNCs-loaded liposome and the liposome. The overlay images clearly indicate that the AuNCs are trapped inside the liposome.

Spectral characterization of luminescent AuNCs

The optical properties of the synthesized AuNCs were systematically investigated using UV-visible absorption and fluorescence spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 2a, the AuNCs exhibit a strong absorption band in the UV region (∼250–300 nm) with no characteristic surface plasmon resonance peak around 520 nm, confirming the formation of ultrasmall nanoclusters rather than larger gold nanoparticles. This behaviour is consistent with quantum-confined AuNCs possessing discrete energy levels.

UV-Visible absorption spectra of AuNCs (a), excitation and emission spectra of AuNCs (b), fluorescence lifetime of AuNCs (c), and AuNCs under daylight and UV light. Photostability of AuNCs under intense UV light (254 nm) compared with commercial dye rhodamine B.

The photoluminescence properties of the AuNCs were evaluated using excitation and emission spectroscopy (Fig. 2b). The excitation spectrum exhibits a maximum at approximately 400 nm, while a strong emission peak appears at approximately 590 nm, indicating a large Stokes shift. This red emission is characteristic of ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT) transitions and surface state-mediated emission in AuNCs. Digital images under white light and UV illumination at 365 nm further confirm the formation of luminescent nanoclusters, as evidenced by a distinct orange-red fluorescence (Fig. 2d).

The fluorescence quantum yield (QY) of the synthesized AuNCs and Lipo-AuNCs was measured at an excitation wavelength of 400 nm (Fig. S4 and S5). Pristine AuNCs exhibited a QY of 2.68%, consistent with ligand-stabilized nanoclusters that display moderate emission efficiency due to ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT) and surface state-mediated radiative recombination. Encapsulation within the lipid bilayer reduced the QY to 1.97%, indicating partial fluorescence quenching in the Lipo-AuNC system. This decrease is attributed to alterations in the local microenvironment, such as restricted molecular mobility, changes in the dielectric environment, and the introduction of non-radiative relaxation pathways resulting from lipid–nanocluster interactions. Furthermore, partial shielding of excitation light and energy dissipation within the lipid matrix may contribute to the reduced emission efficiency. Nevertheless, Lipo-AuNCs maintain significant fluorescence, indicating that liposomal encapsulation preserves the photoluminescent properties of AuNCs while enhancing colloidal stability and biocompatibility.

Time-resolved fluorescence decay measurements (Fig. 2c) reveal an average lifetime (τ0) of approximately 40 ns, which is significantly longer than that of typical organic fluorophores. This extended lifetime suggests that the emission originates from triplet states or ligand–metal interactions, supporting the LMCT-based emission mechanism. The observed biexponential decay behavior indicates the presence of multiple emissive states or heterogeneous surface environments within the nanoclusters.

The catalytic activity of the AuNCs was assessed by monitoring the degradation of rhodamine B (RhB) under UV irradiation at 254 nm. Fig. 2e demonstrates that the fluorescence intensity of RhB decreases progressively with increasing irradiation time, indicating ongoing degradation. In the presence of AuNCs (Fig. 2f), the degradation process is significantly accelerated, as shown by a more rapid decline in fluorescence intensity. This enhanced photocatalytic performance is attributed to the efficient generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and improved charge separation facilitated by the AuNCs. The combined spectroscopic and catalytic results demonstrate that the synthesized AuNCs exhibit ultrasmall size, strong red emission with a large Stokes shift, long fluorescence lifetime, and excellent photocatalytic activity. These properties make them promising candidates for applications in bioimaging and environmental remediation.

Interaction between AuNCs and HSA: UV-vis absorption study

Albumin, synthesized in the liver, constitutes approximately 55% of blood plasma proteins. Beyond its role in maintaining osmotic pressure, albumin also facilitates the transport of lipids and steroid hormones. Given these functions, understanding the interaction between AuNCs and HSA is crucial for assessing the potential of AuNCs as drug delivery systems. HSA exhibits a characteristic absorption peak at 278 nm, which is attributed to aromatic amino acids such as tryptophan, tyrosine, and phenylalanine. Upon addition of AuNCs, the absorbance of HSA increased, indicating a possible interaction between the two (Fig. 3a). Steady-state and time-resolved study

The binding affinity between AuNCs and HSA was investigated using fluorescence emission spectroscopy. The intrinsic fluorescence of HSA arises mainly from its aromatic amino acid residues tryptophan, tyrosine, and phenylalanine, with tryptophan (Trp214) contributing predominantly due to its high quantum yield and sensitivity to microenvironmental changes.29 Upon interaction with ligands or nanomaterials, HSA fluorescence can be quenched via static or dynamic mechanisms, and may also involve nonradiative energy transfer processes such as Förster resonance energy transfer.30

In the present study, increasing concentrations of AuNCs led to a progressive decrease in HSA fluorescence intensity, accompanied by a slight red shift in the emission maximum across various temperatures (Fig. 4a–d). This red shift suggests increased polarity around the Trp214 residue, indicating conformational changes in HSA and partial unfolding of its native structure.31 These observations support the formation of a stable ground-state AuNC-HSA complex, altering the microenvironment of Trp214.

Fluorescence quenching spectra of HSA interaction with AuNCs at various temperatures (a) 20 °C, (b) 30 °C, (c) 37 °C, (d).40 °C, and its corresponding Stern–Volmer plots (e and f), logarithmic plots (g and h).

Fluorescence quenching can occur via dynamic (collisional) or static (complex formation) mechanisms. To distinguish between these processes, the Stern–Volmer plot (F0/F vs. [Q]) was constructed. Fig. 4e shows an upward deviation from linearity at higher AuNC concentrations, indicating mixed quenching behaviour or heterogeneous binding. At lower concentrations (Fig. 4f), a predominant static quenching mechanism is suggested.32 We used lower-concentration data for the Stern–Volmer equation:33–362F0/F = 1 + Kqτ0[Q] = 1 + Ksv[Q]was used to calculate the bimolecular quenching rate constant (Kq). The calculated bimolecular quenching constant (Kq) is close to the diffusion-controlled limit in aqueous media (∼2 × 1010 L mol−1 s−1). Further evidence from fluorescence lifetime and thermodynamic analysis is required to confirm the contribution of static quenching via ground-state complex formation. The fluorescence lifetime of HSA was taken as τ0 = 6.3 ns at 298 K.

Temperature-dependent fluorescence studies (20–40 °C) further supported the quenching mechanism. The binding constant (Kb) increased from 20 °C to 30–37 °C and then decreased at 40 °C, suggesting that complex formation is most favourable near physiological temperature, whereas higher temperature partially weakens the HSA-AuNC interaction.37 The modified Stern–Volmer plots (Fig. 4g and h) showed good linearity, yielding binding site numbers (n) between 1.84 and 2.06, indicative of approximately two binding sites on HSA, consistent with ligand binding at Sudlow's site I (subdomain IIA) and site II (subdomain IIIA), which are well-known hydrophobic pockets responsible for drug and nanoparticle binding.38 High correlation coefficients (R2 = 0.98–0.99) confirm the reliability of the binding model.

Thermodynamic parameters obtained from the van't Hoff analysis revealed negative Gibbs free energy (ΔG°) values at all studied temperatures, indicating a spontaneous binding process (Table 1). The positive enthalpy change (ΔH° = 23.7 kJ mol−1) and positive entropy change (ΔS° ≈ 4.0 J mol−1 K−1) suggest that the interaction is predominantly entropy-driven. According to established thermodynamic interpretations, positive ΔH° and ΔS° values are characteristic of hydrophobic interactions, in which the release of structured water molecules from the protein-ligand interface increases the system's entropy.39

HSA contains hydrophobic cavities in subdomains IIA and IIIA that play a crucial role in stabilizing hydrophobic ligands via nonpolar interactions. The observed increase in binding constant with temperature further supports an endothermic, hydrophobically driven binding mechanism. Similar thermodynamic signatures have been widely reported for HSA interactions with nanomaterials and small molecules, in which desolvation effects dominate the binding process.40 Overall, the combined spectroscopic and thermodynamic analyses indicate that AuNCs form a stable and spontaneous complex with HSA, primarily driven by hydrophobic interactions, with possible minor contributions from van der Waals forces.

While steady-state fluorescence spectroscopy monitors the quenching of the intrinsic fluorescence of HSA by AuNCs, time-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy probes changes in the excited-state dynamics of the AuNCs upon HSA binding. Together, these complementary techniques provide a comprehensive understanding of the interaction mechanism.

Time-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy (TCSPC) was employed to investigate the excited-state interaction between AuNCs and HSA at HSA concentrations ranging from 12.5 to 100 nM. The fluorescence decay profiles (Fig. 5) exhibited tri-exponential behaviour, indicating heterogeneous emissive environments associated with glutathione-stabilized AuNCs. The pristine AuNCs (100 µg mL−1) displayed an average fluorescence lifetime of 39.9 ns, which gradually decreased to 38.2 ns upon increasing the HSA concentration to 100 nM (Table 2). Concurrently, the longest lifetime component (τ3) decreased from 43.0 to 41.0 ns, accompanied by small variations in the relative amplitudes, indicating subtle perturbations in the excited-state relaxation pathways following HSA binding.

Although a measurable decrease in fluorescence lifetime was observed, the overall decay profiles remained largely unchanged. The reduction in τavg was only 1.7 ns (approximately 4.3%), whereas the steady-state fluorescence intensity decreased much more substantially. Such behaviour is characteristic of predominantly static quenching, where the fluorophore forms a non-fluorescent ground-state complex with the quencher, resulting in significant intensity quenching with only a minor effect on fluorescence lifetime. If dynamic (collisional) quenching were the dominant mechanism, a much larger decrease in fluorescence lifetime would be expected.

The slight shortening of the fluorescence lifetime suggests that a minor contribution from additional non-radiative relaxation pathways may accompany the dominant static quenching process. This interpretation is consistent with the UV-Vis absorption spectra, Stern–Volmer analysis, binding constant measurements, and FRET calculations, all of which support the formation of a stable AuNC–HSA complex. The binding of HSA alters the local microenvironment around the glutathione-protected AuNCs, resulting in modest changes in their excited-state relaxation dynamics.

Furthermore, the donor–acceptor distance obtained from the FRET analysis indicates that HSA and AuNCs are within the distance range required for non-radiative energy transfer. However, the relatively small decrease in the average fluorescence lifetime of AuNCs (39.9 to 38.2 ns) compared with the pronounced quenching of HSA steady-state fluorescence suggests that FRET is not the primary quenching pathway. Instead, the lifetime data support a predominantly static quenching mechanism arising from ground-state complex formation, while FRET provides an additional non-radiative relaxation pathway resulting from the close spatial proximity between HSA and AuNCs. Overall, the steady-state fluorescence, TCSPC, thermodynamic consistently demonstrate the formation of a stable AuNC-HSA complex governed primarily by static quenching with a minor mixed-quenching contribution.

Fluorescence contour mapping and structural insights

Fluorescence contour plots confirmed significant conformational changes in HSA upon interaction with AuNCs. At an excitation wavelength of 280 nm, the emission spectrum is strongly influenced by tryptophan residues, known for their sensitivity to environmental changes. HSA is composed of three domains (I–III), each containing two subdomains (A and B), where hydrophobic amino acid residues are predominantly buried within the protein core. The binding of AuNCs within these hydrophobic pockets likely perturbs local polarity, leading to the observed conformational alterations.

Fig. 6a and b show the two-dimensional fluorescence spectra of native HSA and the HSA–AuNCs complex, respectively, illustrating marked spectral differences between them. In the native HSA spectrum, two distinct fluorescence peaks, designated as peaks A and B, are evident. Peak B is associated with tryptophan and tyrosine residues and aligns with the characteristic UV-vis absorbance peak at 278 nm, attributed to the π → π* transition of aromatic side chains.

Excitation and emission matrix of HSA (a) interaction with AuNCs (b). Peak a is due to aromatic amino acids, and peak b represents peptide bonds. Synchronous fluorescence spectra of tyrosine (c) and tryptophan (d) from HSA before and after interaction with AuNCs.

In contrast, peak A appears at an excitation wavelength of approximately 230 nm and exhibits strong fluorescence emission. This region corresponds to the n → π* transition of the carbonyl (CO) groups in the polypeptide backbone, consistent with the absorption band observed at 222 nm in the UV-Vis spectrum. Additionally, the CD spectra exhibit negative ellipticity bands at 208 and 222 nm, further supporting the presence of α-helical structures and confirming the contribution of n → π* transitions. These spectral observations suggest that AuNCs binding induces noticeable alterations in HSA's tertiary structure, particularly affecting the environment of aromatic and carbonyl groups associated with secondary structural elements.

An analysis of the intensity variation at peaks A and B, in the presence and absence of AuNCs, reveals intensity ratios of 1 : 0.85 for peak A and 1 : 0.36 for peak B. This decrease indicates changes in the peptide strands and variations in α-helix percentages. Thus, the interaction between HSA and AuNCs induces a slight unfolding of the polypeptide strands, exposing previously buried hydrophobic regions. These findings suggest that HSA–AuNCs binding leads to conformational changes in HSA. Moreover, the AuNCs peak appears at an emission wavelength of approximately 500 nm, confirming the presence of a complex between the protein and AuNCs.

Synchronous fluorescence spectroscopy

Synchronous fluorescence spectroscopy (SFS), also known as Stokes shift spectroscopy (SSS), is a sensitive and insightful technique for probing protein conformational changes. This method enhances spectral resolution by scanning excitation and emission wavelengths with a fixed wavelength offset (Δλ). It simplifies complex spectra, particularly for analyzing systems with multiple fluorophores. One of the key advantages of SFS is its ability to minimize spectral overlap and reduce background interference, thereby improving signal specificity. Additionally, it provides valuable information about the local environment of chromophoric residues, such as tryptophan and tyrosine, enabling a detailed investigation into protein structural dynamics and interactions. These attributes make SSS a powerful tool for elucidating molecular-level changes during ligand binding or environmental perturbations.41

Stokes shift spectroscopy helps us understand two amino acids: tyrosine (Tyr) and tryptophan (Trp). Tyrosine has a shift value of 15 nm, while tryptophan has a shift value of 60 nm. The impact of AuNCs on the synchronous fluorescence of HSA is presented in Fig. 6c and d. The introduction of AuNCs led to a notable decrease in fluorescence intensity for both tyrosine and tryptophan residues. This reduction in intensity correlates with increasing AuNCs concentrations, suggesting an interesting alteration in the microenvironment surrounding these residues. We measured the synchronous fluorescence spectra of tyrosine and tryptophan at 280 nm, in both the absence and presence of varying concentrations of AuNCs. These findings highlight the significant role AuNCs play in shaping the fluorescence characteristics of HSA, warranting further exploration of the underlying mechanisms.

Fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET)

The fluorescence quenching observed in the interaction between HSA and AuNCs can be attributed to Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET), a distance-dependent nonradiative energy transfer process. A significant spectral overlap between the photoluminescence (PL) emission spectrum of HSA (donor) and the UV-visible absorption spectrum of AuNCs (acceptor) was observed (Fig. 7a), fulfilling the primary requirement for FRET.29,30 The AuNCs exhibit a broad absorption band in the 350–450 nm region that effectively overlaps with the emission profile of HSA.

(a) The overlap of the normalized fluorescence emission spectrum of HSA and the normalized absorption spectrum of AuNCs. (b) Schematic diagram explaining the FRET mechanism from the HSA (donor) to AuNCs (acceptor).

The PL spectrum of HSA substantially overlaps with the absorption spectrum of the AuNCs, confirming that HSA functions as the donor in the FRET system. This interaction clarifies the proposed FRET mechanism, where the AuNCs absorb energy from the donor HSA (Fig. 7b).

The Förster distance (R0), which represents the donor–acceptor separation at 50% energy transfer efficiency, is given by:4R06 = 8.8 × 10−25 [κ2 n−4 Φ J]where κ2 is the orientation factor (κ2 = 2/3), n is the refractive index of the medium (n = 1.33), Φ is the quantum yield of the donor (Φ = 0.11), and J is the overlap integral. The calculated value of J = 2.8 × 10−14 cm3 L mol−1 indicates moderate spectral overlap. The calculated Förster distance in this study is R0 = 2.6 nm.

Using the experimentally determined efficiency (E = 25.8%), the donor–acceptor distance was calculated to be r = 3.1 nm.

Since the calculated distance satisfies the condition 0.5 R0 < r < 1.5 R0. The r value suggests that AuNCs are in close proximity to the primary fluorophore responsible for intrinsic emission.38 According to Valuer et al., the maximum academic value for R0 should be <10 nm, and r should be less than 8 nm. In the present study, both R0 and r values are well within the permissible limits. These results indicate a high probability of energy transfer from HSA to AuNCs.43

The FRET results complement the fluorescence lifetime, UV-Vis absorption, Stern–Volmer, and thermodynamic analyses. Although the calculated donor–acceptor distance supports close molecular proximity and the possibility of energy transfer, the relatively small decrease in the average fluorescence lifetime of AuNCs (39.9 to 38.2 ns) compared with the pronounced steady-state fluorescence quenching indicates that FRET is not the dominant quenching mechanism. Instead, the interaction is governed primarily by ground-state complex formation (static quenching), while FRET provides an additional non-radiative relaxation pathway arising from the close spatial arrangement of HSA and AuNCs. These findings are consistent with the hydrophobic binding interactions inferred from the thermodynamic analysis and the conformational changes observed by spectroscopic measurements.

Protein structural stability and secondary structure analysis

The FT-IR spectroscopy technique is a powerful tool for probing biochemical changes in the macromolecular microenvironment, particularly in protein structures. Proteins contain a high density of amide and peptide bonds in their secondary structural regions, making FT-IR especially useful for detecting conformational changes upon interaction with small molecules and nanoparticles. Fig. 8a presents the FT-IR spectra of free HSA and the HSA–AuNCs complex, covering the wavenumber range of 400–2000 cm−1. In proteins, the amide I and amide II bands typically appear in the 1500–1700 cm−1 region, primarily due to CO stretching and N–H bending vibrations in the peptide backbone. Among these, the amide I band, located near 1650 cm−1, is more intense and informative for structural analysis. In the free HSA spectrum, a prominent amide I peak appears at around 1651 cm−1. Upon complexation with AuNCs, this peak slightly shifts to approximately 1653 cm−1, suggesting perturbation in the N–H bending and CO stretching vibrations, likely due to interactions between AuNCs and the protein backbone. Furthermore, the FT-IR spectrum of the HSA–AuNCs complex displays two additional peaks in the 800–1200 cm−1 range, which are absent in the spectrum of free HSA. These new features indicate the successful incorporation of AuNCs into the HSA microenvironment. The observed spectral changes, particularly the shift in the amide I region and the emergence of new bands, suggest that AuNCs interact with HSA and may induce rearrangements in its native secondary structure.

(a) FT-IR study of HSA interaction with AuNCs (number of scans n = 64). (b) Circular dichroism study of HSA interaction with AuNCs (number of scans n = 3).

CD spectroscopy is a highly sensitive technique for monitoring conformational changes in protein secondary structure. The application of energy-transfer mechanisms in AuNCs for fluorescence-based sensing is well established. Interactions between AuNCs nanoparticles and proteins such as HSA may alter key structural motifs, including α-helices, β-sheets, and β-turns. To explore this possibility, we recorded the CD spectra of HSA both in the absence and presence of AuNCs.

Secondary structural analysis was performed using the BeStSel online tool, which is optimized for interpreting CD spectra and predicting protein folding patterns (Micsonai et al., 2025).44 CD spectra were acquired in the far-UV range (200–250 nm) with incremental additions of AuNCs. As shown in Fig. 8b, the CD spectrum of native HSA exhibited characteristic features of a predominantly α-helical structure. Upon the addition of 1260 µg mL−1 AuNCs, the α-helical content of HSA decreased from 66.5% to 32.4%, suggesting a partial disruption of its native secondary structure. This observed reduction in α-helical content suggests that AuNCs interact with HSA, inducing only minor conformational changes in its secondary structure upon addition of 180 µg mL−1. These findings underscore the significant impact of AuNCs on protein structure and function, highlighting the critical need for comprehensive toxicity assessments and a deeper understanding of safety-by-design considerations when AuNCs are used above 180 µg mL−1.

Based on Koopman's theorem, globalized descriptors were analyzed and tabulated in Table 4. The chemical hardness (η) and softness (σ) quantify the resistance and ease, respectively, of a molecule to deformation of its electron cloud. A lower η and higher σ indicate greater polarizability and charge-transfer ability. Before docking, glutathione (GSH) has a high chemical hardness (η = 2.52 eV), which means that its electronic structure is stable and not very polarizable. The ionization potential (I), electron affinity (A), and electronegativity (χ) give us more information about how GSH behaves electronically. The ionization potential stays almost the same (6.53–6.60 eV), which means that GSH's ability to give up electrons is mostly the same even after docking. The chemical potential (µ) reflects the escaping tendency of electrons from a system. A negative chemical potential value indicates greater electron stability and a stronger electron-attracting ability. The electrophilicity index (ω) quantifies the stabilization energy upon acquiring additional electron density. A higher ω value corresponds to a stronger tendency to accept electrons. The electrophilicity of GSH increased sharply from 3.20 eV (before docking) to 60.23 eV (after docking), reflecting a drastic enhancement in its electron-accepting ability within the HSA environment.

Cell viability and localization of AuNCs

In our research, we examined the biocompatibility of AuNCs through cell viability assays and cellular uptake experiments. Cell viability was measured in fibroblasts, HeLa cells, and A549 cells (Fig. 12). After 24 hours of exposure, all cell lines showed nearly 100% viability, indicating minimal cytotoxicity even at concentrations up to 1000 µg mL−1. At the 48 hour mark, a slight decrease in viability (less than 20%) was observed across all cell types, remaining considerably lower than the toxicity levels typically associated with heavy-metal nanomaterials. To further evaluate biocompatibility, cellular uptake was visualized using confocal fluorescence microscopy. All three cell lines, fibroblasts, HeLa, and A549, showed successful internalization of AuNCs (Fig. 13). Notably, there was no detection of AuNCs moving into the nucleus. Similarly, we assessed Lipo-AuNCs uptake in fibroblasts (Fig. S9 and S10), HeLa (Fig. S11 and S12), and A549 (Fig. S13 and S14). Compared with AuNCs, the uptake appears more intense around the nucleus, and fibroblast cells are more morphologically sensitive than other cell types. The internalization of AuNCs/Lipo-AuNCs is likely mediated by multiple endocytic pathways, including clathrin- and caveolae-mediated endocytosis, macropinocytosis, phagocytosis, and mechanisms independent of clathrin and caveolin.

Conclusions

Glutathione-stabilized, red-emitting gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) with ultrasmall crystalline cores (<3 nm by TEM) and a hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 5 nm were synthesized. These AuNCs exhibited a long fluorescence lifetime (∼40 ns) and maintained excellent photostability under continuous UV irradiation. The interaction between AuNCs and human serum albumin (HSA) was systematically investigated using spectroscopic, thermodynamic, computational, and biological methods to elucidate the molecular mechanisms at the nano-bio interface. Steady-state fluorescence, UV-Vis absorption, and Stern–Volmer analyses demonstrated strong binding between AuNCs and HSA through a stable ground-state complex. Thermodynamic analysis indicated that this interaction is spontaneous and predominantly hydrophobic. Time-resolved fluorescence measurements revealed only a slight decrease in the average fluorescence lifetime, supporting static quenching as the dominant mechanism. Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) analysis identified a donor–acceptor distance of 3.1 nm, indicating limited non-radiative energy transfer as a secondary relaxation pathway due to the close proximity of HSA and AuNCs. Circular dichroism, FTIR, and synchronous fluorescence spectroscopy showed only minor conformational changes in HSA (low concentration), suggesting that its overall secondary structure is largely preserved upon binding to AuNCs. Molecular docking and density functional theory (DFT) calculations provided additional evidence for the stability and specificity of the AuNC–HSA interaction, corroborating experimental results. The AuNCs also demonstrated excellent biocompatibility with fibroblast, HeLa and A549 cells across a broad concentration range. Confocal fluorescence microscopy confirmed efficient cellular internalization and perinuclear localization, while only concentration-dependent reductions in cell viability were observed at higher doses. Overall, these findings demonstrate that glutathione-stabilized AuNCs interact with HSA primarily through hydrophobic interactions and ground-state complex formation, resulting in predominantly static fluorescence quenching, with FRET as a secondary non-radiative process. This study provides new molecular-level insights into protein–nanocluster interactions and establishes a framework for the development of biocompatible AuNC-based platforms for bioimaging, biosensing, drug delivery, and future nanomedicine applications.
