(3R, 7S)-11-hydroxy-jasmonic acid is a major oxidative shunt product of jasmonic acid catabolism in Arabidopsis thaliana
Authors: Kotaro Matsumoto, Maria Mitsui, Takuya Kaji, Naoki Kitaoka, Ruiqi Gao, Taketomo Otaki, Yuho Nishizato, Hideyuki Matsuura, Minoru Ueda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Jasmonoyl-L-isoleucine (JA-Ile) is a pivotal oxylipin hormone that coordinates growth-defense trade-offs, and its precursor jasmonic acid (JA) is therefore subject to tight metabolic control. For more than a decade, the prevailing dogma has held that jasmonate turnover is dominated by C12-hydroxylation, with JOX dioxygenases acting on JA and CYP94 monooxygenases acting on JA-Ile. Here we revise this dogma by showing that hydroxylation at the C11 position constitutes a major deactivation shunt of JA turnover. Using chemically synthesized (3 R,7S)−11-hydroxy-JA (11-OH-JA) of naturally occurring stereochemistry as a standard, we unambiguously identify 11-OH-JA as the principal shunt product that accumulates in wounded Arabidopsis thaliana. In vitro enzymatic assays, in silico docking, and metabolite analyses in the jox quadruple mutant (joxQ) reveals that JOX1/2/3/4 exclusively converted JA to 11-OH-JA, whereas 12-OH-JA arose from distinct pathway. Moreover, 11-OH-JA failed to bind the COI1–JAZ co-receptor, establishing it as a biologically inactive endpoint of JA catabolism. These findings correct a long-standing misattribution of the JOX product to 12-OH-JA and redefine the metabolic framework of jasmonate. This study provides insights into JA catabolism, highlighting 11-OH-JA as a crucial factor in the attenuation of jasmonate signaling, and thereby redefining the framework of JA turnover in A. thaliana.

Introduction

The oxylipin hormone (3 R, 7S)-jasmonoyl-L-isoleucine (JA-Ile, Fig. 1A) is biosynthesized from C18/C16 polyunsaturated fatty acids in response to wounding, pathogen infection, or insect herbivory1–5. JA-Ile governs a wide array of physiological processes, collectively referred to as jasmonate responses, including growth, fertility, senescence, cold acclimation, and specialized metabolites production etc. JA-Ile exerts these effects by initiating profound changes in the cellular transcriptome through a unique co-receptor complex comprising the F-box protein CORONATINE INSENSITIVE 1 (COI1) and the JASMONATE ZIM DOMAIN (JAZ) transcriptional repressor6,7. Because constitutive JA-Ile signaling can compromise plant fitness8, signal termination must be as robust as activation. Across organisms, termination rarely relies on a single switch; rather, layered architectures prevent the costs of prolonged signaling. In Arabidopsis thaliana, the principal mechanism for JA-Ile attenuation is catabolic inactivation3,9,10. JA-Ile is sequentially oxidized by cytochrome P450 monooxygenases CYP94B1, CYP94B3, and CYP94C1 to (3R, 7S)-12-hydroxy-jasmonoyl-L-isoleucine (12-OH-JA-Ile, Fig. 1A) and then to (3R, 7S)-12-carboxy-jasmonoyl-L-isoleucine (12-COOH-JA-Ile, not shown)11–16. Whereas 12-OH-JA-Ile retains weak bioactivity17–19, 12-COOH-JA-Ile is inactive. Additionally, JA-Ile and 12-OH-JA-Ile are hydrolyzed by the amidohydrolases ILL6/IAR3 to form (3R, 7S)-jasmonic acid (JA) and (3R, 7S)-12-hydroxy-JA (12-OH-JA), respectively (Fig. 1A)20. Parallel to JA-Ile turnover, free JA is also oxidized into 12-OH-JA, a catabolic conversion historically attributed to 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases JASMONATE-INDUCED OXYGENASE/JASMONIC ACID OXIDASE (JOX/JAO)21,22 (Fig. 1A, #1). Because 12-OH-JA does not assemble the COI1–JAZ complex18, 12-OH-JA is viewed as a shunt product that helps quench signaling upstream of JA-Ile oxidation. Notably, another oxidized product, (3R, 7S)−11-hydroxy-JA (11-OH-JA, Fig. 1A), has been detected in multiple plant species23–26, and accumulates in A. thaliana leaves following wounding, at levels even higher than 12-OH-JA26. However, analytical conflation of 11- and 12-hydroxy JAs has further obscured product assignment.

Here we resolve this ambiguity and reposition jasmonate turnover within a layered termination logic. By using stereochemically defined 11-OH-JA standards, we reveal a parallel inactivation route in which JOX/JAO dioxygenases oxidize JA selectively at C11 to generate biologically inactive 11-OH-JA in upstream of JA-Ile oxidation into12-OH-JA-Ile (Fig. 1A, #2). This framework reframes jasmonate catabolism overview from a single terminal outlet to parallel outlets of layered architectures (Fig. 1A), which provide fast, fail-safe shut-off of costly defense signaling.

Results

Chemical syntheses of (3 R,7S, 11 R)-11-OH-JA and (3 R,7S, 11S)-11-OH-JA

To elucidate the biological role of 11-OH-JA, we first synthesized a stereochemically pure reference standard. Previous synthetic efforts yielded a mixture of trans-(3R, 7R)- and cis-(3R, 7S)- isomers at the C7 position, predominantly furnishing thermodynamically stable (3R, 7R)-11-OH-JA24. Here, we optimized the synthetic route by introducing ketone functional group in the cyclopentane ring at later stages of the synthesis, thereby minimizing isomerization to the thermodynamically more stable (3R, 7R)-form (Fig. 1B).

Following the approach of Nonaka et al. for cis−12-oxo-phytodienoic acid (cis-OPDA) synthesis27, we started the synthesis from 1 (Fig. 1B). After a series of transformations (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. 1), we obtained (3R, 7S, 11S)-11-OH-JA (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B) and the retention time was compared with those of a mixture of (3R, 7S, 11R/S)-11-OH-JA to determine the stereochemistry (Supplementary Fig. 4C)28. Comparison of the 1H-NMR spectra of the isolated stereoisomers, cis-(3R, 7S, 11R)-11-OH-JA and cis-(3R, 7S, 11S)-11-OH-JA, with those of the separately synthesized mixture of cis-(3R, 7S, 11R/11S)-11-OH-JA and trans-(3R, 7R, 11R/11S)-11-OH-JA (4:96)24 indicated the absence of trans-(3R, 7R, 11R/S)-11-OH-JA in our synthetic samples (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 3). These results allowed us to unambiguously characterize the structures of the two synthetic standards: (3R, 7S, 11R)-11-OH-JA and (3R, 7S, 11S)-11-OH (Supplementary Fig. 4C).

This refined synthetic strategy provided sufficient quantities of highly pure (3R, 7S, 11R)-11-OH-JA and (3R, 7S, 11S)-11-OH-JA, enabling accurate analytical separation from its isomeric counterpart, 12-OH-JA, and facilitating functional assays that had been previously hampered by lack of a suitable standard.

We observed the accumulations of 11/12-OH-JAs in wounded leaves of wild-type A. thaliana (Col-0) at 60, 120, 300, and 480 min (Fig. 3A). Interestingly, both 11R and 11S stereoisomers were detected, suggesting that oxidation at the C11 position in wounded A. thaliana occurs in a non-stereoselective manner. Furthermore, 11-OH-JAs with both 7S and 7 R configurations were found in plant extracts, although the 7S form is the biosynthetically derived stereoisomer. This likely reflects the intrinsic lability of the C7-stereochemistry in 11-OH-JA, which undergoes unstable cis-(3R, 7S) into thermodynamically stable trans-(3R, 7R) isomerization during extraction, as seen for 12-OH-JA, JA, and JA-Ile29–32. The level of (3R, 7S)−12-OH-JA started to accumulate from 60 min and peaked at 120 min post-wounding (Fig. 3A–C). In contrast, the four stereoisomers of 11-OH-JA, (3R, 7R/S, 11R/S)−11-OH-JA (Fig. 2), exhibited delayed accumulation from 120 min, reaching maximal levels at 300 min post-wounding (Fig. 3A–C). In parallel with the accumulation of 11/12-OH-JAs, JA levels coordinately declined after 120 min after wounding (Fig. 3B, C). Notably, at 300 min post-wounding, the total level of 11-OH-JA (ca. 2,000 pmol/gFW) exceeded that of 12-OH-JA (ca. 770 pmol/gFW) (Fig. 3B, C), consistent with previous GC-MS analyses indicating that 11-OH-JA is the major hydroxylated JA catabolite in wounded Arabidopsis leaves26. In addition, to evaluate the metabolic mass balance of 11/12-OH-JA, we examined the in planta metabolic dynamics of cis-OPDA-d533. We administered cis-OPDA-d5 (1 nmol) to Col-0 plants and analyzed downstream metabolites by UPLC–MS/MS (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Based on the quantified products, we calculated the 11-OH-JA: 12-OH-JA ratio (22 ± 0.42 pmol: 8.8 ± 1.6 pmol = 2.5: 1.0 at 5 h) (Supplementary Fig. 5B, C), which confirmed that 11-OH-JA is the major shunt product of JA catabolism.

Biological assays using 11-OH-JA

Given that jasmonic acid (JA) elicits diverse defense-related and developmental responses via its bioactive conjugate, JA-Ile, we next assessed the ability of 11-OH-JA to bind the COI1–JAZ co-receptor complex, which is essential for triggering jasmonate responses. A pull-down assay using GST-tagged COI1 (GST–COI1) and various JAZ degron peptides (JAZP1–6/9–12, containing Jas-motif required for interaction with JA-Ile)34,35 revealed that (3R, 7S, 11R)−11-OH-JA and (3R, 7S, 11S)-11-OH-JA (up to 100 µM) did not promote COI1–JAZ interaction (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. 6). This result align with prior result on 12-OH-JA, which also lacks binding affinity for COI1–JAZ18. Thus, similar to 12-OH-JA, 11-OH-JA acts as a non-bioactive catabolite of JA in A. thaliana.

In silico and in vitro analysis of JOX2-mediated oxidation of JA to 11-OH-JA

Multiple studies have implicated a family of 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases, JASMONATE-INDUCED OXYGENASE/JASMONIC ACID OXIDASE (JAO/JOX), in the direct oxidation of JA21,36,37. However, these earlier reports generally attributed the enzymatic product to “12-OH-JA” without explicitly ruling out 11-OH-JA, largely due to the absence of an authentic 11-OH-JA standard. To clarify the precise enzymatic product, we focused on JOX2, a key member JAO/JOX that is highly selective for JA37.

We began by examining the crystal structure of JOX2 bound to trans-(3R, 7R)-JA (PDB ID: 6LSV)37. Because the commercially available JA is typically a mixture of cis-(3R, 7S) and trans-(3R, 7R) isomers, the crystallized ligand was predominantly trans-(3R, 7R)-JA. To model the more biologically relevant cis-(3R,7S)-JA, we performed in silico docking simulations that placed (3 R, 7S)-JA within the JOX2 active site (Fig. 4A–D and Supplementary Fig. 7). This analysis revealed a set of conserved arginine residues (R225, R350, R354) forming hydrogen bonds with the carboxy and keto groups of JA, as well as a hydrophobic triad (F157, F317, F346) stabilizing the ligand through π-stacking and Van der Waals interactions37. Additional hydrophobic interactions are provided by Y135, L142, L241, P247, and I353 (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. 7C). These interactions ensure the precise positioning of (3R,7S)-JA, critical for oxidation. The iron center in JOX2 was located 4.04 Å and 4.37 Å from both C11 and C12 of JA, respectively (Fig. 4D). According to the previous study37, these distances are consistent with being within the range where oxidation can occur, starting with the abstraction of the first hydrogen atom, followed by rapid hydroxylation38. To test this docking-guided prediction experimentally, we heterologously expressed full-length JOX2 in Escherichia coli and purified the recombinant protein (Supplementary Fig. 8A, B). When incubated with a mixture of (3R, 7S)-JA and (3R, 7R)-JA, the enzyme yielded major products at the same retention times with our synthetic 11-OH-JAs, while no 12-OH-JAs were detected (Fig. 4E, F). These results directly contradict previous understanding that JOX2 converts JA into 12-OH-JA21,22,37,39.

We next asked whether other members of the JOX family (JOX1, JOX3, JOX4) also produce 11-OH-JA exclusively. Each isoform was expressed, purified (Supplementary Figs. 8A, C–Eand 9A–C), and assayed with (3R,7S)-JA and (3R,7R)-JA in parallel reactions. Like JOX2, all three enzymes converted JA solely into 11-OH-JA, without 12-OH-JA production (Fig. 4G–I). These findings demonstrate a shared substrate specificity and catalytic outcome across the JOX family (Kinetics parameters on (3R,7S)-JA27: Km: 68.9 μM for JOX1, 1.14 μM for JOX2, 11.5 μM for JOX3, 11.3 μM for JOX4; Kcat: 0.000261 s-1 for JOX1, 0.00930 s-1 for JOX2, 0.0315 s-1 for JOX3, 0.0315 s-1 for JOX4; Vmax: 33.2 nM/min for JOX1, 77.5 nM/min for JOX2, 267 nM/min for JOX3, 279 nM/min for JOX4, Supplementary Fig. 10), firmly establishing 11-hydroxylation as their primary route for JA turnover.

11-OH-JA Accumulation Is Abolished in the joxQ Quadruple Mutant

To confirm the physiological relevance of JOX-mediated 11-hydroxylation in planta, we measured the accumulation of 11-OH-JA in the joxQ quadruple mutant, in which JOX1, JOX2, JOX3, and JOX4 genes are impaired (Fig. 5A, C)21. Under mechanical wounding, the level of 11-OH-JA in WT plants peaked at 300 min (69% of 11/12-OH-JAs) (Fig. 5B). In contrast, the level of 11-OH-JAs in a joxQ mutant line decreased to approximately one-fourth of WT (17% of 11/12-OH-JAs), despite accumulating normal levels of 12-OH-JA (Fig. 5C). We next compared the accumulation of 11-OH-JA and 12-OH-JA in the single mutant lines jox1, jox2, jox3, and jox4 (Supplementary Fig. 11). 11-OH-JA levels were reduced in jox4, whereas no significant decrease in 11-OH-JA accumulation was detected in jox1, jox2, or jox3 relative to Col-0. For JOX1, previous studies have reported markedly low JOX1 expression level22, suggesting that JOX1 has little contribution in planta. In contrast, the significant reduction of 11-OH-JA accumulation in jox4 indicates that JOX4 contributes to 11-OH-JA accumulation in planta. However, 11-OH-JA levels were further decreased in the joxQ mutant compared with jox4 (Supplementary Fig. 11), indicating that JOXs other than JOX4 also contribute to 11-OH-JA accumulation. Although jox2 and jox3 single mutants did not show a significant reduction in 11-OH-JA accumulation, both JOX2 and JOX3 showed catalytic activity in in vitro assays using an E. coli expression system (Fig. 4F, 4H), and their expression has been reported to be induced upon wounding. These results support a model in which JOX2 and JOX3 function redundantly to contribute 11-OH-JA accumulation in planta.

11-OH-JA levels are unchanged in the cyp94b1b3c1 triple mutant

Finally, we examined whether CYP94 enzymes, which are responsible for stepwise oxidation of JA-Ile to 12-OH-JA-Ile and 12-COOH-JA-Ile9,11–15, might also contribute to 11-OH-JA formation via a possible “bypass” route—namely, 11-hydroxylation of JA-Ile followed by hydrolysis. To address this question, we profiled 11-OH-JA and 12-OH-JA in the cyp94b1b3c1 triple mutant (Fig. 5A)11,40. As anticipated, 12-OH-JA levels were not detected in this background (Fig. 5D), reflecting decreased conversion of JA-Ile to 12-OH-JA-Ile. However, 11-OH-JA remained at wild-type levels (Fig. 5D). This finding refutes the existence of a CYP94-dependent bypass that could produce 11-OH-JA. Instead, it affirms that JOX enzymes are solely responsible for oxidizing JA to 11-OH-JA, whereas CYP94 act upon JA-Ile to form 12-OH-JA-Ile (Fig. 1). Furthermore, we observed the ion peak corresponding to glucosylated and sulfoxylated hydroxy jasmonic acid (O-Glc-JA: 387 > 207 and HSO4-JA: 305 > 97) in wounded cyp94b1b3c1 mutant (Supplementary Fig. 12), suggesting the existence of further conversion of 11-OH-JA to 11-O-Glc-JA and 11-HSO4-JA, respectively.

In addition, we confirmed that the only trace of MS/MS (MRM) signal corresponding to 12- or 11-OH-JA-Ile (m/z 338 → 130; Supplementary Fig. 13) was observed in the extract of the wounded cyp94b1cyp94b3cyp94c1 triple mutant. This observation demonstrated that 11-OH-JA-Ile was present only at trace levels, indicating that JOX-mediated 11-OH-JA undergoes little, if any, conversion to 11-OH-JA-Ile under wounding conditions.

In summary, our results establish that 11-OH-JA is synthesized exclusively by the JOX family through direct 11-hydroxylation of JA. Unlike previous understanding, none of the JOX isoforms appreciably yield 12-OH-JA. Coupled with our evidence that 11-OH-JA lacks jasmonate-like activity in vitro COI1-JAZ co-receptor affinity, these data highlight a major yet previously underrecognized route for JA inactivation in A. thaliana.

Distinct effect of two JA catabolism pathways on jasmonate signaling

To dissect the distinct physiological roles of two JA metabolic routes (the CYP94 branch and the JOX branch), we compared wound-induced expression profiles of JA marker genes among WT, joxQ, and cyp94b1b3c1 lines (Fig. 5E).

The late-responsive marker VSP241–43 was expressed at a significantly higher level in joxQ than in the other genotypes at 300 min after wounding (Fig. 5E). Because conversion of JA to11-OH-JA mediated by JOX1–4 becomes a dominant metabolic route around 300 min after wounding (Fig. 3A–C), these results suggest that 11-OH-JA production via the JOX pathway strongly contributes to the regulation of late JA-responsive marker gene expression (Fig. 3B, C). In contrast, the early-responsive genes44 AOS and JAZ10 showed significantly higher induction in cyp94b1b3c1 than in the other genotypes at 60 and 120 min after wounding, whereas their expression in joxQ was not significantly different from that in WT (Fig. 5E).

Collectively, these data indicate that early wound responses (1–2 h) are primarily regulated by the CYP94B1/B3/C1-dependent pathway, which expresses within a few hours12, whereas later responses are mainly influenced by the JOX-dependent pathway, which functions several hours after wounding22, producing 11-OH-JA. Thus, the two metabolic branches likely play distinct, time-resolved roles in shaping JA-responsive transcriptional programs.

Discussion

In this study, we synthesized stereochemically pure (3 R,7S,11 R)-11-OH-JA and (3 R, 7S, 11S)-11-OH-JA (Fig. 1B, C) and leveraged it to uncover a previously underappreciated pathway in JA catabolism. By establishing UPLC analytical conditions that fully separate 11-OH-JAs from 12-OH-JAs, we revealed that 11-OH-JAs are the major hydroxylated JA catabolite that accumulates in A. thaliana after wounding. Moreover, our biochemical (Fig. 4E–H) and genetic (Fig. 5C) experiments demonstrate that JOX1/2/3/4 specifically convert JA into 11-OH-JA (Fig. 1A, #2). This finding contrasts with earlier understanding that JOX enzymes primarily produce 12-OH-JA21,22,37,39,45, highlighting the importance of chemically synthesized authentic reference compounds and careful analytical separation in elucidating hormone catabolism pathways.

Our time-course analyses indicate that 12-OH-JA begins to accumulate around 60 min post-wounding, whereas 11-OH-JA levels rise later (300 min) (Fig. 3A). This stepwise pattern underscores the distinct enzymatic routes leading to 11-OH-JA and 12-OH-JA in A. thaliana. Specifically, JOX-mediated 11-hydroxylation of JA emerges as a late time-course in diverting JA away from conversion into bioactive JA-Ile. In contrast, 12-OH-JA mostly arises from JA-Ile via CYP94-dependent oxidation (to form 12-OH-JA-Ile) followed by hydrolysis (Fig. 1A). The decreased level of 11-OH-JA in the joxQ quadruple mutant (Fig. 5C), which lacks JOX1/2/3/4, further confirms the role of JOX enzymes in 11-hydroxylation of JA. Notably, cyp94b1b3c1 mutants remain fully capable of producing 11-OH-JA (Fig. 5D), ruling out a bypass route via 11-hydroxylation of JA-Ile.

Upon wounding, expression of CYP94B3 genes rise sharply within 0.5–2 h, with CYP94B1 and CYP94C1 peaking at 1 h and 2 h, respectively12. In contrast, JOX expressions show a more gradual induction over 1-6 h, with JOX2/3 reaching peak levels around 6 h21. These distinct transcriptional kinetics provide a plausible explanation for the different accumulation profiles of 11/12-OH-JA after wounding and suggest that the two oxidative routes contribute independent physiological functions in shaping jasmonate deactivation dynamics.

Our biochemical and structural analyses suggest why JOX family members favor the allylic C11 of JA over the more terminal C12. In silico docking simulations of JOX2 with (3 R,7S)-JA indicate that C11 is optimally positioned for hydrogen abstraction and subsequent hydroxylation, yielding 11-OH-JA exclusively (Fig. 4A–D). The marked preference for C11 oxidation is also supported by in vitro assays in which recombinant JOX2 (as well as JOX1, JOX3, and JOX4) failed to produce 12-OH-JA (Fig. 4F). This finding underscores the potential pitfalls: earlier studies likely misattributed 11-OH-JA to 12-OH-JA because the two compounds are coelute under conventional UPLC–MS/MS conditions21,22,37,39,45.

Beyond defining a key oxidative route, our data establish 11-OH-JA as a biologically inactive metabolite. Like 12-OH-JA, 11-OH-JA shows no affinity for the COI1–JAZ co-receptor (Fig. 3D). Thus, 11-OH-JA functions as a shunt product, terminating JA signaling by depleting the pool of JA available for conversion into bioactive JA-Ile. This inactivation mechanism parallels the CYP94-driven pathway that targets JA-Ile itself; however, the two routes operate on different substrates (JA vs. JA-Ile) and rely on different enzymes (JOX vs. CYP94). In doing so, A. thaliana deploys multiple, nonredundant oxidative strategies to fine-tune jasmonate levels in response to developmental cues and environmental stresses.

The prominence of 11-OH-JA accumulation after wounding suggests that JOX-mediated oxidation plays an especially important role in modulating JA signaling under stress. Once formed, 11-OH-JA may also serve as a precursor to further modifications, such as conjugation, that remain to be fully characterized in our dataset (Supplementary Fig. 12). Indeed, while 12-OH-JA is further metabolized to forms such as 12-hydroxy jasmonoyl sulfate (12-HSO4-JA)3,26,39,46 or 12-hydroxy jasmonoyl glucoside (12-O-Glc-JA)3,26,47,48 (Supplementary Fig. 12), effectively removing it from the JA catabolism pathway.

The existence of parallel catabolic routes suggests that rapid JA inactivation confers critical fitness advantages. JA-related defense responses, while essential for survival under biotic assault, can inhibit growth and reproduction8. Accordingly, having multiple enzymes that deactivate both JA and JA-Ile helps balance defensive needs against developmental demands. The prevalence of 11-OH-JA in other plant species23–26 indicates that this mechanism is likely widespread. Future comparative studies may illuminate whether JOX-mediated 11-hydroxylation is similarly dominant in other species and how plants modulate the relative fluxes between 11- and 12-hydroxylated intermediates.

Heitz et al. reported that Botrytis infection favored the JOX/JAO-dependent hydroxylation route at the free-JA, with accumulation of OH-JA, whereas wounding predominantly engaged the CYP-dependent route in which JA-Ile is oxidized and subsequently deconjugated to yield 12-OH-JA. In addition, prior studies also report distinct induction kinetics after wounding for the C11-hydroxylating JOX/JAO versus the C12-hydroxylating CYP94s expressions (ca.30–120 min for CYP94s; ca.60–300 min for JOX/JAO)12,21,22, which is consistent with our interpretation. Taken together, these observations support the view that the C11-hydroxylation route functions as an “inactivation shunt” that rapidly depletes the pool of free JA not routed into JA-Ile during wound responses.

In summary, our results demonstrate that 11-hydroxylation of JA by JOX1/2/3/4 is a major route of jasmonate inactivation in A. thaliana (Fig. 1A, #2). By highlighting the exclusive production of 11-OH-JA, these findings update the previous understanding that JOX enzymes produce 12-OH-JA, and may reposition 11-OH-JA within the overall framework of JA turnover in A. thaliana. Because stereochemically pure synthetic standards of 11-OH-JA were unavailable in previous studies21,22, JA metabolites produced by JAO/JOX were analyzed without optimizing separation conditions of 11-OH-JA from 12-OH-JA, likely leading to misassignment of metabolites, highlighting the indispensability of using synthetic standards with unambiguous stereochemical definition for plant hormone metabolite analyses. More broadly, this expands our view of hormone turnover, revealing an additional layer of complexity in how plants attenuate JA responses. Our findings position jasmonate turnover within a layered signal-termination framework. Rather than relying on a single catabolic outlet, plants deploy plural non-redundant routes that act on different nodes of the jasmonate biosynthetic pathway: oxidation of JA to 11-OH-JA and JA-Ile to 12-OH-JA-Ile. Functionally, such layering increases robustness and speed, distinct modules can be differentially wired to environmental inputs, thereby limiting the duration and amplitude of defense signaling that otherwise imposes severe growth costs. Conceptually, this echoes a wider understanding in plant science: plant hormone signals are often terminated by distributed safeguards including ligand degradation, receptor desensitization or destruction, and transcriptional shut-off, which together create fail-safe attenuation. In jasmonate bioscience, placing 11-hydroxylation next to JA-Ile oxidation expands this repertoire and suggests testable predictions about how plants tune the growth–defense trade-off under fluctuating stress.

Chemical syntheses of (3 R, 7S, 11 R)-11-OH-JA and (3 R, 7S, 11S)-11-OH-JA

To a solution of diol 5 (18.5 mg, 62 μmol) in acetone (15.0 mL) was added Jones reagent (4.0 M solution, 600 μL, 2.4 mmol) at −20°C. After 1 h of stirring at −20°C, i-PrOH (800 μL) was added to quench the remaining reagent. After 1 h of stirring at −20°C, EtOAc/n-hexane (1/1, 10 mL) and H2O (20 mL) were added, and the water layer was extracted with EtOAc. The combined organic layers were washed with brine, dried over Na2SO4, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The residue was purified by silica gel medium-pressure chromatography (n-hexane/0.1%AcOH-EtOAc+0.1%AcOH = 84/16–0/100) to give a carboxylic acid intermediate (18.7 mg, 97%) as a colorless oil.

To a solution of the carboxylic acid intermediate (18.7 mg) in Et2O (10 mL) was added MgBr2•OEt2 (31.1 mg, 120 μmol). The resulting white suspension was stirred at room temperature for 1.5 h and dried under reduced pressure. The residue was dissolved with 35% aq. MeOH containing 0.1% acetic acid and syringe-filtered through PTFE membrane. The crude product was directly purified by preparative RP-HPLC (ODS-HG-5 ϕ20 × 250, detection: 210 nm, flow rate: 6.0 mL/min, eluent: 35% aq. MeOH + 0.1%AcOH) to give (3R, 7S, 11R)-OH-JA (tR = 27 min, 2.34 mg, 17%) and (3R, 7S, 11S)-OH-JA (tR = 32 min, 2.92 mg, 21%) as a colorless amorphous solid.

UPLC-MS/MS analyses of endogenous 11-OH-JAs and 12-OH-JAs in A. thaliana and the mutant lines

The six leaves per plant (ca. 100–200 mg FW) of the 5-week-old A. thaliana Col-0 and cyp94b1b3c1 were nipped tightly with tweezers. The wounded leaves after each time point and unwounded leaves were frozen in liquid nitrogen. The frozen sample was ground using a Micro Homogenizing System (Micro Smash™; TOMY, Tokyo, Japan) with a zirconia bead and extracted in subjected to extraction with EtOH (1.5 mL) for 16 h at 4°C. After centrifugation at 4300 rpm for 10 min, the supernatants were collected, and the volatile components of the extract were removed by using nitrogen blowdown evaporator, and the residue was dissolved in 80% aq. MeOH (150 μL). The solution was applied to an SPE C18 cartridge column (Sep-Pak C18 Plus Short Cartridge, 360 mg, Waters, Milford, MA, USA) and successively eluted with 80% aq. MeOH (700 μL × 3). After the eluent was removed by evaporation, the remaining residue was dissolved with 80% aq. MeOH (100 μL).

The five leaves per plant (ca. 50–200 mgFW) of the 7-week-old A. thaliana joxQ were nipped carefully with tweezers to avoid the main veins. The wounded leaves after 300 min and unwounded leaves were frozen in liquid nitrogen, crushed similarly, and subjected to extraction with EtOH (15 mL). After the plant residues were filtered, the volatile components of the extract were removed under reduced pressure, and the residue was dissolved in 80% aq. MeOH (2 mL). The solution was applied to a Bond Elut C18 cartridge column (1 g, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and successively eluted with 80% aq. MeOH (2 mL × 2). After the eluent was removed by evaporation, the remaining residue was dissolved in 80% aq. MeOH (600 μL).

The sample was examined by LC-MS/MS using an Ultivo Triple Quadrupole LC/MS system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) operating in the negative mode with a gas temperature at 230°C with a flow of 5.0 L/min, nebulizer at 20 psi, capillary voltage of 1500 V, sheath gas at 400 °C with a flow of 10.0 L/min and nozzle voltage of 0 V. The dwell times was 100 ms. For the detection of JA, 11/12-HSO4-JA, and 11/12-O-Glc-JA, UPLC-MS/MS analyses were performed with ZORBAX RRHD Eclipse Plus C18 1.8 μm (ϕ 2.1 × 50 mm; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used at 40°C with a flow rate of 0.350 mL/min. The elution was performed using a gradient of water (solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B), both containing 0.1% formic acid (v/v). The proportion of solvent B in the eluent was 5% for 2 min and was increased linearly from 5%–100% for 10 min, followed by a flow of 100% B for 2 min. The column was then re-equilibrated with 5% solvent B for 3 min. For the detection of 11-OH-JA and 12-OH-JA, Poroshell 120 PFP 1.9 μm (ϕ 2.1 × 50 mm; Agilent Technologies) was used at 23°C and a flow rate of 0.300 mL/min. The elution was performed using water-MeOH (95:5, v/v, isocratic), both containing 0.2% acetic acid (v/v). The liquid chromatograms were extracted by multiple reaction monitoring with the following ion transitions: JA: 209 > 59 (Fragmentor 75 V, collision energy 10 V), 11/12-HSO4-JA: 305 > 97 (Fragmentor 75 V, collision energy 10 V), 11/12-O-Glc-JA: 387 > 59 and 387 > 207 (Fragmentor 75 V, collision energy 10 V), 11-OH-JA: 225 > 59 (Fragmentor 65 V, collision energy 14 V) and 12-OH-JA :225 > 59 (Fragmentor 65 V, collision energy 14 V). Absolute quantification was performed using calibration curves of synthetic standards.

In silico docking study of (3R, 7S)-JA with JOX1-4

The initial structures of the (3R, 7R)-JA with JOX2 that were obtained were based on a crystal structure (PBD ID: 6LSV)37. Using MOE (version 2024.06, Chemical Computing Group Inc., Montreal, QC, Canada), hydrogen atoms were added and structurally optimized. (3R, 7S)-JA were modeled with ChemDraw and Chem3D software (Revvity Signals Software, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) and were subjected to MOE to add partial charges and minimize their energy. The whole structure was calculated with energy minimization with Amber10:EHT force field. The best-docking pose, those which have similar interactions as (3R,7R)-JA on a crystal structure, was selected. The homology models of JOX1/3/4 were obtained based on the crystal structure of the JOX2-2OG-iron-JA complex (PDB ID: 6LSV) using MOE. Docking study was performed similarly as JOX2.

Enzymatic activity assay of JOX1/2/3/4 with UPLC-MS/MS analyses

The 200 μM (-)-JA was dissolved in 10 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.4), containing 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 2 mM α-ketoglutarate, and 5 mM ascorbic acid to be adjusted to pH 8.0. To this solution, JOX1/2/3/4 (10 μg) was added respectively. and the solution was adjusted to 100 99 μl. Finally, 1 μL of 100 mM FeSO4 was added to the solution to initiate the reaction. The sample solution was incubated at 20°C for 2 h and then preserved on ice. After filtration, sample was diluted ten-fold with 5% aq. MeOH and 10 µL of the diluted sample solution was subjected to UPLC-MS/MS analysis observing produced 11-OH-JA or 12-OH-JA. A negative mode UPLC-MS/MS analysis was performed on an Ultivo Triple Quadrupole LC/MS system equipped with Poroshell 120 PFP 1.9 μm UPLC column (ϕ 2.1 × 50 mm; Agilent Technologies) (column temperature: 23°C, flow rate: 0.300 mL/min). The isocratic UPLC analysis was performed using water-MeOH (95:5, v/v) containing 0.2% acetic acid (v/v). The chromatograms were extracted by multiple reaction monitoring with the following ion transitions: 11-OH-JA 225 > 59 and 12-OH-JA 225 > 59.

Determination of Km and Vmax of JOX1-4

A substrate mixture containing 5 mM DTT, 5 mM ascorbic acid, 400 μM α-ketoglutarate, 0.6 mg/mL catalase, various concentrations of (3R, 7S)-JA (0–400 μM), and 50 mM Tris/HCl (pH 7.5) was prepared to 80 μL and incubated at 20°C for 5 min. Enzyme mixture containing JOX1 (10 μg) or JOX2/3/4 (600 ng), 100 μM FeSO4, 5 mM DTT, and 5 mM ascorbic acid was prepared to 20 μL and incubated at room temperature for 2 min. The reaction was initiated by mixing 80 μL substrate mixture with 20 μL enzyme mixture. Reactions were incubated for 90 min for JOX1, 10 min for JOX2, and 5 min for JOX3 and JOX4, and products was quantified by UPLC–MS/MS. Curve fittings in the Michaelis−Menten diagram was performed with Kaleidagraph (v4.1.1, Synergy Software).

Gene expression analysis
