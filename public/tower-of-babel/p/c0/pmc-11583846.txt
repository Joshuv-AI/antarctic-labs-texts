Regulation of maize growth and immunity by ZmSKI3‐mediated RNA decay and post‐transcriptional gene silencing
Authors: Jie Gao, Na Zhang, Guohui Liu, Jinjun Tian, Mengyao Chen, Ying Wang, Ye Xing, Ying Zhang, Chenyang Zhao, Xiaohuan Mu, Yanwen Yu, Hongbin Niu, Jiankun Li, Jihua Tang, Mingyue Gou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDisease resistance is often associated with compromised plant growth and yield due to defense‐growth tradeoffs. However, key components and mechanisms underlying the defense‐growth tradeoffs are rarely explored in maize. In this study, we find that ZmSKI3, a putative subunit of the SUPERKILLER (SKI) complex that mediates the 3′‐5′ degradation of RNA, regulates both plant development and disease resistance in maize. The Zmski3 mutants showed retarded plant growth and constitutively activated defense responses, while the ZmSKI3 overexpression lines are more susceptible to Curvularia lunata and Bipolaris maydis. Consistently, the expression of defense‐related genes was generally up‐regulated, while expressions of growth‐related genes were mostly down‐regulated in leaves of the Zmski3‐1 mutant compared to that of wild type. In addition, 223 differentially expressed genes that are up‐regulated in Zmski3‐1 mutant but down‐regulated in the ZmSKI3 overexpression line are identified as potential target genes of ZmSKI3. Moreover, small interfering RNAs targeting the transcripts of the defense‐ and growth‐related genes are differentially accumulated, likely to combat the increase of defense‐related transcripts but decrease of growth‐related transcripts in Zmski3‐1 mutant. Taken together, our study indicates that plant growth and immunity could be regulated by both ZmSKI3‐mediated RNA decay and post‐transcriptional gene silencing in maize.

INTRODUCTION

Plant disease causes huge yield losses in crop production, threatening global food security (Yang et al., 2017). Plants deploy sophisticated mechanisms to fend off microbial pathogens with many strategies (Jones and Dangl, 2006; Gou et al., 2023). A series of disease resistance genes have been cloned in major crops including maize, rice, and wheat (Zhu et al., 2021; He et al., 2022; Wulff and Krattinger, 2022). However, enhanced disease resistance is often accompanied by undesired adverse effects on growth and yield in different plants (He et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023). For example, significantly compromised plant growth was observed in maize lesion mimic mutants like Les4, Les8, and lls1/les30 that show enhanced resistance to multiple diseases (Simmons et al., 1998; Mu et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022a, 2024). Loss of function of RBL1 and MLO genes also caused constitutively activated defense responses and retarded plant growth in rice and wheat (Li et al., 2022b; Sha et al., 2023). Intriguingly, targeted gene editing of RBL1 and MLO conferred broad‐spectrum disease resistance without yield loss in rice and wheat (Li et al., 2022b; Sha et al., 2023), providing an effective strategy of resistance improvement by manipulating key components mediating defense‐growth tradeoffs.

RNA is under control of a sophisticated surveillance and turnover system consisting of an RNA exosome that catalyzes the 3′–5′ degradation of RNA and XRN4 that catalyzes the 5′–3′ cleavage of RNA (Januszyk and Lima, 2014; Zinder and Lima, 2017). The SUPERKILLER (SKI) complex, consisting of SKI2, SKI3, and SKI8 subunits, is the central cofactor of the cytoplasmic RNA exosome. Among them, SKI3 is a tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) protein, acting as the bridging subunit of the SKI complex typically mediating protein–protein interactions (Halbach et al., 2013; Kögel et al., 2022). SKI2 is an RNA helicase that contributes to RNA binding with substrate specificity (Schmidt et al., 2016), and SKI8 is a seven‐bladed β‐propeller with the wheel‐like characteristics of WD40‐repeat protein (Halbach et al., 2013; Schmidt et al., 2016).

Due to the functional importance of the SKI complex, studies of these proteins were performed in various species. In humans, mutation in either SKIV2L (SKI2) or TTC37 (SKI3) genes can cause severe disease like syndromic diarrhea (Fabre et al., 2013). In plants, pleiotropic roles of SKI complex were observed. For example, loss of function of SKI8 resulted in dwarfism and aberrant flower development in Arabidopsis (Dorcey et al., 2012). Instead, loss of function or silencing of Bsr‐k1, a close homolog of SKI3 did not affect plant growth but led to broad‐spectrum disease resistance in rice and cotton (Zhou et al., 2018b; Li et al., 2021). Further, post‐transcriptional gene silencing (PTGS) was found initiated when SKI functioning was disrupted in Arabidopsis (Yu et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2015; Zhao and Kunst, 2016). These studies collectively suggest that SKI complex is likely involved in the regulation of plant defense‐growth tradeoffs, although the underlying regulatory mechanisms in different plant species remain ambiguous. Therefore, SKI complex could be a candidate hub gene that is worthy of deeper investigation in different crops.

In this study, we find that dysfunction of ZmSKI3 and its interacting protein ZmSKI2 affects plant growth and kernel development. ZmSKI3's negative role in defense regulation is also confirmed by comprehensive genetic study and network dissection. Our study highlights the critical role of the ZmSKI3 in regulating both plant growth and immunity, thereby providing a valuable target for genetic modification toward balanced plant growth and disease resistance in maize.

RESULTS

ZmSKI3 is required for kernel development in maize

To investigate the role of putative SKI3 subunit in maize, we searched for the SKI3 homolog gene by protein sequence alignment. Only one protein, encoded by Zm00001d029833, shows high sequence similarity to the SKI3 homologs in human, yeast, Arabidopsis, and rice. We thus named it as ZmSKI3 (Figure S1A, B). Subsequently, we obtained two ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) mutants, Zmski3‐1 and Zmski3‐2, each harboring a premature stop codon in the coding region of ZmSKI3 (Figure 1A). Randomly distributed small kernels were observed on all ears of the Zmski3‐1/+ and Zmski3‐2/+ heterozygous plants (Figure 1B). To confirm the genetic relevance of Zmski3 mutations to maize kernel development, we performed allelic test by crossing the two heterozygous plants. We observed the same small kernel phenotype in all the F1 ears, confirming that ZmSKI3 is responsible for kernel development in maize (Figure 1B). Intriguingly, we observed an obvious less frequent occurrence of small kernels (Figure 1B; Tables S1, S2). In a segregation population on a heterozygous ear, 24 of the 184 kernels (13%) were small kernels, and all small kernels were of the homozygous genotype (Figure S2A; Dataset S1). For those morphologically normal kernels, most were wild type (WT) or heterozygous genotypes. However, 16 out of 160 normal kernels were of the homozygous genotype (Figure S2A; Dataset S1). Although those kernels were morphologically normal, all of the seedlings were dwarf, indicating the Zmski3/Zmski3 genotype is associated with retarded plant development, although a small portion of kernels could develop into visually normal kernels (Figure S2B). The ratio of homozygous kernels was 22% according to our genotyping data, which is consistent with the 1:3 mendelian segregation ratio, indicating the normal gametophytic development of Zmski3 mutant (Dataset S1; Table S3).

We then measured the kernel length, width, thickness, and 100‐kernel weight of those small kernels, and all the traits were significantly reduced in the Zmski3‐1 and Zmski3‐2 homozygous small kernels, which displayed obvious developmental defect in their longitudinal sections (Figures 1C–F, S3A–D). Further, when the Zmski3‐1 mutation was introgressed into the Chang7‐2 and Huangzao4 backgrounds, similar defective kernels were observed (Figure S3E, F).

To dissect the developmental morphologies of kernels in detail, paraffin sections of 18‐d‐old kernels of WT and Zmski3‐1 mutant were analyzed. In contrast to the well‐developed embryo of WT kernel (Figure 1G), the embryo of the mutant kernel was severely impaired (Figure 1H). In addition, defective filling of endosperm was revealed by strikingly reduced safranine O‐staining of starch granules in the endosperm of Zmski3‐1 mutant (Figure 1G, H). In line with this, irregular cell shape and lack of obvious cell wall ingrowth in the basal endosperm transfer layers (BETLs), critical structure for maize kernel filling, were observed (Figure 1G, H). Consistently, the expression levels of BETL marker genes, including BETL‐1, BETL‐2, BETL‐9, and BETL‐10, were all significantly down‐regulated in the kernels of Zmski3‐1 mutant (Figure 1I). These data collectively demonstrated that ZmSKI3 is required for maize kernel development.

ZmSKI2 works with ZmSKI3 to regulate kernel development and plant growth in maize

Since ZmSKI3 is a putative subunit of maize SKI complex, we explored whether the other subunits of SKI complex, ZmSKI2 and ZmSKI8, could interact with ZmSKI3. By protein sequence alignment, we obtained the putative ZmSKI2 (encoded by Zm00001d015314) and ZmSKI8 (encoded by Zm00001d048691) proteins (Figure S1C, D). By yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) assay, we found that ZmSKI2 could interact with ZmSKI3 (Figure 2A). However, due to the self‐activation effect of ZmSKI8, we could not verify the interaction between ZmSKI3 and ZmSKI8 (Figure S4). Further, a strong interaction signal between truncated ZmSKI3 (72‐465) containing three TPR domains and the full‐length ZmSKI2 was detected in luciferase complementation assay (LCA), further confirming the interaction between the two subunits (Figure 2B, C).

Since ZmSKI2 and ZmSKI3 interact to form the SKI complex, we wondered whether ZmSKI2 is also required for kernel development. We therefore obtained two EMS mutants, Zmski2‐1 and Zmski2‐2, each harboring a premature stop codon in the coding region of ZmSKI2 (Figure 3A). As expected, small kernels were observed on all the ears of the Zmski2‐1/+ and Zmski2‐2/+ heterozygous mutant plants (Figure 3B). Subsequent allelic test of Zmski2‐1/+ and Zmski2‐2/+ further confirmed the essential role of ZmSKI2 in kernel development (Figure 3B). Similar to the Zmski3 mutants, the kernel length, width, thickness, and 100‐kernel weight were all significantly reduced and obvious developmental defect was observed in the longitudinal section of Zmski2 mutants (Figures 3C–F, S5A–D).

Defense response is activated in the Zmski3‐1 mutant

Since Bsr‐k1, the close homolog of ZmSKI3, confers disease resistance in rice, and severe dwarfism is often caused by constitutive activation of defense response in autoimmune mutants (Zhou et al., 2018b; Freh et al., 2022), we wondered if the severe growth defect of Zmski3 mutant is associated with enhanced immunity. We first checked the expression levels of several defense‐related marker genes. Our reverse transcription – quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR) assay indicated that the expressions of ZmPR1, ZmPR5, ZmAOS, and ZmAOC were all up‐regulated in Zmski3‐1 mutant compared with that of WT (Figure 4C). The accumulation of defense hormones including jasmonic acid (JA) and salicylic acid (SA) and their precursor or derivate 4‐(3‐oxo‐2‐(pent‐2‐enyl) cyclopentyl) butanoic acid (OPC‐4) and SA β‐glucoside (SAG), were also significantly increased (Figure 4D). In particular, the content of SAG was increased by 21.9‐fold in the mutant compared to the WT (Figure 4D). Taken together, these results indicate that defense response is activated in Zmski3‐1 mutant.

The Zmski3‐1 mutant displays enhanced disease resistance to C. lunata

Based on the above results, we sought to investigate the disease resistance phenotype of the Zmski3‐1 mutant. Due to the severe growth defect of the Zmski3‐1 mutant plants in B73 background, it was hard to perform pathogen inoculation and resistance evaluation. According to a previous study, the growth vigor of autoimmune plants may be partially recovered when the mutation was introgressed into different genetic backgrounds (Olukolu et al., 2014; Wu et al., 2020). Indeed, when the Zmski3‐1 mutation was introgressed into Chang7‐2 and Huangzao4, the Zmski3‐1 mutants in new backgrounds displayed better growth than the mutant in B73 background (Figure S6A–C). Since the Curvularia lunata (Wakker) Boed strain CX‐3 is a pathogen strain convenient for inoculation and phenotypic observation in maize (Mu et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022a, 2024), we used it to inoculate and evaluate the resistance levels of WT and Zmski3‐1 mutants. Obviously, a significant reduction of fungal colonies was observed on the Zmski3‐1 mutant leaves compared to that on WT leaves at 8 d after inoculation (Figure 5A). The enhanced resistance of Zmski3‐1 mutants was further verified since less amount of C. lunata DNA was detected in the inoculated leaves by RT‐qPCR (Figure 5B). Meanwhile, the expressions of defense marker genes were also found significantly up‐regulated in the Zmski3‐1 mutant plants in Chang7‐2 and Huangzao4 backgrounds (Figure 5C).

We then generated the overexpression (OE) transgenic lines of the ZmSKI3 (ZmSKI3‐OE) in B104 background. Three independent transgenic lines ZmSKI3‐OE #1, ZmSKI3‐OE #2, and ZmSKI3‐OE #3 with significantly up‐regulated expression of ZmSKI3 were obtained (Figure 6A, B). Interestingly, significantly down‐regulated expressions of the defense marker genes ZmPR1 and ZmPR5 were detected in the ZmSKI3‐OE #1 plants compared to the nontransgenic B104 control (Figure 6C), implying compromised disease resistance in these transgenic lines. At 8 d after inoculation of CX‐3, more fungal colonies were observed in leaves of all overexpression lines compared with the nontransgenic B104 control (Figure 6D, E). The enhanced susceptibility of ZmSKI3‐OE lines was further confirmed by significantly higher amounts of C. lunata DNA detected by RT‐qPCR (Figure 6F). When naturally infected, the ZmSKI3‐OE plants were also more susceptible to Bipolaris maydis, which causes southern leaf blight, than the B104 nontransgenic plants (Figure 6G, H), which was also confirmed by significantly higher amounts of B. maydis DNA detected by RT‐qPCR (Figure 6I). In sum, these results confirmed the negative role of ZmSKI3 in maize disease resistance.

Differentially expressed genes associated with plant immunity and growth were identified in Zmski3‐1 mutant and ZmSKI3‐OE plants

To investigate the underlying mechanism of ZmSKI3‐regulated disease resistance, we performed transcriptomics analysis of the Zmski3‐1 mutant and ZmSKI3‐OE #1 plants using their B73 WT and B104 nontransgenic plants as controls respectively. A total of 265,796,890 clean reads were obtained from the resulting 12 RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) libraries (Dataset S2). Pearson correlation coefficients varied from 0.981 to 0.991 among Zmski3‐1 mutant samples, and 0.982 to 0.996 among ZmSKI3‐OE #1 samples, indicating high consistency of biological replicates (Figure S7A, B). Compared with B73 WT, a total of 1,459 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 1,163 up‐regulated and 296 down‐regulated genes, were identified in the Zmski3‐1 mutant (Figure 7A; Dataset S3). Compared with the nontransgenic B104 control, a total of 705 DEGs, including 202 up‐regulated and 503 down‐regulated genes, were identified in the ZmSKI3‐OE #1 line (Figure 7A; Dataset S4). RT‐qPCR data of randomly selected genes matched well with the RNA‐seq data, indicating the reliability of the RNA‐seq data (Figure S8A–D).

We then performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis to dissect the activated or suppressed pathways in Zmski3‐1 mutant. Consistent with the significantly elevated disease resistance, many defense‐related GO terms including “defense response to other organism,” “defense response to oomycetes,” and “defense response to bacterium” and so forth, were significantly enriched in the up‐regulated DEGs (Figure 7B; Dataset S5). In contrast, the vast majority of enriched GO terms of the down‐regulated DEGs were related to plant growth, such as “photosynthesis,” “response to light stimulus,” and “photosynthetic electron transport chain” (Figure 7B; Dataset S5). These data imply that Zmski3‐1‐conferred disease resistance and growth defects are due to activated expression of defense‐related genes and repressed expression of growth‐related genes.

Since SKI3 is a key subunit of the cytoplasmic cofactor SKI complex that mediates the 3′–5′ degradation of RNA by the RNA exosome complex, we were particularly curious about the degradation targets of ZmSKI3. We assume that the degradation targets of ZmSKI3 shall be up‐regulated in Zmski3 mutant but down‐regulated in ZmSKI3‐OE lines. Indeed, a total of 223 such common differentially expressed genes (CDGs) were identified and considered as the potential targets of ZmSKI3 (Figure 7A, C; Dataset S6). Further GO analysis of these genes indicated they were mainly significantly enriched in defense‐related terms (Figure 7D; Dataset S7). These defense‐related genes include SA‐related genes (CBP60g and SARD1), nucleotide‐binding site – leucine‐rich repeat R genes (RPM1), pathogenesis‐related genes (PR1, PR3, PR9, PR10 and PR11), reactive oxygen species production‐related genes (POD23, POD47), terpenoid biosynthetic genes (ZX5, ZX6, ZX8, KO2 and KS4), phenylpropanoid and lignin biosynthetic genes (HCT, 4CL and LACs) and other defense‐related genes (Table 1). In addition, we compared these genes to previously published transcriptomics data using the Plant Regulomics interface (Ran et al., 2020) and found ~81% of CDGs (181/223) were shared with genes previously reported to be responsive to pathogen infection (Dataset S8). These data collectively indicate that ZmSKI3 likely regulates plant defense by targeting the defense‐related gene transcripts for RNA exosome‐mediated degradation.

Defense‐ and growth‐related gene transcripts are regulated by ZmSKI3‐mediated RNA decay

To verify the above hypothesis, we conducted the RNA decay assay to investigate the ZmSKI3‐mediated messenger RNA (mRNA) decay in Zmski3‐1 mutant and WT (Figures 7E, F, S9A–C). To eliminate the effects of transcription on mRNA accumulation, cordycepin, a compound that strongly blocked the process of transcription, was employed (Zhang et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2022), and its effect was confirmed by the analysis of ZmHSPRO2 mRNA degradation as a positive control as previously described (Perea‐Resa et al., 2012; Figure S9A). Significantly decreased mRNA decay of defense‐related genes, such as PR3, CBP60g, KS4 and LRR‐RLK, in the Zmski3‐1 mutant were observed (Figures 7E, S9B), supporting our hypothesis that ZmSKI3 mediates the degradation of defense‐related gene transcripts in maize.

According to a previous study, SKI complex also targeted growth‐related gene transcripts, such as those related to Calvin cycle of photosynthesis (Wang et al., 2022). Considering that the accumulation of photosynthesis‐related gene transcripts was reduced in Zmski3‐1 mutant (Figure 7B), we were curious about its relevance with ZmSKI3‐mediated RNA decay. We therefore examined a few of those genes, including FBP2, LHCA2, LHCB1, and GPA1, in the RNA decay assay. Our data indicate that the RNA decay of those genes was also repressed in the Zmski3‐1 mutant (Figures 7F, S9C). Based on the data, there is an obvious contradiction between the disabled RNA decay and the reduced growth‐related mRNA accumulation in Zmski3‐1 mutant, which implies the existence of additional regulatory layer responsible for the fine control of mRNA level.

Small interfering RNA‐mediated regulation of immunity and plant growth in Zmski3‐1 mutant

According to previous studies, mutation of SKI2 in Arabidopsis leads to siRNA‐induced PTGS (Zhang et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2022), which is one additional layer of mRNA regulation other than the SKI‐mediated RNA decay. To check if PTGS is indeed initiated in Zmski3 mutant, we performed small RNA sequencing to detect siRNA levels in the Zmski3‐1 mutant and B73 WT. A total of 212,427,952 clean reads were obtained from the six small RNA‐seq libraries (Dataset S9). Pearson correlation coefficients and overall comparable length distributions of total small RNAs among these samples indicated high consistency and quality of the small RNA‐seq data (Figure S10A, B). We then filtered the 21–22 nt siRNAs from the sequencing data and mapped them to their corresponding transcripts of coding genes. Finally, 190 genes targeted by significantly increased siRNAs and 102 genes targeted by significantly decreased siRNAs were detected in the Zmski3‐1 mutant compared with the WT (Figures 8A, S10C; Dataset S10). Intriguingly, defense‐related GO terms were significantly enriched in the genes targeted by significantly increased siRNAs, while photosynthesis‐ and energy‐related GO terms were specifically enriched in the genes targeted by significantly decreased siRNAs (Figure 8B). For instance, siRNAs targeting the defense‐related genes including RPP13‐like 1, KS4, and PR3 were dramatically increased in Zmski3‐1 mutant compared to that of WT. In contrast, siRNAs targeting the growth‐related genes, including the LHCB1, GPA1, LHCB10, FBP2, LHCA2 and so forth, were decreased in Zmski3‐1 mutant compared to that of WT (Figure 8C; Dataset S10–S12).

We then looked at the expression levels of these 190 and 102 siRNAs target genes to dissect the correlation between mRNA levels of target genes and siRNA levels (Figure S11A, B). For the 190 genes targeted by significantly increased siRNAs, mRNA levels of 106 genes were up‐regulated and the others were not changed in Zmski3‐1 (Figure S11A). For the 102 genes targeted by significantly decreased siRNAs, most mRNAs were not changed, with only one up‐regulated and 26 down‐regulated in Zmski3‐1 (Figure S11B). Therefore, there is no apparently negative correlation between mRNA level and siRNA levels. These data suggest that transcripts of different genes are differentially regulated by siRNAs to combat the increase of transcripts of defense‐related genes and decrease of transcripts of growth‐related genes in Zmski3‐1 mutant, thereby maintaining the balance between plant defense and growth.

DISCUSSION

SKI complex is a central cofactor of the cytoplasmic RNA exosome that catalyzes the 3′–5′ exoribonucleolytic degradation of RNA. In previous studies, SKI complex has been revealed to play roles associated with disease and plant development respectively. However, it was not known if both plant growth and defense could be regulated simultaneously by SKI complex and associated processes. In this study, we provide data supporting that plant growth and immunity could be regulated by ZmSKI3‐mediated RNA decay and PTGS in maize.

ZmSKI3 is a key player involved in plant growth and defense in maize

Previous studies indicate that dysfunction of rice and cotton Bsr‐k1 gene results in broad‐spectrum disease resistance without growth penalty (Zhou et al., 2018b; Li et al., 2021). Based on genetic data from the Zmski3 mutants and ZmSKI3‐OE lines in this study, ZmSKI3 was found to be a negative regulator of disease resistance in maize (Figures 4C, D, 5 and 6). However, the loss of function of ZmSKI3 or its interacting protein ZmSKI2 led to severe defects of kernel development and plant growth, indicating their positive roles in plant growth regulation (Figures 1, 3, 4A, B, S2, S3, S5, and S6). Consistently, expressions of defense‐related genes were up‐regulated and those of growth‐related genes were down‐regulated in Zmski3‐1 mutant (Figure 7B). Interestingly, although all homozygous (Zmski3/Zmski3) plants show dwarf phenotype, a few homozygous (Zmski3/Zmski3) kernels are morphologically normal (Figure S2A, B). The relatively low occurrence of small kernels than the expected ratio (1/4) was previously reported in the study of the cofactors RST1 and RIPR which connect the SKI complex and the cytosolic RNA exosome in Arabidopsis (Lange et al., 2019). Collectively, these data imply a conserved mechanism regulating the variable kernel development phenotype when RNA decay is interrupted, and it will be an interesting point to address for further study.

Defense‐ and growth‐related gene transcripts are regulated by ZmSKI3‐mediated RNA decay and PTGS machinery

Gene expression could be regulated at transcriptional and post‐transcriptional levels. Based on our transcriptomic data and small RNA sequencing data, we find that other than the transcriptional regulation, defense‐ and growth‐related gene transcripts could be regulated at two post‐transcriptional levels.

First, both defense‐ and growth‐related gene transcripts could be regulated by ZmSKI3‐mediated RNA decay. Our transcriptomics data revealed that expressions of defense‐related gene transcripts were dramatically up‐regulated in the Zmski3‐1 mutant but down‐regulated in the ZmSKI3‐OE line (Figure 7C, D). Since SKI3 is a critical subunit of SKI complex associated with cytoplasmic RNA exosome‐mediated mRNA decay, we reasoned that the differentially accumulated defense‐related gene transcripts are potential targets of ZmSKI3. Based on our RNA decay assay, the decay of defense‐related mRNAs was indeed repressed in the Zmski3‐1 mutant compared with that of the WT (Figures 7E, S9B). Further, the decay of growth‐related mRNAs was also repressed in the Zmski3‐1 mutant (Figures 7F, S9C). We could not exclude other gene transcripts that are also targeted by the ZmSKI complex. It also remains to be clarified whether some gene transcripts are selectively targeted and if the extents of mRNA decay vary for different targets.

Second, defense‐ and growth‐related gene transcripts could be regulated by siRNA‐mediated PTGS. Previous studies indicated that mutations in genes encoding SKI subunits and exosome subunits could result in the accumulation of siRNAs (Zhang et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2022). Based on the small RNA sequencing data of this study, 190 genes targeted by significantly increased siRNAs and 102 genes targeted by significantly decreased siRNAs were identified in Zmski3‐1 mutant compared to that of WT (Figures 8, S10C). Intriguingly, the 190 genes are enriched in defense‐related pathways, while the 102 genes are enriched in growth‐related pathways. Further, our data showed there is no apparently negative correlation between mRNA level and siRNA level (Figure S11), which could be due to two reasons. On one hand, when the defense‐related gene transcripts are over‐accumulated due to the compromised RNA decay, PTGS is triggered to reduce these transcripts in the Zmski3‐1 mutant. On the other hand, when the transcriptions of growth‐related genes are repressed likely due to over‐activation of defense response, PTGS is repressed to increase the growth‐related transcripts in Zmski3‐1 mutant. Collectively, these data imply that defense‐ and growth‐related gene transcripts could be differentially regulated by PTGS machinery to fine tune the balance between plant defense and growth.

The repression of growth‐related gene expression by activated defense response has also been previously reported. For instance, photosynthesis‐ and carbohydrate‐related genes were enriched in the down‐regulated genes in the maize mutant Les8 that showed multiple disease resistance (Li et al., 2024). Similarly, growth‐related genes were generally repressed in three other lesion mimic mutants that showed constitutively activated defense response (Mu et al., 2021). In addition, OE of the transcription factor gene IPA1 in rice resulted in increased disease resistance but attenuated plant growth (Liu et al., 2019), which also suggests suppression of plant growth‐related gene expression upon immunity activation.

Based on the present and previous studies, we propose a working model on the regulation of plant growth and defense in maize (Figure S12). In the WT, the transcripts of both growth‐ and defense‐related genes could be regulated at the transcriptional level and post‐transcriptional level by the SKI‐complex‐mediated RNA decay and PTGS machinery to maintain their appropriate level. In the Zmski3 mutant, the SKI complex‐mediated decay of the defense‐related transcripts is repressed, leading to overaccumulation of defense‐related gene transcripts and consequently activated defense response. The activated defense response in turn causes the feed‐back repression of growth‐related gene transcription in a yet unknown mechanism, resulting in decreased accumulation of growth‐related gene transcripts. After sensing the overaccumulation of defense‐related gene transcripts, the corresponding siRNAs are over‐produced to trigger PTGS that partially repress the overaccumulation of defense‐related gene transcripts, while fewer growth gene‐associated siRNAs are produced to avoid over‐repression of growth‐related gene expression. Taken together, the transcripts of defense‐related and growth‐related genes are under strict control by SKI complex‐mediated RNA decay and PTGS machinery to maintain the defense‐growth tradeoffs.

Since dysfunction of Bsr‐k1 gene in rice and cotton results in broad‐spectrum disease resistance without growth penalty, it is possible that SKI3 has evolved to be functionally specified in different species or different accessions of the same species. Therefore, it will be promising to screen different haplotypes of ZmSKI3 in natural populations to obtain elite maize germplasms with enhanced disease resistance but no growth penalty. Further, considering the success of obtaining disease‐resistant and high‐yield rice and wheat by gene editing of RBL and MLO genes, it is worth carrying out saturated gene editing of ZmSKI3 to look for its favorable variants in the future.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials and growth conditions

The maize (Zea Mays) Zmski3 and Zmski2 mutants were obtained from the Maize EMS‐induced Mutant Database (MEMD) (https://elabcaas.cn/memd/). All seedlings used for imaging, phytohormone quantification and RNA‐seq were planted in the greenhouse (28°C, 16 h/8 h photoperiod). All other plants were grown in the fields at the experimental station of Henan Agricultural University in Zhengzhou, Henan Province, China, in summer and the experimental station of the Henan Agricultural University in Ledong, Hainan Province, China, in winter.

Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis

All protein sequences of SKI3 homologs were acquired from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) by Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST). The conserved domains of ZmSKI3 were predicted using the Conserved Domain Database website of NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd). Multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis of proteins were performed using BioXM 2.6 and MEGA‐X, respectively.

Light microscopy of cytological sections

Wild type and Zmski3‐1 mutant kernels were acquired from the same heterozygous ear at 18 d after pollination (DAP) and cut along the longitudinal axis into three parts. The central part, containing the embryo and endosperm, was fixed for 1 d at room temperature in FAA solution (5% formalin, 5% glacial acetic acid, and 45% ethanol). The fixed material was then dehydrated in an ethanol gradient series (70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100% ethanol), embedded in paraffin, and subsequently cut into 6‐ to 10‐μm sections. The sections were stained with safranin O and fast green and observed using a Nikon Ti Microscope.

RNA extraction and RT‐qPCR

Total RNA used in this study was extracted using the RNA extraction kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was generated using the NovoScript®Plus All‐in‐one First Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Novoprotein, Suzhou, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RT‐qPCR was performed using NovoStart® SYBR qPCR SuperMix (Novoprotein) on the Roche real‐time PCR platform (LightCycler®480) using the ZmGAPC1 gene as the internal control for normalization of gene expression. Three biological replicates were used, and the relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The primers used in RT‐qPCR are listed in Dataset S13.

Generation of constructs for Y2H assay, LCA and gene overexpression

For the Y2H assay, the full‐length coding sequence of ZmSKI3 was cloned into the pGBKT7 vector to generate the bait construct BD‐ZmSKI3, and the full‐length coding sequences of ZmSKI2 and ZmSKI8 were cloned into the pGADT7 vector to generate prey constructs AD‐ZmSKI2 and AD‐ZmSKI8. For LCA, the full‐length coding sequence of ZmSKI2 was cloned into the pCAMBIA1300‐35S‐cLUC vector to obtain the construct cLUC‐ZmSKI2, the truncated coding sequence of ZmSKI3 containing the TPR domains was constructed into the pCAMBIA1300‐35S‐nLUC vector to obtain the construct ZmSKI3 (72‐465)‐nLUC. For gene OE, the full‐length coding sequence of ZmSKI3 was amplified from the cDNAs of B73 WT and constructed into the pCAMBIA3301 vector driven by Ubiquitin promoter.

Yeast two‐hybrid assay

The bait and prey constructs were transformed into Y2H Gold yeast strain and cultured on synthetic defined (SD)/‐Leu/‐Trp (SD/‐LT) and SD/‐Leu/‐Trp/‐His/‐Ade (SD/‐LTHA) plates for detection of self‐activation. After that, co‐transformed colonies were selected on SD/‐LT plates. Positive cells were serially diluted and plated on the selective medium SD/‐LT and SD/‐LTHA. Images were taken 4 d after culture in the growth chamber at 28°C in dark.

Luciferase complementation assay

The LCAs were performed as previously described (Zhou et al., 2023). Briefly, Agrobacterium solutions, harboring different constructs, were injected into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. After growth for 48 h, the leaves were spread with D‐Luciferin potassium salt (150 μg/mL, Beyotime, No. ST198), and kept in darkness for 1 min. The resulting luciferase signals were captured using the NightShade LB 985 In Vivo Plant Imaging System. The interaction among proteins was detected at least three times using independent leaves.

Quantification of JA, SA, and their precursors and derivatives

Samples for qualification were taken and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen from plants grown for 35 d in the field. Quantifications of JA, SA, and their precursors and derivatives in Zmski3‐1 mutant and WT were performed by Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd at Wuhan, China, using the AB Sciex 20QTRAP 6500 liquid chromatography – tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS) platform as previously described (Guo et al., 2021).

Assessment of pathogenicity of Curvularia lunata and Bipolaris maydis

Curvularia lunata (Wakker) Boed strain CX‐3 was cultivated as previously described (Mu et al., 2021). The CX‐3 spores were suspended at the concentration of 1 × 106 mL with distilled water containing 0.02% Tween 20. At least 10 first above‐ear leaves from independent plants of each genotype were sprayed at silking stage, and images were taken at 8 d after spraying. For quantification of DNAs of C. lunata and B. maydis, the leaves from three plants at 8 d after inoculation for CX‐3 and natural inoculation for B. maydis were pooled as one sample, and three biological replicates were used for DNA extraction using the hexadecyltrimethyl‐ammonium bromide (CTAB) method. The RT‐qPCR for quantification of CX‐3 and B. maydis were performed using CX‐3‐specific primers of the ClgD gene (CX‐3‐ClgD‐F, CX‐3‐ClgD‐R) and B. maydis specific primers of the ITS gene (BMF‐3, BMR‐3), respectively. Quantification of plant DNA used the primers specific for ZmGAPC1 (ZmGAPC1‐DNA‐F1, ZmGAPC1‐DNA‐R1).

RNA decay assay

RNA decay assay was performed according to previous studies (Zhang et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2022). Briefly, the 20 d‐old first fully unfolded leaves of WT and Zmski3‐1 mutant seedlings were quickly cut into 3–5 mm strips and placed in a pretreatment solution (1 mmol/L ethanesulfonic acid, 1 mmol/L sodium citrate, 1 mmol/L potassium chloride, 15 mmol/L sucrose, pH = 6.5) for oscillatory culture (22°C, 160 rpm, 30 min). Then cordycepin was added with a final concentration of 1 mmol/L, and a brief (30 s) vacuum was applied to each sample. Samples were collected at 0, 15, 30 and 45 min after vacuum release, frozen by liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C for RNA extraction. Total RNA was then extracted by Trizol method. RT‐qPCR was performed using NovoStart® SYBR qPCR SuperMix (Novoprotein) on the Roche real‐time PCR platform (LightCycler®480) using the ZmTIP41 gene as the internal control for normalization of gene expression. The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The primers used are listed in Dataset S13.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

For RNA‐seq, total RNAs of the Zmski3‐1 mutant and WT were extracted from 35‐d‐old plants grown in the field, and total RNAs of the ZmSKI3‐OE #1 and the nontransgenic B104 WT were extracted from 15‐d‐old seedlings grown in the greenhouse using the method described above. Three biological replicates with each replicate consisting of leaves from three independent plants were used. RNA sequencing was performed using Illumina HiSeq platform (Illumina HiSeq X‐ten) and pair‐end reads of 150 bp were generated. After removal of low‐quality regions and adapter sequences from the raw reads, clean reads were then aligned to the maize B73 RefGen_v4 reference genome by HISAT2. Expression value is quantified as fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads (FPKM) and DEGs were identified with edgeR with a filter threshold of false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2Fold change| > 1. The GO term enrichment analyses were performed using agriGO v2.0 (http://systemsbiology.cau.edu.cn/agriGOv2/index.php).

Small RNA sequencing and data analysis

The Zmski3‐1 mutant and WT samples used for small RNA‐seq were the same as that used for RNA‐seq described above. Total RNAs used for small RNA‐seq were extracted with Trizol reagent (Takara, Dalian, China). The small RNA‐seq libraries were constructed with VAHTS Small RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina V2 (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq platform (Illumina NovaSeq. 6000). The 21–22 nt clean reads after adaptor‐removal and filtering were mapped to the maize B73 RefGen_v4 reference genome using the Bowtie program. Featurecount (v1.6.0) was used for transcript abundance estimation and normalization of expression values as RPKM. Significantly differentially mapped gene transcripts by 2,122 nt siRNAs were identified with a filter threshold of |log2Fold change| > 1, P < 0.005, FDR < 0.1. The Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) genome browser was used to visualize the detailed changes of 21–22 nt siRNAs at protein‐coding DNA loci in Zmski3‐1 mutant and WT.

Statistical analysis

Significant differences between means were determined using Student's t‐test. One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) procedure implemented in IBM SPSS Statistics 26 were used for comparison analysis between multiple groups of data and the comparison difference was tested by significant difference at P < 0.05.
