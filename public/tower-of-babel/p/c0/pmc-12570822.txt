A severe and chronic socio-economic crisis, how much can Lebanese workers take?
Authors: Sabine Saade, Annick Parent-Lamarche, Laetitia Feghali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople in Lebanon have been facing what has been dubbed one of the most severe socio-economic crisis in its history. Over the past few years, suicide rates have spiked, fueled by deteriorating working and living conditions. Research conducted on the mental health of populations residing in low-income countries is meager. Amongst these countries, very little research has examined the repercussions of a severe socio-economic crisis on Lebanese people's mental health.MethodsThis study is based on a community sample of 220 workers residing in Lebanon. The workers were between 18 and 64 years of age and were either English/and or Arabic literate. In terms of their profession, participants worked in various professions and work sectors. For the purpose of this study, we conducted a multiple regression analysis. We used STATA 16 to run these analyses. The multiple regression analysis allowed us to verify the association between several independent variables (work-related, reduced access to electricity, the internet and gas due to the socio-economic crisis, life stressors and family income) and our dependent variables (stress and psychological distress). We also ran mediation analysis to examine the indirect role our variables could play on psychological distress via their effect on stress. We used MPlus Version 8 software to run mediation analysis.ResultsThrough a community sample of workers, lack of access to electricity due to the crisis was found to be associated with stress. Similarly, stress related to the crisis, deteriorating working conditions, job insecurity, lack of internet access, and other life stressors were also found to be associated with a higher level of psychological distress. Higher family income and recognition at work seemed to be associated with a lower level of psychological distress. Lastly, lack of access to electricity seemed to be positively associated with psychological distress.ConclusionThe repercussions of the current situation could have broad implications for a large number of low-income countries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03471-9.Keywords: Psychological distress, Stress, Lebanon, Mental health, Low-income country, Crisis

Introduction

On March 11, 2020, the Coronavirus disease (COVID-19) was declared a pandemic by the World Health Organization [1]. Nations worldwide responded by adopting various lockdown measures. A number of studies reported on the adverse physical and mental health effects of the pandemic [2–4]. In their recent systematic review, Xiong et al. [4] reported on mental health problems associated with the pandemic. Amongst these, are relatively high rates of anxiety (ranging between 6.33% and 50.9%), depression (14.6% to 48.3%), Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD; 7% to 53.8%), psychological distress (34.43% to 38%) and stress (8.1% to 81.9%) [4]. Those mental health problems may be attributed to a constellation of variables, such as the unpredictability of disasters. Such is the case for the COVID-19 pandemic [5], the fear of infection or mortality, ruminations about the pandemic [6], social isolation [7] and the ensuing job insecurity.

While the pandemic has received quite a bit of attention in high-income countries, not much is known about its repercussions in low-income countries. Examining the repercussions of the pandemic in low-income countries is important, given that this accounts for 83% of the global population [8]. Additionally, most low-income countries were already struggling economically before the pandemic. The arrival of the pandemic sent shock waves through global economies [9]. The COVID-19 crisis only exacerbated inequalities within and across countries [9]. Similarly, to other countries, the emerging literature in Lebanon has highlighted the association between the COVID-19 pandemic and stress-related disorders [10] and other negative psychological effects [11]. Unlike other countries, over the past few years, Lebanon has been facing a unique set of circumstances that have been very challenging for the Lebanese people.

In addition to the COVID-19 pandemic, on August 4th 2020, a massive explosion at the Port of Beirut killed at least 200 people, injured thousands, and left over 300 000 people homeless [12]. According to Al Hariri et al. [13], this blast’s magnitude is considered the third most severe after Hiroshima and Nagasaki’s atomic bombings. In addition to the physical repercussions of the Beirut blast, studies have reported on its mental health effects such as severe psychological distress [2, 13]. Due to the compounded crises Lebanon has been facing over the past few years, people have been struggling with a variety of mental health issues [14].

Besides the COVID-19 pandemic and the Beirut Port explosion, over the past few years, Lebanon has been experiencing economic and political turmoil [15, 16]. This turmoil has been coupled with job insecurities, an alarming growth in debt, and an influx of about 1.5 million Syrian refugees [17] all resulting in the collapse of the banking system. Banks in Lebanon had to freeze bank accounts, resulting in depositors being unable to access their funds. Due to their inability to access their funds and a deteriorating economy, a large number of Lebanese people had to return to work after retirement or take on additional jobs [18]. According to Human Rights Watch [19], over 220,000 people lost their jobs and others fell victim to pay cuts and a declining Lebanese pound value. Unemployment rates in Lebanon seem to range between 25 and 40% [20]. According to the UN, Lebanon is in the midst of a devastating economic crisis, plunging more than 80% of its population into poverty [19]. A staggering 82% of the Lebanese people are believed to be reeling under “multidimensional poverty” encompassing access to education, healthcare, public utilities and money-related problems [21].

Amid the ongoing socio-economic crisis, the electricity sector collapsed in 2021, leaving Lebanese people without power through most of the day. The severe power cuts, lack of funds and scarcity of foreign currency have significantly affected the country's internet service and gas availability [19, 22]. According to the UN, reliable gas and electricity supplies are urgently needed in Lebanon to avert a potential “humanitarian catastrophe” [19]. The economic crisis, coupled with the COVID-19 pandemic and the Beirut Port explosion, placed additional strains upon an already strained population [23]. The compounding of crises against a backdrop of political and social turmoil only seemed to fragilize a population still struggling with Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) symptoms due to a history of war. While most countries go through challenging times at some point in their history, Lebanon does not have sufficient social and health infrastructure, adequate public and mental health services, or the financial resources to help support its people [24].

Faced with these glaring inequalities, paying attention to people’s mental health is especially important to avoid having it become tomorrow’s silent epidemic. Mental health, though often neglected, is an essential component of good health [25].

The emerging literature on workers in Lebanon highlighted an increased workload [26], stress [18], fatigue [27], job insecurity and salary deductions [26]. For instance, most employees working in banks in Lebanon over the past few years have been suffering from a number of work-related stressors that negatively affected their mental health [18]. The stress and pressures faced at work have been accentuated during the economic crisis coupled with very limited resources. Similar findings were reported with regard to healthcare workers [26]. Information International, a Beirut-based research consultancy firm, has recently published a study showcasing a significant uptick in registered suicides [28]. These alarming suicide rates are believed to have been exacerbated by deterious economic and living conditions.

While the emerging literature on the mental health of Lebanese people has mainly focused on healthcare workers [26, 27] and bank employees [18], less is known about different workers from various sectors and industries. Most previous studies relied on convenience sampling [29], and favored non-empirical designs (e.g., [30]). To the best of our knowledge, no study to date has evaluated the mental health repercussions of the socio-economic crisis on a community sample of Lebanese workers. Furthermore, none have attempted to evaluate the associations between specific work-related variables, reduced access to electricity, the internet and gas on stress and psychological distress.

Psychological distress is often used in epidemiological studies as one of the key indicators for measuring mental health as well as being used as an outcome measure in clinical trials and intervention studies. Mirowsky and Ross [31] define it as a state of emotional suffering accompanied by symptoms of depression (e.g., hopelessness, loss of interest, low mood) and anxiety (feeling tense; [31]). In the general population, between 5 and 27% of the population is believed to suffer from psychological distress [32]. As for workers, those facing stressful work conditions are believed to be at high risk for experiencing psychological distress [32]. The tenants of the stress-distress model advance that psychological distress usually results from a stressful event [33]. This stressful event could harm an individual's physical or mental health, their ability to cope with this stressor, and the resulting emotional turmoil [34, 35]. Psychological distress is generally the first sign of a more serious mental health problem and could be considered a marker for psychiatric disorders (e.g., major depression and generalized anxiety [36, 37]). In addition to mental health problems, psychological distress could be associated with high blood pressure, alcohol, drug abuse, cardiovascular disease, and suicide [32]. It can also result in employment issues such as poor work performance [33] and increased absenteeism [38]. In the context of a global and national financial crisis, paying close attention to employees’ mental health is all the more important. From an ethical point of view, workers' mental health should not have to suffer for the sake of an organization's performance [39]. From an organizational and an individual level, monitoring mental health with the help of early intervention is a critical element of its protection.

To help address the gaps identified in the scientific literature, the current study aims to examine the association between the the socio-economic crisis and Lebanese workers’ mental health. We expect this crisis to be associated with stress and psychological distress. In addition, we were interested in identifying the specific work organization conditions and broader crisis-related variables (reduced access to gas, electricity and the internet due to the crisis), life stressors, and family income that might negatively affect workers' mental health. Increased understanding of how a severe socio-economic situation could alter workers' mental health and deciphering its effect on distinct variables could help us better address and prevent these challenges in the future. The current Lebanese situation offers a unique learning opportunity as the socio-economic crisis is considered one of the worst in its history. The results obtained from this study could be generalizable to other low-income countries who might be grappling with socio-economic problems. As unfortunate as the situation is, it provides a unique learning opportunity. More specifically, it will help us understand the repercussions of a severe crisis on a population's mental health and formulate prevention strategies.

Theoretical background

This study is based on the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory [40], a well-established framework for understanding how individuals respond to stress and maintain psychological well-being in the face of adversity. According to this theory, individuals strive to obtain, retain, and protect valued resources. When these resources are threatened or depleted, psychological strain often ensues. People in Lebanon have been facing repeated and chronic resource losses over the span of several years. These resources cut across several domains: financial (currency devaluation, loss of savings in banks, salary devaluation), infrastructural (electricity, fuel and gaz shortages), and psychological (chronic uncertainty, job insecurity, traumatic symptoms due to the Beirut port explosion and years of armed conflicts) to name a few. Each loss can pull an individual into a cycle of cumulative stress and resource depletion. Faced with these losses, people usually turn to their government or look for social safety programs to help mitigate the loss spiral. In Lebanon, however, massive brain drain, and institutional corruption have dampened recovery efforts, leaving individuals largely reliant on personal or family-based ressources such as family income. This loss spiral not only increases vulnerability to stress but also makes it harder to come out unscathed. In the context of Lebanon, we hypothesized that some stressors could drain an individual’s resources. These stressors refer to: Stress related to the socio-economic crisis, income loss due to the socio-economic crisis, work organization conditions worsening due to the socio-economic crisis, workload increase due to the socio-economic crisis, job insecurity due to the socio-economic crisis, electrical power access affected due to the socio-economic crisis, internet access affected due to the socio-economic crisis and gas access affected due to the socio-economic crisis and life stressors. Faced with these stressors, we were interested to see whether certain resources (family income and job recognition) could support an individual’s ability to adapt and maintain well-being under heightened stress. According to the COR theory, resources serve as protective factors against psychological strain. Conversely, when resources are limited or depleted, individuals become increasingly susceptible to external stressors, potentially entering a “loss spiral” [40]. In this loss spiral, initial resource depletion intensifies distress and further undermines existing resources.

As such, the variables included in our conceptual model are rooted in the Conservation of Resources theory [40]. It is also informed by the Western literature on workers and more nascent literature in Lebanon. These scientific findings point to the role job insecurity, job recognition, work organization conditions, workload, stress, income, life stressors play in terms of stress and psychological distress [18, 41–49]. Although not identical, we also drew on studies examining the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on workers’ mental health [50, 51]. While distinct in nature, both the COVID-19 pandemic and the Lebanese crisis represent periods of crises and societal disruption. These scientific findings were coupled by the first author's experience of living and working in Lebanon. This author felt that adding variables unique to the Lebanese context (electrical power access affected due to the socio-economic crisis, internet access affected due to the socio-economic crisis, gas access affected due to the socio-economic crisis) was crucial for understanding worker's mental health and represents a unique contribution to the literature. Methods

Study design

Before launching the study, IRB approval was obtained from the American University of Beirut (AUB) and Université du Québec à Trois Rivières (UQTR) Institutional Review Boards. This study relied on a community sample of workers in Lebanon whose ages varied between 18 and 64 years old. We adopted this inclusion criterion (limiting participants' age range) to make sure participants had a job at the time of data collection. Participants were recruited online on various social media platforms (e.g., WhatsApp, Facebook) or in person (community sample through a snowball approach). The study flyer was posted on the research team's WhatsApp application, Facebook groups, and LinkedIn profiles, and was also disseminated via email to individuals they knew in the community. These individuals were then asked to further distribute the flyer within their networks. For in-person recruitment, research assistants (RAs) approached individuals randomly in public places (e.g., university campus). They presented the study, obtained consent, and either sent participants a link to complete the questionnaire online or provided them with paper copies to fill out. The RAs then came back a few minutes later to collect the paper questionnaires. We were interested in a community sample of workers in various professions and work sectors who are either English/and or Arabic literate. To increase the reach of our study and to avoid excluding participants who might not have access to electricity and the Internet, participants were given the option of either completing the questionnaires online or on paper. A sample of 220 workers residing in Lebanon originally took part in the study. All questionnaires were translated into Arabic. In terms of the translation process, the translation back-translation method was used [52]. Data collection spanned a period of approximately six months (February to August 2022). Lastly, participants had a chance to win two prizes of 400,000 Lebanese Pounds (LBP) in cash in exchange for participating in our study (lottery system).

Participants

Informed consent was obtained from all subjects. After consent was secured, all participants were asked to complete a number of questionnaires. Participation in this study took approximately 30 min.

On average, participants were 34.04 years old, 57.27% of the sample were women and 50.45% were part of a couple. Our study sample was quite diverse; participants worked in 19 different job sectors and occupying various job positions.

Measures

To measure psychological distress, we relied on the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6) [53]. Participants were asked to think back to the previous month to indicate how often they felt nervous, hopeless, restless or fidgety, so depressed that nothing could cheer them up, that everything was an effort and worthless. A psychological distress total score was derived by summing the results obtained on all items of the scale. Lower scores indicated lower levels of psychological distress while high scores indicated higher scores of psychological distress (α = 0.913).

To capture the effect of the socio-economic crisis adequately and fully, we developed our own questionnaire. Given the uniqueness of the crisis experienced in Lebanon, this was the first time this questionnaire has been used and has therefore not been published in other studies (please see supplementary file). Stress related to the socio-economic crisis was measured using the following question: "How has the socioeconomic crisis affected your overall stress level?" Income loss due to the socio-economic crisis was evaluated based on the following question: "During the socio-economic crisis, did you experience a loss of income?" Work organization conditions worsening due to socio-economic crisis was measured using the following question: "How has the socio/economic crisis affected your work organization conditions?" Workload increase due to socio-economic crisis was measured using the following question: "How has the socio-economic crisis affected your workload?" Job insecurity due to the socio-economic crisis was measured using the following question: "Could you lose your job in the coming months due to the socio-economic crisis?" Access to electrical power due to the socio-economic crisis was measured using the following question: "Has the socio-economic crisis affected your access to electricity?" Access to the internet due to the socio-economic crisis was measured using the following question: "Has the socio/economic crisis affected your access to the internet?" Access to gas affected due to the socio-economic crisis was measured using the following question: "Has the socio/economic crisis affected your access to gas?" Regarding Life stressors, participants were asked to tick the box(es) pertaining to several life events that had happened to them during the previous year (e.g., death of a spouse, detention in jail or another institution, major change in living conditions, etc.) [54]. As for family income, participants were asked to report on their household income before taxes [55]. Job Recognition was evaluated using the following items: "I receive the respect I deserve from my superiors; I receive the respect I deserve from my colleagues; I experience adequate support in difficult situations; I am treated unfairly at work; Considering all my efforts and achievements, I receive the respect and prestige I deserve at work" (α = 0.827) [56]. Lower scores indicated lower levels of job recognition while higher scores indicated higher levels of job recognition.

The model used in this study accounted for variables that have been shown to be associated with the dependent variables. In order to fully grasp the effect of the independent variables on the dependent variables, we controlled for the following variables: age, sex, and marital status. Sex was coded as 1 = Woman and 0 for Male or others. Age was coded in terms of number of years and Marital Status was coded as 1 when the participant reported being part of a couple/married. Being single; separated; divorced; widowed were all coded as 0.

Data analyses

All analyses were conducted using STATA 16 and Mplus 8.

Firstly, we were interested in evaluating the direct association between work organization conditions, variables associated with the socio-economic crisis (e.g., reduced internet, electricity and gas access), life stressors and family income on Lebanese people's stress and psychological distress (direct effect). Secondly, we were interested in examining the indirect role these variables could play on psychological distress via their effect on stress (mediation effect).

We adopted the following guidelines to evaluate the mediation effect psychological distress could play [57, 58]. The first step consisted of evaluating direct associations. To do this, work-related variables related to the socio-economic crisis (income loss, worsening work organization conditions, workload increase, job insecurity, and job recognition), variables associated with the socio-economic crisis (reduced internet, electricity and gas access), life stressors and family income, and control variables were all included in the model. This first step allowed us to evaluate the main effects of the independent variables on the dependent variables (stress as well as psychological distress). More specifically, this first model allowed us to determine which variable was significantly associated with stress and psychological distress. This same model allowed us to determine whether these same independent variables were indirectly associated with psychological distress via their effect on stress.

On average, the results point to a moderate level of psychological distress [59]. The standard deviation indicates that some participants are displaying a worrisome level of psychological distress. 79% of the respondents reported suffering from stress related to the socio-economic crisis, 64% reported a loss of income due to the socio-economic crisis, 60% reported a deterioration of their working conditions, 51% saw an increase in their workload, 81% saw a change in access to electricity, 65% to the internet and 79% to gas.

The correlation matrix depicting the correlations between our variables is displayed in Table 2.

The results obtained indicate that almost all of our predictor variables are correlated with psychological distress. More specifically, stress related to the socio-economic crisis was positively correlated with psychological distress (r = 0.35, p < 0.001). The following variables were also positively correlated with psychological distress: income loss due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.25, p < 0.001), work organization conditions worsening due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.32, p < 0.001), job insecurity (r = 0.29, p < 0.001), access to electrical power affected due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.24, p < 0.001), access to the internet affected due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.34, p < 0.001), access to gas affected due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.24, p < 0.001), and life stressors (r = 0.28, p < 0.001). Inversely, family income (r = −0.36, p < 0.001) and job recognition (r = −0.26, p < 0.001) were both found to be negatively correlated with psychological distress. The only variable that was not correlated to psychological distress, was workload increase due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.10, p = 0.14).

Stress related to the socio-economic crisis was positively correlated with income loss (r = 0.27, p < 0.001), with work organization conditions worsening due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.26, p < 0.001), with access to electrical power affected due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.34, p < 0.001), with access to the internet affected due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.28, p < 0.001), and with access to gas affected due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.20, p < 0.001).

Income loss due to the socio-economic crisis was positively correlated with work organization conditions worsening due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.44, p < 0.001), with job insecurity (r = 0.33, p < 0.001), with access to electrical power affected due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.31, p < 0.001), with access to the internet affected due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.27, p < 0.001), and with access to gas affected due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.17, p = 0.01). Inversely, income loss was negatively correlated with family income (r = −0.22, p = 0.001) and so was job recognition (r = −0.19, p = 0.006).

Work organization conditions worsening due to the socio-economic crisis was positively correlated with job insecurity (r = 0.19, p = 0.004), with access to electrical power affected due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.24, p < 0.001), with access to the internet affected due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.21, p = 0.002), with access to gas affected due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.19, p = 0.004), and with life stressors (r = 0.15, p = 0.03). Inversely, work organization conditions worsening due to the socio-economic crisis were negatively correlated with family income (r = −0.23, p < 0.001) and with job recognition (r = −0.22, p = 0.001).

Workload increase due to the socio-economic crisis was positively correlated with limited access to electrical power (r = 0.15, p = 0.029), with limited access to the internet (r = 0.18, p = 0.01), and with life stressors (r = 0.25, p < 0.001).

Job insecurity was positively correlated with access to electrical power affected due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.18, p = 0.007), with access to the internet affected due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.27, p < 0.001), and with access to gas affected due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.15, p < 0.03). Inversely, job insecurity was negatively correlated with family income (r = −0.24, p < 0.001).

Electrical power access affected due to the socio-economic crisis was positively correlated with access to the internet affected due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.59, p < 0.001), with access to gas affected due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.46, p < 0.001), and negatively associated with family income (r = −0.2, p = 0.005).

Internet access affected due to the socio-economic crisis was positively correlated with access to gas affected due to the socio-economic crisis (r = 0.49, p < 0.001), and negatively correlated with family income (r = −0.3, p < 0.001) and job recognition (r = −0.19, p = 0.004).

Access to gas affected due to the socio-economic crisis was positively correlated with life stressors (r = 0.16, p = 0.017), while negatively correlated with family income (r = −0.22, p < 0.001) and job recognition (r = −0.17, p < 0.013). Lastly, family income was positively correlated with job recognition (r = 0.14, p = 0.044).

Lastly, regarding the control variables, only the following variables were significantly correlated. Age was positively correlated with income loss due to socio-economic crisis (r = 0.15, p = 0.029); age was negatively correlated with gas access affected due to socio-economic crisis (r = −0.15, p = 0.026) and age was positively correlated with marital status (r = 0.34, p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this study, we were interested in evaluating the association between work-organization conditions and broader conditions on the mental health of workers in Lebanon. To pursue the stated objective and be able to paint a representative picture of all workers in Lebanon, we recruited a sample of 220 English or Arabic-speaking people. These people were between the ages of 18 and 64 years old and worked in various industries and jobs in Lebanon. While previous studies have mainly focused on the contribution of work-related variables to psychological distress, little is known about the effect of a severe socio-economic crisis on Lebanese people's mental health. The current Lebanese situation offers a unique learning opportunity as the socio-economic crisis is considered to be one of the worst in its history. Its results could be generalizable to other low-income counties who are also grappling with socio-economic problems. As unfortunate as the situation is, it provides a unique learning opportunity in terms of understanding the repercussions of a severe crisis on a population's mental health. More broadly, the results obtained from this study could offer avenues of prevention and intervention for other countries.

The high level of psychological distress obtained in our sample is not surprising. While most nations have had to grabble with one or two crises, Lebanese people have been facing what feels like a tornado of consecutive stressors. In our study, stress related to the socio-economic crisis was found to be associated with psychological distress. This finding aligns with the stress-distress hypothesis which postulates that psychological distress usually results from stressful events. The dire mental health of the Lebanese people has been manifested by an upsurge in suicide rates over the past few years. Suicides that have been prompted, for the most part, by worsening living conditions [28]. These numbers are alarming and speak to the importance of national and international prevention and intervention efforts.

The results of our study point to the importance of access to electricity and a reliable internet connection. Difficulties with internet access were associated with psychological distress. Similarly, lack of access to electricity was strong enough to indirectly be associated with psychological distress via its effect on stress. Given the challenges associated with electricity and internet, offering Lebanese people, particularly those with high levels of psychological distress, access to mental health resources for free is recommended. These could be offered in hospitals, community or religious centers. An example of such a program is the Step-by-Step program by the World Health Organization (WHO). Wider dissemination of mental health information in the form of pamphlets distributed via medical clinics and workplaces is worth considering. In these pamphlets, we would recommend including useful and practical mental health tools which Lebanese people can apply themselves. Such tools include breathing exercises, grounding work, and tapping (part of Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) therapy). In addition to those practical tools, listing mental health clinics and Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs) offering free-of-charge services could be pertinent. This is especially true for individuals who would otherwise not be able to access these resources.

Additionally, given the worldwide repercussions of the COVID-19 pandemic and a free-falling local economy, the association between job insecurity and psychological distress is not surprising. Job insecurity threatens social identity [60]. According to Kekesi and Agyemang (2014), job insecurity is viewed as unpredictability and lack of control at work. Job insecurity's adverse mental health repercussions can be explained by the psychological contract theory [61]. The negative outcomes associated with job insecurity can be viewed as a breach of the psychological contract [62]. Individuals feeling insecure in their jobs could perceive this insecurity as a threat in general and, in Lebanon, a threat to their basic survival. These workers are more likely to be unsure as to how to protect themselves [63], subsequently resulting in frustration and distress. Given the failing economy coupled with a global pandemic and loss of life savings, is it any wonder that Lebanese workers feeling insecure at work are more likely to suffer from stress and psychological distress? Faced with these adverse circumstances, adopting transparent and timely communication in workplaces could help reduce workers' sense of insecurity [43]. This could, in turn, help increase their sense of predictability and control. Targeting those variables is likely to be beneficial in terms of workers' mental health, particularly in a time of crisis.

Likely due to a loss of their life savings, significant salary cuts and a number of experienced life stressors (as is usually the case in the midst of a pandemic), household income was negatively associated with psychological distress. Household income is a resource. In the context of a crisis, salary cuts, and assets frozen in the bank, having some money coming in is likely to dampen the negative effect of these stressors on workers' mental health. Due to deteriorating working conditions in Lebanon, a number of Lebanese people fled the country looking for opportunities abroad. The remittance inflows in Lebanon are estimated at 38% of the country’s GDP [64].

Lastly, we were not surprised that work recognition was negatively associated with psychological distress. The scientific literature has repeatedly highlighted the positive role recognition at work could play in terms of reducing psychological distress with regard to various professions [41–43]. According to the impaired health hypothesis, demanding jobs exert strain on workers' mental and physical resources, depleting their energy levels and fueling their health concerns (e.g., psychological distress; [65]). That said, this finding is still surprising in the current context, given the constellation of variables with which Lebanese people are having to grapple. The fact that job recognition still plays a role in reducing the risk of suffering from psychological distress despite the mountain of crises faced by Lebanese people, speaks to the importance of this variable.

While we do realize that organizations in general and those in Lebanon in particular have more or less been in a state of survival over the past few years, attention still needs to be paid to workers' mental health. After all, employees are at the heart of an organization. We recommend that appreciation of employees is built into every workplace. This could be achieved through social events and rewards for good work and long service, and manager engagement [42]. Some organizations in Lebanon, such as universities, offer quite attractive benefit packages. These packages include allowing employees' children to study for free, paying a percentage of rent fees, covering medical expenses, etc. Faced with salary cuts, we would recommend leveraging those benefits as a means of recognizing employees' efforts. In the context of brain drain, higher-level job positions might become available and thus, the active facilitation of promotions and professional training could help retain more workers. Based on the results of this study, offering employees access to reliable internet and electrical power as well as gas coupons could help reduce the deleterious effects of the crisis on workers' mental health.

Lastly, some variables were not associated with either stress and/or psychological distress and deserve to be mentioned. While family income was negatively associated with psychological distress, income loss due to the socio-economic crisis was not associated with either stress or psychological distress. This is an interesting finding and could point to the more important role family income could play in such extreme circumstances. More specifically, a loss of income could be temporary and meant to adjust to a momentary crisis. During the crisis, a number of workers were told that their salary cuts were only temporary, and that their salaries will be fully restored once the socio-economic crisis had passed. Household income, on the other hand, is usually based on the aggregation of more than one worker’s salaries.

Interestingly, some variables were only found to be associated with psychological distress but not stress. Amongst those variables is work-organization worsening due to the socio-economic crisis.. The same goes for job insecurity, internet access affected due to socio-economic crisis, life stressors, family income and job recognition. Some of these findings align with those of previous studies (e.g., [44]). That said, their lack of association with stress is somewhat surprising and does not align with some previous studies (e. g. [66, 67]). Compared to previous research, one should keep in mind that this study was conducted under extreme circumstances.

Stress responses are normal reactions to environmental turmoil and can be viewed as adaptive [68]. Stress refers to a real or perceived change to an individuals’ physiological and psychological well-being. Importantly, stress reactions, can result from situations that are unrelated to stress [68]. Those reactions alone are therefore not sufficient to indicate that these stressors are aversive [68]. Although stress is an inevitable part of life, its chronicity, intensity, and perceived load can result in distress [69]. While some variables may not be perceived as stressful at one point in time, their chronicity and compounding onto other variables could result in cumulative perceived psychological distress.

Interestingly, lack of access to electrical power was associated with stress but not psychological distress. Stress was also found to mediate the association between lack of access to electrical power and psychological distress. In fact, this was the only significant mediating effect found in our study. Loss of access to electrical power is stressful in the short-term. This is especially truefor vulnerable groups such as those needing medical assistance and for those worried about the preservation of their food. While some studies highlighted the important role access to electrical power could play in terms of well-being [70, 71], this is the first study in the Arab world to highlight the mediating role stress could play between lack of access to electrical power and psychological distress in times of crisis.

The findings of this study extend well beyond the Lebanese context. As the world grapples with growing instability, including pandemics, wars, layoffs, and other disruptive events, it is increasingly vital for organizations to help support workers’ mental health. Avenues of intervention include fostering job security, ensuring reliable access to the internet and electricity, and helping employees feel valued and supported. When possible, leveraging workplace benefits against salary cuts is advised. The results of this study provide insights that can help guide prevention and intervention strategies to support mental health in high-stress environments. In turn, nurturing employees’ well-being may benefit the organizations they work for and underscore a shared responsibility for promoting employee well-being [72–74].

Lastly, in environments with limited resources, practical ways to support and monitor employees' mental health include using simple, low-cost mental health screening tools such as questionnaires as part of regular HR practices. Training non-specialist staff in mental health first aid and establishing peer-support groups can help them detect and respond to early signs of mental health problems. Mobile Apps or online medical checkups can also be useful, particularly in settings where formal mental health services are scant. Importantly, favoring an organizational culture that is open and encouraging of mental help-seeking is essential for the long-term success of these practices.

Limitations and future directions

Study limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, the study design is cross-sectional impeding us from inferring causality. While this type of design is not surprising given the uniqueness of the situation people in Lebanon have had to face, the results of the study should still be interpreted with caution. In the same vein, the unique and chronic nature of the crises in Lebanon may limit the generalizability of our findings. Even though the results obtained from this study are informative and could provide prevention guidelines for other low-income countries in crisis, the unique sample and circumstances people in Lebanon have experienced over the past few years limit the generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, we had limited insight into the pre-pathological states of the sample studied. Future researchers are advised to complement subjective measures based on self-reporting with more objective ones. We do not know what percentage of respondents completed the questionnaires online, and how many on paper. Similarly, we do not know what proportion of respondents was recruited through social platforms, and how many in-person through a snowball approach. In the future, we would advise researchers to keep track of these different metrics. The overrepresentation of certain occupational sectors and demographic groups should also be considered. More specifically, the use of a snowball approach could have opened us up to the possibility of having a number of participants in similar demographic and socio-economic status. Although we tried to expand our inclusion criteria to as many different people across the country, this limitation still needs to be considered. In the same vein, a certain sub-group of people could have been missed such as informal workers. In the future, running subgroup analysis based on employment sector or gender is recommended. These types of analyses could be informative and help deepen our understanding of the topic. We did not control for participants' place of residency and level of education but believe that those two variables should be controlled for in the future. Throughout its history, Lebanon has been and continues to face a complex socio-political landscape including foreign interference that has contributed to its socio-economic challenges. In the future, we would advise researchers to take into consideration this complex socio-political landscape while examining the mental health of people in Lebanon. Lastly, we would encourage researchers to psychometrically evaluate some of the newly developed questions used in this study as our sample characteristics did not allow us to run such analyses. Very few scales have been validated in the Arab region and we would encourage future researchers to contribute to this worthwhile endeavor.
