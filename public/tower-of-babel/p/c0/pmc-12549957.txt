Examining the predictive effect of physical activity, daily step count and perceived stress level on cognitive flexibility
Authors: Özgü İnal Özün, Esma Özkan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study was planned to determine how stress, physical activity, and daily step count predict cognitive flexibility. Studies used socio-demographic forms, the Cognitive Flexibility Inventory (CFI), the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), and the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). The study also recorded participants’ 1-week average step count. The study had 88 participants, with an average age of 20.72 ± 2.10 years. In the study, a moderate negative correlation was determined between the CFI total score and the PSS total score (r = − 0.596, p = 0.000), and a moderate positive correlation was determined between the daily step count (DSC) (r = 0.539, p = 0.001). It was determined that there was a moderate statistical relationship between the CFI total score and IPAQ (r = 0.559; p = 0.001). It was observed that the contribution of physical activity, step count, and PSS variables to the model created to explain the variation in the CFI score was statistically significant (p = 0.019, p = 0.004, and p = 0.000, respectively). The perceived stress variable, whose standardized regression coefficient was − 0.392, was observed to have the highest contribution to the model. The three independent variables included in the model explain 33.9% of the change in CFI score. In conclusion, it was determined that while the perceived stress level decreased, and physical activity and the number of daily steps increased, the level of cognitive flexibility increased.

Introduction

Physical activity (PA) refers to any bodily movements that necessitate the utilization of skeletal muscles, resulting in energy expenditure1. In 2007, the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) introduced the concept of ‘Exercise is Medicine’ based on scientific evidence demonstrating the positive effects of regular exercise on health. Individuals who regularly participate in physical activity have a lower incidence of diseases affecting the musculoskeletal system and heart health2. Furthermore, there is a recognized correlation between overall physical well-being and engagement in physical activities. The fourth strategic objective of the World Health Organization’s 2018–2030 Physical Activity Global Action Plan is to enhance data systems for screening studies conducted at national and local levels. This aims to promote physical activity, reduce sedentary time, and encompass all age groups and areas of physical activity3.

Currently, walking is the most prevalent form of physical activity. Given that walking is widely regarded as the most practical form of physical activity, there is growing interest in the number of steps individuals take and the accuracy of their step counts. Adult individuals with fewer than 5000 steps per day are considered sedentary, it is recommended that they take at least 10,000 steps per day to be considered active, and it is stated that taking an average of 10,000 steps per day is equivalent to 30 min of physical activity4,5. Research shows that most adults take 4000 − 6000 steps in their normal daily activities, while the additional number of steps gained from other moderate-intensity activities should be between 3000 − 6000. This means about 10,000 steps in total6. Engaging in physical activities, such as walking, not only improves an individual’s physical health but also provides psychological benefits for both the individual and society. Engaging in physical activity enhances an individual’s overall sense of well-being and happiness. It promotes optimism and resilience in dealing with stress and enhances the individual’s capacity for social adjustment.

In recent years, studies showing that physical activity has positive effects on cognitive processes have increased. It has been reported that executive function components such as attention-focus, selective attention, and working memory, which are especially under the control of the frontal lobe, are reduced in sedentary individuals. It is seen that these decreasing skills cause individuals to experience failures in their academic success and social lives7. While some of the studies in the literature covering all age groups reported that PA had positive effects on executive functions8–10, some others reported that there was no significant difference11,12. The main differences between the studies arise from the participants’ ages, health conditions, physical fitness, tests used, exercise duration and intensity, duration of application of cognitive tests, and experimental designs used. Physical activity (PA) seems to have beneficial impacts on executive functions, specifically cognitive flexibility. However, there is still limited knowledge regarding the intensity, type, and timing of exercise that influence executive functions. Furthermore, the underlying mechanisms responsible for the observed benefits have not been fully elucidated13.

Cognitive control and cognitive flexibility are important characteristics that play a role in an individual’s ability to adapt to constantly changing environments and goal-oriented behaviors. From a cognitive science perspective, cognitive flexibility has been viewed as an aspect of cognitive control or multiple cognitive control processes operating sequentially or in parallel14. Individuals with cognitive flexibility have the ability to investigate the environment to identify emerging changes, develop a collective understanding of situations, and create multiple strategies to be prepared for whatever may arise15.

Cognitive flexibility has been shown to have a positive effect on individuals’ well-being, including self-esteem and physical health, and a negative effect on mental problems. Previous research has shown that cognitive flexibility is negatively associated with depression and anxiety and positively associated with psychological well-being16,17. Cognitive control and flexibility are involved in regulating emotion as well as facilitating goal-directed behaviors, and the lack of these skills plays an important role in mental health and anxiety disorders. High levels of cognitive flexibility have been associated with significantly reduced emotional stress using cognitive reappraisal and restructuring18. In addition, researchers have found a positive correlation between low cognitive control and flexibility scores and problem-focused coping19.

As an essential outdoor activity, walking has become a key public health strategy to promote active lifestyles. Despite the fact that walking has been shown to have a favorable effect on physical health, little is known about the psychological effects of walking and how it can improve wellbeing. In addition, the effects of PA on cognitive flexibility are generally positive, however, the results may vary20. The relationship between cognitive flexibility and stress has become a subject of increasing research in recent years, and although some data has been obtained on this subject, the number of studies is insufficient21,22.

The purpose of this study is to understand how lifestyle factors such as physical activity and stress affect cognitive flexibility, an important cognitive function related to mental agility and the ability to adapt to new information or changing environments. Health professionals’ perceptions and behaviors of healthy living enable them to be role models in guiding patients to change their health behaviors23. The sample group was created considering that the results obtained from the study would contribute to increasing the awareness of students studying in the field of health regarding issues such as increasing the level of physical activity. This study was planned as a descriptive cross-sectional study because the data were collected from a sample representing a population at the same and specific time and focused on understanding whether there is a relationship between predictor variables (physical activity, number of steps per day, perceived stress) and the outcome variable (cognitive flexibility) and whether these predictors can reliably predict cognitive flexibility levels.

Methods

The sample of the study consists of university students between the ages of 18–25 who do not have any diagnosed disease, use smartphones, and study at the Faculty of Health Sciences.

Individuals diagnosed with any disease were excluded from the study. The students were informed about the study by the responsible researcher, and individuals who volunteered and met the inclusion criteria were included in the study. The necessary ethics committee permission for the study (Health Sciences University, Gülhane Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Project/Decision number: 2022 − 213)), and written consent of the participants were obtained at the beginning of the study.

A socio-demographic form, the Cognitive Flexibility Inventory, the Perceived Stress Scale, and the International Physical Activity Scale were used in the study. Variables for assessment tools were collected by the authors. In addition, within the scope of the study, the participants’ 1-week average step count was recorded.

Cognitive Flexibility Inventory (CFI); it was developed to measure the cognitive flexibility required for individuals to question their maladaptive thoughts and successfully replace their maladaptive thoughts with compatible thoughts24. The measurement tool, which has two factors: alternatives and control, consists of a total of 20 items, with 10 items in both factors. The adaptation of the scale to Turkish was carried out by Gülüm and Dağ (2012)25. It has been reported that the two-factor structure is valid on the Turkish sample. Cronbach Alpha reliability coefficients were calculated as 0.90 for alternatives, 0.89 for control, and 0.85 for the entire scale. As the score obtained from the scale increases, cognitive flexibility also increases.

Perceived Stress Scale (PSS); it was developed by Cohen, Kamarck and Mermelstein (1983)26. PSS, consisting of a total of 14 items, is designed to measure how stressful some situations in a person’s life are perceived. Participants evaluate each item on a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from “never (0)” to “very often (4)”. 7 of the items containing positive statements are reverse scored. PSS-14 scores range from 0 to 56. A high score indicates a person’s perception of stress is high. A Turkish version study was conducted, and the internal consistency reliability coefficient was found to be 0.8427.

International Physical Activity Questionnaire; The validity and reliability study of the questionnaire in Turkey was carried out by Öztürk in 200528. In our study, the self-administered short form of the questionnaire, consisting of 7 questions covering the last week, was used to evaluate the physical activity level. The calculation of the short form total score includes the sum of duration (minutes) and frequency (days) of walking, moderate-intensity activity, and vigorous activity. The seating score is calculated separately. The survey includes questions about PA performed for at least 10 min in the last week. A score of “MET-minute/week” is obtained by multiplying the minute, day and MET value. To calculate the walking score, walking time (minutes) was multiplied by 3.3 METs. In the calculation, a value of 4 METs was taken for moderately intense activity and 8 METs for vigorous activity29.

Daily Step Count; physical movement was assessed using the number of steps validated as an indicator of physical activity30,31. At the beginning of the study, the participants were asked how long they kept their smartphones with them during the day, and the Step Counter Pedometer ITO Technologies, Inc. was used. This application is a 4.8 rated pedometer program with 50 M + downloads on the Google Play app store. It has been widely used in different studies in the literature32,33.

All individuals participating in the study downloaded this program to their mobile phones and were taught how to use the pedometer (This interview was provided by the same researcher). Participants were asked to use the program on weekdays, excluding sleeping time and water-based activities such as bathing. The total number of steps after a week was divided by the number of days, and the average number of steps per day was recorded. Only participants who wore their pedometer for ≥ 8 h per day were included in the analysis34.

Statistical analysis

Numerical variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation and median [Min–Max] values, and categorical variables were summarized as numbers and percentages. Whether numerical variables showed normal distribution was examined with the Kolmogorov Smirnov test. Pearson Correlation Analysis was used to examine the relationship between Scales/Questionnaires according to normality distribution. The cut-off points used for the interpretation of the obtained correlation coefficients are as follows; 0.00-0.19 very weak, 0.20–0.39 weak, 0.40–0.69 moderate, 0.70–0.89 high and 0.90-1.00 very high relationship35. The estimation model in which physical activity, daily step count and stress variables were included as explanatory variables was obtained by Multiple Linear Regression analysis. The Least Squares parameter estimation method and Stepwise variable selection method were used. Multicollinearity was examined with Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), correlation between errors (Autocorrelation) with Durbin-Watson (D-W) test, influential observations with Covariance Ratio, distant observations with Cook’s distance, Homoscedasticity, normal distribution of errors and extremely outlier observations were examined with standardized residual plots and the adequacy of the model was evaluated. The model found because of the regression analysis was found to be statistically significant. Data were analyzed with the IBM SPSS 21 (IBM SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL) package program. The statistical significance level was taken as 0.05. Sample calculation was made for Multiple Linear Regression analysis (fixed effect) with G*Power Version 3.1.9.4 package program. When Type 1 error was taken as 0.05 and Power as 0.80, and the effect size coefficient Cohen f2 was taken as medium level (0.14), the total sample number was 82.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to investigate the predictive impact of physical activity, daily step count, and perceived stress level on cognitive flexibility. A relationship was found between cognitive flexibility and physical activity, daily step count, and stress. Also, it was found that physical activity, the number of steps taken each day, and stress also played a role in explaining cognitive flexibility. Furthermore, it was seen that the stress variable made the most significant contribution to the regression model.

In this study, it was seen that cognitive flexibility was related to physical activity level and daily step count, and these variables contributed to the regression model. In one study37, lower daily step counts were associated with poorer executive function in the group. In this study, it was determined that there was a significant relationship between the level of cognitive flexibility and the level of physical activity and number of steps. Step count includes both light and moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, and determining step count is a common method of assessing daily physical activity5,38. A major drawback of pedometers and accelerometers, which gauge walking activity, is their inability to ascertain the intensity and duration of exercise39. However, pedometers offer behavioral feedback and serve as a motivational tool. The popularity of the “step count” concept is evident in the presence of programs on smart mobile phones that measure and promote physical activity by displaying the number of steps taken. It is widely accepted that walking is a physical activity that every individual can do, and the number of steps taken plays an important role in indicating the extent of physical activity40. To reduce sedentary lifestyles, more studies need to be conducted to investigate the relationship between international physical activity inventories and the number of steps measured via pedometer, and when recommending the number of steps, achievable and realistic targets should be set by considering factors such as age, gender, education, profession, and the presence of comorbidities.

Low-intensity PA is generally referred to as sedentary (SD), but the fact that energy expenditure is between 1.6 and 2.9 MET distinguishes it from SD (for example, slow walking, writing, cooking, etc.)41,42. Moderate-intensity physical activities are defined as activities of at least 3 METs and above, such as brisk walking or running41. Information about the number of steps varies in physical activity guidelines. Given that walking is a popular activity for both daily routines and leisure time, the number of steps is commonly used as a motivational guideline, particularly for increasing physical activity levels39. In studies, it is important to emphasize that 3000 steps/30 min or 100 steps/min is worth 3 METs for moderate exercise, and to be consistent with current PA recommendations, physical activity should be 5 days/week, approximately 3000—4000 steps/30 min (1000 steps/30 min). It is stated that it should be (step/10 min x 3)43,44. The sharpest decline in physical activity occurs during adolescence and early adulthood. The World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines indicate that sedentary individuals can achieve added health advantages by making slight adjustments to their behavior, such as transitioning from a sedentary lifestyle to a certain level of physical activity. A 5-week follow-up study conducted in young adults highlighted that the mere utilization of the pedometer application or involvement in the study could potentially be enough to enhance the step count or influence motivation45. Nevertheless, an 8-week study revealed that the control group experienced an initial surge in step count, followed by a return to the original level46. According to Normand et al.47, pedometers are appealing for monitoring physical activity because they can provide a clear and easily comprehensible measurement while walking and running. A study conducted on individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia48 revealed that the quantity of steps taken per day proved to be a more accurate measure of cognitive function compared to physical activity of moderate-to-vigorous intensity. Again, a study conducted in patients with schizophrenia showed that the number of steps per day was associated with better cognitive functions in all areas, including attention, reaction time, motor speed, and processing speed49. A cross-sectional study in middle-aged adults50 examined associations of daily step count with brain volumes measured by magnetic resonance imaging. The results showed that among individuals who did not meet physical activity guidelines, reaching 7500 steps or more per day was associated with higher total brain volume. Research has shown that cognitive flexibility can be improved by exercising51. In a study conducted in university students52, individuals with lower initial cognitive performance found greater cognitive flexibility benefits from 20 min of high-intensity interval exercise and 20 min of moderate-intensity continuous exercise, whereas in those with higher baseline cognitive performance, cognitive flexibility increased with only 20 min of high-intensity interval exercise. These findings suggest that baseline cognitive performance is a variable that influences the beneficial effects of acute exercise on cognitive flexibility. We recommend that young adults consider their individual cognitive capacities when adopting effective and appropriate exercise methods to improve cognitive performance52. A study using the short form IPAQ with 128 university students53 showed that people with greater cognitive flexibility were more likely to use flexible self-regulation, which led to more physical activity. Evidence suggests that regular walking has a positive effect on cognitive functions. While sustained cognitive development requires continuous exercise, acute exercise (single-session exercise), which is an effective and low-cost health behavior, can lead to temporary improvements in cognitive flexibility development54,55. In this study, it was determined that as the perceived stress level decreased, the level of cognitive flexibility increased. Perceived stress is defined as “the degree to which people find their lives unpredictable, uncontrollable, and overloaded”26. Upon examining the literature, it becomes evident that perceived stress has detrimental impacts on the cognitive, emotional, social, and physical functions of individuals who experience it56. In line with this viewpoint, cognitive flexibility has a beneficial effect on the ability to handle both internal and external sources of stress. Cognitive control and flexibility are important factors in regulating emotions and promoting goal-directed behavior. Deficiencies in these abilities are closely linked to mental health issues. Utilizing cognitive reappraisal and constructive thinking has been linked to a substantial decrease in emotional stress, thanks to elevated levels of cognitive flexibility16. In their study, Lieberman et al.57 demonstrated a negative correlation between cognitive flexibility and intolerance of uncertainty in a group of adults who had been diagnosed with panic disorder. Cognitively inflexible individuals lack the ability to control disruptive thoughts and emotions and to think about and handle different views in a variety of ways19,24. A study conducted among university students58 showed that hopelessness was negatively related to cognitive flexibility and positively related to intolerance of uncertainty and perceived stress. Additionally, cognitive flexibility was found to be negatively related to intolerance of uncertainty and perceived stress. Findings from the study suggest that cognitive flexibility is an important trait that helps individuals cope competently with ambiguous situations. It also points out that under stressful conditions, cognitive flexibility will reduce intolerance of uncertainty and the level of perceived stress58. Cognitive flexibility is also thought to interact with working memory to update existing knowledge with new information and inhibitory control to avoid applying previous strategies that are no longer appropriate to the task at hand, enabling children to switch to better strategies59,60. Given these important functions, it is surprising that research to date has paid relatively little attention to potential factors influencing the early development of cognitive flexibility.

This study has some limitations. Firstly, sedentary behavior types were not examined separately in the study. Although this study shows that the level of physical activity makes a positive contribution to cognitive flexibility, it does not provide sufficient evidence to explain the connection between sedentary behavior and cognitive flexibility. Future studies need to examine the specific relationships of sedentary behaviors (e.g., screen time, sitting time) with cognitive flexibility in more detail. Secondly, this study did not investigate different aspects of cognitive function that may be affected differently by physical activity and sedentary behavior. The third limitation is that step count only provides data for a single week, which may not be a true reflection of habitual behavior61. Furthermore, while we conducted a standard interview with participants regarding their use of pedometers, we recognize that there may be variations in pedometer usage. Another limitation of our research is that no causality can be determined because the data are cross-sectional. In addition, data were obtained cross-sectionally and from the Faculty of Health Sciences at only one university. Therefore, the results may not be representative of all university students. The use of convenience sampling in the study may introduce bias and limit the generalizability of the results, which is another limitation of the study62. Further studies should be conducted in different demographic groups to validate and generalize the results in different contexts. While this study measured physical activity and stress, it did not consider other factors that may affect cognitive flexibility, such as sleep, nutrition, or social environment, which is a limitation of the study. Despite these limitations, this study investigates the mediating roles of physical activity, step count, and stress on cognitive flexibility and provides an understanding of the connection between these factors. Incorporating these aspects into future research will not only improve the accuracy of the results but also enable a more profound and practical comprehension of the factors that affect cognitive flexibility. It is thought that the findings obtained from this study will raise awareness for professionals and researchers interested in the health of young individuals. Additionally, the fact that the participants’ step count was measured objectively using a standard application can be considered a strength of the study.

From a practical perspective, these findings highlight the importance of incorporating physical activity into daily routines and implementing stress management strategies in educational and work environments. Structured exercise regimens, mindfulness-based stress reduction methods, and lifestyle modifications can enhance cognitive flexibility, essential for problem-solving, decision-making, and overall mental health. A study by Koser and Kolukısa (2024) identified a positive correlation between physical activity levels and cognitive flexibility among students at the Faculty of Sports Sciences63. The study revealed that cognitive flexibility improved with higher levels of physical activity. A study by Yazıcıoğlu Çalışan and Koçak (2022)64 on children requiring protection revealed that an 8-week physical activity program enhanced cognitive flexibility64. Future research ought to investigate the enduring causal relationships among these variables through longitudinal study methodologies. Furthermore, examining the neurobiological mechanisms by which physical activity and stress influence cognitive flexibility may yield more profound insights. Research involving more extensive age ranges and larger sample sizes would facilitate the generalization of these findings to broader populations.

This research indicated that perceived stress, physical activity, and daily step count are substantial predictors of cognitive flexibility. Our findings indicated that elevated physical activity and increased daily step count correlated with enhanced cognitive flexibility, while heightened perceived stress levels were associated with diminished cognitive flexibility. Among these variables, perceived stress exerted the most significant detrimental effect on cognitive flexibility, underscoring the essential importance of stress management in cognitive performance. These results provide valuable insights for healthcare professionals, educators, and mental health practitioners. The positive relationship between physical activity and cognitive flexibility suggests the potential benefits of integrating structured exercise programs or encouraging an active lifestyle to support cognitive health. Additionally, the strong negative impact of perceived stress on cognitive flexibility underscores the importance of implementing stress management strategies in educational and workplace settings. In conclusion, advocating for physical activity and employing effective stress management techniques may enhance cognitive flexibility. These findings underscore the significance of lifestyle factors in cognitive health and offer critical insights for formulating interventions designed to improve cognitive adaptability across diverse populations.
