Discovery of two new actinobacteria, Micromonospora palythoicola sp. nov. and Streptomyces poriticola sp. nov., isolated from marine invertebrates
Authors: Pawina Kanchanasin, Thanarat Salahong, Paranee Sripreechasak, Chanwit Suriyachadkun, Enjuro Harunari, Yasuhiro Igarashi, Somboon Tanasupawat, Supannikar Tawinwung, Sornkanok Vimolmangkang, Chatchai Chaotham, Wongsakorn Phongsopitanun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMarine invertebrates represent an underexplored reservoir for actinobacteria, which are known to synthesize novel bioactive compounds. This study isolated 37 actinobacterial strains from five distinct marine invertebrate hosts, namely Chondrilla australiensis, Palythoa sp., Favia sp., Porites lutea, and Acropora cervicornis, while no strains were obtained from Lissoclinum sp. and Lithophyllon sp. These isolates were taxonomically classified into six genera: Gordonia, Microbacterium, Micromonospora, Nocardia, Rhodococcus, and Streptomyces, with Streptomyces and Micromonospora being notably predominant. Comparative genomic analysis facilitated the identification of two novel species: Micromonospora palythoicola sp. nov. (strain S2-005T = TBRC 18343T and NBRC 116545T) and Streptomyces poriticola sp. nov. (strain C6-003T, =TBRC 17807T and NBRC 116425T). Both species exhibited substantial genetic differences from their nearest known species as demonstrated by digital DNA-DNA hybridization and average nucleotide identity scores, which fell below the thresholds of 70% and 95%, respectively. Streptomyces poriticola C6-003T displayed significant antimicrobial activity and selective cytotoxicity against human breast cancer MCF-7 cells, with reduced toxicity towards human dermal papilla cells. Micromonospora palythoicola S2-005T manifested antimicrobial properties against Streptococcus mutans and Kocuria rhizophila. These findings highlight the considerable diversity of actinobacteria within marine invertebrates and underscore their potential as a source of new species with promising biological properties for therapeutic applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73040-4.Keywords: Marine actinomycetes, Coral, Palythoa, Polyphasic taxonomy, Anticancer, AntibioticSubject terms: Bacteria, Drug discovery, Ecology, Microbiology

Introduction

Actinobacteria, which are characterized by being Gram-positive and having a high G + C content, play an important role as producers of diverse bioactive compounds1. Recent research has uncovered a variety of new substances derived from marine actinobacteria. According to Subramani and Sipkema2, 167 novel bioactive compounds have been synthesized by 58 distinct species of rare actinomycetes across 24 genera. These compounds predominantly exhibit antibacterial, antifungal, antiparasitic, anticancer, and antimalarial properties, highlighting their potential pharmaceutical applications. For instance, guanahanolide A, a meroterpenoid from the coral-derived Streptomyces sp. RKBH-B7, demonstrated moderate cytotoxic effects against several cancer cell lines, including MCF-7, HTB-26, and HCT-1163. Notably, a sponge-derived Streptomyces sp. NBU3104 produced eight novel antimicins, I-P, among which antimicin I showed exceptional antifungal activity against Candida albicans, Penicillium expansum, Penicillium citrinum, and Botrytis cinerea4.

The ocean, which encompasses three-quarters of the Earth’s surface, is the largest ecosystem and is considered to be a rich source of actinobacteria2. Marine environments have different environmental conditions compared to terrestrial ones, which suggests that actinobacteria found in the ocean may have distinctive characteristics different from their land-based counterparts5. Marine actinobacteria can be found in diverse habitats such as coral, sponges, marine vertebrates, sediment, and sea water6–11. Besides forming associations with other organisms, marine actinomycetes can thrive in planktonic forms or within biofilms12. Between 2013 and 2017, ninety-seven new species of novel marine actinobacteria have been reported. Most of these new species were classified within the Pseudonocardiaceae, Demequinaceae, Micromonosporaceae and Nocardiaceae families2. Notable examples of newly described marine actinobacteria over the past decade include Streptomyces corallincola and Kineosporia corallincola, which were discovered in the coral Favites pentagona6. Additionally, Streptomyces spongiicola and Streptomyces tirandamycinicus were isolated from marine sponges7,8, while Glycomyces sediminimaris and Micromonospora orduensis were derived from marine sediments9,10.

Global prokaryotic diversity is estimated to include approximately 0.8 to 1.6 million species, of which only about 1% have names that are officially recognized under the International Code of Nomenclature of Prokaryotes (ICNP)13. The discovery of new bacterial species enhances our understanding of biodiversity, particularly in underexplored habitats. Describing new species of culturable bacteria ensures the availability of well-characterized strains for scientific research, commercial exploitation, and the preservation of genetic material that may be crucial for future discoveries and applications. In this study, we hypothesized that isolating actinobacteria from untapped habitats, tropical marine invertebrates, would lead to the discovery of novel actinobacteria with potential biological activity. The main objective of this work was to isolate marine actinobacteria from invertebrate samples (the sponges Chodrilla australiensis and Collyspongia subarmigera, the stony corals Favia sp., Lithophyllon sp., Porites lutea, and Acropora cervicornis, the zoanthid Palythoa sp., and the marine tunicate Lissoclinum sp.) collected from Sichang Island in Chonburi Province, Thailand. We successfully isolated two new actinobacterial strains, designated as Micromonospora sp. S2-005T and Streptomyces sp. C6-003T, from the zoanthid species Palythoa sp., and the stony coral Porites lutea, respectively. As of this writing, the genera Streptomyces and Micromonospora contain 730 and 127 validly published names, respectively14. This paper details the taxonomic classification of strains S2-005T and C6-003T using a polyphasic approach and describes the antimicrobial and anticancer activities of the newly described species.

Result

Isolation and identification of marine actinobacteria

The details of the strains obtained in this study are summarized in the table provided (Table 1). We successfully isolated a total of 37 marine actinobacterial strains from five marine invertebrate samples, which include Chodrilla australiensis, Palythoa sp., Favia sp., Porites lutea, and Acropora cervicornis. Conversely, no actinobacteria strains were isolated from Lissoclinum sp. and Lithophyllon sp. Through analysis of the 16 S rRNA gene, these actinobacterial strains were classified into six genera: Gordonia, Microbacterium, Micromonospora, Nocardia, Rhodococcus, and Streptomyces (Table 1). Among these, Streptomyces and Micromonospora emerged as the predominant genera, reflecting the culture-dependent method employed in this research. Notably, two isolates, S2-005T and C6-003T, exhibited low 16 S rRNA gene similarity compared to known species and were thus selected for further taxonomic study to ascertain their status as potentially novel species.

Polyphasic taxonomy of the potentially novel actinobacterial species

Genome analyses and phylogeny

The draft genome of strain S2-005T comprises 135 contigs, totaling 6,100,814 base pairs (bp) with an average G + C content of 71.6%. The genome harbors secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs), including Type I polyketide synthases (T1PKS), Type III polyketide synthases (T3PKS), non-ribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPS) and lanthipeptide-class III. The detected BGCs within the S2-005T genome are detailed in Table S1. Strain S2-005T showed the highest 16 S rRNA gene sequence to Micromonospora sedimimaris CGMCC 4.3550T with similarity of 99.1%. The phylogenetic analysis based on the 16 S rRNA gene revealed that strain S2-005T clusters closely with the type strain of Micromonospora sedimimaris CGMCC 4.3550T (Fig. S1). This phylogenetic relationship was corroborated by the phylogenomic tree, which demonstrated a bootstrap value of 100, indicating a robust association between S2-005T and Micromonospora sedimimaris (Fig. 1). Consequently, Micromonospora sedimimaris CGMCC 4.3550T was identified as the closest phylogenetic neighbor to strain S2-005T, meriting further comparative analysis. The comparative analysis included digital DNA-DNA hybridization (dDDH) and average nucleotide identity (ANI) between strain S2-005T and Micromonospora sedimimaris CGMCC 4.3550T. The digital DNA-DNA hybridization value and average nucleotide identity (ANIb and ANIm) between strain S2-005T and Micromonospora sedimimaris CGMCC 4.3550T were 45.6%, 91.3% and 92.3%, respectively (Table 2). This value was found to be below the established thresholds of 95–96% for Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI)15 and 70% for digital DNA-DNA hybridization (dDDH)16, which are used to delineate strains within the same species. This evidence suggests that strain S2-005T represents a novel species within the genus Micromonospora. Consequently, we propose naming the newly identified species Micromonospora palythoicola sp. nov. The phenotypic comparison between strain S2-005T and Micromonospora sedimimaris CGMCC 4.3550T, detailed in the phenotypic sections and species description, further supports this classification.

The draft genome of strain C6-003T comprises 133 contigs, totaling 7,207,802 base pairs (bp) with an average G + C content of 72.4%. The genome harbors secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs), including Type I polyketide synthases (T1PKS), Type II polyketide synthases (T2PKS), non-ribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPS), terpenes, siderophores, and ectoine. The detected BGCs within the C6-003T genome are detailed in Table S2. The 16 S rRNA gene sequence of strain C6-003T exhibits the highest similarity value of 99.1% to Streptomyces parvulus NBRC 13,193T (Fig. S2). Phylogenetic analysis places C6-003T in close relation to Streptomyces thermoviolaceus subsp. apingens and Streptomyces parvulus, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The phylogenomic tree analysis suggests that strain C6-003T represents a novel species candidate, forming a distinct branch separate from closely related strains. Based on the highest 16 S rRNA gene similarity and phylogenetic tree, the type strain of Streptomyces thermoviolaceus subsp. apingens and Streptomyces parvulus were selected for further genomic and phenotypic comparisons. The DNA-DNA relatedness between strain C6-003T and its closely related type strains confirmed that strain C6-003T is the novel Streptomyces species. The dDDH between strain C6-003T and Streptomyces thermoviolaceus subsp. apingens and Streptomyces parvulus were 25.9 and 27.9%, respectively (Table 3). While ANIb and ANIm between C6-003T and Streptomyces thermoviolaceus subsp. apingens and Streptomyces parvulus were 80.8–83.4% and 82.3 and 87.0%, respectively (Table 3). Given that both the dDDH and ANI values fall below the established thresholds for species delineation, it is evident that strain C6-003T cannot be assigned to the same species as its closest relatives. Therefore, we propose the designation of strain C6-003T as a new species, with the name Streptomyces poriticola sp. nov. The phenotypic comparison between strain C6-003T and the closely related Streptomyces type strains are provided in the phenotypic sections and species description.

Phenotypic properties and chemotaxonomy

The peptidoglycan layer of strain S2-005T incorporates both meso-DAP and hydroxy-meso-DAP. Analysis of the whole-cell hydrolysate revealed the presence of ribose, xylose, mannose, and glucose. Its polar lipid composition includes diphosphatidylglycerol, phosphatidylglycerol, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylinositol mannoside, alongside three unidentified lipids (Fig. S3). The strain’s menaquinones are primarily MK-9(H2) at 71.9%, followed by MK-9(H6) at 24.2%, and MK-9(H10) at 3.9%. The predominant cellular fatty acids of strain S2-005T were iso-C15:0 (18.9%), anteiso-C17:1 ω9c (16.1%), C17:0 (11.3%), iso-C16:0 (10.2%), iso-C17:0 (9.0%), C17:1 ω8c (7.0%), anteiso-C15:0 (6.5%), and anteiso-C17:0 (5.3%) (Table S3). Strain S2-005T grew well on ISP-3, ISP-4, and nutrient agar, showed moderate growth on ISP-2, ISP-5, and ISP-6, but displayed poor growth on ISP-7 (Table S4). This strain did not develop aerial mycelium; only substrate mycelium was observed. The colony exhibits a vivid orange yellow color on ISP-2, ISP-3, ISP-5, and ISP-7 media, whereas it appears strong orange yellow on ISP-6 and nutrient agar. On ISP-4 medium, the colony is observed to be brownish orange (Table S4; Fig. S4). No pigment production was evident on any media tested. The monomeric spore could be observed on the substrate mycelia. The mature spores are circular in shape, with diameters ranging from 0.6 to 0.9 μm, and feature warty surfaces (Fig. 3). Antimicrobial activity

Streptomyces strain C6-003T demonstrated effectiveness in inhibiting the growth of Gram-positive bacteria, B. subtilis, K. rhizophila, S. aureus, S. epidermidis, S. mutans, and S. sobrinus and Gram-negative bacteria, P. aeruginosa. The results indicate that the crude extract could suppress both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial species. However, it showed no inhibitory effect on yeast strains.

Micromonospora strain S2-005T showed negative result to most tested microorganisms used in this study, but it exhibited antimicrobial activity against S. mutans and K. rhizophila. The size of the inhibition zones produced by the crude extract is shown in Table S6.

Cytotoxicity

As evaluated cell viability by MTT assay, human colon cancer Caco-2 and breast cancer MCF-7 cells treated with varying concentrations of the strain C6-003T-derived crude extract for 72 h significantly diminished their viability. These cancer cells were susceptible to 20–100 µg/mL of the crude extract, and the C6-003T crude extract showed a cytotoxic effect in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5A). According to the cytotoxic impact of most chemotherapies in normal cells, human dermal papilla cells (DPCs) were treated with the C6-003T crude extract to evaluate its safety profile. Although this crude extract was also toxic to DPCs in a dose-dependent manner, a relatively higher degree of resistance to C6-003T crude extract was presented in DPCs when compared to the Caco-2 and MCF-7 cells (Fig. 5A), indicating that this crude extract is less cytotoxic to the normal cells. In contrast to cisplatin-treated cells, DPCs and cancer cells (Caco-2 and MCF-7) showed a relatively similar cell death degree (data not shown). Moreover, the IC50 value of cisplatin in DPCs was not significantly different from the MCF-7 cancer cells (Fig. 5B), suggesting that cisplatin is more harmful to normal cells than the C6-003T crude extract. As calculated the IC50 values of C6-003T-derived crude extract, the MCF-7 showed the lowest value, 26.38 ± 3.76 µg/mL, while the IC50 values of DPCs and Caco-2 cells were higher than 100 µg/mL (Fig. 5B), implicating that the cytotoxicity of the strain C6-003T-derived crude extract is specific to the breast cancer MCF-7 cells. Therefore, these findings suggest that the C6-003T crude extract could potentially be developed into anticancer compounds for breast cancer treatment.

Description of Micromonospora palythoicola sp. nov.

Micromonospora palythoicola (pa.ly.tho.i´co.la. N.L. fem n. Palythoa, a marine zoanthid genus; L. suff. -cola (from L. masc or fem. n. incola), inhabitant, dweller; N.L. fem. n. palythoicola, a dweller of Palythoa).

Gram-positive, aerobic filamentous bacterium which produces extensively branched substrate mycelia. Good growth can be observed on ISP-3, ISP-4, and nutrient agar. Moderate growth on ISP-2, ISP-5, and ISP-6 but poor growth on ISP-7. Aerial mycelia is absent on all agar media. The colony appears in orange yellow tone. No soluble pigment is observed. The monomeric spores are produced on the substrate mycelia. The mature spores have circular shapes, with diameters ranging from 0.6 to 0.9 μm, and warty surfaces. Starch hydrolysis is positive but gelatin liquefaction, milk peptonization, milk coagulation and nitrate reduction are negative. D-cellobiose, cellulose, D-fructose, D-galactose, D-mannose, D-raffinose and sucrose are utilized as sole carbon sources. Type strain does not utilize L-arabinose, glycerol, myo-inositol, melibiose, L-rhamnose and D-xylose. Enzymatic activities are positive for alkaline phosphatase, leucine arylamidase, trypsin, α-glucosidase, ß-glucosidase and N-acetyl-ß-glucosaminidas. Weak enzyme activities are observed for esterase (C4), α-chymotrypsine, acid phosphatase but no activities are observed for esterase lipase (C8), lipase (C14), valine arylamidase, cystine arylamidase, naphthol-AS-BI-phosphohydrolase, α-galactosidase, ß-galactosidase, ß-Glucuronidase, α-mannosidase and α-fucosidase. Growth occurs at pH 7–10 and at the temperature of 25–37 °C. Growth in the presence of NaCl up to 5% (w/v). meso-DAP and hydroxy-meso-DAP are detected in the peptidoglycan. Whole-cell sugars are ribose, xylose, mannose, and glucose. The polar lipids comprise diphosphatidylglycerol, phosphatidylglycerol, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylinositol mannoside, alongside three unidentified lipids. Menaquinones are primarily MK-9(H2) MK-9(H6and MK-9(H10. The predominant cellular fatty acids are iso-C15:0, anteiso-C17:1 ω9c, C17:0, iso-C16:0, iso-C17:0, C17:1 ω8c, anteiso-C15:0, and anteiso-C17:0.

The type strain is S2-005T (= TBRC 18343T, NBRC 116545T), isolated from Palythoa sp. The DNA G + C content of type strain is 71.6%. The 16 S rRNA gene sequence and genome accession number are LC799203.1 and JBAGKI000000000.1, respectively.

Description of Streptomyces Poriticola sp. nov.

Streptomyces poriticola (po.ri.ti´co.la. N.L. fem. n. Porites, a coral genus; L. suff. -cola (from L. masc. or fem. n. incola), inhabitant, dweller; N.L. masc. n. poriticola, a dweller of Porites).

Gram-positive, aerobic, filamentous actinobacterium which forms extensively branched aerial and substrate mycelia. Good growth can be observed on ISP-2, ISP-3, ISP-4, ISP-6, ISP-7 and nutrient agar but moderate growth on ISP-5 agar. A white aerial mass can be observed on all agar media. The colony without aerial mass shows a blue hue on the upper side and grey-toned substrate mycelium. A brown pigment is produced on ISP-2, ISP-3, ISP-4, ISP-6, ISP-7, and nutrient agar, but not on ISP-5. Straight chains of cylindrical spores, measuring 0.6 × 0.9–1.0 μm and featuring rough surfaces, are observable on the aerial mycelia. Gelatin liquefaction, hydrolysis of starch, milk peptonization and milk coagulation are positive, but nitrate reduction is negative. Utilizes L-arabinose, D-cellobiose, D-fructose, D-galactose, glycerol, myo-inositol, D-mannose, sucrose and D-xylose as sole carbon source but not utilizes cellulose, melibiose, D-raffinose and L-rhamnose. Enzymatic activities are positive for alkaline phosphatase, leucine arylamidase and N-Acetyl-ß-glucosaminidase. Weak activities are observed for esterase (C4), valine arylamidase, α-mannosidase. No activities for esterase lipase (C8), lipase (C14), cystine arylamidase, trypsin, α-chymotrypsine, acid phosphatase, naphthol-AS-BI-phosphohydrolase, α-galactosidase, ß-galactosidase, ß-glucuronidase, α-glucosidase, ß-glucosidase and α-fucosidase. Growth occurs at pH 6–10 and temperature of 25–37 °C. Tolerate NaCl up to 7% (w/v). Peptidoglycan contains LL-DAP. No diagnostic sugars are observed in the whole-cell hydrolysate. Its polar lipids are diphosphatidylglycerol, phosphatidylglycerol, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylinositol, phosphatidylinositol mannoside, and four unidentified phospholipids. Menaquinones are MK-9(H6) and MK-9(H8). The major cellular fatty acids are iso-C16:0, anteiso-C15:0, C16:0, iso-C15:0, iso-C14:0 and anteiso-C17:0.

The type strain is C6-003T (= TBRC 17807T, NBRC 116425T), isolated from Porites lutea. The DNA G + C content of type strain is 72.4%. The 16 S rRNA gene sequence and genome accession number are LC799202.1 and JBAGKH000000000.1, respectively.

Discussion

Over the past decade, bacterial communities associated with marine-derived organisms have garnered significant interest as potential sources of novel microbial resources for drug development and biotechnological applications. Despite the high diversity within marine ecosystems, our understanding of the bacterial microbiota of marine invertebrates, such as zoanthids and corals, remains limited compared to that of terrestrial habitats. A study by Zun et al.17 employed pyrosequencing to analyze the microbial community of the zoanthid Palythoa australiae collected from the South China Sea, revealing that Pseudomonadota was the most abundant group, followed by Chloroflexota and Actinomycetota. Employing culture-dependent methods, Mahmoud & Kalender18 isolated 19 different actinobacterial genera from the mucus and tissue of three corals, including Coscinaraea columna, Pavona daedalea, and Porites harrisoni. In our current study, six genera of actinobacteria—Gordonia, Microbacterium, Micromonospora, Nocardia, Rhodococcus, and Streptomyces—were isolated from marine invertebrates collected from Sichang Island, Thailand. This supports findings from previous research, confirming the high diversity of actinobacteria in marine environments. Notably, our study led to the discovery of two new species of marine actinobacteria, Streptomyces poriticola C6-003T from stony coral, Porites sp., and Micromonospora palythoicola S2-005T from zoanthid Palythoa sp. This discovery aligns with our previous findings in 2022, where we identified two new coral-derived actinobacteria, Streptomyces corallincola and Kineosporia corallincola, isolated from coral, Favites pentagona, collected from Rayong Province, Thailand6. These results reinforce the concept that marine invertebrates are promising reservoirs for isolating new actinobacterial species. Based on these findings, we believe that numerous undiscovered species of culturable marine actinobacteria remain to be identified.

Actinobacteria from terrestrial sources have been researched and screened for critical antibiotics, anticancer, antitumor, and immunosuppressive agents since the 1950s. However, the repeated discovery of the same compounds from terrestrial actinobacteria has diminished their appeal in recent screening programs. Meanwhile, actinobacteria derived from marine environments have gained significant interest lately due to the structural diversity and unique biological activities of their secondary metabolites19. Nearly two decades since the discovery of the obligate marine actinomycetes, Salinispora, and its well-known secondary metabolite, salinosporamide A, marine actinomycetes have proven to be efficient producers of novel secondary metabolites20,21. The detection of BGCs, including T1PKS, T2PKS, T3PKS, NRPS, siderophore, and ectoine in the genome of new actinobacterial species obtained from this study confirmed that marine actinobacteria had ability to produce various kind of secondary metabolites. Both new strains, C6-003T and S2-005T, exhibited antimicrobial activity. However, strain C6-003T, Streptomyces, shows a wide range of antimicrobial activity when compared to strain S2-005T, Micromonospora. Moreover, the crude extract of strain C6-003T exhibits a specific cytotoxicity to the breast cancer MCF-7 cells. Therefore, this finding indicated that this C6-003T crude extract is potentially further developed as anticancer compounds with high safety profile for breast cancer treatment.

The bioactive compounds in the crude extract of new species require further investigation for identification. However, genome mining results can predict the compounds that might be produced by the new species. The genome of M. palythoicola S2-005T contains BGCs for NRPS, T1PKS, and NRP-metallophore, which are similar to the BGCs of aurantimycin A. Additionally, it has BGCs for T3PKS similar to those for loseolamycins A1 and A2. Aurantimycin A, a depsipeptide antibiotic from Streptomyces aurantiacus IMET 43,917, exhibits strong activity against Gram-positive bacteria and cytotoxicity against L-929 mouse fibroblast cells22. Loseolamycins A1 and A2, produced by Micromonospora endolithica, are alkylresorcinol natural products with antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive bacteria and inhibition of Agrostis stolonifera (monocot weed) growth23.

For S. poriticola C6-003T, antimicrobial and anticancer activities may derive from several compounds. The genome of strain C6-003T contains BGCs related to T2PKS for resistomycin and curamycin, as well as BGCs for NRPS and T1PKS similar to those for tirandamycin, and T2PKS2 similar to those for rabelomycin. According to literature, resistomycin, produced by Streptomyces aurantiacus AAA5, exhibits potent cytotoxicity against various cell lines, including HepG2 and HeLa, and shows antimicrobial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria24. Additionally, curamycin is an antibiotic produced by Streptomyces curacoi25. Rabelomycin, an angucycline antibiotic from Streptomyces olivaceus ATCC 21,549, is active against Gram-positive bacteria26. Moreover, tirandamycin, first isolated from Streptomyces tirandis, is an acidic antibiotic produced by several Streptomyces species, including marine isolates, demonstrating antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive bacteria and bacterial RNA polymerase27.

The promising genomic findings of M. palythoicola S2-005T and S. poriticola C6-003T suggest that these strains are valuable sources for the discovery of bioactive compounds. Future research should prioritize the isolation and characterization of these predicted compounds to confirm their antimicrobial, anticancer, and other therapeutic activities. Additionally, elucidating the biosynthetic pathways of these compounds could reveal novel enzymatic processes, enhancing our understanding of secondary metabolism in these species. Employing genetic engineering approaches may enable the production of these compounds in greater quantities, as well as the creation of novel derivatives with improved efficacy and selectivity. Moreover, investigating the ecological roles of these compounds in their native environments could uncover new applications in environmental management and biotechnology. Overall, the study of these new species offers significant opportunities for advancing our knowledge in natural product discovery and development. Based on this present study, it can be concluded that marine habitat is a promising source of new actinobacterial species with potential biological activity for further development in pharmaceutical applications.

Method

Sample collection and isolation of marine actinobacteria

Eight marine invertebrate samples, including the sponges Chodrilla australiensis and Collyspongia subarmigera, the stony corals Favia sp., Lithophyllon sp., Porites lutea, and Acropora cervicornis, the zoanthid Palythoa sp., and the marine tunicate Lissoclinum sp., were collected from Sichang Island (13°09’06.8"N 100°50’03.1"E), Chonburi Province, Thailand. The samples were kept in a conical tube underwater and maintained in an ice box prior to the laboratory. Both samples were rinsed with sterile seawater, and then crushed using sterile mortars. One gram of the crushed sample was used to make a ten-fold serial dilution (from 10− 1 to 10− 4). Each 0.1 mL of the resultant dilution was spread on marine agar (Himedia, Mumbai, India) and sea water-humic acid vitamin agar28. The isolation plates were incubated at room temperature for 14 days. Finally, the actinobacterial colony was collected and purified on ISP-2 agar29. The pure culture of each strain was maintained on ISP-2 agar slope. For the stock culture, the strains were preserved using lyophilization techniques with 10% (w/v) skim milk as a cryoprotect.

16 S rRNA gene analysis

Genomic DNA of actinobacterial isolates was extracted using DNA extraction Kit (Invitrogen) from the cell grown in ISP-2 broth in shaking conditions at 180 r.p.m. 30oC for 4–5 days. The 16 S rRNA gene amplification was achieved through the use of universal primers, 20F (5′-GAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1500R (5′-GTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′)30. Sequencing of the PCR-amplified products was conducted at Macrogen (Seoul, Korea) employing universal primers including 27F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3′), 518F (5′-CCAGCAGCCGCGGTAATACG-3′), 800R (5′-TACCAGGGTATCTAATCC-3′), and 1492R (5′-TACGGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). Subsequent BLAST analysis for the 16 S rRNA gene sequences via the EzBioCloud web-service31.

Genomic analyses and phylogeny

The genomes of strains S2-005T and C6-003T were sequenced using an Illumina HiSeq X Ten sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The genomic DNA (gDNA) sequencing libraries were prepared with QIAGEN FX kit (QIAGEN, Venlo, Netherlands) and the quality of the initial reads was verified through FASTQC32 by Babraham Bioinformatics. Any reads with a quality score below 20 and adapters were eliminated using Trim Galore33, also by Babraham Bioinformatics. The genome assembly was conducted using Unicycler34 using the Illumina-only assembly approach with SPAdes included in the assembler’s interface35. Both strains’ genomes underwent analysis via the PATRIC web service, showing them to be of high quality according to the annotation statistics. The whole-genome shotgun sequencing projects for S2-005T and C6-003T have been submitted to DDBJ/ENA/GenBank with the accession number JBAGKI000000000.1 and JBAGKH000000000.1 respectively. The SRA accession numbers for S2-005T and C6-003T are SRS20442147 and SRS20442146, respectively.

The 16 S rRNA gene sequence phylogeny was derived using the GGDC web server utilizing the DSMZ phylogenomics pipeline36. The trees based on maximum likelihood (ML) and maximum parsimony (MP) were generated from the alignments using RAxML37 for ML and TNT38 for MP, respectively. For the ML analysis, rapid bootstrapping combined with the autoMRE bootstopping criterion39 was employed. The MP analysis utilized 1000 bootstrap replicates along with tree-bisection-and-reconnection branch swapping and ten iterations of random sequence additions. In addition, to examine the evolutionary tree between strains S2-005T, C6-003T, and closely related type strains, a phylogenomic tree was utilized.

This tree and the digital DNA-DNA hybridization (dDDH) values were generated using the Type Strain Genome Server (TYGS)36. The average nucleotide identity (ANI) between the strains was assessed through pairwise genome comparison on the JspeciesWs platform40. The biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) in both strains were identified with the help of antiSMASH version 7.1.041.

Phenotyping characterization

To investigate the cultural characteristics of the new strains, a variety of ISP media29 were employed, including yeast extract-malt extract agar (ISP-2), oatmeal agar (ISP-3), inorganic salts-starch agar (ISP-4), glycerol-asparagine agar (ISP-5), peptone-yeast extract iron agar (ISP-6), tyrosine agar (ISP-7), and nutrient agar (NA). The color of the colonies, substrate mycelia, and soluble pigments were assessed using the NBS/ISCC color chart42. Spore morphology was examined with a scanning electron microscope (JSM-IT500HR, JEOL). Carbon source utilization, gelatin liquefaction, peptonization of skimmed milk, and starch hydrolysis tests were conducted following methods outlined by Arai43, Shirling and Gottlieb29, and Williams and Cross44. Enzymatic activities were analyzed using the API ZYM kit (bioMérieux) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Growth conditions across a range of pH levels (4.0–11.0, in 1.0 pH unit increments) were tested using acetate buffer (pH 4.0–5.0), phosphate buffer (pH 6.0–7.0), Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0–9.0), glycine-sodium hydroxide buffer (pH 9.0–10.0), and carbonate-bicarbonate buffer (pH 11.0). Additionally, the ability to grow at various temperatures (8, 25, 28, 30, 37, 40, 45, 50, and 55 °C) and salt concentrations (1.0–12.0% w/v, in 1.0% increments) was observed on ISP-2 agar at 30 °C over a 14-day period.

Chemotaxonomy

Chemotaxonomic features were assessed using lyophilized cells grown in ISP-2 broth at 180 r.p.m. for 5 days. The types of diaminopimelic acid (DAP) and sugars in the whole cells were identified employing the thin-layer chromatography (TLC) method45. Menaquinones were isolated following the protocol provided by Collins et al.46 and analyzed with high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). The analysis utilized a Cosmosil 5C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm, Nacalai Tesque), operating in isocratic mode with a solvent mixture of isopropanol and methanol in a 1:4 volume ratio. The system maintained a flow rate of 1.2 mL per minute, an injection volume of 20 µL, and ultraviolet detection at 270 nm. Polar lipids were extracted and examined using two-dimensional TLC, as described by Minnikin et al.47. The preparation of cellular fatty acid methyl esters followed Sasser’s method48, analyzed by gas chromatography using the MIDI, Sherlock Microbial Identification System, with the RTSBA6 6.21 column and Sherlock software version 6.2B.

Fermentation and preparation of crude extract

Strain C6-003T was cultured in 5 L of ISP-2 broth under shaking conditions at 180 r.p.m. and room temperature for 14 days. At the end of fermentation, the secondary metabolites in the culture broth were extracted three times using ethyl acetate in a 1:1 volume ratio with the culture media. The ethyl acetate layer was then concentrated using a rotary evaporator until dry. This crude ethyl acetate extract was further used to determine antimicrobial activity and cytotoxicity against cancer cell lines.

Antimicrobial activity

Antimicrobial activity of the selected strain was conducted using the agar disc diffusion method49. In total, 13 microorganisms were used in the study: 10 bacterial strains including Bacillus subtilis ATCC 6623, Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 13883, Kocuria rhizophila ATCC 9341, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853, Salmonella abony NCTC 6017, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923, Staphylococcus epidermidis ATCC 12228, Streptococcus mutans, and Streptococcus sobrinus; and two yeast strains including Candida albicans ATCC 10231, and Candida glabrata TISTR 5006. Each paper disc with a 6 mm diameter was soaked in 60 µL of a 10 mg/mL crude extract dissolved in methanol. Subsequently, the discs were placed on the surface of an agar plate, onto which a test microorganism was spread. Then, the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h; yeast strains were incubated at 30 °C for 48 h. Following incubation, the inhibition zone was measured using a vernier caliper.

Cytotoxicity

Human colon cancer Caco-2 [HTB-37], human breast cancer MCF-7 [HTB-22] and human dermal papilla cells (DPCs) were obtained from The American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Caco-2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco) with 20% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS), while MCF-7 cells and DPCs were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS. All culture media were supplemented with 2 mmol/L L-glutamine and 100 units/mL of penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 until reaching 70–80% confluence before being used for further experiments.

The cytotoxicity of the extract obtained from the strain C6-003T was assessed using the MTT viability assay. Prior to treatment with the C6-003T extract, cells were seeded into a 96-well plate (DPCs: 8 × 103 cells/well, Caco-2: 1 × 104 cells/well, MCF-7: 1 × 104 cells/well) for overnight. Cells were treated with varying concentration of C6-003T crude extract (0, 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 µg/mL) for 72 h. After the incubation, cells were exposed to 0.4 mg/mL of MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (Sigma-Aldrich Chemical) at 37 °C for 3 h and kept away from light. Subsequently, DMSO was added to the 96-well plates to dissolve the formazan crystals, and their optical density (OD) at 570 nm was determined using a microplate reader (Anthros, Durham, NC, USA). Control cells were treated with 0.5% DMSO, while cisplatin, a current anticancer drug, was used as a positive control. The OD ratio of treated to control cells, which were incubated with 0.5% DMSO as a solvent vehicle, was calculated and presented as percent cell viability.
