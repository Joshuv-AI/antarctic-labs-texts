Design of Synthesizable PROTACs through Synthesis Constrained Generative Model and Reinforcement Learning
Authors: Mingyuan Xu, Chaoming Huang, Li Pang, Qirui Deng, Hao Zhang, Anjie Qiao, Zhiwen Luo, Zhen Wang, Chang-Yu Hsieh, Zhang Zhang, Tie-Gen Chen, Hongming Chen, Jinping Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proteolysis targeting chimeras (PROTACs) have emerged as a promising technology in degrading disease-related proteins for drug design. Recent deep generative models can accelerate PROTAC design, but the generated molecules are often difficult to synthesize. Here, we develop the SynPROTAC model, which integrates chemical reaction path-driven molecule assembly with reinforcement learning for the design of synthesizable PROTACs together with favorable binding properties. Specifically, the synthesis-constrained generative model employs a Graph Transformer-encoded warhead or E3 ligand as input and autoregressively samples reaction templates and building blocks through transformer-based decoder for PROTAC construction. The comprehensive evaluations indicated that SynPROTAC is capable of generating new PROTACs with feasible synthetic routes and reasonable physicochemical and binding-related properties. We further applied SynPROTAC to design PROTAC molecules degrading bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4), and two selected compounds were successfully synthesized according to the synthetic routes proposed by SynPROTAC. In the following biological experiments, both of them exhibited nanomolar-level degradation activity against BRD4 and potent antiproliferation activity against MV411 tumor cells. These results demonstrate the capability of SynPROTAC to design novel bioactive PROTAC molecules with feasible synthesis routes.

Introduction

Proteolysis targeting chimeras (PROTACs) have emerged as an effective tool for drug discovery since 2001. − A standard PROTAC molecule contains three parts: two ligands that bind to the protein of interest (POI) and the E3 ubiquitin ligase, respectively, and a linker that connects these two ligands. The formation of ternary complex allows the ubiquitination and degradation of POI by the ubiquitin proteasome system. Thus, unlike the traditional occupancy-driven small-molecule inhibitors that need a functional binding site, the PROTAC aims to degrade target protein and is capable of modulating the undruggable targets which lack the classical binding pocket. In addition, PROTAC can play a role at low dosing and avoid accumulation of target proteins, reducing the off-target and side effects. Moreover, through eliminating all functions of POI by protein degradation, PROTAC might address the drug resistance problem caused by mutations at the POI binding site. Currently, several PROTAC drugs have entered into clinical trials. ,−

The rational design of PROTAC involves designing of three components including a warhead binding to POI, an E3 ligand binding to E3 ubiquitin ligase, and the linker between them. − Thus, compared to the design of conventional small-molecular inhibitors, the design of PROTACs possesses multiple challenges, including the structure–activity relationship optimization for the formation of stable and specific ternary complexes, the optimization of degradation efficiency and DMPK properties, and the synthetic difficulties of the heterobifunctional PROTAC molecules. Especially, design of the PROTAC linker is particularly challenging, due to its cruciality for maintaining the conformational stability of ternary complex and its influence on ADME properties and degradation efficiency of the PROTAC. Traditional methods for designing PROTAC mainly rely on extensive experimental tests and experience, and physical simulation approaches − have been employed in the design of PROTAC.

Recently, deep learning-based generative models have been developed and applied on PROTAC linker design. ,,− For example, the previously developed SyntaLinker and PROTAC-RL utilize a transformer architecture to treat fragment linking as a sentence completion task using the 1D SMILES-based representation. Meanwhile, Delinker and AIMLinker , leverage the 2D molecular graph representation to capture the connectivity and spatial relationships of the terminal fragments and linkers. These models are limited to produce PROTAC linkers in 1D or 2D formats, neglecting the 3D geometric information on the molecule. Recently, Igashov et al. proposed a diffusion model DiffLinker that uses graph neural network (GNN) to learn and predict noise for linker generation. Subsequently, Li et al. developed DiffPROTAC, which combines GNN and transformer for generating new PROTAC linkers. However, these 3D PROTAC generation methods do not consider the 3D binding conformation of PROTAC in the ternary complex. To overcome this limitation, Guo et al. recently reported a 3D isotropic diffusion model LinkerNet that jointly learns binding poses of the two terminal fragments and generative process of the linkers. However, LinkerNet does not consider the geometric information on the protein environment during the generation process, which affects its performance on predicting 3D binding conformation. Very recently, we developed a 3D generative model PROTAC-INVENT, which generates both the PROTAC and its 3D putative binding conformation associated with the target protein and E3 ligase, and it is jointly trained with reinforcement learning (RL) to generate PROTAC with specified 2D and 3D based properties. However, a major limitation of these models is that the synthesizability of the generated PROTACs is missing, even though a synthetic accessibility score, SAscore, , is included in some models. The high synthetic accessibility scores of generated molecules still do not guarantee that these compounds can be synthesized due to the fact that the scores cannot explicitly predict a synthetic routes.

To overcome these challenges, we propose a generative model SynPROTAC that employs chemical reaction templates and building blocks to assemble molecules and also incorporates reinforcement learning to generate structurally novel, synthesizable PROTACs with desired binding properties. The synthetic route-based generative model employs Graphormer-encoded POI warhead or E3 ligand as input and iteratively samples reaction templates and building blocks through a transformer-based decoder for PROTAC construction. Specifically, the synthetic route-based generative model was trained on 20 million chemical reaction trees that were constructed by Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) − and meet the requirements for PROTAC construction. Under the guidance of RL, SynPROTAC can iteratively explore appropriate reaction templates and building blocks for generating PROTACs with desirable binding properties. The performance evaluation of SynPROTAC revealed that it can produce novel PROTACs with high synthetic feasibility and desired 2D and 3D properties.

As a proof of concept, we selected bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4) − as the target protein and generated ∼4000 PROTAC molecules for evaluation. Two compounds were selected through a screening workflow and successfully synthesized using similar synthetic routes proposed by SynPROTAC. The in vitro testing revealed that both PROTACs show potent degradation activity against BRD4 with DC50 values of 20.03 and 67.6 nM, respectively, and strong antiproliferation activity in MV411 tumor cell. These wet-lab results clearly demonstrated the capability of SynPROTAC to design bioactive PROTACs together with feasible synthesis schemes.

Results

Overview of SynPROTAC Model

Framework

Here a PROTAC molecule is constructed through a series of reactions as shown in Figure a, and such a sequence of reaction can be regarded as a reaction tree. As illustrated in Figure a, the SynPROTAC model consists of two main components: a chemical reaction-path-driven generative module for PROTAC generation along with synthetic route prediction, and a reinforcement learning module that navigates PROTAC generation toward desired binding related properties. The generative module is a transformer-based network that employs a Graph Transformer encoded POI warhead or E3 ligand and reaction center embedding as input and iteratively samples reaction templates and building blocks for PROTAC construction. Under the guidance of reinforcement learning, SynPROTAC can iteratively explore in reaction and building block space to search for molecules with favorable binding scores. By combining the forward synthetic route generation and reinforcement learning, SynPROTAC can not only generate PROTACs and their synthetic routes but also improve PROTACs by enhancing their desirable binding scores.

Molecule Assembly

The molecule generation along with synthetic route prediction (Figure b, Figure S1) involves autoregressively sampling of reaction templates and building blocks under the control of reaction centers of POI warhead and E3 ligand. The synthetic pathway is a sequence consisting of three types of actions: the selection of a building block or reaction template, and end of the synthetic route. The action type in each step is predicted based on the sequence of actions generated so far and the reaction center enhanced molecular context of POI warhead and E3 ligand. In the molecule assembly workflow, the reaction templates and corresponding building blocks are sequentially sampled based on the possibility distribution predicted by transformer in each step, and processed until the “end” action is sampled. Upon sampling the “end” action, the product PROTAC is generated by selecting a final reaction template to connect the remaining E3 ligand or POI warhead. The rewards associated with the product PROTACs are computed using the scoring function of RL, and the products are optimized based on the computed docking scores.

Training

To train the synthetic path driven generative model for SynPROTAC, we first constructed a dataset of 20 million chemical reaction trees sampled from 91 reaction templates − and 483 filtered building blocks from the Enamine database to meet the requirements for PROTAC constructions (details can be found in the Methods section) (Figure c). The reaction trees were sampled by using the MCTS − algorithm, which includes four steps of selection, expansion, simulation, and backpropagation, for PROTAC-like molecule assembling based on the pairs of POI warhead and E3 ligand in PROTAC-DB 3.0 database.

SynPROTAC Enables Generating PROTACs with Desired Properties and Feasible Synthesizability

The performance of SynPROTAC was evaluated and compared with the real PROTACs in the PROTAC-DB 3.0 database on a range of metrics related to physicochemical properties and synthetic feasibility (Figure ), such as number of hydrogen bond donors (HBD) and acceptors (HBA), number of rotatable bonds (RB) and aromatic ring (NAr), topology polar surface area (TPSA), log P (octanol–water partition coefficient), molecular weight (MW), and synthetic Bayesian accessibility (SYBA) score. We selected a dataset of PROTACs with 608 pairs of ligands targeting POI and E3 ligase from PROTAC-DB 3.0 as the reference set, and 100 PROTACs were generated by SynPROTAC for each pair to perform an assessment. The common physicochemical properties were calculated by using RDKit. The AutoDock Vina docking scores (Figure S2) were evaluated based on the 3D binding conformations generated by SynPROTAC (details can be seen in the Methods section).

Physico-chemical Properties

Several physicochemical properties (Figure a–g) are compared among the SynPROTAC and reference sets. In general, the physicochemical properties of the SynPROTAC set are as good as those of reference ligands from PROTAC-DB. For example, the PROTACs assembled by SynPROTAC exhibit similar distributions on HBA, RB, TPSA, HBD, and NAr to the reference molecules. Interestingly, PROTACs generated by SynPROTAC show larger average Log P and molecular weight than the reference set. These demonstrate that SynPROTAC not only considers the overall synthesizability of the compounds but also learns the contribution of building blocks to molecular properties.

The AutoDock Vina docking score was used as the surrogate for estimating the binding affinity of the PROTAC with the POI/E3 complex. Here, a similar constrained docking protocol proposed in PROTAC-INVENT was adopted in the RL of SynPROTAC. Results in Figure S2c show that SynPROTAC can generate PROTACs with more favorable binding scores than the reference set, because the average docking score for SynPROTAC set is −12.6 kcal/mol and larger than that for the reference set (−12.3 kcal/mol).

Synthesizability

The synthesizable properties (Figure h, as well as Figure S2a and S2b) revealed that SynPROTAC enables generating PROTACs with high synthetic feasibility as reference molecules. As illustrated in Figure h, most of the molecules generated by SynPROTAC exhibit SYBA scores larger than 0, indicating that PROTACs produced by SynPROTAC are easy to synthesize. The PROTACs generated by SynPROTAC can be synthesized within four steps, and the top three reaction templates employed demonstrate that most popular reactions are nucleophilic substitution, amidation, and amination reactions (Figure S2b). The majority of molecules generated by SynPROTAC require four reaction steps for synthesis (Figure S2a). These multistep reactions could lead to generation of PROTACs with more diverse scaffolds and result in a more extensive exploration of chemical space. The t-SNE, UMAP, and PCA plots (Figure i and Figure S3) of chemical space distribution based on Morgan fingerprint embeddings of the combined SynPROTAC and reference sets revealed that the SynPROTAC generated molecules cover a similar space as the reference set.

Case Studies

The performance of SynPROTAC in designing PROTACs against cyclin-dependent kinase 9 (CDK9), WD40 repeat domain protein 5 (WDR5), and SMARCA2 was investigated as case studies by using the existing PROTACs without ternary crystal structures as reference compounds for RL. The distribution of AutoDock Vina scores shown in Figure S4 revealed that the average score of SynPROTAC-generated PROTACs is lower than that observed in the reference compound. In addition, the three selected example PROTACs (Figures S5–S7) exhibited similar binding conformations but higher binding affinities than the reference compound. Thus, SynPROTAC can generate synthesizable PROTACs with higher binding affinity than those already exist.

In summary, the SynPROTAC model is capable of generating new PROTACs with reasonable physicochemical and binding-related properties together with feasible synthetic routes. This makes SynPROTAC potentially a useful tool for designing PROTACs in drug discovery projects.

Application of SynPROTAC for Designing Potent BRD4 PROTACs

To further validate the capacity of SynPROTAC in real-world PROTAC design, we selected BRD4 as the degradation target for wet-lab validation. BRD4, a member of bromodomain and extra terminal (BET) family proteins, is involved in controlling expression of oncogenes and has emerged as an attractive anticancer therapeutic target. Several BRD4-targeting PROTAC degraders, such as dBET6, , dBET1, dBET57, MZ1, and AT7, that recruit the CRBN or VHL E3 ligase have been reported.

PROTACs Design

We chose the degrader dBET6 developed by Winter et al., and its ternary complex (PDB ID: 6BOY) was used as the reference structure for running SynPROTAC to design structurally novel and synthesizable PROTACs. The E3 ligand and POI components of dBET6 (Figures c and c) were used as the input and terminal building blocks, respectively, for PROTAC assembly along with synthetic route prediction (Figure a). The crystal conformation of dBET6 in the ternary complex was used as a reference in the docking protocol of RL, and the docking score evaluated from 3D binding pose predicted by SynPROTAC was used as the reward for RL.

Screening

The workflow for screening of generated PROTACs is shown in Figure a. The screening began by removing molecules that are invalid, duplicate, or highly similar to the reference ligand. Then, molecules with improper 3D binding conformations such as distorted structures or far away from binding sites of POI or E3 ligase were filtered out. Following this, molecules exhibiting good AutoDock Vina docking scores (with criteria of less than −12.02 kcal/mol) and high 3D shape similarity (i.e., ROCS shape similarity of >0.65) between docking poses of generated PROTACs and the crystal conformation of reference ligand were kept. Furthermore, only molecules capable of forming crucial nonbonded interactions with POI and E3 ligase were chosen after binding mode analysis. Subsequently, we evaluated the proposed synthetic routes, focusing on the commercial availability of building blocks and ease of reaction conditions, to select two compounds for synthesis. Additionally, both molecules underwent molecular dynamics (MD) simulations for further evaluation.

As shown in Figure b, the selected compounds have significant lower docking scores than reference ligand dBET6 (−12.02 kcal/mol), and the docking poses are similar to the conformation of dBET6, in which several key interactions are observed, including the crucial hydrogen bond interactions between the glutarimide group of E3 ligand and residues His378 and Trp380 of CRBN ligase, two hydrogen bonds between the triazole group of BET warhead and side chains of Cys136 and Asn140 in BRD4 protein, and the π-π stacking between benzene ring of E3 ligand and His353 of CRBN and Trp81 of BRD4 protein. The MD simulations on 1 μs time scale confirm that the ternary complexes of both compounds are stable, since the above-mentioned interactions with POI and E3 ligase are generally maintained during the simulations (Figures S8 and S9).

Synthesis

The synthetic routes proposed by SynPROTAC in Figure c showed that both compounds can be obtained through a four-step synthesis process, which only involves simple nucleophilic substitution, amidation, and amination reactions. The actual carried out synthetic routes along with reagents and reaction conditions for the two selected compounds are shown in Figures S10 and S11. As seen from Figure c, as well as Figures S10 and S11, the real-life synthetic procedures are very much consistent with those proposed by our SynPROTAC model. The structures of both PROTAC molecules and their intermediates were verified by 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and ESI-HRMS spectroscopic measurement (shown in the Supporting Information).

Biological test

The antiproliferative efficacy of the two synthesized PROTACs was determined and compared with existing BRD4 PROTAC degraders dBET1, dBET57, and dBET6 across various tumor cell lines including MV411, K562, MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-453, SK-BR-3, AGS, A549, MIA PaCa-2, Hela, and KATO III. It can be seen from Table and Figure S12a and S12b, both compounds exhibited potent antiproliferative activities against acute myeloid leukemia cell MV411, chronic myeloid leukemia cell K562, and triple-negative breast cancer cells (MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453) as dBET1, dBET57, and dBET6 (Figure S13a–c). Notably, compound 1 showed superior antiproliferative activities than compound 2, dBET1, dBET57 in these four cell lines, and comparative activity with dBET6 in an MV411 cell line. Moreover, both compounds 1 and 2 demonstrated most potent antiproliferation activities against MV411 cell with IC50 values of 100 and 380 nM, respectively.

Discussions

Previously reported generative models mostly relied on using simple synthetic accessibility scores to ensure generated compounds are synthesizable, whose results, very often, are not satisfactory due to the lack of explicit synthetic routes. Here, the SynPROTAC model was developed to address the synthesizability issue of generative models. Unlike these models, we employ a transformer-based generative model to iteratively sample chemical reaction templates and building blocks , composing the synthetic routes of a PROTAC molecule. The generative model is further complemented by reinforcement learning to optimize the binding properties of the generated PROTACs. These strategies enable SynPROTAC to generate novel PROTACs that have high synthetic feasibility and favorable physicochemical properties as well as docking scores to the given POI/E3 ligase complex structure.

To showcase the application of SynPROTAC on real-life PROTAC drug design, it was applied to generate BRD4 PROTACs, and two generated compounds were selected by a screening workflow. Both compounds were successfully synthesized via the synthetic routes in accordance with those proposed by SynPROTAC. The following in vitro biological tests revealed that these two compounds exhibit strong degradation effect against BRD4 and significant antiproliferation effect in various tumor cell lines. This indicates the capability of SynPROTAC for designing synthesizable PROTACs, which differentiates from most existing PROTAC generative methods. We expect that SynPROTAC can be a useful tool for rational PROTAC design.

Materials and Methods

Datasets Processing of Reaction Template and Building Block

The linker portions of existing 6111 active PROTAC molecules in the PROTAC-DB 3.0 database are typically simple linear chains, with ≤10 heteroatoms, ≤4 rings, ≤2 aromatic rings, and <30 heavy atoms. To maintain the simplicity of linkers for PROTACs construction, ,, we selected 483 simple building blocks containing two reactive centers from the Enamine 2024 database and filtered alert structures (Table ) based on specific criteria, including the number of heavy atoms (N Heavy < 10), halogen atoms (N Hal ≤ 1), nitrogen atoms (N N ≤ 3), oxygen atoms (N O ≤ 2), and benzene as the sole aromatic ring. The reaction templates were selected from two public datasets collected by Hartenfeller et al. and Button et al., containing 91 reaction types covering common reactions in chemical synthesis.

The possibility of each building block to be applicable in every reaction template was analyzed, and a 2D relation matrix C mask was created to store the relation between building block set and reaction template set. The value of “1” is set to the element if the building block can be applicable for the reaction template, otherwise “0” is set (Figure S1c).

Construction of Reaction Trees for Model Training

Since synthetic routes of the 6111 PROTACs in the PROTAC-DB 3.0 database are currently not available, we constructed a dataset of 20 million PROTAC-specific reaction trees on the pairs of POI warhead and E3 ligand in PROTAC-DB 3.0 for model training. The reaction pathways within four steps were sampled from the above 91 reaction templates and 483 building blocks by the MCTS − algorithm to reduce the synthetic difficulty in wet-lab experiments. The nodes of a reaction tree represent intermediate molecular states by SMILES strings, and the edges represent chemical reactions by SMARTS strings. The MCTS search is guided by a reward function that prioritizes molecules capable of reacting with POI warhead or E3 ligand, and includes four fundamental phases: selection, expansion, simulation, and back-propagation as shown in Figure c.

Selection

The process begins at the root node (POI warhead or E3 ligand) and traverses the tree by selecting the child node with the highest score until a leaf or expandable node is reached. We utilize an enhanced Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) score to balance exploration and exploitation as shown in eq . 1 S(n)=Q(n)N(n)+clnN(parent(n))N(n)+Bconn+Bdepth(n) where the first two terms are basic score of UCB, Q(n) is the sum of reward R(n child) of n’s child nodes in simulation phase, N(n) is the visit counts, and c is the exploration constant. Importantly, this basic score is augmented with heuristic bonuses: a significant connection bonus B conn is awarded if the molecule in node n can react with POI warhead or E3 ligand at the user-defined reaction center (as verified by a connection function), and a depth diversity bonus B depth encourages the exploration of shallower nodes. If the selected node cannot be expanded, a backtracking mechanism identifies the best expandable node in the tree. In this work, C = 1.4, B conn = 15, B depth(n) = max (0, 4 – D(n) × 0.5), where D(n) represents the depth of node n.

Expansion

Once a promising node is selected, the tree can be expanded. A reaction tree generator proposes all feasible single-step chemical transformations from the node’s molecular state. Once an unexplored reaction is randomly chosen from this set, the resulting product molecule will be added as a new child node. The node is marked as fully expanded once all possible reactions have been instantiated as child nodes, ensuring the search progresses systematically through the chemical space.

Simulation (Rollout)

A random simulation is conducted from the newly expanded node to estimate its potential. Starting from the node’s state, a sequence of up to k random reactions is performed. The simulation is terminated early if a state that can react with the remaining E3 ligand or POI warhead is reached. The reward R for the new node is computed based on the best state encountered during this rollout, evaluated by a state evaluation function that considers molecular complexity and target similarity, with a substantial bonus for connectable states and a penalty for excessive depth. 2 R(n)=Maxs∈Ssimulated(r(s))+Jconn−Pdepth(n) Here, r(s) is the 2D Tanimoto similarity of state s to the target PROTAC molecule with the same POI warhead and E3 ligand in PROTAC-DB 3.0 database. We set J conn = 50 if ∃s ∈ S simulated, else J conn = 0, P depth = D(n) × 2, and k = 1 to accelerate the MCTS search.

Back-propagation

The reward obtained from the simulation phase is propagated backward along the path from the new node to the root. The visit counts and cumulative reward values for all of the ancestor nodes are updated. This process refines the tree’s understanding of promising regions in the search space and future optimizes selection decisions.

The MCTS repeats these steps until a predefined number of iterations (maximum reaction steps = 4) or successful pathways are found. The final output is a set of ranked synthetic routes, each route includes a sequence of reactions from POI warhead/E3 ligand to a PROTAC molecule, in which the E3 ligand/POI warhead is successfully connected.

In this way, we enumerated all pairs of POI warheads and E3 ligands in PROTAC-DB 3.0 in two directions: from POI warhead to E3 ligand and vice versa. For each pair, we performed 40 000 times of MCTS search. Eventually, we obtained 20 million of reaction trees, which were then randomly split into training, validation, and test sets according to their POI warheads and E3 ligands at a ratio of 8:1:1. Then, this PROTAC-specific reaction tree data set was used to train the generative model for generating molecules along with forward synthetic routes.

Synthetic-Route-Based Generative Model

SynPROTAC adopts an encoder–decoder architecture that includes a Graph Transformer-based , encoder and a transformer decoder. The former encodes molecular graph of POI warheads and E3 ligands and employs embedding of reaction centers as control information, and the decoder autoregressively generates synthetic routes under control. SynPROTAC is trained on the above PROTAC-specific reaction tree data set to generate PROTAC-like molecules. Starting from the E3 ligand or POI warhead, the synthetic route is represented as a sequence of actions in the SynPROTAC. There are three types of possible actions to choose at each time step t: (1) [B], the selection of a building block (BB), (2) [R], the selection of a reaction template (RT); and (3) [E], end of the synthetic route by selecting a reaction template to connect the remaining POI warhead or E3 ligand. Additionally, the start point is also defined as an action type, denoted as [S].

Graph Encoder for Molecular Context Embeddings

The given pair of POI warhead and E3 ligand are represented as 2D molecular graph G={H,E} , including the node features H={hv} for all nodes ∀v∈V , and edge features E={ev,w} for all bonded pairs ∀(v,w)∈B . The Graph Transformer denoted as GE is employed to encode the molecular graph. Each graph is encoded as a set of node-level representations {hvEMB}v∈V along with a padding mask through several graph attention layers with relative positional encodings as shown in eq . 3 {hvEMB}v∈V=GE(G) The embedding of reaction centers in both POI warhead and E3 ligand (predefined by users) are identified, and their representations are concatenated with all node features, enhancing context awareness. Synthetic Action Decoder

SynPROTAC employs a multilayer transformer to autoregressively predict synthetic actions. The transformer takes the encoded molecular context {h v context} as input and generates a sequence of actions, which are defined by the action type, reaction type, and building block identifier. The decoder utilizes masked self-attention to capture dependencies among previous actions and employs cross-attention to incorporate the molecular context.

For a given synthetic route A = {a i }, action a i is represented as a one-hot vector of reaction types T RT if action type T i A is [R] or [E]. Reinforcement Learning for Generating PROTACs with Desired Properties

Reinforcement Learning Workflow

The prior model generates molecules by iteratively sampling synthetic actions based on the input POI warhead and E3 ligand, and the action space comprises three types of action: “B”, “R”, and “E”. To generate PROTAC molecules with desirable 2D/3D based properties, we integrated the prior model into a policy-based reinforcement learning workflow for molecular optimization. Here, we employ a similar RL algorithm proposed by Olivecrona et al. to maximize the reward of the generated reaction path.

The molecular generation starts from an initial synthetic action and processes through a transformer network, where the decoder samples actions to fill into the action sequence. The process is terminated upon sampling the action type “E”. The reaction path can be represented as a sequence of actions A = {a 1, a 2, ..., a n–1}, including a list of sampled building blocks and reaction templates. The states in S = {s 1, s 2, ..., s n–1} capture the status of previous sampled actions at each time step. The policy π can be represented by the learned distribution of molecular structures from the data set, and the reward R is calculated using a given scoring function after the entire sequence is sampled. The product of action probabilities represents the model likelihood of the sequence: 17 P(A)=∏i=0t−1π(ai|si) Here, t denotes the time step, and π­(a i |s i ) represents the probability of sampling action a i given the current state s i .

Subsequently, the terminal reward R(g) for molecule g is computed by a scoring function once the agent decodes and generates a complete action sequence. Let R(g) ∈ [0,1] be the scoring function that measures the expected value of a desired generation, and g is the final assembled molecular graph. The objective of RL is to update the agent policy π to improve the desired property of generated molecules. Similar to REINVENT, we represent the prior likelihood modulated by sequence rewards as an augmented likelihood log P(A)augmented. 18 log⁡P(A)augmented=logP(A)prior+σR(g) Here, σ is a scalar coefficient. We finally achieved the goal of agent by minimizing the cost function J(θ) to learn a policy that maximizes the expected return: 19 J(θ)=[logP(A)augmented−logP(A)agent]2

Binding conformation of PROTACs

For predicting 3D binding conformations of PROTACs and scoring them, we utilized a similar constrained docking workflow as that in our previously reported PROTAC-INVENT model. This method takes the generated PROTAC linker, a pair of fragments (i.e., E3 ligand and POI warhead), and a reference PROTAC ternary structure (PTS) as input, then returns an initial 3D conformation of the complete PROTAC compound at the reference PTS binding site by doing a constrained force field minimization. Finally, the AutoDock Vina , docking is carried out to further optimize the docking poses of generated PROTACs, and the docking scores are obtained.

Then, constrained AutoDock Vina docking was conducted for the generated PROTACs that pass-through validity and uniqueness filtering. Molecular Dynamics Simulations

The molecular dynamics simulations for the selected ternary complexes were performed via AMBER20 package. The AM1-BCC charges were assigned to the PROTAC by antechamber. The general amber force field (GAFF) parameters were assigned and used for PROTAC, and the Amber14SB force field was used for protein. Each system was immersed in TIP3P water molecules using a truncated octahedral box with a 9 Å buffer distance, and the Na+ counterions were added to neutralize the system. The energy minimizations and equilibration MD simulations were following the same protocol as in our previous studies. , First, the system was minimized with 2500 steps of steepest descent followed by 2500 cycles conjugate gradient minimization by restraining the PROTAC and heavy atoms of protein (with restraint force constant of 50 kcal·mol–1·Å–2). While reducing this restraint to 25 kcal·mol–1·Å–2, the system was first minimized (1000 steps of steepest descent followed by 1000 cycles conjugate gradient), then equilibrated with a 100 ps NVT MD simulation (T = 10 K) followed by another 100 ps NPT (P = 1 atm) MD simulation. Then, the system was heated up from 10 K to 300 K with a 200 ps NVT simulation, and equilibrated with a 200 ps NPT simulation, during which the restraint force constant was reduced to 10 kcal·mol–1·Å–2. Next, two 200 ps NPT equilibration simulations were performed with looser restraint force constants of 1 kcal·mol–1·Å–2 and 0.1 kcal·mol–1·Å–2, respectively. Finally, the 1000 ns NPT production simulation was carried out without restraint. In all MD simulations, the long-range electrostatic interactions were treated with particle mash Ewald (PME) , method, 8 Å cutoff was used for both van der Waals (vdW) and short-range PME interactions, and a time step of 1 fs and the SHAKE algorithm were used.

For each selected PROTAC ternary complex, we performed three parallel 1000 ns NPT production simulations with different initial velocities. The snapshots from each MD trajectory were extracted at an interval of 5 ns for statistical analysis. The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and distance analysis of crucial nonbonded interactions as a function of simulation time in the MD trajectories were performed by the cpptraj module in AMBER20.

In Vitro Validation

Cell Viability Assays

The tumor cell lines were purchased from Shanghai Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences. The tumor cells MV411, K562, MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-453, SK-BR-3, AGS, A549, MIA PaCa-2, Hela and KATO III were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (11875093, Invitrogen, USA) or DMEM medium (11995065, Invitrogen, USA). The medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, FSP500, ExcellBio, CHN), 100 μg/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (15140-122, Invitrogen, USA). Then, the tumor cells were incubated at 37 °C in a constant temperature incubator (with 5% CO2). A masterbatch of 10 mM was configured with DMSO solution for compound 1 and compound 2, and then dispensed and stored frozen in a −20 °C refrigerator. Cells in logarithmic growth phase were inoculated in 96-well plates and cultured overnight in a 37 °C constant temperature incubator (with 5% CO2). On the next day, compound 1 and compound 2 were diluted into different concentration gradients, and an equal volume of DMSO solution was added to tumor cells for blank control group. Then, the 96-well plates were placed in a constant temperature incubator for 72 h. After that, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 and 650 nm of each well in the 96-well plate were measured on an enzyme labeler (BioTek). Cell survival was determined as V = (A – A 0)/(A s – A 0) × 100%, where A is absorbance of the experimental group, A s is the absorbance of a DMSO control group, and A 0 is the absorbance of the blank group (no cells). IC50 values were calculated using Graphpad Prism.

Western Blot Studies

MV411 cells were inoculated into 6-well plates and treated with different concentrations of compounds 1 and compound 2. For the exploration of proteasome pathway, cells were treated with proteasome inhibitor MG132 (2 μM) for 2 h, followed by addition of compound 1 for 16 h. After that, cells were lysed with RIPA (P0013B, Beyotime, CHN) lysing solution (containing protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors) and centrifuged at 14 000 rpm for 20 min to obtain the protein solution. Protein concentration was measured using BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0009, Beyotime, CHN). Thirty micrograms (30 μg) of protein was taken and added to a 5× loading buffer and heated in a metal bath at 98 °C for 10 min. Protein heat denatured samples were stored at −20 °C in a refrigerator. Protein samples were electrophoresed on SDS-polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto PVDF (IPVH00010, Merck Millipore, DE), which were then closed with 5% skim milk powder for 1 h and subsequently incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. After that, membranes were washed 3 times with TBST and then incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies. Finally, the strips were washed again with TBST and imaged for development. For primary antibodies, BRD4 (83375S) and Ubiquitin (58395S) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology, and β-actin (AA128-1) was purchased from Beyotime.
