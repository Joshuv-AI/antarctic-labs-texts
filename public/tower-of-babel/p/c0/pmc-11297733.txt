Can magnetic resonance imaging findings effectively diagnose the instability observed on radiographs in patients with degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis?
Authors: Farid Moradi, Seyed Reza Bagheri, Mohammadali Ataee, Ehsan Alimohammadi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDegenerative lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is a common condition that involves the narrowing of the spinal canal. Diagnosing instability traditionally requires standing lateral radiographs to detect dynamic translation, but there is debate about relying solely on radiographs due to challenges like patient discomfort and radiation exposure. This study aimed to evaluate if Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) findings could effectively diagnose instability observed on radiographs.MethodsWe reviewed 478 consecutive patients with degenerative LSS who had surgery at our institution. Instability was defined as a sagittal translation exceeding 3 mm on standing lateral radiographs in both extension and flexion. Patients were divided into stable (those with < 3 mm translation) and unstable groups (those with > 3 mm translation). The study assessed potential variables for instability, including MRI findings like facet joint effusion, facet joint angle, disk height index, intradiscal vacuum presence, endplate sclerosis, ligamentum flavum hypertrophy, and multifidus muscle fatty degeneration, comparing these factors between the two groups.ResultsA total of 478 consecutive patients diagnosed with degenerative Lumbar Spinal Stenosis (LSS) were included. The average age of the patients was 66.32 years, with 43.3% being male. Approximately 27.6% of the cases exhibited signs of instability on the standing lateral radiograph during extension and flexion. The multivariate analysis using binary logistic regression revealed that facet joint effusion (odds ratio [OR] 2.73; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.27–3.94; P = 0.002), disk height index (OR 2.22; 95% CI 1.68–3.35; P = 0.009), and the presence of the Vacuum sign (OR 1.77; 95% CI 1.32–2.84; P = 0.021) were identified as factors associated with instability.ConclusionsOur findings showed thata higher facet joint effusion, the presence of Vacuum sign, and a greater Disk Height Index were associated with the presence of instability on the standing lateral radiograph in extension and flexion in patients with degenerative LSS.Keywords: Degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis, Instability, Facet joint effusion, Vacuum sign, Disk height index

Introduction

Degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is a condition characterized by the narrowing of the spinal canal, foramen, or lateral recess due to degenerative changes in facet joints and intervertebral discs [1–3]. This can lead to issues such as facet joint hypertrophy, disc bulging, and ligamentum flavum thickening [4, 5]. LSS can cause varying levels of pain and disability, with its prevalence increasing with age [6, 7]. Diagnosis typically involves clinical assessment, with confirmation through MRI to visualize the spinal canal narrowing [7, 8].

Initially, non-surgical treatments like physiotherapy and pain management are attempted for LSS [8]. If these are ineffective, surgery may be considered to decompress neural structures and alleviate symptoms [6]. Studies have shown that adding fusion to decompression in cases of LSS associated with instability can significantly improve low back and leg pain compared to decompression alone [9, 10]. Accurately identifying patients with LSS and spinal instability is crucial for optimal treatment [6, 11].

Spinal stability refers to the spine’s ability to maintain motion while preventing pain, neurological deficits, and abnormal angulation [4, 7]. Traditionally, diagnosing instability involves using standing lateral radiographs in extension and flexion to detect dynamic translation of more than 3 mm [12, 13]. However, there is debate about relying solely on radiographs for this diagnosis since some patients may have difficulty moving due to pain, and repeated radiographs expose them to radiation [13, 14]. Therefore, exploring if MRI findings can be used to identify segmental instability instead of relying on radiographs is important. The study aimed to assess the effectiveness of using MRI results to diagnose instability observed on radiographs.

For the diagnostic imaging analysis, we utilized the EasyViz software from Medical Insight located in Chicago, IL. All MRIs and radiographs were evaluated by the primary investigator, who was not part of the patient treatment team. Instability was defined as a sagittal translation exceeding 3 mm on the standing lateral radiograph in extension and flexion [2, 15]. MRI scans of the lumbar or full spine taken in a supine position with good quality, including T1 and T2 weighted sequences, sagittal, and coronal views, were used for assessment [Fig. 1].

A visual comparison between a standing lateral radiograph (A) and a sagittal, T2 weighted sequence magnetic resonance imaging (B) to evaluate the presence of instability

Magnetic resonance imaging findings that could be indicative of segmental instability were evaluated in the study. These included assessing the extent of facet joint effusion, facet joint angle (FJA), disk height index (DHI), presence of intradiscal vacuum, extent of endplate sclerosis, hypertrophy of the ligamentum flavum, and the presence of fatty degeneration in the multifidus muscle [Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5]. The DHI is calculated by dividing the sum of the anterior and posterior disk heights by the sagittal disk width, with disk height measured at the midpoint of the inferior endplate in the mid-sagittal MRI projection [Fig. 3]. FJA is defined as the facet joint angle, determined by bilateral measurements, while facet joint effusion is measured in millimeters, with fluid levels within the facet joint resembling cerebrospinal fluid intensity [Fig. 2]. The presence or absence of intradiscal vacuum phenomenon and endplate sclerosis (determined as hypointense signal changes in T1-weighted and T2-weighted sequences) were noted based on observations in the MRI images [Figs. 4 and 5]. Hypertrophy of the ligamentum flavum measuring ≥ 4 mm on axial T2-weighted MRI was indicative of hypertrophy being present [15, 16].

The patients were categorized into two groups: the stable group (patients with < 3 mm sagittal translation exceeding 3 mm on the standing lateral radiograph in extension and flexion) and the unstable group (patients with > 3 mm sagittal translation exceeding 3 mm on the standing lateral radiograph in extension and flexion). Potential variables for the presence of instability were compared between these groups.

Statistical analysis

Our data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 23 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois). Descriptive statistics were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median and range. A comparison of continuous variables was done using the Student’s t-test, while categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test between the stable and unstable groups. Furthermore, a binary logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the factors related to treatment failure. The significance threshold for all statistical tests was set at below 0.05.

Results

In this study, 478 consecutive patients diagnosed with degenerative Lumbar Spinal Stenosis (LSS) were examined. The average age of the patients was 66.32 years, with 43.3% being male. Around 27.6% of the cases showed signs of instability on the standing lateral radiograph during extension and flexion. There were 94 (19.7%) smokers among all cases, and the Vacuum sign was present in 159 (33.3%) subjects. The descriptive characteristics of the sample are detailed in Tables 1, 2.

Discussion

Our results show that a higher facet joint effusion, the presence of Vacuum sign, and a greater Disk Height Index were associated with the presence of instability on the standing lateral radiograph in extension and flexion in patients with degenerative LSS.

On axial T2-weighted MRI, the presence of lumbar facet joint effusion is characterized by a high signal cleft [17, 18]. The width of the effusion corresponds to the quantity of joint effusion, with a noteworthy link between effusion extent on MRI and instability, as reported by Chaput et al. Initially identifying facet joint effusion as an indicator of lumbar dynamic spondylolisthesis, Chaput et al. highlighted that a facet joint distention of 1.5 mm or greater signified significance [17]. Any clinically measurable facet effusion (≥ 1 mm) suggests the necessity for lateral flexion-extension radiographs to identify lumbar instability. The comparison of lateral flexion-extension X-rays with supine MRI demonstrated that larger effusion sizes escalated the likelihood of experiencing instability. Facet joint fluid is seen as a dependable indicator of dynamic spondylolisthesis with a high positive predictive value of 93.22% [1, 18].

Degenerative lumbar disease’s biomechanics and pathophysiology are intricate and not entirely understood. The facet joints are typical diarthrodial joints comprised of a joint capsule, hyaline articular cartilage, and subchondral bone along the inferior articular process of the cranial vertebra and superior articular process of the caudal vertebra [1, 12]. Similar to other synovial joints affected by osteoarthritis, they undergo degeneration involving cartilage deterioration, subchondral sclerosis, osteophyte formation, and joint fluid accumulation. Recent biomechanical research underscores the lumbar facet joints’ role as primary restraints to extension and axial torque and as vital secondary restraints to anterior and lateral shear, along with axial compression, particularly during significant spinal segment displacements [18]. The lumbar facet joints bear approximately 15% of axial compressive load, with the intervertebral disc bearing the remaining axial load [1].

Degeneration of facet joints and intervertebral discs in a lumbar spinal segment leads to instability, evident in weight-bearing flexion lateral lumbar radiographs as anterior subluxation. In supine position, the unstable lumbar spinal segment unloads and can reduce posteriorly, creating a gap within the degenerated facet joint. This fluid accumulation in the gap manifests as a hyperintense signal in T2-weighted MRI sequences [1, 12].

The DHI estimated in the group with instability was notably higher compared to the DHI measured in the stable group. Consequently, the authors suggested that increased intervertebral height at the affected level could be a significant factor in the progression of spondylolisthesis in degenerative lumbar stenosis and could act as a risk factor for the condition. When the spine is excessively extended and flexed, a taller disc height is associated with reduced stiffness and an increased risk of deformation, possibly linked to disc structure, calcification, or degenerative changes [19, 20]. This results in a higher burden on the spine’s posterior column during activity, leading to greater tension on the small joint capsule and ligament. Studies have demonstrated that discs can deform rapidly in response to pressure changes, with this rapid deformation being connected to flow within the nucleus pulposus and endplate [2, 20]. Therefore, discs with higher mobility and deformability are more prone to disc degeneration due to increased nucleus pulposus flow. Instability in both discs and facet joints can subject the lumbar spine to shearing forces, potentially leading to grade 1 degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis. With progressive disc degeneration, disc height decreases, resulting in reduced support pressure and spinal flexibility [19–21].

Our analysis indicated a significant association between the Vacuum sign and instability. Instability can cause excessive intervertebral distraction, leading to negative pressure within the disc and allowing gaseous nitrogen to accumulate within the degenerated disc. This accumulation is believed to be a common factor in the presence of the vacuum phenomenon, which is often linked to vertebral instability [22, 23].

The changes in disc height observed when transitioning from a supine to sitting position likely stem from a combination of disc elasticity and instability resulting from disc degeneration. The Vacuum sign has been proposed as an indicator of both disc degeneration and intervertebral instability. In clinical practice, Liao and colleagues highlighted the Vacuum sign at the spondylolisthesis segment as an additional marker of instability [24]. They recommended a treatment approach involving instrumented posterolateral fusion in conjunction with intervertebral fusion using a cage to address this issue effectively [24].

Limitations

The current study has several limitations that warrant consideration. It is important to note that this study was retrospective and conducted at a single center, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the lack of long-term follow-up and the small sample size may impact the robustness of the results. To address these limitations, we recommend larger prospective trials conducted across multiple centers to provide more comprehensive insights and enhance the validity of the conclusions. While our study included 478 participants, a larger sample size would increase the statistical power and the applicability of the results to a broader population. The definition of instability in our study focused solely on sagittal plane instability and did not consider rotational motion or instability in the coronal plane. This limitation could be addressed in future studies by incorporating a more comprehensive assessment of spinal instability. There may have been variations in the definition of radiological instability in the existing literature, which could have influenced our findings. However, we employed a commonly used definition that is widely accepted in the field to maintain consistency and comparability with previous research. Inaccuracies in measuring facet joint distance may have occurred if the MRI plane was not perfectly perpendicular to the facet joint, potentially affecting the precision of our measurements. It is essential to minimize such errors in future studies to ensure data accuracy and reliability.

Anatomical factors such as the vertebral slope angle in the standing position and the sagittal angle of the intervertebral joints play a significant role in the development of degenerative spondylolisthesis, as highlighted in previous research by Aono et al. in 2010. The unique characteristics of different lumbar levels, such as varying vertebral slope angles and facet joint features at L3-L4, L4-L5, and L5-S1, must be carefully considered when assessing instability in spondylolisthesis cases to avoid potential confounding factors and ensure the accuracy of the results [25]. When evaluating instability for potential immobilization, spine surgeons commonly rely on criteria such as a 5 to 10-degree kyphotic disc angle increase in flexion X-rays and a slip of 3 mm or more in lumbar spine anterior-posterior flexion. These parameters serve as crucial indicators for treatment decisions in clinical practice and should be taken into account when managing patients with spinal instability. While our study did not specifically investigate the relationship between kyphotic disc angles and MRI findings, future research could explore this association along with evaluating posterior disc enlargement on MRI to better understand the role of anatomical factors in degenerative spondylolisthesis. Addressing these factors in future studies is vital for improving diagnostic accuracy and optimizing treatment outcomes for patients with spinal instability.

Furthermore, it is important to note that due to its high spatial resolution and excellent contrast resolution for gases, CT is the most sensitive imaging modality for identifying the Vacuum Phenomenon [26]. Although MRI is commonly used for assessing spinal pathology and is usually the preferred imaging method, it struggles with gas detection because protons are sparse in gas, resulting in low or nonexistent signals [27, 28]. This limitation makes it challenging to distinguish gas from calcifications, cortical bone, and reduced signal intensity in degenerative discs. Additionally, the inherent noise in MRI can obscure the faint or absent signals from intradiscal gas, which can lead to misleading results and low sensitivity for detecting vacuum phenomena [28, 29].

However, a study by Buttiens et al. demonstrated that vacuum phenomena can be accurately identified on lumbar spine MRI by incorporating Gradient Echo (GE) sequences into the scanning protocol, yielding high accuracy and almost perfect interobserver agreement [28].

Clinical Significance: The identification of MRI parameters, such as facet joint effusion, Vacuum sign presence, and Disk Height Index, as markers for instability in degenerative LSS patients provides valuable insights for clinical practice. These findings suggest that MRI can serve as a complementary tool in diagnosing instability, potentially reducing the reliance on radiographs and addressing concerns regarding patient discomfort and radiation exposure. Incorporating these MRI parameters into the diagnostic approach for degenerative LSS could enhance the accuracy and efficiency of identifying patients at risk of instability, guiding treatment decisions and optimizing patient care outcomes. Future research and clinical practice may benefit from utilizing these MRI markers to improve diagnostic precision and personalized management strategies for degenerative LSS patients.
