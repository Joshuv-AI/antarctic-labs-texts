Survival against anatomical odds: Management of a 143-cm transdiaphragmatic, transspinal rod impalement-A case report
Authors: Suman Adhikari, Aashutosh Jha, Binod Bade Shrestha, Sushil Mishra, Mrigendra Raj Pandey, Pradeep Ghimire
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionImpalement injuries are rare but often fatal injuries due to their complex nature involving multiple organ systems and the risk of massive hemorrhage. Patient outcome depends upon early resuscitation, the location, extent of organ involvement and post-operative care.Presentation of the caseWe report a case of a 55-year-old man who fell onto an exposed rod from approximately 10 ft in height. Meticulous postoperative care is crucial for optimal recovery.ConclusionThis report aims to emphasize that not all extensive impalement injuries lead to fatal outcomes. Introduction

Impalement injuries are rare, severe, and often require a multidisciplinary approach. Due to the complexity of these injury patterns, they are associated with high morbidity and mortality [1]. Only a small number of patients survive long enough to reach the emergency department, particularly in cases involving thoracoabdominal penetration where multiple vital structures may be injured [2]. Survival following a through-and-through penetrating abdominal injury with a large, heavy iron rod is even rarer and often considered miraculous.

Impalement trauma is generally categorized into two types: Type 1 injuries, which occur when the body strikes a stationary object, and Type 2 injuries, which result when a moving object penetrates a stationary body [3]. No standardized management guidelines exist due to the rarity and heterogeneity of these cases [4], and treatment relies primarily on Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) principles and published case reports. Prognosis depends upon factors such as involvement of vital organs, the degree of blood loss, the effectiveness of blood component replacement, and prevention of late trauma complications [1].

We report an exceptionally rare case of a 164 cm tall man who sustained a 143 cm Type 1 thoracoabdominal impalement following a fall from approximately 10 ft onto a protruding metal rod. The rod traversed the abdomen, thorax, and vertebral canal, causing complete spinal cord transection at T7-T8 level, with remarkable sparing of major vascular and visceral organs. The patient was successfully managed through immediate ATLS-guided resuscitation, expedited surgical intervention, and meticulous post-operative care. This case highlights the critical importance of systematic evaluation, individualized surgical planning, and vigilance for complications in managing complex impalement injuries.

Method

This case has been reported in line with SCARE criteria [5].

Presentation of the case

A 55-year-old male farmer with no known comorbidities was brought to the emergency department in an ambulance after falling from a height of approximately 10 ft. While repairing the roof of his house on the first floor, at around 2 PM, he lost his balance while standing at the edge and slipped, falling onto an exposed metal rod protruding vertically from the ground. The rod was placed there for structural support for plants in the farm below. The rod impaled his left thigh on impact. He was not under the influence of any substance. At presentation, he complained of severe right shoulder pain and loss of sensation and motor functions in both legs. He was lying supine on a spinal board. He was conscious, with airway, breathing and circulation intact, with pulse 86 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 18 breaths per minute, blood pressure of 140/80 mm Hg, oxygen saturation of 98 % on room air, temperature of 98 °F, and a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) of 15/15. Cardiovascular examination revealed normal heart sounds without murmurs. Respiratory examination showed normal bilateral air entry without added sounds. Abdomen was soft and non-tender. Emergency management was initiated by establishing two large-bore intravenous lines, followed by the administration of normal saline for anticipated hemodynamic compromise, tranexamic acid was administered, and fentanyl was administered for pain control.

On examination, a penetrating injury was noted on the lateral aspect of the left thigh, with a retained rod impaled at an approximate 15-degree angle relative to the longitudinal axis of the limb, without active bleeding [Fig. I]. On sensory examination, upper limb sensation was intact to pain, touch, temperature, and vibration; however, bilateral lower limbs showed complete sensory loss, with deficits extending to the trunk below the T7-T8 dermatomal levels. Motor assessment revealed preserved tone and power (5/5) in the upper limbs, while the lower limbs demonstrated flaccid paralysis (0/5 power), atonia, areflexia, and absent plantar responses. The right scapular region was tender on movement but had no visible deformity, swelling, or restriction of movement. Spinal examination showed tenderness over the thoracolumbar region without obvious deformity or step-off. Given the presence of a sensory level at T7-T8, bilateral lower limb weakness, and back pain, a thoracic spinal cord injury was suspected. The spinal cord injury was clinically classified as American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Grade A in accordance with the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) [6]. Immediate spinal immobilization was initiated using a rigid cervical collar and a spinal backboard to minimize any secondary injuries due to potential spinal instability. The patient was placed in a neutral position during transfers to prevent axial rotation or flexion/extension movements of the spine. Extended Focused Assessment with Sonography in Trauma (E-FAST) was negative for thoracic or abdominal collections.

Contrast-enhanced CT revealed a linear hyperdense rod extending from the left mid-thigh (12 mm from the femur, 5.1 cm from the femoral artery), traversing diagonally through the posterior abdominal wall into the thoracic spine, crossing the midline at 6th thoracic vertebra and ending in right posterior paravertebral muscles at the level of the 7th cervical/1st thoracic vertebra. It was seen passing near 7th thoracic vertebra, close to the left kidney and abdominal aorta, with spinal cord involvement. No other fractures, displacements, major vascular injuries, bowel lacerations, hematomas or collections were identified. The neck, chest, abdomen and pelvis showed no significant abnormalities. [Fig II] However, the beam-hardening artifacts around the rod obscured adjacent vascular structures and limited accurate assessment of vessel/visceral injury. Our assessment of the Injury Severity Score was 43, in view of complete spinal cord transection (Abbreviated Injury Scale AIS-5), and no disruption of abdominal or thoracic viscera (AIS 3 each). Hence, we classified the injury as severe polytrauma, and initiated the damage control resuscitation protocol.

The patient was transferred to the operating theater for exploratory laparotomy. Central venous catheterization was performed. An arterial line was opened for precise blood pressure monitoring. Blood products were arranged as massive blood loss was anticipated. A nasogastric tube was placed and Foley's catheterization was done. Prophylactic antibiotics (ceftriaxone 1 g and metronidazole 500 mg) and tetanus prophylaxis were administered. The patient was anesthetized using ketamine. A 3 cm long incision was made, and a 10 mm port was placed at the 7th intercostal space along the right midaxillary line because imaging revealed the rod's trajectory in the right thorax. Given the patient's hemodynamic stability and CT showing no intrathoracic vascular injury, hemothorax, or pneumothorax, Video-assisted thoracoscopy was performed. No injuries were found in the right lung, heart, or pleural space. (Fig. III) The thoracoscope was then withdrawn and a 28F size chest tube was placed and was connected to water-seal drain.

Video-assisted thoracoscopy showing no injuries to right lung, pleura and thoracic cage.

Subsequently, laparotomy was performed by giving an incision from the xiphoid to the pubic symphysis. We opted for an open approach directly rather than a minimally invasive approach because visceral and vascular injury were highly anticipated during extraction. No hemoperitoneum was observed. The large bowel was mobilized, lesser sac opened, and transverse colon along with hepatic flexure was mobilized for visualization of inferior vena cava and aorta. On exploration, there were no obvious injuries to the major vessels, stomach, small bowel, large bowel, kidneys, or spleen. However, the rod had pierced the mesocolon, compressed sigmoid colon, coursed retroperitoneally injuring the spinal cord. It had passed the diaphragmatic hiatus and ended in the right scapular area. After that, the abdominal aorta was palpated and secured. After noting the course of the rod (Fig. IV-A) relative to the aorta, the rod was pulled out from the entry point (left distal thigh). The route of the rod within the body was measured to be approximately 143 cm. After successful extraction of the rod, re-exploration was done. A circular perforation measuring approximately 2 × 2 cm was noted in the mesentery (Fig. IV-B). Hemostasis was confirmed. The defect was repaired. Peritoneal lavage was performed, and a drain was placed in the pelvic cavity. The anterior abdominal wall was closed, and a sterile dressing was placed.

The entry point at the left lateral thigh was cleaned with betadine and hydrogen peroxide. Then it was packed with betadine-soaked ribbon gauze, and the entry wound (Fig. IV-C) was closed with gauze pieces and tape. In view of possible meningitis, a lumbar drain was placed to assess the patient's CSF.

Postoperatively, the patient was placed on a ventilator, and portable chest X-ray was performed to confirm the location of the chest tube. He was then administered blood products, and broad spectrum antibiotic coverage was initiated, and blood investigations were sent. Patient was kept nil per oral.

On postoperative day (POD) 1, the patient's vitals were stable. He was extubated and put on a face-mask delivering 6 l of oxygen per minute, and incentive spirometry was started every 2 h. Lumbar drain was non-sanguinous, with approximate volume of 10 ml. Intercostal tube drain was serosanguinous, with approximate volume of 50 ml. Abdominal drain was minimal and serosanguinous. Urine output was adequate. Intrabdominal pressure was monitored. Oral Gastrograffin study was planned to rule out subtle injuries to bowel. However, it showed no abnormalities [Fig V]. Lower limb physiotherapy was initiated with bilateral foot-drop splints.

Oral gastrograffin study showing no spillage of contrast.

On POD2, a contrast-enhanced CT of the whole body was performed to rule out hemorrhage. By POD3, oral feeding was initiated with a liquid diet, and compression stockings were applied for deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis. On POD4, the lumbar drain, nasogastric tube, and central venous catheter were removed. The patient developed a fever of 100.8 F, and subsequent investigations revealed features of infectious meningitis likely due to metal rod contamination as suggested by Table II findings. We then initiated empirical antibiotics with Vancomycin and Ceftriaxone. On POD5, a thoracic-lumbar-sacral orthosis (TLSO) was fitted, and wheelchair mobilization started. By POD6, the fever subsided; the right intercostal drain (ICD), abdominal drain, and the remaining central venous catheter were removed.

On POD8, the patient was discharged with stable vitals, and advised for regular follow-up.

At follow-up 1 month later in the out-patient setting, he hadn't still regained any neurological function of lower limbs. He was bedridden, needing constant care of the family members. However, he hadn't developed any new complications. Entry and surgical wounds were healed. He was also under regular psychiatric consultation. No significant change in condition was observed at the 3-month follow-up.

Discussion

Thoracoabdominal impalement can injure multiple vital organs, and immediate mortality can occur due to complications such as hemorrhage, hemothorax, pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, etc. [7]. Only isolated case reports were found due to the rarity of impalement injuries. However, penetrating thoracoabdominal injuries carry mortality rates ranging from 5.9 % to 31 %, increasing to 59 % for patients requiring combined thoracic and abdominal surgical procedures [2,8].

Despite the lack of dedicated guidelines for management of impalement injuries [4], some uniformity in approach has emerged based on published case reports and anecdotal experience. Patients presenting under these conditions should be managed and resuscitated in accordance with Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) guidelines. Immediate attention must be given to the patient's airway, breathing, and circulation, and hemodynamic stability must be ensured before performing definitive radiographic studies to determine the trajectory of the rod. Under no circumstances should the embedded rod be removed prior to surgery due to the risk of major vessel laceration and torrential hemorrhage [9,10], as well as further contamination and increased risk of postoperative morbidity [7]. Point-of-care ultrasonography is a rapid, non-invasive modality to identify and manage immediately life-threatening tamponade or pneumothorax. After stabilization, the ideal modality to assess the rod trajectory and spinal injuries/hematoma would be multiplanar computed tomography [7]. In our case, after ensuring hemodynamic stability and proper immobilization, we performed bedside E-FAST followed by a contrast CT scan.

After resuscitation and imaging, removal of the impaled objects must be done under direct vision in the operating room by the most experienced surgical and anesthesia team. The surgical approach should be individualized based on the patient's hemodynamic status and imaging findings [7]. In hemodynamically stable patients where imaging demonstrates no major vascular or visceral injury, minimally invasive techniques may be appropriate. Video-assisted Thoracoscopic Surgery (VATS) allows direct thoracic visualization and eliminates the need for conventional thoracotomy in stable patients [11]. Conversely, laparotomy is often preferred over laparoscopy for abdominal penetrations due to better visualization and ability to rapidly control hemorrhage if major vascular injury is encountered [8]. The combined thoracic and abdominal approach has been employed successfully in similar cases. Valsef et al. reported multiple impalement by fence posts involving facial, thoracic, and abdominal injuries, managed with thoracotomy and laparotomy with rod extraction under direct vision [12].

In our case, the patient's hemodynamic stability and CT findings guided a stepwise approach. Ketamine was preferred for anesthetic induction as it supports circulation by increasing sympathetic outflow [11]. We first performed VATS to evaluate the thoracic cavity, which confirmed the absence of lung, cardiac, or pleural injuries, thereby avoiding the morbidity of an unnecessary thoracotomy [11]. We then proceeded with laparotomy given the extensive length of the rod (143 cm in-situ) and its close proximity to the abdominal aorta and inferior vena cava. Injuries to these structures are well-documented complications in such cases [12,13], and open exploration provides optimal visualization and immediate vascular control capability if needed. Fortunately, despite the rod's trajectory, there were no injuries to the major vessels or vital organs. Regarding the timing of surgery, the patient's complete spinal cord transection (ASIA-A at T7-T8 level) indicated that immediate surgical decompression would not improve the neurological outcome. However, urgent rod removal was still necessary to prevent infectious complications from the contaminated foreign body and to enable subsequent rehabilitation.

Intraoperatively, rod extraction should be done carefully to prevent unintended trauma to the viscera during manipulation. Excessive irrigation or debridement while the rod remains in situ should be deliberately avoided, and the rod should be removed in the reverse direction of impalement [14]. The impaled object may be bent or broken inside the body, complicating extraction. In our case, the leading end of the rod was bent, likely from impact with the vertebrae, which made extraction difficult and required multiple forceful attempts. Similar cases in the literature demonstrate variable presentations and outcomes of rod impalements. Kolahdouzan et al. reported a 25-year-old male with thoracoabdominal penetration by multiple metal rods without visceral involvement, managed successfully with resuscitation and exploratory laparotomy [13]. In contrast, Philip et al. described a 63-year-old female with abdominal impalement through the popliteal fossa causing descending colon and diaphragm injury who presented hypotensive and required aggressive resuscitation and surgical repair [15]. Our case is notable for the exceptional rod length (143 cm) and remarkable absence of major vascular or visceral injury despite traversing multiple body cavities and causing complete spinal cord transection.

Postoperatively, continuous monitoring of vital signs, urine output, and drain output is paramount. ICU admission, fluid resuscitation, blood transfusions, and DVT prophylaxis may be required depending on injury severity [16]. Broad-spectrum antibiotics and tetanus prophylaxis are recommended for all impalement injuries [9]. Infectious complications warrant particular vigilance. In our patient, the CSF analysis on POD4 revealed features of meningitis, likely due to contamination from the rod traversing the spinal canal and piercing through the dura mater. We promptly initiated empirical therapy with vancomycin and ceftriaxone [17], which successfully resolved the infection, based on subsequent CSF reports, and fever resolution. This highlights the importance of monitoring for CNS infection when the impaled object invades the meninges. Beyond infectious complications, vigilance is suggested for hemorrhage, pain management, and the psychological impact of such injuries. Long term, neurological disability represents the most significant challenge in rehabilitation after spinal cord injuries. While our patient's complete spinal cord transection (ASIA-A) precluded neurological recovery [18], this case underscores that further research is needed to identify factors that influence outcomes in patients with incomplete injuries and to optimize rehabilitation for those with irreversible deficits.

Looking toward future advances in trauma care, emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence may be of particular benefit in managing cases like ours. Beyond simple detection, deep learning algorithms could analyze complex spatial relationships [19], for example, between impaled objects and vital structures to generate probabilistic risk assessments for vascular injury, organ damage, and neurological compromise. Real-time surgical navigation systems enhanced by AI could provide intraoperative guidance during extraction, alerting surgeons to proximity of critical structures and suggesting optimal manipulation angles. AI could also establish complication prediction models by integrating multiple data streams including vital signs, laboratory values, and imaging findings [20]. However, the heterogeneity of impalement injuries necessitates large datasets before AI algorithms can achieve clinically meaningful accuracy.

Limitations

This case may not be generalizable to most cases; many associated injuries are commonly encountered in such massive impalements. Our management was relatively straightforward as our patient was fortunate enough to have avoided vascular and visceral involvement.

Conclusion

Impalement injuries remain rare and challenging, with limited epidemiological data and no standardized management protocols. Our case demonstrates that even massive impalement injuries involving multiple body cavities can be successfully managed through adherence to ATLS principles, individualized surgical approaches, and meticulous postoperative care. The extensive rod length (143 cm), complete spinal cord transection with survival, and absence of major vascular or visceral injury make this case particularly instructive.
