Artificial Intelligence Use in Academic Applicant Screening: A Systematic Review
Authors: Timothy Jiang, Seray Er, Matthew J. Heron, Katherine J. Zhu, Robin Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Escalating application volumes challenge holistic admissions review; artificial intelligence (AI) offers potential screening efficiencies. This systematic review examined AI algorithm use in academic admissions, categorizing types and evaluating objectives, performance, and ethical considerations.Methods:A systematic search of 5 databases through October 2024 identified original research on AI screening in undergraduate to medical residency programs. Data extraction covered study characteristics, AI types, performance metrics, and ethical considerations. Bias risk was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, and objective outcomes were compared statistically.Results:Eighteen studies (61,327 applicants) were included. The primary uses of AI were interview selection (n = 7), admissions decisions (n = 4), and reviewer scoring (n = 2). Algorithms included traditional machine learning (TML) and natural language processing (NLP). In the subset of studies reporting area under the receiver operating characteristic metrics, TML models averaged 0.89 versus 0.77 for NLP models. Ethical concerns, notably bias, were reported in 50% of studies.Conclusions:TML algorithms excelled with structured data; NLP showed value for unstructured data despite challenges (eg, transparency and data needs). Ethical concerns highlight the need for transparency and bias mitigation in AI adoption. Findings emphasize continued research to address ethical and methodological challenges.

Takeaway

Question: How is artificial intelligence (AI) currently being used to manage surging applicant volumes in higher education and residency admissions?

Findings: Eighteen empirical studies show that AI tools already score structured metrics (traditional machine learning) and parse narrative documents (natural language processing) at several decision points; half explicitly reported bias concerns.

Meaning: AI can streamline applicant triage—especially for high‑volume fields such as plastic surgery—but must be deployed with transparent scoring rules, subgroup audits, and prospective validation.

INTRODUCTION

Academic institutions in the United States have experienced a steady rise in applicant volumes over the past several decades, a trend that accelerated significantly with the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Between 2020 and 2021, undergraduate applications increased by 11%,1 and applications to master’s programs increased by 16%.2 Healthcare education programs have experienced an even more pronounced rise in applications. The Association of American Medical Colleges reported an 18% increase in applications between 2020 and 2021, a significant escalation compared with the 1%–3% annual increases observed during the preceding decade.3 Plastic surgery programs are exposed to a steep rise in volume. In the 2023–2024 cycle, 93% of integrated programs adopted the Plastic Surgery Common Application (PSCA) and 54% required it exclusively; by stripping away duplicate fees and paperwork, the PSCA lets each applicant broadcast the same dossier to dozens of programs with a single click, multiplying submissions per applicant and swelling overall volume.4,5

Rapid advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) technologies have introduced timely AI solutions for the processing and screening of applicants. AI is defined as the ability of technology to analyze inputs and produce outputs, with some degree of autonomy, to achieve a specific goal.6 The AI algorithms used in application screening can be broadly categorized into 2 main types: traditional machine learning (TML) and natural language processing (NLP). TML algorithms identify patterns within structured datasets and use these patterns to make predictions.7 In practice, TML works best when every applicant can be summarized by the same set of numerical fields, such as grade point average (GPA), test scores, number of publications, and clinical hours. The computer reviews thousands of past cases, learns how each number nudges the chance of getting an interview or offer, and then combines those learned weights to score new applicants. In contrast, NLP algorithms can analyze unstructured human language and make predictions about meaning, emotional sentiment, and other abstract concepts.8 NLP is designed for text-heavy materials such as personal statements and letters of recommendation (LORs). The software first converts each sentence into a set of numerical signatures that together encode its grammar and meaning, then searches for recurring patterns that distinguish previously successful candidates from those who were not selected. These 2 types of AI may be used together or separately in the setting of admissions.

The rapid advancement of AI across disciplines means that this technology is likely unavoidable in the future of academic admissions processes. Therefore, this systematic review aims to examine the landscape of AI for applicant selection in the setting of higher education and medical admissions. Specifically, we investigate AI’s role in screening, interview selection, and admissions to synthesize the available evidence on the interventions, outcomes, and limitations of algorithms that have been implemented in practice.

METHODS

Systematic Review

We conducted a comprehensive search of 5 databases—PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, and Cochrane—for peer-reviewed articles published from inception to October 2024 on the use of AI in academic admissions. The search focused on 3 primary concepts: AI, interview selection and screening, and admissions decisions. Each concept was represented using a combination of free-text keywords to ensure comprehensive coverage. (See appendix, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the full search criteria inputs, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E376.)

Eligibility Criteria

We included original research on AI use in undergraduate, graduate, medical school, or residency admissions. We excluded investigations aimed at differentiating AI from human work, as well as those that described an algorithm created by a third party to predict admission chances. Excluded studies focused on nonacademic hiring. Also excluded were non-English articles, reviews, case reports, and abstracts. Each article was screened by 2 of 3 independent reviewers (S.E., K.J.Z., M.J.H.) using Covidence (Covidence systematic review software; Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia). Disagreements between reviewers were resolved by discussion.

Data Extraction and Bias Assessment

Three blinded reviewers (S.E., K.J.Z., M.J.H.) independently extracted information from each article. Data were gathered on the first author, publication year, journal, methodology (eg, algorithm type, structured versus unstructured application data), decision stages (eg, initial screening, interview selection), evaluation metrics (eg, area under the receiver operating characteristic [AUROC]), and mention of ethical considerations. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, which ranges from 0 (highest risk of bias) to 9 (lowest risk of bias), was used to assess the risk of bias for each article.

Statistical Approach

Because only 9 studies reported AUROC and study methods were heterogeneous, we summarized performance metrics and bias counts descriptively; no formal significance testing was performed. Results from this systematic review were reported in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement, as shown in Supplemental Digital Content 2.9 (See appendix, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses systematic review checklist, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E377.)

RESULTS

Study Characteristics

The literature search returned 2495 articles, from which 18 articles and 61,327 total applicants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Nine studies assessed the use of AI in residency admissions, 4 in graduate school admissions, 3 in undergraduate admissions, and 2 in medical school admissions. The primary outcomes of the studies included interview invites (n = 7), admissions decisions (n = 4), internal reviewer ratings (n = 2), and student performance after matriculation (n = 1). The studies varied in terms of their stage of AI adoption. Seven studies reported full implementation of AI, whereas 11 reported on AI in pilot stages with limited empirical validation. When stratifying by algorithm type, 5 of 11 TML studies noted concerns about bias within their algorithms, whereas 4 of 7 NLP studies noted ethical concerns related to bias in their algorithms. AI Tool Categorization and Evaluation

The included articles used both TML and NLP algorithms. TML algorithms were generally used to rank or score applicants based on specified criteria, such as grade point averages (n = 11). In contrast, NLP algorithms were used to assess LORs, essays, and other excerpts of unstructured writing (n = 7).

Nine studies reported AUROC values, which measure the performance of AI systems in comparison to human reviewers. These studies reported an average AUROC of 0.84, demonstrating good discriminative ability. Among studies that reported AUROC, 6 used TML algorithms, and 3 used NLP algorithms. Across the 9 studies that reported AUROC, TML models showed a higher mean discriminative performance than NLP models (0.89 versus 0.77); the small, heterogeneous sample precluded formal significance testing.

Use of AI in Interview Invitations

Seven studies reported on the use of AI for interview invitations. Boolchandani et al10 introduced the use of NLP to analyze applicant narratives in the interview selection process. Although they found no significant association between language and interview status, their findings showed AI’s potential to evaluate unstructured data for prioritizing interview candidates. Similarly, Mahtani et al16 expanded on this premise by integrating structured and unstructured data to develop NLP algorithms with high accuracy (AUROC = 0.92) and found that phrases indicating active leadership, research, or social justice were associated with interview invitations.

Drum et al12 developed NLP algorithms to identify values associated with resident physician success, such as compassion, professionalism, and communication, within applicant personal statements. This study linked values such as compassion and professionalism in personal statements to interview invitations, showing that AI can incorporate an institution’s priorities into holistic review.16

The role of AI in analyzing qualitative data was further explored by Vasan et al,24 who applied NLP to LORs. Their study revealed that moderately positive language in recommendation letters correlated with fewer interview invitations, suggesting it subtly signaled applicant weakness.12

Four studies used TML algorithms to facilitate interview selection. Triola et al23 developed a virtual TML-based faculty screener that mimicked human decision-making for interview selection. Their randomized trial showed no significant difference between AI- and faculty-driven recommendations.24 Keir et al14 emphasized their exclusion of demographic identifiers, such as names and ZIP codes, in their TML algorithm to mitigate bias that may arise from such variables. Similarly, Burk-Rafel et al11 illustrated how machine learning algorithms can facilitate holistic reviews for applicants initially excluded during the screening process, showcasing the potential of TML to promote equitable review for all candidates while preserving the significant time-saving benefits of automated screening.

Use of AI in Admissions Decisions

Four articles reported on the use of AI to streamline and standardize admissions decisions. Lux et al15 pioneered a supervised TML model to predict admissions outcomes, achieving high accuracy while reducing the manual review burden. Young and Caballero25 applied a TML model to physics PhD admissions and found that undergraduate GPA and physics Graduate Record Examinations score were the strongest predictors (≈75% accuracy). Rees et al21 used a random forest (TML) model to predict both admissions and matriculation in internal medicine residency; it was highly accurate for ranking admissions candidates (AUROC = 0.925) but performed poorly in predicting matriculation (AUROC = 0.597), highlighting the influence of external factors on final enrollment.25

AI has also been applied to unstructured data when predicting final admissions decisions. Ortiz et al19 reported that NLP analysis of neurosurgery recommendation letters had moderate accuracy (AUROC = 0.75) and could complement structured data (TML) methods in admissions decisions. Similarly, Zhao et al26 showed that unstructured text could be converted for use in a TML model, enabling analysis of narrative data with the transparency of TML. This is noteworthy because narrative text analysis was long regarded as achievable only through NLP techniques.19

Use of AI in Verifying Internal Reviewer Ratings

Two studies used AI algorithms to verify internal reviewer scoring in admissions. Martinez Neda et al18 demonstrated that TML algorithms can standardize reviewer ratings, reducing bias and variability, whereas Shao et al22 further developed the mathematical, quantitative framework underlying TML algorithms within this context. Halagur et al13 advanced these efforts by using TML algorithms to simulate selection committee members, revealing consistent biases favoring applicants who shared demographic characteristics with the simulated committee members. Their findings suggested how TML may uncover institution-specific patterns of bias in decision-making.22

Zhao et al27 extended these advancements to unstructured data, using NLP to evaluate LORs on relevance, specificity, and positivity. Their algorithms showed moderate success, particularly in predicting specificity (AUROC = 0.76).13 Their results demonstrated that unstructured data may not be sufficient to use in isolation but may complement structured scoring systems such as those reported by Martinez Neda et al18.

Risk of Study Bias

Across 17 studies assessed with the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (1 methodological study excluded), the mean score was 6.65 out of 9.00 (SD 1.06), indicating a moderate risk of bias. Beyond criteria taken into account by the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale score, 3 recurrent bias mechanisms specific to AI algorithms emerged: (1) demographic feature leakage, in which potentially confounding variables unrelated to applicant merit—such as race, sex, citizenship status, or ZIP code—were directly incorporated into ranking algorithms; (n = 7, 39%); (2) linguistic proxy bias arising from narrative text, where words that correlated with gendered or racialized language inadvertently reduced interview odds for women and under represented minority applicants (n = 5, 28%); and (3) performance-metric bias, where algorithms relied heavily on standardized tests (USMLE Step 1, Graduate Record Examinations, Scholastic Aptitude Test/American College Test, Medical College Admissions Test) that themselves exhibit known score gaps by socioeconomic status (n = 4, 22%).28

DISCUSSION

In this systematic review, we examined the use of AI in admissions across higher education institutions. In the limited studies that reported performance metrics, TML models showed higher AUROC point estimates than NLP models. NLP algorithms demonstrated moderate success in evaluating unstructured data such as LORs, but their performance was often lower and highlighted insufficiencies when used independently. Ethical concerns, including bias in algorithm design and data inputs, were noted in half of the studies, emphasizing the need for transparency and fairness in AI implementation. Overall, AI has shown promise in streamlining admissions processes, but its effectiveness varies by application type, and its integration requires careful consideration of ethical and methodological challenges.

In this review, we have categorized AI algorithms used in application screening into 2 main types: TML and NLP. Because unstructured applicant data tend to be the most time-consuming to review, the integration of NLP algorithms into admissions processes has been of particular interest. However, due to the unstructured nature of human language and the complexity of NLP algorithms, these features (eg, vocabulary, writing style, sentiment) are not always explicitly defined.27 This can result in reduced transparency compared with TML algorithms, which typically operate on structured and well-defined datasets.29,30

Consistent with the conceptual differences between TML and NLP, the subset studies that reported AUROC showed higher mean discriminative performance for TML models (0.89 across 6 studies) than for NLP models (0.77 across 3 studies). This gap likely reflects the greater complexity of extracting a reliable signal from unstructured text: NLP models often act as “black boxes” and require substantially more context-specific training data to reach accuracy levels comparable to structured data TML approaches.31–34

The use of AI in admissions carries notable limitations, particularly the risk of perpetuating biases present in training data. Because AI models learn from historical data, which can embed human biases, they may replicate those inequities. For example, Zhao et al26 showed that biased language in recommendation letters led an NLP screening tool to amplify disparities. These findings underscore that AI algorithms are fundamentally limited by the quality of the data on which they are trained, making it imperative for institutions to use robust bias mitigation strategies. Without these safeguards, AI risks entrenching rather than addressing inequities in admissions processes. ML models offer greater transparency by quantifying each feature’s influence on the outcome, which helps identify and correct biases both in the algorithm’s decisions and in past admissions decisions.35–37

The PSCA, now used by nearly all US programs, is an ideal test bed for the AI pipelines cataloged in this review. Its fields mix structured, quantitative data—USMLE scores, publication counts—with narrative “bucket” statements and faculty remarks, yielding a multimodal dataset suited to a 2-stage model: a transparent, weighted TML score for structured metrics plus a lightweight NLP flagger that highlights mission-aligned themes such as health-equity advocacy.5 Because PSCA data can harbor demographic leakage, linguistic proxies, and test score bias, any algorithm must undergo subgroup parity audits and multisite prospective validation before deployment. Additionally, retrospective pilots on de-identified prior cycles can estimate time saved and false-negative rates without affecting live applicants.38

Studies reviewed span diverse academic and algorithmic contexts. They encompass undergraduate, graduate, medical-school, and residency programs; use both TML and NLP pipelines; interrogate decision points ranging from initial interview invitation to ultimate matriculation; vary between pilot and fully implemented settings; and, crucially, disclose AUROC metrics in only 9 of 18 cases. Therefore, this pronounced methodological heterogeneity, coupled with fragmentary performance reporting, necessitates a narrative-based, rather than statistically oriented, cross-study comparison.

Our review poses a number of limitations. The restricted number of included studies (n = 18) underscores the nascent stage of research on AI applications in admissions. Two-thirds (n = 12) of these investigations were published in 2023–2024, and only 4 predate 2020, confirming that the evidence base is both young and rapidly evolving. Sample sizes varied widely (10 to 22,258 applicants; median ≈ 2000), and most projects (11 of 18) remained in the pilot phase. Crucially, AUROC metrics were reported in only half (n = 9) of the studies, preventing us from performing a statistical analysis. Such small, recent, and heterogeneous evidence limits external validity and highlights the need for large, multi-institutional prospective trials before routine deployment.

Furthermore, the focus on healthcare and science, technology, engineering, and mathematics programs introduces a potential bias, restricting the applicability of findings to other academic disciplines. Only 3 studies reviewed (17%) focus on liberal arts or institution-wide admissions. Because these programs likely require different applicant data and custom bias mitigation strategies that have not been studied, our conclusions may not generalize accurately to these admissions contexts. Taken together, the reviewed studies exhibited a moderate risk of bias, with an average Newcastle-Ottawa Scale score of 6.65 out of 9.

Finally, we observed that many models were built on predictor sets that quietly encode structural inequities, such as demographic markers that “leak” identity, narrative phrases that stand in as gendered or racial proxies, and heavy reliance on achievement tests with well-documented socioeconomic score gaps. Therefore, an apparently stellar AUROC can still turn women, under represented minorities, or lower income applicants away from interviews or offers, warranting caution when interpreting our pooled performance estimates. Emerging “distance-traveled” metrics (eg, the UC Davis Scale, which credits applicants who overcome socioeconomic or educational disadvantages) offer 1 potential remedy, but these tools remain unvalidated in AI-driven admissions and rely on self-reported hardships, which could themselves introduce new biases. Prospective validation is needed before embracing such approaches as a solution.39,40

CONCLUSIONS

This systematic review highlights the growing interest in using AI to address challenges in academic applicant screening. TML algorithms tended to demonstrate stronger predictive performance, whereas NLP algorithms showed promise but were hindered by challenges related to transparency, data requirements, and interpretability. Inconsistent application of AI across institutions and limited real-world implementation reveal significant gaps in research and underscore the immaturity of the field. Given the increasing demands for equitable and efficient admissions processes, the field is nevertheless likely to move toward broader implementation of AI tools tailored in standardized ways to specific institutional needs. The continued use of AI in admissions will likely place a strong emphasis on mitigating bias as well as standardizing algorithm parameters across institutions, allowing for transparent, fair decision-making.
