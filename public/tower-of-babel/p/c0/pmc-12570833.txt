The activation of hippocampus microglia in the occurrence of airway hyperresponsiveness comorbidity anxiety
Authors: Haoyue Zhang, Ying Zhou, Yanbo Liu, Kaixuan Zhao, Haotian Chen, Quanle Liu, Qianyu Wang, Juan Zhi, Jin Xu, Dong Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAirway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) is often associated with mood disorders such as anxiety and depression which makes the airway symptoms more complicated and refractory. However, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. This study investigated whether this was related to the activation of microglia in the hippocampus. Female Balb/c mice were randomly assigned into an AHR model induced by ovalbumin (OVA) and an anxiety model induced by chronic restraint stress (CRS). Anxiety-like behavior was assessed by open field test, elevated plus maze and tail suspension test. Airway resistance following methacholine inhalation was measured by using the FlexiVent apparatus. Hematoxylin and eosin, periodic acid-Schiff as well as Masson trichrome were performed to evaluate inflammatory cell infiltration in the lung tissue. In addition, the status of microglia in the hippocampus was evaluated by immunofluorescence, while the activation of inflammation signaling pathway was assessed by Western blots. Compared with the Control group, mice in OVA and CRS groups exhibited higher airway resistance, spent less time in the center area of the open field and the open arms of the elevated plus maze, and showed increased immobility time in the tail suspension test. Microglia in hippocampus exhibited pronounced morphological alterations, increased expression of activation markers Iba1 and CD86, and upregulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway. Minocycline treatment significantly reduced the airway resistance and anxiety-like behavior in OVA mice, while also inhibiting activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway. This study provides evidence for the comorbidity between AHR and anxiety, potentially mediated by hippocampal microglial activation and the NLRP3/Caspase-1/IL-1β pathway. These findings enhance our understanding of the underlying mechanisms, and offer potential targets for treating AHR-related anxiety.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03565-6.Keywords: Airway hyperresponsiveness, Anxiety, Microglia, Hippocampal, NLRP3

Background

Airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) is a common clinical feature of respiratory diseases and is displayed as increased sensitivity to direct pharmacologic bronchoconstrictor stimuli, such as inhaled histamine and methacholine [1]. Patients with AHR are prone to tracheal or bronchospasm during perioperative anesthesia management, leading to hypoxemia and even life-threatening “silent lung“ [2]. Asthma, the most prevalent and representative form of AHR, affecting over 330 million people worldwide [3]. Although traditionally classified as a pulmonary disease, asthma is increasingly recognized as a systemic disorder with emotional changes, such as anxiety, depression, and sleep disorder [4]. Among these, anxiety is particularly common and clinically significant. In individuals with asthma, the pooled prevalence of anxiety symptoms and anxiety disorders was 32% and 24%, respectively. Compared to non-asthmatic controls, asthma patients demonstrated a significantly elevated risk for both anxiety symptoms and anxiety disorders, with odds ratios of 1.89 and 2.08 [5]. The European U-BIOPRED asthma cohorts further suggest that individuals with severe asthma experience higher levels of anxiety and depression [6]. A similar trend has also been observed in children with asthma who exhibit a higher prevalence of anxiety during adolescence than their peers without asthma [7]. The comorbidity of asthma and anxiety disorders reduces quality of life, complicates treatment management, and increases the healthcare burden [4]. However, the underlying neural mechanisms linking asthma and anxiety remain poorly understood.

Multiple brain regions are implicated in the regulation of asthma-anxiety comorbidity. For instance, the hypothalamus integrates diverse peripheral and central inputs to maintain physiological homeostasis by coordinating behavioral responses, autonomic nervous system(ANS) activity, and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal(HPA) axis function [8–10]. The nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) also plays a critical role. Ablation or inactivation of dopamine β-hydroxylase (Dbh) -expressing neurons in the NTS significantly reduces airway hyperreactivity [11]. In addition, NTS activity may alleviate depressive symptoms by promoting serotonin (5-HT) release in the dorsal raphe nucleus (DRN) [12]. Moreover, inhibition of GABAergic neurons in the central amygdala (CeA) has been shown to suppress cytokine storms and improve survival in murine models of lethal pneumonia [13]. While in this study, we focused specifically on the hippocampus, a key brain region essential for learning, memory, and cognitive function [14], which may play a long-term and critical role in the progression and persistence of chronic diseases. Hippocampus is structurally and functionally differentiated between the dorsal and ventral portions. The ventral hippocampus is closely related to emotion, and its injury particularly impacts anxiety-related functions, whereas the dorsal hippocampus plays a preferred role in learning and spatial memory. The hippocampus serves as a major target of stress mediators and is closely related to anxiety modulation [15]. Notably, a large-scale neuroimaging study found that hippocampal volume was significantly reduced in adults with asthma compared to those without, suggesting a potential link between asthma-related inflammation or hypoxia and hippocampal structural alterations [16].

Microglia are considered as the resident macrophages of the central nervous system (CNS), originated from mesoderm and migrating to the CNS during its development [17]. Numerous studies have demonstrated a pivotal involvement of microglia in neuropsychiatric disorders, including negative affection, social deficit, compulsive behavior, fear memory, pain and other symptoms associated with major depression disorder, anxiety disorder, autism spectrum disorder and schizophrenia [18]. Therefore, these cells are the focus of studies on neuroinflammation. Microglia are rapidly activated in response to various stimuli, including infectious and pathological stimuli. Activated microglia secrete many proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β. The NLRP3 inflammasome is the most well-defined inflammasome and is a multiprotein complex. The activation of inflammasomes leads to the maturation and secretion of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and IL-18. These inflammatory factors can amplify the inflammatory cascade response of the body and induce pyroptosis, causing tissue damage [19].

Materials and methods

Animals

6-week-old specific pathogen-free female BALB/c mice (weight, 18–22 g) purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. Mice were housed under standard laboratory conditions at 25 °C with 50 ± 5% humidity and 12-h light/dark cycles in the experimental animal center of the Plastic Surgery Hospital (Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College; Beijing, China) for 1 week prior to the start of the experiments The animals were provided with sterilized food and water ad libitum. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Plastic Surgery Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (NO.2025030513521995).

Experimental protocols

Mice were randomly assigned into three groups and were submitted to an AHR model induced by ovalbumin (OVA) and an anxiety model induced by chronic restraint stress (CRS). Mice in the CRS group were subjected to restraint stress by being placed in well-ventilated 50-ml polypropylene conical tubes for 4–5 h per day for 25 consecutive days. After each day of restraint stress, mice were returned to their home environment, where they were housed in normal plastic cages with free access to food and water. Mice in the OVA group were sensitized on days 1, 7 and 14 with an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 100 µg ovalbumin (OVA; Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA), 10 mg aluminum hydroxide (Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA) in saline (200 µl). The Control group was injected with 200 µl saline. From day 21 to 25, the Control group was intranasally administered 30 µl saline once a day, and each OVA group received a daily intranasal administration of 200 µg OVA in 30 µl saline. After that, all groups of mice underwent behavioral evaluation and airway resistance measurement. The mice were euthanized using an i.p. administered overdose of 2% pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg); bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), lung and brain tissue were then collected.

Assessment of airway resistance

Airway resistance was detected 24 h after the last challenge. The mice were anesthetized with 2% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg) and the airway resistance was measured using FlexiVent (SCIREQ). Aerosolized methacholine (Mch) at different concentrations (0, 6, 12, 24 and 48 mg/ml) was continuously nebulized through a catheter in each animal for 6 min. The airway resistance was collected every minute and the final result of each concentration for each animal was presented as the average of 6 min. The airway resistance is presented as respiratory resistance (Rrs) and tissue damping resistance (G).

Analysis of BALF

The mice were anaesthetized with 2% pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg) 24 h after the last challenge. After inserting a catheter into the trachea, the lungs were flushed with 0.8 ml cold PBS three times, and 85–95% of the lavage volume was collected through the catheter. BALF was centrifuged at 1000 × rpm at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and analyzed by ELISA. Commercial ELISA kits (IL-4, cat. no. EK0405; IL-5, cat. no. EK0408; and IL-13, cat. no. EK0425; all from Boster Biological Technology) were used to detect the levels of IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13 in BALF according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The optical density was spectrophotometrically measured at 450 nm using a multimode plate reader (PerkinElmer, Inc.).

Histopathology analysis of lungs

Mice were performed with thoracotomy under anesthetized with pentobarbital (10 mg/kg, i.p.) and lung tissues were obtained after transcardial perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated with ethanol and then embedded in paraffin. Five-millimeter sections were cut from the paraffin blocks and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), periodic acid Schiff (PAS), and Masson trichrome staining. Images were observed and captured with Nikon SMZ1500 inverted microscope (Nikon).

According to the previous studies [20, 21], the severity of lung inflammation was measured by the inflammation score based on a 5-point scoring system as follows: 0 = no inflammatory cells were observed, 1 = few inflammatory cells were observed, 2 = bronchi or vessels were surrounded by 1 layer of inflammatory cells, 3 = bronchi or vessels were surrounded by 2–4 layer of inflammatory cells, and 4 = bronchi or vessels were surrounded by more than 4 layers of inflammatory cells. The quantification of goblet cell hyperplasia in the bronchi and bronchioles was represented with a 5-point scoring system: 0 ≤ 0.5% PAS-positive cells, 1: <25%, 2: 25–50%, 3: 50–75%, and 4: >75%. The collagen deposition in the lungs was quantified by the percentage of the area occupied by collagen (blue) of the total area examined.

Behavioral assessments

All behavioral tests were performed during the light cycle in a dedicated sound-proof behavioral facility; assessments were performed by experimenter blind to treatment. Mice were brought to the testing room 30 min before the start of each behavioral test and remained in the same room throughout the test. After each behavioral test, the instruments were cleaned with 75% ethanol. All the video of the animal’s track were recorded by Visutrack software (version 3.0, Xinruan Information Technology Co., Ltd.).

Open field test (OFT)

The OFT was used to evaluate locomotor activity of mice. Mice were placed in the center of a polystyrene enclosure (40 cm × 40 cm × 35 cm) and allowed to move freely for 5 min and were videotaped individually. The “center area” was defined as the 20 cm × 20 cm rectangular area centered on the midpoint of the field. The total distance traversed as a measure of locomotor activity while the distance and time spent in the center area as an (inverse) measure of anxiety-related behavioral measures.

Elevated plus maze test (EPM)

The EPM consisted of two risk-laden arms (each with a 30 cm × 5 cm open area without sidewalls) and two “safe” arms (each with a 30 cm × 5 cm area enclosed by 20 cm high walls on the sides and the end), connected with a central platform (5 cm × 5 cm) elevated 40 cm above the floor. Mice were placed in the central platform such that they faced an open arm and were allowed to explore freely for 5 min. Time and frequency entered in the open arms was extracted as a measure of the anxiety levels of mice. The total number entries made into the 4 arms was extracted as a measure of the animal’s locomotor activity.

Tail suspension test (TST)

Mice were suspended by the tail, which was taped and secured to a horizontal bar 30 cm above the floor, which ensured that the mice could not make any other contact or climb during the assay. Mice were habituated for the first 1 min, and the time spent immobile during the subsequent 5-minute test period was counted by an observer blinded to animal treatment.

Nissl staining

Mice were performed with thoracotomy under anesthetized with pentobarbital (10 mg/kg, i.p.) and brain tissues were obtained after transcardial perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated with ethanol and then embedded in paraffin. Five-millimeter sections were cut from the paraffin blocks and were used for Nissl staining. The damage in the hippocampal region was captured with Nikon SMZ1500 inverted microscope (Nikon).

Minocycline treatment

Mice were randomly assigned into three groups: Sham, OVA and Mino (minocycline, MedChemExpress). Mice in the OVA and Mino group were sensitized on days 1, 7 and 14 with an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 100 µg ovalbumin (OVA; Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA), 10 mg aluminum hydroxide (Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA) in saline (200 µl). The Sham group was injected with 200 µl saline. From day 21 to 24, mice in the Mino group received daily intraperitoneal injections of minocycline (10 mg/kg), followed 60 min later by intranasal administration of 200 μg OVA in 30 μl saline. The OVA group received the same intranasal OVA challenge once daily, whereas the Control group was given 30 μl saline intranasally.

Immunofluorescence

For the immunofluorescence staining experiment, the brains were fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C, then immersed in 30% sucrose for 3 days, and embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT). 20 μm-thick slides were cut from the OCT-embedded tissues. After permeabilization and blocking were done, brain sections were incubated with anti-iba1 primary antibody (1: 300, CST, America) overnight at 4 °C. The tissue slices were washed with PBS and then incubated with DyLight CY3-labeled IgG secondary antibody (1: 300, BOSTER, China) and DAPI for visualization. The number and morphological changes of microglia in the hippocampus CA1 region were captured by Nikon SMZ1500 inverted microscope (Nikon).

Western blot

The hippocampal tissues from each group were weighed and homogenized with RIPA buffer (Applygen, China) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, China). Protein concentration was determined by the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China), and then, samples were loaded into SDS-PAGE gels. After electrophoresis at 100 V for 90 min, the gels were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, US) for wet electric transfer at 250 mA for 70 min. After being blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h to block the nonspecific sites, the PVDF membranes were then incubated in primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibodies used for western blot included anti-NLRP3 (1 : 800, ET1610-93, HUABIO), anti-Caspase 1 (1 : 800, ET1608-69, HUABIO), anti-IL-1β(1 :800, #HA601002, HUABIO), anti-β-Tubulin (1 : 7000,10094-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-Iba1(1:2000, 17198 T, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-CD86(1:2000, BM4121, Boster), and anti-β-actin (1 : 7000, 20536-1-AP, Proteintech). Anti-mouse or anti-rabbit horseradish peroxidase-conjugated-IgG secondary antibody (Proteintech, China) was used to detect the binding of primary antibodies and imaged with BeyoECL Plus detection reagent (cat. no. P0018S; Beyotime Biotechnology Institute of Biotechnology) using the Chemi-Doc Imaging System (cat. no. 12003153; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.). The semi-quantification of the bands was performed using ImageJ software (version 1.8.0) according to a publicly available protocol.

Statistics and reproducibility

All summarized data are expressed as means ± standard error of mean (SEM). The statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 for normal distribution data, statistical significance between groups was assessed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test. Ordinal data or those with heteroscedasticity was analyzed using Kruskal-Wallis followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Both OVA and CRS group mice showed airway inflammation and airway remodeling

Since inflammatory cell recruitment, mucus overproduction and airway remodeling are the main characteristics of AHR, the subsequent experiments aimed to determine whether AHR as well as CRS could lead to these symptoms. Inflammatory cell infiltration was assessed using H&E staining. The production of mucus was assessed using PAS staining. Airway smooth muscle hyperplasia was assessed using Masson staining (Fig. 2A). Compared with the Control group, lung tissue from mice in the OVA and CRS groups exhibited a significantly higher number of inflammatory cells. The PAS-positive area in the lung sections of these two groups were significantly increased than that in the controls. Moreover, mice in OVA and CRS group exhibited prominent and increased peribronchial collagen deposition than those in the vehicle-treated group, as shown by Masson’s trichrome staining. (Fig. 2B).

B Inflammatory cell infiltration and mucus production was based on the layers of cells around the airways and the percentage of PAS-positive cells, respectively, Kruskal-Wallis test. Collagen area was based on the percentage of collagen fiber content in lung sections, one-way ANOVA. Data were presented as mean ± SEM (n = 3). *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001

Both OVA and CRS group mice showed anxiety-like behavior

The OFT was performed to assess spontaneous locomotor activity, a reflection of novel environment exploration, as measured by the ambulatory distance and the time spent in the center zone. Reduced time spent in the center is usually interpreted as anxiety-like behavior. Increased immobility time in the TST is commonly considered to reflect behavioral despair, indicating a loss of hope in escaping Compared with Control group, OVA and CRS mice showed little interest in entering the center field (Fig. 3A-B), spent less time in the open arms of EPM than mice in the Control group (Fig. 3C-D), and showed increased immobility time in the TST (Fig. 3E). These behavioral tests indicated that both OVA and CRS group mice showed anxiety-like behavior, further suggesting that OVA-induced AHR may share the same neurocircuit with the stress response pathway observed in CRS models.

E statistical analysis of TST, one-way ANOVA. Data were presented as mean ± SEM (n = 6). *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001

Hippocampal neuroinflammatory responses in OVA and CRS mouse models

The hippocampal formation plays a critical role in memory formation and spatial cognition. Anxious mice often exhibit morphological damage in the hippocampus and activation of microglia. In the present study, histopathological changes were examined via H&E and Nissl staining. Compared with the Control group, mice in both the OVA and CRS groups exhibited loosely arranged cells, neuronal loss and pyknotic nuclei in the CA1 region of the hippocampus (Fig. 4A). Microglia are the resident immune cells of the central nervous system, play a crucial role in maintaining brain homeostasis. Compared with Control group, OVA and CRS mice showed enhanced morphological changes, increased expression of Iba1 and CD86 which are the activation marker of microglia. This was further supported by increased expression of NLRP3, Caspase-1, and IL-1β in the hippocampal tissue, indicating the involvement of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway (Fig. 4C). These results imply that neuroinflammation, potentially driven by peripheral immune activation, may be a key mechanism underlying the observed behavioral changes. Although both models induced microglial activation and neuronal injury, the CRS group exhibited a more pronounced neuroinflammatory response than the OVA group. The difference in severity also provides valuable mechanistic insight. The stronger response in the CRS model may reflect the direct and widespread influence of chronic psychological stress on neuroimmune circuits, in contrast to the relatively indirect effects mediated by peripheral allergic inflammation.

Minocycline treatment can relieve AHR and anxiety-like behavior

Minocycline, an inhibitor of microglial activation, was injected intraperitoneally to assess its effect on AHR (Fig. 5A). Compared with the Sham group, both OVA and minocycline groups exhibited attenuated AHR and anxiety-like behaviors (Fig. 5B, C). This therapeutic effect was accompanied by suppression of NLRP3/Caspase-1/IL-1β signaling in the hippocampus (Fig. 5E), further supporting the role of microglia-mediated neuroinflammation in the comorbidity between respiratory and affective symptoms. These findings provide new insights into the neuroimmune crosstalk in allergic airway diseases and highlight potential therapeutic targets for addressing their associated neuropsychiatric comorbidities.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the comorbidity between airway hyperresponsiveness and anxiety, focusing on the potential role of hippocampal microglial activation in mediating this association. Our findings demonstrate that both OVA-induced AHR and CRS-induced anxiety result in significant alterations in behavior and immune responses. These results suggest that the co-occurrence of AHR and anxiety may involve complex interactions between immune and neuroinflammatory processes.

First, our results showed that both OVA and CRS models exhibited significant increases in airway resistance, along with increased inflammation, mucus production, and airway remodeling. Interestingly, mice subjected to either OVA sensitization or CRS exposure also exhibited anxiety-like behaviors, as shown by their reduced exploration in the open field and elevated plus maze tests. These behavioral changes suggest a potential link between peripheral inflammation and central nervous system dysfunction, highlighting the systemic impact of both allergic and stress-related insults. Asthma is the most prevalent and representative form of AHR. Therefore, we used the OVA-induced asthma model in this study to represent AHR. Traditional models such as OVA for AHR (assessed via lung function, histology, etc.) and CRS with behavioral assays for anxiety are well established [22–25]. Now our result may raise significant questions in the field regarding whether these models are suitable for studying individual diseases. However, this complexity reflects the clinical reality where diseases rarely follow a singular pattern but result from multiple biological, psychological, and environmental interactions. Therefore, we believe these models still have certain value for research. In order to minimize the impact of comorbidities on the study of individual diseases, we think it might be possible to shorten the CRS protocol moderately. This adjustment may still effectively induce anxiety-like behavior while exerting a more limited effect on AHR.

Previous studies have proposed that psychological stress can exacerbate airway inflammation, while chronic airway diseases like asthma are often accompanied by anxiety and depression [5, 26]. Our findings align with these reports, reinforcing the notion of a bidirectional relationship between the immune and nervous systems. Specifically, microglia-mediated neuroinflammation has been implicated in various neuropsychiatric disorders [27]. Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in microglia has been shown to contribute to anxiety and depressive-like behaviors in preclinical models [28]. Consistent with these findings, we observed hippocampal microglial activation and NLRP3 upregulation in both OVA and CRS mice, supporting the hypothesis that peripheral inflammation may trigger central immune responses, thereby linking airway disease with emotional dysregulation. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to verify the airway inflammation and remodeling in a mouse model of anxiety. Previous studies have primarily focused on the effects of asthma on mental health, in contrast to the effects of anxiety on airway structure and function in a non-asthmatic model. Furthermore, our findings provide novel evidence that neuroinflammation may serve as a shared mechanism linking AHR and anxiety across different etiological models. This highlights microglial signaling as a potential therapeutic target for the comorbidity, moving beyond the traditional approach of treating each condition in isolation.

Currently, the clinical management of these comorbid patients remains fragmented: bronchodilators and corticosteroids are prescribed for asthma, while selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) or cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) are used to treat anxiety. This treatment targeting specific symptoms ignores the underlying common mechanisms, which may result in poor clinical outcomes. Furthermore, spontaneous reports have suggested that certain asthma medications may be associated with an increased risk of neuropsychiatric adverse events [29]. Our findings directly address this unmet clinical need. For instance, reutilized or newly developed microglial cell regulators, such as minocycline, may enhance the therapeutic effect while reducing the drug burden on patients. Therefore, the significance of our work lies in promoting a more comprehensive and holistic treatment approach to address this common comorbidity issue.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Minocycline is known to influence various cellular targets, including astrocytes and peripheral immune cells, and can modulate multiple signaling pathways beyond the NLRP3 inflammasome. We acknowledge the absence of a dedicated negative control group (OVA–/Mino–), which would have helped rule out potential confounding effects of minocycline alone. Due to funding constraints, this group was not included in the current study; however, we intend to incorporate it in future experiments to improve the interpretability and rigor of the findings. While our results support the involvement of neuroinflammation in the observed comorbidity, future studies utilizing more specific microglia-targeted interventions, such as conditional knockout models or selective pharmacological inhibitors, are necessary to clarify the precise role of microglia in mediating these effects. In addition, our study was conducted exclusively in female mice, which limits the generalizability of the findings. A growing body of evidence highlights sex-based differences in immune system function, stress reactivity, and susceptibility to both respiratory and psychiatric disorders.

Future research would aim to identify the specific molecules from the lungs that activate microglia through the lung-brain axis, leading to neuroinflammation. Clarifying these mechanisms would provide critical insights into the crosstalk between peripheral airway pathology and central nervous system dysfunction. This approach may improve both airway hyperresponsiveness and anxiety-like behaviors, providing a novel and localized treatment for respiratory diseases with neuropsychiatric comorbidities.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study revealed that there are comorbidities between AHR and anxiety, that may be mediated by activates microglia in the hippocampus and the NLRP3/Caspase-1/IL-1β pathway, which provides a novel therapeutic strategy for airway hyperresponsivity.
