Serological detection of acute and chronic toxoplasmosis in infertile women in the north of Iran
Authors: Mahbobeh Montazeri, Fariya Rostamkalai, Mahdi Fakhar, Salomeh Peyvandi, Marzieh Zamaniyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundToxoplasma gondii (T. gondii) infection is a common parasitic disease worldwide and has been suggested as a potential factor affecting female fertility. This study aimed to determine the seroprevalence of anti-T. gondii immunoglobulin G (IgG) and immunoglobulin M(IgM) antibodies among infertile women attending an in vitro fertilization (IVF) clinic in Mazandaran province in northern Iran and to investigate associations with demographic and clinical characteristics.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted on 130 infertile women referred to the IVF department at Imam Khomeini Hospital, Sari, from 2019 to 2020. Serum samples were collected and analyzed for anti-T. gondii IgG and IgM antibodies using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Demographic and clinical data were recorded. Statistical analysis was conducted using chi-square tests and binary logistic regression to compute adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), aiming to identify potential factors linked to T. gondii IgG and IgM seropositivity. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsAnti-T. gondii IgG antibodies were detected in 52.3% (68/130) of participants, indicating past exposure, while IgM antibodies were found in 10% (13/130). No significant associations were observed between T. gondii seropositivity and variables such as age, residence, type of infertility, history of abortion, polycystic ovary syndrome, fibroma, or previous surgeries (P> 0.05).ConclusionsThe study revealed a high seroprevalence of chronic T. gondii infection among infertile women infertile women at an IVF clinic in Mazandaran province, while only 10% showed IgM antibodies. No significant links were found between T. gondii seropositivity and various demographic or clinical factors, suggesting that the impact of T. gondii on female infertility may be less significant than previously thought. These findings suggest that while exposure to T. gondii is common in this population, it may not be a direct contributor to infertility in this context. Further research with larger sample sizes is warranted to clarify potential implications.Keywords: Toxoplasma gondii, Acute toxoplasmosis, Chronic toxoplasmosis, Infertile women, Serology, Iran

Background

Toxoplasmosis is a disease caused by the obligate intracellular protozoan Toxoplasma gondii and is prevalent worldwide, with human seroprevalence rates ranging from below 10% to over 90% [1, 2]. Felids—including both domestic and wild cats—are crucial in the parasite’s life cycle as definitive hosts, and following a primary infection, they can excrete large quantities of infectious oocysts into the environment [3]. Human infection typically occurs either through ingestion of T. gondii tissue cysts present in undercooked meat or by consuming infectious oocytes contaminating food or water due to contact with infected cat feces [4]. Also, congenital toxoplasmosis, acquired via placental transmission during pregnancy, can result in fetal or neonatal death, congenital abnormalities, or spontaneous abortion [5, 6]. Severe neonatal symptoms include hydrocephalus, intracranial calcifications, and chorioretinitis. Other common signs are hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice, and seizures. Some infants may appear asymptomatic at birth but develop issues such as hearing loss or learning disabilities later in childhood [7, 8].

Serological screening is a valid procedure for the diagnosis of T. gondii infection. Serological tests for T. gondii detect IgM (potential recent infection) and IgG (past exposure), but IgM tests are prone to false positives [9, 10]. To improve accuracy, IgG avidity testing is used: low avidity with positive IgM suggests recent infection, while high avidity rules out infection within the past 3–4 months. This combined approach enhances diagnostic reliability, reducing unnecessary interventions and costs [10, 11].

Chronic T. gondii infection, beyond its established role in congenital disease, has been increasingly investigated as a potential factor in reproductive health disorders, including female infertility [12–14]. Infertility is a medical issue affecting the reproductive systems of males and females, as described by the World Health Organization (WHO). It is characterized by the inability to achieve pregnancy after a year of regular, unprotected sexual intercourse [15]. Infertility can be classified into two types: primary and secondary. Primary infertility refers to individuals who have never been able to conceive, while secondary infertility applies to those who have experienced at least one previous pregnancy [15].

Several seroepidemiological studies indicate a higher prevalence of chronic T. gondii infection (as evidenced by positive IgG antibodies) in infertile women compared to fertile controls, suggesting a potential association [12–14]. However, this link remains controversial, as other studies from different geographical regions have reported no significant relationship [16]. The inconsistency among these findings points to a gap in current knowledge, implying that factors such as climate, dietary habits, and parasite strain diversity may influence the association. Consequently, the specific dynamics and mechanisms underlying the relationship between chronic T. gondii infection and female fertility are still unclear, underscoring the need for further research to clarify its implications.

Northern Iran (Mazandaran province), with its distinct humid subtropical climate and specific cultural practices, presents a unique epidemiological setting for T. gondii, highlighting the relevance of this investigation for the local population [17, 18]. Therefore, this study aimed to establish an updated seroprevalence baseline for T. gondii infection among infertile women seeking assisted reproduction at an in vitro fertilization (IVF) center in northern Iran. Furthermore, given our prior observation of a significant association between toxoplasmosis and primary infertility [12], this investigation aims to identify and analyze the demographic and clinical risk factors associated with T. gondii seropositivity in this vulnerable population. These factors include age, residence, type of infertility, history of abortion, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), uterine fibroids, prior surgeries, contraceptive use, history of IVF and intrauterine insemination (IUI), and underlying conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, and thyroid disorders. The findings are intended to inform clinical screening protocols and risk assessment strategies in reproductive care.

Methods

Ethics

Approval was obtained from the Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences (Ethic No. IR.MAZUMS.REC.1399.7037), ensuring that all procedures complied with relevant ethical standards.

Study design and population

This descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted at the IVF department of Imam Khomeini Hospital in Sari, Mazandaran Province, northern Iran, between 2019 and 2020. The study included 130 infertile women undergoing infertility treatment.

Inclusion criteria

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were women of reproductive age (18–45 years) seeking infertility treatment at the hospital. Those with a confirmed diagnosis of infertility, whether primary or secondary, were recruited into the study. Participants also needed to provide written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Exclusion criteria

Women were excluded from the study if they were currently pregnant, had undergone recent surgery affecting reproductive health, or had a known active infection that could influence immune response. Additionally, individuals with incomplete medical histories or those who did not provide consent were not included in the study.

Data collection

A comprehensive questionnaire was administered to gather demographic characteristics (e.g., age and place of residence), duration of infertility, prior history of abortion, and relevant medical history (e.g., PCOS, surgical history, presence of uterine fibroids, contraceptive use, and previous fertility treatments such as IVF or IUI). Participants’ underlying medical conditions, including diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, and thyroid disorders, were also recorded. Blood samples were collected under sterile conditions, and serum was isolated, stored at − 20 °C, and subsequently transported to the laboratory at Imam Khomeini Hospital for further examination.

ELISA protocol

Serum levels of anti-T. gondii IgM and IgG antibodies were measured using a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (PishtazTeb, Tehran, Iran) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The optical density (OD) of the samples was recorded at a wavelength of 450 nm using a plate reader. Results were classified based on international units (IU)/ml: values < 0.9 IU/ml were considered negative, between 0.9 and 1.1 IU/ml as borderline, and > 1.1 IU/ml as positive. All borderline results were re-evaluated two weeks later using the same kit to ensure accuracy. Positive and negative controls were included throughout the testing process to verify assay reliability. Sensitivity and specificity of the IgG test were confirmed at 100%, and the IgM test exhibited a sensitivity of 100% and a specificity of 99%.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software version 23.0. The chi-square test was employed to evaluate relationships between qualitative variables, and a P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Additionally, a binary logistic regression analysis was performed to calculate adjusted Odds Ratios (ORs) and 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) to identify potential factors associated with T. gondii IgG and IgM seropositivity.

This study investigated T. gondii infection in 130 infertile women (mean age 34.4 ± 6.1 years) in northern Iran (Table 1). The overall seroprevalence was 52.3% for anti-T. gondii IgG antibodies (Table 2), indicating chronic infection, and 10% for IgM antibodies (Table 3), suggesting acute infection.

Regarding clinical characteristics, the majority of participants (73.1%) had secondary infertility. As illustrated in Table 1, uterine fibroids were present in 45.4%, and 21.5% had a history of abortion. Common fertility treatments included IVF (25.4%) and IUI (21.5%).

Statistical analyses revealed no significant associations between T. gondii seropositivity (IgG or IgM) and any of the examined demographic or clinical variables. Factors such as age, residence, type or duration of infertility, history of abortion, PCOS, and uterine fibroids all yielded non-significant P-values (P > 0.05) (see Tables 2 and 3). Logistic regression confirmed these findings, with ORs close to 1.0, indicating that none of the studied factors were significant predictors of T. gondii infection (P-values > 0.05) in this cohort (Table 4).

Discussion

Chronic infection with T. gondii has emerged as a potential factor influencing infertility and pregnancy loss on a global scale [5]. Increasing evidence indicates that asymptomatic toxoplasmosis might negatively affect female reproductive health through mechanisms such as endometrial inflammation, disruptions in ovarian function, and hormonal imbalances, which could subsequently lead to subfertility or infertility [19, 20]. However, the relationship between T. gondii infection and infertility remains a topic of considerable debate, with inconsistencies reported across various studies.

In our cohort study focused on infertile women in northern Iran, we observed a notably high seroprevalence of T. gondii, with 52.3% of participants testing positive for anti-T. gondii IgG antibodies and 10% for IgM antibodies. This finding raises important questions about the effects of chronic T. gondii infection on reproductive health, especially when compared to previous research. For example, Elhan et al. [16] reported a lower seropositivity rate of 29.1% among women undergoing IVF and found no statistically significant association between T. gondii infection and infertility (P = 0.851). On the other hand, Zamaniyan et al. [12] recorded a higher seroprevalence of 65.77% in a larger group of infertile women, highlighting a significant correlation with demographic factors such as age and educational background (P < 0.05). However, specific infertility factors did not exhibit significant associations in their study (P = 0.2). The discrepancies in seroprevalence observed across different studies may be reflective of diverse demographic and geographic factors influencing T. gondii infection rates. Factors such as regional climate, dietary practices, and cultural habits—especially in areas like Mazandaran province with its humid subtropical climate—could elevate exposure to the parasite [17, 18]. Furthermore, the prevalence of secondary infertility in our cohort (73.1%) resonates with findings from Malik et al., which indicated high rates of secondary infertility among women with chronic infections [21].

Interestingly, our analysis did not reveal significant correlations between T. gondii seropositivity and demographic variables such as age, residence, type or duration of infertility, or past medical history. This finding suggests that our population may exhibit distinct dynamics compared to previous studies that identified associations between seropositivity and factors like education and age. These inconsistencies underscore the importance of conducting larger, multicentric research initiatives to unravel the multifaceted relationship between T. gondii infection and infertility.

A particularly noteworthy finding from our study is the absence of significant links between T. gondii seropositivity and specific infertility-related factors, including primary versus secondary infertility, abortion history, uterine fibroids, or the presence of PCOS. This lack of correlation diverges from several earlier reports that have suggested chronic toxoplasmosis is associated with reproductive challenges. Variability in sample sizes, population characteristics, and diagnostic methodologies may contribute to these inconsistencies, emphasizing a need for further research to better understand the potential causal link between T. gondii and infertility.

The observed 10% rate of IgM seropositivity carries significant implications. While the presence of IgM antibodies may indicate a recent infection, it is important to recognize that these antibodies can persist for extended periods, which might lead to false-positive results [10, 11]. The absence of IgG avidity testing within our study further limits our ability to interpret the IgM findings accurately. Future research endeavors should incorporate avidity assays to enhance diagnostic precision, as high avidity results can effectively rule out recent primary infections. Moreover, recent findings by Ren et al. point to the prevalence of TORCH (T. gondii, Rubella virus, Cytomegalovirus, Herpes simplex virus) infections, specifically toxoplasmosis, among infertile women, emphasizing the need for routine screening to mitigate fetal transmission risks [22]. However, the varying seroprevalence and associations with infertility across different geographical regions highlight the necessity of localized screening protocols. Although Iran reports high exposure rates to T. gondii, this prevalence does not always correlate with specific infertility causes, as evidenced by contrasting results from China regarding the implications of TORCH infections.

Our investigation also uncovered discrepancies between our anticipated findings and the actual results concerning the relationship between T. gondii seropositivity and infertility factors. The lack of significant correlations suggests that T. gondii may serve as an independent risk factor for infertility. This inconsistency could stem from variations in study designs, sample sizes, and the characteristics of the populations examined, indicating a need for a more broadened understanding of chronic infections and their roles in infertility.

The strengths of our study include examining a unique cohort of infertile women in a high-prevalence area, providing crucial epidemiological insights. However, there are notable limitations: the absence of a control group hinders contextualizing seroprevalence rates, and our cross-sectional design limits causal inferences about the relationship between T. gondii infection and infertility outcomes. Furthermore, our sample size may affect the statistical power of our findings, and the single-center nature could restrict the generalizability of the results.

In conclusion, this study reveals a notable seroprevalence of anti-T. gondii IgG antibodies among infertile women attending an IVF clinic in Mazandaran province, with a prevalence of 52.3%. However, IgM antibodies were present in a limited proportion of the study population (10%). Importantly, no significant associations were identified between T. gondii seropositivity and various demographic or clinical factors, suggesting that the role of T. gondii in female infertility may not be as pronounced as previously hypothesized. These findings highlight the need for further research to explore the implications of T. gondii infection in infertility, particularly regarding the interpretation of IgM seropositivity, which requires additional avidity testing for a more accurate clinical assessment.
