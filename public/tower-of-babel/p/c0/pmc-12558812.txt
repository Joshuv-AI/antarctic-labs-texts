Budd–Chiari Syndrome in the Background of Chronic Kidney Disease: A Hypercoagulability‐Driven Association or Coincidence? A Case Report
Authors: Anish Paudyal, Sanjay Dhungana, Sachin Bhatta, Sphurna Karki, Sapana Koirala, Parbatraj Regmi, Sagar Mani Regmi, Subhash Paudel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBudd–Chiari syndrome (BCS) is a rare condition characterized by hepatic venous outflow obstruction and is often associated with thrombosis or fibrous membranes (webs). To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of BCS occurring secondary to chronic kidney disease (CKD). This case emphasizes the complex reno‐vascular‐hepatic interplay and expands current literature. A 48‐year‐old female with stage V CKD on hemodialysis presented with signs of portal hypertension. Imaging revealed an IVC web causing BCS. Procoagulant workup showed elevated fibrinogen, PAI‐1, and homocysteine, suggesting CKD‐induced thrombosis as a possible trigger. She was managed with anticoagulation (enoxaparin, warfarin), diuretics, and beta‐blockers. Her symptoms stabilized, explaining BCS as a hypercoagulability‐driven complication rather than a coincidence. CKD creates a prothrombotic state through impaired fibrinolysis, endothelial and platelet dysfunction, and elevated procoagulants, potentially contributing to BCS. Though inherited or acquired thrombophilias are common causes of BCS, CKD‐related hypercoagulability may also play a significant role. Diagnosis relies on Doppler ultrasound and confirmatory CT or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Management ranges from anticoagulation to endovascular or surgical interventions. This case illustrates a rare overlap, where CKD may have triggered IVC web formation causing venous obstruction, emphasizing the need to consider thrombosis risk in CKD patients with unexplained signs of liver dysfunction to prevent cirrhosis. BCS should be suspected in patients on chronic hemodialysis presenting with unexplained ascites, hepatosplenomegaly, and abdominal pain. Timely imaging, hypercoagulability assessment, and individualized therapy are essential for optimal outcomes in this rare but critical complication of CKD.Keywords: Budd–Chiari syndrome, case report, chronic kidney disease, coexistence, inferior vena‐cava web

Introduction

Budd–Chiari syndrome (BCS), a rare disorder characterized by hepatic venous outflow obstruction (HVOO), manifests as portal hypertension and liver dysfunction due to increased sinusoidal pressure [1]. While primary BCS arises from endoluminal thrombosis or a fibrous membrane (web) in the hepatic veins or inferior vena cava (IVC)—often linked to inherited or acquired hypercoagulable states—secondary BCS results from extrinsic compression by malignancies, cysts, or infections [2, 3]. Recent evidence challenges the congenital origin of IVC webs, proposing instead that they represent post‐thrombotic sequelae, where organized thrombi evolve into fibrous obstructions [4]. This shows the role of prothrombotic conditions in BCS pathogenesis, particularly in regions like Asia and South Africa, where IVC webs are prevalent [5].

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a well‐established risk factor for thrombotic complications due to impaired fibrinolysis and elevated procoagulant markers like fibrinogen, tissue factor (TF), and plasminogen activator inhibitor‐1 (PAI‐1) [6]. Despite these mechanisms, the association between CKD and BCS remains poorly documented, with limited insights into how CKD‐associated hypercoagulability contributes to HVOO.

Here, we report the first documented case of chronic BCS secondary to an IVC web in a patient with stage V CKD, illustrating the potential interplay between CKD‐induced hypercoagulability and BCS development. This case underscores the diagnostic complexity of BCS in CKD. By integrating imaging findings, clinical data, and mechanistic insights into CKD‐related prothrombotic pathways, this report advances our understanding of the CKD‐BCS interplay, highlighting hypercoagulability as a critical, underrecognized driver in the pathogenesis of hepatic venous obstruction. We also share our experience managing this condition and provide a literature review of this correlation. This case is being reported due to its rarity and educational value. This case report has been reported in line with Case Report (CARE) 2013 Criteria and is compliant with TITAN guidelines 2025 [7].

Case History/Examination

A 48‐year‐old South Asian female with a 14‐year history of CKD, currently on maintenance hemodialysis via an arteriovenous fistula for the past 6 years, and known hypertension for 10 years managed on medications, presented to the Internal Medicine outpatient department with complaints of abdominal pain, abdominal distension, and bilateral lower limb swelling for the past month, along with yellowish discoloration of the eyes and mucous membranes for the past week. The abdominal pain was localized to the epigastric and right hypochondriac regions, gradually worsening over the past few weeks without any specific aggravating or relieving factors. It was not related to posture or food intake. She reported progressive abdominal distension and limb swelling.

There was no associated history of fever, vomiting, hematemesis, shortness of breath, chest pain, palpitations, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, night sweats, hemoptysis, pruritus, dark‐colored urine, clay‐colored stool, altered sensorium, burning micturition, hematuria, early satiety, dysphagia, weight loss, or recent drug intake. She also denied the use of complementary or alternative medicine, a history of joint pain, skin rashes, blood transfusions, high‐risk behavior, or oral contraceptive use. She is a non‐smoker and non‐alcoholic, with an unremarkable menstrual and family history.

On examination, the patient was afebrile with a blood pressure of 100/70 mmHg, a pulse rate of 108 beats per minute, and a respiratory rate of 24 breaths per minute. Icterus and bilateral pedal edema were present. Abdominal examination revealed engorged abdominal veins, flank fullness, everted umbilicus, and mild tenderness in the epigastric region. The liver was palpable, nodular, and extended 3 cm below the right costal margin. Mild splenomegaly was also noted.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

Hematologic investigation revealed thrombocytopenia (85,000/cubic mm). Renal function tests were significantly deranged, with elevated serum creatinine (6.23 mg/dL) and serum urea (89 mg/dL). Liver function tests showed mild hyperbilirubinemia, with total bilirubin (1.28 mg/dL) and indirect bilirubin (0.99 mg/dL). Other parameters, including hemoglobin, blood glucose, electrolytes, and serum albumin, were within normal limits (Table 1).

Serological testing revealed negative results for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBs Ag), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) antibody, hepatitis C virus antibody (HCV Ab), anti‐HAV (hepatitis A virus) IgM, and anti‐HEV (hepatitis E virus) IgM, ruling out common viral infections.

For procoagulant workup, autoimmune studies like antinuclear antibody (ANA) and anti‐neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) were negative, which ruled out connective tissue disease and vasculitis. Thrombophilia screening was negative for factor V Leiden mutation, anti‐phospholipid antibody (APLA), protein C, and protein S deficiency. Negative JAK2 mutation and normocellular bone marrow biopsy excluded underlying myeloproliferative disorders. The hypercoagulability panel revealed markedly elevated fibrinogen (520 mg/dL), increased PAI‐1 (68 ng/mL), and elevated homocysteine levels (28 μmol/L). Ascitic fluid analysis revealed a high serum‐ascites albumin gradient (SAAG 2.41 g/dL) with low total protein (3.29 g/dL), consistent with portal hypertension.

Given a platelet count of 85,000/μL at presentation (mild thrombocytopenia) and imaging‐confirmed hepatic venous outflow obstruction, the risk–benefit favored anticoagulation. We initiated dose‐reduced enoxaparin (accounting for ESRD‐related impaired renal clearance and bleeding risk) and transitioned to warfarin with a target International Normalized Ratio (INR) of 2.0–3.0.

Abdominal ultrasonography (USG) revealed a hyperechoic area within the lumen of the IVC, suggestive of an IVC web causing luminal obstruction (Figure 1). Color Doppler imaging demonstrated intrahepatic collateral formation, indicative of compensatory changes secondary to IVC obstruction.

Subsequent contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CECT) of the abdomen confirmed the presence of a hyperdense web in the suprahepatic segment of the IVC (Figure 2A). Associated findings included dilated portal and splenic veins, consistent with portal hypertension (Figure 2B). Additionally, CECT revealed bilaterally shrunken kidneys with cortical thinning, suggestive of CKD (Figure 2C).

Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with chronic BCS. The diagnosis of chronic BCS in this patient was considered in the context of her CKD, which is known to contribute to prothrombotic states.

Treatment Outcomes and Follow‐up

The patient was admitted for urgent management of portal hypertension and hepatic congestion. Hemodynamically stable, she received furosemide (40 mg IV daily) and albumin infusions (20%, 50 mL/day) to reduce ascites and maintain intravascular volume; paracentesis was deferred due to the absence of tense ascites. Immediate anticoagulation with enoxaparin (0.5 mg/kg SC twice daily) was initiated to prevent thrombus progression. Interventional radiology proposed endovascular intervention for the chronic IVC web, but the patient declined invasive procedures. Upon discharge, warfarin (3 mg/day) was continued after overlapping with enoxaparin for 5 days with INR monitoring (target 2.0–3.0), alongside propranolol (20 mg/day) for variceal prophylaxis. Hemodialysis was continued with adjusted heparin dosing to prevent over‐anticoagulation, and lactulose was advised for encephalopathy prevention.

At 3 months, ascites, edema, and abdominal discomfort had improved; liver function tests remained stable; the patient continued maintenance hemodialysis on the pre‐existing schedule; and no bleeding or thrombotic events were documented. She reported improved daily comfort and was able to resume routine household activities.

Discussion

HVOO, commonly known as BCS, was first described in the 1800s by George Budd and Hans Chiari. Chiari described hepatic‐vein thrombosis, while Budd reported the presence of membranous material within the hepatic‐vein [8]. The development of the Budd–Chiari syndrome is predisposed by a number of factors, such as inherited and acquired hypercoagulable states and numerous other reasons found in approximately 75% of cases. There is growing recognition that a patient may have more than one cause [9]. One‐third of patients have an invisible cause. Among the causes of BCS are hematologic conditions, most commonly myeloproliferative disorders [9]. Additional hematologic causes of the disease include paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, which is characterized by complement‐induced hemolytic anemia [10], congenital deficits of protein C, protein S, and antithrombin III [11]. Women who utilize oral contraceptives had a 2.37% relative risk of hepatic‐vein thrombosis [12]. Sarcoidosis, systemic lupus erythematosus, inflammatory bowel illnesses, Sjogren's syndrome, and Behcet's disease are examples of chronic inflammatory diseases that produce nonspecific superficial phlebitis, which lowers prostaglandin levels and causes hepatic‐vein thrombosis. Chronic infections such as hydatid disease, aspergillosis, syphilis, tuberculosis, and amoebic cysts can lead to mechanical obstruction or narrowing of the inferior vena cava, either through thrombotic or non‐thrombotic processes. Hepatic‐vein thrombosis or stenosis can also be brought on by malignant conditions such as hepatocellular carcinoma, atrial myxoma, leiomyosarcoma, Wilms' tumor, and kidney and adrenal gland cancer [13].

Although thrombus formation is the most common cause of obstruction in BCS, other factors such as extrinsic compression from tumors, abscesses, or cysts; membranous webs in the IVC; and postoperative complications, including those following liver transplantation, can also contribute to the pathophysiology [13].

Portal hypertension and elevated hepatic sinusoidal pressure are the outcomes of obstruction of the hepatic venous outflow pathway. Neighboring hepatocytes suffer hypoxemic damage as a result of portal venous stasis and congestion. Following that, there is nodular regenerative hyperplasia, centrilobular fibrosis, and finally cirrhosis. Atrophy of the peripheral areas and hypertrophy of the caudate lobe and central liver regions are the primary changes in hepatic morphology [14, 15]. Liver function does, however, improve if the hepatic sinusoidal pressure is decreased by the establishment of a portal venous collateral system or a portosystemic shunt [16].

In our case, the patient exhibited similar features with the presence of intrahepatic venous collateral. The clinical presentation of BCS is determined by the extent and speed of hepatic‐vein occlusion as well as whether venous collateral circulation has formed to decompress the hepatic sinusoids.

The clinical presentation of BCS varies widely in both severity and onset. There are four main clinical varieties of BCS: fulminant, acute, subacute, and chronic liver disease. Within 8 weeks following the onset of jaundice, a patient with the fulminant type of the condition presents with hepatic encephalopathy. This is an unusual presentation. Patients with the acute form experience transient, uncontrollable ascites. The most prevalent type, subacute, is characterized by a more insidious onset, ascites, and little liver destruction. Almost all cases of BCS include ascites, hepatomegaly, and abdominal discomfort [14]. The following symptoms should always raise suspicions of BCS: a chronic liver disease with an unknown cause; a liver disease in a patient with a prothrombotic state; the triad of hepatomegaly, ascites, and upper abdominal pain; or the presence of fulminant liver failure with hepatomegaly and ascites [4]. However, 20% of cases of hepatic‐vein thrombosis have also been reported in asymptomatic patients, in whom the hepatic sinusoids were decompressed by massive intrahepatic portosystemic collaterals [17].

Hypercoagulability is a common condition occurring in end‐stage renal disease (ESRD) patients. There is activation of procoagulant markers and elevated levels of D‐dimer, C‐reactive protein (CRP), fibrinogen, factor VII, factor VIII, and von Willebrand factor due to increased synthesis out of proportion to urinary losses. We can also observe decreased endogenous anticoagulants due to urinary losses, enhanced platelet activation and aggregation, sustained low‐grade inflammation, and decreased activity of the fibrinolytic system. This can present with a variety of complications such as ischemic stroke, myocardial infarction, peripheral artery occlusion, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and vascular access thrombosis [18].

CKD induces a prothrombotic state via impaired fibrinolysis (reduced tPA and elevated PAI‐1), platelet hyperactivity, and upregulated procoagulants (fibrinogen, TF). RAAS activation amplifies fibrinogen, D‐dimer, and PAI‐1, correlating with cardiovascular/renal damage. PAI‐1 promotes endothelial dysfunction, fibrosis, and atherosclerosis, while fibrinogen enhances platelet aggregation and inflammation. CKD also elevates von Willebrand factor, coagulation factors (XIIa and VIIa), and thrombomodulin. However, the direct mechanistic roles of these markers in CKD progression or atherothrombosis, alongside interactions with cardiovascular risk factors, remain unclear and warrant further investigation [13]. Hyperhomocysteinemia contributes to thrombotic tendencies in CKD patients, thereby increasing their cardiovascular risk [19].

These mechanisms account for the increased levels of homocysteine, fibrinogen, and PAI‐1 observed in our patient, contributing to hypercoagulability associated with CKD.

Diagnostic options include imaging and Doppler ultrasound, which is the first‐line diagnostic tool due to its high sensitivity and specificity (> 85%). It detects high flow velocities, turbulence at the stenosis site, and intrahepatic collaterals. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and CT scans are used for confirmation or in complex cases. Liver biopsy is also an option, but it is not done routinely due to its invasive nature [20]. In our case as well, the IVC web was initially detected on USG, along with impaired blood flow and collateral formation observed on Doppler, which was subsequently confirmed by CECT.

Therapy for BCS starts with anticoagulation to manage thrombotic changes, monitored by hematologic parameters. If ineffective, angioplasty, stenting, or thrombolysis may be considered in the early stages. Transjugular Intrahepatic Portosystemic Shunt (TIPS) is an option for patients with significant venous obstruction, offering lower morbidity and mortality than surgery. In cases of liver failure, liver transplantation is the definitive treatment, with continued anticoagulation therapy post‐transplant. Close monitoring for hepatocellular carcinoma is essential following transplantation. The approach is personalized based on the disease severity [20].

Conclusion

This case highlights CKD as an underrecognized prothrombotic risk factor for BCS, emphasizing the need to evaluate hypercoagulable markers like fibrinogen, PAI‐1, and homocysteine in CKD patients with unexplained hepatic signs. Early diagnosis via imaging and prompt anticoagulation are pivotal to prevent progression to cirrhosis. An integrated multidisciplinary approach involving nephrologists, hepatologists, radiologists, and hematologists is vital for personalized management, ensuring a balanced consideration of both renal and hepatic complications. Understanding the underlying renal‐vascular‐hepatic interplay as the etiology of BCS is crucial, and regular follow‐up to monitor for thrombotic complications remains paramount in reducing morbidity in patients with this condition.
