Transition from Subdural Electrocorticography to Stereo-electroencephalography in Presurgical Evaluation of Epilepsy: Single-institutional Experience
Authors: Yosuke OKAZAKI, Takahiro HAYASHI, Keiya IIJIMA, Yuiko KIMURA, Yuu KANEKO, Shota TANAKA, Masaki IWASAKI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stereo-electroencephalography has recently gained attention as a less invasive and effective technique for presurgical evaluation in patients with drug-resistant epilepsy. Several studies have reported favorable outcomes when compared to conventional subdural electrode implantation. This study aimed to compare the target regions, complications, additional surgeries, and surgical outcomes between stereo-electroencephalography and subdural electrode implantation. A retrospective review was conducted on 83 consecutive patients who underwent intracranial electrode implantation between 2018 and 2024. Clinical variables were compared between the subdural electrode implantation (n = 41) and stereo-electroencephalography (n = 42) groups. In the stereo-electroencephalography group, 28 cases (66.7%) were implanted in the insular gyrus as the deep area, which was significantly different from the subdural electrode implantation group (8 cases, 19.5%). Resective surgery was performed in 39 subdural electrode implantation cases. In the stereo-electroencephalography group, 20 patients underwent resection, 14 received thermocoagulation, 5 were scheduled for surgery, and 3 were not eligible for surgery because the epileptogenic zone could not be identified. Intracranial hemorrhage occurred in 3 cases in each group. Two subdural electrode implantation cases were symptomatic and required reoperation, whereas all stereo-electroencephalography-related hemorrhages were asymptomatic. One year after surgery, seizure freedom was achieved in 57.9% (22 of 38 cases) of subdural electrode implantation and 64.0% (16 of 22 cases) of stereo-electroencephalography patients (p = 0.606). Seizure outcomes and complication rates were similar between stereo-electroencephalography and subdural electrode implantation, with fewer serious complications in the stereo-electroencephalography group. Stereo-electroencephalography was more frequently used in cases involving deep lesions or prior subdural implantation, highlighting its utility in technically challenging cases.

Introduction

Patients with drug-resistant focal epilepsy may be considered for surgical treatment.1) Intracranial electrode implantation is performed for further investigation when the epileptogenic zone is not adequately identified through non-invasive presurgical evaluation.2) Historically, subdural electrode implantation (SDE) has been the primary method used in Japan. However, this approach is challenging for evaluating deeply located areas such as the insula and hippocampus.3) According to Zanello,4) stereo-electroencephalography (SEEG) was established by Talairach and others in France during the 1950s as a technique for identifying the organization of epileptogenic networks, and it has since spread to Europe and the U.S. since the 2000s.5)

In Japan, the stereotactic robot system was approved for stereotactic neurosurgery by the Pharmaceutical and Medical Devices Agency in 2015, and the procedure for stereotactic electrode implantation for SEEG has been covered by insurance since 2020. Though an increasing number of institutions have begun to implement SEEG, some technical limitations remain. The availability of robotic systems is still limited, and small-diameter electrodes and anchor bolts for electrode fixation have not yet been approved in Japan. As a result, the effectiveness and safety of SEEG have not been well investigated in Japan. We previously implanted a combination of subdural electrodes and a small number of depth electrodes to densely map the epileptogenic areas, including deep cortices. However, we found that the “dense mapping” approach has not improved the detection of EEG onset, and the seizure outcome was not as good as expected, especially in temporal lobe epilepsy. These experiences led to a reconsideration of the strategy for intracranial electrode implantation and an attempt to actively introduce SEEG as a way to assist in the evaluation of seizures.6)

The purpose of this study is to compare the characteristics and differences between subdural electrocorticography and SEEG, providing a guide for invasive presurgical evaluation of epilepsy. Specifically, we examine patient characteristics, complication rates, surgical outcomes, and indications for both procedures at our institution, supplemented by a literature review.

Materials and Methods

Study design and ethics approval

This is a retrospective descriptive study, approved by the ethics committee of the National Center of Neurology and Psychiatry, Japan. The requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective study design.

Participants and data collection

This study included all patients who underwent intracranial electrode implantation for the presurgical evaluation of drug-resistant focal epilepsy at our hospital between January 1, 2018, and July 30, 2024. We retrospectively collected the following clinical data from the electronic medical record: gender, age at onset, age at electrode implantation, duration from epilepsy onset to implantation, duration from implantation to curative epilepsy surgery, seizure frequency (daily, weekly, monthly), etiology, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) abnormalities, prior craniotomy or other epilepsy surgeries, type of implantation (SDE or SEEG), the number of implanted electrodes, anatomical areas explored, surgical complications related to electrode implantation, type of surgical treatment following intracranial electroencephalogram (EEG) study, follow-up period after epilepsy surgery, and postoperative seizure outcome. The grade of complications was classified according to the Clavien-Dindo classification.7) Seizure outcomes were evaluated at 1 and 2 years after the subsequent resective/ablative epilepsy surgery using the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) classification.8) If reoperative epilepsy surgery was performed within 1 or 2 years, follow-up was terminated at that point, and seizure outcomes were not evaluated.

Presurgical evaluation

All patients underwent comprehensive presurgical evaluation, including long-term video-EEG monitoring, 3T MRI, fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography (FDG-PET), magnetoencephalography, and neuropsychological testing. Ictal and interictal Technetium-99m Ethyl Cysteinate Dimer single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) was performed as needed, and subtraction ictal SPECT co-registered to MRI (SISCOM) was used to visualize ictal hyperperfusion. MRI and FDG-PET co-registration was routinely performed to localize glucose hypometabolism. Experienced neuroradiologists interpreted all imaging findings. The indication and strategy for electrode implantation were determined after reviewing the patient's clinical history and the above evaluations at a patient management conference attended by adult and pediatric epileptologists, neurosurgeons, and other relevant professionals. No standard criteria for selecting subdural electrode implantation and SEEG existed at the time of the study; however, since SEEG became covered by insurance in 2020, it has been actively included as an option.

Electrode implantation

Subdural electrodes were implanted during craniotomy under general anesthesia. Grid and strip-array disc electrodes (diameter: 2.3 mm, center-to-center distance: 10 mm, Unique Medical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) were often used in combination. If needed, depth electrodes (diameter: 1.5 mm, inter-electrode distance: 5 mm, Unique Medical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) were also implanted under navigation guidance (VarioguideⓇ, Brainlab AG, Munich, Germany).6)

Stereotactic implantation of depth electrodes for SEEG was performed using the Leksell stereotactic frame (Elekta AB, Stockholm, Sweden) or using navigation guidance (VarioguideⓇ, Brainlab AG, Munich, Germany) under general anesthesia. We used a 1.5-mm-diameter electrode (Unique Medical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) for all SEEG cases. Electrode trajectories were planned using iPlanⓇ (Brainlab AG, Munich, Germany). In preoperative planning, blood vessels were visualized on contrast-enhanced T1-weighted images, and each trajectory was carefully adjusted to ensure it was at least 2 mm away from the vessels. After confirming electrode position with intraoperative X-ray, the electrode lead was threaded and secured with a screw fixed to the skull.

All patients underwent computed tomography (CT) scans postoperatively to localize electrodes and check for hemorrhagic complications. Video-EEG monitoring was typically initiated on the day of implantation and continued for about 1 week until the patient's habitual seizures were recorded. Seizure induction tests and functional mapping using direct electrical stimulation were performed as needed. Surgical treatment, if indicated, was performed at the time of electrode removal in patients evaluated with subdural electrodes. Epilepsy surgery was generally scheduled separately from electrode removal for patients evaluated with SEEG.

Statistical analysis

Clinical characteristics were compared between patients evaluated with SDE (SDE group) and those with SEEG (SEEG group). We used the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Confidence intervals (CIs) for the odds ratio (OR) were calculated using the Baptista-Pike method. A significance level of 0.05 was used throughout. GraphPad 9.0 (Dotmatics, Inc. Boston, MA, USA) was used for these analyses.

Results

Patient demographics and epilepsy-related variables

The study included a total of 83 intracranial evaluations performed in 78 patients, comprising 41 cases with SDE and 42 cases with SEEG. Five patients underwent invasive evaluations once with SDE and later with SEEG. Thus, they were counted twice in the study period.

Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. There were no significant differences in sex, age at onset, and age at electrode implantation between the SDE and SEEG groups. The period between electrode implantation and surgery was shorter in the SDE group (median 11 days, n = 36) than in the SEEG group (median 92.5 days, n = 34) (p < 0.001). A past history of epilepsy surgery was noted in 9 patients in the SDE group and 12 in the SEEG group. One patient in the SDE group had a history of vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) implantation, whereas the other patients had received craniotomy. Nine patients, 1 in the SDE group and 8 in the SEEG group, had a history of subdural electrode implantation, followed by resective epilepsy surgery in 8 patients and by VNS in 1 patient. Notably, 2 patients in the SEEG group had an invasive evaluation with subdural electrodes and resective surgery twice before, and SEEG was performed as the third presurgical evaluation. The most frequent etiology was focal cortical dysplasia in both groups. Other malformations of cortical development included heterotopia (n = 3), polymicrogyria (n = 2), and tuberous sclerosis (n = 1). Ulegyria was seen only in the SDE group. Other etiologies included hippocampal sclerosis (n = 3), encephalocele (n = 2), arteriovenous malformation, cavernous malformation, tumor, trauma, and porencephaly (n = 1 each). The number of cases without a visible MRI lesion was 6 patients (14.6%) in the SDE group and 10 patients (23.8%) in the SEEG group (p = 0.405) (Table 1).

Electrode implantation

In the SEEG group, depth electrodes were implanted using the Leksell stereotactic frame in 29 cases and the VarioguideⓇ system in 3 cases. The number of electrodes implanted was 87 ± 27 in the SDE group and 79 ± 20 (mean ± SD) in the SEEG group, with no significant difference (p = 0.172). A mean of 3.8 ± 1.7 depth electrodes was implanted along with subdural electrodes in 36 patients (87.8%) of the SDE group. The number of depth electrode leads was 8.4 ± 1.9 in the SEEG group (mean ± standard deviation). More patients in the SEEG group explored deep regions such as the hippocampus, amygdala, insular gyrus, and cingulate gyrus, whereas more patients in the SDE group explored areas closer to surface functional areas, such as the pars triangularis, pars opercularis, and the precentral gyrus (Table 2).

Surgical outcome

The total complication rate was not different between the 2 groups (Table 3). According to the Clavien-Dindo classification, complications requiring surgical intervention (grade III or higher) were observed in 2 of 4 patients (50%) in the SDE group and 1 of 8 patients (12.5%) in the SEEG group. There were 3 cases of intracranial hemorrhage in each group (7.3% in the SDE and 7.1% in the SEEG group). Two patients in the SDE group were symptomatic and required surgical intervention. One patient in the SEEG group underwent electrode removal earlier than planned due to an infection. Other complications were mild and did not significantly affect the treatment course.

Curative epilepsy surgery was indicated in 94.0% of patients, with no difference between the SDE and SEEG groups (Figure 1). Fourteen patients in the SEEG group underwent stereotactic radiofrequency thermocoagulation. Five patients were scheduled for surgery at the time of the last follow-up. One of them experienced better seizure control after SEEG. Postoperative seizure outcomes at 1 and 2 years were available for 63 and 50 patients, respectively. Seizure freedom was achieved at 1 year in 22 patients (57.8%) in the SDE group and in 16 patients (64.0%) in the SEEG group (Table 4). Discussion

This single-institutional observational study showed that postoperative outcomes were not remarkably different between SDE and SEEG, but the area to be explored was largely determined by the methods used. Deep structures such as the hippocampus, amygdala, insula, and cingulate gyrus were more frequently explored with SEEG, whereas the superficial functional cortices, such as the inferior frontal gyrus and the precentral gyrus, were more frequently explored with SDE. The rate of surgical complications was not different between the 2 methods. However, it is noteworthy that serious hemorrhagic complications were only observed in the SDE group. Since SEEG does not require craniotomy, it is relatively safe and easier to perform as a method for reevaluating patients with previous epilepsy surgery. About 20% of SEEG cases were performed in patients who had failed epilepsy surgery after evaluation with subdural electrodes. Notably, this study represents the largest Japanese cohort to date and provides data obtained under unique circumstances, where anchor bolts and thin electrodes were not used. SEEG is known as an excellent method for understanding epilepsy from a network perspective by recording from a wide area of the bilateral hemispheres.9,10) SEEG is generally selected as a method of invasive preoperative evaluation for epilepsy, but the appropriate method should be chosen depending on the location to be explored.

SEEG is less invasive and can investigate a wider area of the brain, making it easier to perform in technically complex cases, including deeper or multilobar lesions and reoperation cases. However, this depends on the degree to which SEEG is actively performed. There were no differences in the surgical indication between the 2 groups in this study. Katz and Abel11) reported that resective surgery was performed in 69%-97% of the SDE group and in 82%-87% of the SEEG group with no significant difference. On the other hand, Tandon et al.3) reported that the rate of surgical indications was lower in SEEG (74.4%) than in SDE (91.4%) (p < 0.01). Due to its less invasive nature, SEEG tends to be used more frequently in cases of “difficult-to-localize” epilepsy where resective surgery is expected to be difficult. In contrast, SDE tends to be reserved for patients who are expected to undergo resection surgery. Even in cases where the chance of success is not optimal, some form of surgical resection may be performed because craniotomy is necessary to remove electrodes.3)

Our study showed that SEEG is associated with fewer serious complications, which is consistent with previous reports. The risk of intracranial hemorrhage was reported to be lower in the SEEG group (1.0%) than in the SDE group (4.0%).12) Two patients in the SDE group had postoperative cerebral edema and intracranial hemorrhage that required emergency decompression surgery. No patients with electrode-related intracranial hematoma required surgical intervention in the SEEG group of this study. It is essential to double-check that the electrode tracts do not coincide with the vasculature when planning. Another study reported that 9.6% (95% CI: 7.3%-12.5%) had a major surgical complication after SDE implantation, compared to 4.4% (95% CI: 2.9%-6.6%) after SEEG implantation.13)

Although no significant difference in seizure outcomes was observed between the 2 groups at our institution, previous reports have indicated comparable or more favorable outcomes in patients evaluated with SEEG.13,14) Tandon et al.3) reported that the seizure-free rate at 1 year after resective surgery was better for SEEG cases (57 of 75 [76.0%]) compared with SDE cases (59 of 108 [54.6%]). The reason for the good outcome is that technical advancements in surgery and a deeper understanding of electro-clinical phenomena have made it possible to perform more precise implantation based on accurate strategies.9) We must be cautious about interpreting our results because our study was focused on the transitional period to SEEG. Seizure freedom was achieved at 1 year after surgery in 6 of the first 12 (50.0%) patients and in 10 of the second 12 (83.3%) patients in the SEEG group. It is likely that we have become more experienced with the SEEG procedure and subsequent indication for surgery during this period. It has been reported that favorable outcomes after SEEG are associated with how clearly the seizure hypothesis can be established preoperatively.2) To determine the true efficacy of SEEG, we must focus on longer follow-up studies. Cardinale et al.15) reported that 48.9% (230/470) of patients achieved Engel class I outcomes after surgical treatment following SEEG, with a mean follow-up period of 11.7 years. Focusing on postoperative longitudinal outcomes, a study involving 414 SDE cases demonstrated that the estimated probability of complete seizure freedom was 61% at 1 year, 47% at 3 years, 42% at 5 years, and 33% at 10 years after surgery.16) Bulacio et al.17) found seizure freedom rates of 55.5%, 44%, and 39% at 1, 3, and 5 years after SEEG-guided resection, respectively, indicating a gradual decline over time. These findings suggest that seizure control tends to decrease over time, and there is no definitive long-term advantage of SEEG over SDE.

Although prior craniotomy rates were comparable between groups, SEEG was more often used for re-evaluations following subdural electrode placement and for repeated assessments of the same cortical region, indicating that SEEG can be appropriate even for cases where craniotomy is considered high risk for a second surgery. Yang et al.18) assessed the safety and accuracy of SEEG in patients who had undergone craniotomy compared with those without prior craniotomy. The results showed no difference in complication rates and that the accuracy of electrode implantation was equivalent in both groups. They also reported that SEEG was performed for 17 patients with a history of craniotomy, and 12 of them (70.6%) underwent resective surgery, suggesting the usefulness of SEEG in repeated surgery cases.18)

The introduction of SEEG has raised interest in the stereotactic approach in epilepsy surgery. About one-third of the patients in the SEEG group were treated with RFTC in this study. As deep limbic areas were more frequently evaluated with SEEG, a stereotactic approach is increasingly required for treatment. Selective treatment of areas such as the insula and hippocampus is virtually impossible without stereotactic surgery. A previous report on volume-based RFTC for insular epilepsy showed a 53% seizure freedom rate and an 89% responder rate (Engel Class I-III).19) Takayama et al.20) demonstrated the effectiveness and safety of volume-based RFTC in 2 pediatric patients with operculo-insular epilepsy. Currently, laser Interstitial Thermal Therapy (LITT) has emerged as an effective approach to the treatment of deep epileptogenic zones, such as those in the medial temporal lobe and insula. LITT enables precise thermal ablation under real-time MRI guidance with temperature monitoring, but its availability remains limited.21) According to systematic reviews, the seizure-free rate following LITT was 58% for temporal lobe epilepsy and 57% for insular epilepsy.22,23) Though it is essential to consider the characteristics of these novel approaches in stereotactic surgery, it is equally important to maximize the precision and accuracy of preoperative planning.21)

Limitations

There are several limitations in this study. The sample size is small, and the data were gathered retrospectively from a single institution, both of which introduce potential bias in patient and treatment selection. This study focused on the transitional period from SDE to SEEG; therefore, patient characteristics were not controlled between the 2 groups. The indications, techniques, and interpretations for SEEG were evolving, and subsequent interventions such as repeat surgery or RFTC were changed during this period, all of which may have influenced seizure outcomes and complications. Additionally, the follow-up period was relatively short, with 1-year or longer outcomes available for only a fraction of patients in the SEEG group. Long-term prospective studies involving a larger cohort of patients are needed to further validate these findings. Thus, this study is not a direct comparison between SEEG and SDE, and careful interpretation is necessary. This study did not evaluate comprehensive data on medical costs and patients' quality of life. To demonstrate the true usefulness of SEEG, consideration from such a perspective is essential.

Conclusions

Despite comparable surgical outcomes and complication rates between the 2 groups, SEEG was more frequently selected for cases with deeply located lesions and prior subdural electrode implantation. Although the overall complication rates were similar, severe intracranial hemorrhages were more commonly associated with SDE. These findings support SEEG as a safe and effective approach, particularly in technically challenging cases.
