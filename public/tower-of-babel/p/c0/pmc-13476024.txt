Reply to the Editor: [Acute Normovolemic Hemodilution Significantly Reduces RBC Transfusion and Lactic Acidosis Following Cardiac Surgery—A Propensity-Matched Study]
Authors: Rosa Labanca, Domenico Pontillo, Alessandro Belletti, Alessandro Pruna
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the quasi-experimental study by Sebt, Sharifi, and colleagues, which utilized propensity-score matching in a cohort of 778 patients undergoing heart surgery to clarify the effects of mild-volume (5–8 mL/kg of blood) acute normovolemic hemodilution (ANH) on patient outcomes.[1] We fully agree with the authors that minimizing allogeneic blood products remains a critical objective in modern perioperative care. ANH is recognized as a valuable blood-sparing strategy, with 13.7% of cardiac surgery patients worldwide actually receiving ANH.[2]

Sebt and colleagues observed that mild-volume ANH significantly reduced the rate of red blood cell transfusion by 16 percent and the number of transfused units by 0.24 per patient.[1]

However, we disagree with the findings and the conclusions by Sebt et al. since our group recently published the ANH trial, a large multicenter randomized controlled trial, which included 2,010 patients undergoing cardiac surgery, documenting no significant reduction in overall hospital transfusion rates when ANH was used with a median volume of 650 mL.[34]

The differences between the positive results of Sebt et al. and the neutral findings of the larger ANH randomized trial may stem from methodological variations. Observational studies, even with propensity matching, cannot account for all potential variables. Furthermore, in retrospective studies, the intervention group may have been managed by more skilled clinicians, potentially leading to improved outcomes due to their higher level of expertise and attention to detail. Ultimately, in the ANH RCT, about 7% of patients receiving ANH had autologous blood reinfused before or during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) due to anemia or hypotension. In observational studies, these higher-risk patients are typically included in the “control” group.

We have several suggestions for future studies aimed at identifying patients most likely to benefit from ANH. One suggestion is to investigate patients with shorter CPB duration, as they were more likely to benefit from ANH in the ANH RCT.[4] Furthermore, it could also be valuable to explore the effect of weight-adjusted ANH volume compared to absolute volumes. Lastly, investigating the optimal timing of ANH reinfusion (before or after reinfusion of residual CPB blood) would be of interest, as some studies suggest this may influence coagulation function and transfusion rates.[5]
