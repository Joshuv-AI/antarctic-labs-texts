Biological Signatures of Inflammation Biomarkers Identified Using Data Reduction Methods in Adult Population: A Scoping Review Protocol
Authors: Gary Sánchez‐Gordón, Elena Plans‐Beriso, Cristina Barahona‐López, Berta Anta, Oscar Millet, Pablo Fernández‐Navarro, Nerea Fernández de Larrea‐Baz, Adela Castelló‐Pastor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsChronic inflammation contributes to various chronic diseases, emphasizing the need for reliable biomarkers for early detection and understanding its causes. Measuring chronic inflammation poses challenges since most biomarkers are influenced by both acute and chronic states and recent exposures. Joint analysis of inflammatory biomarkers may help identify specific biological signatures linked to chronic inflammation. The scoping review whose protocol is described here, aims to map the existing literature identifying these signatures in adults using data reduction methods in epidemiological studies.MethodsThe review will follow the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and the extension for Scoping Reviews of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis checklist (PRISMA‐ScR). An electronic search will be performed in Medline (via PubMed) and Embase (via Elsevier) for studies published until October 2023, using relevant terms related to inflammation biomarkers, and data reduction methods. Eligible studies include systematic reviews and primary experimental, quasi‐experimental, and observational research analyzing inflammation biomarkers in blood, urine, or saliva from adults. Studies focused on acute infectious diseases, transient inflammatory conditions, and those not published in English or Spanish will be excluded. Four independent reviewers will conduct a two‐phase screening of studies (title/abstract and full‐text), with a third reviewer resolving disagreements. Data extraction will be handled by one reviewer and verified by another using a predefined form.ResultsFindings will be summarized in a PRISMA flowchart. Interactive visual maps will illustrate the identified inflammatory signatures, categorized by study design, data reduction method, population type, and clinical condition. All resources will be publicly available.ConclusionThis review will provide a comprehensive overview of inflammation biomarker signatures derived from data reduction methods while identifying existing knowledge gaps in the field.Scoping Review RegistrationThis protocol was registered on January 25, 2024, on Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/85XUF).

Introduction

Background

Inflammation is a normal mechanism of defense that protects the organism from infections and other types of potentially hazardous stimuli, and initiates processes to restore tissue homeostasis. Once the threat has been controlled and the damage repaired, the involved mechanisms conclude the inflammatory process. However, failure to resolve inflammation or continued exposure to triggering agents can lead to the chronicity of the inflammatory processes [1].

Chronic inflammation has been associated with the development of numerous diseases, such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer, and autoimmune diseases [2, 3, 4, 5]. Therefore, identifying modifiable factors involved in this type of inflammation might represent a great opportunity for prevention. For this purpose, availability of reliable measures of chronic inflammation is essential. However, inflammation is a complex process, resulting from the interplay between several cell types and inflammatory mediators, which hinders the identification of stable biological markers. Among the most commonly used biomarkers, interleukins 1β (IL‐1β), 4 (IL‐4), 6 (IL‐6), and 10 (IL‐10), C‐Reactive Protein (CRP), or Tumor Necrosis Factor α (TNF‐α) are included [5, 6]. Nevertheless, most of them are not specific for chronic inflammation, and their circulating levels could be strongly influenced by recent exposures, such as diet, physical activity, or environmental pollutants [5]. The use of biomarkers that also reflect acute inflammation could make it difficult to distinguish between transient and persistent inflammatory processes, potentially leading to biased estimates of the chronic inflammatory or anti‐inflammatory effects of certain exposures.

Some authors argue that employing data reduction statistical techniques to identify biological signatures of inflammation that account for potential interactions among individual markers could enhance their predictive and discriminatory capacity for measuring chronic inflammation [1]. Consequently, this approach may improve the ability to establish reliable associations between exposures and inflammation, as well as between chronic inflammation and diseases [1]. Several studies have followed this research line, mostly involving populations with specific pathologies [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. However, the extent to which data reduction statistical techniques have been applied to identify chronic inflammation patterns or signatures, as well as resulting signatures, has not previously been summarized. A preliminary search in March 2023, in Medline, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, Prospero, Epistemonikos, Google Scholar, and JBI Evidence Synthesis, did not identify ongoing systematic reviews or scoping reviews on this topic.

Objective

The aim of this scoping review is to identify the existing literature on biological signatures of inflammation in the adult population obtained using data reduction methods, and summarize methods and findings in order to inform future research in this field.

Review Question

Following the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) PCC (Population, Concept and Context) framework [12], the review question was formulated as follows:

Which inflammation biomarker signatures have been identified in adult populations, what data reduction statistical methods have been used to derive these signatures, and in which clinical or epidemiological contexts have they been studied?

Methods

This scoping review will adhere to the methodology proposed by the JBI [12], and will be reported following the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) [13]. The protocol, developed according to recommendations from the JBI [12] and Lely et al. [14], has been registered on the Open Science Framework for public consultation (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/85XUF) [15].

Population

In order to ensure direct applicability of findings to the human adult population and to define a homogenous population, we decided to restrict the search to studies in human adult population (≥ 18 years). Studies on pregnant women and individuals undergoing acute processes such as infectious diseases, cardiovascular diseases (CVD) in the acute phase, acute exacerbation of chronic diseases (asthma crisis, flare‐up of rheumatoid arthritis, etc.), with autoimmune pathologies, or receiving immunosuppressive or anti‐inflammatory therapy will be excluded. These temporary conditions/exposures can alter inflammation biomarkers for a brief period, making their levels and patterns unrepresentative of basal status. Nevertheless, we will retain studies involving individuals with chronic diseases, as they may exhibit similar circulating levels of inflammation biomarkers compared to healthy individuals repeatedly exposed to pro‐inflammatory factors. No restrictions related to country of origin or race will be applied.

Concept

We defined an inflammation biomarker as a molecule that can be objectively measured as an indicator of normal or pathological inflammatory processes. We considered biomarkers that could serve as measures of systemic inflammation and be quantified in easy‐to‐collect specimens. The selection of biomarkers included in this review was guided by the scientific evidence available on their role as systemic inflammation indicators in epidemiological and clinical studies, including two comprehensive reviews by Calder et al. and Furman et al., as well as the scientific experience of the coauthors [5, 6, 16, 17]. Biomarkers involved in the regulation of the inflammatory response such as cytokines, and in the acute phase response, such as acute‐phase proteins were considered. The selected biomarkers were: C‐reactive protein, interleukin‐1 alpha (IL‐1α), IL‐1β, IL‐4, IL‐6, IL‐10, gamma interferon, TNF‐α, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, ceruloplasmin, glycA, glycB, haptoglobin, d‐dimer, and procalcitonin.

A signature of inflammation biomarkers was defined as any combination of soluble immune or inflammation‐related molecules that, by its own or together to other biomarkers or clinical characteristics, constitutes a common profile that allows to characterize individuals with different profiles and degrees of chronic inflammation.

We focus on signatures of inflammation derived using standard data reduction methods intended to construct new variables that efficiently summarize the information available from a higher number of variables [18]. The included methods should use mathematical, objective techniques to group inflammation biomarkers into biologically interpretable signatures, and the grouping procedure should be independent from any outcome or exposure. Taking this into account, the following statistical methods were considered: principal component analysis, singular value decomposition, independent component analysis, factor analysis, cluster analysis, multidimensional scaling analysis, t‐distributed stochastic neighbor embedding, uniform manifold approximation and projection, non‐negative principal component analysis and non‐negative matrix factorization. To provide further clarity, we briefly describe these data reduction methods in the supporting material (Table S1). Supervised approaches such as Partial Least Squares (PLS) and PLS‐Discriminant Analysis (PLS‐DA) were excluded from this scoping review. Although widely used, these methods are designed to maximize the prediction of a pre‐specified outcome [19], which is in conflict with our main focus. Also, deep‐learning and network‐based approaches do not fully align with our inclusion criteria since many such approaches involve supervised or semi‐supervised training. Even in their unsupervised form (e.g., classical auto‐encoders), the resulting representations are highly architecture‐ and training‐dependent or heavily influenced by algorithmic and parameter choices, often lacking interpretability and not readily comparable across studies [20, 21, 22]. Data reduction methods should have been applied over, at least, three of the selected inflammatory biomarkers.

Given our interest in systemic inflammation, we will focus on circulating levels of inflammatory biomarkers, and therefore, we will exclude studies measuring biomarkers in samples other than blood, saliva, or urine.

We will further exclude studies using inflammation biomarkers to assess the effectiveness of pharmacologic treatments, as this could artificially alter results.

Context

We will include studies published up to October 2023 in clinical or epidemiological settings from any geographic and cultural context and written in English or Spanish.

Types of Evidence

We will consider umbrella, systematic, and scoping reviews, meta‐analysis, and primary studies, including randomized and non‐randomized clinical trials and observational studies. Grey literature, editorials and opinion articles, narrative reviews, protocols, qualitative studies, conference abstracts and reports, clinical practice guidelines, basic research, case reports, and pilot studies will be excluded as they often lack reliable datasets or have not undergone peer review.

Study Selection Process

The screening process will consist of two separate phases: title/abstract and full‐text screening.

For the title/abstract screening, a pilot phase will be conducted in which three independent reviewers will assess the title and abstract of a randomly selected sample of 1% of the records from the initial search. Discrepancies will be resolved by consensus, and the inclusion/exclusion criteria will be refined if necessary. Subsequently, an additional 10% of the records will be randomly selected and evaluated, with any disagreements addressed in a follow‐up meeting. Additional rounds will be repeated if required until a 75% agreement among reviewers is achieved.

For the full‐text screening, a pilot sample of 10% of the records selected in the previous phase will be assessed by four reviewers to resolve possible discrepancies in the application of the selection criteria. This process will be repeated until a 75% agreement in inclusion/exclusion is achieved. No adjustments to the inclusion or exclusion criteria will be made at this stage.

In both phases, after piloting, the title/abstract or full‐text of each article will be screened by two reviewers independently and discrepancies will be resolved by consensus among at least three of the four reviewers involved.

The Rayyan software will be utilized during both screening phases.

Synthesis and Presentation of Results

The findings of the scoping review will be presented in accordance with the guidelines of the PRISMA‐ScR [13], and the study selection process will be illustrated using a flow diagram. A comprehensive report will be prepared, including tables, figures, and a narrative summary of the main findings. To facilitate the synthesis and visualization of this descriptive information, the included studies will be coded using predefined categories (based on study design, inflammatory biomarkers, biomarker functional category— for example, signaling molecule, tissue degradation product, direct effector molecule, or acute‐phase protein—, data reduction methods, and population characteristics). This coding will enable the generation of interactive visual maps that synthesize the findings for initial data processing and the creation of static matrices, and the development of interactive visualizations to present the results. The matrices will organize biomarker subcategories (rows), potentially reflecting emerging inflammatory signatures, by population types (columns). A color code will differentiate study designs, and symbols will indicate the data reduction methods employed (e.g., principal component analysis, clustering). Interactive filters will allow exploration of specific subgroups, such as particular biomarkers or combinations indicative of inflammatory signatures, data reduction techniques, or clinical conditions. This will allow to identify systematically under‐represented biomarkers, populations, or study designs, as well as methodological variability and gaps in the application of data reduction methods. These patterns will help highlight areas where evidence is sparse or inconsistent and may benefit from future research, such as diversification of biomarkers, use of novel statistical techniques, or improve validation strategies.

All resources, including R scripts, cleaned databases, and static maps, will be shared as supporting material to ensure transparency and replicability.

Stata v.18.0, R studio and EPPI‐Mapper platform [25] will be used.

Ethics and Dissemination

Given the nature of the present investigation, ethical approval and informed consent are not required, as no primary data collection involving human participants will be conducted. The results will be disseminated through publication in a peer‐reviewed scientific journal.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first scoping review that systematically examines the available literature regarding the identification of biological signatures of inflammation using data reduction methods, with a specific focus on the biomarkers used, the applied statistical methods, and the resulting signatures. Presently, there is no consensus on the optimal biomarkers of chronic inflammation or the most suitable statistical methods to derive biological signatures of inflammation. Additionally, the most commonly identified signatures and the populations over which they are identified remain unclear. Through this scoping review, we aim to consolidate existing knowledge, identify research gaps, and highlight areas where future studies can contribute to scientific advancement.
