Realizing Landau levels with open equifrequency contours around band singularities
Authors: Cuicui Lu, Oubo You, Yanji Zheng, Shaojie Ma, Qingdong Yang, Hsun-Chi Chan, Zhihao Wang, Wenshuo Ma, Yong-Chun Liu, Shuang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Charged particles in a magnetic field undergo circular motion and result in quantized energy levels known as Landau levels (LLs). LLs around band singularites are particularly interesting due to their nontrivial topological properties. To date, through the introduction of the artificial magnetic field, LLs have been observed in systems only with closed equifrequency contours (EFCs) around band singularities, i.e. type-I Dirac or Weyl systems. Here, we demonstrate the existence of LLs with open EFCs around band singularities through combined action of artificial electric (E) and magnetic (B) fields, whereas they cannot be generated by B only. We construct an artificial platform of inhomogeneous metasurfaces of the two-dimensional type-II Dirac system with open EFCs, create suitable artificial gauge fields of E&B, and observe the emergence of E&B-induced LLs experimentally. This work presents a new insight for realizing LLs, and provides a platform for controlling optical waves propagating using artificial gauge fields.

Introduction

The quantization of energy is a fundamental concept in modern physics and a key outcome of quantum mechanics. In 1930, L. Landau proposed the notion of Landau levels (LLs), which represent the quantization of energy in the presence of a magnetic field1. From the semi-classical perspective for the LLs, when electrons are confined to a two-dimensional plane and are subjected to an out-of-plane magnetic field, they move in circular cyclotron orbits as a result of the Lorentz force. The semiclassical quantization of electronic states can be described by Onsager’s rule under a magnetic field, and the dispersion of the LLs can reflect the geometrical features of the Fermi surface2–6. From the quantum perspective, the LLs exhibit distinct plateaus in the Hall conductivity, with the number of filled LLs corresponding to the Chern number7. Consequently, LLs facilitate the emergence of topological states with a series of flat bands8,9. However, photons do not directly respond to a magnetic field, so it is necessary to construct a pseudo magnetic field, also known as an artificial gauge field10.

Dirac systems, as the singularities of two-dimensional (2D) structures, such as Dirac photonic crytals11–14, metamaterials15, metasurfaces16,17, Dirac-like plasmons18, have been widely used to study artificial gauge fields and topological photonics. Dirac systems, characterized by the crossing of two energy bands in momentum space, can be generally classified as type-I or type-II based on the tilt degree of the Dirac cone19. Type-I Dirac systems have opposite slopes for the two crossing energy bands along any direction in the momentum space, resulting in closed equifrequency contours (EFCs) with circular or elliptical shapes. Conversely, type-II Dirac systems have slopes of the same sign along the tilting direction, leading to open EFCs with a hyperbolic profile. Since the initial exploration of straining the graphene lattice20, systems with closed EFCs, especially the type-I Dirac systems, have been extensively investigated for generating LLs using deformed artificial structures in optical systems12–14,21–26. Recently, the LLs in the type-I Dirac system have been experimentally realized on an integrated nanophotonic platform of a silicon photonic crystal slab, which provides a new way to govern light fields and lays a foundation for the applications of nanophotonic devices based on LLs12,13. In addition, the proposal and the realization of Landau rainbow14 indicate that Landau modes are likely to play a pivotal role in optical field localization, slow-light phenomena, and wavelength demultiplexing.

The systems with open EFCs, such as type-II Dirac systems, breaks Lorentz invariance and introduces unique topological properties and interesting phenomena, leading to widespread interests16,27–33. However, it is thought that LLs do not exist in systems with open EFCs, which are defined as those unconfined in the local momentum space near the band singularity, according to the Onsager’s rule because of the diverging area of EFCs2,6,34. Specifically, under a magnetic field, the trajectory in the momentum space for photonic systems is the same as the EFC since the kinetic energy of the particle does not change. Hence, a closed EFC is essential to the existence of the LLs under a magnetic field, while an open EFC will result in the collapse of the LLs.

In this work, we demonstrate that LLs can be generated in optical systems with open EFCs around band singularities through the combined action of artificial electric (E) and magnetic (B) fields. Unlike the open particle trajectory in the momentum space under the B field only, the introduction of both E&B alters the particle trajectory into a closed one, so that the existing condition of LLs buried in Onsager’s rule can be applicable. In the experiment, we construct a metasurface system with open EFCs, and the band structures are verified to exhibit type-II Dirac dispersion with hyperbolic EFCs. By elaborately engineering the geometric parameters of unit structures of the metasurface, we introduce spatial variations in both the frequency and the wave vector of the Dirac point, creating appropriate artificial gauge fields of E and B, which lead to observation of LLs. For comparison, we investigate another metasurface with an artificial gauge field of B, and find that no LLs are generated. Our work represents the first observation of LLs in optical systems with open EFCs, which holds important implications for the investigation of artificial gauge fields and controlling wave propagation by engineering the dispersion.

Results

Theoretical construction of Landau levels

We begin with a semi-classical picture of the trajectories for quasiparticle (or wave packet) in both real and momentum space to understand the role of the gauge fields in 2D systems with closed and open EFCs. Under a magnetic field, the trajectory in the momentum space is the same as the EFC since the magnetic field does not do work. Hence, a closed EFC is essential to the existence of the LLs. If the EFC is open, there would be no confined states, and the LLs would collapse. On the other hand, when both the magnetic field and the electric field are in play, the trajectory of the particle in the momentum space would differ from the EFC. In contrast, for the other two samples, i.e. the metasurface with a dominant B field (Fig. 4d, e) and the metasurface without gauge field (Fig. 4f, g), no LLs are observed. Their bulk modes look very similar to each other: there exists a crossing between the two bands around the Dirac point. The measured band dispersions agree well with the simulated results. Their electric field distributions also show nearly uniform magnitude across the whole sample along the y-direction, as plotted in Fig. 4h. The field distributions for each unit of the super cells in y-direction can be found in Fig. S6 in the SI. Thus, our experiments directly confirm that the LLs in a system with an open EFC cannot be generated by B alone but should be induced by an appropriate combination of gauge fields E&B.

Discussion

In conclusion, we have realized LLs in type-II Dirac systems with open EFCs around band singularities by the joint action of artificial E and B fields. The introduction of both E&B renders a closed particle trajectory in the momentum space, so that Onsager’s rule can be applicable and the LLs can be realized. We also clarify the Dirac systmes are always at the collapse phase of LLs with only B field and without E field when the tilt of the Dirac cone is greater than the group velocity. We have experimentally constructed metasurfaces with two-dimensional type-II Dirac bands to exhibit open EFC with hyperbolic profile, further built inhomogeneous metasurfaces by elaborately engineering the lattice parameters in the microwave regime, and verified the generation of the gauge fields of both E&B, which led to direct observation of LLs in a type-II Dirac system with open EFCs. Similarly, the electric energy levels of a condensed matter system will also become quantized when combined electric and magnetic fields are applied to the type-II Dirac material, where the magnitude of E&B should satisfy the conditions discussed in this work. Taking MoTe₂ as an example, the tilt velocity for its type-II Dirac cone is approximately 106m/s. To observe Landau levels in this material, the E/B ratio must also be on the order of 106m/s. This implies that a magnetic field of 1 T needs to be accompanied by an electric field of approximately 106V/m (see section VIII in SI). Moreover, these Landau levels are co-determined by both E and B fields. Consequently, the Hall resistivity is not solely dependent on the magnetic field, and its dependence on B deviates from a simple linear relationship. In contrast to the localized modes enabled by flat bands of LLs in type-I Dirac systems, the LLs in type-II Dirac systems manifest as a series of tilted discrete topological bands, presenting unique opportunities for enriching the manipulation of optical wave applications, such as directional propagation at a constant velocity across different frequencies. In addition, the LLs in type-II Dirac systems also have potential applications for structure deformation sensors by measuring wave propagating. This work provides a new mechanism to realize LLs and opens a way for controlling waves propagating based on LLs by designing aperiodic structures using artificial E&B.

Methods

Theoretical calculations

The derivation of the particle’s motion is based on the canonical equation, and the details are shown in Section I of SI. The energy eigenvalues are obtained analytically by solving the Hamiltonian equations; the extensive, lengthy calculations are shown in Section II of the SI. The numerical solution for the eigen-states is based on the Fock states38,39, and the details are shown in Section III of the SI. The band structures are simulated by using the finite element method based on the commercial software COMSOL Multiphysics.

Experimental measurements

The samples are fabricated using a standard Printed Circuit Board (PCB) fabrication technology. We employ a microwave vector network analyzer and a near-field antenna acting as a source to provide broadband frequency excitation of electromagnetic surface waves, and another near-field antenna controlled by a three-dimensional translation stage is used to detect the electric field with both real and imaginary parts. The frequency step is set to be 0.01 GHz with a whole frequency range covering 5 GHz to 13 GHz (see details in Section V of SI). The scanning step of the probe is set to be the lattice constant, and the number of scanned data points is determined by the number of units in each sample. The details of the samples can be found in Section IV, Fig. S3, Tables S1–S3 of SI. The band structure and the EFCs of the samples are then obtained from the Fourier analysis of the scanned complex electric field in real space at each frequency.
