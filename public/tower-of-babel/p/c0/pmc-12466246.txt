Growth conditions shape the proteome and diversity of Neurospora crassa extracellular vesicles
Authors: Daniel A Salgado-Bautista, Meritxell Riquelme
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtracellular vesicles (EVs) are nanosized, lipid bilayer-enclosed particles secreted by all living organisms. While EV research has primarily focused on mammalian systems, fungal EVs are gaining attention for their biological significance. Here, we investigated how growth conditions influence the protein cargo of EVs produced by Neurospora crassa, a non-pathogenic filamentous fungus and well-established model organism. EVs were isolated from cultures grown on glucose for 16 h (G16) and on sucrose for 16 (S16) and 24 h (S24). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) revealed similar size distributions for S16 and S24 EVs (24–165 nm), whereas G16 EVs exhibited a broader range (32–825 nm). Across all conditions, particles <50 nm were detected, potentially corresponding to mitochondrial-derived vesicles (MDVs) or exomeres, EV subtypes described in mammalian systems. Proteomic profiling identified 682 proteins in G16, 668 in S16, and a reduced set of 367 proteins in S24. Regardless of condition, EVs were enriched in proteins related to cell wall remodeling, protein synthesis, and carbohydrate metabolism. A high proportion of intracellular proteins confirms that fungal EVs participate in unconventional secretion. In addition, the detection of proteins involved in vesicle biogenesis and trafficking suggests that EV formation may also involve the classical secretory pathway. These findings demonstrate that EV composition and biogenesis in N. crassa are modulated by growth conditions and highlight the importance of physiological context in fungal EV research. Notably, the data reveal a diversity of EV types, including forms potentially unrelated to exosomes, expanding our understanding of fungal EV complexity.Keywords: Vesicles, Neurospora crassa, Unconventional secretion, Proteome

Introduction

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are nanoscale, membrane-bound structures secreted by all living organisms. In filamentous fungi, interest in EVs has grown significantly over the past decade, driven by increasing recognition of their roles in health, industry, and biotechnology (Rodrigues et al., 2024). Fungal EVs are involved in a variety of functions, including intercellular signaling, host-pathogen interactions, cell wall remodeling or biogenesis, and the extracellular transport of metabolites, proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids (Bitencourt et al., 2022; Garcia-Ceron et al., 2021; Medina-Castellanos et al., 2023; Zarnowski et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2019). The cargo, size, and abundance of EVs vary widely depending on species, environmental conditions, and methods used for isolation and analysis (Gil-Bona et al., 2018; Nenciarini et al., 2024; Salgado-Bautista et al., 2024). Despite this variability, fungal EVs are often categorized using classifications derived from mammalian systems as: microvesicles or ectosomes (∼50–1000 nm), apoptotic bodies (500–1000 nm), and exosomes (∼40–160 nm) (Cocucci and Meldolesi, 2015; Kalluri and LeBleu, 2020). Additional EV subtypes, such as migrasomes (500–3000 nm), large oncosomes (1000–10,000 nm) (Kang et al., 2021), and mitochondrial-derived vesicles (MDVs) (70–100 nm) (Heyn et al., 2023), as well as non-vesicular extracellular particles (NVEPs), such as exomeres (28–50 nm) and supermeres (22–32 nm) (Jeppesen et al., 2023), have been described in mammals, but whether such diversity exists in fungi remains largely unexplored. In yeasts, EVs are generally considered to be exosome-like due to their similarities with mammalian exosomes (Bleackley et al., 2019; Rodrigues et al., 2008). Exosome biogenesis begins with the inward budding of the late endosomal membrane, forming intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) within multivesicular bodies (MVBs). These MVBs can then fuse with the plasma membrane (PM) to release the enclosed ILVs (now termed exosomes) into the extracellular space (Bebelman et al., 2019). This process is regulated by motor proteins (e. g., kinesins, ADP-ribosylation factor-8 ARL8B), Rab GTPases (especially Rab7), and components of the Endosomal Sorting Complex Required for Transport (ESCRT) machinery (Jongsma et al., 2020; Kalluri and LeBleu, 2020; Raposo and Stoorvogel, 2013). These proteins mediate membrane deformation and scission, marking a pathway distinct from classical secretion mechanisms (Schmidt and Teis, 2012).

Gene deletion studies in Saccharomyces cerevisiae have demonstrated that ESCRT components VPS influence EV morphology, cargo loading, and secretion (Oliveira et al., 2010; de Oliveira et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2019). In the model filamentous fungus N. crassa, proteomic analyses have identified VPS-5 and VPS-13 – components involved in endosomal sorting – as part of the EVs cargoes (Medina-Castellanos et al., 2023). VPS-5 is part of the retromer complex, which governs endosomal sorting and vesicle formation in yeast (Tu and Seaman, 2021). In yeasts, deletion of classical secretion pathway proteins (e.g., Sec1-1, Sec-4, and Bos1-1) has been shown to affect EV protein composition and size, suggesting that both conventional (ER/Golgi-dependent) and unconventional (ER/Golgi-independent) pathways contribute to EV biogenesis (de Oliveira et al., 2021; Miura and Ueda, 2018; Oliveira et al., 2010). Additionally, GRASP proteins, typically associated with Golgi reassembly, may also contribute to EV release, similar to the roles of VPS proteins in membrane dynamics (de Oliveira et al., 2021). EV biogenesis in fungi may share similarities with the formation and transport of secretory vesicles during hyphal growth. In N. crassa, secretory vesicles accumulate at the hyphal apex within the Spitzenkörper (SPK), a dynamic structure essential for hyphal morphology, growth, and directionality (Riquelme et al., 2014). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) studies have revealed the presence of MVBs in subapical and distal hyphal regions, beyond 30 to 40 μm from the tip (Rico-Ramírez et al., 2018), consistent with the idea that EVs in N. crassa may originate as exosomes. EVs isolated from N. crassa typically range from 20 to 120 nm in diameter, consistent with exosomal sizes. However, proteomic studies have so far identified few proteins directly linked to EV biogenesis. Instead, most identified proteins are associated with functions like cell wall remodeling and intercellular communication (Medina-Castellanos et al., 2023).

Fungal physiology undergoes dynamic changes according to growth conditions, which are reflected in the variability of secreted proteins (Helmel et al., 2014; Oda et al., 2006). Previous studies have shown that the duration of culture incubation influences protein secretion, stress responses, and metabolic profiles. Recently, the evaluation of the impact of culture time on EVs from S. cerevisiae revealed differences in cargo composition and a greater capacity to induce collagen synthesis in early-stage cultures (Jeon et al., 2025). Therefore, incubation time is a critical factor likely to influence the protein composition of EVs. In addition, media composition has been shown to affect lipid content and protein cargo of EVs in Histoplasma capsulatum (Cleare et al., 2020).

We hypothesized that evaluating EV proteomes under varying culture conditions – specifically different growth durations and carbon sources – could offer insights into how physiological state influences EV content and biogenesis. In this study, we compared EVs isolated from N. crassa cultures grown for 16 h on glucose or sucrose, and 24 h on sucrose. We observed notable differences in the diversity and abundance of EV-associated proteins, including those linked to vesicular transport, cell wall remodeling, and potential moonlighting functions.

Materials and methods

Fungal strains and growth conditions

N. crassa N1 strain (FGSC# 988) was grown in Vogel's minimal medium (VMM) supplemented with 1.5 % sucrose (Vogel, 1956). Stocks of conidia were prepared and conserved as previously reported (Medina-Castellanos et al., 2023). For conidia production, mycelium was grown on flasks containing solid VMM at 30 °C for 72 h in the dark and then exposed to light at room temperature for 48 h. Finally, the conidia were collected in 1 M sorbitol solution and stored at −20 °C.

Isolation of extracellular vesicles

Conidia (2 × 108) of N. crassa N1 strain were inoculated into 400 mL of liquid VMM (with sucrose or glucose as carbon source at 1.5 %) and incubated in an orbital shaker (150 rpm) at 30 °C for 16 h (sucrose: S16 and glucose: G16) or 24 h (sucrose: S24). After incubation, the mycelium was filtered using Whatman® filter paper #1 (11 μm pore size). EVs from filtrates were recovered by differential centrifugation. First, filtrates were centrifuged sequentially at 300 g for 5 min, followed by 4000 g for 30 min to remove cell debris and possible apoptotic bodies. The final supernatant (358.8 mL) was distributed in 12 Optiseal tubes of 29.9 mL (361,625, Beckman Coulter) and ultracentrifuged (Optima MAX-XP Beckman Coulter) at 150,000 g (MLA-50 fixed angle rotor) for 3 h. The resulting pellet was resuspended with 200 μL of 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.2) to wash and eliminate traces of media. This suspension was placed in Optiseal tubes (361,621, Beckman Coulter), filled with 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.2), and ultracentrifuged at 350,000 g (TLA-100 fixed angle rotor) for 4 h. The pellet was resuspended with 200 μL for DLS analysis, or with 200 μL of Exosome Resuspension Buffer (1–2.5 % Triton X-100) from the Total Exosome RNA and Protein Isolation Kit (Invitrogen, Thermo-Fisher Scientific) to release the protein from EVs for proteomic analysis.

Dynamic light scattering analysis

The hydrodynamic diameter (dh) of EVs was measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using Zetasizer Nano ZS equipment (ZEN3600, Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). One hundred μL of the suspension of purified EVs in 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.2) were analyzed using a disposable plastic micro cuvette (Malvern Panalytical Inc., ZEN0040). The measurements were done in triplicate (9 measurements with automatic runs (12–16 runs)) and analyzed using NanoSight software (version 3.2.16). Only those particles with a presence >0.2 % were considered.

Cleaning and separation of proteins by SDS-PAGE, and tryptic digestion

The concentration of proteins from EVs was quantified with a BCA Protein Assay Kit (23,225, Thermo-Fisher Scientific). Aliquots containing 20 μg of purified EV protein were cleaned under the following conditions (Lebendiker, 2002): four volumes of cold acetone (kept at −20 °C) were added to one volume containing 20 μg of protein. After mixing, the protein and acetone mix were kept for 10 min at −80 °C and then for 90 min at −20 °C. Samples were centrifuged for 15 min at 4 °C at 15,000 g. The resulting pellet was dissolved in 10 μL of 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.2) and 10 μL of Laemmli sample buffer (Mini-PROTEAN® Tetra Cell-Bio-Rad). The solubilized proteins were heated (95 °C, 4 min) and centrifuged (10,000 g, 10 min) to eliminate suspended solids. Finally, the denatured proteins were separated in a 12 % SDS-PAGE (TGX FastCast Acrylamide Kit, 12 %, Bio-Rad). Electrophoresis was run at 300 V (PowerPac Basic power supply, Bio-Rad) until the running front of Laemmli buffer reached the end of the gel. Protein bands were visualized by staining with Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 (161–0406, Bio-Rad). For Gel-LC analysis, each gel lane was cut into different regions (specific molecular weight regions with low abundance of proteins and regions with high abundance of proteins) to resolve possible issues such as dynamic range. Gel pieces were first washed with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate and 50 % acetonitrile solution until the Coomassie Blue stain was removed. Then, the proteins were reduced with 10 mM DTT in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate and alkylated with 55 mM iodoacetamide in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate. The digestion was performed with trypsin (6 ng/μL) at 37 °C overnight. The digested peptides were extracted from the gel slices twice using formic acid/acetonitrile/water (1:2:97) and water/acetonitrile (50:50), respectively. Finally, the tryptic digested peptides were concentrated in a centrifuge concentrator (SpeedVac, Thermo Savant) and kept at −80 °C until the analysis by LC-MS/MS.

Protein identification by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry analysis

The sample analysis was performed according to Huber et al. (2022) and Paez and Callegari (2022). The tryptic peptides were analyzed in an Easy nLC 1200 using a trapping and desalting online trap column (300 μm × 20 mm Acclaim PepMap C18 100 Å, Thermo-Scientific), and separated by an Easy-Spray PepMap RSLC 2 μm, 75 μm × 15 cm, nanoViper (Thermo Scientific) coupled to a nanoESI orbitrap Exploris 240 HR/MA mass spectrometer. The chromatography conditions were as follows: 0–1 min, 1 % B isocratic; 2–60 min, 1–25 %, 61–90 min 25–50 % and 92–101 min 50–100 % B linear. Mobile Phase A (Water/Formic Acid, 99.9:0.1 % v/v), and phase B (Water/Acetonitrile/Formic Acid, 20:80:0.1 % v/v). The solvent flow rate was 300 nL min−1. The Orbitrap Exploris 240 instrument was operated under dependent mode (DDS) top 12 mode to automatically switch between full scan MS and MS/MS acquisition. Based on predictive AGC, the ions were analyzed in positive MS ion mode (m/z 375–1500) with 120,000 resolution (m/z 200) after accumulation with target ions to a 1 × 106 value. The MS/MS ions selection was set at m/z 65–1800 to 1 × 105 counts. The spectrum was deconvoluted and analyzed using the Mascot Distiller v2.6 (www.matrixscience.com) and Proteome Discoverer v2.5 (Thermo Scientific). A mascot generic format list (MGF format) was generated to identify +1 or multiple charged precursor ions from the MS data file.

Bioinformatics analysis of proteins

Mascot server v2.8.1 (www.matrix-science.com, UK) in MS/MS ion search mode (local licenses) was applied to conduct peptide matches (peptide masses and sequence tags) and protein searches against UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org) (N. crassa v20230809; 10,305 sequences; 5,323,048 residues) database. The following parameters were set for the search: carbamidomethyl (C) on cysteine was fixed; variable modifications included asparagine and glutamine deamidation, and methionine oxidation. Two missed cleavages were allowed; monoisotopic masses were counted; the precursor peptide and fragment mass tolerance were set at 15 ppm and 0.02 Da, respectively; the ion score or expected cut-off was set at 5. The MS/MS spectra were searched with MASCOT using a 95 % confidence interval (% C.I.) threshold (p < 0.05), with which a minimum score > 22 was used for peptide identification. The comparative study was performed using ProteoIQ v2.8 (local license) (Bonilla et al., 2023; Bonilla et al., 2020; Paez and Callegari, 2022) under the following parameters of filtration: min peptide length = 4 aa; number of spectra = 2; min peptides per protein = 2; FDR = 1 % and the proteins were identified in three replicates (Supplementary Table 1).

Additional information was searched in UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org), the identification of signal peptide, transmembrane regions, or intracellular protein was done with Phobius (https://phobius.sbc.su.se/). The database STRING (https://string-db.org/) was used to identify the interaction of select proteins using medium confidence = 0.7, and the functional enrichment was done for the category, cellular component, using the sort terms Strength.

Determination of β-glycosidase activity by EVs from N. crassa

The β-glucosidase activity was measured by determining the hydrolysis of p-nitrophenyl-β-D-glucopyranoside (pNPG) as previously reported with some modifications (Zhang et al., 2017). A 30 μL aliquot of N. crassa EVs from G16, S16, and S24 were mixed with 180 μL of pNPG substrate (0.8 mg/mL) in 50 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 7.0). The resulting p-nitrophenol via enzymatic hydrolysis is measured by an Multiskan Sky Microplate Spectrometer (Thermo-Scientific) in kinetic loop settings to measure absorbance at 405 nm every 4 min for 3.75 h using a reaction temperature of 40 °C. The absorbance for each sample (Absf) was calculated by subtracting the absorbance of the blank (180 μL pNPG +30 μL 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.2).

Results

EV size distribution depends on nutrient and time

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analyses revealed that EVs from S16 and S24 had hydrodynamic diameters (dh) ranging from 24 nm to 165 nm, while G16 EVs exhibited a broader distribution, ranging from 32 to 825 nm (Fig. 1). Despite heterogeneity, specific EV sizes predominated. In G16 and S24, the most abundant particles had an Hd around 78.82 nm (10.3 % and 12.7 %, respectively). In S16, the dominant size was smaller, with an Hd of 58.7 nm (14.3 %).

Proteome complexity of EVs over time

A proteomic analysis was performed across all evaluated conditions to investigate potential functional and compositional differences in EVs. A total of 668 proteins were identified in S16 EVs (Fig. 2a). This number dropped to 367 in S24, indicating a marked reduction as cultures aged. In contrast, G16 EVs contained 682 proteins, comparable to S16.

We categorized identified proteins into four functional groups: those with transmembrane (TM) domains, those with signal peptides (SP), those with both TM and SP, and intracellular proteins (Fig. 2b). In S16 and G16, intracellular proteins made up about 55 % of the total. In S24, intracellular and SP-containing proteins were present in nearly equal proportions.

STRING enrichment analysis based on predicted cellular component (Fig. 3) revealed strong enrichment for proteins associated with the cell wall, mitochondria, and ribosomes in S16 and G16. In contrast, S24 EVs were enriched mainly in cell wall and extracellular proteins, while ribosomal proteins were notably reduced.

Abundant proteins associated with cell wall biogenesis in S16 and S24 highlight EV roles

The estimation of the relative abundance of each protein using the relative spectral abundance factor (RSAF), revealed 17 highly abundant proteins exceeding 1 % RSAF values in S16 (38.6 % total), 13 in G16 (32.02 %), and 21 in S24 (50.62 %) (Fig. 4). Many of these proteins are associated with the cell surface (Supplementary Table 1). Glycosidase Q7S222 (NCU05974), a GPI anchored protein, was notably abundant in all conditions: 8.74 % in S16, 9.64 % in G16, and 5.06 % in S24. It was the most abundant protein in S16 and G16, whereas ACW-12 (NCU08171), another GPI anchored protein, was the most abundant in S24 (6.20 %). According to UniProt, Q7S222 is a cell wall-associated glycosyltransferase involved in chitin metabolism. NCBI refers to it as glucanosyltransferase mwg-1. BLAST analyses indicate similarity to concanavalin A-like lectins, which are carbohydrate-binding proteins involved in cell aggregation (Varrot et al., 2013).

Abundant proteins from N. crassa EVs at different culturing times (RSAF (%) >1 %). A) G16, B) S16, and C) S24.

In total, 22 proteins were identified as GPI-anchored proteins according to UniProt and Ao et al. (2016). These represent the 3.23 %, 3.29 %, and 5.99 % of the total of identified proteins in G16, S16, and S24, respectively. However, S24 showed a higher abundance of GPI- anchored proteins with % RSAF>1. These include a glycosidase (Q7S222/NCU05974), ACW-12, three 1,3-β-glucanosyltransferases (Q1K677/NCU08909; Q1K8Y9/NCU06781; Q7S962/NCU07253), a WSC domain-containing protein (Q7S766; NCU01395), and the GPI-anchored cell wall β-1,3-endoglucanase EglC (Q7S340/NCU09175). These proteins were also detected in G16 and S16 but with variations in % RSAF (Supplementary Table 1).

EVs as vehicles for unconventional secretion

STRING analysis using only intracellular proteins revealed two major clusters in S16 and G16: one comprising ribosomal proteins and the other with mitochondrial proteins (Fig. 5). The number of proteins in both clusters decreased in S24, where a distinct cluster associated with glycolysis and amino acid metabolism became more prominent.

We identified 47 and 48 ribosomal proteins (small and large subunits) in S16 and G16, respectively, but only 17 in S24 (Supplementary Table 1). A similar temporal pattern was observed for mitochondrial proteins, many of which are components of the respirasome (Marques et al., 2007). These included: ATP synthase subunits (8 total; all in G16 and S16, only 3 in S24), cytochrome oxidase subunits (12 total; all in G16 and S16, only 4 in S24), NADH-ubiquinone oxidoreductase (7 total, present only in G16 and S16), and other mitochondrial components predominantly found in EVs from 16 h cultures.

Other interesting proteins observed in all conditions and associated with carbohydrate metabolism included enolase, malate dehydrogenase, triosephosphate isomerase, fructose-bisphosphate aldolase, and transaldolase, to mention just a few.

Proteins associated with EV biogenesis in N. crassa

We identified 22 proteins associated with secretory vesicle formation and trafficking, many of which varied by culture time (Fig. 6). In S24, only five such proteins were detected: YPT-1 (NCU08477), ADP-ribosylation factor (NCU08340), YOP-1 (NCU06702), and SEC-14 (NCU02263). In contrast, more vesicle-related proteins were found in S16 and G16, with YPT-1 being the most abundant (Fig. 6). Additional YPT/Rab GTPases included YPT-3 (NCU01523), YPT-5 (NCU00895), and YPT-7 (NCU03711), and SEC proteins included SEC-13 (NCU04063), SEC-14 (NCU02263), SEC-17 (NCU01674), SEC-31 (NCU06738), and SEC-61 (NCU08897). We also detected three VPS proteins: VPS-10 (NCU02669) (in all conditions), VPS-1 (NCU04100) (S16 and G16), and VPS-8 (NCU01470) (G16). We identified the mitofilin homolog in N. crassa, MICOS complex subunit mic60 (NCU01470), in EVs from S16 and G16.

β-Glucosidase activity in EVs

The determination of β-glucosidase activity in EVs samples was carried out through the hydrolysis of pNPG (Supplementary Fig. S1). Despite the variability among biological replicates for G16 EVs, β-glucosidase activity was higher in G16 EVs than in S16 and S24. Overall, EVs from the S24 condition exhibited the lowest enzymatic activity.

Discussion

Proteomic approaches are invaluable for uncovering the function and regulation of EVs in fungi, enhancing our understanding of how these microorganisms adapt to environmental conditions and regulate developmental processes (Ball et al., 2019). Among the various “omics” approaches used to study EVs, such as metabolomics, lipidomics, and transcriptomics, proteomics remains the most widely applied to explore EVs functions, including roles in intercellular communication (Bitencourt et al., 2022), biofilm formation (Kulig et al., 2022; Zarnowski et al., 2021), and cell wall remodeling or biosynthesis (Zhao et al., 2019). However, most fungal EVs proteomic studies are conducted at a single time point, limiting insights into the dynamic nature of EV cargo. To address this gap, we profiled N. crassa EV proteomes across different carbon sources and culture durations - 16 h sucrose (S16), 24 h sucrose (S24), and 16 h glucose (G16) – to assess how nutrient availability and growth phase influence EV composition and biogenesis. Similar time or condition-dependent EV variations have been reported in H. capsulatum and S. cerevisiae, (Cleare et al., 2020; Jeon et al., 2025).

The DLS values of N. crassa EVs align with previous reports for fungal EVs, including S. cerevisiae (50–220 nm; 80–250 nm) (Mencher et al., 2020), Fusarium graminarium (50–500 nm) (Garcia-Ceron et al., 2021), Aspergillus fumigatus (35–595 nm) (Souza et al., 2019) and Zymoseptoria tritici (50–250 nm) (Hill and Solomon, 2020), and Candida species (80–245 nm) (Kulig et al., 2022). Our findings in sucrose-based cultures are consistent with previous reports of N. crassa EVs collected at 48 h of cultures grown with sucrose ranging from 24 to 190 nm (Medina-Castellanos et al., 2023).

Although most fungal EVs are larger than 50 nm (Garcia-Ceron et al., 2021; Hill and Solomon, 2020; Kulig et al., 2022; Mencher et al., 2020), we detected a consistent population of particles smaller than 50 nm across all conditions (Fig. 1). These may correspond to exomeres, NVEPs recently described in mammals that are <50 nm, lack a lipid bilayer, and are ESCRT-independent (Anand et al., 2021). Exomeres differ in both composition and function from classical exosomes and microvesicles and are enriched in specific proteins and RNA (Zhang et al., 2018). In our study, these small particles accounted for approximately 20 % of the putative EV population in S16 and S24, but only ∼6 % in G16, suggesting that exomere-like particle secretion is favored under sucrose-based growth conditions. Supporting this, prior TEM analysis also reported particles as small as 16 nm in N. crassa EV samples (Medina-Castellanos et al., 2023).

These findings indicate that EV size distribution and subpopulation abundance are influenced by both carbon source and growth stage. The predominance of particles smaller than 50 nm under sucrose conditions may reflect a shift in extracellular particles (EPs) biogenesis or cellular physiology, possibly linked to energy availability or stress responses. While fungal EVs are often considered exosomal, our results support the secretion of alternative EV types, such as mitochondrial-derived vesicles (MDVs), and NVEPs, such as exomeres, during early growth.

Significantly, the number of proteins identified in fungal EVs varies widely across fungal species, from 61 in S. cerevisiae (Mencher et al., 2020) to over 2000 in Aspergillus fumigatus (Rizzo et al., 2020). Such variability reflects both biological diversity and methodological factors in sample preparation, digestion protocols, mass spectrometry platforms, analysis pipelines, and database selection (Salgado-Bautista et al., 2024). In our study, culture duration had the greatest impact on protein abundance, with a 45 % reduction from S16 to S24, while S16 and G16 yielded similar profiles. In contrast, a previous study identified only 252 proteins at 48 h in sucrose-based cultures (Medina-Castellanos et al., 2023), This trend highlights how growth phase, more than carbon source, shapes EV cargo.

Interesting, the diversity and abundance of cell wall-associated proteins in EVs across conditions reinforce the hypothesis that EVs participate in cell wall biosynthesis or remodeling during early fungal development. This function is consistent with findings in A. fumigatus (Rizzo et al., 2020), S. cerevisiae (Zhao et al., 2019), and Candida albicans (Gil-Bona et al., 2015). Furthermore, the chitin-associated protein MWG-1 (NCU05974), absent in 48-h cultures (Medina-Castellanos et al., 2023), emerges as a potential early-stage EV marker. This glycosidase protein was the most abundant protein in G16 and S16. Interestingly, the β-glucosidase activity was higher in G16 followed by S16, this activity could indicate that EVs interact with glycans that are part of the cell wall and possibly facilitating the release of EVs to outside of cell, like was observed with Histoplasma capsulatum EVs, which exhibit chitinolytic activity to facilitate secretion (Valdez et al., 2023).

Similarly, GPI-anchored proteins involved in cell wall biogenesis and hyphal development (Bowman et al., 2006), such as, glucanosyltransferases and anchored cell wall proteins (ACW) (Ao et al., 2016; Bowman and Free, 2006; Maddi et al., 2009; Patel and Free, 2019; Martínez-Núñez and Riquelme, 2015), highlight the role of EVs in fungal morphogenesis.

Fungal EVs also transport intracellular proteins to the extracellular space. The consistent presence of intracellular proteins across all conditions indicates that N. crassa EVs serve as a route for unconventional secretion, potentially involving moonlighting proteins – multifunctional molecules whose activity depends on context or localization (Piffer et al., 2021). Moonlighting proteins have been observed in diverse fungi (Curtis et al., 2023; Dasari et al., 2019; Satala et al., 2021). These proteins are typically involved in core metabolic processes such as glycolysis, the TCA cycle, protein synthesis, or chaperone activity (Arvizu-Rubio et al., 2022). Of note, ribosomal proteins were abundant in S16 but declined in S24 and are nearly absent in S48 (Medina-Castellanos et al., 2023). Ribosomal proteins have also been detected in EVs of several fungi, including C. albicans (Gil-Bona et al., 2015), Paracoccidioides brasiliensis (Longo et al., 2014), Cryptococcus neoformans (Rodrigues et al., 2008), C. glabrata (Kulig et al., 2022), and Fusarium oxysporum (Bleackley et al., 2020). While their extracellular role remains unclear, they may act as moonlighting proteins. For instance, in Aspergillus glaucus, ribosomal protein RPL44 is linked to osmotic stress resistance (Liang et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2014). Ribosomal proteins have also been identified in mammalian and insect extracellular environments, particularly in exomeres and supermeres (Levenbook et al., 1976; Tosar et al., 2022; Tosar et al., 2020). In humans, about one-third of the ribosomal proteins are EV-associated, especially in cancer cells with dysregulated growth (Ochkasova et al., 2023). This suggests that extracellular ribosomal proteins could be linked to active cell growth. However, little is known about the role of extracellular ribosomal proteins in fungi, pointing to the need for further investigation.

Similar to ribosomal proteins, mitochondrial proteins or fragments of them can be exported via EVs (Miliotis et al., 2019) or through MDVs (Todkar et al., 2021). Although the mechanisms of MDVs' biogenesis are not fully understood, in human cells MDVs may be incorporated into MVBs and released outside the cell (Heyn et al., 2023). Tábara et al. (2024) reported that MDV scission requires endocytic-related protein sorting nexin-9 (SNX9), and a fission event driven by the mitochondrial Rho GTPases MIRO1/2, followed by dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1)-mediated membrane separation. A BLAST search with human SNX9 (Q9Y5X1) revealed two candidates in N. crassa: VPS-5 (NCU04137) (23 % identity, 56 % coverage) and SNX-3 (NCU01914) (26.4 % identity, 19 % coverage). These results suggest a potential role for sorting nexins in MDV formation in N. crassa, though further studies are required.

The type of EVs released by cells may indicate the underlying biogenesis pathway. Fungal EVs are generally grouped into three main types by analogy to mammalian EVs: (1) microvesicles, which bud directly from the plasma membrane and carry cytoplasmic content; (2) exosomes, released via MVBs; and (3) apoptotic bodies, which contain residual cellular material. Additional EVs subtypes, such as MDVs, and NVEPs, such as exomeres, may also be present, but mechanisms of EPS formation in fungi remain poorly understood.

Proteins such as YPT-1 (NCU08477 and YPT-3 (NCU01523), involved in exocytosis and vesicle trafficking (Segev, 2001; Sánchez-León et al., 2015) were detected in S16 and G16, but not at 48 h of growth (Medina-Castellanos et al., 2023). Other trafficking-related proteins identified include kinesin (vesicle trafficking), Sec13/31 (COPII components and vesicle releasing), Sec14/17 (lipid transport), and Erp1 (cargo selection) (D'Souza et al., 2023; Melero et al., 2022; Bhattacharya et al., 2012; Holič et al., 2021; Springer et al., 2000).

Additionally, VPS proteins were identified. VPS-10 (NCU02669), involved in vacuolar protein sorting, and VPS-1 (NCU04100), required for Golgi-endosome transport and ESCRT interaction (Hayden et al., 2013), were consistently detected. ESCRT components are central to MVB formation and EV cargo selection (Schmidt and Teis, 2012; Zarnowski et al., 2021). These proteins may serve as future EV markers, especially when considering culture time.

Similar vesicle-associated proteins have been found in other fungal EVs. For example, S. cerevisiae EVs contain Vps4, Vps5, and ESCRT component Vps24 (Zhao et al., 2019). In Botritis cinerea, several vesicle-related proteins were identified as markers: coat proteins (BcSec26 and Bcin02g05260), ESCRT proteins (BcVps4, BcDid2, and BcSnf7), and several SEC proteins (De Vallée et al., 2023). In mammalian systems, tetraspanins CD9, CD63, and CD81, and mitofilin are common EV markers (Kowal et al., 2016; Lischnig et al., 2022). A mitofilin homolog, MICOS complex subunit MIC60 (NCU00894), was identified in N. crassa S16 and G16 EVs, suggesting it may serve as a potential fungal EV marker as well.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the diversity of EVs cargo proteins in early-stage Neurospora crassa cultures highlights culture time as a critical factor in understanding EVs function and suggests the involvement of distinct secretion and biogenesis pathways. Our findings also reinforce the role of EVs as a mechanism for the unconventional secretion mechanism typically intracellular proteins, such as mitochondrial and ribosomal components, hinting at previously unrecognized functions for these proteins outside the fungal cell. Together, these insights pave the way for future studies to understand the extracellular roles of ribosomal and mitochondrial proteins and support the presence of EV subtypes and NVEPs such as supermeres, exomeres, or MDVs in fungi.
