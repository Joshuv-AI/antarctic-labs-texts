Scedosporium apiospermum endocarditis in an immunocompetent patient: a case report and literature review
Authors: Jingjing Zhang, Wenqing Sun, Lei Li, Dandan Liu, Qingbin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScedosporium apiospermum endocarditis is an exceptionally rare condition, predominantly observed in immunocompromised individuals or those undergoing invasive interventions such as central venous catheterization or prosthetic cardiac valve implantation. Its occurrence in immunocompetent adults with native valves is even more uncommon. This case represents the first documented instance in China.Case summaryThis study presents the first documented case of Scedosporium apiospermum endocarditis in an immunocompetent male patient in China.ConclusionS. apiospermum endocarditis carries a grave prognosis. Among 18 reviewed articals, 19 patients succumbed to the infection, 7 of whom were diagnosed postmortem, precluding therapeutic intervention. Only one survivor was reported following implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) extraction and voriconazole treatment. These findings underscore the insidious onset, diagnostic challenges, frequent therapeutic delays, and extremely high mortality associated with this infection.Keywords: Scedosporium apiospermum, Endocarditis, Mitral valve vegetation, Case report

Background

S. apiospermum, a filamentous fungus, is the most clinically significant pathogen within the Scedosporium genus. Ubiquitous in soil, sewage, and polluted water [1], it is primarily recognized for causing respiratory infections via spore inhalation and ranks as the second most frequently isolated fungus in cystic fibrosis patients after Aspergillus fumigatus [2]. Although capable of dissemination, S. apiospermum rarely causes infective endocarditis, with native valve involvement being exceptionally uncommon. To date, no cases of S. apiospermum endocarditis have been reported in China. In this case report we presented a confirmed case of S. apiospermum endocarditis treated at our institution, analyzed its clinical course, and conducted a literature review to enhance awareness of this rare disease.

Case presentation

A 44-year-old male, with no history of HIV infection, solid organ transplantation, use of corticosteroids/immunosuppressants, or history of drug abuse, had fallen into a sewage four months prior to admission, resulting in aspiration pneumonia that required over 60 days of treatment at another medical institution. Although successfully weaned from mechanical ventilation, he was left with persistent lower back pain, left lower limb discomfort, and right eye visual impairment.

He was admitted for management of lower back and left lower limb pain. Lumbar imaging revealed L3/4 and L4/5 disc herniations with corresponding dural sac compression and reduced effective sagittal diameter of the spinal canal. The cardiac ultrasound examination revealed no significant abnormalities. Thoracic CT showed scattered patchy and nodular hyperdense lesions in both lungs. Two days after admission, he underwent lumbar spine surgery. Postoperatively, his pain symptoms improved, and lesion cultures were negative. On postoperative day (POD) 12, the patient developed incoherent speech, restlessness, and somnolence, progressing to deterioration of consciousness. Neuroimaging revealed an acute cerebral infarction. By POD 16, he developed recurrent chills and high-grade fever (up to 40 °C), prompting initiation of intracranial pressure reduction, anticoagulation, and antibacterial therapy. On POD 30, he manifested symptoms of acute left heart failure, including chest tightness and dyspnea. Chest CT demonstrated pulmonary edema and bilateral pleural effusion (Figure 1), necessitating diuretic therapy. As the patient’s condition deteriorated, he was transferred to the ICU.

Chest CT demonstrated pulmonary edema and bilateral pleural effusion

Upon ICU admission, the patient was comatose with irregular Cheyne-Stokes respirations and elevated blood pressure of 156/98 mmHg. Neurological examination revealed anisocoria, with the left pupil measuring 2 mm in diameter and demonstrating a sluggish light reflex. The right eye exhibited proptosis with bullous corneal edema, obscured pupil (covered by a white membranous structure), anterior chamber collapse, and absent light reflex (Fig. 2). Cardiac auscultation detected a grade 4/6 pansystolic murmur, most prominent at the apical region.

A: Ophthalmic examination demonstrated bullous corneal elevation and a white membranous opacity over the pupillary area. B: MRI revealed abnormal morphology and heterogenous signal intensity in the right eyeball

His lab values displayed (1,3)-β-D-glucan was elevated at 488.43pg/mL, with PCT reaching 12.130ng/mL. Hematological tests revealed WBC of 31.75 × 109/L, Hb of 86 g/L, and NEU% of 89.2%. Cardiac biomarker test showed cTnI of 0.310 µg/L and NT-proBNP of 21,635pg/mL. Renal impairment was evident with Scr 190µmol/L. The patient’s intracranial pressure was markedly increased (300 mmH2O), with CSF analysis showing elevated total protein (8,021 mg/L) and albumin (5,280 mg/L), alongside hypoglycorrhachia (glucose 1 mmol/L). Blood cultures obtained from the right upper and left lower extremities were inoculated onto Sabouraud dextrose agar and incubated at 37 °C. After 4 days, white cotton-like colonies with black centers developed. The culture morphology on blood agar was similar. Lactophenol cotton blue staining revealed septate hyphae with variably sized conidiophores terminating in ovoid conidia (Fig. 3), morphologically consistent with Scedosporium apiospermum. Repeat blood cultures confirmed the identification.

A: Blood culture demonstrating filamentous fungal colonies exhibiting white aerial mycelium with central dark pigmentation. B: Microscopy (lactophenol cotton blue preparation) showed sepate hyphae and branched conidiophores producing ovoid conidia at their apices

His Echocardiography (Fig. 4) demonstrated a 1.03 × 1.06 cm vegetation on the mitral valve with severe regurgitation. Cranial CT (Fig. 5) revealed right ocular structural abnormalities with heterogeneous signals, along with hypodense areas in the right cerebellum, occipital lobe, basal ganglia, thalamus, frontoparietal lobe, and corona radiata, with water-density shadows in some parts, consistent with multifocal cerebral infarctions.

Transthoraic echocardiography performed post-ICU admission demonstrated mitral valve vegetations

Neuroimaging reveals right-sided lesions affecting multiple territories: cerebellum occipital lobe, basal ganglia, thalamus, frontoparietal lobe, and corona radiata

Based on the 2023 Duke-ISCVID criteria, the patient was diagnosed with S. apiospermum endocarditis complicated by septicemia, acute heart failure, multiple cerebral infarctions, meningitis, and acute kidney injury. The patient underwent endotracheal intubation for airway protection and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). Antifungal therapy with voriconazole (6 mg/kg twice daily initially, then reduced to 4 mg/kg twice daily) and liposomal amphotericin B (200 mg daily) was initiated, along with mannitol (25 g every 8 h). Surgical intervention was deemed contraindicated due to high risk. On ICU day 3, the patient experienced blood pressure drop, necessitating norepinephrine infusion. By day 4, cardiopulmonary collapse occurred and required resuscitation, after which sinus tachycardia (120 bpm) was restored without return of spontaneous respiration. Mannitol was increased to 50 g every 8 h. Neurological imaging could not be performed due to instable condition of the patient. Serial chest radiographs demonstrated progressive cardiomegaly (Fig. 6) with escalating vasopressor requirements, reflecting worsening shock. The patient’s clinical condition deteriorated progressively, culminating in death from septic shock on the 14th day of ICU admission.

Discussion and conclusions

Scedosporium infections predominantly affect immunocompromised individuals, including solid organ transplant recipients and patients with malignancies, though immunocompetent hosts may also become infected, typically following surgical procedures or traumatic events. Scedosporium spp. can either spread locally to adjacent tissues or disseminate hematogenously to distant organs. In a retrospective study by Danila Seidel et al. [3], which included 264 patients (208 with Scedosporium infections), 47 cases involved ocular infections, of which 6 exhibited invasion into adjacent sinuses or the central nervous system (CNS).

Cardiac involvement by S. apiospermum is uncommon. A systematic review of English-language literature from 1977 to 2025 using PubMed, with search terms including “Scedosporium apiospermum” and “endocarditis,” along with secondary references from retrieved articles, identified only 18 articals reporting a total of 19 reported cases of S. apiospermum endocarditis (Table 1) [4–21]. Among these cases, 7 patients had undergone solid organ transplantation, 1 had received a bone marrow transplant, 7 had undergone intravascular invasive procedures (e.g., valvular surgery, pacemaker implantation, or catheter placement), 1 had acquired immunodeficiency, and another had a history of intravenous drug abuse. Nearly all cases with disseminated infection involving commonly reported sites (brain, lungs, eyes, joints, skin) as well as rarer locations (liver, kidneys, thyroid, pleura, spleen). Notably, 7 patients were diagnosed only postmortem via autopsy or delayed blood culture/pathological confirmation, underscoring the diagnostic challenges, rapid progression, and high lethality of this infection.Only one patient survived—a 58-year-old African American male who developed fever and hypotension 2 weeks after implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) placement.Echocardiography revealed vegetations on the ICD leads, tricuspid valve, and right atrium, prompting surgical debridement and complete lead removal. Tissue cultures identified S. apiospermum, and voriconazole therapy was initiated.

Voriconazole remains the first-line therapeutic agent for Scedosporium infections, with multiple studies confirming its in vitro activity against this pathogen [22, 23]. Micafungin has also demonstrated some antifungal activity against Scedosporium in laboratory studies. A 2013 investigation revealed that miltefosine exhibits in vitro activity against Scedosporium and shows synergistic effects when combined with voriconazole or posaconazole [24]. However, clinical outcomes remain suboptimal even with voriconazole therapy, particularly in disseminated Scedosporium infections. For localized infections, surgical intervention combined with antifungal therapy is recommended [25], with several successful cases documented [26]. A 2020 retrospective study established a significant correlation between surgical management and improved clinical outcomes [27].

The patient developed persistent visual impairment in the right eye following a near-drowning incident in a sewage treatment pool, which resulted in toxic gas inhalation.We hypothesize this may represent either sinonasal-ocular transmission of S. apiospermum spores or direct ocular inoculation during the accident. His intact immune system initially contained the infection, preventing dissemination. Postoperatively, the patient exhibited progressive encephalopathy, recurrent fever, and respiratory distress. Two separate blood cultures (> 12-hour interval) confirmed S. apiospermum infection, and transthoracic echocardiography identified mitral valve vegetation with severe regurgitation, fulfillment of both the microbiological and imaging major criteria of the 2023 Duke-ISCVID criteria [28]. Confirming the diagnosis of S. apiospermum endocarditis. His neuroimaging demonstrated multiple hypodense lesions, and the CSF analysis is suggestive of meningitis despite a negative microbial culture, potentially indicating CNS involvement—a recognized neurotropic propensity of Scedosporium per the 2014 ESCMID/ECMM guidelines, which document frequent CNS complications including cerebral abscesses and meningoencephalitis [25].

In summary, disseminated S. apiospermum infections, particularly S. apiospermum endocarditis, present significant diagnostic challenges, characterized by low incidence, poor pathogen culture yield, and a typically fatal outcome. Our findings underscore the importance of early pathogen culture and PCR testing in individuals with relevant exposure histories, such as near-drowning. While voriconazole remains the first-line antifungal agent, its limited efficacy supports consideration of combination regimens with echinocandins, miltefosine, or other agents. In cases of localized infection, surgical intervention combined with antifungal therapy should be evaluated whenever feasible.

Limitations

Despite the positive culture for S. apiospermum, neither susceptibility testing nor further molecular, sequencing, or mass spectrometry-based identification was performed.
