Frailty, physiological reserve, and surgical resilience: toward a dynamic stress–recovery model
Authors: Rafal Cudnik, Mirko Barone, Luigi Marano, Simone Gargarella, Ruslan Duka, Robert Molchanov, Sergii Girnyi, Tomasz Cwalinski, Mahmoud Al-Balas, Yogesh Vashist, Lorenzo Cobianchi, Silvia Malerba, Francesco Paolo Prete, Mario Testini, Valentin Calu, Archil Aladashvili, Kamal Mahawar, Arda Isik, Virginia Boccardi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronological age remains widely used in perioperative decision-making but incompletely captures the biological heterogeneity that influences postoperative outcomes. Frailty has substantially advanced surgical risk assessment by shifting attention from age toward multidimensional vulnerability and diminished physiological reserve. Nevertheless, patients with similar frailty profiles often experience markedly different recovery trajectories, suggesting that baseline vulnerability alone may not fully explain postoperative adaptation.MethodsThis narrative conceptual review was developed through a targeted search of PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Google Scholar, focusing on frailty, physiological reserve, physical resilience, intrinsic capacity, prehabilitation, and postoperative recovery. Peer-reviewed clinical studies, reviews, and conceptual frameworks from geroscience and perioperative medicine were synthesized to develop an integrated model of surgical resilience.ResultsEmerging evidence indicates that frailty and resilience represent related but distinct constructs. Frailty primarily identifies vulnerability before stress exposure, whereas resilience reflects the dynamic capacity to withstand, adapt to, and recover from physiological perturbation. Recent perioperative studies suggest that postoperative outcomes are influenced not only by baseline reserve but also by the interaction between reserve, surgical stress exposure, and recovery capacity. Within this framework, skeletal muscle function, nutritional status, cognition, inflammatory burden, and functional independence appear to contribute to an individual’s adaptive potential. Surgery represents a uniquely informative human model through which stress–response biology and recovery trajectories can be studied longitudinally.ConclusionsWe propose surgical physiological resilience as a dynamic stress–recovery phenotype emerging from the interaction among three interconnected domains: baseline reserve, surgical stress load, and recovery capacity. This framework extends current frailty-based approaches by incorporating longitudinal adaptation and recoverability after surgical stress. Reframing perioperative medicine around resilience may support trajectory-based risk stratification, targeted prehabilitation strategies, longitudinal functional monitoring, and the future development of a dynamic Surgical Resilience Index capable of capturing postoperative heterogeneity more accurately than chronological age or static vulnerability measures alone.

Introduction

Chronological age remains deeply embedded in perioperative decision-making because it is readily available, intuitively interpretable, and historically associated with adverse postoperative outcomes [1]. However, contemporary surgical practice increasingly highlights the limitations of age as a surrogate for biological vulnerability. Older adults of similar chronological age frequently experience markedly different postoperative trajectories, ranging from rapid recovery to prolonged hospitalization, major complications, institutionalization, or loss of autonomy [2–8]. These observations suggest that tolerance to surgical stress depends less on chronological age itself than on the physiological reserve with which an individual enters surgery. Over the past two decades, frailty has fundamentally reshaped perioperative medicine by shifting attention from chronological age toward multidimensional biological vulnerability [9–11]. Whether conceptualized as a physical phenotype or as an accumulation of health deficits, frailty reflects impaired homeostatic reserve, multisystem dysregulation, and increased susceptibility to stressors [1, 12–14]. Across surgical populations, frailty consistently predicts postoperative morbidity, prolonged hospitalization, institutionalization, and mortality, often independently of age and conventional comorbidity indices [3, 5, 6, 15, 16]. Consequently, frailty assessment has become a cornerstone of contemporary perioperative risk stratification.

Nevertheless, clinical experience and emerging evidence indicate that frailty alone does not fully explain postoperative adaptation. Patients with similar frailty profiles often experience markedly different recovery trajectories, suggesting that baseline vulnerability incompletely captures the biological processes governing recovery after surgical stress [17–19]. In a multicenter cohort of 223 adults undergoing elective abdominal surgery, frailty independently predicted prolonged hospitalization (adjusted OR 3.12, 95% CI 1.72–5.67), whereas chronological age lost independent explanatory value after multivariable adjustment. Moreover, substantial discordance between chronological age and frailty status was observed: 58% of patients aged ≥ 70 years were classified as fit or pre-frail, whereas 7.7% of younger individuals already fulfilled frailty criteria [3]. These findings reinforce the concept that operative risk is more accurately described by multidimensional biological reserve than by chronological age alone.

Among the determinants of physiological reserve, skeletal muscle function has emerged as a particularly informative marker [20–22]. Beyond its locomotor role, skeletal muscle functions as a metabolically active organ involved in inflammatory regulation, mitochondrial homeostasis, glucose metabolism, and systemic adaptation to stress [23, 24]. Handgrip strength (HGS), owing to its simplicity and reproducibility, has become an accessible surrogate of integrated physiological reserve. Reduced preoperative HGS has consistently been associated with prolonged hospitalization and adverse postoperative outcomes [25–27]. Marano et al. demonstrated that baseline HGS independently predicted postoperative length of stay beyond chronological age [8], while more recent multicenter studies combining frailty and muscle strength identified distinct reserve phenotypes associated with different recovery trajectories [3, 5–7].

These observations naturally lead to the concept of physiological resilience. Whereas physiological reserve reflects the biological capacity available before stress exposure, resilience describes the dynamic ability to withstand, adapt to, and recover following physiological perturbation [28]. Frailty and resilience are therefore related but distinct constructs: frailty primarily characterizes vulnerability before stress exposure, whereas resilience reflects the trajectory of recovery after disruption of homeostasis [29, 30]. Accordingly, the absence of frailty does not necessarily imply preserved resilience, just as frail individuals may retain meaningful adaptive capacity despite baseline deficits.

This distinction is particularly relevant in perioperative medicine because surgical outcomes emerge from the interaction between baseline reserve, the magnitude of surgical stress, and the efficiency of recovery over time [31, 32]. Surgery represents a uniquely informative human model of physiological stress, characterized by a well-defined onset, measurable biological perturbation, and an observable recovery phase [33].

In parallel, evolving concepts such as physical resilience and the World Health Organization framework of intrinsic capacity increasingly emphasize functional adaptation and recovery rather than deficit accumulation alone [28, 30, 34, 35].

Against this background, we propose that surgical physiological resilience should be interpreted as a dynamic stress–recovery phenotype arising from the interaction among three interconnected domains: baseline reserve, surgical stress load, and recovery capacity. Baseline reserve encompasses frailty status, muscle strength, nutritional integrity, cognition, comorbidity burden, and functional independence [30]. Surgical stress load includes operative complexity, oncological burden, intraoperative events, and postoperative complications [32], whereas recovery capacity reflects the speed and quality of functional restoration following surgery [36]. Rather than replacing frailty, this framework extends current perioperative thinking by incorporating the longitudinal biology of recovery after surgical stress. Accordingly, the objective of this narrative review is to synthesize current evidence on frailty, physiological reserve, and resilience, and to propose an integrated dynamic stress–recovery framework for understanding postoperative adaptation.

Methods

This article was conceived as a narrative and conceptual review examining the relationship between frailty, physiological reserve, intrinsic capacity, and surgical resilience in perioperative medicine. The aim was to integrate current biological and clinical concepts into a stress–recovery framework for interpreting postoperative adaptation. A targeted literature search was conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Google Scholar. English-language publications from 2000 to March 2026 were preferentially considered, with earlier landmark studies included when conceptually relevant. Search terms included “frailty”, “physiological reserve”, “physical resilience”, “surgical resilience”, “intrinsic capacity”, “stress response”, “geroscience”, “prehabilitation”, “postoperative recovery”, “muscle strength”, “handgrip strength”, and “perioperative medicine”. Additional studies were identified through reference screening and citation tracking of influential reviews, consensus papers, and highly cited original investigations. Because this was a narrative conceptual review, no formal systematic review methodology was applied. Studies were selected according to conceptual relevance, methodological quality, and contribution to understanding baseline vulnerability, adaptive reserve, surgical stress, and postoperative recovery. Grey literature was not systematically searched, as the review prioritized peer-reviewed evidence to ensure methodological consistency and scientific rigor. The final synthesis integrates evidence from aging biology, geriatric medicine, resilience science, exercise physiology, and perioperative research to propose a dynamic model of surgical physiological resilience centered on baseline reserve, surgical stress load, and recovery capacity.

Frailty as the foundation, not the endpoint

The incorporation of frailty into perioperative medicine represented a major conceptual shift away from traditional age-centered surgical assessment. For decades, chronological age served as the dominant proxy for operative vulnerability because it was simple to quantify, universally available, and consistently associated with adverse outcomes [37]. Yet patients of similar chronological age frequently demonstrate markedly different responses to surgical stress, postoperative recovery trajectories, and probabilities of returning to baseline function [3, 38]. Frailty emerged not merely as an additional risk factor, but as an attempt to operationalize the biological heterogeneity underlying aging itself.

Two principal frameworks shaped contemporary frailty research. The phenotype model proposed by Fried et al. defines frailty through physical characteristics, including weakness, slowness, exhaustion, weight loss, and reduced activity, interpreted as manifestations of impaired physiological reserve and reduced stress tolerance [39]. In parallel, the deficit accumulation model developed by Rockwood et al. conceptualizes frailty as the cumulative burden of multidimensional deficits spanning medical, cognitive, psychological, and functional domains [40]. Although grounded in different theoretical assumptions, both frameworks capture overlapping dimensions of vulnerability and consistently demonstrate prognostic relevance across medical and surgical populations.

Within surgery, frailty evolved from a geriatric research construct into a clinically meaningful component of perioperative assessment. Multiple observational studies and meta-analyses associated frailty with postoperative complications, prolonged hospitalization, institutionalization, readmission, loss of functional independence, and mortality across surgical specialties [3–8, 41]. Makary et al. demonstrated that frailty independently predicted postoperative complications, length of stay, and discharge to assisted-care facilities even after adjustment for age and comorbidity burden [22]. Subsequent analyses by Hewitt et al. reinforced the prognostic significance of frailty across both elective and emergency surgical settings [42].

Beyond risk prediction, frailty contributed to a broader transformation within perioperative medicine. Surgical decision-making progressively expanded beyond chronological thresholds to incorporate mobility, cognition, nutrition, muscle performance, social vulnerability, and baseline autonomy [43, 44]. This transition facilitated the integration of geriatric co-management programs, multidimensional perioperative assessment pathways, frailty screening initiatives, and prehabilitation strategies into contemporary surgical care [6, 7, 45].

At the same time, the widespread incorporation of frailty assessment exposed important conceptual limitations. Patients within similar frailty categories frequently exhibit markedly different biological profiles, adaptive capacities, and postoperative trajectories. Such heterogeneity suggests that frailty incompletely characterizes the biological processes governing postoperative adaptation [3, 14, 18].

This limitation becomes particularly evident when recovery is examined longitudinally rather than through isolated perioperative endpoints [3, 5–8]. Frailty assessment is typically performed before surgery and interpreted as a static descriptor of vulnerability. Surgery, however, represents a major physiological perturbation involving inflammatory activation, endocrine stress responses, metabolic disruption, immobilization, and catabolic burden [17, 46, 47]. Postoperative outcomes are therefore shaped not only by baseline reserve, but also by the efficiency with which physiological systems restore equilibrium after perturbation [48]. Consequently, patients with comparable frailty scores may experience substantially different recovery kinetics and probabilities of regaining baseline autonomy.

Recent perioperative investigations support this interpretation. In multicenter abdominal surgery cohorts, postoperative recovery clustered more closely according to multidimensional reserve phenotypes integrating frailty and handgrip strength than according to chronological age alone [3, 5–8]. Frailty independently predicted prolonged hospitalization and postoperative morbidity, whereas chronological age showed limited discriminatory value after multivariable adjustment. These findings suggest that operative vulnerability cannot be fully represented through a single static classification, but instead emerges from the interaction between baseline reserve, stress exposure, and adaptive recovery processes.

Another unresolved issue concerns the biological interpretation of frailty itself. Frailty is frequently described as diminished resilience; however, frailty and resilience may not represent opposite ends of a single continuum [28, 30]. Frailty primarily reflects vulnerability before stress exposure, whereas resilience more accurately describes the capacity to maintain or restore functional equilibrium after perturbation [49]. Some frail individuals retain substantial adaptive capacity despite baseline deficits, whereas some apparently non-frail patients experience disproportionate deterioration after operative stress [30]. These observations suggest that vulnerability and recovery capacity, although interconnected, constitute partially distinct biological domains.

The growing interest in intrinsic capacity and physical resilience further supports this transition beyond exclusively deficit-centered models. The World Health Organization framework of intrinsic capacity emphasizes preserved physical and mental abilities rather than deficit accumulation alone, potentially complementing frailty assessment by focusing on functional reserve and adaptive potential [50]. Likewise, Whitson et al. proposed that physical resilience should be interpreted as the ability to resist or recover from functional decline after health stressors, emphasizing longitudinal trajectories rather than resting-state vulnerability alone [28].

Importantly, none of these considerations diminish the transformative contribution of frailty to perioperative medicine. Frailty fundamentally reshaped surgical care by challenging simplistic age-based assumptions and introducing multidimensional vulnerability into perioperative decision-making. Nonetheless, emerging evidence increasingly suggests that understanding postoperative adaptation requires a broader framework integrating baseline reserve, physiological perturbation, and longitudinal recovery trajectories within the same conceptual model [51]. In this sense, frailty may represent not the endpoint of perioperative geriatric assessment, but the foundation upon which future models of surgical resilience can be developed.

Physiological reserve beyond chronological age

One of the central challenges in perioperative medicine is the marked heterogeneity observed among patients of similar chronological age. Older adults undergoing comparable surgical procedures frequently experience profoundly different postoperative trajectories [17, 52]. Some maintain functional independence and recover rapidly despite advanced age and comorbidity burden, whereas others develop prolonged hospitalization, major complications, persistent disability, or loss of autonomy after apparently routine interventions. Such variability suggests that chronological age alone inadequately reflects the biological processes governing vulnerability and recovery after surgical stress [53, 54].

This discrepancy progressively shifted attention toward the concept of physiological reserve. Broadly defined, physiological reserve refers to the latent capacity of biological systems to maintain or restore homeostasis under stress conditions [28, 30, 34]. Unlike chronological age, reserve emerges from the integrated function of multiple domains, including skeletal muscle performance, nutritional status, inflammatory regulation, metabolic flexibility, cognition, autonomic balance, and functional independence [55]. Decline across these systems does not occur uniformly, meaning that individuals of identical age may possess markedly different capacities to tolerate physiological perturbation.

Frailty represented one of the earliest attempts to operationalize diminished reserve. By shifting perioperative assessment away from chronological thresholds toward multidimensional vulnerability, frailty substantially transformed geriatric surgery and perioperative medicine. Boccardi et al. previously emphasized that chronological age alone insufficiently captures operative vulnerability and that frailty assessment may better identify patients at risk for adverse postoperative outcomes [11]. Yet physiological reserve likely extends beyond frailty classification alone. Patients meeting similar frailty criteria frequently differ in muscle strength, inflammatory burden, nutritional integrity, cognition, mobility, and postoperative recovery potential, suggesting that frailty incompletely captures the dynamic processes underlying stress adaptation and recovery [30, 34, 48].

Within this framework, skeletal muscle function emerged as a particularly informative surrogate of reserve. Skeletal muscle is increasingly recognized not only as a locomotor organ, but also as a metabolically active tissue involved in inflammatory signaling, glucose homeostasis, mitochondrial regulation, and systemic adaptation to stress [24, 56, 57]. Several perioperative investigations associated reduced muscle strength with prolonged hospitalization, postoperative complications, delayed mobilization, institutionalization, and mortality across surgical populations [58–60].

Handgrip strength gained particular attention because it combines biological relevance with practical feasibility. In abdominal surgery, Marano et al. demonstrated that reduced preoperative HGS independently predicted prolonged postoperative hospitalization, supporting muscle strength as a clinically meaningful indicator of physiological reserve rather than merely a marker of aging itself [8].

More recent multicenter analyses refined this concept by integrating frailty and muscle strength into combined reserve phenotypes. In a cohort of 223 adults undergoing elective abdominal surgery, postoperative length of stay clustered more closely according to multidimensional reserve profiles than according to chronological age alone. Fit–Strong individuals experienced the shortest hospitalization, whereas Frail–Weak patients demonstrated the longest and most heterogeneous recovery trajectories. Frailty independently predicted prolonged hospitalization, while chronological age lost explanatory value after multivariable adjustment [3]. Similarly, the prospective multicenter study by Montanari et al. demonstrated substantial discordance between chronological age and frailty classification, further supporting operative vulnerability as a multidimensional functional continuum rather than an age-defined construct [6].

Importantly, physiological reserve should not be interpreted as a purely physical construct. Nutritional integrity, chronic inflammation, cognition, emotional state, comorbidity burden, and baseline autonomy likely contribute substantially to the organism’s ability to absorb and recover from surgical stress [61, 62]. Physiological reserve therefore appears to emerge from the interaction of multiple biological systems rather than from any isolated biomarker alone.

Recent geriatric frameworks further support this multidimensional interpretation. The World Health Organization concept of intrinsic capacity emphasizes the composite of an individual’s physical and mental abilities rather than deficit accumulation alone [63]. Likewise, Whitson et al. described physical resilience as the ability to resist or recover from functional decline following stressors, emphasizing that resilience is inherently dynamic and best interpreted longitudinally rather than through static baseline assessment alone [28].

This distinction may be particularly relevant in perioperative medicine. Physiological reserve describes the capacity available before stress exposure, whereas physiological resilience reflects the adaptive response unfolding after stress has occurred [28, 30, 34]. A patient may enter surgery with apparently preserved reserve yet demonstrate poor recovery kinetics after operative stress, while another individual classified as vulnerable at baseline may recover more efficiently than anticipated [64]. Reserve and resilience therefore appear closely related, yet not interchangeable constructs.

In this context, surgery offers a uniquely informative translational model through which reserve and resilience can be investigated longitudinally. By integrating frailty assessment, muscle performance, intraoperative stress exposure, and postoperative recovery trajectories, perioperative medicine may provide one of the most clinically accessible frameworks through which adaptive physiology and resilience biology can be prospectively studied. Defining surgical physiological resilience

Despite growing interest in resilience across geriatric medicine, rehabilitation, and aging biology, the concept remains inconsistently defined within perioperative care. In clinical practice, resilience is often interpreted as the capacity to “bounce back” after stress [23, 57, 65]. While intuitively appealing, such definitions may oversimplify a biologically complex phenomenon involving interactions among physiological reserve, stress-response systems, recovery kinetics, and functional reintegration over time. In surgical patients, resilience may be more appropriately understood as the ability to preserve, restore, or reorganize functional equilibrium after operative stress [61].

This distinction is particularly relevant because surgery represents a uniquely intense physiological perturbation. Tissue injury, inflammatory activation, neuroendocrine stress responses, metabolic disruption, immobilization, pain, and nutritional imbalance collectively challenge multiple biological systems simultaneously [66]. Consequently, postoperative recovery depends not only on the severity of the insult itself, but also on the efficiency with which physiological systems coordinate adaptive responses and progressively re-establish homeostasis [33, 67]. Resilience may therefore be better interpreted as a dynamic systems property rather than a fixed patient characteristic.

Recent aging literature increasingly supports this interpretation. Whitson et al. described physical resilience as the ability to resist or recover from functional decline after health stressors, emphasizing that resilience should be evaluated longitudinally and in relation to a defined perturbation [28]. Importantly, resilience does not simply imply the absence of vulnerability. Older adults with apparently similar baseline characteristics frequently demonstrate divergent trajectories after comparable physiological insults, suggesting that adaptive recovery mechanisms may operate at least partially independently from baseline frailty burden [30, 68].

From a biological perspective, resilience likely depends on coordinated interactions among inflammatory pathways, endocrine signaling, mitochondrial energetics, neuromuscular integrity, immune competence, autonomic regulation, nutritional reserve, microbiome composition, and cognitive-functional adaptation [17, 69, 70]. Aging may impair these adaptive mechanisms through chronic inflammation, anabolic resistance, sarcopenia, mitochondrial dysfunction, and reduced metabolic flexibility [71]. Yet the extent of such alterations remains highly heterogeneous among individuals, potentially explaining variability in postoperative recovery trajectories.

Within perioperative medicine, resilience may therefore be conceptualized not as a binary attribute, but as a stress–recovery trajectory. Some patients experience limited physiological perturbation followed by rapid restoration of baseline function, whereas others demonstrate delayed recovery, persistent impairment, or progressive decline despite technically successful surgery [33]. These patterns may represent distinct resilience phenotypes rather than merely different complication profiles.

This perspective highlights an important limitation of conventional perioperative assessment models. Traditional frailty tools primarily estimate vulnerability before stress exposure [39, 40]. Postoperative outcomes, however, are influenced not only by baseline reserve, but also by the efficiency with which biological systems adapt after perturbation has occurred. Patients with comparable frailty scores may therefore exhibit markedly different inflammatory responses, mobilization patterns, muscle strength trajectories, and probabilities of regaining functional independence.

Recent perioperative investigations from our research group support this interpretation. In multicenter abdominal surgery cohorts, postoperative recovery patterns clustered more closely according to integrated reserve phenotypes combining frailty and handgrip strength than according to chronological age alone [5–7]. Similarly, analyses of perioperative muscle dynamics suggested that isolated measurements of acute postoperative handgrip decline provide limited explanatory value unless interpreted within the broader context of baseline reserve and surgical stress exposure [3, 8].

Accordingly, surgical physiological resilience may be defined as the dynamic capacity to preserve, restore, or reorganize functional reserve after exposure to surgical stress [72]. Importantly, this definition extends beyond the concept of resistance alone. Some individuals may recover through rapid restoration of preoperative equilibrium, whereas others may achieve functional adaptation despite incomplete recovery of baseline status. Resilience should therefore not necessarily be interpreted as the absence of decline, but rather as the capacity to maintain meaningful functional recovery despite physiological perturbation.

Equally important, resilience should not be interpreted as the opposite of frailty. Frailty primarily describes baseline vulnerability, whereas resilience reflects the adaptive trajectory unfolding after stress exposure. These constructs are interconnected but not interchangeable. A frail individual may still retain substantial adaptive capacity, while an apparently fit patient may experience disproportionate physiological deterioration after operative stress (Fig. 1).

From a translational standpoint, surgery offers one of the most clinically accessible human models through which resilience biology can be prospectively investigated. By integrating serial functional measurements, muscle strength trajectories, inflammatory responses, mobilization patterns, and recovery outcomes, perioperative medicine may move beyond static vulnerability assessment toward a physiology-centered understanding of adaptation and recovery after surgical stress.

A dynamic stress–recovery framework for surgical resilience

The growing recognition of resilience as a dynamic biological phenomenon raises a central question for perioperative medicine: how should surgical resilience be operationally conceptualized? Existing perioperative models largely rely on static preoperative risk estimation centered on chronological age, comorbidity burden, or frailty status [46, 73]. Although clinically valuable, such approaches incompletely capture the dynamic physiology through which postoperative trajectories emerge. Surgical outcomes are shaped not only by baseline vulnerability, but also by the interaction between preexisting reserve, magnitude of physiological perturbation, and the capacity to adapt after disruption of homeostasis has occurred.

From this perspective, surgical physiological resilience may be interpreted as an emergent property arising from the interaction among three interconnected domains: baseline reserve, surgical stress load, and recovery capacity. Rather than functioning independently, these domains interact continuously across the perioperative trajectory, collectively shaping the organism’s response to operative stress (Fig. 2).

Baseline reserve

Baseline reserve represents the biological substrate upon which surgical stress acts [74, 75]. It encompasses multidimensional factors reflecting the integrity of physiological systems before perturbation, including frailty status, skeletal muscle performance, nutritional condition, inflammatory burden, cognition, autonomic regulation, metabolic flexibility, comorbidity complexity, and functional independence [76]. Reserve should therefore not be interpreted simply as the absence of deficits, but rather as the latent capacity of biological systems to mobilize adaptive responses under stress conditions.

Within this framework, frailty and handgrip strength may be viewed as complementary rather than competing constructs. Frailty captures multidimensional vulnerability, whereas muscle strength may provide a more direct estimate of anabolic reserve and physiological adaptability. Our multicenter investigations support this interpretation, demonstrating that integrated reserve phenotypes combining frailty and handgrip strength identify markedly different postoperative trajectories despite overlapping chronological age distributions.

At a mechanistic level, diminished reserve may involve impaired mitochondrial energetics, chronic low-grade inflammation, anabolic resistance, autonomic dysregulation, endocrine alterations, and reduced metabolic adaptability [77]. Importantly, these alterations progress heterogeneously across individuals, potentially explaining why some older adults tolerate major surgery remarkably well whereas others experience disproportionate deterioration after limited operative stress.

Surgical stress load

The second domain involves the magnitude and complexity of the physiological perturbation itself. Surgery constitutes a highly integrated stressor combining tissue injury, inflammatory activation, neuroendocrine responses, blood loss, catabolic stimulation, immobilization, pain, nutritional disruption, and psychological stress [32, 33]. Importantly, the biological impact of surgery extends beyond technical complexity alone. Procedures of similar duration may generate profoundly different systemic consequences depending on postoperative complications, oncologic burden, ICU requirement, infectious events, or failure of early mobilization [78].

Stress load may therefore include operative complexity, extent of tissue dissection, duration of surgery, intraoperative blood loss, anesthesia-related burden, perioperative pain, postoperative complications, prolonged immobilization, and cancer-associated systemic stress. From a surgical perspective, factors such as emergency versus elective setting, minimally invasive versus open approach, adequacy of source control, anastomotic integrity, and lymphadenectomy extent further modulate this burden. The interaction between reserve and perturbation intensity appears particularly important. A physiological insult that remains clinically manageable for one patient may exceed adaptive reserve in another individual with reduced biological flexibility, particularly in the context of frailty, sarcopenia, or impaired organ reserve.

Recovery capacity

The third domain, recovery capacity, represents the adaptive trajectory unfolding after stress exposure. Traditional perioperative models emphasize preoperative risk estimation and early postoperative endpoints, whereas recovery itself remains comparatively underexplored as a biological process [54].

Recovery capacity may encompass the ability to restore, or reorganize, functional equilibrium after physiological disruption [79]. Importantly, recovery should not necessarily be interpreted as complete return to baseline. Some individuals rapidly regain preoperative function, whereas others experience delayed recovery, persistent impairment, or progressive decline despite technically successful surgery. These trajectories may represent distinct resilience phenotypes rather than merely different complication profiles.

Recent perioperative findings support this interpretation. In longitudinal analyses of perioperative muscle dynamics, isolated measurements of acute postoperative handgrip decline provided limited explanatory value when interpreted independently from baseline reserve and surgical context. Such observations suggest that resilience cannot be adequately characterized through isolated postoperative measurements alone, but instead requires longitudinal evaluation of recovery kinetics over time [27, 80, 81].

Serial functional trajectories may therefore become more informative than static assessments. Recovery velocity, restoration of mobility, regain of muscle strength, nutritional recovery, discharge disposition, and medium-term functional reintegration may collectively reflect the efficiency with which physiological systems re-establish adaptive equilibrium after perturbation. Emerging technologies, including wearable monitoring, digital biomarkers, and continuous mobility tracking, may further expand the ability to characterize recovery dynamically in future perioperative models [82].

Surgical resilience as an emergent phenotype

Within this framework, surgical resilience should not be viewed as a fixed baseline trait nor as the simple opposite of frailty. Rather, resilience emerges dynamically from the interaction between reserve, perturbation, and adaptive recovery [83]. This distinction is critical because it implies that resilience cannot be fully inferred from preoperative vulnerability alone.

Several resilience phenotypes may hypothetically emerge after surgical stress exposure, including stable resilience, recovering resilience, persistent vulnerability, and hidden vulnerability [84]. Although currently theoretical, such phenotypes may provide a useful framework for interpreting postoperative heterogeneity longitudinally rather than categorically.

From a translational standpoint, perioperative medicine may offer one of the most clinically accessible human models through which resilience biology can be prospectively investigated. By integrating preoperative reserve markers, intraoperative stress exposure, and longitudinal recovery trajectories, perioperative research may move toward a more dynamic understanding of human adaptation to surgical stress.

Ultimately, such a framework could support the future development of trajectory-based resilience phenotyping, longitudinal physiological monitoring, and potentially a dynamic Surgical Resilience Index integrating reserve, perturbation, and recovery over time.

Clinical and translational perspectives on surgical resilience

Adopting resilience as a dynamic perioperative construct may substantially reshape both clinical practice and translational research. Importantly, this perspective does not replace frailty assessment, but rather extends it toward a more longitudinal interpretation of surgical adaptation and recovery. In this framework, perioperative evaluation moves beyond identifying vulnerability before surgery toward understanding how biological systems respond after physiological perturbation has occurred.

One immediate implication concerns preoperative assessment itself. Current perioperative pathways primarily estimate operative risk through chronological age, comorbidity burden, and static frailty classification [26, 46]. Although clinically valuable, these approaches may incompletely characterize adaptive potential. Patients with similar frailty profiles frequently demonstrate markedly different capacities for inflammatory regulation, mobilization, functional recovery, and postoperative reintegration [85]. A resilience-oriented framework may therefore encourage clinicians to evaluate not only vulnerability, but also recoverability through integration of muscle performance, mobility, nutritional reserve, cognition, inflammatory status, and longitudinal functional trajectories.

This perspective may also alter how postoperative outcomes are conceptualized. Conventional surgical research frequently relies on binary endpoints such as complications, mortality, or readmission. While clinically important, these measures incompletely reflect the broader biological processes governing recovery after stress exposure. Patients surviving the same complication may experience profoundly different trajectories in mobility, cognition, autonomy, and return to baseline function [86]. A resilience-based framework may therefore encourage greater emphasis on recovery kinetics, restoration of function, and medium-term reintegration rather than isolated perioperative events alone.

From a translational standpoint, perioperative medicine may increasingly evolve toward trajectory-based and personalized care. Rather than viewing recovery as a uniform process, clinicians may begin to recognize distinct postoperative adaptation patterns characterized by rapid restoration, delayed recovery, persistent vulnerability, or progressive decline, with individualized risk stratification and tailored interventions guiding perioperative decision-making. Such trajectories could potentially be identified through longitudinal assessment of physical performance, inflammatory dynamics, mobility, nutritional recovery, and patient-reported outcomes [87]. This approach aligns closely with broader developments in geroscience and systems biology, where dynamic responses to stressors are increasingly considered more informative than resting-state measurements alone [3–8, 88, 89].

The concept of resilience may also reshape prehabilitation and postoperative recovery pathways. Current prehabilitation strategies largely aim to optimize operative fitness through exercise, nutritional support, and multimodal conditioning before surgery [90]. Reframing these interventions through the lens of resilience shifts attention from reducing frailty alone toward enhancing adaptive recovery capacity. Similarly, postoperative interventions such as early mobilization, nutritional support, pain control, sleep preservation, and delirium prevention may influence not only complication rates, but also the efficiency with which adaptive recovery mechanisms are restored [48].

Emerging technologies may further expand the ability to characterize resilience longitudinally. Wearable sensors, digital biomarkers, remote physiological monitoring, and patient-generated health data may allow increasingly precise assessment of postoperative recovery beyond the hospital setting. Parameters such as gait variability, activity recovery, sleep quality, heart rate variability, and recovery velocity could potentially provide dynamic insight into adaptive physiology after surgery [82].

Artificial intelligence and longitudinal data modeling may become particularly relevant within this framework. Recovery after surgery rarely follows linear trajectories, and conventional statistical approaches may inadequately capture the complexity of adaptive responses over time. Trajectory analysis, longitudinal clustering, and machine-learning approaches may eventually help identify distinct resilience phenotypes and predict individualized recovery patterns after operative stress [91].

More broadly, adopting resilience as a perioperative framework may fundamentally alter how operative candidacy itself is interpreted in aging populations. Frailty assessment is often perceived primarily as a tool for identifying risk and limiting intervention in vulnerable individuals. A resilience-oriented approach, by contrast, encourages a more dynamic interpretation centered not only on vulnerability, but also on adaptive potential and recoverability.

Ultimately, integrating resilience into perioperative medicine may support a transition from static risk stratification toward a physiology-centered model of surgical care. Rather than focusing exclusively on whether a patient is frail before surgery, future perioperative pathways may increasingly seek to understand how biological systems respond to stress, how recovery trajectories evolve longitudinally, and which mechanisms distinguish adaptive recovery from persistent physiological decline. In this sense, resilience may represent not simply a new terminology within perioperative geriatrics, but a broader attempt to redefine surgical recovery through the dynamic biology of human adaptation.

Current challenges and limitations

Although surgical physiological resilience is conceptually attractive, it remains an emerging framework rather than a validated clinical instrument. Several limitations should therefore be acknowledged. First, no validated biomarker or biomarker panel currently defines surgical resilience, and candidate domains such as muscle strength, inflammation, nutrition, autonomic function, and metabolic flexibility remain insufficiently standardized. Second, resilience is inherently longitudinal and can only be fully interpreted after exposure to surgical stress, making it more difficult to measure prospectively than baseline frailty. Third, optimal outcome measures remain uncertain, since conventional endpoints such as complications, mortality, readmission, or length of stay may not adequately capture recovery capacity. Functional recovery, mobility, cognition, patient-reported outcomes, and return to baseline independence may be more informative but require further validation. Finally, resilience models may not be generalizable across surgical specialties, operative settings, and stress loads, and their implementation in routine practice would require feasible, reproducible, and cost-effective assessment pathways. Accordingly, surgical physiological resilience should currently be viewed as a promising conceptual framework that complements, rather than replaces, established frailty assessment. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether resilience-based models provide incremental prognostic value beyond existing perioperative risk tools.

Conclusion

Perioperative medicine is progressively moving beyond chronological age and static vulnerability models toward a more dynamic understanding of surgical risk and recovery. Frailty has been fundamental in this transition, but postoperative outcomes cannot be fully explained by baseline frailty alone. Surgical physiological resilience offers a complementary framework by integrating baseline reserve, surgical stress load, and recovery capacity into a longitudinal stress–recovery model. In this perspective, resilience does not replace frailty; rather, it extends frailty-based assessment by emphasizing adaptation, recoverability, and functional reintegration after surgery. Future prospective studies are needed to validate resilience phenotypes, identify clinically meaningful recovery trajectories, and determine whether resilience-based models provide incremental prognostic value beyond established frailty assessments.
