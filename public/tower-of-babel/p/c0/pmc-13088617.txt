Epidemiological characteristics of patients hospitalized with severe complicated influenza in Taiwan from 2010 to 2020: a retrospective study
Authors: Yu-Huei Wei, Fu-Huang Lin, Chi-Jeng Hsieh, Yu-Ching Chou, Chia-Peng Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Influenza epidemics and pandemics are causes of morbidity and mortality. We explored the epidemiological differences and trends of domestic and imported severe complicated influenza cases in Taiwan from 2010 to 2020. To reinforce the efficiency of surveillance, the Taiwan Centers for Disease Control (TCDC) has established a surveillance system, called the Taiwan National Notifiable Disease Statistics System (TNNDSS). We created a trends study from the TNNDSS. A total of 14,844 domestic and 63 imported severe complicated influenza cases were reported. The number of reported cases and influenza positivity rate were the lowest in 2020. The incidence of severe complicated influenza was highest in 2019, ranging from 19 to 99 per 1,000,000 individuals from 2010 to 2020. In total, 1672 deaths were reported (case fatality rate, 11.2%). Most cases were in men (57.3%), were in those aged > 60 years (55.9%), occurred during winter (46.4%), and were in those residing in Northern Taiwan (46.9%). Comparing the gender, age, season and place of residence of the cases each year is statistically significant (p = 0.002, < 0.001, < 0.001, < 0.001, respectively). Furyhermore, comparing the total number of cases in the gender from 2010 to 2020, there are significant differences in the age groups (p < 0.001). In addition, comparing the total number of cases in each age group from 2010 to 2020, there are significant differences in the season and residency (p < 0.001, < 0.001, respectively). Influenza A virus subtype H1N1 was the dominant virus strain, and China was the most common country of origin for the imported cases.

Introduction

Influenza is an acute viral respiratory disorder caused by the influenza virus [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), annual influenza epidemics, which occur globally, result in nearly 1 billion infections, of which 3–5 million develop a serious illness and approximately 290,000–650,000 die. In the 20th century, influenza pandemics occurred in 1918, 1957, and 1968; in the 21st century, only one influenza pandemic (in 2009) has been recorded thus far [2]. Of these pandemics, the one that occurred in 1918–1919 was the most catastrophic; it affected approximately one-third of the world population (i.e., about 500 million individuals) and led to approximately 50 million deaths [3–5]. However, this death toll may have been considerably underestimated; it may have been as high as 100 million [5].

The influenza virus is divided into four types: influenza A, B, C, and D [6]. Influenza A virus is the only influenza virus that has caused influenza pandemics thus far. Most influenza A pandemics have occurred when newer genotypes of influenza A virus, which could both infect and spread efficiently among humans, emerged. Influenza A virus is subclassified on the basis of the composition of its surface glycoproteins [hemagglutinin (H) and neuraminidase (N)] [7]. Influenza B virus, which was first isolated in 1940, infects only humans and has no animal reservoirs [8]. Influenza C virus infection typically causes mild illness, but it may not cause human epidemics. Influenza D virus primarily affects cattle; it may not infect or cause illness in human individuals [9]. Of all influenza virus types, only influenza A virus—mainly the A(H3N2) and A(H1N1) subtypes—and influenza B virus—mainly the B/Victoria and B/Yamagata strains—cause seasonal epidemics [10].

Influenza is mainly transmitted through droplets that are produced by an infected person when they cough or sneeze. Because influenza virus particles can survive for a short time on the surfaces of objects, influenza may also be transmitted through contact with these objects [11, 12]. Although humans are the most infectious hosts of viruses that cause seasonal influenza, several influenza A virus subtypes originate in the animal hosts such as mammals other than humans (mainly pigs) and aquatic birds (e.g., ducks). In these hosts, these viruses may undergo genetic recombination into new influenza virus subtypes and then proliferate and spread among humans, thereby causing influenza pandemics [13, 14].

Clinically, influenza virus infection may result in colds, laryngitis, bronchitis, viral pneumonia, and other similar acute respiratory diseases. The influenza virus incubation period is typically 1–4 days (average, 2 days); complications appear 1–2 weeks after the onset [15]. The main symptoms after influenza infection are fever, headache, muscle ache, fatigue, runny nose, sore throat, and cough. Some patients may demonstrate symptoms such as diarrhea and vomiting; however, they typically resolve within 1 week of their onset. During annual epidemics, approximately 10% of patients with influenza demonstrate gastrointestinal symptoms, such as nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea, which accompany the typical respiratory symptoms [15]. Most patients with influenza recover without treatment; however, few may develop severe complications—namely pneumonia, encephalitis, myocarditis, and other serious secondary infections or neurological diseases [16]. Influenza research and prevention are clinically important because influenza can cause rapid outbreaks, spread extensively, and lead to severe complications (specifically bacterial and viral pneumonia). During an outbreak, severe illness and death are more common in older patients (aged > 65 years), infants, pregnant women, immunocompromised individuals, and high-risk individuals with chronic diseases (e.g., asthma; diabetes; cardiovascular disease; as well as disorders of the lungs, liver, and kidney) or a body mass index of ≥ 30 kg/m2 [17]. Regular influenza vaccination is the most effective method for the prevention of influenza-related complications [18].

Taiwan (23°4′N, 121°0′E) is a developed country with a subtropical climate (monthly average temperature, 16–29 °C; monthly average relative humidity, 75%–90%; per capita gross domestic product, US$35,244) [19]. In Taiwan, most severe complicated influenza cases are domestic in origin; nevertheless, a few imported cases have been noted [20]. The current preventive measures may not be completely effective in reducing influenza or at least limiting its spread. Most confirmed cases and clusters of severe complicated influenza in Taiwan are sporadic. However, big data–based epidemiological data on severe complicated influenza risk in Taiwan are limited. Since 1999, Taiwan has established a monitoring and reporting network for severe influenza patients through collaboration between academic institutions and medical institutions, providing key data for the study of severe influenza to monitor influenza virus infection patterns and epidemiological and demographic trends. In this study, we used data from the Taiwan National Notifiable Disease Statistics System (TNNDSS) to explore the epidemiological differences and trends of domestic and imported severe complicated influenza cases in Taiwan from 2010 to 2020.

Materials and methods

Ethical policy

Because we used appropriately anonymized data from a freely available open dataset, which is in the public domain, the requirement for ethical approval was waived. The current methodology conforms to the appropriate public use of government reports [21].

Definition of reported and confirmed cases and epidemiological investigation

All cases of hospitalization for severe complicated influenza are nationally notifiable in Taiwan. To reinforce the efficiency of surveillance, the Taiwan Centers for Disease Control (TCDC) has established a surveillance system, called the TNNDSS [22], across Taiwan. Cases are defined as individuals hospitalized for a community-onset, laboratory-confirmed influenza infection [23]. In Taiwan, the Communicable Disease Control Act requires that all suspected cases of severe complicated influenza be reported and that the required specimens of these cases be collected and sent to the TCDC through the TNNDSS. Case patients are admitted to a surveillance-area medical center during the influenza season and within 14 days having a positive influenza test. Respiratory secretions are collected through throat swabs, nasopharyngeal aspirates, or bronchoalveolar lavages. Laboratory confirmation of influenza is performed through viral culture, immunofluorescence staining, or reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) or by using a commercially available rapid diagnostic test [24]. A confirmed case with severe complicated influenza is defined as a case of influenza with the evidence of pneumonia, neurologic symptoms, myopericarditis or an invasive bacterial infection as well as any confirmatory test results for influenza. Here, we identified confirmed cases of severe complicated influenza with illness onset between January 2010 and December 2020 from the TNNDSS. We then extracted their data, including time of illness onset, influenza RT-PCR result, demographics, preexisting medical conditions leading to increased influenza complication risks, and in-hospital mortality [24].

Data source

We used the TNNDSS, a public database established by the TCDC, as our data source. The TNNDSS database includes data on five communicable disease categories listed in the Communicable Disease Control Act. The database is updated with the latest epidemiological data each morning; these data include latest charts (e.g., run charts), reports (in the XLSX format), distributions (county, city, or township), and information regarding different disease types. As such, TNNDSS data provide epidemiological information that is transparent and up-to-date. To ensure information security and privacy, the public TNNDSS database includes only secondary data (i.e., notification date, onset date, confirmation date, and the number of confirmed domestic and imported influenza cases) but no case details (e.g., medical history, signs, symptoms, and laboratory data). Incidence rates used population data which source were from Taiwan Ministry of the Interior statistical database [25].

Data analysis

Here, we used a retrospective design to enumerate confirmed cases of severe complicated influenza from 2010 to 2020 and examine the distribution of and differences in their epidemiological characteristics (i.e., sex, age, season of onset, and area of residence). Descriptive data are presented as means and summaries, where appropriate. We compared categorical variables by using the chi-square test.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 21; Asia Analytics Taiwan, Taipei, Taiwan). All statistical tests were two-sided with α = 0.05. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Epidemiological features

In total, 29,753 suspected severe complicated influenza cases were identified from the TNNDSS data from January 2010 to December 2020; of them, 14,907 were confirmed cases (average positivity rate, 50.1%; Figs. 1 and 2). Next, we compared the epidemiological features (i.e., sex, age, season of onset, and area of residence) of these confirmed severe complicated influenza (Table 1). The domestic and imported cases demonstrated significant differences. Table 2 demonstrates the sex differences in age, season of onset, and area of residence among the confirmed cases. Among the confirmed cases, 4745 men and 3594 women were aged ≥ 60 years; as such, age and sex demonstrated a significant association (P < 0.001). Moreover, 3944 and 2973 confirmed cases in winter were in men and women, respectively, whereas 3942 and 3054 cases were in men and women, respectively, residing in northern Taiwan. Table 3 demonstrates the age differences in season of onset and area of residence among the confirmed cases. In winter, 654, 826, 2017, and 3420 cases were in those aged < 20, 20–39, 40–59, and ≥ 60 years, respectively; as such, we noted a significant disparity between age and season of onset (P < 0.001). Moreover, 530, 802, 1856, and 3808 cases were in those residing in northern Taiwan aged < 20, 20–39, 40–59, and ≥ 60 years, respectively; as such, we noted a significant disparity between age and area of residence (P < 0.001). The imported cases originated in various countries across all five continents (Fig. 3); in particular, China was the most frequent country of origin.

Incidence and case fatality rate

Incidence (per 1,000,000 individuals) peaked in 2019 during the study period (Fig. 4A). Cases in men and those aged ≥ 60 years had the highest incidence (Fig. 4B and C). Winter was noted to be the main season of severe complicated influenza epidemic (Fig. 4D). Incidence was highest for cases in those residing in eastern Taiwan throughout the study period, except in 2016 (Fig. 4E). In 2010, 2011, 2012, 2013, 2014, 2015, 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019, and 2020, we noted 21, 46, 67, 22, 67, 60, 221, 140, 93, 184, and 76 deaths, respectively, among the cases in men and 24, 34, 84, 27, 76, 89, 226, 211, 150, 231, and 89 deaths, respectively, among the cases in those aged ≥ 60 years. The male, female, and ≥ 60-year-old patients demonstrated the fatality rate in 2020 were 26.9%, 29.8%, and 34.0%, respectively; Table 4).

Discussion

From 2010 to 2020, the TCDC recorded 14,907 confirmed severe complicated influenza cases, most of which were caused by influenza A virus subtype H1N1; moreover, 1672 influenza-related deaths (case fatality rate, 11.2%) occurred during the study period. Although previous literature has reported on influenza trends in Taiwan from 2003 to 2023 [26], this study focuses on the detailed demographic description and viral distribution of influenza patients. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study investigating the incidence, fatality rate, and virus characteristics in severe complicated influenza cases recorded in Taiwan from 2010 to 2020. According to the 2015 publicly funded influenza vaccination data, the vaccination rate of high-risk chronic disease patients was 9.6% in Taiwan. Moreover, approximately 95% of the confirmed severe complicated influenza cases reported from 2015 to 2016 were in those not vaccinated against influenza in 2015, and approximately 70.5% of the cases were in patients with a history of chronic disease (metabolic disease, 45.6%; cardiovascular disease, 29.5%; kidney disease, 17.4%; chronic lung disease, 16.2%; and liver disease, 9.1%) [27].

Sex affects the outcomes of various viral respiratory infection—ranging from infections of respiratory syncytial virus to those of influenza viruses and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 [28, 29]. In particular, sex is associated with differences in clinical presentation and complications of influenza A virus infection. In general, both younger and older men are more susceptible to severe respiratory viral infection outcomes [30]. A study indicated that among 514 influenza A–positive patients (51.2% women), the male patients had a higher intensive care unit (ICU) admission rate (9.6% vs. 4.6%, P = 0.03) [31]. Similarly, among our 8538 influenza A–positive cases (57.3% men), the male patients had a higher ICU admission rate.

Individuals aged ≥ 65 years have a higher risk of severe complicated influenza than do younger, healthier adults—partly because of the immune system weakens with age [32]. Influenza seasons vary in severity; however, during most seasons, individuals aged ≥ 65 years represent the highest proportion of severe influenza cases. For instance, 70%–85% of deaths and 50%–70% of hospitalizations related to seasonal influenza have occurred in individuals aged ≥ 65 years [33].

Influenza can cause seasonal epidemics in a cyclical pattern. Because Taiwan is located in both the tropics and subtropics, influenza cases occur all year round; nevertheless, the highest numbers of cases are noted in fall and winter. The case number starts increasing gradually at the beginning of November and then peaks from December to March of the following year. Approximately 14% of individuals seek medical attention for pneumonia or influenza annually [34]. In addition, the number of weekly emergency visits for influenza-like symptoms peaks (n = 26,000–62,000) during the epidemic period but then decreases (n = 10,000) after the epidemic period [35]. The proportion of emergency visits for influenza-like symptoms ranges from 16% to 26%. The extension of the epidemic period has led to an increase in the cumulative number of severe complicated influenza cases. In terms of season of onset, the numbers of severe influenza and severe complicated influenza cases reach their peak in fall and winter [36, 37]. Our results corroborate this observation. Therefore, season-specific influenza prevention and treatment strategies might aid in avoiding influenza-related serious complications or death.

Severe influenza infection is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally [38]. A study indicated that the influenza-associated incidence rate of outpatient acute respiratory illness was the highest in 2017–2018 (16.9%) and the lowest in 2019–2020 (4.8%) [39]. Similarly, we observed that The spread of other major respiratory infections (e.g., COVID-19) was followed by the reduction in severe complicated influenza incidence; during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, the case numbers and incidence of other respiratory infectious diseases (e.g., measles or German measles) also decreased. In a study on influenza surveillance activities in Australia in 2011–2018 [40], adults aged ≥ 65 years and those with comorbidities demonstrated increased influenza-associated hospitalization and mortality rates. Similarly, we noted that the fatality rate of severe complicated influenza was the highest among those aged ≥ 60 years (34.0%).

In general, influenza easily spreads in areas with a low temperature and a large number of travelers. Air transport–related services are thus a major source of the transmission of diseased of zoonotic origin (e.g., influenza); moreover, lively airports are hotbeds for pathogens (e.g., viruses and bacteria). To maintain effective epidemic control, the Taiwan government have a responsibility to provide necessary information to travelers arriving from disease hotspots and establish the relevant control measures.

These differences may be due to differences in the diagnostic methods or reagents used. Because the signs and symptoms of COVID-19 and influenza are similar, the COVID-19 pandemic may have negatively affected the enumeration of influenza cases and positivity rates in 2020. Therefore, strengthening surveillance for the predominant virus strains is essential. A study focused on identifying the clinical features and mortality associated with severe influenza A or B virus infection in patients admitted to the ICU. Similarly, we noted that the incidence of influenza B virus infections peaked in 2012 and 2018.

This study has two limitation. First, the TNNDSS only provides the basic epidemiological features of patients with infectious diseases, such as influenza, in its public domain. Therefore, we could not observe differences or trends related to the patients’ clinical symptoms. Second, the TNNDSS does not provide data related to the genotype or antigenic structure of the influenza virus. As such, we could not compare the genetic association between influenza viruses in Taiwan and those in other countries. The main strength of this study included its research design, which incorporates pathogen and demographic data, as well as national-level surveillance data with consistently high quality, and the application of big data in disease epidemiology control and prevention.

In conclusion, this study revealed the epidemiological characteristics and trends of domestic and imported severe complicated influenza cases in Taiwan from 2010 to 2020. The highest incidence of severe complicated influenza was noted in 2019 (98.5 per 1,000,000 individuals). Areas with the highest incidence were noted in easeern Taiwan. The current results may aid Taiwanese policymakers and clinical experts in developing strategies for the direct prevention and control of severe complicated influenza. They may also facilitate further influenza surveillance and research in Taiwan.
