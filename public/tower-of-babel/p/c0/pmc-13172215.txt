PHC2 as a novel predictive biomarker and therapeutic target for cisplatin resistance in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Xue Pan, Xuejiao Sun, Yufei Xing, Zengli Zhang, Minhua Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the highest global mortality among all malignancies, lung cancer remains a critical concern for public health, burdening both clinical management and healthcare delivery systems.Currently, platinum-based combination chemotherapy remains the standard first-line treatment option for many lung cancer patients.Nevertheless, the emergence of intrinsic or acquired resistance to platinum agents poses a major challenge in clinical management, highlighting the urgent need to discover and characterize genetic determinants of resistance.MethodsUsing CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing technology in conjunction with a Human Epigenetic Library, we performed a genome-wide screen to identify novel genes associated with cisplatin resistance in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) cell lines. We further explored the biological function of PHC2 by analyzing The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database and validating its role through extensive in vitro functional assays.ResultsNotably, high-throughput sequencing and cisplatin resistance assays identified PHC2 as a novel gene implicated in cisplatin resistance. Functional experiments, including cell proliferation assays, wound healing assays, and apoptosis analyses, demonstrated that PHC2 substantially influences LUAD cell proliferation, migration, and apoptosis. Immunohistochemistry revealed a correlation between PHC2 expression levels and smoking history in LUAD tissue samples. However, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis indicated no statistically significant association between PHC2 expression and overall survival among LUAD patients. Mining of the TCGA database showed that in LUAD tumors, the PHC2 mRNA expression was significantly elevated relative to nearby noncancerous tissues and exhibited a significant link with lymph node metastasis and smoking history. Interestingly, immune infiltration analysis revealed that elevated PHC2 expression correlated with increased infiltration of macrophages, natural killer cells, dendritic cells, immature DCs, neutrophils, eosinophils, and mast cells. RNA sequencing analysis suggested that PHC2 may contribute to cisplatin resistance through the epigenetic reactivation and transcriptional upregulation of genes associated with the ABC transporter family, axon guidance pathways, and complement/coagulation cascades.ConclusionCollectively, these findings suggest that PHC2 could serve as a predictive biomarker for cisplatin resistance and represent a promising therapeutic target for treating cisplatin resistance in LUAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04920-6.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the malignancy with the highest global incidence, accounting for 11.4% of all newly diagnosed cancers [1, 2]. According to Cancer Statistics 2025, in the United States, 2,041,910 new cancer cases and 618,120 cancer-related deaths are estimated. Of these, approximately 85% (106,150) of lung cancer deaths in 2025 (124,730) are anticipated to result directly from cigarette smoking, with an additional 3,500 deaths attributed to second-hand smoke exposure and 15,100 to the combined effects of other combustible tobacco products [3]. The consistently elevated rate of lung cancer occurrence and related deaths highlight its substantial burden on global public health [4]. Around 85% of all lung cancer diagnoses are classified as non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [5]. Advances in targeted therapies and immunotherapies have markedly improved outcomes for patients with NSCLC [4, 6]. In the absence of actionable driver mutations, treatment strategies for NSCLC employ a multimodal approach that includes chemotherapy, immunotherapy, surgery, radiation therapy, and the use of antibody-drug conjugates, as well as emerging modalities such as cellular therapies, T-cell engagers, cancer vaccines, and external devices [7–10]. Among chemotherapeutic agents, cisplatin remains a cornerstone of first-line treatment; however, resistance to cisplatin represents a major challenge limiting therapeutic success [11, 12].

This study aimed to identify genes associated with cisplatin resistance in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) using CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing technology combined with a Human Epigenetic Knockout Library. Through this screening, we identified polyhomeotic homolog 2 (PHC2) as a novel gene linked to cisplatin resistance in LUAD. Further investigations were conducted to elucidate the biological function of PHC2 using cellular functional assays, immunohistochemical analysis of LUAD tissue samples, and bioinformatics analyses based on The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data to uncover its biological significance and potential mechanisms of action in LUAD.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and compounds

The cell lines A549 LUAD and HEK293T were sourced from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures. An A549 cisplatin-resistant cell line, A549/DDP, was purchased from Shanghai Yuchun Biological Science and Technology Co., Ltd. Both cell lines were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) with 10% FBS (Biological Industries, BI). To maintain their resistance phenotype, A549/DDP cells were continuously cultured in the presence of 2 µg/mL cisplatin (Selleck, USA; CAT#: S1166). All cell lines were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Amplification of the human epigenetic knockout library

The pooled Human Epigenetic Knockout Library was acquired from Addgene. Electrocompetent cells (Weidi Biotechnology, Shanghai; CAT#: DE1080S), stored at -80 °C, were thawed before use. A total of 50 ng of the sgRNA library (Addgene, USA; Human Epigenetic Knockout Library, CAT#: 162256) was mixed with 1 µL of pUC19 plasmid (10 pg/µL; Weidi Biotechnology, Shanghai; CAT#: DE1080S) and 25 µL of electrocompetent cells. Electroporation was performed using a Bio-Rad electroporator under the conditions of 25 µF capacitance, 200 Ω resistance, and 1.8 kV voltage. After electroporation, 1 mL of sterile S.O.C. medium was added immediately, and the mixture was incubated at 30 °C and 250 rpm for 90 min to allow recovery. For library titration, 100 µL of serially diluted mixtures containing the sgRNA library and pUC19 plasmid were plated onto ampicillin-resistant LB agar plates (10 cm dishes; 100 µg/mL working concentration), inverted, and incubated at 37 °C for 20–24 h. The remaining resuscitated cells were transferred into a liquid LB medium containing ampicillin and cultured at 37 °C with continuous shaking overnight.

Subsequently, plasmid DNA was isolated using the EndoFree Maxi Plasmid Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing; CAT#: DP117). To evaluate library amplification, PCR was conducted using the following primers:

Forward Primer (F): ACTCTTTCCCTACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCT

Reverse Primer (R): GACTGGAGTTCAGACGTGTGCTCTTCCGATCT

(Primers were synthesized by Suzhou GENEWIZ Biotechnology Co., Ltd., with 5–8 bp random bases appended to the specific sequences.) Real-time PCR amplification utilized the Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity 2X Master Mix (NEB, USA; CAT#: M0494X). The PCR products were subsequently subjected to next-generation sequencing (NGS) to assess the abundance distribution of sgRNAs and evaluate the coverage and uniformity of the amplified library.

Lentiviral packaging and cell infection

A stable, genome-scale knockout A549/DDP cell line was generated via lentiviral infection, with puromycin (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai; CAT#: A610593-0025) used for post-transduction selection. For lentivirus production, 13.6 µg of the library plasmid, 6.8 µg of psPAX2, and 3.4 µg of pMD2.G were combined with 195 µL of polyethylenimine (PEI; Polysciences, USA; CAT#: 24756) and transfected into HEK293T cells at the logarithmic growth phase. Viral supernatants were filtered using membranes (0.45 μm) 48 h after transfection and stored at -80 °C for later use. To quantify viral titer, HEK293T cells were seeded at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and transduced with varying volumes (0, 5, 10, 20, 40, and 60 µL) of sgRNA-expressing lentivirus in the presence of polybrene (Beyotime, China; CAT#: C0351). Upon reaching confluence, cells were divided into two groups: one subjected to 2 µg/mL puromycin selection and the other left untreated as a control. Once cells in the 0 µL control wells were completely eliminated, the number of viable cells in each condition was counted. For infection experiments, a total of 3.6 × 107 A549/DDP cells were transduced with 3.6 × 107 IFUs of lentivirus, covering 2508 target genes with 8 sgRNAs per gene plus 50 non-targeting controls. The infection was conducted at a multiplicity of infections of 1, aiming for an infection efficiency of approximately 30%. Cells were seeded into 15 cm culture dishes at 4 × 106 cells per dish alongside uninfected controls. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM containing 2 µg/mL puromycin, and selection was continued until the complete death of the uninfected control group. The surviving cells were subsequently expanded and cryopreserved for downstream experiments.

Cisplatin treatment and sample preparation

After the pooled cell library was established, cells were divided into control and cisplatin treatment groups (20 µM; Selleck, USA; CAT#: S1166). Each group was seeded 4 × 106 cells. Twenty-four hours post-seeding, the experimental group received 20 µM cisplatin treatment for a duration of one week, while the control group remained untreated. Following the treatment period, approximately 5 × 107 cells were harvested from each group. Cell pellets from both the control and cisplatin-treated groups were stored at -80 °C and subsequently submitted to Suzhou GENEWIZ Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for whole-genome sequencing analysis.

Western blotting

Cells were collected into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes and lysed using RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors to isolate total cellular proteins. Protein concentrations were measured to ensure equal amounts were loaded per lane. Samples were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. At room temperature, the membrane was blocked by skim milk (5%) for one hour, followed by incubating with a primary antibody targeting specific proteins at 4 °C overnight (Abcam, USA; CAT#: ab314436, dilution 1:2000). After washing with PBST, membranes were treated with an HRP-linked secondary antibody (Proteintech, USA; CAT#: SA00001-2, dilution 1:10,000) for 1 h at room temperature. Signal detection was performed using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL), with uniform application of the ECL reagent to visualize protein bands.

Half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) assay

After a 24-hour incubation in an incubator, 100 µL of cisplatin-containing medium was added to each well. The cisplatin concentrations were set as follows: 1 µM, 10 µM, 20 µM, 40 µM, 80 µM, 160 µM, 320 µM, and 640 µM. Each concentration was tested in five replicate wells. Wells treated with a PBS-containing medium served as the negative control group. After 72 h of drug exposure, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by a further 2-hour incubation at 37 °C. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was subsequently measured using a microplate reader. Cell proliferation assay

Cells from both the A549/DDP and A549/DDP sgPHC2 lines in logarithmic growth were seeded into 96-well plates with 3 × 103 cells/well. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. To assess proliferation, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well at predetermined time points (4, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h), followed by a 2-hour incubation under identical conditions. Wound healing assay

To evaluate cell migration, logarithmic phase A549/DDP and A549/DDP sgPHC2 cells were serially diluted and plated into 6-well plates with 2.5 × 105 cells/well, and three wells were assigned to each group. After an overnight incubation allowing for monolayer formation, a linear scratch was introduced across the confluent cell layer using a sterile 10 µL pipette tip, oriented perpendicular to pre-marked reference lines. Detached cells and debris were carefully removed by washing the wells three times with PBS. Microscopic images of the wound areas were taken at 0, 24, and 48 h post-scratch. The migration distances were subsequently measured and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Apoptosis assay

A549/DDP and A549/DDP sgPHC2 cells at the logarithmic growth phase were exposed to cisplatin at concentrations of 10 µM and 20 µM for 48 h. After treatment, approximately 5–10 × 104 cells were harvested, resuspended, and centrifuged at 1000×g for 5 min. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was carefully removed. Cell pellets were then processed using an apoptosis detection kit (Beyotime, China; CAT#: C1062M). In brief, 195 µL of Annexin V-FITC binding buffer was added to each pellet, followed by the addition of 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 10 µL propidium iodide (PI) staining solution. Samples were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 10–20 min, then immediately placed on ice to halt the staining reaction. Apoptotic cells were detected by flow cytometry, with Annexin V-FITC staining indicative of green fluorescence and PI staining representing red fluorescence. Flow cytometric data were subsequently analyzed using FlowJo software.

Generation of A549/DDP sgPHC2 stable low-expression LUAD cell line via CRISPR-Cas9

A stable low-expression A549/DDP sgPHC2 cell line was established using a three-plasmid lentiviral system consisting of psPAX2, pMD2.G, and pLenti-CRISPR V2 (a sgRNA-expressing plasmid carrying a puromycin resistance marker). A549/DDP cells were seeded into 6-well plates with 2.5 × 10⁶cells in each well and incubated overnight at 37 °C until approximately 40% confluency was achieved. The plasmid mixture was then transfected into the cells, and viral supernatants were collected 24 to 48 h after transfection. Following viral infection, cells underwent puromycin selection to enrich for successfully transduced populations. The selection was continued until the complete death of the non-transduced control cells was observed. Surviving cells were subsequently expanded as a polyclonal pool for use in downstream experiments.

Bioinformatics analysis

TCGA database was used to analyze the differential expression of PHC2 in tumors and matched adjacent normal tissues. Based on the median expression level, patients were stratified into low and high PHC2 expression groups. Kaplan-Meier analysis was performed to assess overall survival (OS) disparities between these two cohorts, with statistical significance evaluated via the log-rank test. To determine whether PHC2 expression served as an independent prognostic indicator, a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model was employed, adjusting for pathological and clinical variables, including age, sex, and tumor stage. Additionally, KEGG analysis was performed to explore pathways associated with PHC2 and its co-expressed genes. Significantly enriched pathways were visualized to elucidate the potential functional roles of PHC2 in the progression of LUAD.

ROC curve analysis

ROC curve analysis was conducted using the R package “pROC” to determine the cutoff value of PHC2 expression for distinguishing between tumor and normal tissues.

Immunohistochemical examination

A tissue microarray (TMA) comprising 52 pairs of primary lung cancer tissues and adjacent normal tissues (Zhuoli Biotechnology, Shanghai; CAT#: ZL-LUC1041) was utilized for analysis. IHC staining was performed using the DAKO EnVision FLEX Kit (DAKO, Denmark; CAT#: K5007). Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded LUAD sections were first deparaffinized and rehydrated through graded alcohols. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by treatment with 3% H2O2 and NaN3. Antigen retrieval was conducted using a citrate-based retrieval buffer. To minimize non-specific binding, sections were blocked with 5% BSA. The primary antibody targeting PHC2 (Abcam, USA; CAT#: ab314436) was diluted 1:30, following preliminary optimization experiments that tested dilutions of 1:20 and 1:40, and incubated with tissue sections overnight at 4°C. Subsequently, sections were treated with an HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody. Signal development was carried out using a 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogenic substrate. Finally, the sections were dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with resin-based medium. Quantitative image analysis was performed using Visiopharm software (https://visiopharm.com/) equipped with its Deep Learning (AI) module. Cell nuclei were automatically recognized, and total cell counts were obtained. The HDAB-DAB color filter was applied to segment regions of interest (ROIs) according to staining intensity. Based on intensity thresholds, cells were classified into four categories:

Negative (0): No detectable staining.

Weakly positive (1+): Faint staining.

Moderately positive (2+): Distinct but moderate staining.

Strongly positive (3+): Intense staining.

RNA-seq analysis

Volcano plots were utilized to display the global distribution of gene expression differences between A549/DDP and A549/DDP sgPHC2 cells, with each point representing the relationship between statistical significance (P-value) and log2 fold change (LFC). Genes meeting the criteria of P < 0.05 and |LFC| > 2 were identified as significantly differentially expressed. Genes exhibiting both extremely low P-values (–log10P ≥ 4) and large expression changes (|LFC|≥5) were specifically annotated. In the plots, downregulated genes (negative screening candidates) were visualized in blue, upregulated genes (positive screening candidates) in red, and genes without significant changes in gray.

To explore potential functional implications, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed and illustrated using scatter plots. The y-axis indicated enriched pathway names, while the x-axis reflected the rich factor, the proportion of genes with differential expression relative to all genes participating in each pathway. Dot size represented the number of enriched genes, and color gradients corresponded to Q-value ranges.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.0 and R Studio were used for all statistical evaluations. All statistical analyses with P < 0.05 were considered significant. Spearman’s rank correlation was employed to evaluate associations between variables. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to analyze survival-related outcomes.

Results

Amplification of the human epigenetic knockout library

The amplified plasmid library underwent reverse transcription, followed by electrophoresis to verify the presence of target fragments. Subsequently, the library was submitted for NGS to determine the distribution and abundance of individual sgRNAs. Sequencing data were used to assess both the coverage and uniformity of the library, with uniformity quantified by the skew ratio. In this study, the skew ratio was calculated to be 3.09, well within the predefined acceptable range (< 10) (Fig. 1A and B), confirming the library’s quality and its suitability for downstream functional screening applications.

Genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 screening identifies cisplatin resistance-related genes in LUAD

The half-maximal inhibitory concentration ( IC50) of cisplatin was measured for A549 and A549/DDP cells, yielding values of 9.351 ± 0.979 µM and 37.7 ± 17.9 µM, respectively (Fig. 1C). A549/DDP cells were transduced with the Human Epigenetic Knockout Library, and puromycin selection was used to enrich successfully infected populations. The selected cells were divided into two experimental groups: one exposed to 20 µM cisplatin and the other maintained as an untreated control. High-throughput sequencing was performed, and differential gene expression analysis was conducted to identify genes associated with cisplatin resistance. As cisplatin-sensitizing genes possess limited clinical relevance, the analysis emphasized genes linked to cisplatin resistance. MAGeCK software, utilizing the Robust Rank Aggregation (RRA) algorithm, was applied to prioritize differentially essential genes based on RRA scores (with lower scores indicating higher essentiality for cell survival). Notable negatively selected genes identified included MLLT10, ZNF217, ERCC6, DR1, SIRT6, ERCC5, CUL5, RB1, PHC2, and ZHX1 (Fig. 1D and E).

Identification and validation of PHC2 as a cisplatin resistance gene in LUAD

Based on the literature review, five candidate genes, ZNF217, DR1, RB1, PHC2, and ZHX1, were selected for further functional assessment. CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene knockouts were generated to establish A549/DDP stable low-expression LUAD cell lines for each target. The effect of gene silencing on cisplatin resistance was evaluated by determining the half-maximal IC50 of cisplatin. The IC50 of cisplatin for the A549/DDP sgPHC2 cells was determined to be 12.09 ± 5.473 µM. Additionally, RNA-seq data (in the format of transcripts per million, TPM) and corresponding clinical information from the TCGA-LUAD cohort were obtained through the GDC Data Portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). Preprocessed datasets were subjected to LASSO using the R package glmnet. The optimal regularization parameter (lambda.min) was identified as 0.154960917 (Fig. 1F). Among the prognostic LASSO coefficients, PHC2 emerged as the most promising candidate for subsequent experimental validation.

Exploration of the biological functions of PHC2

To elucidate the involvement of PHC2 in cisplatin resistance, Western blotting was employed to measure PHC2 protein levels in both A549/DDP parental cells and A549/DDP sgPHC2 cells. As shown in Fig. 2A, PHC2 was detectable in A549/DDP cells but was markedly reduced in the sgPHC2-modified cells. These results confirm efficient CRISPR/Cas9-mediated silencing of PHC2 and support its functional significance in regulating cisplatin resistance in LUAD. Further, cisplatin sensitivity assays revealed that the IC50 value for cisplatin in A549/DDP sgPHC2 cells was 12.09 ± 5.473 µM, significantly lower than that observed in parental A549/DDP cells (37.7 ± 17.9 µM). This marked decrease in IC50 indicates that the knockdown of PHC2 enhances cisplatin sensitivity in LUAD cells, highlighting PHC2 as a pivotal contributor to cisplatin resistance mechanisms in LUAD.

PHC2 knockdown promotes cell proliferation and enhances cisplatin sensitivity in LUAD cells

As depicted in Fig. 2B and C, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated silencing of PHC2 significantly promoted the proliferation of A549/DDP LUAD cells. By 96 h, A549/DDP sgPHC2 cells exhibited a markedly higher proliferation rate compared to the parental A549/DDP line (t = 7.155, P < 0.0001).To further investigate the involvement of PHC2 in cisplatin resistance, cells were exposed to 20 µM cisplatin. At 96 h post-treatment, proliferation of the A549/DDP sgPHC2 cells was substantially suppressed relative to untreated A549/DDP cells, with statistically significant differences observed (t = 6.512, P = 0.0002) (Fig. 2B and C). These findings indicate that PHC2 knockdown sensitizes cisplatin-resistant LUAD cells to cisplatin, supporting the role of PHC2 as a potential regulator of cisplatin resistance mechanisms in LUAD.

PHC2 knockdown impairs migration and invasion capacities in LUAD cells

As shown in Fig. 2D and E, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated depletion of PHC2 markedly impaired the migratory and invasive abilities of A549/DDP LUAD cells. At 48 h, the wound closure rate in A549/DDP sgPHC2 cells was significantly reduced compared with that of parental A549/DDP cells (t = 8.619, P = 0.0132). To further evaluate the impact of PHC2 on cisplatin resistance, both cell lines were exposed to 20 µM cisplatin. After 48 h of treatment, A549/DDP sgPHC2 cells exhibited a reduction in wound healing ability compared to A549/DDP cells, with statistically significant differences (t = 11.7, P = 0.0013) (Fig. 2D and E). Collectively, these results suggest that PHC2 knockdown diminishes the invasive capacity of cisplatin-resistant LUAD cells, further supporting its role as a potential modulator of cisplatin resistance.

PHC2 knockdown enhances cisplatin-induced apoptosis in LUAD cells

As shown in Fig. 2F and G, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated silencing of PHC2 in A549/DDP LUAD cells significantly increased the apoptotic rate following treatment with 20 µM cisplatin, compared to cisplatin-treated parental A549/DDP cells (t = 38.59, P < 0.0001). These results indicate that PHC2 knockout sensitizes cisplatin-resistant LUAD cells to apoptosis, thereby reducing the cisplatin resistance index. Collectively, the findings reinforce the role of PHC2 as a critical regulator of cisplatin resistance in lung adenocarcinoma.

PHC2 expression is elevated in LUAD tissues and correlates with smoking history

Immunohistochemical staining was conducted to assess PHC2 protein levels in LUAD tissues and their paired adjacent normal lung tissues. As depicted in Fig. 3, PHC2 immunoreactivity was predominantly localized within the nucleus, aligning with the expected subcellular distribution specified for the antibody. Quantitative analysis demonstrated that the positive rate of PHC2 expression was significantly higher in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (P = 0.0281) (Table 1). Subsequently, the association between PHC2 expression status (high versus low) and clinicopathological parameters was evaluated across 52 paired LUAD samples (Table 2). No statistically significant correlations were observed between PHC2 expression and variables such as primary tumor size, distant metastasis, lymph node metastasis, gender, age, histological grade, or clinical stage (all P > 0.05). In contrast, an association was identified between high PHC2 expression and a history of smoking (P = 0.0321), suggesting that PHC2 may contribute to smoking-related tumorigenesis in LUAD.

Comprehensive analysis of PHC2 expression, immune correlation, and prognostic significance in LUAD and pan-cancer cohorts

RNA-seq data in TPM format were retrieved from the TCGA Pan-Cancer project via the GDC Data Portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). Tumor samples, along with their matched adjacent normal tissues, were selected for analysis. Expression levels were log₂-transformed using the formula log2(value + 1). Statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.1), utilizing packages including ggplot2 (v3.4.4), stats (v4.2.1), and car (v3.1-0). Wilcoxon signed-rank tests revealed significantly upregulated PHC2 expression in tumor tissues relative to normal tissues across multiple malignancies, such as colon adenocarcinoma, kidney chromophobe, kidney renal clear cell carcinoma, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and LUAD, with all P values below 0.001 (Fig. 4A). Focusing on LUAD, RNA-seq data from the TCGA-LUAD cohort were processed after excluding samples with incomplete clinical information or duplicates, followed by log2 transformation. Mann-Whitney U tests confirmed that PHC2 expression was significantly higher in LUAD tumor tissues than in normal lung tissues(U = 1.23 × 104, P = 0.0043) (Fig. 4B). This was further validated using Wilcoxon signed-rank testing, which also showed elevated expression in tumor tissues compared to paired normal samples (z = 290, P = 1.22 × 10− 5) (Fig. 4C). To assess the diagnostic capability of PHC2, ROC curve analysis was performed using the pROC package, yielding an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.613 (95% CI: 0.547–0.680), indicating moderate diagnostic performance. Results were visualized with ggplot2 (Fig. 4D). The relationship between PHC2 expression and immune infiltration was further explored using Pearson correlation analysis. PHC2 levels were significantly positively correlated with infiltration scores of various immune cell types, including NK cells, DCs, iDCs, neutrophils, eosinophils, and mast cells, all showing P < 0.001. These associations were visualized using lollipop plots (Fig. 4E).

The potential mechanism through which PHC2 mediates cisplatin resistance may involve epigenetic reactivation and transcriptional upregulation of genes associated with the ABC transporter family, axon guidance pathways, and complement/coagulation cascades, though additional experimental validation remains necessary (Fig. 5B). As detailed in the new Supplementary File 2, we have now performed a gene-level analysis and identified the specific ABC transporter family members that are modulated by PHC2. These findings suggest that disrupting the PHC2, for example, by combining epigenetic inhibitors with ABC transporter antagonists, could offer a promising approach to overcoming cisplatin resistance.

Discussion

Lung cancer continues to pose a significant global health burden and remains one of the primary causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide [13]. Although therapeutic strategies have improved considerably [14], obstacles such as drug resistance, adverse effects, tumor metastasis, and disease recurrence still substantially hinder patient prognosis and quality of life. Chemotherapy, particularly cisplatin-based regimens, continues to play a pivotal role in cancer management [13, 15, 16]. Nevertheless, the development of cisplatin resistance following an initial period of remission is a frequent and critical problem encountered in clinical practice [17]. Overcoming cisplatin resistance remains a major therapeutic challenge in the treatment of lung cancer. While the molecular mechanisms underlying cisplatin resistance have been partially elucidated, effective therapeutic targets for patients who develop resistance have yet to be fully identified.

In the present study, we employed a genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9-based screening approach using the Human Epigenetic Knockout Library to systematically identify genes associated with cisplatin resistance in lung cancer cell lines. Through this comprehensive analysis, PHC2 was ultimately identified and validated as a novel gene implicated in mediating cisplatin resistance in lung cancer.

Prior to the present study, PHC2 was primarily recognized as an essential functional component of class II Polycomb group (PcG) complexes, particularly during embryonic development [18]. The organization and accessibility of chromatin are key factors that regulate gene expression programs critical for determining cellular fate and functionality in both developmental processes and disease states [19]. PcG proteins are broadly classified into two major families: Polycomb repressive complex 1 (PRC1) and PRC2, both of which play pivotal roles in histone modification and chromatin remodeling [20]. Within PRC1, the Phc subunit has been shown to influence the morphology and dynamic behavior of condensates, promoting the establishment of distinct and stable chromatin domains [21]. Additionally, Bae J, et al. [22] provided compelling evidence that PHC2 exerts a critical extrinsic influence on hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell (HSPC) mobilization, marking the first demonstration of an epigenetic regulatory mechanism in HSPC mobilization.

Epigenetic modifications regulate gene expression primarily by altering chromatin architecture, thereby facilitating or inhibiting the initiation of mRNA transcription. Chromatin structure can be modified through various mechanisms, including methylation, phosphorylation, and acetylation. Given the critical roles of Polycomb group (PcG) and Trithorax group (TrxG) proteins in the regulation of androgen receptor (AR) transcription, Wang S et al. [23] reported that genes such as CBX2, PCGF6, PHC2, EZH2, and TRIM27 exhibited high methylation levels, whereas CBX8 and PCGF2 showed low methylation levels in prostate cancer cell lines. Although few studies have specifically addressed PHC2, existing evidence suggests that elevated PHC2 expression may be associated with poor clinical outcomes in cancer patients.

Cisplatin, a first-line chemotherapeutic agent widely used for various malignancies, exerts its cytotoxic effects by forming DNA adducts through intra- and inter-strand crosslinking, leading to extensive DNA damage that disrupts transcription and replication processes [24, 25]. Given that PHC2 functions as a transcriptional regulator, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of PHC2 may reduce drug resistance by modulating transcriptional programs, offering a novel strategy to enhance cisplatin efficacy.

A substantial body of epidemiological and experimental evidence dating back to the 1970s has consistently established long-term tobacco smoking as the most significant risk factor for lung cancer development [26, 27], a conclusion that remains valid today. In the present study, PHC2 protein expression levels were found to be significantly associated with smoking status, indirectly implicating PHC2 in the pathogenesis of LUAD. Moreover, our findings suggest that PHC2 may function as an oncogenic factor, as it was highly expressed across multiple cancer types and has been shown to regulate key cellular processes such as proliferation, migration, and apoptosis in lung cancer cells in vitro. However, PHC2 expression did not demonstrate a statistically significant association with patient prognosis in lung cancer, possibly due to limitations in sample size. In addition, we observed that elevated PHC2 expression was positively correlated with increased infiltration of various immune cell populations, including NK cells, macrophages, DCs, iDCs, neutrophils, eosinophils, and mast cells. These immune cells are known to secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF, which may paradoxically promote tumor progression under certain conditions [28–30].

Our initial KEGG pathway analysis indicated that genes dysregulated upon PHC2 knockdown were significantly enriched in the “ABC transporters” pathway. We acknowledge that protein-level validation of these targets, such as by Western blotting, will be an important next step to fully confirm these regulatory relationships. Crucially, a thorough review of the literature confirms that many of these specific PHC2-regulated genes are well-established mediators of chemotherapy resistance, thereby strongly validating the biological relevance of our RNA-seq results. For instance, recent high-impact studies have linked them to chemoresistance: Li et al. [25] reported that ABCA12 down-regulation impairs cancer stemness and chemoresistance [31]. Wang W, et al. found that high expression of ABCA1 is associated with poor patient outcomes and acquired carboplatin resistance in ovarian cancer, and that reducing ABCA1 expression increases chemosensitivity [32].

Therefore, targeting PHC2 may not only enhance cisplatin-mediated cytotoxicity in lung cancer cells but also potentially improve responses to immunotherapy-a hypothesis that warrants further experimental validation.

This study also has certain limitations. The primary validation of PHC2’s role was conducted primarily within a single cellular model system, which may not fully represent the heterogeneity of lung cancer. Future investigations across diverse in vitro and in vivo models are warranted to confirm its broader relevance.

Conclusion

In summary, this study identifies PHC2 as a novel gene associated with cisplatin resistance in lung cancer for the first time. The underlying mechanism may involve epigenetic reactivation and transcriptional upregulation of genes associated with the ABC transporter family, axon guidance pathways and complement/coagulation cascades, although further experimental validation is needed. These findings suggest that PHC2 represents a promising therapeutic target for LUAD patients who develop resistance to cisplatin, offering a potential new avenue for chemotherapeutic drug development.
