Adenovirus Disease Following Pediatric Liver Transplantation: 10‐Year Experience From a Large Pediatric Transplant Program
Authors: Anna M Banc‐Husu, Sara Hassan, N Thao N Galvan, Elizabeth A Moulton, Jeremy M Schraw, Kathleen Hosek, Dana Cerminara, Flor M Munoz, Krupa Mysore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHuman adenovirus in immunocompromised patients can be life‐threatening. We describe the prevalence, clinical presentation, and treatment of adenovirus after pediatric liver transplantation at a large transplant center.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study of adenovirus infection in children from 2013 to 2022. We compared incidence, clinical characteristics, and outcomes of post‐transplant children by adenovirus treatment status.ResultsAdenovirus disease developed in 26% (84/320) children after liver transplant. Median age at liver transplant was 17.5 months, 48% were female; 50% had biliary atresia. Fever (53%), gastrointestinal symptoms (48%), and hepatitis (41%) were the most common clinical presentations at diagnosis. Median time to adenovirus diagnosis was 80 days (IQR 19–260) with 40% (n = 31/84) identified within 30 days post‐transplant. Disseminated adenovirus (≥ 2 organ involvement) occurred in 24% (20/84). Fourteen patients (17%) received cidofovir, and most (13/14, 93%) had DNAemia, compared to 57% untreated patients with DNAemia (p = 0.013). Median peak adenovirus load was 491 805 copies/mL (IQR 24 800–1 900 000) in treated vs. 1000 copies/mL (IQR 595–794 794) in untreated patients (p < 0.001). Overall mortality was 8% (7/84).ConclusionThe incidence of symptomatic and disseminated adenovirus disease was high in our pediatric liver transplant patients, particularly within 30 days post‐transplant. Patients who received cidofovir treatment presented with high viral load and had the highest mortality. There is a critical need for evidence‐based guidance for early antiviral management of adenovirus disease after pediatric liver transplant.Keywords: adenovirus disease, adenovirus infection, cidofovir, disseminated adenovirus, pediatric liver transplant

Introduction

Liver transplantation (LT) is an established treatment for a variety of diagnoses in children from chronic liver diseases to metabolic diseases and acute liver failure. Safe options for immunosuppression have improved both graft and patient survival over time [1, 2]. While the goal of immunosuppression is to prevent allograft rejection, it carries an increased risk for infection, particularly in young children who may be exposed to pathogens for the first time during the initial months following LT when immunosuppression is highest. Human adenovirus infection during this vulnerable period could cause significant morbidity and mortality in LT recipients.

Adenoviruses are non‐enveloped, double‐stranded DNA viruses that can cause significant and potentially life‐threatening disease in neonates and immunocompromised patients [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Many adenovirus serotypes are associated with primary illness and signs and symptoms such as fever, cough, conjunctivitis, and gastrointestinal (GI) complaints [9, 10]. Importantly, some adenovirus serotypes are capable of latent infection that can reactivate with immunosuppression [11, 12]. Adenovirus infection during solid organ transplantation (SOT) carries especially high risk for young children (< 5 years old) who are more likely to be immunologically naïve. In pediatric LT recipients, reported adenovirus infection incidence ranges from 4% to 38% [13, 14]. Adenovirus typically presents with GI manifestations, hepatitis, fever, or respiratory symptoms, but is occasionally asymptomatic. The mainstay of adenovirus treatment is supportive care with immunosuppression reduction as suggested by guidelines published in 2019 from the American Society of Transplantation (AST) Infectious Disease Community of Practice [13]. Additionally, AST differentiates adenovirus infection, defined as viral detection in the absence of symptoms, from adenovirus disease, which requires viral detection in the presence of signs/symptoms and in the absence of other diagnoses [13].

Treatment with antiviral agents, such as cidofovir, is controversial with variation in practice seen both between as well as within centers as no FDA approved antivirals for adenovirus currently exist [13]. Cidofovir, a nucleotide cytosine analog that inhibits adenovirus DNA polymerase, is active against many adenovirus serotypes; however, significant nephrotoxicity (in up to 50% recipients) and neutropenia (in up to 25% recipients) can be seen, thus limiting its use [13, 15]. While many transplant centers incorporate cidofovir into treatment regimens for patients with severe or disseminated adenovirus disease, much of the data on cidofovir use in pediatric LT recipients is limited to small case series with few patients [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Deciding when to initiate cidofovir and in which clinical scenarios remains challenging for pediatric LT recipients. We describe a large cohort of pediatric LT recipients with adenovirus infection and disease, their clinical presentation, diagnostic and treatment approaches.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adenovirus cases in pediatric LT recipients at Texas Children's Hospital, a large quaternary pediatric transplant center. Children who received an isolated LT over a 10‐year period between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2022, were included, and patients who received dual solid organ, multi‐visceral transplantation or required repeat LT were excluded. Patient demographics, date and indication for LT, post‐LT length of stay (LOS), source of adenovirus detection, and time‐to‐infection from LT were collected from electronic medical records. We evaluated the use of antiviral treatment for adenovirus disease, viral level, duration of DNAemia, response to therapy, and overall patient outcomes. Cause of death was attributed to adenovirus if the patient continued to have positive adenovirus testing at the time of death with symptoms consistent with ongoing infection. This study was approved by the Baylor College of Medicine Institutional Review Board #H‐39130.

Adenovirus Testing

At our institution, when patients present with clinical symptoms concerning for infection, routine testing is performed to identify a potential source. Routine infectious testing includes respiratory viral panel, plasma CMV PCR, whole blood EBV PCR, plasma human herpes virus‐6 PCR, urinalysis/urine culture, blood cultures, in addition to stool infectious studies including GI viral pathogen, stool culture, and Clostridium difficile testing as appropriate. Adenovirus testing post‐transplant at our institution is performed only for‐cause (e.g., in the setting of elevated liver enzymes, fever, elevated white blood cell count, respiratory symptoms, diarrhea, or other concerning infectious symptoms) and is at the discretion of the clinical team, and it is sent in most patients with these clinical symptoms. Routine surveillance testing for adenovirus in asymptomatic patients is not performed post‐transplant. We routinely screen all children for respiratory viruses prior to transplant independent of symptoms. If a patient's infectious work‐up at the time of presentation was negative for all other infectious etiologies and adenovirus was the sole positive PCR, clinical symptoms were attributed to adenovirus.

Adenovirus was detected by quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) in whole blood, plasma, and urine samples using a viral nucleic acid small volume kit on MagNA Pure 96 instrument for DNA extraction using a 3 M integrated cycler in the Texas Children's Hospital pathology lab. Qualitative respiratory viral testing also occurred at Texas Children's Hospital on Hologic Panther Fusion via real‐time PCR and via BioFire FilmArray nested multiplex PCR platforms. Quantitative adenovirus PCR on respiratory specimens and qualitative PCR on tissue and stool specimens are performed by Eurofins Viracor (Lenexa, KS). Respiratory, stool, or urine samples were collected by clinicians based on each patient's symptoms and clinical concerns. Additionally, if elevated liver enzymes prompted a for‐cause liver biopsy, liver tissue was tested for adenovirus by PCR and immunohistochemistry testing on formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded liver tissue using a mouse monoclonal antibody (M58 + M73 clone, Novus Biologicals).

Definitions of Adenovirus Infection and Disease

We adopted the definition of adenovirus infection and disease described by AST [13]. Accordingly, adenovirus infection is defined as PCR detection of the virus in the absence of symptoms. Adenovirus disease requires the detection of the virus, plus presence of attributable signs and symptoms in the absence of an alternative diagnosis [13]. As all patients had broad infectious work‐up performed as above, if no other etiology was identified, clinical symptoms were attributed to adenovirus. Adenovirus DNAemia is defined as the detection of adenovirus in the blood or plasma via PCR above the lower limit of quantification. Adenovirus can establish latency; therefore, isolating the virus from different sites does not necessarily correlate with invasive disease [13]. Disseminated adenovirus disease is when at least two organs are involved, excluding DNAemia. Viral clearance is defined as viral load below the limit of quantification upon repeat testing [3, 13].

Cidofovir Treatment Regimens

Initial standard immunosuppression following liver transplantation at our institution includes intravenous methylprednisolone intra‐operatively with a steroid taper as induction immunosuppression, followed by maintenance immunosuppression with tacrolimus. In addition to supportive care and reduction of immunosuppression when feasible, cidofovir is the main antiviral agent used at our center for treatment of adenovirus. Immunosuppression reduction was determined by the clinical team and often included reduction in tacrolimus goal by ~20% when feasible and/or holding additional agents such as mycophenolate when patients were on > 2 agents. Initiation of cidofovir is at the discretion of the multi‐disciplinary team including Transplant surgery, Hepatology, Transplant Infectious Disease at our institution and is determined based on the patient's clinical status and risk factors for severe disease without a specific adenovirus PCR threshold in place. Two dosing regimens are used depending on the patient's renal function. The first consists of weekly cidofovir (5 mg/kg) in combination with 3 doses of probenecid 1 g/m2, with the first dose given 3 h before, and subsequent doses given 2 and 8 h after cidofovir administration. Careful pre‐hydration with normal saline run at 150 mL/m2/h starting 3 h before cidofovir is initiated with reduction to 100 mL/m2/h at time of cidofovir initiation and is continued at this rate for 2 h post‐cidofovir administration. In patients with impaired kidney function, a lower dose of cidofovir (1 mg/kg) is given more frequently (3 times per week) in combination with 3 doses of probenecid 1 g/m2 and pre‐ and post‐hydration to further mitigate renal toxicity. Typically, patients on renal replacement therapy do not receive probenecid or pre/post hydration as fluid balance is managed by dialysis and patients are maintained with a positive fluid balance before and after cidofovir administration. Cidofovir continues until adenovirus viral load is undetectable.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive data are presented as proportions, non‐normally distributed continuous variables as median with interquartile ranges (IQR), and normally distributed continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviation. Time to illness was calculated as the timespan between LT and the first positive adenovirus test. The first positive source was the first source (tissue, plasma, stool, or respiratory panel) that resulted positive. Chi‐square and Fischer's exact test were used for categorical comparisons. Non‐parametric tests were used to compare non‐normally distributed continuous variables. Overall mortality and adenovirus‐related mortality was calculated for the entire cohort. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY) and GraphPad Prism (10.2.2, San Diego, CA).

Results

Study Population

A total of 320 children under 18 years of age received isolated liver transplants at Texas Children's Hospital during the 10‐year study period (2013–2022). Eighty‐four patients (26%) developed adenovirus disease. There were no patients that tested positive for adenovirus PCR in the absence of clinical symptoms, and thus, no patients were categorized as having adenovirus infection alone based on the AST criteria. The patients' demographics and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age at transplant was 17.5 months (IQR 10–34 months). The most common underlying diagnosis was biliary atresia (n = 42, 50%), followed by metabolic disease (n = 15, 18%), and hepatic tumors (n = 12, 14%). The median length of stay of index transplant hospitalization was 24 days (IQR 11–55.5). Most patients received a whole‐organ graft (n = 79/84, 94%). Only 30% (n = 25) of patients had been hospitalized pre‐transplant, either on the acute care floor or in the intensive care unit, while the remainder of patients arrived from home for transplant.

Clinical Manifestations and Timing of Adenovirus Disease Following LT

The majority of patients who developed adenovirus disease following LT (n = 72/84, 86%) were tested at least one time for adenovirus infection prior to transplant through clinical testing. Of these, only 4 patients (6%) had adenovirus detected prior to transplant. Two patients had gastroenteritis, one presented with acute liver failure secondary to adenovirus, and one had a respiratory infection. The remaining patients did not have a history of known prior adenovirus positivity.

After transplantation, the most common presenting symptom at diagnosis was fever (n = 44/84, 53%), followed by gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea) (n = 40/84, 48%) (Figure 1). Hepatitis, defined by clinically significant elevation in aminotransferases, was the initial manifestation of adenovirus disease in 41% (n = 34/84) of patients. All patients with adenovirus infection were outwardly symptomatic. Disseminated adenovirus occurred in 24% (n = 20/84). Adenovirus positivity most frequently occurred within 30 days after transplant (n = 31/84, 37%), with an additional 27% (n = 23/84) occurring 2–6 months and fewer cases in 7–12 months (n = 18/84, 21%) post‐transplant period. The remaining patients (12/84, 14%) were positive > 1 year after LT. Overall, the median time from transplant to first adenovirus detection was 80 days (IQR 19–260). Five patients did not require hospitalization for their adenovirus disease. Of the remaining, one third (32%, n = 25/79) of patients developed adenovirus disease during their initial transplant hospitalization, while 68% (n = 54/79) required a unique hospital admission for adenovirus disease. Of these patients hospitalized for adenovirus outside the initial transplant period, median LOS was 7 days (IQR 4–14).

Initial presenting symptoms of adenovirus infection in treated and untreated patients.

The initial source of adenovirus detection was a respiratory site (nasal swab or wash, tracheal aspirate, bronchoalveolar lavage) in 32% (n = 27) of patients, plasma in 30% (n = 25), and stool in 30% (n = 25), while only 8% (n = 7) had positive tissue as the initial source. Of all patients, 90% had plasma adenovirus testing performed, 85% had testing of respiratory samples, 69% had stool testing performed, while only 36% had liver biopsies performed during the time of adenovirus positivity.

Characteristics of Patients Receiving Cidofovir Therapy

Of the 14 patients (17%) who received treatment with cidofovir, 13 (93%) had DNAemia and 6 (43%) had disseminated disease (Figure 2). The median age at time of transplant in this subgroup was 15 months (IQR 10–18), which was comparable to those in the untreated subgroup (Table 1). When comparing the characteristics of cidofovir‐treated vs. untreated patients, there were no statistically significant differences in the baseline demographics, underlying diagnosis, history of adenovirus positivity prior to LT, time of post‐LT adenovirus infection, or initial source of adenovirus positivity (Table 1, Figure 2). Treated patients were more likely to present with fever (86% vs. 46% untreated, p = 0.006), lower respiratory symptoms (29% vs. 4% untreated, p = 0.013) and hepatitis (64% vs. 36% untreated, p = 0.047) (Figure 1). Treated patients were also more likely to have positive adenovirus testing in plasma (93% vs. 57% untreated, p = 0.013) (Figure 2) and a higher peak plasma adenovirus level (median 490 000 copies/mL vs. 1000 copies/mL untreated, p < 0.001). In contrast, there was no statistically significant difference in detection in overall respiratory, stool, or tissue samples across treated and untreated groups (Figure 2).

Adenovirus detection at different sites by treatment group.

Five of 14 patients received three times weekly 1 mg/kg cidofovir with a median of 2.5 doses given (range 1–7 doses). Five patients received 5 mg/kg dosing with a median of 2 doses given (range 1–3 doses). The remaining patients (n = 4) received both 1 mg/kg and 5 mg/kg dosing with a median of 4 doses given (range 2–5 doses). Only one patient without DNAemia was treated with cidofovir due to a severe lower respiratory infection with peak adenovirus viral load in a bronchoalveolar lavage sample of 926 000 copies/mL and received 1 mg/kg dose only once. All but one of the treated patients achieved complete viral clearance prior to stopping cidofovir. One patient who did not clear adenovirus DNAemia despite cidofovir treatment experienced fulminant respiratory failure and died from complications of multi‐organ failure. In treated patients with DNAemia, viral clearance was achieved in median time of 24 days (range 4–79 days).

Outcome

The overall mortality in all patients with adenovirus disease was 8% (n = 7/84). All but 1 patient died during their index hospitalization where adenovirus infection occurred. Of these 7 patients, only 1 patient died secondary to adenovirus infection with severe, irreversible respiratory failure. The remaining patients had all cleared adenovirus at the time of death and died either from multi‐organ failure in the setting of sepsis (n = 3), cerebral edema in the setting of acute graft failure (n = 1), cardiac arrest (n = 1), or unknown cause (n = 1). There was no detectable difference in overall mortality when comparing patients with disseminated disease (10%, n = 2/20) to those without disseminated disease (8%, n = 5/64) (p = 0.67).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, 84 pediatric liver transplant recipients developed adenovirus disease corresponding to an incidence of 26%. More than one‐third of these infections occurred in the first month post‐transplant, a period marked by intense immunosuppression, suggesting that newly acquired infection is most common in this pediatric population. Seven patients with adenovirus disease died, most within 3 months of adenovirus infection, and one death was attributed to severe adenovirus‐induced respiratory failure. Thus, these findings raise the importance of early identification and close follow up of the trend in DNAemia and clinical status for a timely treatment initiation before elevated levels of DNAemia and severe disease occur.

Our findings show higher rates of adenovirus disease after LT as compared to previously published single‐center experiences where only ~10% of patients had adenovirus detected on PCR testing after LT [21], possibly related to improved diagnostics over time as well as possibly increased testing in symptomatic patients. The source of infection remained unidentified in our patients, but plausible sources include viral reactivation, donor‐derived, and community‐acquired infection. Routine pre‐transplant serologic testing of donors or recipients is not performed for adenovirus, in contrast to other DNA viruses such as cytomegalovirus, making accurate risk assessment for infection during the post‐transplant period challenging. In our cohort, 85% of patients had pre‐transplant clinical adenovirus testing for symptoms or routine pre‐operative screening, and of these, more than 90% of patients were negative and did not have any known prior history of adenovirus infection.

The AST guidelines support the use of cidofovir for disseminated adenovirus disease while concomitantly reducing immunosuppression [13]. There is a paucity of data to guide timing for initiation of therapy or proper dosing regimens. There is currently no consensus about the viral load threshold for initiation of cidofovir treatment, especially in SOT recipients. In our study, 17% (14 patients) were treated with cidofovir, with the majority experiencing high levels of DNAemia and disseminated adenovirus disease. All but one patient achieved viral clearance following treatment. Use of cidofovir for adenovirus disease is limited to small, single‐center experiences and often addresses all SOT recipients without specific focus on treatment of pediatric LT recipients. Further, many published protocols also do not provide guidance as to which patients should be considered for cidofovir treatment or when treatment should be initiated [3, 16, 18].

Given these limitations in literature, we propose an algorithm for testing and treating adenovirus infection in pediatric LT recipients (Figure 3). As suggested by AST, routine adenovirus surveillance post‐LT is not necessary. However, when patients present with fever, GI symptoms, respiratory symptoms, or biochemical elevations in liver enzymes, evaluation for adenovirus via PCR in the plasma and other pertinent samples, along with testing for other viruses such as Epstein–Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, and human herpesvirus 6 is encouraged, especially within the first year following LT [22]. Detecting adenovirus from whole blood, plasma, urine, stool, and/or respiratory samples confirms the presence of adenovirus infection. The presence of attributable symptoms in the absence of other alternative diagnoses confirms the diagnosis of adenovirus disease. Adenovirus quantitative PCR testing from different sites (blood, nasopharyngeal, tracheal aspirate or bronchoalveolar lavage, stool, urine, etc.) can assess multi‐organ involvement, and repeat testing evaluates progression of disease.

Suggested algorithm for evaluation and treatment of adenovirus in pediatric liver transplant recipients.

Following detection of adenovirus DNAemia, we first encourage reducing immunosuppression, as feasible, plus supportive care. To establish a trend, repeat quantitative adenovirus PCR in the blood or plasma should occur at least weekly, or more frequently, as clinically indicated (high initial level [site specific threshold], recent transplant < 6 months, young age < 5 years‐old, augmented immunosuppression, clinical evidence of adenovirus disease). Given the substantial variability in quantitative adenovirus testing between laboratories, repeat testing at the same laboratory enables interpretation of trends [23]. We recommend using therapeutic drug monitoring for immunosuppression to ensure that drug levels remain at the lower end of target ranges and consider lowering targets for patients at high risk of severe adenovirus disease. A multi‐disciplinary team approach and early involvement of the Infectious Disease service are critical for managing adenovirus disease and determining antiviral treatment with cidofovir. Decision‐making regarding treatment initiation should consider a patient's clinical status, trend of plasma viral load, presence of severe or disseminated disease, and weigh potential treatment benefits against the risk of toxicity. A rising adenovirus plasma viral load, detection of adenovirus in multiple sources, and further clinical deterioration (e.g., acute respiratory failure, hepatitis, or multiorgan failure) may be indications for antiviral treatment. Our study found that patients with higher viral loads fare poorly despite treatment with increased mortality compared to patients with lower viral loads. A threshold for initiating treatment is difficult to establish given that other patient/host factors are also likely to contribute to final outcomes. Serial viral load assessments and clinical course guide the frequency and duration of treatment. Prospective studies evaluating early treatment's effect on viral load and mortality are necessary to guide management. Similarly, developing antivirals or therapies (such as cytotoxic T‐cells) with more specific adenovirus activity could further improve survival chances in pediatric patients with adenovirus disease.

Our study has several limitations. First, given the retrospective nature of data collection, it is difficult to ascertain all of the clinical reasoning that went into management decisions. Cidofovir treatment was generally reserved for children who were sicker but not guided by specific patient criteria or a particular laboratory viral load threshold. Thus, this may have led to biased results as the patients treated were likely more critically ill, leading to higher mortality than those not receiving treatment. Overall, immunosuppression practices have not changed substantially at our center during the study period, but heterogeneity across different providers' approaches to immunosuppression reduction may have influenced the results. Additionally, given the definition used to classify patients as having “adenovirus disease” based on clinical symptoms such as fever that could have been attributed to other etiologies rather than adenovirus, it is possible that our described rate of adenovirus disease is higher than may be expected. Mortality attributed to adenovirus was seen in only one patient; however, overall mortality was high in the treated group, which may be biased as these patients were likely critically ill from other etiologies after their adenovirus infection. Lastly, a number of other risk factors that we did not track, both pre‐ and post‐LT, may exist in this patient population and contribute to the development of adenovirus infection and disease post‐LT.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the importance of early detection and treatment of adenovirus disease in pediatric liver transplant patients. A high prevalence of adenovirus disease and mortality after adenovirus infection were observed in our cohort, particularly when infection occurred within the first month of transplant, when immunosuppression is at its highest, regardless of the presence or absence of disseminated disease. Evidence‐based guidance on how to safely reduce immunosuppression and how to decide on the most effective timing, regimen, and duration of antiviral treatment is desperately needed. Research on alternative options for antiviral therapy and novel approaches for treating adenovirus in patients with altered humoral and cellular immunity is critical because young pediatric liver transplant and other SOT recipients remain at high risk of serious morbidity and high mortality from this common endemic virus.
