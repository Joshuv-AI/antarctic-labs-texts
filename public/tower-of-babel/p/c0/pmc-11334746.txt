Improvement in signs and symptoms of severe dry eye disease after dual therapy with high-potency steroids and 0.05% cyclosporine
Authors: Chia-Yi Lee, Li-Chun Chang, Chao-Kai Chang, Pei-Ting Lu, Jing-Yang Huang, Shun-Fa Yang, Chi-Chin Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the effectiveness and safety of combining 0.05% cyclosporine A (CsA) with high-potency steroids for treating severe dry eye disease (DED).Materials and methodsThis retrospective comparative case series included 93 patients treated with 0.05% CsA for severe DED. Among them, we included data from 54 eyes of 27 patients who received high-potency steroids in the study group and from 132 eyes of 66 patients who did not receive high-potency steroids in the control group. Data on demographic characteristics, comorbidities, medications and intraocular pressure (IOP) were recorded. The primary outcomes were changes in symptom and sign scores. The ocular surface disease index was used as the symptom score, whereas tear break-up time, Schirmer I test without anaesthesia, ocular surface staining scores and presence of meibomian gland dysfunction were considered as sign scores. Repeated one-way ANOVA and generalized linear mixed models were used to evaluate differences.ResultsIn the control group, symptom scores decreased from 1 to 2 months and from 2 to 3 months after treatment (p = .002 and .049). In the high-potency steroid group, symptom scores improved during these intervals (p = .003 and .005). The sign score in the control group remained unchanged (all p > .05), while the high-potency steroid group exhibited progressive improvement in sign scores (all p < .05). The high-potency steroid group had more favourable symptom (p = .035) and sign (p < .001) scores than did the control group. However, multiple systemic diseases were associated with poor symptom (p = .025) and sign (p = .014) scores. The risks for glaucoma and cataract formation were similar between the two groups (all p > .05).ConclusionsDual therapy combining high-potency steroids and 0.05% CsA significantly improved the signs and symptoms of severe DED compared with 0.05% CsA monotherapy, without severe complications.Keywords: Cyclosporine, dry eye, steroid, inflammation

Introduction

Dry eye disease (DED) is a prevalent ocular surface disorder that primarily affects older individuals and women [1,2]. Although the precise pathophysiology of DED remains unclear, a study proposed the involvement of aqueous deficiency, excessive tear evaporation and multiple inflammatory cytokines in DED development [3]. Common treatments for DED include the use of artificial tears and overnight ointments. In severe cases, punctal occlusion and amniotic membrane transplantation are performed [4]. The risk factors for DED include diabetes mellitus (DM) and various autoimmune diseases (ADs), such as Sjögren’s syndrome (SS), Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS) and rheumatoid arthritis (RA); these conditions are closely associated with the occurrence and severity of DED [5–11].

Cyclosporin A (CsA) is a calcineurin inhibitor developed in the early 2000s, and it is widely used for the treatment of severe DED [12,13]. CsA application was demonstrated to improve tear film breakup time (TBUT) in patients with severe meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) [14] and ocular surface disease index (OSDI) scores in patients with DED [15]. Furthermore, in a previous study, CsA instillation alleviated the symptoms and signs of DED in patients with SS [16]. However, the extent of symptomatic improvement following CsA application in DED varies among different populations [17].

Steroids have been used in conjunction with CsA for the treatment of DED due to their anti-inflammatory effect [18,19]. According to a report from the Dry Eye Workshop, the use of topical low- or high-potency steroids for a prolonged period is recommended when CsA treatment alone is inadequate for managing DED [20]. Although a previous study reported that the use of high-potency steroids is beneficial for the ocular surface in the general DED population [20], whether these effects are equally beneficial for those with severe DED requiring CsA treatment remain to be determined. Moreover, the degree of improvement in signs and symptoms after the instillation of high-potency steroids has not yet been thoroughly investigated. Thus, a study examining the effect of the combined use of CsA and a high-potency steroid on severe DED is warranted.

The current study evaluated the effectiveness and safety of a dual therapy involving 0.05% CsA and a high-potency steroid in treating severe DED.

Materials and methods

Ethics declarations

This retrospective comparative case series adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its later amendments. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Keelung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (Registration No.: 202000799B0, approval date: 18 May 2020). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the same institute.

Patient selection

This single-centre study was conducted at Keelung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, which serves approximately one million residents. We reviewed medical charts and enrolled patients with severe DED who (1) had an OSDI score of >60, (2) had a TBUT of <5 s, (3) values <5 mm on the Schirmer I test without anaesthesia, (4) had an ocular staining score of >3 in accordance with the Oxford scheme [21], (5) received 0.05% CsA for the treatment of DED as per the medical record, (6) had a complete follow-up period of >1 year in the ophthalmology department with intraocular pressure (IOP) measurements, (7) had available ophthalmic examination records of DED-related signs and symptoms before and 3 months after starting 0.05% CsA treatment and (8) received treatment in both eyes due to severe DED. We excluded patients who (1) had legal blindness, (2) underwent eyeball removal surgery of any type, (3) had phthisis bulbi, (4) received a diagnosis of ocular tumour, (5) had total corneal opacity or neovascularization prior to inclusion and (6) received high-potency steroid treatment in only one eye. Patients with severe DED who received high-potency steroid therapy served as the high-potency steroid group, whereas those who did not receive this therapy served as the control group. High-potency steroids, specifically topical dexamethasone (Delone, Sinphar Pharmaceutical, Dongshan Township, Taiwan) and betamethasone (Rinderon-A, Taiwan Shionogi & Co., Taipei, Taiwan), were prescribed by an experienced ophthalmologist (C.-C. Sun) on the basis of a previous study [22]. The ophthalmologist (C.-C. Sun) prescribed these high-potency steroids at the initial visit if patients met the following criteria: (1) having an OSDI score of >80, (2) having a TBUT of <2 s, (3) values <2 mm on the Schirmer I test without anaesthesia and (4) having an ocular staining score of ≥4. The routine dosage for topical high-potency steroids at our institution was one drop twice daily without a loading dose, continued for at least 3 months. If symptoms and signs improved after 3 months or longer, the dosage was tapered to once daily for 2 weeks and discontinued if no worsening of signs and symptoms was observed. Data on demographic characteristics, pronounced ocular comorbidities, systemic diseases, DED-related medications and IOP were recorded from medical charts. For each patient, IOP was measured three times per visit by using a pneumatic tonometer (Topcon c60, Topcon Corp., Tokyo, Japan), and the average value of these three measurements was recorded as the IOP.

Scores for symptom and sign of DED

The severity scores for the symptoms and signs of DED were calculated using various indexes, as outlined in this section. The severity of the symptoms of DED was calculated by dividing the OSDI score by 100 for each visit, and the final symptom score ranges from 0 (no symptoms) to 100 (worst symptoms). The signs of DED assessed included TBUT, scores on Schirmer I test without anaesthesia, ocular surface staining and the presence of MGD. TBUT was defined as the time from the opening of the eye until the appearance of the first dark spot on the corneal surface, as observed using a slit-lamp microscope. For the Schirmer I test, a Schirmer strip was placed at the outer one-third of the lower lid margin without anaesthesia; patients were then asked to close their eyes for 5 min. Subsequently, the Schirmer strip was removed, and the length of the wet side was measured. For ocular surface staining, fluorescein was applied on the inferior bulbar conjunctival surface. Subsequently, patients were asked to blink the eye two to three times, and the pictures of the ocular surface, including the cornea and conjunctiva, were captured using the accessory camera of the slit-lamp microscope. Epithelial damage in the cornea was scored from 1 (absence) to 5 (diffuse punctate keratitis in the entire cornea) in accordance with the Oxford Scheme [21]. A corneal specialist (C.-C.S.) determined the presence of MGD by examining the patient’s upper eyelid under a slit-lamp microscope. A diagnosis of MGD was established if the average of the meibum quality score (0 = clear, 1 = cloudy, 2 = granular and 3 = toothpaste-like) and meibum expressibility score (0 = minimal, 1 = light, 2 = moderate and 3 heavy pressure) was greater than 1 [17]. To standardize the DED signs, all the aforementioned parameters were converted into scores ranging from 0 to 1 (Table 1). For sign parameters that fell between the quartile points of each sign score, linear interpolation was used to convert these values into appropriate scores. Subsequently, the average score of these four sign parameters for each participant was calculated without weighting, and this average served as the final sign score. In addition, patients’ responses to high-potency steroids were recorded, including their comfort level (rated as excellent, acceptable or discomfort) and any ocular side effects (e.g. conjunctival congestion, conjunctival hyperaemia, superficial keratitis and subjective ocular irritation).

Factors predicting treatment outcomes

We evaluated factors that may predict treatment outcomes in terms of the signs and symptoms of DED. The potential predictors included age, sex, systemic diseases, ocular comorbidities and the use of adjunctive therapy with 0.05% CsA for DED. The systemic diseases considered were SS, SLE, RA, DM and SJS. The ocular comorbidities considered were (1) keratopathy (corneal ulcer, recurrent corneal erosion, corneal dystrophy and history of penetrating keratoplasty), (2) glaucoma (open-angle glaucoma and normal-tension glaucoma), (3) eyelid disorders (exophthalmos, lagophthalmos, entropion, blepharoptosis and dermatochalasis) and (4) cataract, where surgery has been performed before initiating 0.05% CsA treatment. The adjunctive agents examined were (1) low-potency steroids (0.02% and 0.1% topical fluorometholone), (2) acetylcysteine and (3) autologous serum eye drops. Only patients who received adjunctive therapy for >3 months were regarded as having utilized adjunctive therapy.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL). We obtained a copyright license to use this software. Descriptive statistics was used to present the characteristics of the high-potency steroid and control groups. Differences in basic characteristics between these two groups were determined using an independent t-test and Fisher’s exact test. Repeated one-way ANOVA was also used to evaluate significant changes in symptom and sign scores after 0.05% CsA treatment across four timepoints: pretreatment and 1, 2 and 3 months after treatment. For the outcome predictors, we used a generalized linear mixed model to examine the effects of combined high-potency steroid and CsA therapy on sign and symptom scores before and 3 months after treatment. The results are presented as the adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We adjusted for the effects of all potential demographic, systemic, ocular and medical confounders, including acetylcysteine and autologous serum use, on sign and symptom scores in the generalized linear mixed model. In addition, the effect of each confounder on sign and symptom scores was analysed using the same method. In this analysis, the presence of systemic diseases was categorized into three groups: no systemic disease, the presence of one type of systemic disease, and the presence of more than two types of systemic diseases. Moreover, changes in IOP over a 1-year follow-up period in both groups were analysed using the generalized linear mixed model, considering the effects of age, sex and pre-existing glaucoma. Furthermore, the number of cataract surgeries after treatment, new glaucoma events, increases in antiglaucoma medications, and trabeculectomy episodes between the high-potency steroid and control groups during the one-year follow-up for IOP were analysed using Fisher’s exact test. A p value of <.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

We enrolled 93 patients with 186 eyes, of which 27 patients with 54 eyes were included in the study group and 66 patients with 132 eyes in the control group. The mean ages of the high-potency steroid and control groups were 63.44 ± 13.55 and 60.85 ± 12.92 years, respectively, with no significant difference between them (p = .222). The male-to-female ratios were 5:22 and 6:60 in the high-potency steroid and control groups, respectively (p = .071). The high-potency steroid group had a higher incidence of cataract surgery, a lower usage of low-potency steroids, and a higher usage of acetylcysteine and autologous serum than did the control group (Table 2).

The initial symptom score was higher in the high-potency steroid group than in the control group (0.563 ± 0.224 vs. 0.504 ± 0.229). The symptom scores in the control group decreased to 0.495 ± 0.207, 0.456 ± 0.213 and 0.433 ± 0.192 at 1, 2 and 3 months after treatment, respectively, and a significant difference was observed between the last two intervals (p = .002 and .049, respectively). Furthermore, the symptom score in the high-potency steroid group significantly decreased to 0.443 ± 0.200 and 0.408 ± 0.241 at 1 and 2 months after treatment (p = .003 and .005, respectively). In the high-potency steroid group, the symptom score at 3 months after treatment (0.393 ± 0.195) was similar to that at 2 months after treatment (0.408 ± 0.241, p = .861). The initial sign score was higher in the high-potency steroid group than in the control group (0.807 ± 0.138 vs. 0.709 ± 0.155). The sign score in the control group decreased to 0.695 ± 0.162, 0.698 ± 0.164 and 0.688 ± 0.166 at 1, 2 and 3 months after CsA treatment, respectively; all these values were nearly identical (all p > .05). However, the sign scores in the high-potency steroid group were 0.765 ± 0.102, 0.721 ± 0.123 and 0.691 ± 0.126 at 1, 2 and 3 months after CsA treatment. Significant differences were noted among these three scores (all p < .05). Overall, the high-potency steroid group exhibited greater decreases in both sign (15.2% vs. 2.96%) and symptom (30.7% vs. 14.7%) scores than did the control group. Regarding the predictors of changes in symptom scores 3 months after treatment, the use of high-potency steroids was associated with a lower symptom score (aOR: 0.733, 95% CI: 0.676–0.895, p = .035), whereas the presence of more than two types of systemic diseases was associated with significantly higher symptom scores (aOR: 2.026, 95% CI: 1.994–2.058, p = .025). The other factors did not significantly affect symptom scores (Table 3). In terms of sign scores, the use of both high-potency steroids (aOR: 0.642, 95% CI: 0.535–0.740, p < .001) and low-potency steroids (aOR: 0.854, 95% CI: 0.821–0.990, p = .025) was associated with a significant decrease in sign scores, whereas the presence of multiple systemic diseases was associated with a significantly higher sign score after treatment (aOR: 2.108, 95% CI: 1.986–2.441, p = .014; Table 3).

With regard to safety, the IOP between the high-potency steroid and control groups was similar before treatment (13.12 ± 4.23 vs. 14.31 ± 3.36, p = .089) and at 3 months (13.31 ± 3.49 vs. 14.67 ± 4.70, p = .076), 9 months (13.91 ± 2.88 vs. 13.72 ± 2.91, p = .469) and 12 months (13.61 ± 2.39 vs. 13.58 ± 3.08, p = .978) after treatment. However, the IOP was significantly lower in the high-potency steroid group than in the control group at 6 months after treatment (12.84 ± 3.30 vs. 14.24 ± 3.29, p = .026; Figure 2). Two patients in the control group underwent cataract surgery. However, no cataract surgery was performed in the high-potency steroid group at 1 year after treatment (p = .632). Furthermore, no new cases of glaucoma, no increases in antiglaucoma medications, and no trabeculectomy arrangement were noted for both the high-potency steroid group and control group. With regard to comfort, none of the patients treated with high-potency steroids reported discomfort, although mild conjunctival congestion was observed in one patient who received dexamethasone.

Discussion

According to the results, dual therapy with 0.05% CsA and high-potency steroids improved the symptoms and signs of DED, whereas therapy with 0.05% CsA alone improved only the symptoms of DED. Furthermore, according to the multivariable analysis results, the use of high-potency steroids is an independent protective factor against DED. In addition, in this study, the incidence of IOP elevation and cataract formation within 1 year was similar between the high-potency steroid and control groups.

Inflammation is believed to be the primary pathophysiology of DED, involving interleukins, tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-alpha and matrix metalloproteinases. These inflammatory mediators contribute to tear film instability and subsequent DED development [3, 9,10, 23]. Once DED develops, the apoptosis of ocular surface cells leads to nerve stimulation, causing ocular surface stress and inflammatory cytokine release, thereby initiating a vicious cycle of DED [13, 23]. In addition, MGD is a type of chronic inflammation where an elevated inflammatory response is observed [24]. Furthermore, tear samples of patients with DED exhibited higher levels of inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-alpha, interleukin-6 and interleukin-8, than those of healthy individuals [25]. DED is often associated with both local and systemic inflammatory disorders [5, 26]. Effective anti-inflammatory treatments for DED include azithromycin, l-carnitine, acetylcysteine and autologous serum [20]. The aforementioned findings indicate the relationship between inflammation and DED, consistent with the results of the current study.

Few studies have determined the predictors of therapeutic effectiveness in severe DED. In this study, the combined use of 0.05% CsA and high-potency steroids significantly improved both sign and symptom scores compared with 0.05% CsA monotherapy. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate the effectiveness of this dual therapy in reducing the signs and symptoms of severe DED after a variety of covariates were adjusted for. The findings of the generalized linear mixed model revealed that the use of acetylcysteine and autologous serum did not affect DED-related signs and symptoms and thus did not significantly influence dual therapy outcomes. Initially, the high-potency steroid group exhibited higher initial sign (14.0%) and symptom (11.3%) scores than did the control group. However, at the end of the follow-up period, the high-potency steroid group exhibited greater decreases in both sign (15.2% vs. 2.96%) and symptom (30.7% vs. 14.7%) scores compared with the control group. This finding indicates that adding high-potency steroids may lead to a similar degree of resolution in signs and symptoms, even in patients with more severe DED, demonstrating the effectiveness of high-potency steroids in managing severe DED. Topical steroids have been used as an effective treatment for inflammation in DED [9, 13, 16]. In previous studies, the use of loteprednol etabonate improved TBUT, Schirmer test and subjective symptoms in DED compared with 0.05% CsA alone [27,28]. However, these studies have primarily included patients with general DED and did not focus on those with severe DED. Although CsA is frequently prescribed for patients with severe DED [13], some patients may still experience inadequate symptom improvement [29]. The findings of the present study suggest that high-potency steroids instead of low-potency steroids could serve as an effective alternative or adjunctive therapy for severe DED, possibly due to their stronger anti-inflammatory effect. However, the long-term use of steroids may lead to steroid-induced ocular hypertension, glaucoma and cataract formation, which pose threats to vision [22, 30]. However, in our study, no cases of new-onset glaucoma, no increases in antiglaucoma medications, and no trabeculectomy arrangements were observed within 1 year in the high-potency steroid group. Moreover, this group exhibited a significantly lower IOP 6 months after treatment than did the control group. In addition, we observed no cases of vision-threatening cataracts requiring surgery in the high-potency steroid group, whereas two patients in the control group underwent cataract surgery during the 1-year follow-up. These results indicate that dual therapy with 0.05% CsA and high-potency steroids might be safe for patients with severe DED. However, regular monitoring for IOP changes and potential glaucoma events is strongly recommended.

In this study, sign scores generally improved more after dual therapy with 0.05% CsA and high-potency steroids compared with 0.05% CsA monotherapy, demonstrating the effectiveness of this treatment. Moreover, the symptom scores decreased more rapidly in the high-potency steroid group than in the control group. Because the participants in the control group only visited our ophthalmology department during the study period according to the records in Taiwan’s National Health Insurance system, they might not have received other treatments during the study period. A possible explanation for the different outcomes between the groups is that although the effectiveness of a high concentration of CsA (up to 2%) in treating DED has been confirmed, adverse effects, including burning, irritation, itching and other forms of ocular discomfort, are likely to occur [31–33]. Moreover, approximately 30% of patients experience pain at the instillation site after CsA use, and the OSDI did not significantly improve following CsA instillation alone [34]. Many patients in our study group had chronic inflammatory diseases, which can exacerbate the symptoms of DED [17], thus making the ocular irritation from CsA even more pronounced. Hence, the addition of high-potency steroids may help alleviate the irritation caused by CsA and subsequently reduce DED symptoms in individuals with inflammatory diseases.

Regarding other factors, the presence of more than two systemic diseases was associated with a poorer prognosis for DED, possibly because multiple diseases indicate a more severe inflammatory response. This phenomenon can lead to recalcitrant ocular surface inflammation, resulting in unchanged symptom and sign scores after treatment. Thus, more rapid and aggressive management of DED should be considered in such patients. A previous study reported the effectiveness of topical acetylcysteine and autologous serum eye drops in slowing the progression of DED [9]. However, the use of the two medications did not improve the symptoms and signs of severe DED in this study. This finding may be due to autologous serum being less effective in secondary SS, which occurred in many of our patients [20], and the lack of sufficient evidence supporting the benefits of acetylcysteine in severe DED [20]. Thus, the use of these two medications might not provide additional benefit for managing severe DED.

The present study has some limitations. First, the retrospective nature and nonrandomized design of the current study along with unmatched groups may introduce selection bias and reduce the homogeneity of the study population. Second, the severity of ADs and DM could be different, which was not accounted for in multivariate analysis. Moreover, the follow-up period for 0.05% CsA treatment was only 3 months, which is relatively short. Some patients discontinued CsA therapy prematurely and immediately after 3 months of 0.05% CsA because they felt that they were cured, thus we cannot conduct a longer follow-up period. Moreover, because of the retrospective design of our study and the lack of objective measurement devices for MGD at our institution, we were unable to obtain objective evaluations of MGD for this study population; this may have affected the precision of MGD evaluation. Finally, some diagnostic tests used in this study, including TBUT and the Schirmer I test without anaesthesia, are not recommended by the Tear Film & Ocular Surface Society Dry Eye Workshop II (TFOS DEWS II), which may reduce the integrity of this study.

In conclusion, the combined therapy of 0.05% CsA and high-potency steroids significantly reduces sign scores and rapidly improves symptom scores in severe DED. Furthermore, the safety profile of this dual therapy in terms of glaucoma and cataract development within 1 year is acceptable. Thus, the use of 0.05% CsA combined with high-potency steroids is recommended for patients with severe DED in clinical practice. Further large-scale, prospective studies should be conducted to determine the optimal formulation of this dual therapy and to compare its safety and efficiency with those of loteprednol for managing recalcitrant DED.
