Gestational lipid profile partially mediates adverse obstetric outcomes associated with polycystic ovary syndrome: a multicentre cohort study in China
Authors: Fangfang Wang, Leyi Fu, Tianyi Zhou, Feng Yun, Ying Li, Xinyin Hu, Lifang You, Tianjing Wang, Xi Xi, Yimin Zhou, Jinghua Zhang, Zhengqiang Jiang, Danqing Chen, Paul J Hardiman, Jae Youl Cho, Dawei Xie, Fan Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is associated with dyslipidaemia and adverse obstetric outcomes. Even among nonobese women, triglycerides (TG) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels differ between those with and without PCOS. This study aimed to elucidate the role of gestational lipid profiles in the association of PCOS with obstetric outcomes.MethodsThis multicentre retrospective cohort study enrolled 2,515 pregnant women from 2016 to 2021 at 3 hospitals (1 provincial and 2 municipal) to assess the associations between maternal PCOS (exposure), obstetric outcomes (cesarean delivery, gestational complications, foetal ultrasonographic biometric measurements and neonatal unit admission), and lipid profile markers (mediators) using multivariate linear and logistic regression models and mediation analyses.ResultsPregnant women with PCOS were more likely to have a cesarean delivery (OR = 1.28, 95% CI 1.08, 1.52) than those without PCOS were. The levels of maternal lipid profile markers were associated with cesarean delivery [triglyceride (log TG): OR = 1.62, 95% CI 1.29, 2.03; high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C): OR = 0.67, 95% CI 0.53, 0.85]. These associations were independent of maternal age, educational attainment, the rate of total gestational weight gain (GWG), gravidity, parity, and neonatal sex. In terms of the association between maternal PCOS and cesarean delivery, the mediation effect of TG was 0.010 (95% CI 0.005, 0.016), and that of HDL-C was 0.007 (95% CI 0.002, 0.013). With respect to the secondary outcomes, both TG and HDL-C partially mediated the link between maternal PCOS and pregnancy hypertensive disorders and biparietal diameter in late pregnancy, whereas only TG partially mediated the effect of maternal PCOS on gestational diabetes mellitus and preterm delivery. HDL-C partially mediated the effect of maternal PCOS on admission to neonatal units.ConclusionsGestational lipid profile markers partially mediated the link between maternal PCOS and adverse obstetric outcomes, including cesarean delivery, gestational complications, foetal ultrasonographic biometric measurements and neonatal unit admission, across different conception modes, independent of maternal age, educational attainment, the rate of total GWG, gravidity, parity and neonatal sex.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12944-026-03013-1.Keywords: Polycystic ovary syndrome, Gestational lipid profile, Adverse obstetric outcomes, Cesarean delivery, Mediating effect

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most common endocrinopathy among reproductive-aged women, affecting 10–13% of women worldwide [1, 2]. It is characterized primarily by ovulatory dysfunction, clinical and/or biochemical hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology [3]. Women with PCOS frequently exhibit subfertility [4] and are at an elevated risk for encountering pregnancy complications, including miscarriage, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, preterm delivery and cesarean delivery [1, 2, 5–7]. More critically, the offspring of women with PCOS are predisposed to a spectrum of short- and long-term health consequences, such as metabolic diseases and neuropsychiatric disorders [8–11]. A comprehensive understanding of the correlation between maternal PCOS and obstetric outcomes remains elusive, particularly regarding gestational and foetal intrauterine development. On the basis of the Barker hypothesis [12], it has been postulated that a suboptimal intrauterine environment, potentially induced by maternal PCOS, may serve as the genesis of long-term adverse health outcomes in offspring [13, 14]. Utilizing magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), we previously confirmed alterations in the brain metrics, growth, and developmental parameters of PCOS offspring in utero, as well as placental features [15, 16]. However, the aetiology remains largely unclear.

Women with PCOS have an increased prevalence of metabolic syndrome, which includes obesity, dyslipidaemia, hyperglycaemia, insulin resistance and hypertension [17]. PCOS is independently associated with a fourfold higher prevalence of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease characterized by intrahepatic triglyceride accumulation, which demonstrates therapeutic reversibility [18]. Traditional Chinese medicine can also be used to ameliorate lipid peroxidation-mediated ferroptosis in ovarian granulosa cells to alleviate the symptoms of PCOS [19]. Moreover, both PCOS and dyslipidaemia demonstrate significant metabolic interplay with body weight fluctuations [20]. Compared with nonobese women without PCOS, nonobese women with PCOS exhibit a higher prevalence of hypertriglyceridaemia (high-TG) and low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (low-HDL-C), but the prevalence of hypercholesterolemia does not differ significantly between these two groups [21]. Particularly in the nonobese population, the frequencies of high TG levels and low HDL-C levels, which indicate a higher risk of adverse metabolic outcomes such as cardiovascular disease, are still greater among women with PCOS than among those without PCOS [21]. Previous studies have indicated that dyslipidaemia during pregnancy, especially high triglyceride levels and low HDL-C levels, is related to adverse pregnancy outcomes, but the underlying mechanisms remain unclear [22, 23]. The findings showed that lipid profiles are altered, especially among nonobese women with PCOS during pregnancy, following natural conception or ovulation induction, and TG levels might be associated with pregnancy complications [24]. However, no significant association was found between HDL-C levels and obstetric complications [24]. Recently, the post-in vitro fertilization (IVF) pregnancy rate was reported to be significantly lower in women with PCOS and dyslipidaemia than in normolipidaemic women with PCOS [25]. However, the lipid profile of women with PCOS during pregnancy remains poorly characterized, and its role in the relationship between PCOS and obstetric outcomes remains unclear. Hence, the present study utilized multicentre clinical data to characterize lipid metabolism profiles in pregnant individuals with PCOS across different conception modes and to elucidate their potential implications for maternal-foetal clinical outcomes through a longitudinal cohort analysis of peripheral blood samples.

Materials and methods

Study population

To explore the association between maternal PCOS status and obstetric outcomes, an electronic health record (EHR)-based retrospective cohort study was performed at three hospitals: Women’s Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University (Maternal Emergency and Critical Care Center of Zhejiang Province); Hangzhou Linping District First People’s Hospital; and Linhai Maternal and Child Health Hospital. Data were retrieved from the EHRs from January 2016 to December 2021. Pregnant women who received routine prenatal care at the hospital were screened, and those with a prepregnancy diagnosis of PCOS were included in the PCOS group, whereas those without PCOS were randomly selected as controls. The randomization sequence was generated using the RAND function in Microsoft Excel (version 2016) [26]. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age < 20 years or > 40 years; (2) multiple pregnancy, miscarriage, abortion or stillbirth; (3) insufficient evidence for grouping (PCOS or non-PCOS); prepregnancy cardiovascular, endocrine (including diabetes mellitus and other glucose metabolism abnormalities, with the exception of PCOS), haematologic, autoimmune, hepatobiliary, renal diseases, or mental disorders; (4) prenatally diagnosed foetal abnormalities, including structural malformations, genetic anomalies and intrauterine foetal demise; (5) neonates with postnatally diagnosed congenital diseases; and (6) missing data for obstetric outcomes, lipid profiles or foetal ultrasonography. Finally, 1,143 pregnant women with PCOS and 1,372 pregnant women without PCOS were included (shown in Fig. 1). The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Hospital (ethical approval number IRB-20220164-R). The included women provided written consent for the use of their information from their medical records.

Main exposure: PCOS

The diagnosis of PCOS was reconfirmed at the first visit according to the Rotterdam Consensus diagnostic criteria [27] and previous research in China [28]. The diagnostic criteria for PCOS required the presence of any two of the following three features: (1) oligo-ovulation and/or anovulation; (2) clinical and/or biochemical signs of hyperandrogenism; and (3) ovarian morphology showing characteristic polycystic features on ultrasound. Patients with other aetiologies of oligomenorrhea or hyperandrogenism, such as Cushing syndrome, delayed adrenocortical hyperplasia, and adrenal androgen-secreting tumours, were excluded.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was maternal cesarean delivery, and the eight secondary outcomes included GDM, pregnancy hypertensive disorders, preterm delivery, two foetal ultrasonographic biometric measurements (z scores of biparietal diameter (BPD) and femur length (FL)), large-for-gestational age (LGA), small-for-gestational age (SGA) and offspring admission to the neonatal unit. Pregnant women were diagnosed with GDM on the basis of a 2-h 75 g oral glucose tolerance test at a gestational age of 24–28 weeks when they met any of the following thresholds: [1] a fasting plasma glucose concentration ≥ 5.1 mmol/L (≥ 92 mg/dL); [2] a 1-h plasma glucose concentration ≥ 10.0 mmol/L (≥ 180 mg/dL); or [3] a 2-h plasma glucose concentration ≥ 8.5 mmol/L (≥ 153 mg/dL) [29]. Pregnancy hypertensive disorders were a composite of preeclampsia, eclampsia, and gestational hypertension, excluding individuals with chronic hypertension [30]. Preterm delivery was defined as delivery < 37+ 0 weeks. With respect to foetal ultrasonographic biometric measurements, including z scores of BPD and FL, we collected the foetal ultrasound measurement records from mid- and late pregnancy. If multiple measurements (BPD and FL) were obtained in one period, the mean values of the z scores were considered for the statistical analyses [31]. LGA and SGA were defined as neonatal birth weights greater than the 90th percentile and less than the 10th percentile for gestational age, respectively, according to the country-adjusted reference curve for the Chinese population [32]. Neonatal unit admission was defined as admission to the neonatal unit in the first 3–5 days of life for the following predefined criteria: respiratory distress, bradycardia or tachycardia, hypotonia, lethargy or difficult to arouse, hypertonia or irritability, poor feeding or emesis, hypoglycaemia, oxygen desaturation or cyanosis, the need for oxygen, apnoea, seizures or seizure-like activity, hyperbilirubinemia, and/or temperature instability, infection or the need for antibiotics as well as observation criteria [33]. To avoid the analytical complication of repeated measures, we included only the most recent live birth in this analysis.

Mediators of interest

Venous blood samples were collected from all participants after an overnight fast for lipid profile measurement from the second trimester (gestational weeks 24–28) to the third trimester (gestational weeks > 28) of pregnancy. Each sample was analysed to determine the concentrations of TG, total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and HDL-C. The levels of TC and TG were measured using the cholesterol oxidase-phenol aminophenazone (PAP) enzymatic method and the glycerol-3-phosphate oxidase-PAP enzymatic method, respectively. LDL-C and HDL-C concentrations were quantified through homogeneous enzymatic colorimetric assays. All lipid measurements were performed on an automated biochemical analyser (Abbott Architect C16000, Abbott Laboratories, USA) utilizing specific detection reagents for TC, TG, LDL-C and HDL-C (Abbott Diagnostic Kit, Abbott Laboratories, USA). The normal lipid concentration ranges measured by Architect C16000 were less than 1.71 mmol/L for TG, less than 5.20 mmol/L for TC, less than 3.12 mmol/L for LDL-C and from 1.29 to 1.55 mmol/L for HDL-C. A linear range of 0.08 to 16.05 mmol/L was used for TG, a range of 0.18 to 18.26 mmol/L was used for TC, a range of 0.13 to 4.66 mmol/L was used for HDL-C and a range of 0.03 to 20.69 mmol/L was used for LDL-C. For the lipid profile testing, the intra- and interassay variations were set at ≤ 5% for TG, ≤ 3% for TC, ≤ 4% for HDL-C and < 4% for LDL-C. Given that gestational levels of lipid profile markers, including elevated TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, and TC levels, increase from the second to the third trimesters [34], the peak value was selected to reflect the maximal intensity of metabolic stress during pregnancy, when multiple lipid profiles were available during the second and third trimesters [35].

Covariates

Covariates of interest included demographic information (age and educational attainment) and obstetric information (prepregnancy body mass index (BMI), the rate of total gestational weight gain (GWG), gravidity, parity, mode of conception and neonatal sex). Age, educational attainment (lower than college education, college education, or higher than college education), gravidity (1, 2, 3 or > 3), parity (nulliparity or multiparity) and mode of conception (natural, ovulation induction, intrauterine insemination (IUI), or IVF-embryo transfer (ET)) were obtained from the EHRs. Prepregnancy BMI (underweight (< 18.5), normal weight (18.5–23.9), or overweight/obese (≥ 24)) was determined using self-reported body weight and height (without shoes) before pregnancy [36]. The total gestational weight gain was defined as the difference between self-reported prepregnancy weight and the last clinically measured weight recorded [37, 38], and the rate of total gestational gain was calculated by dividing the total kilograms gained by the gestational age [37]. Gestational age was estimated according to the participant’s self-reported last menstrual period (LMP) date or recalculated by crown–rump length at the scan of translucency thickness if the LMP was obscure or if there was an obvious difference between the ultrasonographic measurement and LMP dating (> 7 days) [39, 40].

Statistical analysis

On the basis of the previous rates of primary outcomes [5, 41] and the rates of obstetric outcomes in our hospital, the required sample size was at least 1,074 per group (α = 0.05 and power = 0.95). The sample size was calculated using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) (www.gpower.hhu.de/en.html). Therefore, we used data collected over 6 years to achieve the required sample size. Normality assumptions were checked first, and the continuous variables are reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (25th quartile, 75th quartile), whereas the categorical variables are expressed as percentages. A logarithmic transformation of TG levels was used for regression analysis and mediation analysis. Unadjusted differences between participants with and without PCOS were assessed using independent t tests (for continuous variables) or chi-square tests. The Mann‒Whitney U test was used for nonnormally distributed data. Effect estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported. A two-tailed p value of 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Descriptive statistics and statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS (version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

On the basis of the results of the preliminary analysis above, outcomes demonstrating significant differences (p < 0.05) between pregnant women with PCOS and the control group were further analysed using multivariable regression. Multivariate logistic regression models were created to test the potential associations between PCOS or lipid levels and outcome variables (cesarean delivery, GDM, pregnancy hypertensive disorders, preterm delivery, and admission to neonatal units). Multivariate linear regression models were constructed to investigate the association between PCOS or lipid levels and foetal BPD or FL variables. Similar analyses were performed for PCOS (exposure) and lipid levels (outcomes). Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs were determined, along with β coefficients and their corresponding standard errors (SEs). The covariates were adjusted in models based on clinical relevance [42], and all the models were adjusted for age, educational attainment, the rate of total GWG, gravidity, parity and neonatal sex [43–45] after preliminary screening for mediating effects between the covariates and the exposure and the primary outcome. Because prepregnancy BMI and the mode of conception both acted as complete mediators in the association between maternal PCOS status and primary outcome, they were excluded from the adjustment factors [46, 47]. All remaining covariates were adjusted for in the final model to minimize residual confounding. We performed a sensitivity analysis by including prepregnancy BMI and the mode of conception in the regression model, and complete mediation was supported if their adjustment attenuated the association between maternal PCOS status and the primary outcome to nonsignificance. Prior to the regression analysis, we evaluated multicollinearity among the confounders by calculating variance inflation factors to confirm the absence of multicollinearity problems. Additionally, we conducted subgroup analyses to determine the specific concentration thresholds of lipid profiles that were significantly associated with the outcomes of interest. This was achieved through multivariable regression models evaluating both per 1-SD increments lipid concentrations and the variations across the interquartile ranges of individual lipid subfractions. We further evaluated the potential effect modification by maternal age (< 35 or ≥ 35 years) and parity, which included appropriate interaction terms with lipid concentrations as continuous variables for outcomes with significant differences in the subgroup analyses. All reported p values are two-tailed at 0.05 and were not adjusted for multiple testing [30].

In the above regression models, if a lipid variable was not associated with the exposure or the outcome (p > 0.05), then it was not a mediator and was therefore excluded from the subsequent mediation analysis. The mediation analysis was conducted using the lavaan package in R software (version 4.4.2) to assess the mediating role of qualifying lipid variables in the association between PCOS and the outcome variables above, adjusting for potential confounding factors, including age, educational attainment, the rate of total GWG, gravidity, parity and neonatal sex. In this analysis, we estimated the direct effect, indirect effects through the mediators, and the total effect of PCOS on the outcome variables. The proportion of the effect mediated by each mediator was also calculated. The indirect effects represent the effects of PCOS on the outcome variables mediated through the qualifying lipid variables, whereas the direct effect represents the remaining effect of PCOS on the outcome variables after accounting for these mediators. To ensure robust inference, the 95% CI for the effects was estimated using the nonparametric bootstrap method with 5,000 bootstrap samples. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

As the data drawn from the EHR were not complete for LDL-C levels (Table S4) and ultrasonographic biometric measurements (BPD and FL, Table S5), missing data were assumed to be missing at random. Multiple imputations were performed using multivariate imputation by the chained equations technique. Predictive mean matching, a method suitable for continuous variables, was applied for the imputations. Five imputed datasets were generated using the observed covariates as predictors of missing values.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

Pregnant women with PCOS had a higher BMI before pregnancy (P < 0.001) but comparable total GWG rates after routine pregnancy weight management, mainly in terms of lifestyle guidance (Table 1). The proportion of nulliparous women (73.3% vs. 68.9%, P = 0.015) was greater among pregnant women with PCOS than among those without PCOS, and the modes of conception were significantly different between the groups (P < 0.001). In terms of the peak values of the lipid profiles (assessed as continuous variables), higher TG levels (P < 0.001) but lower HDL-C (P < 0.001) and LDL-C (P = 0.003) levels were detected in pregnant women with PCOS than in the controls. With respect to pregnancy complications, a higher prevalence of GDM (22.7% vs. 16.4%, P < 0.001) and pregnancy hypertensive disorders (10.4% vs. 4.0%, P < 0.001) as well as preterm delivery (7.3% vs. 4.0%, P < 0.001) were observed among pregnant women with PCOS. In addition, a higher rate of cesarean delivery (37.9% vs. 32.1%, P = 0.003) was observed in the PCOS group than in the control group. With respect to foetal growth, we found lower z scores for BPD and FL during mid- and late pregnancy (all P < 0.001) and comparable rates of LGA and SGA among newborns in the PCOS group than among newborns in the control group. Therefore, we did not include these two outcomes in the regression or mediation analyses. Furthermore, a higher percentage of offspring admitted to neonatal units (43.7% vs. 27.1%, P < 0.001) was observed in the PCOS group than in the control group.

Logistic regression analyses

Initially, the multicollinearity among the covariates adjusted in the regression models was evaluated by calculating their variance inflation factor values, and all of them were less than 2, indicating that there was no excessive multicollinearity. Multiple logistic regression models confirmed that compared with those for pregnant women without PCOS, the adjusted odds of cesarean delivery for those with PCOS were 1.28 (95% CI 1.08, 1.52) after adjusting for age, educational attainment, total GWG, gravidity, parity and neonatal sex (Fig. 2A). After additional adjustment for prepregnancy BMI and mode of conception (Table S6), the association between PCOS and cesarean delivery became nonsignificant, which is consistent with their complete mediating role. Greater odds of GDM (OR = 1.51, 95% CI 1.23, 1.86), pregnancy hypertensive disorders (OR = 2.69, 95% CI 1.92, 3.77), preterm delivery (OR = 1.86, 95% CI 1.30, 2.65) and offspring admission to neonatal units (OR = 2.10, 95% CI 1.77, 2.50) were also observed among pregnant women with PCOS after adjusting for age, educational attainment, the rate of total GWG, gravidity, parity and neonatal sex.

The components of the lipid profile, log TG, TC, HDL-C and LDL-C (assessed as continuous variables), were introduced as independent exposure variables (Fig. 2A). We found positive associations between log TG and cesarean delivery (OR = 1.62, 95% CI 1.29, 2.03), GDM (OR = 2.08, 95% CI 1.60, 2.71) and pregnancy hypertensive disorders (OR = 3.18, 95% CI 2.16, 4.69), but negative associations between log TG and preterm delivery (OR = 0.58, 95% CI 0.36, 0.93) after adjusting for age, educational attainment, the rate of total GWG, gravidity, parity and neonatal sex. Furthermore, negative associations were observed between HDL-C levels and cesarean delivery (OR = 0.67, 95% CI 0.53, 0.85), pregnancy hypertensive disorders (OR = 0.39, 95% CI 0.25, 0.62) and admission to neonatal units (OR = 0.68, 95% CI 0.54, 0.86) among offspring after adjusting for age, educational attainment, the rate of total GWG, gravidity, parity and neonatal sex. Additionally, a negative association between TC and offspring admission to neonatal units (OR = 0.91, 95% CI 0.84, 0.98) was observed after adjusting for age, educational attainment, the rate of total GWG, gravidity, parity and neonatal sex. A negative association was observed between multiple imputed LDL-C levels and pregnancy hypertensive disorders (OR = 0.77, 95% CI 0.65, 0.92) after adjusting for age, educational attainment, the rate of total GWG, gravidity, parity and neonatal sex, which was consistent with the results of the nonimputed data (Table S4).

Linear regression analyses

To explore the roles of maternal PCOS status (exposure) and lipid profile levels (potential mediators, assessed as continuous variables) in foetal growth (outcome), we performed linear regression analyses and found that maternal PCOS was negatively associated with BPD and FL in mid- and late pregnancy after multiple imputation (Fig. 2B), mostly corresponding to the results of the nonimputed data (Table S5), and both the imputed and nonimputed results were adjusted for age, educational attainment, the rate of total GWG, gravidity, parity and neonatal sex. Compared with those without PCOS, pregnant women with PCOS had significantly higher levels of TG and lower levels of HDL-C and LDL-C in peripheral blood (Table S1) after adjusting for age, educational attainment, the rate of total GWG, gravidity, parity and neonatal sex. In addition, the associations between the lipid profile and foetal growth were nonsignificant across most linear regression analyses (Fig. 2B), which corresponded to the results of the nonimputed data (Table S5), and both the imputed and nonimputed results were adjusted for age, educational attainment, the rate of total GWG, gravidity, parity and neonatal sex. However, we found that the level of TG was positively associated with BPD in late pregnancy and that the level of LDL-C was positively related to BPD in mid-pregnancy, but the level of HDL-C was negatively related to BPD in late pregnancy after multiple imputation (Fig. 2B) after adjusting for age, educational attainment, the rate of total GWG, gravidity, parity and neonatal sex.

Mediation analyses

The associations between PCOS and adverse obstetric outcomes mediated by maternal lipid profile markers are shown in Fig. 3 and Table S2 according to the results of the regression analyses, with adjustments for age, educational attainment, the rate of total GWG, gravidity, parity and neonatal sex. First, TG and HDL-C significantly partially mediated the relationship between maternal PCOS and cesarean delivery, with the indirect effect of TG being 0.010 (95% CI 0.005, 0.016), accounting for 18.4% of the total effect, and with the indirect effect of HDL-C being 0.007 (95% CI 0.002, 0.013), accounting for 13.1% of the total effect. Second, the association between maternal PCOS and GDM was partially mediated by TG, with the indirect effect of TG being 0.010 (95% CI 0.006, 0.016), accounting for 16.8% of the total effect. Third, TG and HDL-C levels significantly partially mediated the relationship between maternal PCOS and pregnancy hypertensive disorders, with the indirect effect of TG being 0.007 (95% CI 0.004, 0.011), accounting for 11.8% of the total effect, and with the indirect effect of HDL-C being 0.004 (95% CI 0.002, 0.007), accounting for 7.1% of the total effect. However, we did not find a mediating effect of LDL-C on the relationship between maternal PCOS and pregnancy hypertensive disorders. Fourth, TG partially mediated the effect of maternal PCOS on preterm delivery, with the indirect effect of TG being − 0.003 (95% CI -0.006, -0.001), accounting for − 10.6% of the total effect. Fifth, the relationship between maternal PCOS and admission to the neonatal unit was partially mediated by HDL-C, with the indirect effect of TG being 0.005 (95% CI 0.001, 0.010), accounting for 3.3% of the total effect. Finally, TG and HDL-C levels significantly partially mediated the relationship between maternal PCOS and BPD in late pregnancy, with the indirect effect of TG being 0.017 (95% CI 0.005, 0.029), accounting for − 9.3% of the total effect, and with the indirect effect of HDL-C being 0.018 (95% CI 0.007, 0.030), accounting for − 9.8% of the total effect. However, we found no mediating effect of LDL-C on the relationship between maternal PCOS and BPD during mid-pregnancy.

Subgroup analyses

On the basis of the results above, multiple logistic regressions were performed to explore the associations between maternal TG and HDL-C levels and obstetric outcomes in the subgroups of pregnant women with and without PCOS (Table S3), with adjustments for age, educational attainment, the rate of total GWG, gravidity, parity and neonatal sex. The continuous and quartile models of TG and HDL-C levels were similarly significant. TG gestational peak concentration in the third quartile and above was associated with higher odds of cesarean delivery (all P < 0.05, OR = 1.46 at quartile 3 and 1.84 at quartile 4) among pregnant women with PCOS than among those in the reference quartile (lowest quartile). However, compared with those in the reference quartile, pregnant women without PCOS were 1.65 times more likely to have a cesarean delivery (P < 0.01) when the gestational peak TG concentration reached the highest quartile. In addition, compared with those in the reference quartile, both pregnant women with and without PCOS had increased risks of GDM and pregnancy hypertensive disorders (all P < 0.05) when the gestational peak TG concentration was in the highest quartile. Moreover, compared with those in the reference quartile (lowest quartile), pregnant women with PCOS with a gestational peak HDL-C concentration in the highest quartile were 0.53 times more likely to have GDM and 0.44 times more likely to have pregnancy hypertensive disorders (all P < 0.01). However, compared with those in the reference quartile (lowest quartile), pregnant women without PCOS with a gestational peak HDL-C concentration in the highest quartile had 0.59 times the odds of cesarean delivery and 0.67 times the odds of admission to neonatal units (all P < 0.05).

In particular, compared with the first quartile, the highest two quartiles (OR 1.46; 95% CI 1.02, 2.01 for quartile 3; OR 1.84; 95% CI 1.30, 2.63 for quartile 4) of the gestational peak TG concentration in pregnant women with PCOS were significantly associated with increased odds of cesarean delivery, whereas the highest two quartiles (OR 0.61; 95% CI 0.41, 0.90 for quartile 3; OR 0.53; 95% CI 0.35 0.79 for quartile 4) of gestational peak HDL-C concentrations in pregnant women with PCOS were significantly associated with decreased odds of GDM (P < 0.05), reflecting that the increasing risk of cesarean delivery might be displayed by increasing concentrations of TG and the increasing risk of GDM might be displayed by decreasing concentrations of HDL-C in pregnant women with PCOS. In addition, we modelled potential effect modification by including interaction terms by maternal age or parity with TG or HDL-C levels as continuous variables in multivariable logistic regressions, focusing on outcomes that demonstrated significant differences in the subgroup analyses. There was no significant effect modification by maternal age or parity on any of the associations examined.

Discussion

The present study was the first to address the mediating role of gestational lipid profiles in the impact of maternal PCOS on adverse obstetric outcomes across different conception modes. Data from our multicentre cohort indicated that both maternal PCOS and lipid profiles were associated with adverse obstetric outcomes, including cesarean delivery, common gestational complications (GDM, pregnancy hypertensive disorders and preterm delivery), foetal ultrasonographic biometric measurements and neonatal unit admission, with a significant correlation between maternal PCOS and lipid profiles. These associations were independent of maternal age, educational attainment, the rate of total GWG, gravidity, parity and neonatal sex. Mediation analyses confirmed that maternal lipid profile markers, especially TG levels, act as partial mediators in the association between PCOS and these adverse obstetric outcomes.

Notably, compared with pregnant women without PCOS, pregnant women with a history of PCOS had higher rates of overweight/obesity, higher proportions of IVF-ET conception and nulliparity (with comparable parity history), and lower educational attainment. These findings reinforce the notion that PCOS is characterized by metabolic dysfunction, fertility impairments and obstetric challenges, as well as a lower socioeconomic status, necessitating tailored clinical strategies to mitigate gestational risks in this population. Previous studies have shown that elevated serum TG concentrations are related to adverse pregnancy outcomes, including miscarriage, pregnancy-induced hypertension, preeclampsia, GDM, preterm delivery, LGA and SGA, in European women with PCOS following natural conception or ovulation induction [24]. On the basis of a different population and a larger sample size, our study not only confirmed that increasing TG levels were associated with maternal PCOS but also found that decreasing HDL-C levels were related to maternal PCOS across different conception modes in Asian women. Moreover, we provided new insights by showing that alterations in gestational lipid profiles were associated with cesarean delivery and offspring admission to neonatal units among women with PCOS. Lipid profiles were mostly correlated with obstetric outcomes when serum levels reached the highest quartile. Nevertheless, these lipid changes should not be overinterpreted as pathological hyperlipidaemia without clinical correlation, given that the understanding of the effects of gestational dyslipidaemia on both maternal and foetal outcomes is limited [22].

Our subsequent analyses explored mainly the potential mediating role of lipid profiles in the relationship between PCOS and obstetric outcomes, with adjustments for confounding factors to minimize confounding effects. The results showed that maternal TG and HDL-C levels partially mediated the association between maternal PCOS and cesarean delivery, mediating 18.4% and 13.1% of the effects, respectively. Moreover, we found that TG partially mediated the effects of maternal PCOS on pregnancy hypertensive disorders, GDM and preterm delivery, which accounted for 16.8%, 11.8% and − 10.6% of the total effect, respectively. HDL-C levels were found to partially mediate the effect of maternal PCOS on pregnancy hypertensive disorders, accounting for 7.1% of the total effect. The relationship between maternal PCOS and offspring admission to neonatal units was mediated only by HDL-C levels, which accounted for 3.3% of the total effect. Thus, lipid profiles play an important role in the association between PCOS and adverse obstetric outcomes.

The cesarean delivery rate, a key perinatal outcome indicator, has been experiencing a persistent global surge and is projected to increase further in the upcoming decade [48]. Here, we corroborated the association between PCOS and an elevated cesarean delivery rate after adjusting for confounding factors. Lending support to our results, a recent meta-analysis suggested that increased odds of cesarean delivery were associated with PCOS but not with postassisted reproductive technology pregnancies [49]. However, a notable absence of biomarkers for predicting the risk of cesarean delivery remains, particularly among pregnant women diagnosed with PCOS. After higher serum TG levels but lower HDL-C levels were verified in the PCOS group during pregnancy, we found that the gestational lipid profile, specifically the TG and HDL-C levels, might hold promise as biomarkers for monitoring women with PCOS to control the cesarean delivery rate.

To gain insights into the potential factors contributing to the elevated cesarean delivery rates observed among women with PCOS, we focused our attention on the prevalence of common gestational complications, GDM, pregnancy hypertensive disorders and preterm delivery, which are clinically correlated with cesarean deliveries guided by specific medical indicators [50–52]. Our findings demonstrated that GDM, pregnancy hypertensive disorders and preterm delivery were linked to PCOS, with TG levels serving as a mediator of these gestational complications after the contributions of confounding factors, including maternal age and weight, were excluded. Nevertheless, a concern arising from the present results warrants attention: although PCOS was positively associated with TG [21], TG was negatively associated with preterm birth, resulting in a negative mediating effect of TG on the association between PCOS and preterm delivery. Given that the association between TG levels and preterm delivery remains controversial [53–56], our findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive, and large-sample, high-quality prospective studies are needed to clarify the uncertainty of these findings. To the best of our knowledge, this study introduces a novel concept by proposing the TG level as a potential biomarker for adverse maternal outcomes in pregnant women with PCOS, which encompasses cesarean deliveries and common gestational complications. Hence, women with a history of PCOS should receive appropriate monitoring and management of their lipid profiles during pregnancy to ensure optimal health outcomes, regardless of maternal age or weight.

With respect to the health outcomes of offspring, increasing evidence over several decades has shown that offspring born to mothers with PCOS exhibit a significantly higher risk of admission to a neonatal intensive care unit and higher perinatal mortality unrelated to multiple births [57]. A recent cohort study suggested that maternal PCOS status was associated with lower birth weight, shorter birth length, and smaller head circumference, suggesting the presence of growth restriction in newborns [58]. Elevated TC levels might decrease the cumulative live birth rate after the first ovarian stimulation in women with PCOS [59]. Consistently, our present study revealed that offspring born to mothers with PCOS demonstrated a greater rate of admission to the neonatal unit, and prenatal ultrasonography is suggestive of impaired foetal growth. Given the strong link between the maternal lipid profile and neonatal unit admission, we investigated the potential mediator in the association between maternal PCOS and offspring admission and found that HDL-C levels partially mediated the association above. In addition, we also revealed that TG and HDL-C levels partially mediated the association between maternal PCOS and foetal BPD during late pregnancy. Thus, further studies are needed to understand the mechanisms underlying the role of lipids in the impact of maternal PCOS on offspring health.

The strengths of our multicentre cohort study lie in its comprehensive gestational lipid profiles and foetal ultrasonography data collected throughout the gestational period, demonstrating the independent effects of maternal PCOS status on obstetric outcomes and the mediating role of dyslipidaemia. Moreover, our findings underscore the importance of gestational monitoring and the potential for modifying lipid metabolism, particularly in women with PCOS and their offspring. However, our study has several limitations. First, the sample size is relatively small. Thus, the conclusions drawn require validation through future studies with larger populations. Second, owing to the observational features of cohort study designs, we were unable to establish causal relationships between PCOS and obstetric outcomes. Moreover, as BMI and the conception mode may simultaneously function as mediators, confounders, and markers of disease severity, the causal relationships between BMI/conception mode and both the exposure and outcomes are complex, and observational mediation analyses cannot fully disentangle these intertwined roles, even after we excluded them from the primary models. Third, our mediation analysis assumptions (e.g., no unmeasured confounding) cannot be empirically verified. Although we adjusted for all confounding variables identified in the literature, residual confounding may still exist. Fourth, women with more frequent lipid measurements may have a higher probability of exhibiting extreme values, potentially introducing measurement-related bias. Using peak lipid levels, which may overestimate risk, along with missing data from foetal ultrasonographic biometric measurements during gestation, limits the generalizability of our results. Fifth, the multiple secondary outcomes examined may increase the risk of type I errors, as the current study is an exploratory analysis and did not adjust for multiple comparisons. Our results should be interpreted with caution and serve as hypothesis-generating for future studies. Some statistically significant findings, particularly secondary and subgroup analyses, should be interpreted as exploratory. Sixth, the metabolism of lipid profile markers, including TG and HDL, is strongly related to glucose metabolism, and PCOS is typically associated with insulin resistance. In this study, dichotomizing blood glucose into a GDM dummy variable may reduce the statistical power and obscure dose‑response relationships. Seventh, the primary outcome of our study was cesarean delivery and we excluded the individuals with miscarriage, abortion and stillbirth, due to pregnancy interruption and a preference for vaginal delivery. This exclusion might have an impact on the accurate assessment of cesarean risk in women with PCOS. Finally, the definition of neonatal unit admission, one of the secondary outcomes, includes a broad range of clinical indications with varying severity. Therefore, future research should focus on validation in larger, more diverse populations, potential intervention in the gestational lipid profile of women with PCOS, and elucidation of its underlying molecular mechanism.

Conclusions

The present study provides the first multicentre clinical evidence that gestational lipid profile markers act as partial mediators in the link between maternal PCOS and adverse obstetric outcomes, including cesarean delivery, GDM, pregnancy hypertensive disorders, preterm delivery, foetal biparietal diameter in late pregnancy and neonatal unit admission, across different conception modes, independent of maternal age, educational attainment, and the rate of total GWG, gravidity, parity and neonatal sex.
