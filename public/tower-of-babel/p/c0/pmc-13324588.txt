Bioprocess Development of a Human Monoclonal Antibody against MERS-CoV: Analytical and Preclinical Studies
Authors: Jongsun Lim, Hansaem Lee, Joo-Yeon Lee, Dong-Gyu Jo, Sangwon Hong, So-Young Lee, Byoung Kwon Park, Yungjun Jeong, Jimin Seo, Soya Park, Hwanui Jung, Chan Ki Park, Kyung-Chang Kim, Ickhyun Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Middle East Respiratory Syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV) is a zoonotic virus first identified in 2012, responsible for severe respiratory illness and a high case fatality rate of approximately 35%. MERS-CoV is primarily transmitted from dromedary camels to humans, but human-to-human transmission, particularly in healthcare settings, has led to outbreaks beyond the Middle East, including a major epidemic in South Korea in 2015. Despite its significant public health threat and potential for future outbreaks, no approved vaccines or therapeutic agents are available for MERS-CoV. To address this urgent need, the Korea National Institute of Health (KNIH) developed therapeutic monoclonal antibodies derived from B cells of convalescent MERS patients. Among these, KNIH-88, a non-receptor-binding domain (non-RBD) S1-specific antibody, was selected for preclinical development. In this study, KNIH-88 was produced with high purity under controlled manufacturing conditions and extensively characterized for structural and physicochemical properties, including molecular weight, charge, hydrophobicity, and high molecular weight (HMW) profile. KNIH-88 demonstrated strong binding affinity to MERS-CoV Spike protein and potent neutralizing activity in vitro. To ensure stability and facilitate rapid deployment in outbreak scenarios, a lyophilized formulation of KNIH-88 was developed, maintaining structural integrity and biological activity under long-term storage. Collectively, these findings support KNIH-88 as a promising therapeutic antibody for MERS-CoV, combining potent antiviral efficacy, structural stability, and a formulation suitable for stockpiling and emergency use in endemic regions.

INTRODUCTION

Since its emergence in 2012, Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (MERS), caused by a zoonotic coronavirus (MERS-CoV), has been reported in 27 countries, with the majority of cases occurring in the Middle East. Although its human-to-human transmission rate is relatively low, the case fatality rate of approximately 35% underscores its significant public health threat (Ki, 2015; Kim et al., 2016; Rabaan et al., 2021; World Health Organization, 2025). Given its potential for sporadic outbreaks and global transmission, continuous surveillance and the development of effective therapeutic strategies remain crucial.

MERS-CoV is primarily transmitted from dromedary camels to humans through direct or indirect contact, making it a zoonotic disease with significant epidemic potential (Azhar et al., 2014). Bats are considered a potential natural reservoir, although no direct transmission from bats to humans has been confirmed. Following zoonotic transmission, human-to-human transmission can occur, particularly in healthcare settings through respiratory droplets and close contact, often leading to nosocomial outbreaks (Ki, 2015). The viral entry mechanism involves binding of the MERS-CoV Spike (S) protein to dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP4/CD26) on host cells, facilitating viral fusion and entry (Raj et al., 2013). Once inside the host, MERS-CoV primarily targets lower respiratory tract epithelial cells, alveolar macrophages, and endothelial cells, causing severe pulmonary inflammation and tissue damage (Chu et al., 2016). This pathology is exacerbated by a dysregulated immune response, including the excessive production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, which contributes to acute lung injury and high mortality rates (Zhou et al., 2014). Thus, understanding MERS-CoV transmission dynamics and pathogenesis is essential for developing effective therapeutic antibodies that can block viral entry and modulate host immune responses.

In 2015, South Korea experienced the largest outbreak of Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (MERS) outside the Middle East, resulting in 186 confirmed cases and 38 fatalities (World Health Organization, 2025). This outbreak underscored the potential for global spread of MERS-CoV and highlighted the urgent need for effective therapeutic interventions.

Despite the severity of MERS-CoV infections, no specific antiviral treatments or vaccines have been approved to date. In 2020, the Korea National Institute of Health (KNIH) successfully developed therapeutic monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) derived from B cells of convalescent MERS patients, specifically targeting the MERS-CoV S protein (Lee et al., 2025). These mAbs have shown promising neutralizing activity against the virus, marking a significant advancement in MERS therapeutic research.

Among the identified candidates, KNIH-88, a non-receptor-binding domain (non-RBD) S1-specific mAb, was selected for preclinical evaluation of stability and efficacy. To facilitate its development, ProtiumScience collaborated with KNIH to produce high-purity antibody samples under controlled manufacturing conditions, ensuring reproducibility and quality.

The structural, physicochemical, and functional properties of KNIH-88 were comprehensively characterized to assess its potential as a therapeutic antibody. This study included structural modeling-based binding prediction, physicochemical profiling, and in vitro efficacy assessments, providing an in-depth understanding of its therapeutic potential.

To enable stable and rapid deployment of KNIH-88 in regions prone to potential MERS outbreaks, a lyophilized formulation was developed to improve long-term storage stability and facilitate distribution. Stability studies confirmed that the lyophilized antibody maintained structural integrity, purity, and biological activity, making it a viable option for global stockpiling and emergency response.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Production of KNIH-88 for nonclinical stability studies

Cell line development: A high-expression CHO cell line for KNIH-88 production was developed by co-transfecting vectors encoding the heavy and light chains into ExpiCHO-S cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Stable pools were selected using methotrexate (MTX) and puromycin, and antibody titers were measured using a CEDEX Bio analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). High-producing clones were identified by single-cell sorting using FACS Melody (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), and monoclonality was verified using Cell Metric (Sartorius, Royston, UK). A final production clone was selected based on stability and productivity for the establishment of the research cell bank (RCB).

Upstream process development: The upstream process was initiated by thawing a vial from the RCB, followed by inoculum expansion in shake flasks and disposable bioreactors. Cells were cultured in ExpiCHO Stable Production AGT medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 4 mM GlutaMAX™, 1% Anti-Clumping Agent, 500 nM MTX, and 30 µg/mL puromycin up to passage 5. From the N–2 step onward, medium without selection agents was used.

The main culture was performed in a 200 L single-use bioreactor with a working volume of 95 kg for 13 days, under the following conditions: DO 50%, pH 7.1 (± 0.2), seeding density 0.4×10⁶ cells/mL, and 7% feeding ratio. Efficient-Pro AGT Feed 2 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as the feed.

Harvested culture fluid was clarified using depth filters (D0HC and X0HC, Pall Corporation, Port Washington, NY, USA) and 0.2 µm microfiltration, then transferred to the downstream process.

Downstream process development: The purification of KNIH-88 was performed through a three-step chromatography process. Protein A affinity chromatography using MabSelect PrismA resin (Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden) was employed for initial capture, followed by low pH viral inactivation (pH 3.5-3.7) and neutralization to pH 7.5. Impurities and particulates were removed via depth filtration using X0HC filters (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany).

The clarified product was further purified by anion-exchange chromatography (AEX) using Poros HQ resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and mixed-mode chromatography (MMC) using Capto MMC ImpRes resin (Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden). The final product underwent virus filtration, ultrafiltration/diafiltration (UF/DF), and formulation in 20 mM acetate buffer (pH 5.2) containing 75 mg/mL sucrose as a stabilizer, 0.2 mg/mL polysorbate 20 as a surfactant, and 2 mg/mL methionine, to generate the drug substance.

Physicochemical analysis

Molecular weight analysis: For molecular weight analysis, Mobile Phase A consisted of 99.9% distilled water and 0.1% formic acid, while Mobile Phase B consisted of 99.9% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid. The analysis was performed using reverse-phase chromatography with a C4 column (ACQUITY UPLC Protein BEH C4 Column, 300 Å, 1.7 µm, 2.1 mm×100 mm, Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The KNIH-88 was eluted using a 5-55% B gradient and analyzed with Q-TOF mass spectrometry (Synapt XS, Waters, Milford, MA, USA) over an m/z range of 400-4000. The molecular weight data were processed using MassLynx software (ver. 4.2, Waters, Milford, MA, USA).

Size exclusion chromatography: Size exclusion chromatography (SEC) was performed using a TSKgel G3000SWXL column (7.8×300 mm, Tosoh Bioscience, Tokyo, Japan) on a Waters Arc HPLC system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The mobile phase consisted of 50 mM sodium phosphate, 200 mM sodium chloride, and 200 mM L-arginine (pH 7.0). Separation was carried out under isocratic conditions at a flow rate of 1.0 ml/min for 20 min at 25°C, and elution was monitored by UV absorbance at 280 nm.

Strong anion exchange chromatography: Strong anion exchange chromatography (SAX) was carried out using a TSKgel Q-STAT column (4.6×100 mm, Tosoh Bioscience, Tokyo, Japan) on a Waters Arc HPLC system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The mobile phase consisted of Mobile Phase A: 50 mM Tris (pH 9.0) and Mobile Phase B: 50 mM Tris, 250 mM sodium chloride (pH 9.0). The analysis was performed for 35 min, with a gradient elution from 0% to 60% B over 25 min. The absorbance was monitored at 280 nm.

Hydrophobic interaction chromatography: Hydrophobic interaction chromatography (HIC) was conducted using a TSKgel HIC-ADC Butyl column (4.6×100 mm, Tosoh Bioscience, Tokyo, Japan) on a Waters Alliance 2695 system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The mobile phase consisted of Mobile Phase A: 20 mM Tris, 2 M ammonium sulfate (pH 7.0) and Mobile Phase B: 20 mM Tris (pH 7.0). The analysis was performed over 40 min, with an elution gradient from 10% B to 100% B over 30 min. The detection wavelength was set at 280 nm.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

An ELISA was performed to evaluate the binding between KNIH-88 and the MERS-CoV Spike S1 protein (Sino Biological, Beijing, China). Nickel-coated 96-well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were coated overnight at 4°C with 200 ng/well of antigen in PBS. After washing with PBST (0.1% Tween 20 in PBS), the wells were blocked with 5% BSA in PBST for 2 h at room temperature.

Serial 4-fold dilutions of KNIH-88 (2.54 pM to 41.7 nM) were added in duplicate and incubated for 2 h. Human IgG1 isotype control (Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO, USA) was used as a negative control. After washing, wells were incubated with HRP-conjugated rabbit anti-human IgG H&L antibody (1:2,000 dilution, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for 1 h, followed by addition of TMB substrate and stop solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Multiskan GO microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT) assay

African green monkey kidney cells (Vero CCL-81, ATCC) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). MERS-CoV (strain KOR/KNIH/002_05_2015) was propagated in Vero cells, harvested upon high cytopathic effect (CPE), clarified by centrifugation at 1,900×g for 20 min, filtered (0.45 µm), and stored at −80°C. The virus stock was provided by the National Culture Collection for Pathogens (NCCP, Cheongju, Korea).

To evaluate the neutralizing activity of KNIH-88, 2-fold serial dilutions (13 pM to 13.3 nM) were incubated with the MERS-CoV strain for 2 h at 37°C. A human IgG1 isotype control (Novus Biologicals) was included as a negative control. The antibody-virus mixtures were added to confluent Vero cell monolayers in 24-well plates and incubated for 1 h at 37°C for virus adsorption.

After removing the inoculum, cells were overlaid with a 1:1 mixture of Minimum Essential Medium (MEM; Gibco) and 2% low-melting agarose (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland), and incubated for 2 days at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Plaques were counted, and the percentage of plaque reduction was calculated relative to virus-only controls. The 50% inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) was determined using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA).

Animal experiments

Human DPP4 transgenic mice (pCMV-hDPP4-Tg, C57BL/6, female/male, 6-8 weeks, 18-22 g) were obtained from Macrogen (Seoul, Korea) and maintained under ABSL-3 conditions at the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA). All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines and approved by the KDCA Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (KDCA-IACUC-21-003).

To evaluate the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of nonclinical KNIH-88 in liquid formulation, transgenic mice were challenged with MERS-CoV and treated with KNIH-88 at a dose of 0.625 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection, following a previously reported protocol (Lee et al., 2025). Body weight and survival were monitored for 14 days. A human IgG1 isotype antibody (Novus Biologicals) was used as a control.

Binding Kinetics of KNIH-88 to MERS-CoV spike protein

Binding kinetics between the KNIH-88 therapeutic antibody and the MERS-CoV S1 protein were assessed using biolayer interferometry (BLI) on a Protein Analysis System (Octet R4, Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany). HIS1K biosensors (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany) were pre-hydrated in assay buffer prior to immobilization. The MERS-CoV S protein, tagged with a histidine (His) tag, was loaded onto the biosensors at an optimized concentration, ensuring a stable baseline signal before the association phase. Kinetic measurements were performed over a concentration range of 0.49 nM to 31.25 nM, with a defined association and dissociation time at 30°C. The data were acquired in real-time and analyzed using Octet Analysis Studio 13.0 software (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany). Binding parameters, including association rate constant (ka), dissociation rate constant (kd), and equilibrium dissociation constant (KD), were calculated using a global fit to a 1:1 Langmuir binding model.

Lyophilization method

For lyophilized formulation development, 5 mL of antibody formulation was dispensed into 20R glass vials, which were subsequently used for stability evaluation. To determine the appropriate lyophilization cycle, the glass transition temperature (Tg) and collapse temperature (Tc) of the formulation were measured, yielding values of –27.5°C and –16°C, respectively.

Based on these thermal properties, the freezing step was initiated by cooling at a controlled rate of 1°C/min to a final shelf temperature of –45°C. Lyophilization was performed using a Lyostar 4 freeze-drying system (SP Scientific, Warminster, PA, USA). The primary drying phase was conducted at –28°C for 15 h, followed by a secondary drying step at 25°C for 4 h.

RESULTS

Expression of KNIH-88 antibody

KNIH-88 was successfully expressed in a high-producing CHO cell line generated through stable transfection and clonal selection. To evaluate the performance of KNIH-88 clones, a 14-day fed-batch culture was conducted in a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask (initial working volume of 60 mL) with an initial seeding density of 0.3×10⁶ cells/mL. The final titer was measured by CEDEX Bio (Table 1).

Long-term stability was assessed by performing 3-day subpassaging up to Passage 24 at a seeding density of 0.3×10⁶ cells/mL. The titer change in Section 3 (Passages 17-24) relative to Section 1 (Passages 1-8) was –12.6% (Fig. 1, Table 2). Based on the predefined stability criterion (Titer change within ± 30% across 3-day subpassage), KNIH-88 demonstrated acceptable long-term stability. In addition to the VCD presented in Table 1, specific productivity (pcd) across passages was assessed based on the existing dataset. Under 3-day subculture conditions, pcd remained stable across passages 4-24, showing only a minor decrease (~6.6%) from early to late stages, supporting clone stability during long-term cultivation (data not shown).

Production of KNIH-88 antibody

The upstream process was carried out in a 200 L single-use bioreactor with a working volume of 95 kg over a 13-day fed-batch culture. Optimized culture conditions ensured high viability and productivity suitable for toxicology material preparation.

The harvested material was purified through a three-step chromatography process, comprising Protein A affinity chromatography, anion-exchange chromatography (AEX), and mixed-mode chromatography (MMC), combined with depth filtration, virus filtration, and ultrafiltration/diafiltration (UF/DF). The final bulk drug substance achieved a purity of 99.4%, with no detectable aggregates or major product-related impurities. The developed downstream process achieved 91.0% of cumulative yield. The final drug substance was obtained at a concentration of 60 ± 6 mg/mL.

Following purification, a liquid formulation was developed to support the stability of KNIH-88 for nonclinical use. The optimized formulation contained 20 mM acetate buffer (pH 5.2), 60 mg/mL antibody, along with sucrose, polysorbate 20, and methionine as stabilizing excipients. This formulation was further adapted into a lyophilized form. The resulting lyophilized product exhibited a well-formed cake, rapid reconstitution, and retained structural and functional integrity comparable to the liquid formulation.

Structural and physicochemical properties of KNIH-88

Molecular weight analysis under non-reducing conditions revealed mass heterogeneity due to glycosylation and C-terminal lysine (Lys) clipping of the HC (Fig. 2). The predominant glycan species was G0F (1,445 Da), lacking terminal galactose residues, with an overall glycan contribution of approximately 2,890 Da. The major intact antibody species exhibited a mass of 146,323 Da. Under reducing conditions, the disulfide bonds were cleaved, revealing individual HC and LC species. The HC primarily appeared as a Lys-clipped form, with a mass of 49,976 Da, and showed a mass shift corresponding to glycan removal. The LC mass was consistent with theoretical value, measured at 23,193 Da (Supplementary Fig. 1).

SEC chromatography analysis demonstrated high monomer purity, with the main peak accounting for 99.4% of the total area. High molecular weight (HMW) and low molecular weight (LMW) species were detected at 0.3% and 0.3%, respectively. HIC analysis revealed hydrophobic heterogeneity, with the main peak representing 78.8% of the total area, along with 17.3% pre-peaks and 3.9% post-peaks, indicating minor variants with altered surface hydrophobicity. SAX chromatography identified charge variants, including basic and acidic species. The main peak comprised 62.8% of the total, with 19.9% basic peaks and 17.3% acidic peaks. icIEF analysis confirmed the charge heterogeneity based on isoelectric point (pI) distribution. The pI range of KNIH-88 was 8.0-8.9, and the main peak was centered at pI 8.7 (Fig. 3, Table 3).

Collectively, these results confirm that KNIH-88 exhibits structural and physicochemical characteristics consistent with a typical human IgG1 mAb, supporting its suitability for therapeutic development.

Biological activity evaluation of KNIH-88

The binding activity of KNIH-88 to the MERS-CoV S1 protein was evaluated by ELISA. As expected, the human IgG1 isotype control showed no binding, whereas all KNIH-88 antibodies demonstrated strong and consistent binding across all formulation types (liquid and lyophilized), storage temperatures, and after three months of storage (Fig. 4).

In the PRNT assay, KNIH-88 exhibited potent neutralizing activity against the MERS-CoV KNIH/002 strain. No significant differences in neutralization potency were observed between formulations or storage conditions (Fig. 5).

In the in vivo efficacy study using hDPP4-transgenic mice, both original and nonclinical batches of KNIH-88 demonstrated complete protection, with 100% survival at a dose of 0.625 mg/kg and no observable weight loss throughout the 14-day monitoring period (Fig. 6). In contrast, mice treated with the human IgG1 isotype control (10 mg/kg) began to show mortality on day 7, with 0% survival by day 9.

Binding kinetics were further evaluated by bio-layer interferometry (BLI) to assess the interaction between KNIH-88 and the MERS-CoV S1 protein. Real-time binding analysis revealed a rapid association and extremely slow dissociation, resulting in a binding affinity that was below the quantifiable detection limit. These results confirm the strong and specific binding of KNIH-88 to the MERS-CoV S1 antigen (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Stability Assessment of KNIH-88 in Lyophilized Formulation

The stability of KNIH-88 in lyophilized form was assessed at baseline (0 time), after 3 months at 4°C, and after 3 months at room temperature (RT). In all conditions, the lyophilized material retained a white powder appearance with a reconstitution time of 30 s. The pH of the reconstituted solution was consistently maintained at 5.5 across all conditions.

SEC analysis demonstrated that the main peak remained ≥99.1%, while HMW and LMW species were consistently low, with HMW ranging from 0.4% to 0.6% and LMW fixed at 0.3%. Although a slight increase in HMW was observed under RT storage, all results were within acceptable limits and showed no indication of degradation (Supplementary Table 1).

Importantly, the biological activity of the lyophilized formulation was fully preserved, as confirmed by equivalent binding and neutralizing activity in ELISA and PRNT assays across all storage conditions (Fig. 4, 5).

Taken together, these results indicate that the lyophilized formulation maintained comparable quality and biological function to the initial material, supporting its equivalence and suitability for long-term storage and global distribution without reliance on cold-chain infrastructure.

DISCUSSION

Summary of its neutralizing activity and strong binding affinity to MERS-CoV spike protein

In this study, we successfully developed KNIH-88, a therapeutic mAb against MERS-CoV, as a nonclinical drug product in both liquid and lyophilized formulations. The neutralizing activity and binding affinity of each formulation were evaluated using ELISA and PRNT assays. Both formulations exhibited strong binding to the MERS-CoV S1 protein and potent neutralizing activity, demonstrating biological equivalence to the original antibody and confirming their stability and functional integrity.

Notably, the lyophilized formulation retained its biological activity after storage for three months at both room temperature and 4°C, as evidenced by consistent performance in ELISA and PRNT assays. These findings indicate that the lyophilized KNIH-88 formulation provides a stable and effective therapeutic option, offering significant advantages for long-term storage, emergency preparedness, and global distribution of MERS-CoV antibody therapeutics.

Discussion on lyophilization’s impact on antibody stability, transportability, and storage

Lyophilization is widely recognized as a critical strategy for enhancing the long-term stability of therapeutic antibodies by reducing chemical degradation and particle-level aggregation under both ambient and stressed storage conditions. In this study, the lyophilized formulation of KNIH-88 demonstrated equivalent structural integrity and biological activity compared to its liquid formulation, even after three months of storage at room temperature and 4°C. These findings underscore the robustness of the lyophilization process in preserving antibody quality over extended periods.

The development of a stable lyophilized formulation offers significant advantages for the transport and global distribution of antibody therapeutics. Unlike liquid formulations, which require strict cold-chain management, lyophilized products can better withstand temperature fluctuations during shipping and storage. In particular, the demonstrated room-temperature (RT) stability of the product further facilitates its rapid deployment in endemic regions with limited infrastructure. RT-stable formulations can be transported without refrigeration, thereby simplifying logistics and reducing reliance on temperature-controlled supply systems. This is especially advantageous in regions where electricity access is unreliable, as it enables stable storage without the need for specialized equipment. Moreover, the ability to use the product directly at the point of care without prior conditioning accelerates field deployment. Collectively, these attributes contribute to reduced distribution costs and improved accessibility, ultimately supporting broader and more efficient implementation in resource-limited settings.

Furthermore, maintaining the therapeutic potency of KNIH-88 without the need for ultra-low temperature storage expands its practical applicability for emergency stockpiling, pandemic preparedness, and field use. The observed physicochemical and biological stability post-lyophilization strongly supports the feasibility of using the lyophilized formulation for future clinical development and global distribution strategies for MERS-CoV therapeutics.

Potential for therapeutic application in post-exposure treatment or prophylaxis

In addition to the in vitro evaluation, the therapeutic efficacy of KNIH-88 was previously confirmed in pCMV-hDPP4 transgenic mouse models, as reported earlier. Given that both the liquid and lyophilized formulations demonstrated comparable functional performance in ELISA and PRNT assays, it is anticipated that these formulations would exhibit similar preventive and therapeutic effects in vivo. However, further nonclinical studies using small animal models and non-human primates are warranted to comprehensively assess the efficacy and safety of KNIH-88 in more complex biological systems. Overall, these findings strongly suggest that KNIH-88 holds high potential as both a therapeutic and preventive agent against MERS, supporting its advancement toward clinical development.
