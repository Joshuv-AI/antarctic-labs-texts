Low-level viraemia and other factors associated with virologic outcomes among children living with HIV on antiretroviral therapy in Kenya, 2017–2023: a retrospective longitudinal analysis

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundKenya has expanded antiretroviral therapy (ART) coverage and improved viral suppression among children living with HIV (CLHIV). However, low-level viraemia (LLV) and clinical or programmatic factors may increase the risk of virologic failure (VF) and HIV drug resistance, threatening long-term treatment outcomes. We estimated LLV prevalence and its association with virologic outcomes among CLHIV in Kenya, along with other relevant factors.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis using data from Kenya’s national HIV program database (2017–2023) for CLHIV aged 0–14 years on ART for ≥24 weeks with plasma-based viral load (VL) results. VL categories were undetectable VL (UVL; ≤50 copies/mL), LLV (51–999 copies/mL), and viral load non-suppression (VLNS; ≥1000 copies/mL). Introduction

Kenya has a generalized HIV epidemic, with an estimated 1.3 million people living with HIV (PLHIV), including approximately 63,000 children aged 0–14 years in 2024.1 Since adopting the “test-and-treat” policy for all PLHIV in 2016, the country has made significant strides towards universal antiretroviral therapy (ART) coverage.2 Despite expanded HIV diagnosis and linkage to ART, including DNA PCR testing for infants (<18 months) and serologic testing for older children, ART coverage among children aged 0–14 years remains lower (75.0%) compared to adults (85.9%).1 Kenya’s ART guidelines for children living with HIV (CLHIV) have evolved from non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI)- and protease inhibitor (PI)-based first-line regimens to dolutegravir (DTG)-based regimens,3 reflecting global efforts to optimize pediatric ART with drugs offering higher efficacy, easier dispensing, improved palatability, lower pill burden, and a high barrier to HIV drug resistance.4 Nevertheless, viral suppression rates among CLHIV remain lower than in adults5 and CLHIV face additional adherence challenges, including stigma, lack of disclosure, caregiver barriers, and treatment fatigue.6

Routine VL monitoring was introduced in Kenya in 2014, initially scheduled at six and 12 months after ART initiation and annually thereafter. By 2022, national guidelines recommended VL at three and six months post-initiation and every six months for individuals aged 0–24 years.3 Viral suppression is defined as an undetectable viral load (UVL, <50 copies/mL).7 Patients on ART may present with detectable viral load, referred to as low-level viraemia (LLV), which does not meet the threshold for virologic failure (VF). Definitions of LLV and VF vary. In many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), LLV is defined as 51–999 copies/mL, and VF as two consecutive high VL measurements (≥1000 copies/mL) three months apart after enhanced adherence counseling (EAC).7 In high-income countries, LLV is often defined as between the lower limit of detection and 200 copies/mL, and VF as failure to maintain VL < 200 copies/mL.8,9 Kenya’s guidelines classify individuals with VL ≥ 1000 copies/mL as suspected treatment failure, recommending EAC and repeat VL testing after three months; confirmed treatment failure (two VL ≥ 1000 copies/mL) prompts regimen evaluation. VL 200–999 copies/mL is classified as LLV, with case management including EAC, without specific guidance on regimen modification.3

The mechanisms underlying LLV are incompletely understood but are thought to reflect two broad processes: ongoing or intermittent viral replication, which may be influenced by suboptimal drug exposure or adherence, and passive release of virions from long-lived cell reservoirs through proliferation and clonal expansion of cells harboring replication-competent proviruses.10,11 Characterizing viraemia patterns over time is clinically important, as both recurrent viral blips and persistent LLV have been associated with adverse virologic outcomes.12 Repeat VL testing is important, as most LLV cases show recurrent viraemia patterns, although some achieve re-suppression.13

Globally, LLV has been associated with increased VF risk, HIV drug resistance, and poor clinical outcomes.14–19 Studies from LMICs, including Kenya, report LLV, (51–999 copies/mL) in 16–24% of adults on ART.14–17 Data on children are limited: an Asian cohort found 17% of CLHIV <18 years experienced at least one LLV episode (50–1000 copies/mL), while a study in a Kenyan referral hospital reported a 6% prevalence of persistent LLV (at least two consecutive LLV measurements) among children under nine years, compared with 40% reported in a Tanzanian study.19–21 These studies demonstrated that LLV among CLHIV is associated with an increased risk of VF. Given children’s heightened vulnerability to treatment failure, understanding LLV’s impact on virologic outcomes is critical to optimize HIV treatment strategies.

We conducted a retrospective analysis of Kenya’s National AIDS and STIs Control Programme (NASCOP) database (2017–2023) to estimate LLV prevalence and examine its association with virologic outcomes among CLHIV on ART, alongside other clinical and programmatic factors (age, sex, regimen, calendar year of ART start, region, and facility level). Findings will inform strategies to improve HIV care and achieve durable viral suppression in this population.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a retrospective analysis using Kenya’s NASCOP database (2017–2023). Eligible records included CLHIV aged 0–14 years on standard first- or second-line ART for at least 24 weeks, with a documented ART initiation date and at least one plasma VL result. We extracted demographic variables, ART regimens, and VL measurements for CLHIV during the study period. Kenya’s population is predominantly Black African, and the patients are therefore expected to reflect this demographic composition; individual-level race data are not routinely collected in the national VL database or national census. Sex was recorded as reported in routine documentation; gender identity data are not routinely collected. VL categories were defined as follows: UVL (≤50 copies/mL), LLV (51–199, 200–399, 400–999 copies/mL), viral load non-suppression (VLNS; ≥1000 copies/mL), and VF (≥1000 copies/mL on two consecutive measurements). LLV categorization was informed by prior studies in LMICs demonstrating graded risk across these ranges.14,15,21 We excluded records for CLHIV on third-line or non-standard ART, those with missing key data, or VL results from non-plasma-based samples (e.g., dried blood spot [DBS]). For VLNS analysis, we included CLHIV with at least two VL results where the initial VL was <1000 cp/mL. For VF analysis, we included those with at least three VL results, with the first VL < 1000 cp/mL.

Health system context and laboratory procedures

Kenya is administratively divided into 47 counties; however, for this analysis, facilities were grouped into the former eight provincial regions to align with historical programmatic reporting structures. HIV services are delivered through a tiered health system, ranging from community-based care to dispensaries, health centres, and primary, secondary, and tertiary hospitals (levels 1–6), forming the backbone of Kenya’s national HIV programme.

VL testing is performed using standardized commercial platforms. Initially, the Abbott m2000 Real-Time HIV-1 assay and the Roche COBAS AmpliPrep/COBAS TaqMan system were used. The program has since transitioned to the Abbott Alinity, Roche Cobas 6800/8800, and Hologic Panther platforms. Kenya’s national VL program operates a courier-based sample referral network that maintains strict cold-chain conditions to ensure sample integrity during sample transport from health facilities to central laboratories. Plasma-based VL testing allows for the quantification of HIV RNA to below 20–40 copies/mL, depending on the platform. In contrast, DBS testing has higher limits of detection (approximately 550 copies/mL for two-spot and 839 copies/mL for one-spot protocols), making it unsuitable for LLV classification. Kenya has phased out the use of DBS for VL testing because the higher DBS cut-off values are not aligned with current national treatment guidelines. All VL results are uploaded to the NASCOP database, from which de-identified records were extracted for analysis. Turnaround time (TAT) for VL testing has varied over time, with the longest delays occurring during the COVID-19 pandemic (30–35 days), and the shortest TAT being approximately 3 days.

Statistical analysis and outcomes

We described sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of eligible CLHIV, including age, sex, geographic region, facility level, years on ART, regimen type, and calendar year of ART initiation. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). We defined the initial VL as the first plasma measurement obtained after at least 24 weeks on ART and used it to classify baseline viraemia; subsequent viral load results were used only to assess longitudinal outcomes.

We estimated the prevalence of VL categories (UVL, LLV, and VLNS) at the initial VL result and across all VL results and analyzed associations with VLNS and VF using two different logistic regression approaches: non-time-dependent models and time-dependent models. Non-time-dependent models used the initial VL (earliest VL in the record) to predict VLNS or VF at any later timepoint. To fit time-dependent models for VLNS, we restructured the data so that each consecutive pair of VLs a patient contributed resulted in one record. A patient with three VLs would have two records: one with the initial and second VL and another with the second and third VL. The time-dependent model then used the initial VL to predict VLNS at the second (across all pairs of VLs). Time-dependent models for VF used the same approach, with one record for each triplet of observations a patient contributed, and using the initial VL of the triplet to predict VF at the second and third VLs. Records with initial VL < 1000 copies/mL were included and censored at endpoint of interest. Models included a variable indicating LLV (VL 51–999 or a variable with three categories of LLV) and other covariates of interest (age, sex, regimen, calendar year of ART start, region, and facility level). All variables were entered into the regression model simultaneously and retained in the model to adjust for confounding between variables.

We estimated unadjusted and adjusted relative risks (RR and aRR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) using bivariate and multivariable logistic regression models. One set of models included a dichotomous LLV variable, and one set included a four-level VL classification (≤50, 51–199, 200–399, 400–999 copies/mL. To assess the relationship between ART regimen and LLV, VLNS, and VF, we fit time-dependent logistic models with an interaction term for VL category and regimen type (DTG only, protease-inhibitor (PI) only, NNRTI only, transitioned to DTG, transitioned to PI, or other). All variables entered in the models were retained except non-significant interactions. Analyses were performed using SAS v.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 93,670 records from CLHIV aged 0–14 years who initiated ART between 2017 and 2023 were abstracted. Of these, 59,879 (63.9%) met inclusion criteria for descriptive analyses having a confirmed ART initiation date, being on first- or second-line regimens, and having plasma-based VL tests after at least 24 weeks on ART. Among these, 21,120 (35.3%) were included in VLNS analyses (≥two VL results, baseline VL < 1000 copies/mL), and 9434 (15.8%) were included in VF analyses (≥ three VL results, baseline VL < 1000 copies/mL) (Fig. 1). Characteristics of records excluded due to DBS VL sample type or no valid VL after 24 weeks on ART are presented in Table S1.

Study flow. Analysis of low-level viraemia and clinical outcomes among children living with HIV on antiretroviral therapy in Kenya, 2017–2023.

The median age at initial VL was 7.6 years (IQR 4.2–11.3), with similar distribution across age categories (Table 1). More than half (53.9%, n = 32,215) were female. Most received care in a health facility in Nyanza, 41.4% (n = 24,676), Rift Valley, 14.2% (n = 8456), and Nairobi, 13.1% (n = 7819). The median follow-up duration on ART was 2.3 years (IQR 1.2–3.8), with 83.5% (n = 50,026) initiating ART between 2017 and 2020. Overall, 43.4% (n = 25,982) had ever received tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), lamivudine (3 TC) or emtricitabine (FTC); 29.8% (n = 17,835) NNRTI-based regimens, 55.9% (n = 33,491) DTG-based regimens, and 25.1% (n = 15,053) PI-based regimens. The median time from ART initiation to initial VL was 548 days (IQR 267–1048). Among all participants, 44.3% (n = 26,522) had ≥ two VL results, including 5402 with an initial VL > 1000 copies/mL; 92.2% (101,355/109,967) of tests were performed between 2019 and 2023 (Table 1, Fig. 1). Characteristic comparison of records with one, two, or at least three viral loads showed greater representation from earlier ART initiators and specific regions (e.g., Nyanza) (Table S2).

Prevalence of virologic suppression, low-level viraemia, and VLNS at initial VL

Among 59,879 records, 61.0% (n = 36,524) had UVL (≤50 copies/mL), 20.6% (n = 12,336) had LLV (51–999 copies/mL; 11.5% at 51–199 copies/mL (n = 6885), 4.7% at 200–399 copies/mL (n = 2785), 4.5% at 400–999 copies/mL (n = 2666)), and 18.4% (n = 11,019) had VLNS. Most CLHIV with VLNS (75.7%, n = 8343) were on non-DTG regimens (Table 2).

Prevalence of virologic suppression, low-level viraemia, and VLNS across all VL results

Across all VL results, 73.2% (n = 43,843) achieved UVL at least once, 29.7% (n = 17,755) had LLV, and 22.6% (n = 13,556) had VLNS. VF occurred in 4.4% (n = 2614). Most LLV episodes (80.6%, n = 14,315) were single occurrences, with 11.6% (n = 2056) having two or more LLV episodes (Table 2).

Among VLs from children on DTG (n = 47,219), 73.0% (n = 34,477) achieved UVL, compared to 50.5% (n = 11,715) of those on a PI regimen (n = 23,201). LLV prevalence varied by ART regimen; 17.2% (n = 8123) among CLHIV on DTG-based regimens compared to 22.8% (n = 6150) on NNRTI-based and 25.1% (n = 5822) on PI-based regimens (Table S3).

Between 2017 and 2023, VLNS prevalence decreased from 29.9% to 9.8%, LLV declined from 28.8% to 16.4% (with decreases observed across all LLV categories) and UVL increased from 41.3% to 73.8% (Fig. 2a). A similar trend was observed with increased ART duration (Fig. 2b).

Prevalence of undetectable viral load, low-level viraemia, and viral load non-suppression, by (a) calendar year and (b) years on ART.

Using all possible pairs of consecutive tests (n = 50,088; e.g. a record with five viral loads would contribute four pairs) we found that 79.1% (n = 23,452) with UVL (n = 29,661) at the preceding VL maintained suppression at the subsequent test. In contrast, a smaller proportion of those with LLV achieved UVL at the subsequent test (61.9% [n = 3753] for 51–199 copies/mL, 52.9% [n = 1337] for 200–399 copies/mL, and 48.8% [n = 1216) for 400–999 copies/mL). Children with LLV at their most recent VL were more likely to have VLNS at their next VL result compared to children with UVL (9.4% [n = 571] for 51–199 copies/mL, 13.3% [n = 335] for 200–399 copies/mL, and 17.8% [n = 442] for 400–999 copies/mL). Additionally, more than one-third (39.6%, n = 3705) of children with VLNS at their last preceding VL result remained non-suppressed at their next test (Figure S1/Table S4).

VL transitions over three time points illustrate movement across VL categories (≤50 copies/mL, 51–999 copies/mL, and ≥1000 copies/mL) (Sankey diagram, Fig. 3; Table S5). Most children with baseline UVL remained suppressed, while those with LLV fluctuated between categories. A significant proportion of children with baseline VLNS remained non-suppressed, though some improved. Among children with initial UVL (n = 6521), 65.0% (n = 4237) maintained suppression across three time points, while 10.6% (n = 693) and 4.3% (n = 281) experienced transient LLV and VLNS, respectively, before resuppressing. Among those with baseline LLV (n = 2757), 35.1% (n = 969) suppressed and remained suppressed, 13.2% (n = 365) remained in LLV, and 4.6% (n = 127) progressed to VF. Children with baseline VLNS (n = 2824) had mixed outcomes, with 18.9% (n = 533) achieving UVL and 22.9% (n = 646) remaining in VF.

Association between initial VL and subsequent VLNS and VF

In the non-time-dependent model (Table 3), initial VL result was significantly associated with subsequent outcomes. Children with LLV had increased risk of VLNS (aRR 1.69; 95% CI, 1.56–1.82) and VF (aRR 2.21; 95% CI, 1.78–2.75) compared to those with UVL. Risk increased with higher LLV levels: 51–199 copies/mL VLNS aRR 1.37; 95% CI, 1.24–1.51; VF aRR 1.64; 95% CI, 1.23–2.18; 200–399 copies/mL VLNS aRR 1.87; 95% CI, 1.66–2.12; VF aRR 3.08; 95% CI, 2.26–4.21; and 400–999 copies/mL VLNS aRR 2.27; 95% CI, 2.04–2.53; VF aRR 2.73; 95% CI, 1.99–3.73. CLHIV aged 5–9 years had lower risk of VLNS, (aRR 0.86; 95% CI, 0.79–0.94) compared to those aged 10–14 years. No significant differences were observed by age for VF or by sex for either outcome. ART regimen was significantly associated with VLNS and VF. Compared to CLHIV on DTG-based regimens, those on PI-based only or NNRTI-based only regimens, or who transitioned to PI-based regimens, had higher risk of VLNS (aRR 1.54; 95% CI, 1.38–1.71, aRR 1.24; 95% CI, 1.10–1.39; and aRR 1.39; 95% CI, 1.14–1.70) respectively; and VF (aRR 2.66; 95% CI, 1.95–3.62, aRR 2.40; 95% CI, 1.74–3.30, and aRR 3.53; 95% CI, 2.22–5.61), respectively. DTG transition was not associated with either outcome. By region, children in Rift valley (aRR 1.63; 95% CI, 1.39–1.90, Coast (aRR 1.41; 95% CI, 1.18–1.68), and Central (aRR 1.40; 95% CI, 1.16–1.69) had higher risk of VLNS compared to Western. VF risk was highest in Coast (aRR 3.41; 95% CI, 1.93–6.05) and Rift Valley (aRR 2.64; 95% CI, 1.53–4.57). Children initiating ART between 2017 and 2019 had higher VLNS (aRR 1.49; 95% CI, 1.35–1.64) and VF (aRR 2.49; 95% CI, 1.65–3.77) than those initiating ART between 2020 and 2023. Facility level was not associated with either outcome.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that LLV is common among CLHIV on ART in Kenya and strongly predicts subsequent VLNS and VF, with a clear dose–response relationship. CLHIV with LLV 400–999 copies/mL had the highest risk of progression to VLNS and VF, underscoring the clinical importance of higher-range LLV. Younger children (5–9 years) and those on DTG-based regimens had lower risk, whereas CLHIV in Coast and Rift Valley regions had higher risk of VLNS; Coast region was also associated with increased VF risk.

The prevalence of LLV observed in our study is comparable to reports among adults in LMIC14–17 and the Asian Pediatric HIV Cohort19 but lower than estimates from adolescents and young people in Miami (42.4%) and Tanzania (40.5%).21,22 Differences in LLV definitions and age-specific adherence challenges across childhood and adolescence may account for these discrepancies. Consistent with prior studies, we observed a dose-response relationship: LLV 400–999 copies/mL was associated with a 2.6-fold higher risk of VLNS and a 3.4-fold higher risk of VF compared with UVL. Data from Kenyan adults report an even higher, nearly eight-fold, increased risk of VF among individuals with LLV of 400–999 copies/mL.14 Similarly, the Asian cohort study similarly found an approximately three-fold higher risk of failure among children with LLV, particularly among older children and those with lower baseline CD4 counts, though none were on DTG-based ART and LLV categories were not differentiated.19 A similar dose–response pattern was also demonstrated in the Tanzania study, with a two-fold higher risk for VF among individuals with LLV 400–999 copies/mL.21

In our cohort, older children (10–14 years) were at increased risk of VF. Age-related differences in adherence may explain these findings. Younger children often achieve better adherence due to caregiver-administered treatment and limited autonomy, whereas older children face increasing adherence challenges related to non-disclosure, stigma, peer influence, inconsistent caregiving or other family support, and school environments.6,23 As children transition into adolescence and adulthood, vulnerability to poor outcomes following LLV persists. Our prior analysis among Kenyan adults similarly showed higher VF risk among youth aged 15–19 years, underscoring the importance of strengthened transition support, including adolescent-friendly services, peer support clubs, and transition preparation to adult clinics.24

Pediatric virologic outcomes have improved over time with expanded access to DTG-based regimens, fixed-dose combinations, EAC, and differentiated service delivery models. DTG’s superior performance is consistent with its high genetic barrier to resistance, safety, tolerability, minimal drug interactions, and potent viral suppression.25 Programs in Kenya such as family-centered PAPA and MAMA Care (PAMA Care) and Operation Triple Zero (OTZ) clubs support adherence through family engagement, psychosocial support, linkage to community programs, and peer-drive goals of zero missed appointments, zero missed drugs, and zero detectable viral loads.26

In 2018, Kenya implemented EAC and routine VL monitoring for all patients with VL > 400 copies/mL. Findings from the adult LLV analyses further informed expansion of EAC for VL ≥ 200 copies/mL, with case management for higher-range LLV.3 These strategies likely contributed to high re-suppression rates, as illustrated in our Sankey diagram. Evidence from Uganda similarly shows that intensive adherence counselling doubles the likelihood of re-suppression among individuals with LLV.27 While adherence remains central to ART success, LLV may also stem from biological factors beyond patient control. Timely repeat VL monitoring and clinical evaluation are therefore essential to identify ongoing viral replication and guide management decisions, including consideration of regimen modification where appropriate.

Regional and facility-level variation in outcomes suggests contextual influences. Children in Coast region experienced the highest VF risk after LLV, while those receiving care at level 2 or 3 facilities had higher VLNS risk. Published evidence explaining regional differences in virologic outcomes within Kenya is limited; these findings may reflect broader social and structural challenges affecting routine program implementation. As demonstrated in multiple studies from LMIC, social and structural barriers-including caregiver health literacy, long travel distances, household decision-making norms, stigma, and traditional beliefs may contribute to these disparities.28 Further research is needed to better understand the contextual, structural, and health-system factors that may contribute to the regional differences in virologic outcomes within Kenya. Optimizing standardized referral pathways from lower-level facilities—aligned with national guidelines and the primary healthcare framework—and leveraging existing regional mentorship working groups to support management of complex pediatric cases may improve timely LLV management and virologic re-suppression.

HIV drug resistance may contribute to persistent LLV and progression to VLNS or VF. A study in Cameroon found major resistance mutations among adolescents with LLV 200–999 copies/mL, including those on DTG-based ART.29 Limited access to resistance testing, third-line regimens, and age-appropriate pediatric formulations complicates management and underscores the importance of early identification and intervention to prevent treatment failure.

This study has several limitations. We relied on routinely collected national VL data, which contained missing and duplicate records. Variations in how unique identifiers were entered on VL requisition forms may have contributed to multiple records for the same child being treated as distinct individuals, potentially underestimating repeated VL measurements per child, particularly in earlier years; this limitation has been mitigated over time with the introduction of the online laboratory manifest to log all VL requests. Missing VL testing due to limited follow-up, commodity shortages and COVID-19 related service disruptions may have underestimated LLV prevalence; conversely, prioritization of children with suspected treatment failure for VL testing may have led to overestimation of LLV. VL testing commodities’ shortages limited timely routine VL monitoring and repeat VL testing, particularly for children with viraemia who require intense monitoring. Delays in VL monitoring may underestimate the true burden of LLV and risk of subsequent VLNS and VF and limit immediate programmatic response to LLV. Exclusion of DBS VL results likely further underestimated LLV prevalence. Changes in ART regimens over time and incomplete reporting may have resulted in misclassification of regimen exposure. The long and variable interval between ART initiation and initial VL measurement reflects historical gaps in VL monitoring and may have led to misclassification of initial VL result underestimating the true burden of LLV. Only 35% of patients had at least three VL measurements for VF assessment, potentially reflecting better care engagement among those included. Given serial VL analyses (e.g., VF endpoints which required at least three VLs) had larger representation from earlier ART initiators, specific regions (e.g., Nyanza), and children aged 5–9 years, while under-representing later ART initiations and Eastern/North Eastern regions, estimates may be subject to selection and coverage bias. We adjusted for age, calendar period, and region, and included the number of VLs, but residual bias may remain. Finally, the VL database lacked clinical, behavioral, and social variables relevant to adherence and caregiving context, limiting assessment of their effects.

Despite these limitations, the study cohort was drawn from Kenya’s national paediatric ART programme and is representative of children receiving HIV treatment in the country, with relevance to similar settings in other LMICs. This study represents the largest analysis of pediatric LLV to date and demonstrates its substantial prevalence and strong association with adverse virologic outcomes. A comprehensive understanding of LLV, including biological mechanisms, behavioral factors, and programmatic context, is essential to optimize HIV care for CLHIV. Although Kenya’s 2022 guidelines address LLV management, strengthening implementation of targeted, age- and context-specific interventions remains critical to improving long-term outcomes.

Supplementary Material

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMED and MEDLINE for studies published between Jan 1, 2010, and Dec 31, 2024, using the terms “HIV”, “viral load”, “low-level viraemia”, “virologic outcomes”, and “children”. Prior studies, largely among adults, have demonstrated that low-level viraemia (LLV) is associated with an increased risk of viral load non-suppression (VLNS) and virologic failure (VF). In low- and middle-income settings, LLV prevalence among adults ranges from 16% to 26%, with increased VLNS and VF risk. In Kenya, a large national adult cohort study reported that approximately one quarter of patients experienced LLV, with an almost eight-fold increased risk for VF among those with VL 400–999 copies/mL; dolutegravir (DTG)-based regimens were associated with lower risks of VLNS and VF, while younger individuals aged 15–19 years had a higher risk of VF. Evidence among children living with HIV (CLHIV) is more limited but growing. The Asian pediatric HIV cohort study reported a 17% prevalence of LLV and a three-fold increased risk of VF, while a study in Tanzania found LLV in 40% of CLHIV, with a clear dose–response relationship and up to a two-fold higher risk of VF compared with virally suppressed peers.

Added value of this study

This study represents the largest pediatric LLV analysis to date and strengthens evidence that LLV is a major predictor of poor virologic outcomes among CLHIV. We demonstrate a clear dose–response relationship, with LLV levels of 400–999 copies/mL associated with approximately three-fold higher risks of both VLNS and VF. We also show that younger children (5–9 years) and those receiving DTG-based regimens have lower risk, highlighting the likely contribution of caregiver-supported adherence in younger children and the protective effect of optimized antiretroviral therapy (ART) regimens.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings highlight the importance of optimized pediatric ART with child-friendly DTG-based regimens, regular VL monitoring, and targeted support strategies for CLHIV with LLV. Strengthening these interventions is critical to prevent progression to VF, sustain long-term viral suppression, and improve long-term outcomes for CLHIV.
