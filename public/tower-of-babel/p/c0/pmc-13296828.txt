Role of a Synonymous Mutation at Codon 178 in P1 That Attenuates Potato Virus Y‐Induced Tobacco Vein Necrosis and Its Application for Expressing Human Interferon
Authors: De‐Guo Liu, Yue‐Yue Wang, Qing Zhu, Xin‐Wei Huang, Xi‐Ying Yan, Meng‐Ao Jia, Yu‐Shuang Guo, Ling‐Gai Cao, Jun Jiang, Xiang‐Dong Li, Xiao‐Lian Zhang, Yan‐Ping Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPlant virus‐based vectors are gaining significant attention as bioreactors for recombinant protein production due to their broad host range, robust expression levels and cost‐effectiveness; however, their practical utility is frequently constrained by severe viral pathogenicity. Notably, the utility of potato virus Y (PVY), the type member of the genus Potyvirus, is severely hampered by its elicitation of tobacco vein necrosis (TVN). Here, we demonstrate that a single synonymous substitution (AAA to AAG) at the K178 codon of P1 causes almost no TVN. Mechanistically, the P1‐AAG mutation attenuates the synergistic RNA silencing suppression (RSS) activity typically mediated by the P1/HC‐Pro complex. This functional impairment abrogates the virus‐induced upregulation of specific host microRNAs (miR6020, miR6164 and miR6021). Using this non‐necrotic PVY‐P1‐AAG vector, we engineered an optimised bioproduction platform. By integrating a GFP‐HRV3C fusion tag system with affinity purification and subsequent proteolytic cleavage, we achieved the high‐yield synthesis of recombinant human interferons (IFN‐α1b and IFN‐α2b) in Nicotiana benthamiana and Nicotiana tabacum ‘Xanthi’. Codon optimisation of the cargo genes further enhanced protein accumulation, achieving yields of up to 55.2 μg/g fresh leaf weight. Taken together, these results provide new insights into how synonymous substitutions influence potyviral pathogenicity and validate the symptomless P1‐AAG vector as a robust system for biomanufacturing value‐added proteins.Keywords: heterologous expression, human interferon, potato virus Y, RNA silencing suppression (RSS) activity, synonymous mutation, vein necrosis

Introduction

Various protein expression systems using the plant as the host have been developed due to several advantages, including production scale, economy and safety (Kirk and Webb 2005; Ma et al. 2005; Monreal‐Escalante et al. 2022; Cai et al. 2025; Rehman et al. 2026). The first plant‐derived medicine was produced using the transgenic approach (Haq et al. 1995). However, the development of stable transgenic plants remains labour‐intensive, time‐consuming and cost‐prohibitive. In contrast, plant virus‐based protein expression systems have emerged as compelling alternatives. Despite their compact genomes, plant viruses can mediate the rapid and high‐level accumulation of recombinant proteins. Furthermore, the broad host range characteristic of many plant viruses extends the versatility of these transient expression platforms across diverse plant species. Virus infectious clones of many positive‐strand RNA viruses, such as tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) (Chapman 2008), potato virus X (PVX) (Cong et al. 2019), potato virus Y (PVY) (Cheng et al. 2020), and also the negative‐strand RNA viruses, such as tomato spotted wilt virus (TSWV) (Feng et al. 2020; Jackson and Li 2016), have been established. TMV, the pioneering viral vector (Dawson et al. 1989), exemplifies this potential: optimised TMV vectors can yield foreign proteins up to 10% of total soluble protein (Shivprasad et al. 1999) and successfully produce biologically functional therapeutics, such as protective viral vaccines (Wu et al. 2003). However, traditional viral vectors that rely on duplicated subgenomic promoters, such as TMV, are often hindered by genetic instability during systemic infection (Dawson et al. 1989).

PVY is a representative member of the genus Potyvirus and can infect a variety of solanaceous crops, including tobacco, potato and tomato. It has a single‐stranded, positive‐sense RNA genome of about 9700 nt. It encodes a single polyprotein, which is cleaved into 11 mature proteins by three virus‐encoded proteinases, including the first protein (P1), helper component protease (HC‐Pro) and nuclear inclusion a protease (NIa‐Pro). P1 is the first protein translated from the genome. Although P1 is the most divergent protein within the genus Potyvirus, it is indispensable for efficient viral infection and host adaptation (Bello et al. 2023; Gou et al. 2023). While P1 facilitates viral translation (Martínez and Daròs 2014) and safeguards genome replication against host surveillance (Pasin et al. 2020), its most critical role lies in its functional synergy with HC‐Pro. By acting as a powerful accessory factor, P1 drastically enhances the RNA silencing suppression (RSS) activity of HC‐Pro. This P1‐HC‐Pro synergistic interplay is fundamental for the virus to breach host antiviral barriers and establish systemic infection (Hu et al. 2020; Pan et al. 2025).

Interferons (IFNs) play fundamental roles in reducing the viral loads of several human coronaviruses, including severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS‐CoV) and Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS‐CoV) (Haagmans et al. 2004; Falzarano et al. 2013; Zhao et al. 2022), suppressing the proliferation of fungi and bacteria, and exhibiting therapeutic efficacy against tumour cells (McNab et al. 2015; Boehmer and Zanoni 2025). However, serious obstacles have been identified in the microbial system during the expression of recombinant IFNs. Several potyviruses have been engineered to express foreign proteins (Kannan et al. 2020). Cordero and colleagues inserted the coding sequence of the Rosea1 gene into a PVY vector to enhance anthocyanin expression in planta (Cordero et al. 2017). Furthermore, the PVY‐based vector enables the stable co‐expression of dual reporters, mCherry and GFP, in Nicotiana benthamiana with remarkable genetic stability (Li et al. 2025). Collectively, these insights establish a robust technical framework for leveraging PVY‐based systems to produce high‐value therapeutics. However, most PVY infectious clones constructed by different research laboratories could induce vein necrosis, which poses a barrier to its application. The PVY‐induced vein necrosis will indeed affect foreign protein yield (Nosaki et al. 2021). Consequently, characterising key determinants of vein necrosis induction in tobacco without affecting virus accumulation is essential, and this knowledge will expand the translational application of the PVY‐based vector system.

In this study, we identified lysine 178 (K178) of the PVY P1 protein as a putative acetylation site. Intriguingly, we discovered that the third nucleotide of the K178 codon, rather than the encoded amino acid, is the crucial determinant of PVY‐induced vein necrosis in tobacco. A synonymous mutation at this position (P1‐AAG) compromises the RSS activity of HC‐Pro and downregulates the expression of immune‐suppressive miRNAs, thereby preventing the necrotic symptoms. Capitalising on this symptom‐attenuated vector, we engineered an optimised PVY‐based expression vector that successfully achieved high‐level production of clinically valuable human interferons (IFNs).

Results

Acetylation of the Lysine Residue at Position 178 in P1 Is Not Involved in PVY‐Induced Vein Necrosis in Tobacco

Previous studies have shown that several virus‐encoded proteins, including HC‐Pro, NIa‐Pro and coat protein (CP), are involved in PVY‐induced vein necrosis in tobacco (Faurez et al. 2012; Hu et al. 2009; Tian and Valkonen 2013; Tribodet et al. 2005). P1 can enhance the RSS activity of HC‐Pro. Whether P1 is involved in PVY‐induced vein necrosis is unclear. In addition, we previously identified a potential acetylation site at lysine 178 (K178) of the P1 protein (Figure 1a). Sequence analysis of 600 PVY isolates from various strains, retrieved from the NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information) database, revealed that this K178 residue is highly conserved across all analysed sequences (Figure S1). Acetylation of some fungal effectors can facilitate their infectivity (Li et al. 2022; Villette et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025). We first analysed the role of K178 residue of P1 in determining the virulence of PVY.

Using an established site‐directed mutagenesis approach (Liu and Naismith 2008), we substituted the K178 residue with either an arginine (K178R) to prevent acetylation or a glutamine (K178Q) to mimic the acetylated state. The resulting mutants were named pCamPVYN605P1‐K178R (P1‐K178R) and pCamPVYN605P1‐K178Q (P1‐K178Q), respectively. Agrobacterium cells carrying those constructs were agroinfiltrated into the fully expanded leaves of 4‐ to 6‐week‐old N. benthamiana plants and 5‐ to 7‐week‐old Nicotiana tabacum ‘Xanthi’ plants. The wild‐type (WT) PVY was used as a positive control, and the Agrobacterium carrying empty vector pCam35S was used as a negative control. Results showed that, similar to WT PVY, strong green fluorescence was observed in the systemic leaves of N. benthamiana infected with mutants P1‐K178R or P1‐K178Q under ultraviolet light at 7 days post‐inoculation (dpi) (Figure 1b). Western blot showed that both the CP and GFP accumulation levels of P1‐K178R and P1‐K178Q were lower than those of wild‐type (WT) PVY at 12 dpi (Figure 1c). Similar results were obtained in N. tabacum ‘Xanthi’ plants (Figure 1b,d). Both the P1‐K178R and P1‐K178Q mutants induced vein necrosis in Xanthi. Further analysis of ion leakage in necrotic leaves revealed that the degree of cellular damage was positively correlated with the levels of virus accumulation (Figure 1e,f).

To evaluate the effect of those mutations on PVY RNA replication, we analysed the viral RNA accumulation in N. benthamiana mesophyll protoplasts. Protoplasts were transfected with WT PVY and the substitution mutants (P1‐K178R and P1‐K178Q). At 24 h post‐transfection, total RNA was extracted and the accumulation levels of both negative‐sense (−)RNA and positive‐sense (+)RNA viral strands were quantified using reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR). The results demonstrated that the (−)RNA and (+)RNA accumulation levels of the P1‐K178R and P1‐K178Q mutants were comparable to those of the WT PVY (Figure 1g,h). Taken together, these data indicate that the acetylation of the P1 K178 residue does not significantly affect PVY RNA replication.

To assess whether K178 acetylation affects the intercellular movement of PVY, N. benthamiana leaves were agroinfiltrated with highly diluted cultures (3000 times) of WT and mutant clones to generate isolated infection foci. Confocal microscopy analysis at 72 h post‐inoculation (hpi) showed no significant difference in the number of fluorescent cells between the P1‐K178R, P1‐K178Q mutants and WT PVY (Figure 1i,j), indicating that K178 acetylation is dispensable for viral cell‐to‐cell movement.

The Last Nucleotide of the K178 Codon in P1 Is Critical for the PVY‐Induced Vein Necrosis in Tobacco

To test the effect of mutating to other amino acid residues on PVY infection, we mutated K178 to asparagine (N). Growing evidence suggests that synonymous mutations also play an important role in driving adaptive evolution (Zhang and Qian 2025). At codon position 178, we introduced a synonymous mutation by mutating AAA to AAG (K), alongside nonsynonymous mutations changing AAA to AAC (N) and AAT (N), respectively. The resultant plasmids were named as pCamPVYN605‐P1‐AAG (P1‐AAG), pCamPVYN605‐P1‐AAC (P1‐AAC) and pCamPVYN605‐P1‐AAT (P1‐AAT). The Agrobacterium cells carrying one of those plasmids were infiltrated into fully expanded leaves of 5‐ to 7‐week‐old Xanthi plants. Plants inoculated with WT PVY and empty vector were used as controls. The upper systemic leaves of WT PVY, P1‐AAC or P1‐AAT infected plants showed typical vein necrosis at 12 dpi (Figure 2a). In contrast, the plants inoculated with P1‐AAG only showed mild mosaic symptoms (Figure 2a). Western blot analysis revealed that protein accumulation of both CP and GFP was significantly reduced exclusively in the P1‐AAG mutant compared to WT PVY (Figure 2b). In contrast, no discernible differences in viral protein levels were observed for the other mutants (Figure 2b). Trypan blue staining corroborated these phenotypic observations, revealing an absence of vein necrosis in the systemic leaves of Xanthi infected with P1‐AAG, whereas WT PVY and other mutants triggered vein necrosis (Figure 2c). In agreement, ion leakage assays demonstrated significantly reduced electrolyte leakage in P1‐AAG‐infected tissues compared to the WT control (Figure 2d). Intriguingly, while P1‐AAG exhibited reduced accumulation in Xanthi, no significant difference in CP levels was observed between P1‐AAG and wild‐type PVY in N. benthamiana at 7 dpi (Figure S2a,b).

To investigate the effect of the mutations at codon 178 in P1 on PVY RNA replication, we analysed viral RNA accumulation in transfected N. benthamiana protoplasts. Protoplasts were transfected with either the substitution mutants or WT PVY. At 24 h post‐transfection, RT‐qPCR was performed to measure the accumulation of viral (−)RNA and (+)RNA strands. The results demonstrated that the (−)RNA and (+)RNA accumulation levels of the P1‐AAG mutant were significantly lower than those of WT PVY (Figure 2e,f). In contrast, no significant differences were observed in (−)RNA and (+)RNA accumulation between the other mutants and WT PVY (Figure 2e,f).

To test the effect of the mutation at the position codon 178 on PVY cell‐to‐cell movement, we adjusted the Agrobacterium cells carrying pCamPVYN605, pCamPVYN605‐P1‐AAG, pCamPVYN605P‐1‐AAC, pCamPVYN605‐P1‐AAT or empty vector pCam35S to OD600 = 0.5. Furthermore, the Agrobacterium cell suspensions were diluted 3000 times and infiltrated into fully expanded leaves of N. benthamiana plants. At 72 hpi, about 11 ± 2 cells showed green fluorescence in the regions infiltrated with WT PVY, P1‐AAC, or P1‐AAT and 5 ± 1 cells showed green fluorescence in the infiltrated areas with P1‐AAG (Figure 2g,h).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the third nucleotide of the K178 codon in the P1 gene, rather than the amino acid, acts as a crucial determinant of PVY‐induced vein necrosis. Importantly, synonymous mutations at this site eliminate the necrotic phenotype without compromising the efficiency of the PVY‐based vector for heterologous protein expression.

The Synonymous P1‐AAG Mutation Impairs P1/HC‐Pro Synergistic RSS Activity and Down‐Regulates Immune‐Suppressive miRNAs

In potyviruses, the P1 protein synergistically enhances the RSS activity of HC‐Pro (Hu et al. 2020; Pan et al. 2025), promoting the aberrant accumulation of mature miRNAs that negatively regulate plant immunity (Kasschau and Carrington 1998; Wu et al. 2010). Specifically, PVY induces tobacco vein necrosis via HC‐Pro, which upregulates miR6020, miR6164 and miR6021, thereby targeting TIR‐NBS‐LRR‐class resistance signalling (Yin et al. 2019).

Firstly, we tested the effect of this mutation on the RSS ability of HC‐Pro. We engineered recombinant plasmids expressing Myc‐tagged polyproteins of either the wild‐type P1‐HC‐Pro (Myc‐P1‐HC‐Pro) or the K178 synonymous mutant (Myc‐P1‐AAG‐HC‐Pro). These constructs were co‐infiltrated with a GFP‐expressing plasmid into leaves of transgenic 16C N. benthamiana via Agrobacterium‐mediated transient expression. The tomato bushy stunt virus (TBSV) P19 protein and an empty vector (EV) served as positive and negative controls, respectively. Compared to the wild‐type Myc‐P1‐HC‐Pro, the infiltrated area expressing the Myc‐P1‐AAG‐HC‐Pro mutant exhibited drastically diminished green fluorescence, displaying a weak signal comparable to the EV control and substantially lower than the P19 positive control (Figure 3a). Consistent with these visual phenotypes, western blot and RT‐qPCR analyses confirmed that both GFP protein and mRNA accumulation levels were significantly reduced in the presence of the mutant Myc‐P1‐AAG‐HC‐Pro compared to the wild‐type construct (Figure 3b,c).

Then we analysed the effect of synonymous mutation on the accumulation of miR6020, miR6164 and miR6021. We inoculated N. benthamiana plants with WT PVY and the mutant clones (P1‐AAG, P1‐AAC and P1‐AAT). We then detected the accumulation of three specific immune‐suppressive miRNAs (miR6020, miR6164 and miR6021). RT‐qPCR assays revealed that, compared to WT PVY, only the P1‐AAG mutant caused a significant reduction in the abundance of all three miRNAs, whereas the P1‐AAC and P1‐AAT mutations had no noticeable effect (Figure 3d–f).

Application of PVY (P1‐AAG) Viral System for the Expression of Human Interferon Proteins

PVY can infect tobacco, potato and tomato. The polyprotein expression strategy makes it much easier to express foreign proteins. In addition, the encoding sequence of a foreign protein could be inserted into different parts of the PVY genome. Because the mutant P1‐AAG did not cause vein necrosis in tobacco, thereby enhancing active protein production, we tested its use to express foreign proteins in vivo. Different organisms have different codon usage preferences (Hershberg and Petrov 2008). Here, we optimised the coding sequence of human interferon α1b and α2b based on the codon usage preference of tobacco (Table 1). The original and optimised sequences of α1b and α2b (named as α1b‐ori/α2b‐ori and α1b‐opt/α2b‐opt, respectively) were synthesised by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co. Ltd. and confirmed by DNA sequencing (Figure S3a,b). All these coding fragments were ligated into the pMD18‐T vector.

To express the α1b and α2b in pCamPVYN605‐P1‐AAG, we purified the fragments and vector backbone and ligated them into pCamPVYN605‐P1‐AAG using the homologous recombination method. To remove the GFP tag, we inserted a human rhinovirus 3C (HRV3C) tag sequence (5′‐TTAGAAGTTTTGTTTCAAGGTCCT‐3′) between GFP and the human interferon α1b and α2b. We inserted the original and optimised α1b and α2b coding sequences into pCamPVYN605P1‐AAG by homologous recombination using the primers listed in Table 2. The resultant constructs were named pCamPVYN605‐GFP‐α1b‐Ori (GFP‐α1b‐Ori), pCamPVYN605‐GFP‐α2b‐Ori (GFP‐α2b‐Ori), pCamPVYN605‐GFP‐α1b‐Opt (GFP‐α1b‐Opt) and pCamPVYN605‐GFP‐α2b‐Opt (GFP‐α2b‐Opt) (Figure 4a). These plasmids were each transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101. They were infiltrated into the fully expanded leaves of N. benthamiana and N. tabacum ‘Xanthi’. At 12 dpi, green fluorescence was observed in the systemic leaves of N. benthamiana and Xanthi under ultraviolet light. Compared with the original GFP‐α1b‐Ori and GFP‐α2b‐Ori, the expression of optimised GFP‐α1b‐Opt and GFP‐α2b‐Opt showed stronger green fluorescence (Figure 4b,c). Total protein was extracted and analysed by western blot. Results showed that a band of expected size 47 kDa was detected by GFP‐specific antibodies in all inoculated plants. The protein accumulation level of optimised GFP‐α1b‐Opt and GFP‐α2b‐Opt was higher than that of GFP‐α1b‐Ori and GFP‐α2b‐Ori, respectively (Figure 4d,e).

To determine the amount of GFP‐α1b and GFP‐α2b expressed, we infiltrated Agrobacterium cells carrying pCamPVYN605‐P1‐AAG‐GFP‐α1b‐Opt and pCamPVYN605‐P1‐AAG‐GFP‐α2b‐Opt, respectively, into the fully expanded leaves of 4–6‐week‐old N. benthamiana and N. tabacum ‘Xanthi’. To cleave the GFP tag from the fusion proteins GFP‐α1b‐Opt and GFP‐α2b‐Opt, we treated the purified proteins with HRV3C protease. The final amounts of purified α1b and α2b reached 55.2 and 35.6 μg per gram of fresh N. benthamiana leaves, respectively.

Discussion

P1 Is a New Vein Necrosis Determinant of PVY in Tobacco

PVY is one of the most destructive viruses infecting tobacco, potato and tomato plants. PVY‐induced necrotic symptoms in tobacco can cause 100% yield losses. Four amino acid residues within HC‐Pro, namely D196, N330, K391 and E410 (the original numberings of these four residues are D205, N339, K400 and E419 because the cleavage site between P1 and HC‐Pro was thought to be nine residues upstream), are involved in vein necrosis symptoms caused by PVYN in tobacco (Hu et al. 2009; Tribodet et al. 2005). PVYNTN type isolates PVY‐3202 and PVY‐3411 can induce vein necrosis in tobacco. However, the severity of the necrosis is different. PVYZ‐NTN type isolate PVY‐3303 only causes vein clearing. The amino acid residues I252 and Q412 may be associated with the ability of PVY‐3202 and PVY‐3411 isolates to induce vein necrosis in tobacco (Yin et al. 2019). Substitution of six PVYN‐like amino acid residues (N330, R338, F341, I346, K391 and E410) within the HC‐Pro of PVYOUK confer the ability to induce tobacco vein necrosis (Tian and Valkonen 2013). In addition to the viral protein HC‐Pro, two viral genome regions, nucleotides 5496–5932 and 6233–6444 from PVYN‐type isolates, are also involved in the induction of vein necrosis in tobacco (Faurez et al. 2012). Mutating either amino acid residue D6 or G9 within the D6AGG9 motif in CP abolished the ability of PVY to induce vein necrosis (Ji et al. 2023).

In our study, preventing or mimicking acetylation at the potential acetylation site at position 178 in P1 reduced PVY accumulation levels but did not affect PVY's ability to induce vein necrosis (Figure 1). Traditionally, nonsynonymous mutations are considered the primary drivers of alterations in viral pathogenicity, whereas synonymous mutations are presumed to be phenotypically silent. Interestingly, a mutant carrying a synonymous mutation from AAA to AAG at codon 178 in P1 lost the ability to cause vein necrosis without significantly affecting viral accumulation (Figure 2). The synonymous mutation may affect the local and global RNA secondary structure (Burrill et al. 2013) and may also alter the gene's codon usage bias. A synonymous mutation in the RNA‐dependent RNA polymerase abolished poliovirus replication and infectivity, suggesting that RNA structure plays an important role in viral infection (Burrill et al. 2013). Here, we compared the RNA structure of P1 in WT PVY and P1‐AAG and found no visible difference (Figure S4). HC‐Pro, encoded by tobacco etch virus, is one of the first RNA silencing suppressors identified (Anandalakshmi et al. 1998). P1/HC‐Pro could directly bind and sequester miRNA/miRNA* (Shiboleth et al. 2007; Soitamo et al. 2011). The synonymous mutation at codon K178 of P1 may affect the affinity of P1/HC‐Pro for certain miRNAs/miRNA*, thereby altering tobacco symptoms. Indeed, more recent research has shown that different PVY isolates can selectively affect host miRNAs and their targets, including nta‐miR6020a‐5p and nta‐miR6164a/b, which may be related to the induction of vein necrosis (Yin et al. 2019). Here, the synonymous mutation P1‐AAG attenuated tobacco vein necrosis symptoms by reducing the RSS activity of HC‐Pro, thereby downregulating immune‐negative miRNAs (e.g., miR6020 and miR6164) and subsequently diminishing plant immune activity (Figure 3).

The PVY‐Based Vector Is an Important New Tool for Expressing Foreign Proteins

Plant virus‐based expression vectors offer distinct advantages, notably their broad host adaptability and capacity for high‐yield recombinant protein production.

TMV is the first plant virus engineered as a vector to express the bacterial chloramphenicol acetyltransferase gene (Takamatsu et al. 1987). TMV‐based vectors can express the high levels of foreign protein in plants (Yusibov et al. 1999). It has been used to express GFP (Lindbo 2007), antibodies (Giritch et al. 2006) and vaccine candidates (Gleba et al. 2005). The instability of the TMV‐based vector is a major limitation to its application. In addition to TMV, many plant viruses have also been used to express foreign proteins. For example, an infectious clone of maize chlorotic dwarf virus (MCDV), a member of the genus Waikavirus, has been constructed and can express GFP (Stewart et al. 2023). Furthermore, editing the antiviral gene NbGRP in N. benthamiana enhances the expression levels of exogenous GFP delivered by the tomato brown rugose fruit virus (ToBRFV) vector, without detrimental effects on plant growth (Fang et al. 2026).

Due to the genome expression strategy, the potyvirus‐based expression system has attracted more attention recently. For instance, the TuMV‐ZR isolate has been engineered into a viral expression vector capable of continuous eGFP expression (Yang et al. 2023). Beyond reporter genes, zucchini yellow mosaic virus (ZYMV) has been used to express human interferon‐gamma (IFN‐γ) in planta, achieving maximum yields of approximately 12 μg/g fresh weight (FW) (Hosseini et al. 2013). Furthermore, the tobacco etch virus (TEV) vector has been employed to express viral nanoparticles (VNPs) in plants as a vaccine platform for the prevention and treatment of human viruses (Martí et al. 2022; Merwaiss et al. 2024). To optimise expression levels, researchers have explored diverse genomic integration strategies. In addition to the widely selected insertion site between NIb and CP, Rajamäki and colleagues reported a novel insertion site inside the potato virus A P1 protein (Rajamäki et al. 2005). A PVY infectious clone carrying the coding sequence of Antirrhinum majus Rosea1 transcription factor can activate the biosynthesis of anthocyanin pigments in a dose‐specific and cell‐autonomous manner (Cordero et al. 2017). Despite these advancements, a significant limitation persists: most currently available PVY infectious clones can cause vein necrosis in tobacco. Severe necrotic symptoms typically compromise host viability, thereby severely limiting the accumulation of recombinant proteins (Nosaki et al. 2021). In this study, a synonymous mutation in P1 abolished PVY's ability to induce vein necrosis. Although this mutation has a lower virus accumulation compared to that of wild‐type PVY, we successfully expressed IFN‐α1b and IFN‐α2b from P1‐AAG in N. benthamiana and N. tabacum ‘Xanthi’ plants and obtained higher yield compared with previous studies (Figures 4 and 5). Over the last two decades, many studies have shown that natural sequence biases are useful for boosting protein expression (Chaney and Clark 2015). Indeed, optimisation of the codons of human α1b and α2b according to the codon usage in tobacco could significantly improve their expression level in tobacco plants.

Based on the above results, we propose a working model that during wild‐type potato virus Y (PVY) infection in tobacco, the P1 protein acts as an accessory factor, synergistically enhancing the RSS activity of HC‐Pro. This synergy leads to aberrant overaccumulation of immune‐suppressive miRNAs (specifically miR6020, miR6164 and miR6021), thereby suppressing host defences and causing severe vein necrosis. In contrast, the synonymous mutant P1‐AAG operates via a distinct mechanism that attenuates these necrotic symptoms, thereby ensuring robust plant growth. Using this P1‐AAG‐based expression vector, we expressed human interferon (IFN) fused to a GFP‐HRV3C tag. High‐purity human IFN proteins were successfully recovered following affinity purification and subsequent tag removal via HRV3C protease cleavage. Quantitative analysis revealed that this system achieved yields of 55.2 and 35.6 μg per gram of fresh leaf tissue for IFN‐α1b and IFN‐α2b, respectively (Figure 7). In conclusion, this optimised PVY‐based vector emerges as a powerful biotechnological tool for the scalable manufacturing of high‐value proteins, including human interferons. Ultimately, this platform holds great promise for reducing pharmaceutical production costs, paving the way for more accessible and cost‐effective biologic therapies.

Experimental Procedures

Materials

Nicotiana benthamiana, GFP‐expressing transgenic N. benthamiana (line 16C) and N. tabacum 'Xanthi' plants were grown in an artificial climate chamber at 25°C with a 16 h/8 h (light/dark) cycle.

The infectious clone pCam35S‐PVYN605, driven by the cauliflower mosaic virus 35S promoter and modified by the introduction of introns (Bukovinszki et al. 2007), was provided by Professor Edgar Maiss, University of Hannover, Germany. It was further modified by inserting a GFP encoding sequence between NIb and CP in our laboratory. Guizhou Tobacco Science Research Institute provided plasmids carrying the interferons α1b and α2b sequences. Escherichia coli DH5α and A. tumefaciens GV3101 were maintained in our laboratory.

Plasmid Construction

According to the codon preference in plants, the corresponding codons in the gene sequence of human α1b (Accession Number: J02541.1) and α2b (Accession Number: AY255838.1) were optimised, and the optimised sequences were synthesised by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co. Ltd. The fragments of human α1b and α2b were inserted into PVY infectious clones using homologous recombination. The protease recognition sites were added to the α1b‐GFP and α2b‐GFP fusion proteins. To obtain tag‐less α1b and α2b proteins, we inserted a 24‐nt human rhinovirus 3C (HRV 3C) tag (Leu‐Glu‐Val‐Leu‐Phe‐Gln/Gly‐Pro) between the interferons and GFP‐encoding sequences.

To investigate whether the synonymous mutation in P1AAG affects the RSS activity of HC‐Pro, the P1WT‐HC‐Pro and P1AAG‐HC‐Pro polyproteins were cloned into the pCam35S‐Myc vector using a homologous recombination‐based approach. The resulting constructs were designated as Myc‐P1WT‐HC‐Pro and Myc‐P1AAG‐HC‐Pro, respectively. Site‐directed mutagenesis was performed using the Liu and Naismith (2008) method.

All the resultant plasmids were sequenced by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co. Ltd. The sequencing results were analysed using the DNAMAN software. All the primers used in this study are listed in Table 2.

RNA Extraction and RT‐qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TransZoL by following the manufacturer's instructions (TransGen Biotech). The concentration of total RNA was measured using NanoDrop One. For RT‐qPCR, the total RNA concentration was adjusted to 1000 ng/μL. The genomic DNA was removed using a gDNA wiper (Vazyme). The total RNA was reverse transcribed using HiScript II Q Reverse Transcriptase Select Super Mix (Vazyme) with primers qPVY‐CP‐F (for the detection of negative‐strand RNA) and qPVY‐CP‐R (for the detection of positive‐strand RNA), and the obtained cDNA was used for qPCR in a thermal cycler (LightCycler 96, Roche) with ChamQ SYBR qPCR master mix (Vazyme).

The accumulation of miRNAs (miR6020, miR6164 and miR6021) was quantified via stem‐loop RT‐qPCR, following the methodology and primer design principles described by Yin et al. (2019).

Relative expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001), with NbEF1α serving as the reference gene. All primers used are listed in Table 2.

Virus Inoculation

Viral inoculation was performed according to the protocol described by Fang et al. (2025). Briefly, A. tumefaciens cultures harbouring the WT PVY infectious clone or its mutants were harvested by centrifugation at 6000 g for 10 min. The resulting pellets were resuspended in an infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 150 μM acetosyringone, 10 mM 2‐(N‐morpholino)ethane sulfonic acid, pH 5.6) to a final OD600 of 0.5. These bacterial suspensions were then agroinfiltrated into the lower leaves of 4‐week‐old N. benthamiana or N. tabacum ‘Xanthi’ plants, covering approximately two‐thirds of the total leaf area. Following inoculation, the plants were grown in a controlled growth chamber under standard conditions.

RNA Silencing Suppression Assay

The RSS assay was performed as previously described (Xu et al. 2022). Briefly, A. tumefaciens cultures harbouring a 35S‐GFP construct were co‐infiltrated into the leaves of 16C transgenic N. benthamiana along with strains expressing either Myc‐P1‐WT‐HC‐Pro or the mutant Myc‐P1‐AAG‐HC‐Pro. Agrobacterium strains expressing the TBSV P19 protein or carrying an empty vector (EV) were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. At 60 hpi, GFP fluorescence in the infiltrated patches was visually evaluated and photographed under a long‐wave UV lamp.

Protein Extraction, Protein Purification and Western Blot

Leaves of wild‐type N. benthamiana, transgenic N. benthamiana line 16C and N. tabacum ‘Xanthi’ were collected, ground in liquid nitrogen and then transferred into a precooled, autoclaved 2 mL Eppendorf tube. Protein extraction buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1% Nonidet P‐40, 1 mM dithiothreitol [DTT], and 1 × protease inhibitor cocktail) was added at a ratio of 1:2. The insoluble debris was removed by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 10 min. The supernatant was transferred to a new autoclaved 2 mL Eppendorf tube. For protein purification, the supernatant was filtered with a 0.22 μm membrane. The target protein was purified with anti‐GFP agarose beads according to the manufacturer's instructions (Shenzhen KT Life Technology Co. Ltd.). The concentration of the purified protein was measured using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

For western blot, an equal volume of supernatant and 2 × SDS sampling buffer (4% SDS, 10% β‐mercaptoethanol, 40% glycerol, 0.1 M Tris–HCl pH 6.8, 0.01% bromophenol blue) was mixed and boiled for 10 min and subsequently transferred on ice immediately. The denatured mixture was separated on an SDS‐PAGE gel and transferred to the nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was detected with CP (1:1000, prepared in our laboratory) and GFP (1:1000, prepared in our laboratory) antibodies, respectively. The western blot assay performed in the study is the same as previously described (Xu et al. 2022). To account for variations in protein loading, the nitrocellulose membranes were stained with Ponceau S. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (v. 1.53 k), and the resulting data were normalised against the total protein density of each lane.

Trypan Blue Staining

Leaves were boiled in a mixture of ethanol and stock solution of trypan blue dye solution (10 mL lactic acid, 10 mL glycerol, 10 mL phenol, 10 mL water, 20 mg trypan blue) for 6 min. The leaves were washed with chloral hydrate solution (2.5 g/mL). The treated N. benthamiana plant leaves were photographed using a digital camera on a white light box (Canon 80D).

Ion Leakage Assays

Ion leakage assays in N. tabacum ‘Xanthi’ were performed as previously described (Lapin et al. 2019). Briefly, six 8‐mm diameter leaf discs were harvested from the upper systemic leaves at 12 dpi. The discs were rinsed in 10–20 mL of Milli‐Q water (18.2 MΩ·cm) for 30 min and then transferred to 24‐well plates containing 1 mL of Milli‐Q water per well. Conductivity was measured at 0 and 24 h post‐incubation using a Horiba Twin Model B‐173 conductivity meter.

Isolation and Transfection of N. benthamiana Protoplasts

Nicotiana benthamiana mesophyll protoplasts were isolated as described previously (Chen et al. 2018). Equal densities of the prepared protoplasts were transfected with 2 μg of either WT or mutant PVY genomic RNA. At 24 h post‐transfection, total RNA was extracted and viral RNA accumulation was quantified using RT‐qPCR.

Confocal Microscopy

The N. benthamiana leaves were collected at 72 hpi and observed under a confocal microscope (LSM800 Airyscan, Zeiss). The excitation and emission wavelength for GFP fluorescence were set to 488 nm and 520–540 nm, respectively. Images were further processed with ZEN software (v.3.5).

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed using three independent biological replicates. For qPCR analyses, each biological replicate included three technical replicates. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (v. 8.0.1). Differences among treatments were evaluated using a one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test (p < 0.05). In all figures, different letters denote statistically significant differences.
