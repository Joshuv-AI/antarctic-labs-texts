An unusual bezoar: Elastic hair ties triggering small bowel obstruction
Authors: Angelo Changas, Mena Louis, Nathaniel Grabill, Nathan Creel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBezoars, formed by the accumulation of indigestible material, can lead to significant gastrointestinal complications including obstruction and intussusceptions. A 17-year-old female presented with several days of lower abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting. Imaging demonstrated multiple small bowel intussusceptions, and a stomach distended with radio dense material. Surgical exploration revealed a massive collection of hair ties and hair extending from the stomach into the small bowel and colon. Resection, reduction, and removal of the foreign bodies were performed, and patient was later diagnosis with PICA. Timely imaging studies and surgical evaluation are fundamental to diagnose and manage rare forms of bezoars. Hair tie bezoars represent a unique, though infrequent, cause of bowel obstruction that necessitates an integrated approach encompassing surgical, psychiatric, and nutritional interventions.Keywords: Hair tie bezoar; Pica; Bowel obstruction; Intussusception, Foreign body ingestion

Introduction

Foreign body ingestion remains an important diagnostic consideration in both pediatric and adult populations, with clinical presentations ranging from asymptomatic to life-threatening obstruction [1]. The ingestion of non-food items can occur for various reasons, including accidental swallowing, self-harm, or underlying psychiatric conditions [2]. Ingestion of inorganic materials such as hair ties, rubber bands, or metal objects is comparatively less common, yet may lead to significant morbidity if not promptly recognized and managed [3].

PICA, classified as an eating disorder in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), is characterized by persistent consumption of non-nutritive substances [4]. Individuals diagnosed with PICA may ingest an array of materials such as dirt, paint chips, paper, or fabric [5]. Although PICA is more frequently identified in children, pregnant women, and those with certain psychiatric conditions, it can be observed in adolescents and adults under specific psychosocial or physiological stressors [6]. Complications of PICA can include toxic exposure, intestinal perforations, or obstruction, necessitating a high index of suspicion among healthcare professionals [7].

Within the realm of gastrointestinal disturbances, bezoars occupy a unique position. These conglomerations of indigestible substances can form in the stomach and progress along the gastrointestinal tract [8]. Traditional descriptions of bezoars often involve hair (trichobezoars), plant fibers (phytobezoars), or certain medications (pharmacobezoars) [9]. While the underlying mechanisms relate to the aggregating properties of these substances, the clinical implications depend on size, location, and the individual’s overall health status [10]. Early recognition through imaging or endoscopic evaluation followed by multidisciplinary intervention is crucial for preventing serious complications.

Case presentation

A 17-year-old female with no significant medical or surgical history arrived at the Emergency Department reporting 5 days of progressively worsening lower abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting. She had been evaluated at an outside facility, where a computed tomography (CT) scan showed multiple small bowel intussusceptions, a markedly distended stomach containing serpiginous radiodense material, and mesenteric lymphadenopathy (See Fig. 1, Fig. 2). The same radiodense material appeared within the small bowel and colon. The patient was then transferred for urgent surgical evaluation.

CT abdomen and pelvis (axial view) demonstrating small bowel intussusception (red arrow), small bowel containing serpiginous radio dense material (yellow arrow), and mesenteric lymphadenopathy.

On arrival, her physical examination revealed mild tachycardia but otherwise stable vital signs. Abdominal assessment showed diffuse tenderness, more pronounced in the lower quadrants, without peritonitis. Laboratory findings demonstrated a mild leukocytosis and normal electrolytes aside from borderline low potassium. Nutritional markers revealed iron deficiency anemia and low vitamin D. Serum B12, folate, and thyroid function tests were within normal limits. Psychiatric history was unclear at the time but there were indications of recent psychosocial stressors.

An urgent exploratory laparotomy was performed. The stomach was distended and firm; a gastrotomy uncovered numerous elastic hair ties mixed with hair (See Fig. 3). After removal, the gastrotomy was closed with layered suturing. More than 10 small bowel intussusceptions were reduced. A longitudinal enterotomy in the distal ileum allowed extraction of additional hair ties, and the enterotomy was closed in a transverse fashion. Identical foreign bodies were also noted in the colon but were left in place to pass spontaneously. The abdomen was irrigated and closed, and a negative pressure dressing was applied over the incision. Postoperatively, the patient was supported with a nasogastric tube on intermittent suction. On postoperative day 3, an upper gastrointestinal series demonstrated no leakage, allowing for dietary advancement. A psychiatric consultation established the diagnosis of Pica, likely driven by stress and underlying iron deficiency. With appropriate supplementation and psychiatric follow-up, her recovery was uneventful, and she was discharged on postoperative day 6.

Discussion

The diagnosis of a bezoar requires careful clinical and imaging assessment. In this scenario, the patient had a prolonged history of abdominal discomfort with imaging findings that were suggestive of a significant intraluminal mass. The eventual discovery of hair ties in the gastrointestinal tract established the etiology. The background psychiatric condition of Pica, in which patients ingest non-food items, helps explain the persistent collection of foreign material [6,11]. Recognition of Pica involves understanding a variety of psychological factors, nutritional deficiencies, and stressors that may prompt patients to consume such objects [5]. In many cases, it is critical to evaluate for additional contributors such as metabolic derangements, cultural practices, or underlying psychiatric disorders that could potentiate this behavior [12].

The differential diagnosis for a patient presenting with small bowel intussusceptions and signs of obstruction includes idiopathic causes, polypoid lesions, adhesions, malignancy, and Meckel’s diverticulum [13]. When imaging indicates an unusual intraluminal density, foreign body ingestion must be considered. The accumulation of hair ties is uncommon but can mimic the appearance of other intraluminal masses. The presence of multiple intussusceptions draws attention to a possible lead point in each segment of the bowel, and in this patient, the foreign bodies served as those lead points [14]. Persistent diagnostic vigilance is essential because failing to identify the underlying cause can lead to incomplete resolution and recurrence.

Management involves a balanced approach that addresses both the acute surgical need and the underlying psychiatric condition [15]. The initial step is stabilizing the patient with appropriate fluid resuscitation, correction of electrolyte imbalances, and alleviation of any immediate obstruction [16]. Surgical exploration is warranted when imaging or clinical deterioration suggest a significant risk to bowel viability or complications such as perforation [17]. Intraoperative findings in this patient indicated that removing the foreign bodies through a gastrotomy and enterotomy was essential to prevent ongoing obstruction and to resolve the multiple intussusceptions. Closing each incision with robust suturing techniques helps guard against leaks, while thorough irrigation reduces the risk of contamination.

Follow-up care must emphasize gastrointestinal recovery and psychological evaluation. The nasogastric tube decompression supports bowel rest and allows time for the healing of enterotomies and gastrotomies. An upper gastrointestinal series can reassure that no leaks are present before oral intake is resumed. Long-term follow-up with psychiatry is vital for addressing behavioral triggers and stressors that led to Pica, and nutritional support is equally important to correct deficiencies such as iron or vitamin D [18]. In many cases, relapse prevention hinges on sustained mental health support, ongoing evaluation for recurrent cravings, and a well-defined management strategy to prevent future episodes of foreign body ingestion.

The teaching points in this case extend beyond the immediate surgical context, as they provide insights into the intricate interplay between a psychiatric diagnosis, nutritional deficiencies, and acute surgical emergencies. While the physical removal of the offending material is the critical short-term goal, lasting success and prevention of recurrence depend on a comprehensive plan that includes mental health intervention, dietary guidance, and continued surveillance. Recognizing Pica as an underlying factor can radically change the diagnostic approach and ensures that the patient’s needs are addressed holistically.

Conclusion

Hair tie bezoars represent a distinctive subset of gastrointestinal foreign body accumulations that require prompt recognition and surgical management. They highlight the complex interplay between psychosocial stressors, nutritional imbalances, and mechanical complications. Effective treatment calls for a thorough removal of the obstruction and simultaneous psychiatric evaluation to address the behavior leading to ingestion. Comprehensive awareness and early intervention remain vital for preventing serious complications and recurrence.
