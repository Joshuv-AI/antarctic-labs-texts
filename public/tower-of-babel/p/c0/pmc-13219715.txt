Environmental and Neighborhood Determinants of Psoriasis: A Systematic Review of Pollution, Built Environment, and Socioeconomic Vulnerability on Psoriasis
Authors: Parsa Mokhtar, Josep Riera Monroig, Allison Kranyak, Wilson Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Psoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated skin disease with well-studied genetic and immunologic drivers. Substantial unexplained variability remains, however, in disease onset, severity, and treatment response. There is evidence that implicates the role of environmental and social determinants in these observed differences in psoriasis. We conducted a systematic review to synthesize the current evidence examining the relationship between external exposures, including air and chemical pollution, the built environment, and neighborhood characteristics, and their impact on psoriasis incidence, severity, and exacerbation. Our search in PubMed and Embase (on August 8, 2025 and October 15, 2025, respectively) identified 26 eligible studies. These included nine cohort studies, four ecological studies, and 12 case–control studies. Papers that assessed exposures of ambient air pollutants, chemical pollutants, neighborhood, built environment, income, education, and insurance status were included. The risk of bias for each study was evaluated using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) criteria. Across diverse patient populations and study designs, higher exposure to air pollutants, such as particulate matter and nitrogen oxides, and chemical pollutants, including per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), was associated with increased disease incidence and severity. These were found to contribute to an independently increased risk of psoriasis, even among individuals with a genetic susceptibility to the disease. Climate change associated events, such as wildfires, were linked to increased healthcare utilization for psoriasis. Deleterious neighborhood-level exposures and poor social determinants were also associated with greater psoriasis incidence and exacerbation. Our analysis of existing limitations highlights the need for longitudinal and mechanistic studies to elucidate these relationships, and for the development of standardized definitions for environmental exposures. These findings support a framework for psoriasis that considers environmental and social determinants alongside genetic contributors. Addressing these risk factors requires broader public health initiatives that mitigate exposure and expand healthcare access to at-risk communities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-026-01725-x.

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated skin disease that affects approximately 2–3% of the population in Western countries [1]. The understanding of its pathogenesis has increased dramatically in the past decades, with tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα), interleukin (IL)-17, and IL-23 being key in its development and maintenance. This has led to highly effective targeted therapies. Despite these advances, a considerable number of patients still fail to achieve or sustain a complete response. There is a marked variability in the onset, severity, and unknown triggers of psoriasis, which remains unexplained by genetics and immune pathogenesis alone.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), pollution is considered one of the most significant environmental health risks to humans. There is growing interest in how pollutants may influence immune-mediated skin diseases. Acne, atopic dermatitis (AD), and psoriasis have been shown to be influenced by pollution. Specifically, fine particulate matter and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) have been shown to penetrate the skin barrier and trigger dysregulated immune responses [2]. The specific mediators in these pathways are not entirely understood. Furthermore, data have shown that pollutants such as particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10), NO2, sulfur dioxide (SO2), ozone (O3), and volatile organic compounds can damage the skin barrier, promote oxidative damage, and dysregulate the immune system in atopic dermatitis. Increased environmental exposure to these pollutants is also associated with an increased number of outpatient visits for AD and a higher incidence of eczematous dermatitis [3]. In psoriasis, however, the evidence is more limited and heterogeneous, and the biological pathways through which pollution could modulate disease risk or severity are poorly understood.

Beyond physical environmental exposures, the understanding of how one’s environment and neighborhood factors impact psoriasis incidence and severity is not yet deeply understood. These include factors such as income and education level, access to green and blue space, health insurance status, and other markers of social and environmental vulnerability. This gap in understanding also applies to the interaction between these environmental and social factors and genetic predisposition to psoriatic disease. Existing data suggest that the worsening of psoriasis is associated with specific seasons and weather conditions; however, the biological basis of this relationship remains unclear [4]. Research in this area has shown that neighborhood living environment, socioeconomic status, and psychosocial factors are associated with the development of psoriasis; however, more research is needed to fully understand the basis of this interaction and its mediating factors [5–8].

To address these knowledge gaps, we conducted a systematic review to investigate the relationship between external factors, including pollution and social determinants, and psoriatic disease. In this review, we focus on recent findings, potential mechanisms, and the importance of environmental regulation to mitigate the deleterious impact of environmental and social triggers of psoriatic disease.

Methods

Study Design and Search Strategy

Database searches were conducted in PubMed on August 8, 2025, and Embase in October 15, 2025. MeSH search terms combined “psoriasis” with exposure-related keywords: “pollution”, “income”, “neighborhood”, “climate change”, “global warming”, “living environment”, “soil.” Studies were eligible if they assessed factors related to environmental or social vulnerability and their impact on the incidence, severity, or exacerbation of psoriasis. No exclusion criteria were established based on age, nationality, ethnicity, or geography. Review articles and conference abstracts, as well as qualitative and non-full-text articles in English or Spanish, were excluded.

For the screening process, titles and abstracts were independently examined by two reviewers (JRM and PM) to identify potentially relevant articles. Full-text articles were assessed to confirm eligibility. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (WL). The reference lists of included articles were also screened to identify additional eligible studies, which were assessed using the same criteria. Cross-sectional and cohort studies were independently assessed by reviewers for the risk of bias assessment using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) criteria. The specific guidelines and methods for the CASP criteria are described elsewhere [8]. The CASP does not offer guidelines for the risk of bias assessment for ecological studies, so those studies were assessed according to the appraisal for cohort studies.

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Results

The PubMed search yielded 371 results, and the Embase search yielded 913 results. After filtering, 26 studies were included in the final review (Fig. 1). Of these, nine were cohort studies, 11 were cross-sectional studies, one was a dual case-crossover/cross-sectional study, and four were ecological studies. Summarized characteristics and discrete statistical results for each are presented in Table 1. The risk of bias assessment for cohort studies is presented in Table S1, and that for cross-sectional studies is presented in Table S2.

Air Pollution and Psoriasis

Nine of the 26 studies focused on the association between different types of air pollutants as the primary exposure variable for increased psoriasis incidence, disease severity, or as a surrogate, outpatient visits for psoriasis. Most assessed long-term exposure to particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10), nitrogen oxides, ozone, or composite air pollution scores derived from several pollutants. Cohort and ecological studies generally reported that higher exposure to traffic-related and urban pollutants was associated with an increased risk of incident psoriasis or higher rates of psoriasis-related outpatient visits, with modest elevations in relative risk in the highest exposure categories compared with the lowest. One prospective cohort study of 451,064 patients from the UK Biobank categorized participants into quintiles based on their air pollution score (APS), which was calculated to assess the joint exposure to multiple air pollutants, including particulate matter and nitrous oxide. Patients were followed for 13.79 years and researchers found hazard ratios for incident psoriasis were 1.036 [95% confidence interval (CI) 0.936–1.147], 1.091 (95% CI 0.987–1.206), 1.159 (95% CI 1.048–1.283), and 1.163 (95% CI 1.052–1.286) in the higher APS quintile groups (Q2, Q3, Q4 and Q5, respectively) versus the lowest APS quintile (Q1; p value for trend < 0.05) [16]. Another cohort study of similar size using the UK Biobank supported these findings, showing increased risk of psoriasis incidence, particularly with greater exposure to PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and nitrous oxides (NOx) [17]. Both papers demonstrated that a combination of genetic predisposition to psoriasis and increased exposure to pollutants significantly increases the risk of developing psoriasis [28, 29].

Several studies investigating the impact of air pollution on psoriasis have relied on ecological models. One study, which assessed 54,064 outpatient medical visits over 4 years, found that higher concentrations of SO2, NO2, and ozone increased the relative risk (RR) of outpatient visits for psoriasis (RR 1.023, 95% CI 1.004–1.043; RR 1.170, 95% CI 1.046–1.307; and RR 1.059, 95% CI 1.030–1.090, respectively). The same project found a concentration-dependent effect of exposure to NO2 and ozone on the percent increase of outpatient psoriasis visits. Namely, an increase in 10 µg/m3 of NO2 or ozone resulted in a 2.1% (95% CI 0.7–3.5%) and 0.8% (95% CI 0.4–1.2%) increase in psoriasis visits, respectively [13]. A separate analysis of 13,536 clinic visits and one evaluating outpatient visits in a population of roughly 23 million people also found an increase in the percentage of outpatient psoriasis visits with increased exposure to NO2, as well as PM2.5 (Table 1) [31, 32]. Lastly, one study based in San Francisco investigated the impact of wildfire exposure on outpatient psoriasis visits. They found that following an 8-week lag from a fire 175 miles away, the adjusted rate ratio for psoriasis visits at the University of California, San Francisco was 1.45 (95% CI 1.13–1.86) [27].

Lastly, one study assessed the association between chemical pollutants, specifically per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), and the risk of developing psoriasis. The authors studied five different PFAS and found significant associations between perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS) and psoriasis. More specifically, each doubling of PFOS concentration exposure was associated with a 19% increased odds of having psoriasis (OR 1.19, 95% CI 1.01, 1.41). Their weighted quantile sum regression revealed that exposure to PFAS mixtures was associated with an 11% increased odds of having psoriasis in female subjects (OR 1.11, 95% CI 1.01, 1.22) [33].

Built Environment and Neighborhood Characteristics

Seventeen studies focused on social vulnerability and the lived environment. Two of these relied on composite measures, namely neighborhood deprivation and polysocial risk score (PsRS), to quantify social/neighborhood risk factors. Li et al. followed a large Swedish prospective cohort of 5.4 million individuals over 10 years. After adjusting for ten individual-level sociodemographic variables, they found increased odds of psoriasis among those living in high-deprivation neighborhoods (aOR 1.15, 95% CI 1.00–1.32) [9]. Tian et al. prospectively studied a cohort of 331,631 participants in the UK Biobank. They defined polysocial risk as a composite of 17 social determinants of health, categorized into three domains: socioeconomic status, psychosocial factors, and neighborhood and living environment. Groups with high PsRS had a significantly higher risk of incident psoriasis compared to those in the lowest risk group (aHR 1.53, 95% CI 1.31–1.78) [5].

The association between different neighborhood characteristics and psoriasis was also of interest among the papers reviewed. One study followed a prospective cohort of 385,145 patients in the UK Biobank with a mean follow-up time of 12.45 years. The authors found a greater risk of psoriasis incidence when comparing those in the highest quartile (Q4) of exposure to natural environments (within a 1000-m buffer zone) to those in the lowest quartile group (HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.05–1.29). More specifically, those in the Q4 of exposure to green spaces were at greater risk compared to the Q1 group (HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.04–1.29). The same study found that exposure to domestic gardens and blue spaces was negatively associated with psoriasis when comparing the Q4 and Q1 groups (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.77–0.93) and the Q3 and Q1 groups (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.81–0.98), respectively [11]. A separate analysis of a French prospective cohort, which followed 72,154 women over 28 years, yielded contradictory results. The authors found inverse associations with the risk of psoriasis for individuals born in rural areas (aHR 0.89, 95% CI 0.79–0.96) and for those with farming parents (aHR 0.84, 95% CI 0.72–0.97) [14]. Similarly, in a different cross-sectional analysis, outdoor activity restriction was associated with increased psoriasis severity (aOR 1.39, 95% CI 1.03–1.88) [30]. Lastly, a tree-based machine learning analysis of neighborhood-level data and psoriasis incidence in a population study from Quebec, Canada, found positive predictors of incident psoriasis to include nighttime light brightness, social and material deprivation. Negative predictors were ultraviolet radiation, increased maximum daily temperature, female sex, soil moisture, urbanization, and distance to expressways [10].

Insurance, Income, and Education

Two studies within our search addressed the relationship between insurance status and psoriasis. One study followed a cohort of 842 patients with an index hospitalization for psoriatic arthritis in the USA. The authors tracked any cause of rehospitalization, finding a greater proportion of uninsured patients among the readmitted compared to the index hospitalization cohort (18.6% vs. 4.4%; p = 0.015) [12]. A separate cross-sectional analysis of 4037 participants found a non-significant trend of increased odds of public/no health insurance among those with poorly controlled psoriasis (aOR 1.207, 95% CI 0.983–1.382) [26].

Several studies focused on the relationship between income and education on psoriasis. One evaluated a cohort of 36,003 patients in the pediatric Danish National Birth Cohort and found that lower maternal educational attainment was associated with psoriasis in offspring (aOR 1.62, 95% CI 1.20–2.18), after adjusting for maternal psoriasis and age in the pediatric cohort. The authors also found that the offspring of mothers in the highest quartile of household income had significantly lower odds of psoriasis than those in the lowest quartile (aOR 0.56, 95% CI 0.44–0.80) [15]. Separately, a cross-sectional study of 2,585,484 women from several Danish registries from 1977 to 2017 found that those with psoriasis (n = 77,143) were less likely to have high income (aOR 0.89, 95% CI 0.87–0.91) after adjusting for age [25].

Several studies have aimed to understand the association between these factors and psoriasis severity. Overall, they found that both low income and low educational attainment were associated with increased psoriasis severity [19, 20, 23, 26]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, lower income was also one of the strongest predictors of worsening psoriasis symptoms [22, 30]. Higher income was also generally associated with an improved dermatology-related quality of life and health-related quality of life in psoriasis [21, 24]. While the direct causal mechanisms for these associations have yet to be elucidated, one cross-sectional study found that access to biologics may be related to these associations [18].

Discussion

This systematic review synthesizes current evidence on the relationship between environmental and social determinants and psoriasis incidence and severity. Results across the 26 included studies consistently suggest that air or chemical pollution, higher social vulnerability, and poor neighborhood characteristics significantly contribute to the risk of developing psoriasis, increase the severity of disease, and result in more frequent flares or healthcare use. Environmental air pollutants that meaningfully contribute to this risk include particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10), nitrogen dioxide and other nitrogen oxides, sulfur dioxide, and ozone. Some research has shown a dose-dependent effect of this relationship, although effect sizes were typically modest.

Results from larger population-based cohorts, such as those utilizing the UK Biobank and based in Sweden, have shown that cumulative air pollution and neighborhood deprivation are independently associated with psoriasis incidence, even after adjusting for individual-level factors. These extend the understanding of psoriasis beyond the biological and genetic mechanisms that have defined the existing knowledge of the disease. The studies focused on social determinants, specifically income, educational attainment, and insurance coverage, demonstrating that lower socioeconomic status and higher social vulnerability are associated with poorer patient outcomes in psoriasis. Results regarding neighborhood-level characteristics and the lived environment are somewhat mixed. For example, some studies have shown that exposure to green space is associated with a higher incidence of psoriasis, while others have found it to be protective against the disease.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the development of psoriasis and its severity cannot be solely defined by the biological underpinnings of the disease. The physical and social environments in which one resides also play a role. Whether these factors act directly on the immune pathways that are dysregulated in psoriasis or act through separate mechanisms remains to be elucidated. Nonetheless, the available evidence supports approaching psoriasis within a broader biopsychosocial framework that integrates upstream environmental and social determinants alongside biologic therapies and individual-level risk factors.

Several limitations should be considered. Many studies evaluating social factors, such as income, education, and neighborhood characteristics, employed a cross-sectional design, which precludes the ability to infer causality. These data showed consistent associations across the studies, but the lack of temporal direction prevents one from drawing causal conclusions. Additionally, several cohort studies that maintained the temporality tenet of causality still could not fully mitigate residual confounding. There are also limitations in the existing data collection methods for environmental exposure. Current methods rely on ecological or neighborhood-level indices, which may not accurately reflect individual-level exposure to the variables of interest. Some cohorts, such as the Personalized Environment and Gene Study (PEGS) [29], were conducted in regions where pollution levels rarely exceed hazardous thresholds, which limits their global generalizability. Air pollution and other environmentally based exposures also have inherent seasonal and periodic variations, thereby complicating the interpretation of short-term and cross-sectional associations with disease severity. Lastly, heterogeneity in exposure definitions limits one’s ability to compare study results beyond general trends. Each study uses its own metrics for pollution exposure, social vulnerability, and neighborhood deprivation.

One important factor that was not addressed in the studies included in the review is the mediating role of psychosocial stress in psoriasis pathogenesis. Environmental- and neighborhood-level factors, such as low income, poor educational attainment, and proximity to air pollutants, influence psoriasis severity and incidence through several pathways. Existing research has found consistent associations between poor mental health—assessed using validated measures such as the Patient Health Questionnaire Depression Scale PHQ-9) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7)—and greater psoriasis severity. Poor mental health and psychosocial stressors have also been linked to worse scores on the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) [34–37]. There is limited data, however, which discusses the relationship between geospatial and environmental risk factors on the nature of this relationship. More research is required to elucidate the extent to which the exposure variables discussed in this paper rely on an increase in psychosocial stress as a mediator of their effects on psoriasis incidence and severity.

Although the review process adhered to PRISMA guidelines, some methodological limitations should also be noted. The authors only reviewed papers published in English and Spanish, introducing language bias. Unpublished research was also not considered, which could contribute to publication bias towards only significant findings. Two databases (PubMed and Embase) were systematically searched, but research from other databases may have been missed.

Conclusion

This systematic review underscores the significance of understanding the roles of environmental and social determinants in the pathogenesis and management of psoriasis. Individuals exposed to high levels of pollution and living in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas are at a greater risk for psoriasis development and increased severity. Integrating these results would improve the management and prevention measures that both dermatologists and public health officials could potentially employ. Specifically, large-scale interventions focused on environmental equity, access to healthcare, and mitigating socioeconomic disadvantage have the potential to improve outcomes for individuals with psoriasis. From a policy perspective, the consistent association between ambient air pollution and psoriasis highlights the importance of pollution mitigation and regulation as a means of disease prevention.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal designs to clarify the temporal and cumulative relationships between environmental and neighborhood factors and the onset and progression of psoriasis. Mechanistic studies on how they interact with immune dysregulation are also needed.
