Diversification of plant specific organellar single-stranded DNA binding genes and their roles in mitochondrial genome rearrangements in Arabidopsis and rice
Authors: Yingke Hou, Xiaolin Gu, Liyun Nie, Jie Wang, Zhiqiang Wu, Yi Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOrganelle genome stability is essential for plant development and adaptation. Recombination of repetitive sequences in plant mitochondrial genomes is a key factor influencing this stability and is tightly regulated by nuclear-encoded proteins, including plant-specific organellar single-stranded DNA-binding (OSB) proteins. However, the evolutionary history and overall impact of OSB genes on organellar genome stability remain poorly understood.ResultsHere, we identified 182 OSB genes from 59 species across major plant clades. Seed plant OSB genes formed three phylogenetic groups with varying numbers of conserved PDF domains, while chlorophytes, streptophytes, bryophytes, lycophytes, ferns lay outside these groups with lower copies. Using high-fidelity (HiFi) long-read sequencing, we examined the effects of OSB deficiency on organellar genome stability in both eudicots (Arabidopsis) and monocots (rice). In Arabidopsis osb1 and osb4, and rice osb1 mutants, recombination increased at a subset of nearly identical mitochondrial medium-sized repeats but decreased at repeats longer than 1 kb. These repeats displayed asymmetric accumulation of conformations, and a shift in the dominant genome conformation was also observed in some individuals. Moreover, different regions of the mutant mitochondrial genomes showed uneven HiFi read coverage.ConclusionsOur findings highlight important roles of OSB genes in regulating the stability of plant mitochondrial genomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08952-6.

Background

Genomic stability and variation play critical roles in the survival, reproduction, and adaptation of living organisms. Research on nuclear genome stability has made significant progress, revealing the roles of structural and sequence variations in development and disease [1–3]. In contrast to the extensive research on nuclear genome variation, the study of semi-autonomous organellar genomes in plants has lagged behind.

The paradox of plant mitochondrial genome evolution lies in their highly dynamic structures despite low mutation rates [4–7]. Although synonymous substitution rates are extremely low compared with nuclear genomes, structural variations and rearrangements occur at a high frequency, which are associated with recombination events mediated by various types of repeated sequences [8]. Based on the length, repeats are classified as large (≥ 1,000 bp), medium (50 − 1,000 bp), and short (< 50 bp) groups, with each group potentially exerting distinct effects on genome stability [9–13]. Large repeats often exist as near-identical pairs and frequently mediate homologous recombination, driving the dynamic and variable architecture of plant mitochondrial DNA [9, 14, 15]. Recombination of medium-sized repeats occurs infrequently, typically through ectopic homologous recombination, and is highly sensitive to genetic, developmental, and environmental factors, contributing to substoichiometric shifting and notable phenotypic effects [10, 12, 13, 16, 17]. These rearrangements can generate new open reading frames (ORFs) and some of the ORFs could lead to cytoplasmic male sterility (CMS), altered leaf morphology, and changes in seed germination rate [18–21]. Therefore, maintaining proper recombination of repeated sequences in plant organellar genomes is essential for plant normal survival and environmental adaptation. A key challenge is understanding how the cyto-nuclear interactions regulate organellar DNA recombination to ensure stability while allowing adaptive variation.

The recombination activity of diversified mitochondrial repeats is controlled by nuclear genes. The protein products of these nuclear-encoded genes target to mitochondria and chloroplasts, and they can regulate the replication, repair and recombination (RRR) process of organellar genomes. By affecting the fidelity of these processes, they can lead to the accumulation of recombined alternative mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) configurations, thereby compromising genome stability and causing abnormal plant development [12, 22]. Some RRR genes have been extensively studied, such as MutS-like protein MUTS HOMOLOG 1 (MSH1). The MSH1 maintains mitochondrial genome stability by suppressing aberrant recombination and reducing mutation rates [6]. Previous studies in Arabidopsis have shown that MSH1-mediated mechanisms accelerate the sorting and purging of deleterious organellar variants across generations [13, 17, 23, 24]. Furthermore, disruption and restoration of MSH1 genes generates diverse mitochondrial structural variants, while the chloroplast genome remains stable [25]. However, while dozens of RRR genes have been identified [22], their specific effects on organelle genomes remain largely unexplored comprehensively—including the roles of plant-specific organellar single-stranded DNA-binding (OSB) proteins in maintaining organellar genome stability.

The plant-specific OSB proteins are composed of a N-terminal transit peptide that mediates targeting to mitochondria and/or chloroplasts, a poorly conserved central oligonucleotide/oligosaccharide-binding (OB)-fold-like domain, and one or more C-terminal PDF domains that contribute mainly to bind single-stranded DNA with high affinity [12, 26, 27]. In A. thaliana, there are four OSB genes: OSB1 and OSB4 are targeted to mitochondria, OSB2 localizes to chloroplasts, and OSB3 is dual-targeted to both organelles [26, 27]. Mutations in OSB1 disrupt mitochondrial genome stability by promoting ectopic homologous recombination via only limited known intermediate-sized repeats, resulting in rearrangements across certain regions of the mitochondrial genome [26, 28, 29]. For example, the repeats EE and L, OSB1 suppresses homologous recombination partly through inhibition of R-loop formation and interacts with AtRNH1B/C and single-stranded DNA-binding protein (SSB) to monitor recombination events [28–30]. Consistently, Arabidopsis osb1 mutants exhibit mitochondrial dysfunction-associated phenotypes, and similar phenotypes are observed in osb4 mutants [12, 26]. In contrast, the roles of Arabidopsis OSB3 and OSB2 in organellar genome stability remain unclear. Quantitative real-time PCR analysis showed that the expression of OSB3 in Arabidopsis Col-0 wild type plants was higher than that in the control after 8 h of salt treatment [31]. And osb2 mutants show no detectable increase in chloroplast point mutations or repeat-mediated recombination compared to wild-type plants [32]. However, these findings about organellar stability are limited to model plant of Arabidopsis. Although a study found rice has three OSB genes [26], the effects of OSB genes on organellar genome variation in monocots have not been previously investigated. Furthermore, PCR-based methods can only target known markers lacking the whole genome level resolution. They cannot reveal the comprehensive spectrum of recombination variation, particularly complex conformations and structural heteroplasmy. Despite being briefly mentioned previously [26], the evolutionary history of the OSB family is poorly understood, such as PDF domain expansion [33]. Fortunately, the increasing availability of sequencing data and bioinformatic tools for genome assembly and organellar variant analysis [13, 30, 34] now make it possible to comprehensively explore OSB genes’ effects on organellar genome stability and their evolutionary history.

To test whether OSB genes play conserved roles in regulating organellar genome variation across eudicots and monocots, we first reconstructed a comprehensive phylogenetic tree of OSB proteins across 59 plant species and identified three major groups in seed plants with varying numbers of PDF domains. Then, we investigated mitochondrial genome variations in both Arabidopsis and rice osb mutants based on PacBio HiFi sequencing data. In osb1 and osb4 mutants of Arabidopsis, recombination frequencies of a subset of nearly identical mitochondrial repeats were altered. Specifically, medium-sized repeats showed increased recombination, while large repeats exhibited decreased recombination with asymmetric accumulation of the four recombination conformations. Some mutants displayed altered dominant genome conformations. Similarly, osb1 mutants in rice showed consistent mitochondrial structural variations. Moreover, the mitochondrial genomes in these osb mutants showed uneven sequencing coverage compared to surrounding regions. These results underscore the importance of OSB genes in plant mitochondrial genome dynamics.

Results

Phylogenetic classification and copy number expansion of PDF domains in OSB proteins across land plants

To investigate the evolutionary history of the OSB protein family, we constructed a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree using 182 the conserved sequences of OSB protein sequences identified from land plants and green algae (Fig. 1; Table S1). The tree revealed three well-supported groups (Groups I, II, and III) encompassing seed plant OSB genes, whereas OSB genes from algae, mosses, lycophytes, and ferns were clustered outside these groups with only one or two copies. Gymnosperm species contained OSB genes from Group I (e.g., Cycas panzhihuaensis, Pinus tabuliformis) or Group III (e.g., Ginkgo biloba, Welwitschia mirabilis, Gnetum montanum). Most (60.9%, 28/46) angiosperms contained three OSB genes with one in each of the three groups, as in Liriodendron chinense, O. sativa, and Helianthus annuus (Fig. 1). The phylogenetic distribution suggested the possibility of putative OSB gene family expansion that may have occurred after seed plants diverged from ferns but before angiosperms radiated, an inference drawn from the comparative patterns seen in non-seed and flowering plants. A total of 16 angiosperm species lost OSB genes in at least one group (Fig. 1), possibly resulting from independent lineage-specific loss. For instance, A. thaliana, Brassica rapa, B. oleracea, B. carinata and Chrysanthemum indicum lacked Group I OSBs, while Citrullus lanatus, Cucumis sativus, Cucurbita moschata, Lagenaria siceraria and Platycodon grandiflorus lacked Group III members. Piper nigrum, Amborella trichopoda, Adenosma buchneroides, Brachypodium distachyon, and Salix purpurea only contained Groups I and III OSB. Dendrobium catenatum encoded solely a Group I OSB, suggesting the potential loss of Groups II and III in its lineage, a pattern that requires confirmation across the orchid family through broader sampling. In contrast, several species, including A. thaliana (three Group III OSBs), Zea mays (two Group I OSBs), and P. nigrum (three Group I OSBs), indicating potential gene duplication events within certain family or species.

We next examined the structural diversification of OSB proteins, regarding copy number and sequence features of PDF domains (Fig. 1; Fig.S1; Table S1, Table S2). All 56 Group I OSBs and most (81.5%, 44/54) in Group II contained a single PDF domain. However, several Group II proteins—e.g., from B. carinata, B. oleracea, and Populus trichocarpa—contained multiple PDF domains. In contrast, PDF domain expansion was prevalent in Group III, where 96.7% (59/61) of proteins contained ≥ 2 domains, and A. trichopoda encoding up to five. Although generally conserved, key residues in the PDF domain varied across the three groups (Fig. S1A). For example, the motif at positions 1–5 exhibits distinct preferences among the three groups (Fig. S1A). Notably, Group III OSB proteins frequently displayed extended insertions (> 2 residues) in the conserved region spanning positions 19–30, a feature rare in Group I but absent in Group II (Fig. S1A). In proteins with multiple PDF domains, typically only one copy exhibited this insertion, while others retained the two-residue configuration (Fig. S1B). Conversely, OSB proteins with a single PDF domain almost always maintained a two-residue insertion in this region (Fig. 1; Fig. S1A). These structural diversifications suggest potential group-specific evolutionary paths for OSB proteins in terms of PDF domain copy number and sequence features. Taken together, the phylogenetic distribution and structural divergences indicate that the three OSB groups likely have followed distinct evolutionary trajectories.

Changed recombination and asymmetric conformation accumulation in a subset of nearly identical mitochondrial repeats in osb1 and osb4 mutants in A. thaliana

To investigate the organellar genome structural rearrangements of these osb mutants in A. thaliana, we generated osb1, osb2, osb3 and osb4 mutants (Table S3, Table S4) and sequenced multiple individuals using PacBio HiFi technology (Table S5). Using Col-0 as the reference, the osb1 and osb4 mutants exhibited increased recombination at some medium-sized mitochondrial repeats (e.g., A, B, C, D, F, G, I, K, L, Q, R, BB, EE, HNR14), but reduced frequencies at large repeats (Large1, Large2, Fig. 2A-F; Fig. S2, Fig. S3), with Large1, Large2, A, F, and L most affected compared with wild type (Fig. 2F-G; Fig. S2, Fig. S3). The increases in recombination varied considerably between individual mutants, indicating heterogeneous dynamics and cross generation inheritance (Fig. 2F-G; Fig. S2, Fig. S3). For example, repeats A and F showed much higher recombination frequencies in individuals osb1-F3-14-13 and osb1-F3-9-6 than in other individuals. The affected repeats were a subset of high-identity or perfect repeats in osb1 and osb4 mutants in A. thaliana (Fig. 2F; Table S6, Table S7). In contrast, no recombination changes were observed in the mitochondrial genomes of osb2 and osb3 mutants (Fig. S4) or in chloroplast genomes of any mutants (Fig. S5, Fig. S7).

In osb1 and osb4, recombinant forms (1–2, 2 − 1) of multiple repeats (e.g., Large1, A, F, L, Large2, B, I, G, K, D, EE, R) accumulated asymmetrically across mutants (Fig. 2H; Fig. S8; Table S8). Several repeats (e.g., Large1, A, L, Q, B, I, G, K) also showed biased accumulation of one parental configuration (1–1 or 2–2). Furthermore, uneven coverage regions (near 38-74 kb; near 272-345 kb) in osb1 and osb4 mitochondrial genomes were observed (Fig. 2A-E; Fig. S2, Fig. S3). For example, in sample osb1-F3-14-11, abrupt coverage changes occurred precisely at positions 38,691, 74,374, 272,764, and 344,149. Notably, the regions spanning 38,692 − 74,374 and 272,765 − 344,149 exhibited consistently lower coverage compared to other genomic segments. Overall, in A. thaliana, loss of OSB1 and OSB4 function disrupted mitochondrial genome stability, altered recombination patterns and led to asymmetric molecular conformation and uneven DNA coverage, demonstrating their essential roles for mitochondrial genomic stability.

Altered mitochondrial genome conformations in osb1 and osb4 mutants in A. thaliana

For a genomic region mapping to approximately 345 kb in some osb1 and osb4 mitochondria, reads supporting the reference sequences were profoundly and specifically depleted (Fig. S2, Fig. S3). In osb1-F3-14-11, for instance, coverage precipitously dropped from 325× at position 344,149 to a markedly reduced range of 112–119× across the subsequent region (344,150–345,148), indicating altered dominant conformations compared to Col-0. To further illustrate the structural changes of mitochondrial genomes in A. thaliana osb mutants, we reassembled mitochondrial genomes from osb1 and osb4 plants. Altered assembly graphs supported by reads were detected in 5/11 osb1 individuals from three independent lines and 3/8 osb4 individuals from two lines (Fig. 3A; Fig. S9). These results indicate that dominant mitochondrial genome conformations were altered in some osb1 and osb4 mutants. Interestingly, the mitochondrial genomes of osb1-F3-14-11, osb1-F3-5-7, osb1-F3-5-6, osb1-F3-9-8, osb4-F3-K1-7, osb4-F3-K43-6, and osb4-F3-K43-7 exhibited non-circular assembly graphs (Fig. 3A; Fig. S9); and osb1-F3-9-6 contained two circular mitochondrial genomes of different sizes (Fig. S9). These conformational changes correlated with high-frequency recombination of Large1, repeat L or F (Fig. 2C; Fig. S2, Fig. S3), as shown in osb1-F3-9-6, where repeat F mediated the formation of two circular genomes (Fig. 2C; Fig. S2, Fig. S9).

Within the osb1-F3-14 line, the individual osb1-F3-14-11 lacked Large1 and L repeat copies, whereas siblings retained wild-type master conformations (Fig. 3B; Fig. S9; Table S9). In all osb1 and osb4 mutants, repeats such as A, L, and F (detailed elsewhere) showed altered recombination frequencies compared to Col-0 (Fig. S7, Fig.S8). Despite this individual-level heterogeneity, elevated recombination frequencies at affected repeats (e.g., A, B, C, D, F, G, I, K, L, Q, R, BB, EE, HNR14) were consistently found across multiple lines. These results suggest that recombination changes in osb1 and osb4 mutants are both widespread and heterogeneous at the individual level.

To accurately analyze recombination in the highly divergent osb1-F3-14-11 mitochondrial genome, we generated a lineage-specific reference assembly. Using this new reference, we detected elevated recombination frequencies at repeats A, B, C, D, F, G, I, R, X, EE, and HNR14, along with altered distributions of recombinant configurations (Fig. 3C, Fig. S10). Furthermore, the mitochondrial genome showed uneven read coverage (near 142-202 kb; near 258-295 kb). For example, abrupt coverage changes occurred precisely at positions 141,979, 202,228, 258,637, and 294,324. Notably, the regions spanning 141,980 − 202,228 and 258,638 − 294,324 exhibited consistently lower coverage compared to other genomic segments. Taken together, these results indicated that loss of OSB1 or OSB4 genes altered mitochondrial genome conformations in a subset of individuals likely driven by stochastic or other factors, and highlight the role of OSB gene in maintaining mitochondrial genome architecture and stability.

Conserved functions of rice OSB1 in regulating mitochondrial genome structural variants

To explore the role of rice OSB genes in mitochondrial genome stability, we assembled a mitochondrial reference genome for Oryza sativa cv. Nipponbare (Wu-NIP-mt; 359,093 bp) that is suitable for the hifisr v0.3.0 pipeline [13, 25]. The assembly is non-circular and contains two large repeats (4,081 bp and 1,489 bp) (Fig. S11A-B). Compared to the 2021 assembly [35], Wu-NIP-mt genome retains only a single copy of previously duplicated regions (e.g., 8.8 kb, 58 kb, 72 kb) (Fig. S11C). Validation using HiFi reads from a NIP dataset [36] confirmed uniform coverage and expected repeat-mediated recombination patterns, supporting the reliability of our assembly (Fig. S11A). We generated osb1, osb2, and osb3 lines (Table S4, Table 10) and performed PacBio HiFi sequencing (Table S11). Using NIP as the reference, the osb1 mutants showed increased recombination at several medium-sized repeats (e.g., repeat r5, r8, r10, r14, r15, r105) and reduced recombination at large repeats (r1 and r4), with r1, r4, r5, and r8 showing the largest changes (Fig. 4A-B). Similar to A. thaliana, the affected repeats were a subset of high-identity or perfect repeats (Fig. 4E; Table S12, Table S13); the extent of increased recombination differed across individual mutants, revealing heterogeneous dynamics (Fig. 4A-B). In contrast, the mitochondrial genomes in rice osb2 and osb3 mutants, and chloroplast genomes in all three mutants showed no obvious changes (Fig. S12, Fig. 15). Importantly, recombination in osb1 mutants was frequently asymmetric, with one recombinant form (e.g., 1–2) consistently more abundant than its reciprocal (2 − 1), and certain parental configurations (1–1 or 2–2) disproportionately accumulated (Fig. 4C; Table S14). For instance, repeats r1, r5, r8, and r105 showed biased accumulation of recombinants, while r1, r4, r8, r15, and r105 exhibited dominant enrichment of one parental form. Notably, in the ~ 300 kb region of osb1 mitochondrial genomes, coverage of non-recombined reads dropped abruptly. A representative example showed a sharp decrease from 505× at position 296,188 to 11× at 296,189 in individual osb1_1 (Fig. 4A), indicating a pronounced shift in the dominant genome conformation. De novo assembly confirmed that all osb1 lines harbored altered mitochondrial genome structures (355–364 kb), distinct from the 359 kb wild-type genome (Fig. 4D-E; Fig. S14A-D). These rearrangements involved repeat copy number variations, insertions/deletions, and novel combinations of repeat elements. For instance, the osb1_1 line lacked an 8.7 kb region and carried a new pair of ~ 8.8 kb large repeats, along with altered r5 copy numbers (Fig. 4E; Table S15), coinciding with high-frequency recombination at r5 and r8 (Fig. 4A-B). Moreover, osb1 mutants exhibited uneven mitochondrial sequencing coverage (Fig. 4A), a pattern indicative of mtDNA maintenance defects and consistent with findings in Arabidopsis. Overall, our findings demonstrate that rice OSB1 plays a conserved role in maintaining mitochondrial genome stability by suppressing structural variants in both A. thaliana and O. sativa.

Impact of OSB proteins on small-scale variants in organellar genomes

The overall single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) or insertions/deletions (InDels) frequencies in organellar genomes in A. thaliana and O. sativa osb mutants were not changed (Fig. S15). However, we observed several high-confidence variants (sequencing depth ≥ 20× and supporting reads ≥ 10) were predominantly distributed in non-coding and intergenic regions (Table S16), with notable examples including a T > G mutation in the mitochondrial cosRNA M188 region of Arabidopsis osb1 and osb4 mutants (supporting reads 63–91, sequencing depth 186 − 168) and a polyA repeat variation in the chloroplast ropC2 gene of rice osb3_2 mutants (supporting reads over 3,000, sequencing depth 3,658). Although the global variant profile remained largely unchanged, these findings highlight the possibility that OSB proteins may influence specific structural or sequence contexts, potentially through indirect mechanisms.

Discussion

Nuclear-encoded factors are pivotal in maintaining organellar genome stability, but their evolutionary history and impact on organellar genome variation remain largely unexplored. This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the Organellar Single-stranded DNA-Binding protein family. Firstly, we revealed the complex evolutionary trajectories of OSB genes across 59 plant species, identifying lineage-specific expansions and losses that gave rise to three distinct phylogenetic groups in angiosperms and gymnosperms. Then using long-read HiFi sequencing, we explored the impact of OSBs on organellar genome variation using the eudicot Arabidopsis thaliana and monocot Oryza sativa as model systems. Our functional analyses demonstrated that deficiency in OSB1 gene leads to increased recombination at nearly identical mitochondrial medium-sized repeats but decreased recombination at longer repeats (> 1 kb) in both species, accompanied by asymmetric conformation accumulation and shifts in dominant genome conformations in some mutants. Additionally, these mutants showed uneven mitochondrial genome coverage and skewed recombination product ratios, suggesting genomic instability might arise from aberrant recombination leading to heterogeneous subgenomic molecules, or defects in DNA replication. Parallel functional patterns were observed for Arabidopsis OSB4 gene in mitochondrial genome maintenance. In contrast, no detectable impact on organellar genome variation was observed for other members, such as Arabidopsis OSB2 and OSB3 genes, under standard growth conditions. These findings highlighted the important roles of OSB genes in regulating recombination processes, framing the following detailed discussion on their evolutionary and mechanistic actions in safeguarding organellar DNA.

Evolutionary history of nuclear-encoded OSB genes

The unicellular green algae Chlamydomonas reinhardtii lacks OSB genes [26], whereas Spirogloea muscicola and Mesotaenium endlicherianum contain OSB genes (Fig. 1; Table S1), suggesting that OSB genes likely emerged following the divergence with C. reinhardtii, yet prior to the evolution of terrestrial plants. Furthermore, OSB genes from early-diverging land plants (e.g., bryophytes and ferns) formed a distinct clade outside of the three major groups found in seed plants, suggesting a different evolutionary pattern. In contrast, the OSB genes in seed plant diversified into three distinct phylogenetic groups (Fig. 1). Most angiosperms possess three OSB genes, each located in Groups I, II, and III (Fig. 1), while gymnosperms typically only have two of these Groups (I and III), indicating that Group II OSB genes might have been lost during gymnosperm evolution or could be a feature specific to angiosperms. In addition, lineage-specific absences—for example, the lack of Group I OSBs in Arabidopsis and Brassica, or the absence of Group III OSBs in Cucurbitaceae—reflect differences in the evolutionary retention of OSB genes. The expansion of Group III OSBs in Arabidopsis thaliana, which contains three members in this group, may be attributed to species-specific gene duplication following whole-genome duplication. These patterns reflect the complex evolutionary trajectories of plant gene families, shaped by lineage-specific expansions, contractions, and gene retention dynamics [37].

OSBs bind to single-stranded DNA, inhibit the recombination of mitochondrial repetitive sequences, and maintain mitochondrial genome stability [12, 26, 28]. The PDF domain is a key structural feature of OSBs that mediates their interaction with single-stranded DNA. Although [26] speculated that the copy number of the PDF domain may correlate with DNA-binding capacity, this has not yet been experimentally confirmed. In Group III OSB proteins, an expansion in the PDF domain copy number has been observed (Fig. 1; Table S1, Table S2), often accompanied by structural variation between PDF domain copies. For example, longer insertions (> 2 residues) at conserved positions 19–30 are found in only one of multiple domains, indicating potential structural divergence among OSB gene copies (Fig. 1; Fig. S1). These structural variations suggest functional diversification within OSB proteins, and future studies combining structural biology with DNA-binding assays and genetic complementation will elucidate the mechanistic basis through which PDF domain expansions and modifications regulate organellar genome stability, thereby advancing our understanding of nuclear-organellar coevolution. Whether the insertions in the 19–30 region of Group III OSB proteins affect ssDNA-binding affinity remains to be investigated in future studies.

Roles of nuclear-encoded OSB proteins in DNA replication, repair and recombination

The heterogeneous recombination frequencies observed across individual osb1 and osb4 mutants are biologically meaningful, reflecting the inherent stochasticity of plant mitochondrial genome dynamics. Arabidopsis OSB1 and OSB4 are regulators of mitochondrial homologous recombination [12, 26]. Single-gene mutations in Arabidopsis OSB1, OSB4, and rice OSB1 alter recombination frequencies of near-identical mitochondrial repeats and increase structural heterogeneity (Figs. 2C and 4A-E; Fig.S2, Fig.S3). In some cases, mutant individuals with high heteroplasmy exhibit distinct dominant mitochondrial genome conformations (Fig. S9, Fig.S14), which likely arise from individual variation in recombination frequency and stochastic segregation of mitochondrial variants during single-seed descent. These findings suggest that Arabidopsis OSB1, OSB4, and rice OSB1 function to suppress in a subset of nearly identical mitochondrial repeat-mediated recombination, thereby maintaining native genome conformation and heteroplasmy levels. Compared to the wild type, mitochondrial structural heterogeneity was increased in these mutants. It is speculated that under the influence of MSH1-mediated organellar sorting [23, 24], these heteroplasmic variations may become more frequently fixed in progeny.

In addition, Arabidopsis osb1, osb4, and rice osb1 mutants display uneven mitochondrial genome coverage (Figs. 2B and 4B-D; Fig. S2, Fig.S3) and skewed recombination products (Figs. 2D and 4F; Fig. S8). As the mitochondrial genome undergoes aberrant recombination in the mutants, it could generate a complex population of subgenomic molecules [12]. These molecules vary in their structure, the number and orientation of repeat copies, and their relative stoichiometries within the cellular pool. This inherent heterogeneity—where some genomic regions are over-represented across the collective DNA molecules and others are under-represented—was a plausible explanation for the fluctuating read depth. Another explanation could be a potential defect in the DNA replication machinery. The co-occurrence of both uneven coverage and skewed recombination in osb mutants points to a potential failure at the replication-recombination interface. This is mechanistically plausible given the documented role of recombination in facilitating mitochondrial DNA replication [12, 38]. We propose that OSB proteins loss likely triggers unstable DNA synthesis at repeats (causing uneven coverage) and forces aberrant repair (producing skewed recombination).

In plant mitochondria, nuclear-encoded factors like the OSB proteins fine-tune ectopic recombination, a process that maintains genomic dynamics when controlled but causes instability when dysregulated [12]. In osb1, osb4, and rice osb1 mutants, changed recombination frequencies of a subset of mitochondrial near-perfect repeats were observed (Figs. 2C and 4E; Table S6, Table 12), along with altered dominant genome conformations in some individuals (Fig. S9, Fig. S14). A subset of repeat sequences did not show changes in recombination frequency in the osb1 and osb4 mutants of A. thaliana (Fig. 2F; Table S6, Table S7) and osb1 mutants of rice (Fig. 4E; Table S12, Table S13). The underlying cause of this selective stability remains unclear but could potentially include intrinsic repeat features—such as length, degree of homology, or genomic context—functional redundancy among OSB homologs, or compensatory activity by other DNA maintenance pathways. We propose the following mechanistic hypothesis: the loss of OSB could impair DNA polymerase activity—potentially by disrupting substrate positioning through its oligomeric binding to single-stranded DNA—leading to an increase in DNA breaks and replication errors. These lesions may subsequently favor repair pathways that utilize homologous sequences as templates, thereby promoting ectopic recombination between highly similar repeat sequences. This mechanism accounts for the elevated recombination frequency at specific genomic repeats and the resulting structural variations observed in the mitochondrial genomes of osb mutants, ultimately explaining the preferential use of ectopic recombination and the consequent genome rearrangements.

Despite mutations in the same nuclear genes (e.g., OSB1 or OSB4), different lines often exhibit distinct dominant mitochondrial genome conformations. As mitochondrial genomes inherently exist in multiple structural forms, random drift can lead to the fixation of different conformations even under identical genetic backgrounds. These observations underscore the dynamic nature of plant mitochondrial genome evolution. Overall, Arabidopsis OSB1, OSB4, and rice OSB1 are essential for mitochondrial genome stability. Future research should integrate structural biology, in vivo imaging, and reconstituted biochemical systems to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which OSB proteins suppress ectopic recombination across intermediate and short repeats, participate in microhomology-mediated repair, or coordinate with other DNA damage response pathways. Unraveling these mechanisms will not only uncover fundamental principles governing organellar genome stability but also provide evolutionary insights into how nuclear control maintains genomic order in plant mitochondria, offering potential strategies for enhancing the stability of engineered organellar genomes in crops.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, further studies are needed to clarify the mechanisms that OSBs suppress medium-sized repeat recombination. Second, under standard growth condition, several OSB family members—such as Arabidopsis OSB2, Arabidopsis OSB3, and rice OSB2—showed no detectable impact on the structure variation of their respective organellar genomes. Future work should integrate localization studies, stress-condition experiments, cross-species protein interaction analysis, and higher-order mutants to delineate their potential roles in organellar genome dynamics. Furthermore, the observed small-scale variants in cosRNA M188 and ropC2 may warrant future functional investigation into their potential effects on RNA processing or transcription.

Conclusions

Using long-read HiFi sequencing, we showed that loss of OSB1 and OSB4 genes in Arabidopsis and OSB1 gene in rice disrupts mitochondrial genome stability, causing structural rearrangements and altered dominant conformations in some individuals. The altered recombination patterns supported a role for OSB proteins in suppressing aberrant homologous recombination, while uneven genome coverage in mutants may also suggest involvement in mitochondrial DNA dynamics. Together, our results highlight the critical function of nuclear-encoded OSB genes in maintaining organellar genome stability and advance our understanding of the mechanisms that govern plant mitochondrial genome integrity and variation.

Methods

Plant materials, DNA extraction and PacBio HiFi sequencing

To obtain mutants of OSB genes in A. thaliana and O. sativa (Nipponbare), the osb2, osb3 and osb4 mutants in A. thaliana were generated using a CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing system [39]. The constructed pHEE401E vector was transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 via heat shock and subsequently introduced into plants through floral dip transformation. T1 transformants were selected on ½ MS medium containing 50 mg/L hygromycin, with positive lines verified by PCR amplification of target loci followed by Sanger sequencing. Homozygous osb1 mutants in A. thaliana were kindly provided by Dr. Jie Qian (Southwest University). OSB mutants in O. sativa (Nipponbare) were obtained from Biorun Biotechnology Company (Wuhan, China). The pYL-HU-U3-CCDB-tRNA(K1) vector was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 and Agrobacterium-mediated transformation of embryogenic calli. The information of mutants is shown in Table S3 and Table S10. All mutants were verified by sequencing or genotyping. The primers used are listed in Table S17.

To generate organellar genome mutation accumulation lines of osb mutants in A. thaliana, we followed an approach similar to that described for msh1 mutants [40]. OSB mutants were used as the pollen donor to cross with wild-type Col-0. The F1 plant was self-pollinated, and F2 individuals were screened using allele-specific PCR markers (Table S17) to identify homozygous mutant and homozygous wild-type individuals. Seed from each selected F2 plant were harvested individually and propagated as independent F3 homozygous mutant and wild-type lines.

To investigate mitochondrial genome variation in osb mutants, genomic DNA was extracted from Arabidopsis and rice for long-read sequencing. A. thaliana plants in were grown under short-day (8-h light/16-h dark) condition for six weeks. Rosettes from six-week-old individual plants were collected for genomic DNA extraction. O. sativa plants were cultivated under long-day conditions (14-h light/10-h dark cycle). Fresh leaves at the tillering stage were collected from both wild-type and osb mutant individuals for genomic DNA extraction. And SMRTbell libraries construction was performed according to PacBio’s standard protocol. Sequencing was performed using the PacBio Revio platform. The read statistics of individual plants could be found in Table S5 and Table S11.

Species selection and data acquisition

To investigate the evolutionary history of the OSB gene family across the plant kingdom, a total of 60 plant species were selected, including one chlorophyte species, three streptophytes, three bryophytes, one lycophytes, one ferns, five gymnosperms, and 46 angiosperms (comprising six basal angiosperms, nine monocots, and 31 dicots). Genome sequences were downloaded from public databases. Detailed information is provided in Table S18.

Gene identification and sequence retrieval

To identify OSB homologs across plant species, the four OSB proteins from A. thaliana (OSB1: AT1G47720, OSB2: AT4G20010, OSB3: AT5G44785, OSB4: AT1G31010) were used as queries for BLASTp v2.11.0+ [41] searches against protein databases of all selected species. All candidate proteins with were retained. Domain information (OB-fold-like and PDF domains) of Arabidopsis OSBs was obtained from UniProt: OSB1: Q9SX99; OSB2: Q8GXH3; OSB3: Q8GWJ4; OSB4: Q9FYJ2.

Domain sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.490 (--auto –clustalout; [42]), and the alignment results were converted to Stockholm format for HMM profile construction using hmmbuild. Candidate sequences were scanned with hmmsearch (HMMER 3.0; [43]) to identify proteins containing both the PDF and OB-fold-like domains (E value ≤ 1e-5). Given the low conservation of OB-fold-like domains across species, additional HMM profiles were constructed from the OB-fold-like domain sequences of previously identified OSB proteins to improve detection in species showing weak BLAST hits. A total of 182 proteins containing both domains from 59 species are listed in Table S1.

Sequence alignment and phylogenetic tree construction

To reconstruct the evolutionary relationships of OSB proteins across plant species, protein sequences were aligned with MAFFT v7.490 (-m MFP -b 1000 -T AUTO; [42]), and the resulting alignments were manually trimmed in MEGA v11 [44] to remove ambiguously aligned regions and those with excessive gaps, thereby enhancing phylogenetic signal. This process specifically retained conserved regions, mainly covering OB-fold-like and PDF domains. Maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic trees were inferred using IQ-TREE v2.2.0 [45] within PhyloSuite v1.2.3 [46]. Model selection was set to Auto, with 5,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates, maximum iteration set to 1,000, and a minimum correlation coefficient of 0.90. The resulting trees were visualized and annotated using iTOL v6 [47]. The phylogenetic tree with branch length information (Newick format) is provided in Supplementary File S1.

Assembly of the Nipponbare mitochondrial genome

To generate a reference mitochondrial genome for Nipponbare that is suitable for the hifisr v0.3.0 pipeline (https://github.com/zouyinstein/hifisr; [13, 25]), the genome sequencing data from BioProject PRJNA953663 [36] were used for assembly. The reads were first processed using MECAT2 (v20190314; genome size set to 370 kb; [48]), followed by assembly with Flye v2.9.2-b1795(command: flye --meta --pacbio-hifi mecat_mito.fasta --extra-params output_gfa_before_rr = 1 --genome-size 370 K -t 20 -o flye_mito_370K; [49]). The resulting GFA assembly graph was visualized using Bandage (v0.9.0; [50]).

To evaluate assembly reliability, variant analysis was performed again using the script (https://github.com/zouyinstein/hifisr/blob/main/analysis_scripts/get_variants_in_reads.py; [13, 25]). A continuous and evenly distributed coverage profile indicated a reliable assembly; otherwise, manual correction and re-analysis were performed until a consistent result was obtained.

Organellar genome variant analysis

To characterize structural variation and recombination activity in plant mitochondrial genomes, organellar variants were identified using the hifisr v0.3.0 pipeline (https://github.com/zouyinstein/hifisr; [13, 25]). For A. thaliana, the Col-0 mitochondrial reference genome (NC_037304.1) and chloroplast reference genome (NC_000932.1) were used. For rice (Oryza sativa), a self-assembled mitochondrial reference genome of Nipponbare (abbreviated as “Wu-NIP-mt”) and a previously published chloroplast reference genome (Version: AY522330.1-Nipponbare) were employed. Repeat sequences in the Arabidopsis mitochondrial genome (NC_037304.1) and our assembled rice Nipponbare mitochondrial genome (“Wu-NIP-mt”) were annotated following method described in [25]. The mitochondrial genome repeats of O. sativa were named sequentially as r1, r2, r3, and so forth (Table S19). The recombined conformations (1–2 and 2 − 1) were identified from the repeat coordinates and read support. Recombination frequency was calculated as: the sum of recombined reads (1–2 and 2 − 1) divided by the total number of recombined and non-recombined reads, multiplied by 100%. The SNV/InDel analysis was performed using the hifisr (v0.3.0) pipeline [25].

Assembling mitochondrial genomes of osb mutants

To assemble mitochondrial genomes from osb mutants, full-length mitochondrial reads were first extracted and merged. The pooled reads were merged and assembled using MECAT2 (genome size: 370 kb; [48]), followed by Flye v2.9.2-b1795 (command: flye --meta --pacbio-hifi mecat_mito.fasta --extra-params output_gfa_before_rr = 1 --genome-size 370 K -t 20 -o flye_mito_370K; [49]). Subsequent steps followed the same procedures as described for Nipponbare mitochondrial genome assembly.

Comparison of wild-type and mutant mitochondrial genomes

To identify structural variants (SVs) in the altered mitochondrial conformations, we performed pairwise alignments between the assembled mutant genomes and the rotated reference genome using a custom BLASTn-based script (v2.14.1+; [41]). Results were manually curated to remove redundant alignments from small fragments. Remaining alignments were treated as true SVs.
