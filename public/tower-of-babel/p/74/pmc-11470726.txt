What contributes to a decline in cognitive performance among home care clients? Analysis of interRAI data from across Canada
Authors: Blake Filderman, Nicole Williams, Amanda Mofina, Dawn M Guthrie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe current study examined potential risk factors for experiencing a decline on the interRAI Cognitive Performance Scale (CPS).MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study using secondary data collected with the Resident Assessment Instrument for Home Care (RAI-HC) for all assessments completed in Canada between 2001 and 2020. Eligible home care clients included individuals 65+, with at least two assessments completed within 12 months, and who had a CPS score of zero at baseline (n = 146,187). A decline on the CPS was defined as any increase (i.e., worsening) on the CPS score between the two assessments.ResultsThe mean age of the sample was 80.6 years (standard deviation = 7.7), 67.9% were female and 44.5% were widowed. At the time of the second assessment, 25.2% experienced a decline on their CPS score. In the final multivariate model, age, having a diagnosis of Alzheimer’s dementia/other type of dementia, physical inactivity, and having a caregiver at risk of experiencing burden were the most significant predictors of experiencing the outcome.ConclusionsRoughly one-quarter of Canadian home care clients experienced a cognitive decline, over an average of seven months. Since there are some modifiable risk factors for this outcome, it is important to identify and flag these factors as early as possible. Early identification of modifiable risk factors allows clinicians to create care plans that can optimize the well-being of the client and their family.Keywords: Home care, Cognitive performance scale, Standardized assessment, interRAI

Background

The number of Canadians living with dementia will increase to roughly 937,000 by 2031 [1]. This estimate would be approximately 50% higher if mild cognitive impairment (CI), an early stage of memory loss, was included alongside dementia [1]. This speaks to an increased need for care to ensure that older Canadians, with CI, can maintain the best possible quality of life.

Dementia and other CIs tend to be under-diagnosed, with the diagnosis often coming relatively late in the disease trajectory [2]. Cognitive impairments are associated with difficulties completing activities of daily living (ADLs) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs) [3], increased rates of depression, and a greater likelihood of admission to a long-term care (LTC) home [4, 5]. Cognitive decline is defined as the progressive deterioration in cognitive ability and memory over time. Declines in cognitive functioning and performance can be detrimental to independence and quality of life [6]. Therefore, screening, and early detection, of changes in cognitive performance are essential to try and delay further deterioration.

Increasing age is the biggest non-modifiable risk factor for both dementia [7–9] and continued cognitive decline [7]. However, there are also a number of potentially modifiable risk factors. For example, age-related hearing loss is the top potentially modifiable risk factor for dementia [10–12]. Similarly, vision impairment is associated with an increased likelihood of experiencing poorer cognitive functioning [13] and decline [6, 14, 15]. When hearing and vision impairments are combined (i.e., dual sensory impairment or DSI), cognitive deterioration is further exacerbated compared to those with a single sensory impairment [16–18].

Alternatively, there are factors that are protective against cognitive decline. For example, social connections are an important aspect of successful ageing and provide mental stimulation through communication and interactions with others, which may help build cognitive reserve [6]. A lack of social engagement [19], and self-reported loneliness, are other potentially modifiable factors [8, 14, 15, 19]. Engagement in physical activity, formal exercise, and recreational activities also reduce the risk of Alzheimer’s dementia and have a favourable effect on short-term cognitive decline [20, 21].

In Canada, publicly-funded home care is intended to promote, maintain, or restore the health of individuals who require additional support in their homes [22]. Home care provides a variety of services to people of all ages in the home environment and other community settings (e.g., within assisted living facilities in some provinces). Key factors predicting the need for home care include older age, cognitive impairment, mobility issues and the presence of chronic health conditions [23]. These services can help support older Canadians to remain at home and age in place.

Across Canada, the Resident Assessment Instrument for Home Care (RAI-HC) is widely used among individuals who are expected to receive home care for at least 60 days. The RAI-HC is a standardized, clinical assessment created by interRAI, a non-profit network of researchers, clinicians, and policy makers in 37 countries. The RAI-HC is used to evaluate the overall health and functioning of home care clients to help guide care planning [24] and is completed on admission into the home care program, and then typically every 6–12 months, or following an important change in health status [25].

In this study, cognitive functioning was examined using the Cognitive Performance Scale (CPS) [26]. The CPS is embedded within the RAI-HC and is automatically generated upon completion of the assessment. Given that the RAI-HC is used widely in Canada, and in 20 other countries, understanding the factors driving changes in a Canadian home care population could be beneficial to clients in many other countries. The goal of the study was to identify the most influential risk factors associated with experiencing a decline on the CPS score among Canadian home care clients aged 65 years or older.

Method

Data source

This retrospective cohort study utilized secondary de-identified data collected using the RAI-HC, which is completed fully across Ontario, Newfoundland and Labrador, and Yukon Territory, with partial coverage for British Columbia (excluding Northern Health) and Alberta (excluding Calgary Zone) [27]. It is completed electronically by a trained health care professional (usually a registered nurse) in the person’s home and takes into account observations of the client and evidence gathered from discussions with the individual and their informal caregivers [24]. Assessors are trained in the administration of the RAI-HC and the use of its embedded clinical scales and algorithms. The RAI-HC has roughly 300 items capturing information on things such as demographic characteristics, cognitive functioning, sensory impairments, social engagement, physical functioning, and clinical diagnoses. Assessors are required to complete all items on the RAI-HC so missing data are virtually non-existent.

This research project followed the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [28] and was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Board at Wilfrid Laurier University (REB #6946).

Sample

The study sample included home care clients, aged 65+, who had a baseline score of zero on the CPS (i.e., intact cognitive performance) and had at least two consecutive assessments completed within a twelve-month period, between January 4, 2001, and March 9, 2020 (n = 146,187). If a client had more than two assessments, within the twelve-month period, the first two assessments were kept for analysis. The first RAI-HC assessment was defined as their baseline assessment. The second assessment for the majority of individuals in this cohort (70.1%) was a regular follow-up assessment, or a routine assessment at a fixed interval. Although a longer timeframe was considered, nearly 30% of clients were lost to follow-up (e.g., due to dying, moving to a nursing home/other setting) so the one-year period was chosen to maximize sample size.

The CPS is one of the health index scales that is embedded within the RAI-HC, which is automatically generated upon completion of the assessment. The CPS is a hierarchical scale based on 4 items, including expressive communication ability, cognitive skills for daily decision-making, the ability to eat independently, and short-term memory. The scale ranges from a score of zero (intact) to six (severe impairment). As shown in Fig. 1, the decision-making ability of the individual is the first step in determining their CPS score. If their decision-making ability is not severely impaired (i.e., the person can still make some/all decisions), then the algorithm follows along the top of the diagram. An overall impairment count is calculated (scored from 0 to 3) based on their level of impairment in decision-making, ability to be understood by others, and their short-term memory. If there are no impairments or only a single impairment in one of the above measures, the person would be scored a zero or one on the CPS, respectively. Once the impairment count reaches two or three, the person is considered to have more severe impairment and will be scored a two, three, or four on the CPS based on the number and severity of the impairments the ability to make decisions and be understood. Finally, if the person is severely impaired in their decision-making abilities, the bottom section of the algorithm is followed. A CPS score of five would be assigned if the person is not totally dependent on others in their eating performance, while a score of six would mean that the person was totally dependent on others in their eating performance.

Scoring of the cognitive performance scales (CPS)

Each one-point difference on the CPS represents a clinically significant worsening in cognitive performance. The CPS is a functional measure and meant to act as a brief screener. Roughly 20% of Canadian home care clients experience a worsening on the CPS in one year [18]. There is evidence supporting the criterion validity of the CPS, as it is correlated with both the Mini-Mental State Examination [29–34] and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment [31]. The CPS has been found to have a high degree of sensitivity and specificity [33]. In addition, higher scores on the CPS have been found to be associated with a clinical diagnosis of dementia [31, 35].

The main outcome of interest was experiencing any deterioration (i.e., any increase) on the CPS score within one year (i.e., a dichotomous variable). Any increase on the CPS between the first and second assessment, was considered a worsening in cognitive performance. We chose this as our outcome since 92.9% of clients who experienced a worsening on the CPS score showed a one-point or two-point increase in their score. As such, we did not attempt to separate clients into unique groups based on the degree of change on the CPS.

Measures

There are a number of other health index scales embedded within the RAI-HC that are automatically generated once the assessment is completed. Higher scores across all scales indicate greater impairment in performance.

The Activities of Daily Living (ADL) Self-Performance Hierarchy Scale assesses functional ability in personal hygiene, toilet use, locomotion, and eating. It ranges from zero (independent) to six (total dependence on others), where a cut-point of two or greater represented meaningful ADL impairment. Existing evidence supports the criterion validity of the ADL Self-Performance Hierarchy Scale when compared against the Barthel Index [30].

The Deafblind Severity Index (DbSI) uses the two items that measure hearing and vision, and it ranges from zero (no impairment in either sense) to five (severe impairment in both senses) [36]. A cut-point of three or greater was used to identify those with at least mild impairment in both hearing and vision (i.e., dual sensory impairment; DSI). There is some evidence supporting the concurrent validity of the DbSI, as increasing scores correlate with a greater difficulty preforming IADLs and interacting with others [36].

The Caregiver Risk Evaluation (CaRE) algorithm is used to evaluate the risk of a caregiver experiencing burden. It categorizes caregivers into one of four groups, namely, low, moderate, high and very high risk of experiencing caregiver burden. Other items on the RAI-HC

Several additional items on the RAI-HC were also analyzed as potential risk factors. These included demographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex (recorded as male or female), primary language, marital status, and level of education), and disease diagnoses (e.g., Alzheimer’s dementia or other dementias, stroke, and diabetes), communication ability (e.g., verbal expression and comprehension) and social functioning (e.g., social involvement, change in social activities, and social isolation). Finally, we also explored items around services utilization (e.g., home health aides and occupational therapy) and other health-related factors (e.g., smoking, and physical activity).

Analysis

Individuals who experienced a decline in cognitive functioning were compared to those who did not across a number of potential risk factors. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. Age was assessed as an ordinal variable (65–74 years old, 75–84, 85–94, 95–104 and 105+). In the current analysis, a two-tailed alpha level of 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance. However, due to the large sample size and concern about type 1 error, we also generated odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) to assess clinical significance. A covariate was considered to be clinically meaningful if its OR represented at least a 20% change (i.e., OR of ≤ 0.83 or an OR ≥ 1.20).

Covariates that were statistically significant, clinically meaningful, or important in the literature were considered for inclusion in the logistic model. Multiple regression techniques (e.g., backward elimination, forward selection, stepwise procedure, best subsets) were used to identify the strongest predictors to be included in the final model. Multicollinearity was assessed using polychoric correlations for ordinal variables and tetrachoric correlations for dichotomous variables. A cut-point of 0.40 or higher was used to identify variables that needed to be removed from the model [41]. Verbal comprehension was found to be collinear with hearing impairment (correlation = 0.57) and was excluded from the model.

Potential confounding relationships were explored through stratified analyses using the Cochran Mantel-Haenszel chi-square test and the Breslow Day test. First, we looked at potential confounders for the relationship between a diagnosis of Alzheimer’s dementia/other type of dementia and experiencing a decline on the CPS. Both delirium and having any psychiatric diagnosis were considered as potential confounders. Secondly, we examined potential confounders for the relationship between use of a health aide and experiencing the outcome. These potential confounders included marital status, physical activity, the CaRE algorithm and use of occupational therapy. Finally, we explored all two-way interactions among the covariates there were part of the final main effects model. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software version 9.4 [42].

Results

At the time of their second assessment, 25.2% (n = 36,819) of clients experienced a worsening on the CPS, and the most likely change, was from a zero to a one (13.2%; Table 1). The mean time between assessments was 6.9 months (standard deviation = 3.3). In the univariate analyses, individuals 75–84 years of age were 1.34 times more likely to experience a decline on the CPS compared to individuals 65–74 years of age (OR: 1.34; CI: 1.30, 1.39). Several other factors significantly increased the risk including: a diagnosis of Alzheimer’s dementia/other type of dementia, any psychiatric diagnosis, Parkinson’s disease, hearing impairment, dual sensory impairment, and communication challenges. If a client had a caregiver at high risk of experiencing caregiver burden, they were 1.42 times more likely to have a decline on the CPS. In addition, clients who were less physically active were more likely to experience a deterioration on the CPS. In contrast, females were roughly 22% less likely to experience the outcome (Table 2).

Multivariate model

Based on our analysis, there were several instances of potential confounders. In four cases, we forced these variables into the final model (CaRE, marital status, any psychiatric diagnosis, level of physical activity) and then assessed for multicollinearity before creating the final model with 10 variables (Table 3). In the final main effects model, older age was the most influential risk factor for experiencing a decline on the CPS, and the odds increased across each of the 10-year age groups. Other factors that stand out in this model include clinical indicators of delirium (OR: 1.92; CI: 1.55, 2.39), a diagnosis of Alzheimer’s dementia/other dementia (OR: 4.59; CI: 4.15, 5.07), a caregiver at high risk of experiencing burden based on the CaRE algorithm (OR: 1.45; CI: 1.40, 1.50), and participating in less than two hours of physical activity in the last 3 days (OR: 1.31; CI: 1.28, 1.34).

The interaction between the CaRE algorithm and receipt of a home health aide was the only interaction that was both statistically and clinically relevant. We have opted to report only the main effects model since inclusion of the interaction did not affect the ORs. Receipt of home health aide services appeared to reduce the risk of worsening on the CPS. For example, among clients with a caregiver at high risk of burden, those who received this type of support had an OR of 1.04, as compared to an OR of 1.33 among those who did not receive this service.

Discussion

In this large retrospective cohort, 25% of Canadian home care clients experienced some level of deterioration on the CPS, across an average of seven months. Since dementia and mild CI are on the rise in most developed countries, it is vital for clinicians to recognize the factors that change the risk of a deterioration in cognitive performance since even mild changes may be markers of future dementia [43, 44]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the most influential risk factors for deterioration on the CPS score in this particular population. The best predictors included a combination of factors unique to the individual (e.g., age, sex, marital status), factors related to the caregiver, and utilization of home health aides. This large cohort study utilized data from six Canadian regions, resulting in findings that may be applicable to other publicly-funded home care systems.

While the influence of age and sex are important and supported in the literature [7, 8, 45] a unique result was the influence of caregiver burden. The relationship between CI and subsequent caregiver burden has been previously explored [46–48]. However, what is unique in our research is the finding that clients who had a caregiver at high risk of caregiver burden were nearly 50% more likely to experience a decline on the CPS, compared to those with a caregiver at low risk, after adjusting for all the other factors in the model. Understanding the exact mechanism is somewhat difficult with this design. It may be explained by the fact that the CaRE algorithm is correlated with the client’s level of impairment in completing ADLs and IADLs independently [40] which would contribute to caregiver burden and also to the risk of decline on the CPS.

We also saw that there was an interaction between the receipt of home health aides and the CaRE algorithm, most notably among those with caregivers in the high risk group. In other research, caregiver distress has been shown to be important in differentiating the receipt of home health aides/homemaking services among Ontario home care clients. In fact, caregiver distress was important in the three groups with the highest need for these services [49]. While the current project cannot explore the causal mechanisms at play, the risk of caregiver burden is potentially modifiable, making it an important factor for home care clinicians to consider as they develop a care plan.

We found that a diagnosis of Alzheimer’s dementia, or a related dementia (ADRD) was highly influential increasing the risk for experiencing a worsening on the CPS, with an adjusted OR of nearly five. Including such a covariate warranted careful consideration given that individuals in the cohort all had a baseline CPS score of zero. At first glance, it would be expected that none of the individuals in our cohort should have such a diagnosis. We chose to keep it in our model since it has been shown to be an important predictor in previous research in home care [50] and since the presence of ADRD did not completely explain the outcome. For example, nearly 40% of individuals with ADRD did not experience a decline on the CPS, indicating some variability in the outcome among those with these types of neurodegenerative conditions.

Two recent reports from the Lancet Commission suggest that there are multiple potentially modifiable risk factors for the onset of CI or dementia [51, 52]. Livingston et al. suggest that modifying these 12 risk factors might prevent or delay up to 40% of dementias [51]. In our analysis, we considered five of these factors, namely, those that appear to influence the risk of CI in later life and were available in our dataset. We had no information on air pollution. Of these five, symptoms of depression and physical inactivity were significant on their own, and only physical inactivity remained a significant risk factor in our final model. The amount and intensity of physical activity can have a positive impact on cognitive health and aging. Even low-intensity physical activity has beneficial effects on memory function [21, 53, 54]. Physical activity may help to maintain cognitive functioning and has the potential to delay further declines [51, 55, 56].

Hearing impairment was kept in the final model given its importance based on the literature [57–59]. In addition, hearing impairments are highly prevalent among older adults [60] are often under-detected and untreated [61] and are a known risk factor for cognitive decline [62]. Although it was not the strongest risk factor in our model, based solely on the odds ratio, it was kept given its importance in the literature, the increasing prevalence among older persons, and its potential modifiability.

The current study used secondary data, which limited the analysis to exploring only those potential risk factors collected using the RAI-HC. For example, the assessment included no information about biologic markers such as imaging data and serologic findings, which can be important in determining the risk of CI or dementia [63–65]. Another potential limitation is selective attrition. The study cohort included clients who had two assessments completed within twelve months. After the first assessment, a client may have passed away or been admitted into a LTC home, and thus removed from the cohort. CI is a known risk factor for admission to LTC facilities [43, 44] and as a result, this study likely under-reports the prevalence of experiencing a deterioration in cognitive performance. Selection bias could additionally be a potential limiting factor of this study, since 8.8% of individuals were reassessed due to a change in status. For some, observed changes in cognitive functioning could have prompted the reassessment. However, roughly 70% of individuals in the cohort were re-evaluated as a part of routine planned reassessment, therefore the potential influence of this bias on the results of the study is minimal.

Our results suggest that issues such as difficulty with hearing, caregiver burden, and lack of physical activity are important and potentially modifiable risk factors. If addressed by the home care team, there is the possibility for home care recipients to function more independently, and for their caregivers to be able to continue in that vital role. Ultimately, this could prevent or delay the person’s need to move into LTC, a worthwhile goal for any publicly-funded home care program.
