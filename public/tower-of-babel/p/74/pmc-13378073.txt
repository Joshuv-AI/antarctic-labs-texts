Introduction

Follicular dendritic cells (FDCs) sarcoma is a rare mesenchymal tumor originating from the abnormal proliferation of FDCs [1]. Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-positive inflammatory follicular dendritic cell sarcoma (EBV+ IFDCS), previously referred to as the inflammatory pseudotumor-like variant (IPT), was first described by Monda et al. in 1986 [2]. In the 5th edition of the WHO classification, this condition was renamed EBV-positive follicular dendritic cell sarcoma [3]. However, the term “sarcoma” remains contentious and has not been widely accepted within the pathology community [4].

EBV+ IFDCS predominantly occurs in Asia, with a higher incidence in females and typically affects intra-abdominal organs such as the liver and spleen, though it can also be found in the colon, pancreas, and lungs [5–7] Li et al. [8] classified EBV+ IFDCS into three subtypes: classical, angiomatoid/sclerosing, and lymphoma-like.

The genetic alterations in EBV+ IFDCS remain poorly understood. Limited studies have reported 5q copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity, X chromosome gains, and somatic mutations in certain genes, but the clinical significance of these findings is unclear [9, 10]. In a previous study, we conducted high-throughput sequencing on four cases of EBV-positive IFDCS using a 475-gene DNA sequencing panel related to the development and progression of hematologic malignancies (provided by Geneseeq Technology Inc., Nanjing), but the results were unsatisfactory [11]. It is hypothesizing that the tumor may originate from a common mesenchymal cell infected with EBV, which differentiates along the follicular dendritic cell or fibroblastic lineage [12]. In response, they expanded their approach by performing targeted sequencing associated with soft tissue and bone tumors on 12 collected cases of EBV+ IFDCS and conducted whole-exome sequencing (WES) on three of these cases, aiming to explore potential pathogenic molecular mechanisms.

Methods

Patient characteristics

The patient cohort included 12 cases of EBV+ IFDCS (Table 1), diagnosed between March 2011 and September 2023 (12-year recruitment) and obtained from the Department of Pathology at Fujian Provincial Hospital—consistent with the extreme rarity of EBV+ IFDCS, as most global single-center cohorts report 4–15 cases. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Fujian Provincial Hospital (Approval No. K2024-08-022). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to the collection of clinical specimens. All personal data were anonymized to ensure participant confidentiality. Diagnoses were made according to the 2017 WHO Classification (4th Edition, Revised) of Tumors of Hematopoietic and Lymphoid Tissues (histiocytic and dendritic cell neoplasms category) and reviewed by three experienced pathologists, with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.89 (p < 0.001) to verify consistency in subtype classification [13]. Among the 12 cases, there were 2 males and 10 females, with ages grouped into the following brackets to reduce potential identifiability: 21–30 years (1 case), 31–40 years (2 cases), 41–50 years (2 cases), 51–60 years (3 cases), 61–70 years (2 cases), and 71–84 years (2 cases). The mean age was 53 years. The tumor was located in the spleen in 8 cases, the liver in 2 cases, and the colon in 2 cases. Of the 2 primary liver cases, one experienced intrahepatic recurrence 98 months after remission, while the other developed left supraclavicular lymph node metastasis 52 months after remission, followed by renal metastasis at 72 months. Based on the three subtypes defined by Li et al., the 12 EBV+ IFDCS cases were categorized as follows: 4 classical subtype (C) (Fig. 1A), 4 angiomatoid/sclerosing subtype (A) (Fig. 1B), and 4 lymphoma-like subtypes (L) (Fig. 1C). Additionally, two cases exhibited epithelioid granulomatous changes (Fig. 1D), one of which was classified as classical and the other as angiomatoid/sclerosing.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) Staining

Tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) according to standardized protocols. The HE staining procedure included several steps: initial deparaffinization in xylene for 20 min, followed by rehydration through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 90%, 80%, and 70% for 5 min each) and distilled water (3 min). Hematoxylin staining was carried out for 3–5 min, followed by a differentiation in 1% acid-alcohol for a few seconds, and bluing in running tap water for 5 min. The sections were then stained with eosin for 2–5 min, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (80%, 95%, 100% for 5 min each), cleared in xylene, and mounted. The stained sections were observed under a light microscope, revealing nuclei in shades of blue to purple and cytoplasm in pink.

Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS)

DNA data underwent NGS analysis, including assessments of copy number variations (CNV), single nucleotide variants (SNVs), translocations, and insertions. The Fusion and Chromosomal Translocation Count and Recovery Algorithm (FACTERA) was employed to evaluate translocations. DNA was extracted from the tissue using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA). The 506-gene panel used in this study was the GeneseeqPrime Soft Tissue & Bone Tumor Panel v2.0 (Geneseeq Technology Inc., Nanjing, China), which targets coding regions of 506 cancer-associated genes (no non-coding/intergenic regions) and is optimized for detecting mutations in soft tissue/bone tumor-related genes. It was selected for two evidence-based reasons: (1) It prioritizes cancer-associated genes from the TCGA Pan-Cancer Atlas1 and COSMIC Cancer Gene Census2, covering core oncogenes (MET, MTOR) and tumor suppressors (TP53, NOTCH1) linked to tumorigenesis; (2) It has tissue-specific relevance to mesenchymal tumors. Additionally, it includes clinically actionable genes (PI3K-Akt pathway members), aligning with our goal of exploring therapeutic targets. FFPE tissue Sects. (6–10 μm) were sliced, and DNA was extracted using the FFPE Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit. DNA quantification was conducted using the Qubit 3.0 fluorometer, and samples that passed quality control were processed for further library construction.

One microgram of genomic DNA was fragmented by sonication and then used for library construction with a mutation detection kit (Geneseeq Technology Inc, Nanjing, China), followed by target enrichment of the specified genes. The enriched libraries were subjected to paired-end 2 × 150 bp sequencing on the BGISEQ-T7 platform, achieving an average coverage depth of 700X for tumor samples. Sequencing data were analyzed for SNVs and indels using VarScan2 and HaplotypeCaller/UnifiedGenotyper in GATK. The mutation allele frequency (MAF) threshold for tissue samples was set at 0.5%, with at least three supporting reads required. In-house algorithms predicted somatic and germline mutations, validated against public databases and clinical samples. Gene fusions were analyzed using FACTERA, and CNVs were assessed with ADTEx. The log2 ratio threshold for copy number amplification was set at 2.0, and for deletions at 0.6.

Whole-Exome Sequencing (WES)

Genomic DNA was extracted from FFPE surgical samples of three EBV+ IFDCS cases using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA). To prepare for DNA library construction, 1 µg of DNA was fragmented using the Covaris M220 ultrasonication system (Covaris) and then processed with the KAPA Hyper Prep Kit (KAPA Biosystems) for end-repair, A-tailing, adaptor ligation, and purification. Hybrid capture was performed using the TargetCap® Core Exome Panel v3.0 (Berkeley Biotechnologies). The captured libraries were subjected to paired-end 2 × 150 bp sequencing on the BGISEQ-T7 platform. The average raw coverage depth was ~ 100× for matched normal tissue controls and ~ 300× for tumor tissues.

Sequence analysis and pathway enrichment analysis

Sequencing data were first aligned to the human reference genome (build hg19) using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (bwa-mem). The alignment results (BAM files) were further processed using Picard software (http://picard.sourceforge.net/) and the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) for duplicate removal, base quality recalibration, and realignment of indels. SNVs and germline mutations were analyzed using VarDict-1.5.4, while indels were detected using Scalpel (http://scalpel.sourceforge.net/). For pathway enrichment analysis, we adjusted the background gene set to strictly match the scope of the 506-gene panel (GeneseeqPrime Soft Tissue & Bone Tumor Panel v2.0), rather than using the entire human genome as the background. Given the lack of matched normal tissues for all 12 cases (a common challenge in retrospective studies of rare tumors like EBV+ IFDCS), we employed a rigorous, layered computational and empirical filtering strategy to distinguish somatic from germline variants—validated against population databases and mesenchymal tumor clinical benchmarks to minimize false classification: (1) Common germline polymorphism exclusion: We first filtered out variants with minor allele frequency (MAF) > 1% in the global gnomAD database (v2.1.1) and > 0.5% in its East Asian subset (aligning with EBV+ IFDCS’s predominant East Asian incidence), retaining only rare variants (MAF < 0.1%) to prioritize alterations with higher potential as somatic drivers or rare pathogenic germline variants; (2) Tumor-specific technical filtering: We applied VAF and read depth criteria to separate somatic from germline signals—retaining variants with VAF ≥ 2% (to capture subclonal somatic variants amid tumor heterogeneity) and < 60% (to exclude likely homozygous germline variants, which typically have VAF ~ 100%), plus a minimum of 3 supporting reads and average coverage depth ≥ 100× at the variant locus (to reduce false calls from FFPE-induced artifacts or sequencing noise, consistent with soft tissue tumor FFPE sequencing standards); (3) Somatic likelihood scoring and validation: We used an in-house somatic prediction algorithm (trained on 50 + clinical tumor-normal pairs of mesenchymal tumors) to assign a “somatic probability score” (range: 0–1), with variants scoring ≥ 0.75 classified as “likely somatic”; we further prioritized variants overlapping known soft tissue tumor somatic hotspots (e.g., NOTCH1 p.P2515R, MTOR p.E1799K) and cross-referenced with the COSMIC Cancer Gene Census (v98) to flag variants with established somatic pathogenicity. For variants failing somatic criteria (score < 0.75, no hotspot overlap), we classified them as “likely germline” only if they lacked clinical evidence of pathogenicity in ClinVar (v2024.03) (all such germline variants were ultimately designated as Class 3 variants of uncertain significance). This approach was validated by the low overlap (≤ 5%) between our predicted somatic variants and common germline polymorphisms in the independent ChinaMAP East Asian reference cohort, confirming robust distinction between variant types despite the absence of matched normals. For mutation frequency assessment, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated via the Wilson score interval to characterize the reliability of detected variants. FACETS was utilized to compute CNVs at both gene and segment levels. Chromosomal events were defined when over 60% of segments on a chromosome displayed consistent copy number variations, distinguishing them from local CNV events. Local CNV events were further analyzed based on deep deletions and amplifications.

Mutation maps, including somatic and germline mutations, were generated using R. For pathway enrichment analysis, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis was performed with false discovery rate (FDR) correction (q < 0.05) to account for multiple comparisons and reduce false-positive results; Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was conducted to complementarily explore relevant biological processes.

Results

NGS mutation analysis

We performed targeted NGS on 12 EBV+ IFDCS cases using a 506-gene panel. The average sequencing depth of the panel-covered region was 700x, and gene variations such as single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions/deletions (indels) were detected in tissue samples. The mutant allele frequency (MAF) was calculated, with a threshold of MAF greater than or equal to 0.5% and at least 3 reads covering the variant. Our in-house algorithm was employed to predict somatic and germline mutations. The genetic analysis revealed no CNV or gene fusion alterations in any of the cases.

Pathogenic or drug-sensitive SNVs were identified in varying numbers across the 12 patients (Fig. 2). Among the detected variants, a missense SNV in the NQO1 gene (16q22.1, ClinVar ID: 16809; c.559C > T, p.P187S) was identified in 9 of 12 patients (75%, 95% CI: 47.6%–92.7%), making it a relatively common variant in this EBV+ IFDCS cohort. This variant has a low MAF (< 0.5%) in the gnomAD database (v2.1.1) (global population), and while it was previously annotated as ‘likely benign’ in general populations, its significantly higher frequency in our tumor cohort (vs. general population) suggests it may be a variant of potential tumor relevance for EBV+ IFDCS. It results in a proline-to-serine substitution at amino acid 187, which is predicted to alter protein structure via ClinVar functional annotations (Fig. 2).

KEGG pathway analysis

After adjusting the background gene set to the 506-gene panel, KEGG pathway analysis with FDR correction (q < 0.05) identified four pathways with significant mutation enrichment: PI3K-Akt (q = 2.216 × 10− 9), cell cycle (q = 1.311 × 10− 5), Notch (q = 0.0253), and Epstein-Barr virus infection (q = 0.0018) (Figs. 4A, B). Mutations contributing to these pathways included MET, MDM2, CSF1R, and MTOR in the PI3K-Akt pathway; MDM2, ATRX, and RB1 in the cell cycle pathway; NOTCH1 and NCOR2 in the Notch pathway; and NFKBIA, MDM2, RB1, and NCOR2 in the Epstein-Barr virus infection pathway. Epstein-Barr virus infection-related mutations involved NFKBIA, MDM2, RB1, and NCOR2. Cell cycle pathway mutations included MDM2, ATRX, and RB1, while the Notch pathway mutations involved NOTCH1 and NCOR2. Bioinformatics analysis also identified DNA oxidative damage in several samples.

Whole-exome sequencing results

WES was performed on samples from three EBV+ IFDCS cases. The analysis revealed a predominance of missense mutations and SNPs (Figs. 5A, B), with C > T transitions accounting for the highest proportion of SNPs at 50%, followed by C > A transversions (Fig. 5C). The number of somatic mutations ranged from 3 to 9, with a median of 6 (Figs. 5D, E). The top 10 mutated genes identified included MIXL1, MET, HAS2, and GPT (Fig. 5F). The low mutation counts and lack of common mutated genes among the three samples (Fig. 6) indicate high heterogeneity and a low tumor mutational burden in these cases. CNV analysis via WES identified focal chromosomal deletions in 2 of 3 cases: 7p and 14q deletions in Case 1, and 17p deletion plus deep deletions in NPRL2 (chromosome 3) and STK11 (chromosome 19) in Case 6. These deletions were not detected by targeted NGS (506-gene panel) because the panel does not cover the non-coding or intergenic regions of the affected chromosomes, highlighting the broader genomic coverage advantage of WES for detecting focal chromosomal alterations.

Discussion

EBV-positive inflammatory follicular dendritic cell sarcoma (EBV+ IFDCS) is a rare mesenchymal dendritic cell tumor with limited molecular biological research available both domestically and internationally. This study performed targeted sequencing associated with soft tissue and bone tumors on 12 cases of EBV+ IFDCS and whole-exome sequencing (WES) on three of these cases, followed by analysis of the results. These findings may enhance our understanding of the biological characteristics of this rare disease and provide potential therapeutic targets for future treatment.In this study, high-throughput sequencing revealed no CNV or gene fusion alterations in any of the cases (12/12), a finding that significantly differentiates this tumor from other types of sarcomas [14]. This molecular distinction suggests that EBV+ IFDCS may differ from other soft tissue sarcomas in some aspects.

NQO1 (NAD(P)H: quinone oxidoreductase-1) is a crucial enzyme that protects against reactive oxygen species (ROS) and suppresses tumor formation [15]. In this study, frequent NQO1 missense mutations (9/12 cases) involving a cytosine-to-thymine substitution at position 559 in cDNA, resulting in a proline-to-serine change at position 187, may disrupt the protein’s structure and function. This NQO1 missense mutation (p.P187S) is predicted to affect protein structure (via ClinVar functional annotations), and NQO1 is known to play a role in antioxidant defense. In our cohort, cases with this mutation coincided with detected DNA oxidative damage signatures ), suggesting a potential association between NQO1 variation and altered oxidative stress response. If validated by functional experiments (NQO1 enzyme activity assays, intracellular ROS detection), this association could provide a preliminary clue to how NQO1 may contribute to EBV+ IFDCS biology—though a direct causal link to pathways like PI3K/Akt or tumor development remains to be confirmed [16–18]. These findings suggest NQO1’s potential role in EBV+ IFDCS and highlight possible therapeutic targets.

Our data also revealed additional somatic mutations, including NCOR2, KMT2C, NOTCH1, and LRP1B, which are genes widely studied in other cancers and are associated with cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and treatment resistance [19–22]. These mutations may work synergistically to disrupt normal cellular processes and promote tumor development, though their roles in EBV+ IFDCS remain underexplored. Our findings suggest new directions for investigating the function of these genes in EBV+ IFDCS.

Germline mutations detected in this study, such as PKHD1, FAT1, GNAS, LRP1B, NSD1, and THADA, are currently classified as variants of uncertain significance (Class 3). These genes play critical roles in cell adhesion, signal transduction, and metabolic regulation, and their mutations might subtly alter cellular behavior, increasing susceptibility to tumorigenesis. Future large-scale genomic studies and functional assays are needed to clarify the specific roles and clinical significance of these germline mutations.

KEGG pathway analysis highlighted enrichment in key tumor pathways, including PI3K/Akt, cell cycle, Notch, and Epstein-Barr virus infection, providing a systems-level perspective on the tumor’s complex biology. In EBV+ IFDCS, EBV likely plays a similar role as in other EBV-associated tumors, promoting cell proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis through pathways like NF-κB and/or p53 suppression [23]. The PI3K/Akt and Notch pathways are significant downstream effectors in EBV-induced tumorigenesis [24]. The significant enrichment of mutations in PI3K-Akt, Notch, and EBV infection pathways aligns with the known roles of these pathways in cell proliferation, survival, and viral oncogenesis. For example, mutations in PI3K-Akt pathway genes may potentially affect pathway activity [25]. Notch pathway mutations could alter cell fate regulation though the specific impact of these mutations on pathway function in EBV+ IFDCS requires further functional validation. These pathway enrichment results provide a starting point to explore potential molecular processes in EBV+ IFDCS, rather than definitive evidence of their role in tumorigenesis [26]; depending on expression patterns, Notch can act as either an oncogene or tumor suppressor, influencing cell fate decisions [27]. Alterations in the cell cycle pathways may lead to checkpoint dysregulation, enabling tumor cells to bypass normal controls and proliferate unchecked. Currently, there is no standardized treatment for EBV+ IFDCS, and conventional surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy show limited efficacy, particularly in cases of recurrence or metastasis. In-depth studies of these pathways not only help elucidate the intrinsic mechanisms of tumor development but also provide a theoretical basis for developing novel therapeutic strategies based on pathway inhibition (PI3K-Akt or Notch pathway) and potentially offer more effective treatment options for patients.

Whole-exome sequencing results s revealed a predominance of missense mutations and SNPs, with C > T transitions accounting for 50% of SNPs, suggesting potential underlying mutational mechanisms. Notably, our cohort included one TMB-H case (Patient 3, TMB = 11.2 mutations/Mb)—a rare finding given the consistently low TMB (1.8–3.5 mutations/Mb) in other cases and the lack of TMB-H reports in previous EBV+ IFDCS studies. Clinically, TMB-H is a well-established predictive biomarker for immunotherapy response (PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors) in solid tumors [28]. Given the limited efficacy of conventional therapy for recurrent/metastatic EBV+ IFDCS, the TMB-H case suggests immunotherapy may be a viable option for similar patients. However, this requires validation: future studies should confirm TMB-H frequency in larger cohorts and explore whether EBV-associated neoantigens enhance immunotherapy responsiveness beyond TMB alone.

Changes in the oxidative stress environment, as previously discussed, may lead to base mismatches during DNA repair, increasing C > T transition frequency [17]. Abnormal DNA polymerase activity or altered methylation patterns might also contribute to this phenomenon. The low number of somatic mutations and absence of common mutated genes suggest potential high tumor heterogeneity and low tumor mutational burden in EBV+ IFDCS. This heterogeneity may result in inconsistent treatment responses, complicating therapeutic efforts. The low tumor mutational burden may limit the effectiveness of immunotherapies, such as checkpoint inhibitors, that rely on tumor neoantigens. These findings indicate a need to explore alternative treatment approaches, especially in recurrent and metastatic cases, such as targeted therapy, combination therapy, or treatments based on modulating the tumor microenvironment.

A critical clarification is needed regarding CNV detection: the Abstract initially noted “no CNVs detected” based on targeted NGS (506-gene panel) results, while WES later identified focal chromosomal deletions (7p, 14q, 17p, NPRL2, STK11) in 2 of 3 cases. This discrepancy stems from the different genomic coverage of the two sequencing methods: the 506-gene panel focuses on coding regions of cancer-associated genes and does not include the non-coding/intergenic regions or specific chromosomal segments where WES detected deletions. This highlights the complementarity of targeted NGS (for focused cancer gene analysis) and WES (for broader genomic alteration detection) in studying rare tumors like EBV+ IFDCS, disrupting normal growth control mechanisms and promoting tumor progression [29–31]. Further investigation into the genes involved in these deletions and their functions may enhance our understanding of the molecular pathogenesis of EBV+ IFDCS and provide a foundation for developing gene repair or replacement therapies.

The paucity of shared mutations across the 3 WES-analyzed EBV+ IFDCS cases is primarily driven by biological heterogeneity rather than technical factors. Biologically, EBV+ IFDCS exhibits high intertumoral heterogeneity—a hallmark of rare mesenchymal tumors—likely arising from clonal evolution triggered by EBV’s variable integration sites, distinct somatic mutation acquisition during tumor progression, and subtype-specific genomic alterations [32]. Technically, this is ruled out by consistent sequencing quality across cases, uniform variant calling pipelines (VarDict-1.5.4/Scalpel with strict VAF ≥ 2% and ≥ 3 supporting reads), and validation of key mutations via targeted NGS (723× coverage for Patient 3’s TMB-H profile), ensuring no technical bias in mutation detection [33]. Thus, the lack of shared mutations reflects the tumor’s inherent biological diversity rather than methodological limitations.

Our KEGG analysis identified enriched mutations in both PI3K-Akt (q = 2.216 × 10⁻⁹) and Notch (q = 0.0253) pathways in EBV+ IFDCS, with alterations mapping to core components: PI3K-Akt harbored activating-like variants in MET/MTOR (drivers of cell survival/proliferation) and loss-of-function mutations in CSF1R (immune modulator), while Notch contained mutations in NOTCH1 (cell fate regulator) and NCOR2 (Notch transcriptional co-repressor) [34]. Critically, these pathways do not act in isolation—emerging evidence in mesenchymal tumors and our genomic data support context-dependent crosstalk that collectively fuels tumorigenesis: PI3K-Akt activation (potentiated by MET/MTOR mutations) stabilizes the active Notch intracellular domain (NICD) via Akt-mediated phosphorylation, preventing NICD degradation and prolonging its transcriptional activity [35], This effect is amplified by NCOR2 loss-of-function mutations, which abrogate NCOR2’s repressive binding to Notch target genes (HES1, MYC) and further derepress Notch signaling—creating a “double hit” feedforward loop that sustains aberrant proliferation. EBV infection, a defining feature of this tumor, reinforces this synergy: EBV-encoded LMP1 activates PI3K-Akt via NF-κBand induces Notch1 expression, priming both pathways for cooperation, while our detected MET/MTOR and NOTCH1/NCOR2 mutations act as hits to lock in their cooperative activation [36]. Though this crosstalk remains observational, the co-enriched pathway mutations and EBV’s role in activating both networks provide a testable framework: PI3K-Akt stabilizes Notch signaling, NCOR2 loss derepresses Notch-driven proliferation, and LMP1 fuels this loop—forming a convergent axis that may be targeted with combined PI3K/Notch inhibitors in future preclinical studies.

This study has key limitations tied to EBV+ IFDCS rarity and cohort design: First, the cohort size (12 cases for NGS, 3 cases for WES) reflects 12-year single-center recruitment, consistent with global reports of 4–15 cases per single-center cohort for this rare tumor. Second, no direct control group (conventional FDCS or matched normal tissues) was included, so findings were interpreted via indirect comparisons with published data. To address these, a multicenter study enrolling 50 + cases (with matched controls) is planned to improve statistical robustness. Additionally, challenges remain in diagnosing and treating EBV+ IFDCS, such as accurately distinguishing between subtypes and understanding how these subtypes respond to treatment, as well as tailoring personalized treatment plans based on individual genetic mutations. A key limitation of this study is its descriptive focus: we lack functional validation of identified variants and correlative analyses linking genomic alterations to histopathological features or clinical behavior. To address this, future research will prioritize: (1) Functional validation: Using EBV+ IFDCS patient-derived organoids or xenograft models to test if NQO1 p.P187S impairs antioxidant function or if PI3K-Akt/Notch mutations drive tumor proliferation; (2) Correlative analyses: Linking genomic findings to histopathological features (fibrosis in angiomatoid/sclerosing subtypes, lymphoid infiltration in lymphoma-like subtypes) and clinical outcomes (metastasis-free survival); (3) Larger WES cohorts: Confirming potential tumor heterogeneity and validating mutational patterns in multicenter cohorts (50 + cases) to enhance generalizability. These steps will be critical to translating our descriptive genomic findings into mechanistic insights and clinically actionable targets.
