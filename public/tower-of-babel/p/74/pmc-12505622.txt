Elevated maternal serum aminotransferase in early pregnancy associated with early-onset preeclampsia: A cohort study of 116,967 pregnancies in Shanghai, China
Authors: Shutian Zhang, Jinwen Zhang, Yulai Zhou, Jing Zhu, Juan Li, Yunyun Cao, Li Li, Yanlin Wang, Weibin Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInductionPreeclampsia (PE) is a severe pregnancy-specific complications along with multiple organ impairment. However, whether women with elevated serum aminotransferase levels in early pregnancy are susceptible to PE, and even early-onset PE remains unclear.Materials and methodsA hospital-based retrospective cohort study involving 116,967 pregnant women delivered between 2014 and 2022 in Shanghai, China was conducted. The study employed restricted cubic splines and logistic regression analysis to investigate the associations between early maternal liver function tests, specifically alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and the ALT/AST ratio, and the risk of PE and its subtypes, including early-onset preeclampsia (occurred at < 34 weeks of gestation) and late-onset preeclampsia (occurred at ≥ 34 weeks of gestation).ResultsElevated ALT levels (> 40 IU/L) in early pregnancy were associated with an increased risk of PE (adjusted odds ratio (aOR) = 1.57 [95% confidence interval (CI): 1.38–1.80]), early-onset PE (aOR = 1.78 [95% CI: 1.27–2.48]) and late-onset PE (aOR = 1.53 [95% CI: 1.33–1.76]). Increased risks of PE and early-onset PE were also observed in pregnant women with AST levels > 40 IU/L (aOR = 1.63 [95% CI: 1.33–1.99] for PE; aOR = 1.81 [95% CI: 1.09–3.00] for early-onset PE ) or ALT/AST ratio > 1 (aOR = 1.36 [95% CI: 1.25–1.47] for PE; aOR = 1.66 [95% CI: 1.34–2.06] for early-onset PE). Furthermore, women with abnormal ALT or AST levels in early pregnancy but relieved in later pregnancy periods still had elevated risks of PE and early-onset PE. The stratified analysis demonstrated that women with advanced age complicated with high ALT have increased risk of developing early-onset PE.ConclusionsElevated levels of ALT, AST and their ratios in early pregnancy were associated with an increased risk of PE, especially early-onset PE. Therefore, screening for aminotransferase levels in early pregnancy could be considered for identification of women at high risk of developing liver dysfunction-related PE, or even early-onset PE.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08107-9.Keywords: Liver dysfunction, Alanine aminotransferase, Early pregnancy, Preeclampsia, Early-onset preeclampsia

Background

Preeclampsia (PE) is a severe clinical syndrome affecting 3–5% of pregnant women worldwide and contributes prominently to maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality [1–3]. Although the etiology of PE is still incompletely understood, higher maternal age and body mass index (BMI) were well recognized as a key risk factor across studies [4–6]. In addition, women conceived by in vitro fertilization (IVF), particularly through frozen embryo transfer, was also linked to an increased risk of developing PE [7, 8]. Due to its clinical heterogeneity, PE was commonly classification into subtypes, including early-onset PE (occurs < 34 weeks of gestation) and late-onset PE (occurs ≥ 34 weeks of gestation) [9, 10]. Particularly, early-onset PE could cause worsened pregnancy outcomes compared with late-onset PE [10]. The disease represents a pregnancy-specific complications affecting multiorgan, including kidney, hematological system, brain, liver, etc [1, 2, 11]. Although PE can be accompanied with maternal liver dysfunction after its onset [2], whether elevated liver aminotransferase during early pregnancy was related to the development of PE remained elusive.​.

Screening of serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) are frequently conducted to evaluate liver diseases or injuries in both non-pregnant and pregnant populations, especially in hepatitis virus prevalent regions. However, the clinical significance of maternal abnormal aminotransferase levels in early pregnancy is still obscure. In particular, women with unexplained moderately elevated aminotransferase levels in early pregnancy are commonly considered as a physiological change. Recent studies indicated that abnormal liver biochemical markers during early pregnancy might be associated with the risk of PE or hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP), but did not reach a consistent conclusion [12–14]. A cohort study found that ALT > 30 IU/L in early pregnancy increased the risk of PE [12]. However, another study observed no significant associations observed between ALT and AST levels and the risk of HDP [14]. To date, there is still a lack of evidence showing the relationship between liver function in early pregnancy and the risks of early-onset and late-onset PE. Therefore, a large-scale cohort study is warranted to explain this association is warranted.

Thus, this study was conducted to clarify the relationship between abnormal maternal liver function tests in early pregnancy and the subsequent risk of early-onset and late-onset PE, using a large hospital-based birth cohort including 116,967 pregnant women in Shanghai, China.

Methods

Study participants

This cohort study was conducted at a tertiary maternity care and also a university-affiliated teaching hospital in Shanghai, China. A total of 124,819 pregnant women were recruited from the International Peace Maternity and Child Health Hospital (IPMCH) between January 2014 and December 2022. Data were collected through the hospital’s electronic medical record system, and were anonymous and de-identified.

Pregnant women with full antenatal visit and in hospital delivery records were included. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) conceived two or more fetuses; (2) abnormality in fetal chromosome; (3) fetal loss; (4) pre-existing diabetes, or chronic hypertension before pregnancy; (4) missing liver function test in early pregnancy. Finally, a total of 116,967 participants were included for the analysis in this study.

Data collection

Clinical data including age of pregnant women, maternal pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), parity, education status, ethnic group, methods of conception, pregnancy complications, and adverse perinatal outcomes, etc. were extracted from the medical record system and cleaned by data experts. Gestational age was obtained by considering both the last menstrual period and ultrasonography measurement of fetal size, if necessary. The data of demographic, obstetrical, and fetal characteristics were collected by gynecologists and nurses during routine antenatal medical visits and coded following the International Statistical Classification of Diseases (ICD-9 or ICD-10). The pre-pregnancy BMI was calculated using the pre-pregnant weight reported women herself and the height measured in early pregnancy at the first-visit.

Following local policy for management of pregnant women, liver function was routinely screened during both early and late pregnancies. Maternal fasting serum ALT and AST levels in early pregnancy (8–14 weeks of gestation) as well as in late pregnancy (28–41 weeks of gestation) were assayed in the hospital’s certified clinical laboratory. They were measured by an enzymatic colorimetric method using a Cobas c702 chemistry analyzer (Roche Diagnostics) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The intra-assay and inter-assay variation coefficients for ALT and AST measurements were less than 5%.

Exposure

ALT and AST concentrations > 40 IU/L, respectively, were considered as the cut-off for the elevated levels referring to the upper limit of normal (ULN) [15]. Moreover, ALT/AST ratio > 1 was defined as abnormal, according to the inflection point in the restricted cubic spline curves for exploring its relationship with outcomes.

Outcomes

PE was defined as persistently high blood pressure (reached or above 140/90 mmHg) complicated with proteinuria or dysfunction of other organs after ≥ 20 weeks of gestation [11, 16]. It can be classified into subtypes, including early-onset PE which occurred before 34 weeks of gestation, and late-onset PE which occurred after 34 weeks of gestation [9, 10]. Besides, gestational hypertension (GH) was diagnosed as new-onset hypertension after 20 weeks of gestation without proteinuria [16].

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables were presented as mean values (standard deviation) and frequencies (percentages), respectively. Unpaired t-test, Fisher’s exact test, and non-parametric test were used to compare the differences between groups, as applicable.

A non-linear relationship of maternal ALT and AST levels and ALT/AST ratio with the risk of PE was analyzed using the restricted cubic splines (knots = 3). Model assumptions were assessed by checking the residual plots and exclusion of outliers. Due to their non-normal distribution, ALT, AST levels and ALT/AST were ln-transformed in analysis and back-transformed when showing in plots. Maternal age (continuous), pre-pregnancy BMI (according to the Chinese recommended reference standard [17], underweight: <18.5, normal weight: 18.5–23.9, or overweight/obesity: ≥24 kg/m2), education status (high school and below, college, or above college), parity (nulliparity, or multiparity), medical insurance status (with, or without), race (Han, or non-Han), and in vitro fertilization (IVF; yes, or no) were adjusted. The missing values in covariates were imputed by the missforest method.

Furthermore, logistic regression estimated odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% CIs to study the associations of the elevated levels of ALT, AST, and ALT/AST ratio with PE. Multiple logistic regression was adjusted for confounders above. Three datasets were used: dataset I included all participants eligible; in dataset II, women with pre-existing liver diseases (i.e. gallbladder disease, fibrosis, liver cancer, hepatectomy, autoimmune hepatic disease, and steatohepatitis) were excluded from dataset I; In dataset III, women with hepatitis virus positivity and hyperemesis gravidarum in early pregnancy were further excluded from dataset II.

Based on the ULN for ALT and AST levels (40 IU/L), the data was stratified into categories of ≤ 0.5 ULN, 0.5–1 ULN, 1–1.5 ULN, and > 1.5 ULN. p for trend was utilized to examine the potential dose-response relationship between ALT and AST levels and the risk of developing PE and its subtypes.

To explore the modifying factors of the association between elevated aminotransferase levels and PE and its subtypes, we stratified women according to age categories (≥ 35 years old, or < 35 years old), BMI categories (< 18.5, 18.5–23.9, and ≥ 24 kg/m2), and IVF (yes, or not). Forest plot was showed to visualize the association.

All statistical analyses were performed using the R statistical software (R Project for Statistical Computing, version 4.1.3; packages rms, visreg, and tidyverse), and p values < 0.05 at two sides was considered as a statistical significance.

Results

Population characteristics

The study population included in the final analysis was composed of 116,967 pregnant women (Fig. 1). Among them, 2.7% were diagnosed with PE, including 0.4% early-onset PE and 2.3% late-onset PE. The comparisons of baseline characteristics and clinical features among the non-PE, early-onset PE and late-onset PE groups were listed in Table 1. Compared to non-PE women, patients with early-onset PE or late-onset PE were more likely to be older, overweight or obese (OWO), nulliparous, less educated and had a higher ratio of IVF treatment. Furthermore, the incidence of elevated ALT levels was higher in patients with early-onset PE or late-onset PE than those without PE during early pregnancy (9.4% vs. 8.3% vs. 4.4%; p < 0.001). Similarly, women complicated with early-onset PE or late-onset PE had higher percentage of elevated AST levels during early pregnancy (4.2% vs. 3.7% vs. 1.9%) when compared with those without PE (all p < 0.001). Additionally, the ratio of ALT/AST was higher in patients with early-onset PE or late-onset PE during early pregnancy compared with non-PE women (p < 0.001).

Further logistic regression analyses showed that the associations of elevated maternal ALT and AST levels and ALT/AST ratio > 1 during early pregnancy with PE and its subtypes were statistically significant after adjusting for potential confounders (Table 2). It was demonstrated that elevated ALT levels during early pregnancy were associated with PE (adjusted OR, aOR = 1.57 [95% CI: 1.38–1.80]), early-onset PE (aOR = 1.78 [95% CI: 1.27–2.48]) and late-onset PE (aOR = 1.53 [95% CI: 1.33–1.76]) in dataset I. Similar associations were observed between elevated AST levels in early pregnancy and PE (aOR = 1.63 [95% CI: 1.33–1.99]), early-onset PE (aOR = 1.81 [95% CI: 1.09–3.00]) and late-onset PE (aOR = 1.58 [95% CI: 1.27–1.97]). Furthermore, women with ALT/AST ratio > 1 during early pregnancy were also correlated with PE (aOR = 1.36 [95% CI: 1.25–1.47]), early-onset PE (aOR = 1.66 [95% CI: 1.34–2.06]) and late-onset PE (aOR = 1.31 [95% CI: 1.19–1.43]). In addition, the results in dataset II (further excluded women with pre-existing liver diseases before pregnancy from dataset I) and dataset III (further excluded women complicated with hepatitis virus positivity and hyperemesis gravidarum in early pregnancy from dataset II) were similar to those in dataset I, underscoring the reliability of the findings. It was also revealed that higher levels of ALT and AST in categories (increasing from ≤ 0.5 ULN, 0.5–1 ULN, 1–1.5 ULN, to > 1.5 ULN) in early pregnancy were linked to increased risks of PE, early-onset PE, and late-onset PE (all p for trend < 0.05; Table S1 in the Supplementary material). Moreover, the relationship of abnormal aminotransferase levels with the risk of GH has been illustrated in Table S2 in the Supplementary material.

Furthermore, we investigated whether the risks of PE and its subtypes were changed in women with relieved abnormal ALT or AST in early pregnancy (Table 3). The results showed that women with elevated ALT or AST levels only in early pregnancy but relieved in later pregnancy period were still associated increased risks of PE (aOR = 1.44 [95% CI: 1.25–1.66] for ALT; and aOR = 1.52 [95% CI: 1.22–1.89] for AST) compared with women with normal aminotransferase levels throughout pregnancy. Stratification analysis

Furthermore, the modification effects of maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI categories and IVF on the results of abnormal liver function tests in early pregnancy with PE and its subgroups were explored (Fig. 3). It was showed that elderly pregnant women (age ≥ 35) with elevated ALT levels during early pregnancy had a higher risk of early-onset PE (aOR = 2.39 [95% CI: 1.38–4.14]), compared to their counterparts with normal ALT levels. These results indicated a synergistic effect of elevated ALT level in early pregnancy and advanced maternal age on the risk of developing early-onset PE. What’s more, in the normal weight group, women with elevated ALT levels had significantly increased risks of PE (aOR = 1.78 [95% CI: 1.50–2.10]), early-onset PE (aOR = 2.38 [95% CI: 1.59–3.56]), and late-onset PE (aOR = 1.67 [95% CI: 1.39–2.01]). Associations were amplified in the underweight group greatly, with elevated ALT levels during early pregnancy had significantly higher risks of PE (aOR = 2.36 [95% CI: 1.41–3.97]) and late-onset PE (aOR = 2.22 [95% CI: 1.25–3.94]). Additionally, women who received IVF treatment and complicated with elevated ALT were more likely to developing PE (aOR = 1.74 [95% CI: 1.23–2.46]) and late-onset PE (aOR = 1.71 [95% CI: 1.17–2.49]). Similar findings were observed among women with high AST in the subgroup analysis (Figure S2 in the Supplementary material).

Discussion

The current study revealed that abnormally elevated maternal serum aminotransferase levels in early pregnancy were associated with PE and its subtypes, including both early-onset and late-onset PE. After excluding pre-pregnancy existing diseases and diagnosed adverse liver conditions in early pregnancy, the remaining unexplained abnormal aminotransferase levels in early pregnancy were still associated with the risk of PE, early-onset PE, and late-onset PE. Furthermore, maternal aminotransferase levels that were transiently abnormal during early pregnancy but relieved in later pregnancy period were still at an increased risk of PE as well as late-onset PE.

Previous studies have explored the relationship between liver function and the risk of PE [1, 2, 18, 19]. It was indicated that abnormal liver biochemical markers in early pregnancy might be associated with the risk of PE or HDP [12–14]. A Korean cohort study indicated that early unexplained ALT level increased the risk of PE [12]. Moreover, studies have shown that high ALT levels before pregnancy are associated with an increased risk of PE [20]. AST/ALT also reported to be associated with the subsequent risk of HDP [14]. In addition to clinically elevated levels, high-normal maternal ALT levels are associated with an increased risk of PE [21]. A recent cohort study in Beijing, China, also showed that slightly elevated ALT (≥ 12.90 U/L) and AST (≥ 17.40 U/L) levels in early pregnancy were associated with the risk of GH and PE [22]. Furthermore, our study also found that the consistent adverse effect of abnormal ALT or AST levels transiently existing in early pregnancy or persisting to late pregnancy were associated with increased risks of PE. Thus, our findings provided deeper insights into the connections between early abnormal liver function with increased risks of PE as well as late-onset PE.

Especially, our study suggested that elevated levels of ALT, AST and ALT/AST ratio in early pregnancy should cause alarm for the increased risk of early-onset PE and late-onset PE. Compared with late-onset PE, patients with early-onset PE are more susceptible to adverse pregnancy outcomes [9, 10]. Consequently, identifying high-risk individuals for early-onset PE to reduce adverse perinatal complications for pregnant women and fetuses as early as possible is crucial [23]. Early screening of maternal ALT, AST levels, and ALT/AST ratio could be helpful in identification of women at high risk of liver dysfunction-related early-onset PE. Subsequently, appropriate interventions would be implemented to enhance placental function and reduce the incidence of PE and related adverse pregnancy outcomes [24], which holds a significant value. Furthermore, the potential utilization of low-dose aspirin in early pregnancy as a prophylactic strategy against early-onset PE in these women would be of clinical significance in future studies.

Our study found that unexplained liver dysfunction, which commonly observed in early pregnancy after excluding population with pre-existing liver diseases, and hepatitis virus positivity, and hyperemesis gravidarum in early pregnancy, was still associated with the subsequent risk of early-onset PE. Firstly, a part of women might be complicated with undiagnosed coincidental liver diseases. For example, undiagnosed non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) was proposed by the previous studies. As obesity was a common risk factor for both fatty liver and PE, stratification analysis was used to elaborate on the association in overweight/obesity, normal weight, and underweight groups. Overweight/obesity appears to act as a confounding factor in the relationship between early elevated liver aminotransferase levels and the risk of PE, resulting in the disappearance of the association. However, this association remains significant in normal weight and underweight groups, with a particularly pronounced effect in the underweight group, which is considered a low-risk group for NAFLD, thus additional factors contributing to this observed association. Secondarily, subclinical liver dysfunction which was exacerbated by the physiological and metabolic stresses of the pregnancy was speculated. Pregnancy induces dramatic metabolic changes and complex metabolic interactions in multiple organs, where any small deviations from the norm might result in an increased risk for adverse pregnancy outcomes [25]. Our previous study found that overt or subclinical hyperthyroidism in early pregnancy was associated with liver metabolic dysfunction [26]. The modification effect of other endocrine hormones or metabolites could be investigated by further studies. Thirdly, some women may intake redundant multivitamin tablets or health products in early pregnancy in China, which may be harmful for liver. Our unpublished study also found excessive blood levels of vitamin D and B12 in early pregnancy could be detrimental to maternal liver function. Lastly, the involvement of deteriorating environmental factors, such as cosmetics and pollutants [27–30], could not be fully ruled out, necessitating further investigation.

The mechanism by which liver dysfunction confers increasing the subsequent risk of early-onset PE and late-onset PE was obscure. It is well known that early and late-onset PE have different pathogenetic mechanisms [31–33]. Increased levels of aminotransferase have been reported to be strongly associated with endothelial dysfunction, which ultimately contributes to the initiation of atherosclerosis and inflammation [34–36], leading to the onset of PE but more likely to be late-onset PE if maternal constitutional peripheral vascular dysfunction exists. Early pregnancy is a critical window for trophoblast invasion and uterus spiral artery remodeling which contribute to the pathological basis of early-onset PE [31]. Hepatic dysfunction in early stage of pregnancy and early-onset PE may share some common risk factors and partial similar pathogenesis. It is possible that these pathophysiological abnormalities which impaired the placentation, such as widespread inflammation, oxidative stress, vasospasm, and hypoxia in the early pregnancy [2, 31, 37], may also influence the liver and cause partially elevated liver enzymes [38, 39] before the appearance of early-onset PE symptoms. Changes in the sensitive liver function tests may serve as an early indicator of these pathological conditions. Additional studies are warranted to assess the state of uterine spiral artery remodeling in pregnant women exhibiting abnormal liver function tests in early pregnancy. Such investigations have the potential to yield a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms and potential preventive measures pertaining to the interplay between liver, placental dysfunction, and the onset of PE.

This study is strengthened by the utilization of a large cohort, including 116,967 pregnant women in China from 2014 to 2022. Therefore, the association between aminotransferase levels in early pregnancy and PE ant its subtypes (early-onset PE as well as late-onset PE), was evidently established taking advantage of this large cohort containing complete records liver function screening data in pregnancy. In addition, we confirmed these findings by dividing women into groups of whom liver dysfunction in early pregnancy were relieved or not in later pregnancy period. However, some limitations of the study may be existed. First, data on liver biochemical markers during the second trimester were inadequate to clarify the association during this specific period. Second, we proposed several reasons for unexplained elevated maternal liver biochemical markers in early pregnancy, which required validation in further studies. Last, pre-pregnancy liver function test was unavailable in this cohort. Future multi-center studies with diverse ethnic cohorts and longitudinal follow-up are warranted to confirm the predictive value of first-trimester liver aminotransferase in early-onset PE.

Conclusion

In summary, elevated maternal liver aminotransferase including ALT, AST and their ratio in early pregnancy were significantly associated with PE, and its subtypes, including early-onset and late-onset PE. Unexplained hepatic dysfunction during early pregnancy was also linked to the subsequent increased risk of PE and early-onset PE. Notably, screening aminotransferase levels in early pregnancy could be considered for early identification of women at high risk of developing liver dysfunction-related PE, especially early-onset PE.
