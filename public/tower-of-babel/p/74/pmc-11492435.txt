Metastatic melanoma of the ureter accompanied by pseudomelanosis vesica: A case report
Authors: SriGita Madiraju, Natalie Mainland, Stephen Hong, Isaac Zucker, Rokana Taftaf, Waqar Khan, David Fumo, Firas G. Petros
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Less than 1 % of melanomas metastasize to the genitourinary system. Pseudomelanosis is a rare benign condition due to deposition of melanin-like pigment. We present a 44-year-old male with metastatic melanoma to the left ureter accompanied by pseudomelanosis vesica. He had melanoma resected 20 years prior, and was later found to have diffuse metastatic disease. CT abdomen/pelvis found filling defect of the left mid-ureter. Cystourethroscopy showed brown lesions which on biopsy were pseudomelanosis vesica. Subsequently, the ureteral mass was biopsied with results consistent with metastatic melanoma. This case provides insight regarding management of two rare pathologic presentations in the same patient.

Introduction

Malignant melanoma makes up approximately 5 % of all skin malignancies and can metastasize in the early stages of the disease, with most common locations including the lymphatic system, lungs, brain, and liver.1,2 0.2–1% of all melanoma cases metastasize to the genitourinary system, and even more rarely do these progress to the point of obstructive uropathy.3,4 Pseudomelanosis, by contrast, is a benign condition due to deposition of melanin-like pigment in mucosal cells and is most commonly found in the intestines, with pseudomelanosis of the bladder (pseudomelanosis vesica) being exceedingly rare. Only a handful of cases have been previously described.5 This report presents the case of a 44-year-old male with a 20-year history of melanoma, found to have metastasis to the left ureter, as well as pseudomelanosis present throughout the bladder in setting of diffuse intracranial and intrahepatic metastatic disease.

Case

A 44-year-old male, with history of back melanoma status post-resection 20 years prior, presented to the ED with complaints of slurred speech and was found on head CT to have diffuse intracranial metastatic disease. A CT abdomen/pelvis conducted for staging purposes found a filling defect of the left mid-ureter as well as moderate left-sided hydroureteronephrosis, in addition to suspected metastasis to the liver and left gluteal musculature. Notably, there was no renal parenchymal involvement (Fig. 1). A liver biopsy confirmed metastatic melanoma. The patient's medical history was significant for focal seizures previously well-controlled with medication and a 28-pack-year smoking history. Although the initial treatment plan called for immunotherapy with ipilimumab and nivolumab, the patient was unable to proceed with these in a timely manner as he was concurrently taking dexamethasone 6 mg twice daily for worsening fatigue, lethargy, gait ataxia and cognitive deficits from his brain metastases post radiation.

Upon urologic evaluation, the patient denied any history of flank or pelvic pain, as well as any irritative voiding symptoms or difficulties, hematuria, or dysuria. Cystourethroscopy was performed and showed multiple dark brown flat lesions throughout the bladder, largest on the left lateral wall, with some others peppered on the right lateral wall (Fig. 2). Left ureteroscopy was unable to be completed due to significant distal narrowing of the ureter and a left ureteral stent was placed. Biopsy of bladder lesions showed urothelial mucosa with mucosal pigment macrophages in the absence of melanocytes, as confirmed by lack of SOX10 in the nuclei of the pigment-laden cells (Fig. 3). The patient then completed two months of outpatient targeted therapy with dabrafenib 150 mg twice daily plus trametinib 2 mg once daily with a palliative intent.

Following this, the ureteral stent was removed, and a retrograde pyelogram was performed, which showed that the left-sided filling defect remained but had decreased in size significantly, with resolved hydronephrosis. A ureteroscopy was then performed, in which the filling defect was successfully localized. The tissue in this area was black and appeared fibrotic, consistent with melanoma (Fig. 4). Several biopsies were taken, a new ureteral stent was placed, and the patient was discharged home. Pathology of ureteral biopsy confirmed neoplastic cells negative for CD45 and diffusely positive for SOX10 and Melan-A, consistent with metastatic melanoma.Fig. 4Ureteroscopy reveals a ureteral lesion, confirmed to be metastatic melanoma.Fig. 4

Oncologic plan included restaging MR brain and CT chest abdomen and pelvis every 8 weeks until week 56 of targeted therapy, and then every 12 weeks until disease progression at which point the patient, if tapered off steroids, would be transitioned to Ipilimumab and Nivolumab. However, this plan was unable to be completed, as the patient died during week 19 of therapy.

Discussion

Metastasis of melanoma to the genitourinary system is quite rare.1, 2, 3 In this patient, once the ureteral filling defect was discovered, a neoplasm of urothelial origin was initially suspected due to his extensive smoking history. However, the absence of flank or pelvic pain, irritative voiding symptoms, hematuria, or dysuria with a ureteral filling defect in clinical setting of widespread melanoma should raise suspicion of being a metastatic melanoma. Indeed, further investigation confirmed the ureteral mass being a metastatic melanoma. The mechanism for this metastasis was likely hematogenous in nature, given the lack of renal parenchymal involvement; if the renal parenchyma is involved, shed cells will commonly spread melanoma to the ureter.6

Prior to modern targeted and immunotherapy treatments, prognosis of metastatic melanoma was universally poor, but life expectancies have improved over the last ten years.7 No explicit guidelines exist for management of genitourinary metastasis of melanoma, but surgical resection has been used when possible in conjunction with immunotherapy.3,7 Surgical approaches to ureteral metastasis have consisted of resection of the metastatic mass, partial ureterectomy with anastomosis, or nephroureterectomy.7 Holmium laser ablation has also been used.1,7 In patients with nonresectable metastatic melanoma, targeted and immunotherapy treatments have the potential to shrink the disease burden significantly, as seen in our patient. However, for patients with extensive metastasis (such as in this case) surgical treatment will not be curative. Risk stratification, multidisciplinary team discussion, and patient risk factors can help individualize treatment approach and therefore, improve quality of life.1,3,7

Additionally, this patient's ureteral melanoma was accompanied by pseudomelanosis vesica. This condition is extremely rare, with fewer than five case reports found in the literature, and no other case was accompanied by ureteral melanoma.5,8 Pseudomelanosis is characterized by melanin-like lipofuscin deposits in mucosal cells without detectable melanocytes.8 Pseudomelanosis is most commonly found in the GI tract (particularly the colon) where it appears to be linked with the use of thiazide diuretics, beta blockers, and iron supplements, or with chronic conditions such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, or chronic kidney disease.5 The etiology of pseudomelanosis vesica has not yet been determined.

Conclusion

This case report provides insight regarding presentation and management of two rare synchronous pathologies in the same patient. While metastatic melanoma presents a diagnostic challenge in-itself, ureteral involvement should be considered in melanoma patients presenting with hydronephrosis due to ureteral defect in the absence of flank pain or hematuria.
