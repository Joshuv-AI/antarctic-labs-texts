Outcomes of anterior versus posterior rib fractures and surgical treatment: analysis of 249 patients
Authors: Ya-Fu Cheng, Chang-Lun Huang, Yu-Jun Chang, Ching-Yuan Cheng, Chia-Che Chang, Bing-Yen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTraditionally, rib fracture severity has been quantified by fracture count and radiographic displacement. However, emerging evidence indicates that the outcomes following rib fractures widely vary and are not fully explained by injury burden alone. Whether anterior and posterior fracture locations lead to distinct recovery patterns remains unclear.MethodsA prospective longitudinal cohort study involving patients with traumatic rib fractures was conducted. Fractures were classified as anterior or posterior based on radiographic location. Pain intensity and functional status were serially evaluated for up to 3 months. Multivariable analyses were conducted with adjustment for age, body mass index, injury severity score, fracture number, displacement severity, associated injuries, and surgical stabilization of rib fractures (SSRF).ResultsFracture displacement severity and the number of fracture ribs independently predicted higher pain intensity. After adjustment, anterior and posterior fractures exhibited comparable pain trajectories over time. However, posterior fractures were linked to greater functional status improvement from 1 to 3 months. Surgical stabilization might be associated with greater early pain reduction and higher early functional scores but did not alter the long-term recovery slope. Early health status on day 14 strongly predicted 3-month outcomes, independent of baseline injury severity.ConclusionsThe number of fracture ribs and displacement severity appear to reflect injury intensity, whereas fracture location may influence physiologic consequences. Posterior fractures show similar pain levels to anterior fractures but somewhat greater functional recovery.

Background

Rib fractures are among the most common injuries after blunt thoracic trauma and are consistently linked to higher morbidity and mortality [1]. Traditionally, rib fracture severity has been quantified by the number of fracture ribs and the severity of their displacement, with higher fracture counts associated with higher risk of pulmonary complications, prolonged hospitalization, and mortality [2, 3]. However, accumulating evidence indicates that outcomes following rib fractures are more complex than a single numeric variable and may not be fully explained by fracture count alone [4].

The prognostic value of rib fracture number remains a matter of debate. Whereas early analyses of national trauma databases revealed that a higher rib fracture number did not independently worsen outcomes following adjustment for associated injuries [5], subsequent studies reported threshold effects in elderly populations, where higher rib counts independently predicted adverse outcomes [3]. Beyond fracture number, anatomical distribution may play a pivotal role in physiologic consequence determination. Clinical studies have demonstrated that rib fracture patterns are associated with distinct complication profiles, suggesting that fracture location influences respiratory function and overall recovery. Posterior fractures are often considered as markers of high-energy trauma and are linked to concomitant intrathoracic injuries, whereas lateral and anterior fractures may more directly impair ventilatory mechanics [6]. Nevertheless, most previous studies have focused on acute complications rather than longitudinal recovery trajectories.

The anterior and posterior regions of the rib cage markedly differ. The rib surface exhibits a curvature ranging from 3.8 m− 1 in the anterior region to 17.3 m− 1 in the posterior region [7]. Furthermore, the posterior rib is thicker than the anterior rib. The independent effect of rib fracture location on pain evolution and functional recovery has not been prospectively investigated.

Surgical stabilization of rib fractures (SSRF) has emerged as a key intervention in selected patients with severe rib fracture. Consensus guidelines recommend fixation in cases of flail chest and severe rib fracture [8]. Although SSRF has been shown to be beneficial in these patients, it remains unclear whether fixation of the anterior or posterior site will influence the outcomes.

Therefore, a prospective longitudinal cohort study was conducted to evaluate the independent impact of anterior versus posterior rib fractures on pain intensity and functional recovery. It was hypothesized that the rib fracture location would independently influence pain severity and functional recovery trajectory due to the anatomic difference. Elucidating these relationships may enhance prognostic assessment and inform personalized treatment approaches in patients with rib fractures.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This single-center, pragmatic, cohort study compared the outcomes of patients with anterior and posterior rib fractures. The study enrollment began on June 1, 2023, after obtaining approval from our hospital’s Institutional Review Board (IRB-230137), and concluded on September 30, 2025. Outcome observation lasted until December 31, 2025. Informed consent was obtained from all participants at the time of admission.

All adult trauma patients admitted to our hospital institution were screened for study eligibility. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age < 18 years or > 90 years, (2) < 3 fractured ribs, (3) severe traumatic brain injury (defined as a best resuscitated Glasgow coma scale (GCS) score ≤ 8), (4) severe dementia or any disease that impairs the ability to communicate, and (5) not undergoing chest computerized tomography (CT) scan.

Treatment groups

Eligible patients who agreed to participate were assigned to the anterior and posterior rib fracture groups based on the midaxillary line. If most rib displacements were anterior to the midaxillary line, the patient was allocated to the anterior group, and vice versa. For patients with both anterior and posterior rib fractures, their classification was determined based on which site had a greater number of fractures. If the rib fracture number was similar, classification was determined based on the site with the greater degree of dislocation. Patients with simultaneous bilateral side rib fractures were classified into right or left by the more severe site. Two senior thoracic surgeons (YF Cheng and BY Wang) reviewed the rib displacement severity. The surgical interventions were determined after discussion between the surgeons and patients. SSRF was performed mostly on patients with flail chest or severe rib fractures (> 3 displacements over 50%). SSRF was always performed for at least three severely displaced ribs.

The treatment protocol was quite similar in each patient and consisted of incentive spirometry, oral nonopioid pain management with acetaminophen or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and lidocaine transdermal patches. Opioid drugs, both oral and intravenous infusions, were prescribed as needed only for severe pain. Regional analgesia, such as epidural analgesia or intercostal nerve blockade, was not administered because of the shortage of anesthesiologists in our institute. The surgical technique was also similar to minimizing muscle splitting and wound. Thoracoscopy was performed in every SSRF procedure.

To minimize bias, a case manager recorded each patient’s pain score using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) on days 0 to 7, day 14, day 28, month 2, and month 3. Functional recovery was documented using the EQ-5D-5 L health status questionnaire on day 14, day 28, and month 3. The patients filled the questionnaire during admission and visit to the outpatient clinic. If a patient was unable to return to the clinic, the case manager completed the questionnaire via phone. The health status scale ranged from 0 to 100, with 0 indicating the worst health you can imagine and 100 denoting the best.

Measurement and outcomes

The primary outcome was short- and long-term functional recovery (health status). In our clinical practice, we observed that patients with posterior rib fractures have better recovery and experience less pain and adverse effects than those with anterior rib fractures. However, we are unsure if this assumption is correct and related to the effect of SSRF. Better functional recovery means less private duty care and faster return to work, which is crucial for cost reduction.

The secondary outcomes included the short- and long-term pain score, hospital stay, intensive care unit (ICU) stay, and mechanical ventilation duration. These indices influence medical cost and hospital bed utilization.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were summarized according to fracture site pattern (predominantly anterior vs. posterior). Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages and compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. We analyzed the VAS on day 7, at 1 month, and at 3 months. However, health status was evaluated on day 14 because some severely injured patients were unable to complete the EQ-5D-5 L questionnaire on day 7.

To assess longitudinal changes in pain and functional recovery following a rib fracture, repeated measurements of VAS pain scores and health status were analyzed using generalized estimating equations (GEE), which enables the estimation of adjusted mean trajectories while accounting for correlations among repeated observations within the same patient over time. A first-order autoregressive [AR (1)] working correlation structure was specified.

For pain analysis, the VAS trajectories were evaluated from day 0 to month 3, with day 0 used as the reference time point. Models were adjusted for baseline pain severity, number of fractured ribs, rib displacement severity, and associated thoracic injuries including flail chest, pneumothorax, hemothorax, and pulmonary contusion. SSRF status at baseline was included as a covariate, and the interaction terms between time and SSRF were analyzed to determine whether pain reduction over time differed between surgical and nonsurgical management.

For functional outcomes, longitudinal changes in health status were analyzed from day 14 onward, reflecting an early postinjury stabilization and routine clinical assessment. Day 14 was used as the reference time point. The models were adjusted for fracture site, displacement severity, number of fractured ribs, Injury Severity Score (ISS), and associated injuries including hemothorax, four-limb fracture, sternal fracture, flail chest, pneumothorax, and pulmonary contusion. The interaction terms between time and fracture site were included to determine whether long-term recovery trajectories differed based on the anatomical site of the rib fracture. SSRF status on day 14 and interaction terms between time and SSRF were also included to account for the potential effects of surgical treatment on functional recovery.

GEE analyses were performed using all available repeated observations without imputing missing follow-up measurements. Adjusted mean values with 95% confidence intervals derived from the GEE models were presented to illustrate longitudinal trends. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

During the study period, 473 patients with rib fractures after admission were screened at our hospital. Of them, 174 were excluded due to the following reasons: age < 18 or > 90 years (n = 5), < 3 rib fractures (n = 77), and GCS < 9 (n = 92). The remaining 299 patients were approached for enrollment, with 43 excluded due to communication difficulties and 7 due to the lack of chest CT scan. The study flow chart is presented in Fig. 1. Among the 249 patients, 121 (48.6%) had mixed anterior and posterior fracture patterns. Only two patients were initially classified into different groups by the two reviewing surgeons. Following a thorough discussion, the reviewing surgeons reached a consensus and assigned these patients to the same group.

The mechanism of injury and fracture severity also exhibited no significant difference. Similarly, the ISS did not differ (median 16 vs. 17, p = 0.583). Posterior fractures affected a greater number of ribs (median 6 vs. 5, p = 0.019). The patterns of associated injuries also differed: anterior fractures more frequently involved sternal injury (6.5% vs. 0.7%, p = 0.023) and extremity fractures (23.4% vs. 9.2%, p = 0.002), whereas posterior fractures were more commonly associated with hemothorax (64.8% vs. 50.5%, p = 0.023). Other thoracic injuries were comparable. SSRF was performed more frequently in posterior fractures (40.8% vs. 25.2%, p = 0.010).

Among patients who underwent SSRF (n = 85), the baseline characteristics were similar between patients with anterior and posterior factures (n = 27 and n = 58, respectively) (Table 2). No differences were observed in hospital length of stay, ICU stay, mechanical ventilation duration, or pain scores on day 7, month 1, or month 3. Health status on day 14, month 1, and month 3 was also comparable between the groups. Regarding timing of SSRF, most procedures were performed within 48–72 h after injury. The median time to SSRF was 3 days in both anterior group (IQR: 0–5 days) and the posterior group (IQR: 2–4 days).

Pain significantly decreased over time in the entire cohort (all time points p < 0.001). Compared with day 0, pain decreased by − 3.68 points on day 14 and by − 5.99 points on month 3 (Table 3). Fracture location was not independently associated with pain trajectory (posterior vs. anterior B = − 0.13, p = 0.104). Contrarily, the severity of structural injury strongly influenced pain. Greater displacement predicted higher pain level (50% to100%: B = 0.75; >100%: B = 0.76; both p < 0.001), and a higher number of fractured ribs independently contributed to increased pain (B = 0.07 per rib, p < 0.001). SSRF was tended to enhance pain reduction over time, with significant time–treatment interaction from day 7 to month 3 (all p < 0.001), which was associated with greater pain reduction following fixation.

Markers of injury burden were independently associated with poor functional recovery, including displacement severity (all p < 0.001), number of fractured ribs (B = − 0.76, p = 0.003), and ISS (B = − 0.26, p < 0.001). Discussion

Due to the limited research on rib fracture location, there is currently no established guideline or consensus for classifying fracture location. A previous study classified the anatomical regions into five parts: costal cartilage, anterior, lateral, posterior and paravertebral regions [9]. Another study divided the fracture locations into four regions: anterolateral, lateral, posterolateral and posterior [10]. Given the lack of a standardized classification system and the limited number of patients in our study, we categorized fracture locations into anterior and posterior groups based on the midaxillary line. In this prospective longitudinal cohort, rib fracture location showed domain-specific associations with recovery. After adjustment for injury burden, anterior and posterior fractures exhibited similar pain trajectories; however, posterior fractures seemed to achieve superior functional recovery over time. Fracture displacement severity emerged as the major pain determinant. Surgical stabilization was associated with greater pain reduction and higher early functional status but did not alter the long-term recovery slope. These findings indicate that rib fracture location influences respiratory biomechanics and functional restoration rather than nociceptive intensity per se.

Pain from rib fractures has traditionally been linked to fracture number and displacement severity [11, 12]. Consistent with previous literature, both variables were found to independently predict pain intensity. These data reinforce the concept that rib fracture pain is fundamentally mechanical in nature. Repetitive micromotion at unstable fracture interfaces during respiration causes persistent periosteal and intercostal nerve stimulation. Therefore, nociception reflects instability magnitude rather than anatomic position. Contrary to the common assumption that posterior fractures are more painful due to paraspinal muscle traction, our study observed no significant difference in pain between anterior and posterior fractures. Posterior fractures tended to have lower pain scores, although the difference was not statistically significant. These findings indicate that fracture position alone does not determine nociceptive burden. Rather, instability governs the level of pain experienced.

Age-related differences in pain perception have been observed, with elderly patients often reporting lower pain levels despite experiencing greater morbidity [13]. Although previous studies reported decreasing pain intensity with advancing age, our cohort did not exhibit significant age-related differences in pain scores [14]. Such a discrepancy may reflect standardized analgesic protocols and structured pain assessment in our study. Essentially, the absence of an age effect in pain indicates that functional decline in elderly patients cannot be attributed solely to underreported discomfort. Collectively, these data support a reframing of rib fracture as a disorder of chest wall mechanics rather than a localized skeletal injury [15].

Despite the similar pain resolution, posterior fractures were associated with modestly greater improvement in functional status from 1 to 3 months postinjury. This divergence suggests that persistent disability following anterior fractures arises from biomechanical impairment rather than an unresolved nociception. Previous studies have also reported that fracture site is important for outcomes, with lateral middle rib fractures showing higher complication rates than fractures in other sites [6]. The rib cage is not a mechanically uniform structure. Anterior ribs are flatter with larger curvature radii and substantially contribute to pump-handle motion, increasing anteroposterior thoracic diameter during inspiration. Posterior ribs are thicker, more curved, and stabilized by robust paraspinal muscles, enabling load sharing with the vertebral column [7]. These structural differences cause regional variations in compliance and respiratory motion coupling. Anterior segment disruption may impair thoraco-scapular coordination and upper chest wall excursion, thereby limiting ventilatory efficiency and shoulder girdle function. Even after pain improvement, altered anterior mechanics may limit daily activities requiring trunk rotation and arm elevation. Contrarily, the posterior ribs may benefit from compensatory muscular stabilization, allowing a more efficient adaptation during healing [16].

Associated injuries may further contribute to this difference. In this study, anterior fractures were more frequently accompanied by sternal and extremity fractures, whereas posterior fractures were more often associated with hemothorax. Extremity and sternal injuries can extend disability beyond pulmonary limitations, whereas hemothorax, if effectively treated, may exert a more transient effect. These concomitant injuries may amplify functional impairment in patients with anterior fractures. Essentially, the superior quality-of-life outcomes observed in the posterior group were not attributable to surgical stabilization. In the posterior cohort, patients who underwent SSRF did not demonstrate superior long-term quality of life to those who received conservative management. This indicates that intrinsic biomechanical differences, rather than operative intervention, underlie the observed divergence in recovery.

Previous studies have reported that age, BMI, number of rib fractures, displacement severity, and ISS score are prognostic factors [17, 18]. Although the prognosis of patients with rib fractures is mainly determined at the time of injury, our data indicate that health status on day 14 is also an important factor in the prediction of long-term outcomes. In other words, severely injured patients may have favorable outcomes if they receive a proactive intervention, including rehabilitation, pain control, pulmonary toilet, and SSRF. Day 14 is a pivotal time point for these patients. If the patient fails to achieve a good result at this time, a long-term poor prognosis is probable. That is to say, patients have to be treated sooner rather than later, with intervention at 2 weeks postinjury potentially having a limited effect. Recent research also supports that SSRF is better to be performed within 48–72 h postinjury [19, 20]. Performing SSRF after this time may lead to worse outcomes compared with nonoperative management [21, 22]. As a result, we suggest early multidisciplinary management may be associated with better recovery in selected patients.

This is the first study to compare the treatment outcomes of patients with anterior and posterior rib fractures. However, this study has several limitations. First, this is a single-center study, and the results may not be universally applicable to all countries and institutions. Further larger multicenter studies are warranted to confirm the results. Second, the selection of SSRF and the interventions were not blinded. While most of the patients strongly preferred to avoid surgery due to the cost and fear, the outcome measures could be influenced by patients’ choice bias. A randomized controlled trial would be better to reduce selection bias and strengthen the validity of the conclusions. Third, many factors can influence health status. Although we analyzed the associated injuries, the injury severity and treatment could not be unified. A pelvic or limb fracture can be as severe as paralysis or as minor as observation only. It is hard to find patients with purely rib fractures. In this study, several factors may have acted as confounders, including the heterogeneity of associated injuries (e.g., limb, pelvic, spine, and brain injuries) variations in treatment intensity, rehabilitation protocols, analgesic strategies, and patient selection for SSRF were important confounding factors and might have influenced the results among different patients or at different institutions. Fourth, classification of mixed fracture patterns into a single predominant group may have resulted in exposure misclassification. Such misclassification could potentially attenuate, dilute, or bias the observed associations between fracture location and recovery outcomes. Last but not least, this study lacked regional analgesia techniques such as epidural analgesia or intercostal nerve blockade. These techniques are widely used in rib fracture management and have proven benefits for pain control and respiratory function. Their omission may have influenced pain outcomes. Therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing our findings to healthcare systems with broader access to locoregional analgesic techniques.

Conclusions

In conclusion, rib fracture number and displacement severity appear to reflect the magnitude of structural injury, whereas fracture location may influence physiologic consequences and recovery trajectories in patients without regional analgesia. In this cohort, posterior rib fractures were associated with similar pain trajectories but somewhat greater functional recovery compared with anterior fractures. These findings were observed after adjustment for injury burden and surgical intervention, although residual confounding from associated injuries and treatment selection cannot be excluded. The observed differences may be related to regional biomechanical variation and associated injury patterns. Overall, fracture location may represent a potentially relevant prognostic factor in rib fracture management, and further studies are warranted to clarify its clinical implications and its role in guiding individualized treatment strategies.
