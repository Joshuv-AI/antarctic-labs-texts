Development, validation, and visualization of a novel nomogram for predicting clinical outcomes of postoperative cervical cancer patients
Authors: Yanqi Liu, Jia You, Dandan Liu, Liang Wen, Huimin Liu, Chenglei Gu, Wentian Zhao, Huaiyin Shi, Aijun Liu, Tian Song, Wen Yang, Hongwei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To develop a context-aware multi-instance learning (TransMIL) model based on whole-slide pathological images and integrate it with clinical parameters, thereby constructing a multimodal model for prognostic prediction in postoperative cervical cancer patients. Data (including pathological images and clinical information) were collected from patients diagnosed with cervical cancer via pathological examination who underwent surgery at the First Medical Centre of the Chinese People’s Liberation Army General Hospital between May 2017 and May 2023. After integration and screening, 374 patients were enrolled. To maximize the use of all available samples and obtain a robust performance estimate, we employed a rigorous patient-level nested five-fold cross-validation strategy for model development and evaluation. The CLAM algorithm was employed for attention-based, interpretable foreground segmentation. By integrating TransMIL with global context encoding techniques, spatial dependencies of tissue structures were captured to extract deep image features. And fused them with clinical features to predict the prognosis of patients. The predictive performance of each model was evaluated using the C-index. Overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) were assessed via the Kaplan–Meier method. Model performance was further evaluated using calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) curves. The predictive model integrating clinical-pathological characteristics demonstrated favourable prognostic capability. Models based on pathological images alone and combined clinical-Pathomics Models were used to predict 3-year and 5-year disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS), respectively. For OS prediction, the clinical-Pathomics Model achieved a C-index of 0.700, outperforming the pathomic model (C-index: 0.631) and the clinical model(C-index: 0.690). Similarly, for DFS prediction, the clinical-Pathomics Model (C-index: 0.626) surpassed the pathomic model (C-index: 0.549). Based on model nomogram scores, patients were effectively stratified into high-risk and low-risk cohorts, with Kaplan–Meier curves revealing statistically significant differences in survival rates between groups. Further evaluation via time-dependent ROC analysis, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis confirmed the model’s robust predictive accuracy and clinical applicability. A clinical-Pathomics Model was developed to predict 3-year and 5-year DFS and OS in postoperative cervical cancer patients. This model represents a promising approach for for patient risk stratification.

Introduction

Cervical cancer ranks as the fourth most common cancer among women globally and remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, with an estimated 500,000 new cases and approximately 300,000 deaths occurring annually1. Early-stage cervical cancer responds favourably to surgery and radiotherapy, whereas effective treatments for advanced-stage disease remain limited, often resulting in a poor prognosis1,2. Consequently, accurately predicting postoperative survival is crucial for clinical management. Reliable prognostic assessment aids in determining tumour aggressiveness and identifying critical intervention windows3. It further facilitates the development of personalised treatment plans, enabling clinicians to avoid both overtreatment, which may cause severe complications and the application of less effective therapies to patient cohorts with a favourable prognosis.

Current clinical management of cervical cancer relies on the TNM or FIGO staging systems for prognostic prediction. However, due to the inherent heterogeneity of tumors, patients at identical stages often experience markedly different outcomes, revealing the limited predictive power of these systems. This inadequacy stems from several critical shortcomings. First, TNM staging depends on the manual interpretation of histologic slides by pathologists. This process is not only labor-intensive but also susceptible to inter-observer variability, which can lead to staging inaccuracies3. Second, conventional staging parameters overlook key biological characteristics of the tumor microenvironment, such as stroma-tumor interactions and immune infiltration, thereby failing to capture the full prognostic information embedded in tissue slides and to accurately quantify tumor heterogeneity. Third, the standard sectioning interval of 2–3 mm may miss micrometastases in lymph nodes or small vascular tumor emboli below this resolution threshold and fails to account for inter-sectional tumor dynamics4. Fourth, the system largely ignores molecular characteristics. Critical prognostic factors, including high-risk HPV genotypes (e.g., HPV18-associated adenocarcinoma, which has a propensity for hematogenous metastasis5) and specific gene mutations (e.g., PIK3CA mutations, associated with resistance to chemoradiotherapy6)—are not integrated into the staging criteria. Consequently, within a single TNM stage, there exists a diversity of patient subtypes with varying survival outcomes, underscoring the urgent need for more refined predictive strategies.

The integration of Whole-Slide-Imaging (WSI) with machine learning has significantly improved cervical cancer screening and diagnostic accuracy. However, prognostic prediction based on integrated pathological images and clinical data remains challenging. A primary obstacle is the gigapixel-scale nature of WSIs rendering them incompatible with traditional algorithms models7. Advances in artificial intelligence offer substantial potential to address this issue. By leveraging machine learning methods to quantify intrinsic tumor biology and therapeutic sensitivity, AI shows promise for accurately predicting disease progression and patient prognosis8.

Current machine learning applications in cervical cancer research range from traditional algorithms such as support vector machines and decision trees, to transfer learning. With advancements in digital pathology, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have become a cornerstone technology for pathological image analysis, demonstrating considerable utility in cancer diagnosis, subtype classification, and prognosis prediction9. Despite the proliferation of WSI analyses, the application of Multiple-Instance Learning (MIL) for prognostic prediction remains relatively unexplored. This gap is particularly pronounced in cervical cancer, where a scarcity of long-term patient follow-up data has hindered prognostic studies. To address this gap, we developed a MIL framework that integrates clinical features with WSI representations to stratify patients by Overall Survival (OS) and Disease-Free Survival (DFS).

Methods

Data collection

We retrospectively collected records of patients diagnosed with cervical cancer based on postoperative pathology at the First Medical Center of the Chinese PLA General Hospital between May 2017 and May 2023. After data integration and screening, 374 patients were included in the final cohort. Clinical data consisted of age, BMI, parity, FIGO stage, pathological differentiation grade, and the number of lymph node metastases. Pathological images were hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained whole-slide images obtained from patients who underwent surgical treatment for cervical cancer. The primary endpoints of this study are Overall Survival (OS) and Disease-Free Survival (DFS), defined with the following censoring rules: OS is defined as the time from the date of surgery to the date of death from any cause. For patients still alive at the censoring date, their OS time will be right-censored at the date of the last follow-up confirming survival. DFS was defined as the period from the date of surgery to the date of the first occurrence of either: (1) confirmed disease recurrence, (2) death due to cervical cancer. For patients without such events at the study cutoff date, DFS time was right-censored at the date of the last confirmed disease-free follow-up. Survival status and recurrence information were collected via the hospital electronic medical record system and telephone follow-up. Recurrence was primarily determined by new lesions detected through imaging examinations or known lesions meeting progression criteria. Pathological confirmation was obtained for some patients. In subsequent DFS analyses, local recurrence and distant metastasis were combined as a single event.The inclusion criteria were: (a) underwent radical surgery for cervical cancer; (b) had a postoperative pathological confirmation of cervical cancer; and (c) had complete postoperative pathological data. The exclusion criteria were: (a) incomplete clinical or WSI data; and (b) presence of concurrent other malignancies. Given the overall scale of our dataset, to ensure the most comprehensive and unbiased utilisation of all samples for model performance estimation, we adopted a rigorous patient-level nested five-fold cross-validation strategy instead of a fixed train-test split. The entire cohort of 374 patients was randomly and evenly partitioned into five folds at the patient level, ensuring all WSIs from the same patient remained within the same fold to prevent data leakage. To ensure data integrity and robustness for modeling, we implemented a systematic approach to handle missing data in clinical variables. The extent of missingness was first assessed for each variable. For variables with a low rate of missingness (≤ 5%), which were considered missing completely at random, we imputed the missing values using the mode (the most frequent category) to preserve the sample size and statistical power10. For variables with a higher rate of missingness (> 5%), we treated “missing” as a separate, informative category (e.g., “unknown”) and incorporated it into the analysis. This strategy acknowledges the missing data as a potential source of information and avoids the potential bias introduced by complete-case analysis, which is a recommended practice in clinical prediction model development11. A comprehensive check confirmed that after this process, the final analytical dataset was complete for all features used in model development.

Image processing and feature extraction

From each patient’s tissue sample, two to four representative slides (totalling 1,163 whole slide images, WSI) were selected and digitized into whole-slide images (WSIs) in SVS format using a McAody digital slide scanner (McAody, Transparent Imaging). Since WSIs contain extensive non-diagnostic regions (e.g., blank background, hemorrhage, folds) and comprise billions of pixels, direct processing introduces substantial noise and computational burden12. Therefore, we adopted a patch-based processing strategy for feature extraction. We employed the CLAM WSI Analysis Toolbox, an open-source framework for weakly whole-slide image analysis, to perform foreground segmentation and feature extraction13. During preprocessing, CLAM segments tissue regions, removes background, and divides the WSI into non-overlapping 256 × 256 pixels patches, All generated patches from a WSI were used without upper sampling, resulting in a variable number of Transformer input tokens (N) per slide, ultimately generating 68,766,905 patches. Each patch was then processed using a ResNet-18convolutional neural network (pre-trained on ImageNet) to extract discriminative features. Tissue segmentation thresholds were set as follows: in the HSV colour space, saturation channel > 0.05 and luminance channel values between 0.2 and 0.9, to effectively distinguish tissue foreground from background (e.g., blank areas, folds). Each patch was encoded into a 512-dimensional feature vector by the ResNet-18. All analyses were performed at 20 × magnification (approximately 0.5 microns per pixel. ResNet-18 employs residual learning with skip connections to mitigate degradation in deep networks14, and its efficiency makes it well-suited for computational pathology15.The features generated by ResNet-18’s deep convolutional network are highly abstracted and non-linear deep features, integrating low-level edges, intermediate texture patterns, and even high-level semantic structural information. These feature vectors (512-dimensional) are inherently difficult to interpret directly in terms of traditional pathological vocabulary, yet their efficacy has been validated across numerous computational pathology studies13,16,17.

Feature aggregation

For feature aggregation, we employed the Transformer-based Multi-Instance Learning (TransMIL) framework18. Designed specifically for whole-slide image (WSI) classification, this Transformer-based MIL framework addresses a key limitation of traditional methods by explicitly modeling dependencies between instances. Its central mechanism is self-attention, which dynamically models relationships among instances. The model computes attention scores by measuring the similarity between the query vector of a given image patch and the key vectors of all patches (including itself). These scores are then used as weights to compute a weighted sum of the value (V) vectors across all patches, producing a new, context-aware feature representation. This allows the model to autonomously learn contextual relationships between disparate image patches, thereby capturing long-range dependencies19. The importance of each patch is evaluated by an attention network that integrates positional and instance attention modules. The final comprehensive attention score for each patch—indicating its relevance to diagnostically significant “foreground” regions—is obtained by summing or performing a weighted fusion of the positional and instance attention scores.Furthermore, a clustering constraint strategy leverages the attention scores to generate pseudo-labels. Specifically, the composite attention scores serve weight the summation of all image patch feature vectors, enabling instance-level clustering. This process yields a single, weighted aggregate feature vector that represents the entire WSI.

To enhance the model’s ability to understand the overall semantic context of the whole slide image, we introduced key modifications to the original TransMIL framework. Specifically, in addition to employing standard sinusoidal positional encoding to capture spatial information, we incorporated a slide-level global feature as a positional prior for each image patch. This design was inspired by the diagnostic approach of pathologists, who assess local regions with reference to slide-wide patterns such as tumor grade or inflammation severity20.

To achieve this, we first computed a global feature vector s ∈ R^d from the feature matrix X via Global Average Pooling (GAP), using the formulation s = (1/N) Σ_{i = 1}^N x_i21. This vector encodes the overall characteristics of the entire WSI. Then, inspired by positional embedding in Transformers, we adopted an additive fusion strategy to integrate this global vector ss with each patch feature x_i from the same slide, resulting in an enhanced representation x’_i = x_i + s. Thus, each local feature x’_i carries not only its own visual information but also explicit contextual information from the whole pathological slide.

The fused feature sequence X’ = [x'_1, x'_2, …, x'_N] is fed into a standard Transformer encoder. The encoder models the relationships among patches—now enriched with global context—through self-attention mechanisms. Finally, the output is aggregated into a patient-level feature vector for prediction by the classifier. By introducing global priors to modulate and refine local relationship learning, this enhancement equips the model with both macro-level generalization capacity and micro-level discriminative ability.

Pathomics model development

In the prognostic modeling phase, patient-level feature vectors derived from multi-instance aggregation are processed by a survival prediction module. Owing to the presence of right-censoring in clinical survival data—where some patients do not experience the endpoint event during follow-up—conventional regression methods yield biased estimates22. To address this, we employed a discrete-time survival model optimized using a negative log-likelihood loss (NLLSurvLoss)23. This approach discretizes the continuous time axis into predefined intervals and formulates a likelihood function based on conditional survival probabilities (Eq. 2). The final output is a risk score within the range of 0 to 1, which serves as the imaging risk score. The Imaging Risk Score is a continuous scalar generated through our enhanced TransMIL framework. Construction of multimodal prognosis models

Univariable Cox regression analysis was performed on the collected clinical parameters and laboratory risk scores to identify variables significantly associated with prognosis (P < 0.05). Variables with P < 0.05 were subsequently incorporated into a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model. We employed the ultimately selected clinical variables to establish a Cox proportional hazards regression model, and combined these clinical variables with the imaging risk score to construct a multimodal prognostic model.

Model evaluation

Model performance was evaluated using a patient-level five-fold cross-validation protocol. In each iteration, four folds wereused for training, and the remaining fold served as the validation set. Performance metrics (C-index, AUC, etc.) reported in the Results section are the averages calculated from the predictions across all five validation folds. The proportional hazards assumption for the final multivariable Cox models was assessed using the global test based on Schoenfeld residuals. A non-significant test result (P > 0.05) supports the validity of the assumption.Survival outcomes were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed with the log-rank test. Calculate the area under the ROC curve to assess the overall performance of the model. The agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities was evaluated using calibration curves. Furthermore, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to quantify the clinical net benefit of the predictive model. All statistical analyses were carried out using Python and R software.

Attention heatmap generation for model interpretation

To visualize the regions that most contributed to the prognostic prediction, we generated attention heatmaps based on the model’s internal mechanism. Specifically, we computed the cosine similarity between each patch token and the class token from the final Transformer layer. These similarity scores were then linearly normalized to the range [0, 1] for each slide. The normalized attention scores were color-mapped (red for high attention, blue for low) and overlaid precisely onto the corresponding regions of the original whole-slide image at the patch level, with a scale bar added for reference. This approach allows for intuitive identification of histologically salient areas driving the model’s decision.

Results

Development of the pathomics model

The median follow-up duration for the entire cohort was 52 months (interquartile range: 32 months). A total of 70 overall survival (OS) events (incidence rate: 18.7%) and 64 disease-free survival (DFS) events (incidence rate: 17.1%) occurred. The censoring rate for the overall survival analysis was 81.3%, and for the disease-free survival analysis it was 82.9%.The concordance index (C-index), a core metric in survival analysis, was used to evaluate Pathomics Model performance. It quantifies the probability that the Pathomics Model’s predictions align with the actual observed sequence of events, reflecting its ability to stratify patients by risk25. Pathomics Models were trained separately using OS and DFS as endpoints, with predictions analyzed via Kaplan–Meier curves (Fig. 2). Patients were classified into high- and low-risk groups based on the median hazard scores. The multi-instance model achieved a C-index of 0.631 (95% CI 0.551–0.710) for OS and 0.549 (95% CI 0.473–0.624) for DFS. The resulting Kaplan–Meier curves for OS and DFS are shown in Fig. 1. Additionally, p-values were calculated using the log-rank test to assess the statistical significance of the difference in survival distributions between the risk groups.Fig. 2Kaplan–Meier curves between low-risk and high-risk groups defined by Pathomics Model for overall survival.

We initialized the network parameters using `nn.init()` and employed the Adam optimizer for training. The gradient accumulation step was set to 32, with an equivalent batch size of 256. The total number of training epochs was 300, employing an early stopping strategy (patience: 10, stop cycle: 40). The initial learning rate was set to 0.0001, decaying via a cosine annealing schedule (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017). Model evaluation utilized fivefold cross-validation with the following hyperparameters: Dropout rate 0.25, weight decay 5e-4, histopathology patch size 256 × 256, MIL attention heads 8. All experiments were conducted on a server equipped with one NVIDIA A800-80 GB GPU and a 32-core Intel Xeon Platinum 8358P CPU, with code implemented using the PyTorch framework based on Python 3.7.

Comparison of the C-index with other MIL models

We incorporate the following representative MIL approaches for comparison: (1) The classic work in the multi-instance learning domain, Attention-based MIL (ABMIL)26. (2) Two general pooling strategies discussed in ABMIL, namely mean-based aggregation and max-based aggregation, denoted as MeanMIL and MaxMIL respectively. (3) We also introduce the state-of-the-art Transformer-based approach TransMIL as a comparative model. The results in Fig. 5 demonstrate that the cross-validation results of our model (mean = 0.631) are much better than those of MeanMIL (mean = 0.540) and MaxMIL (mean = 0.501). Discussion

In this retrospective study, we established a weakly supervised multi-instance learning model. Our results demonstrated that a model based solely on pathological images could predict 3- and 5-year survival outcomes. Furthermore, an integrated model incorporating clinical variables showed superior performance in prognosticating these outcomes for postoperative cervical cancer patients, facilitating the implementation of personalised treatment strategies. Current research on cervical cancer prognosis prediction primarily focuses on analysing radiological images, screening biomarkers, and detecting abnormal cells in tissue sections for classification27–29. Few studies employing image analysis methods for cervical cancer pathology have utilised more advanced techniques, with existing approaches still exhibiting limitations.

Studies predicting cervical cancer prognosis have predominantly utilized radiomics, biomarker screening, or traditional cytomorphological analysis. In contrast, research involving deep mining of WSIs, which contain rich tumour microenvironment information, remains relatively scarce. Pathological images harbour substantial biological information with significant value for clinical diagnosis, prognostic prediction, treatment response forecasting, and identifying gene mutation sites30. For instance, predicting microsatellite instability (MSI) status using haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) images (AUC > 0.90) substantially reduces the cost of genetic testing31. Prognostic prediction models based on pathological images are continually being developed. By extracting quantitative spatial biological features from routine haematoxylin and eosin (HE) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) sections, these models overcome the subjective limitations of traditional morphological descriptions by pathologists. Wei Jiang et al. developed and validated a pathomic signature using whole-slide pathological images to assess prognosis and chemotherapy response in colorectal cancer32. Additionally, studies employing convolutional neural networks have quantified tumour microenvironments and cellular heterogeneity, delving deeper into image information to establish models capable of independently predicting survival outcomes33. Pathology-omics has demonstrated favourable predictive performance across breast, gastric, cervical, and hepatocellular carcinomas34–36.

To address the lack of detailed annotations in whole-slide images (WSIs)13,37–39, weakly supervised learning, particularly the multi-instance learning (MIL) framework, has become a mainstream solution. For example, Yang et al. partitioned regions of interest (ROIs) into 512 × 512-pixel patches and trained a multimodal model to predict recurrence risk in breast cancer patients40. However, the gigapixel size of WSIs and the absence of pixel-level annotations constrain the direct application of CNNs. Within CNN survival models, a single survival label corresponds to multiple image blocks, where only a small subset of these patches is pathologically relevant for predicting outcomes. This is problematic because a large proportion of patches in a WSI are uninformative (e.g., normal tissue), while only a small fraction are positive instances containing cancer cells. Consequently, without precise annotations, CNNs may be trained on a majority of mislabeled patches, which can degrade model performance41. To mitigate this, Yao et al. proposed DeepAttnMISL, an attention-based MIL model that incorporates pathological knowledge. Their approach represents each WSI as a set of instances (patches), which are then aggregated into a slide-level representation using an attention mechanism. An aggregation mechanism then synthesises information from all instances to produce the final prediction. The advantage of MIL lies in its robustness in annotation noise and its ability to address partial interpretability requirements—it can reveal key instances of driving model decisions (e.g., a specific tumour region). In contrast, previous end-to-end models often require fine-grained annotations and operate as “black boxes,” despite their strong feature extraction capabilities42.

The CLAM (Clustered Learning-agnostic Attentive Multiple Instance Learning) approach used in this study represents an innovative extension of Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) in digital pathology. It addresses key limitations of traditional MIL by integrating attention mechanisms and instance-level clustering. In recent years, traditional MIL has relied heavily on substantial labelled datasets (requiring thousands of whole slide images, WSIs), whereas CLAM achieves high accuracy with merely 10% training data (e.g., AUC = 0.94 in renal cell carcinoma subtype classification)13. Conventional MIL treats images as a collection of independent instances (image patches), disregarding spatial and semanticrelationships between instances. A key innovation of our study was the incorporation of global semantic information from the WSI as class position encoding into the TransMIL framework. This significantly enhances the model’s ability to model complex internal structures and semantic context within a single WSI, surpassing traditional methods that rely solely on spatial position or simple attention weights. The model framework not only provides accurate prognostic predictions, but its modular design also lays the groundwork for integrating multimodal data in future work. Concurrently, continuous advancements in deep learning are driving the evolution of prognostic models. Weakly supervised frameworks (e.g., CLAM) achieve a prognostic AUC of 0.87 in breast cancer43, while multi-scale Transformers (TransPath) enhance risk stratification across cancer types, achieving a C-index of 0.72. Integrating diverse information types substantially enhances prognostic model performance, making multimodal fusion a new trend. However, multimodal approaches remain underutilised in cervical cancer. Wang Weiping et al. developed CerviPro44, a deep learning-based multimodal prognostic model predicting disease-free survival in locally advanced cervical cancer patients undergoing radical radiotherapy. CerviPro demonstrated robust predictive performance in both internal validation (C-index 0.81) and external validation cohorts (C-index 0.70 and 0.66), effectively segregating patients into distinct high- and low-risk DFS cohorts, thereby demonstrating the application value of multimodal approaches. Future efforts should focus on establishing standardised workflows and advancing the integration of pathology, genomics, and imaging to unlock the prognostic prediction potential of digital pathology.

Additionally, this study employs a weakly supervised learning framework with inherent interpretability. Traditional “black-box” deep learning models constrain their translational application in clinical settings. Our adopted CLAM and its enhanced framework utilise attention mechanisms to automatically identify and weight regions within whole slide images (WSIs) most pertinent to prognosis. This capability allows the model not only to deliver predictions but also to visualise its decision-making basis through heatmaps. For instance, the model may assign high attention weights to tumour invasion fronts, specific areas of stromal reaction, or lymphocytic infiltration zones. This pixel-level interpretability links the model’s predictions to concrete pathological morphological features, providing pathologists with an intuitive validation tool. This facilitates the identification of novel morphological patterns associated with prognosis, thereby enhancing the biological plausibility and clinical translatability of the findings.

To assess the model’s robustness and generalisation potential on single-centre data, we conducted an internal diversity analysis of the training dataset. The analysis encompassed two levels: image visual statistics and deep feature distributions. At the image distribution level, low-order statistics such as colour (RGB channels), brightness, contrast, and saturation across all image patches exhibited broad and continuous distributions, with no extreme peak clustering. This reflects natural technical variation inherent in data acquisition. At the feature distribution level, principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the first two principal components explained only approximately 20% of the total feature variance (see Supplementary Material), indicating high semantic dimensionality and the absence of a single dominant pattern. Further unsupervised clustering (UMAP visualisation) revealed multiple uniformly distributed and highly intermingled semantic subspaces (see Supplementary Materials). Collectively, these findings confirm the dataset’s substantial intrinsic diversity in both visual appearance and deep-level semantics. This diversity provides the essential foundation for models to learn robust and generalisable features, thereby supporting the reliability of the current observational performance. However, this cannot substitute for rigorous external validation.

To our knowledge, this study represents the first application of context-aware multi-instance learning to analyse cervical cancer pathology images. The resulting demonstrated significant predictive performance for 3-year overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) (K-M curve p < 0.05), though 5-year prediction proved ineffective (p > 0.05). The probable causes encompass several aspects. The aggressiveness of the primary tumour is influenced by factors such as HPV integration status and depth of stromal invasion. Recurrence or death occurring beyond five years is predominantly attributable to HPV reinfection or other events, exhibiting diminished association with initial pathological characteristics45. Furthermore, as the proportion of non-tumour-related deaths significantly increases, traditional K-M analysis fails to differentiate causes of death, thereby reducing statistical power. Furthermore, the model’s performance for long-term prediction may have been limited by the cohort size and a low event rate during the five-year follow-up. Additionally, the statistical power of our study, particularly for DFS prediction, requires consideration. Although our cohort of 374 patients is comparable to several pioneering studies in computational pathology13,46, the observed DFS event rate was 17.1%. A low event rate can limit the model’s ability to identify robust prognostic features and may partly explain the relatively lower C-index for DFS (0.626) compared to OS (0.700). This may also be partly attributed to the inherent characteristics of the endpoint analysis methodology37. In the primary survival analysis of this study, we treated non-cervical cancer deaths as censored data. However, non-cancer mortality constitutes a significant competing risk for postoperative patients, particularly elderly individuals with comorbidities. When competing risk events are not negligible, traditional survival analysis may overestimate the cumulative recurrence risk and can potentially obscure the association between predictors and the true risk of recurrence, thereby compromising the model’s ability to discriminate recurrence events37,38. In future studies with larger sample sizes, employing competing risk models (such as the Fine-Gray model) for cumulative incidence function estimation and predictive modeling may help to more purely assess the model’s predictive efficacy for tumor recurrence risk and potentially enhance the discriminatory power of DFS predictions37,38.

The model performance in this study still holds room for improvement. Its current performance results from the combined effects of data characteristics, algorithmic capabilities, and fusion strategies, rather than being attributable to any single factor. At the data level, although this study included 374 patients—a scale comparable to several pioneering works in computational pathology—a larger sample size, especially one containing more event cases, would help learn more robust and subtle prognostic features from highly heterogeneous whole-slide images. In addition, the relatively low event rate for disease-free survival (17.1%) in this cohort, while consistent with the favorable clinical prognosis after radical surgery for early-stage cervical cancer, directly limits the model’s ability to learn discriminative signals from the minority recurrence pattern, thereby imposing a theoretical upper bound on model performance. At the algorithmic level, the ResNet-18 model pre-trained on natural images (ImageNet), as adopted in this study, may not be optimized for histopathological images. Research has shown that models pre-trained on large-scale pathological image datasets can extract more pathology-specific features, which may improve downstream task performance. Meanwhile, despite our enhancements to the TransMIL framework through the introduction of global context, its core attention-based aggregation mechanism may still be suboptimal for identifying critical biological “micro-patterns”—such as specific intercellular interactions—that are sparsely distributed across local regions. Exploring network architectures capable of explicitly modeling multi-scale relationships and cellular topological structures could represent an important direction for future breakthroughs. At the fusion strategy level, this study employs a “late-stage fusion” scheme: an imaging-based risk score is first generated independently and then incorporated as a fixed covariate alongside clinical factors into a Cox model. While this approach is intuitive and decouples the imaging and clinical models, it may not fully capture the complex nonlinear interactions between imaging features and clinical variables. In contrast, more advanced deep fusion strategies—such as integrating deep features with clinical variables at intermediate neural network layers—have been shown to achieve superior performance in prognostic modeling of other cancers13,32,47.

To overcome these limitations, we have outlined clear future research directions: 1) Promoting multicentre collaboration: We are establishing partnerships with several major domestic medical centres to build a high-quality, multicentre validation cohort. This will facilitate assessment of the model’s true generalisability across different technical and demographic contexts while reducing selection bias. 2) Expanding sample size and extending follow-up periods: By pooling multi-centre resources to increase sample size, particularly incorporating more high-risk cases with recurrent events, and prolonging follow-up durations to accumulate additional endpoint events. This will enhance the model’s statistical power and robustness. 3) Employ domain adaptation techniques: At the algorithmic level, we will explore the use of colour normalisation and domain adaptation methods to mitigate technical bias arising from differences in specimen preparation and scanning protocols across centres, thereby enhancing the model’s robustness.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a multi-instance learning model based on pathological features and clinical risk factors that effectively predicts 3-year and 5-year survival outcomes for patients undergoing surgical treatment for cervical cancer.
