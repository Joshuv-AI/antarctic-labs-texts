Prevalence of Kinesiophobia in Patients with Inflammatory Bowel Disease: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Yue Yang, Min Dong, Yaping Xu, Rongrong Ma, Feifei Shi, Congyan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInflammatory bowel disease (IBD) patients often exhibit low adherence to physical activity, potentially influenced by kinesiophobia. While kinesiophobia has been extensively studied in musculoskeletal and cardiovascular populations, its prevalence and patterns in IBD patients remain underexplored. This study aimed to explore the prevalence and distribution patterns of kinesiophobia in patients with IBD and identify factors associated with different kinesiophobia profiles.MethodsThis cross-sectional study recruited 222 IBD patients from a tertiary care hospital in China between January 2023 and September 2024. Kinesiophobia was assessed using the Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia-11 (TSK-11). Latent profile analysis was conducted to identify kinesiophobia subgroups, with additional assessments of disease activity, self-management behavior, self-efficacy, and fatigue. Multinomial logistic regression identified factors associated with profile membership.ResultsAmong 222 IBD patients, 27.5% exhibited moderate-to-high kinesiophobia (TSK-11 ≥29). Latent profile analysis identified three distinct groups: Low-level Kinesiophobia-Cognitive Adaptation (51.4%), Medium-level Kinesiophobia-Somatic Symptom (28.8%), and High-level Kinesiophobia-Comprehensive Distress (19.8%). Complication presence was associated with both medium-level (OR=6.22, 95% CI: 2.63–14.69) and high-level profile membership (OR=4.82, 95% CI: 1.37–16.98), while disease remission showed protective effects against elevated kinesiophobia (OR=0.15 and 0.06 for medium- and high-level profiles, respectively). Fatigue severity emerged as a strong predictor of both medium- (OR=1.45, 95% CI: 1.21–1.73) and high-level profiles (OR=2.40, 95% CI: 1.81–3.18). Enhanced self-management capacity demonstrated protective effects against high-level profile (OR=0.92, 95% CI: 0.86–0.98).ConclusionThis study revealed that 27.5% of IBD patients exhibited moderate-to-high kinesiophobia and identified three distinct profiles. Disease remission was a protective factor, while complications and fatigue severity were associated with elevated kinesiophobia. Routine kinesiophobia screening and profile-based interventions should be integrated into IBD care to improve physical activity engagement.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the gastrointestinal tract with an unclear etiology.1 While pharmacological treatments remain the cornerstone for maintaining disease remission, non-pharmacological interventions, particularly physical activity, have gained recognition for their potential to enhance patients’ quality of life and improve disease outcomes.2,3 International IBD organizations recognize exercise as a crucial protective factor, recommending regular physical activity for all patients with these conditions.4

However, IBD patients often exhibit low adherence to physical activity regimens. Barriers such as fatigue, abdominal discomfort, and concerns about exacerbating gastrointestinal symptoms contribute to this reluctance. Additionally, psychological factors such as kinesiophobia, a fear of movement rooted in the anticipation of pain or injury, can further discourage participation in physical activity. Studies have shown that IBD patients engage in significantly less physical activity compared to healthy individuals.5 After receiving an IBD diagnosis, 33% of patients believe the condition restricts their ability to exercise, and 17.6% even discontinue at least one sport or physical activity.6 Consequently, only a small proportion of IBD patients engage in regular exercise. The causes of kinesiophobia in IBD patients is complicated, potentially driven by legitimate concerns regarding symptom exacerbation, pain experiences, and fatigue-related limitations.7 Gastrointestinal symptoms such as abdominal pain, diarrhea, and extraintestinal complications may create legitimate apprehensions about physical activity engagement.8 While kinesiophobia has been extensively studied in populations with musculoskeletal disorders and cardiovascular diseases, its prevalence and impact in IBD patients remain underexplored. Identifying kinesiophobia profiles within this population is clinically relevant, as fear-avoidance behaviors have been shown to be modifiable through targeted interventions.9 Recognition of different fear patterns may enable clinicians to tailor exercise prescriptions and psychological support strategies, thereby reducing avoidance behaviors and promoting safe physical activity engagement in IBD patients. The limited research available has primarily relied on total scores or binary cutoffs, which may obscure meaningful subgroup differences. Latent profile analysis, a person-centered approach, can identify distinct kinesiophobia subgroups based on individual response patterns, potentially informing profile-specific interventions.

Therefore, this cross-sectional study aims to characterize kinesiophobia profiles among IBD patients using latent profile analysis, examining the prevalence and distribution patterns of movement-related fears and identifying factors associated with different kinesiophobia profiles within this population.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This cross-sectional investigation was conducted between January 2023 and September 2024 at a tertiary care hospital in Bengbu, Anhui Province, China. Patients were recruited using convenience sampling from the gastroenterology inpatient department. The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of Bengbu Medical College (approval number: [2024]270). All participants provided voluntary informed consent prior to enrollment.

Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) confirmed IBD diagnosis; (2) age ≥18 years; (3) disease duration ≥6 months; (4) ability to read and comprehend questionnaire materials. Exclusion criteria included: (1) presence of severe complications such as intestinal obstruction or perforation; (2) documented psychiatric disorders affecting cognitive capacity.

Sample size was determined according to guidelines for latent profile analysis and regression modeling. A minimum of 10–15 participants per observed variable was recommended for stable latent profile solutions.10 Considering 11 indicator variables for the TSK-11 and approximately 10 predictor variables in multinomial regression, a minimum sample of 186 participants was required.

Assessment Instruments

(1) Demographic and Clinical Data Collection A researcher-developed questionnaire captured demographic characteristics including gender, age, occupation, educational attainment, monthly household income, insurance coverage, residence location, living arrangements, and marital status. Clinical variables encompassed disease subtype, disease duration, hospitalization frequency, disease activity status, complication presence, surgical history, and biologic therapy utilization.

(2) Disease activity assessment employed validated condition-specific instruments. The Simple Clinical Colitis Activity Index (SCCAI) was used for ulcerative colitis patients, with scores ≤4 indicating remission and scores >4 indicating active disease. Active ulcerative colitis was further stratified as mild (SCCAI 5–7), moderate (SCCAI 8–11), and severe (SCCAI ≥12) according to the modified Truelove and Witts severity classification. Crohn’s disease activity was evaluated using the Harvey - Bradshaw Index (HBI), with scores <5 indicating remission, 5–7 mild activity, 8–16 moderate activity, and >16 severe activity.11 For combined analyses, disease activity was categorized into three groups: remission (SCCAI ≤4 or HBI <5), mild activity (SCCAI 5–7 or HBI 5–7), and moderate-to-severe activity (SCCAI ≥8 or HBI ≥8).

(3) The Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia-11 (TSK-11) was used to assess kinesiophobia.12 This scale comprises 11 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree” (scored 4–1). Total scores range from 11–44. Based on previous established severity categories, kinesiophobia was classified as minimal (TSK-11 ≤22), low (TSK-11 23–28), moderate (TSK-11 29–35), and high (TSK-11 ≥36).13 Moderate-to-high kinesiophobia (TSK-11 ≥29) was considered clinically relevant. The Chinese version demonstrates strong psychometric properties with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.883.14

(4) The IBD Self-Management Behavior Scale (IBD-SMBS) was used to evaluate patient self-regulatory behaviors across seven domains: medication management, dietary management, disease monitoring, emotional management, exercise management, daily life management, and resource utilization. The 36-item instrument utilizes a 5-point Likert scale (5=“always” to 1=“never”), yielding total scores of 36–180, with higher scores reflecting superior self-management capacity.

(5) The IBD Fatigue Scale (IBD-F) was used to assess fatigue severity and impact.15 The instrument comprises two quantitative sections that are scored separately: Part I (5 items) measures fatigue severity and frequency using a 5-point Likert scale (0–20 points), with higher scores indicating more severe fatigue; Part II (30 items) evaluates fatigue impact on daily functioning (0–120 points), higher scores indicating greater functional impact. Both sections were analyzed independently in this study, as recommended by the scale developers.

(6) The IBD Self-Efficacy Scale (IBD-SES) was used to evaluate disease-specific self-efficacy across four dimensions: stress and emotional management (9 items), medical care management (8 items), symptom management (7 items), and remission maintenance management (5 items).16 The 29-item instrument employs a 10-point Likert scale (10=“completely confident” to 1=“completely unconfident”), with total scores ranging from 29–290.

Data Collection Procedures

Data collection was performed by the principal investigator through face-to-face interviews. Prior to questionnaire administration, participants received detailed explanations regarding study objectives and procedures. Clinical information was extracted from electronic medical records, with disease activity assessments conducted by attending physicians. All questionnaires underwent immediate review upon completion, with any identified omissions or ambiguities clarified directly with participants to ensure data completeness.

Complication presence was defined as the occurrence of IBD-related complications including extraintestinal manifestations (such as arthritis, dermatological manifestations, and ocular complications) and intestinal complications (such as perianal disease including fistulas and abscesses). Severe acute complications requiring emergency intervention such as intestinal obstruction or perforation were exclusion criteria.

Statistical Analysis

Latent profile analysis was conducted using Mplus 8.3 software to identify kinesiophobia subgroups within the IBD population. The 11 TSK items served as manifest indicators, with models testing 1–5 latent profiles. Model selection utilized multiple fit indices: Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and adjusted BIC (aBIC), with lower values indicating superior model fit. Entropy values approaching 1.0 signified greater classification accuracy. The Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMRT) and Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT) determined optimal profile number, with p<0.05 indicating that the k-profile model significantly outperformed the (k-1)-profile alternative.

Descriptive and inferential analyses were performed using SPSS 29.0. Continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviations, while categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons employed analysis of variance for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Multinomial logistic regression analysis identified factors associated with kinesiophobia profile membership. Statistical significance was established at p<0.05.

Results

Participant Characteristics and Kinesiophobia Prevalence

230 patients were initially approached, and 8 were excluded due to incomplete questionnaire responses (>20% missing data), resulting in 222 IBD patients included in the final analysis, yielding an effective response rate of 96.5%. The mean participant age was 35.1 ± 13.4 years, with 141 (63.5%) male participants. Ulcerative colitis represented 52.7% (n=117) of cases, while Crohn’s disease accounted for 47.3% (n=105). Disease duration ranged from 6 months to over 10 years, with 64.4% (n=143) of participants experiencing disease duration between 1–5 years. Thirty-three patients (14.9%) had undergone IBD-related surgery, among whom 10 had stomas.

The overall kinesiophobia score was 25.9 ± 5.7. Based on TSK-11 severity categories, 88 participants (39.6%) demonstrated minimal kinesiophobia (≤22), 73 (32.9%) low (23–28), 40 (18.0%) moderate (29–35), and 21 (9.5%) high (≥36) kinesiophobia. Overall, 61 participants (27.5%) exhibited moderate-to-high kinesiophobia (TSK-11 ≥29). Self-management behavior scores averaged 114.7 ± 9.4, self-efficacy scores averaged 170.8 ± 21.8, and fatigue severity (IBD-F Section I) scores averaged 9.2 ± 3.5, and fatigue impact (IBD-F Section II) scores averaged 54.4 ± 12.4.

Fatigue severity measured by IBD-F Section I emerged as a robust predictor of elevated kinesiophobia profiles, with each unit increase in fatigue severity score associated with 45% increased odds of medium-level membership (OR=1.45, 95% CI: 1.21–1.73, p<0.001) and 140% increased odds of high-level membership (OR=2.40, 95% CI: 1.81–3.18, p<0.001). Enhanced self-management capacity demonstrated protective effects against high-level profile membership (OR=0.92, 95% CI: 0.86–0.98, p=0.015), though this association did not reach statistical significance for medium-level profile (OR=0.96, 95% CI: 0.91–1.01, p=0.087).

Discussion

The observed kinesiophobia prevalence in this study was 27.5% (moderate-to-high, TSK-11 ≥29), which was relatively lower than the findings from cardiovascular rehabilitation literature (61.0%), but similar fear-avoidance behaviors compromise exercise engagement and therapeutic outcomes.17 Prospective studies in musculoskeletal populations have demonstrated that kinesiophobia is a risk factor for poor clinical outcomes and is associated with reduced physical function and quality of life.18,19 Importantly, these fear-avoidance behaviors have been shown to be modifiable through targeted interventions. However, the IBD-specific manifestation appears distinct from purely pain-driven kinesiophobia documented in orthopedic populations. While musculoskeletal kinesiophobia typically stems from injury recurrence concerns, IBD patients demonstrate more complex fear patterns including gastrointestinal symptom exacerbation, fatigue amplification, and disease activity triggers.20,21 The identified somatic symptom profile particularly reflects IBD-specific pathophysiology, wherein inflammatory complications generate legitimate exercise concerns that may evolve into maladaptive avoidance behaviors.22

This study identified three latent profiles of kinesiophobia among patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD): low, moderate, and high levels of fear. The majority of patients (51.4%) were classified into the low-level group, characterized by lower kinesiophobia scores, higher levels of self-management abilities, and a greater proportion of patients in remission. The medium-level group (28.8%) exhibited a higher incidence of complications such as arthritis and perianal disease, leading to increased concerns about exercise. The high-level group (19.82%) had the highest kinesiophobia scores, associated with more severe disease activity, as well as higher levels of fatigue, which limited their physical activity and increased their fear of movement.23,24

Multinomial regression analysis revealed several factors associated with kinesiophobia profile membership. IBD patients with complications were more likely to belong to the medium-level and high-level groups. Complications often involve pain, which is a common symptom in IBD patients. The recurrence of complications or pain can significantly impact both the physical and psychological well-being of patients.25 Additionally, patients may lack proper knowledge about exercise and harbor concerns that physical activity could exacerbate symptoms or trigger disease flare-ups, leading to heightened kinesiophobia levels.26 Conversely, patients in remission with better self-management abilities was more likely to belong to the low-level kinesiophobia group.27 Effective self-management behaviors enable patients to engage in personalized exercise routines tailored to disease activity, thereby reducing fear and promoting physical activity. Moreover, fatigue severity was closely associated with kinesiophobia levels. Fatigue is one of the most common extra-intestinal symptoms in IBD patients and cannot be alleviated by rest.28 It can lead to decreased physical activity, muscle weakness, functional impairment, reduced motivation, and cognitive decline. Our study indicates that patients with higher fatigue levels tend to belong to the medium-to-high kinesiophobia groups. This relationship suggests that fatigue may exacerbate fear of movement, creating a vicious cycle that further limits physical activity and negatively impacts quality of life.

Kinesiophobia is a significant predictor of physical activity levels and quality of life.29 Reducing kinesiophobia can facilitate the transition from sedentary behavior to active physical activity. The International Organization for the Study of Inflammatory Bowel Diseases (IOIBD) emphasizes that exercise is a crucial non-pharmacological intervention for IBD patients and should be encouraged for all patients.4 Therefore, healthcare providers should assess patients’ complications early, assist them in actively coping with the disease, modify their perceptions of exercise, enhance exercise adherence, reduce kinesiophobia, and improve health outcomes.28 Personalized exercise prescriptions should be developed based on disease activity, and multidisciplinary teams should be established to strengthen exercise supervision and management.30 Encouraging patients to maintain exercise diaries and fostering a correct understanding of the relationship between disease and exercise are essential strategies to reduce kinesiophobia levels and improve patients’ quality of life.31

The novelty of this study lies in the application of latent profile analysis to examine kinesiophobia in IBD patients, moving beyond total scores or predefined cutoffs used in previous research. The three identified profiles differed not only in severity but also in their underlying characteristics, suggesting that kinesiophobia in IBD involves qualitatively different fear configurations that support a profile-based approach to clinical assessment and intervention.

Several limitations also should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the relationships between clinical factors and kinesiophobia profiles. Self-reported measures may be subject to recall bias. The convenience sampling from a single tertiary care center may limit generalizability to broader IBD populations. Additionally, the limited number of patients with surgical history and stomas prevented meaningful analysis of the relationship between surgical factors and kinesiophobia, which warrants investigation in future studies with larger surgical cohorts.

Conclusion

This study identified three distinct kinesiophobia profiles among IBD patients, with over a quarter exhibiting moderate-to-high levels. Disease remission and self-management capacity were associated with lower kinesiophobia, while complication presence and fatigue severity were associated with elevated profiles. These findings highlight the potential value of kinesiophobia screening in IBD care.
