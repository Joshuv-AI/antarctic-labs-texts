Neuromelanin-sensitive MRI for identifying dopamine transporter imaging abnormality risk in idiopathic rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder
Authors: Jae Rim Kim, Beomseok Sohn, Jeong Won Jo, Hwan Heo, Soohwa Song, Eung Yeop Kim, Eun Yeon Joo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neuromelanin-sensitive magnetic resonance imaging (NM-MRI) enables non-invasive assessment of the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc). However, its utility in identifying dopaminergic dysfunction in idiopathic rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (iRBD) remains unclear. This study evaluated NM-MRI alterations in the SNpc, stratified by dopamine transporter (DaT) imaging status, to assess its performance in detecting early dopaminergic alterations. This combined retrospective and prospective study included 66 patients with iRBD (45 males, 67.0 ± 6.3 years) and 30 healthy controls (HCs) (11 males, 63.6 ± 6.5 years). Patients with iRBD were divided based on 18F-FP-CIT PET findings into those with abnormal (iRBD-CIT+) and normal (iRBD-CIT−) DaT imaging. NM metrics, including volume, signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and contrast-to-noise ratio, were quantified using a template-based semi-automated method in the whole SNpc and its subregions (sensorimotor, associative, and limbic). Among the 66 patients with iRBD, 37 (56.1%) were classified as iRBD-CIT+. They exhibited significantly reduced NM metrics across the whole SNpc and all subregions compared to HCs. Notably, NM volume and SNR in the associative and limbic area were significantly lower in the iRBD-CIT+ group than in the iRBD-CIT− group, whereas other metrics did not differ. ROC analysis revealed fair performance for NM volume and SNR (AUC 0.73 and 0.75, respectively) in discriminating iRBD-CIT+ from iRBD-CIT−. Multivariable regression analyses confirmed these metrics as independent discriminators of DaT abnormalities. NM-MRI metrics, particularly limbic NM volume and SNR, serve as potential biomarkers for dopaminergic alterations in iRBD. These findings support NM-MRI as a strategic, radiation-free triage tool to identify high-risk patients requiring DaT imaging in iRBD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11682-026-01176-0.

Introduction

Idiopathic rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder (iRBD) is characterized by vivid dreams accompanied by vocalizations or complex motor behaviors during REM sleep (Iranzo et al., 2016). Its prevalence in the general population ranges from 0.68% to 1.18%, with a reported rate of 1.4% in Korea (Cicero et al., 2021; Haba-Rubio et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2023). iRBD is increasingly recognized as a prodromal manifestation of neurodegenerative diseases, particularly alpha-synucleinopathies such as Parkinson’s disease (PD) (Liu et al., 2022; Miyamoto & Miyamoto, 2018). Various biomarkers have been investigated using imaging techniques, polysomnography, and clinical assessments to assess the risk of phenoconversion in iRBD (de Natale et al., 2022).

Among these, dopamine transporter (DaT) imaging, such as positron emission tomography (PET) and single-photon emission tomography, is considered the most promising imaging modality (Palermo et al., 2021). However, its limited accessibility and the exposure to ionizing radiation have prompted efforts to develop magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) based alternatives.

The substantia nigra (SN), particularly nigrosome 1, is a midbrain region rich in dopaminergic neurons that play a pivotal role in the early stages of PD (Sung et al., 2018). Several studies have explored the structural and functional changes in this area as potential markers of iRBD phenoconversion (Miglis et al., 2021). Neuromelanin-sensitive MRI (NM-MRI) has emerged as a promising technique for evaluating the SN pars compacta (SNpc) in both PD and iRBD (Sasaki et al., 2006). This modality enables quantitative analysis of the SNpc, allowing the detection of subtle changes and longitudinal monitoring of disease progression. Previous longitudinal studies have reported alterations in the signal intensity and distribution patterns within the SNpc that correlate with PD progression and clinical symptoms (Biondetti et al., 2020; Gaurav et al., 2021). Similarly, prior studies have consistently demonstrated reduced SNpc volume and signal intensity on NM-MRI in patients with iRBD compared with healthy controls (HCs), supporting its potential as an early imaging biomarker (Pyatigorskaya et al., 2017). However, most prior studies have primarily focused on case-control comparisons and have not accounted for the biological heterogeneity within the iRBD population, particularly with respect to underlying dopaminergic dysfunction.

As a result, it remains unclear whether NM-MRI alterations reflect true nigrostriatal degeneration or are influenced by the inclusion of patients with preserved DaT function. To date, few studies have directly evaluated NM-MRI findings in iRBD in relation to DaT imaging status, which represents a critical reference standard for dopaminergic integrity.

Furthermore, previous NM-MRI studies in iRBD have largely relied on manual or ROI-based approaches, and detailed subregional analyses of the SNpc have been limited. Given the known topographic progression of nigral degeneration, such approaches may not fully capture region-specific vulnerability associated with early dopaminergic changes.

In this study, we aimed to investigate NM-MRI alterations in patients with iRBD stratified according to DaT imaging status. To address the limitations of prior studies, we implemented a template-guided semi-automated quantification pipeline to objectively measure NM metrics. In addition, we performed a subregional analysis of the SNpc, segmenting it into sensorimotor, associative, and limbic territories, to explore region-specific patterns of neurodegeneration associated with dopaminergic dysfunction.

Methods

Study design

In this mixed retrospective and prospective observational study, we recruited patients with abnormal sleep behavior who underwent overnight polysomnography at a tertiary hospital between December 2018 and September 2023. The cohort comprised 47 patients evaluated retrospectively (prior to December 2022), and 19 patients enrolled prospectively (from January 2023, onward).

The inclusion criteria were based on the clinical diagnostic criteria for iRBD as defined in the third edition of the International Classification of Sleep Disorders (Sateia, 2014). To ensure a pure iRBD cohort, we rigorously excluded patients with any evidence of parkinsonism. For the retrospective cohort, the absence of parkinsonism was confirmed through comprehensive clinical and neurological examinations conducted by an experienced neurologist (E.Y.J.) on the first and second visits followed by polysomnography. For the prospective cohort, this was defined as a Movement Disorder Society–Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale Part III (MDS-UPDRS-III) ≤ 6 (Goetz et al., 2008; Skorvanek et al., 2018). Patients with dementia, secondary RBD, or contraindications to MRI were excluded. Of the 71 participants initially screened, 66 were included in the final analysis; two were excluded due to clinical parkinsonism and three due to MRI-related issues.

For comparison, age-matched HCs were recruited via advertisements. Eligibility for HCs was determined through in-person clinical interviews with structured screening questionnaires and neurological examinations to verify the absence of neurological disorders or sleep-related complaints. The HCs did not undergo polysomnography or 18F-FP-CIT PET, and participants with significant structural brain abnormalities detected by MRI were excluded. Of the 31 initially recruited HCs, 30 were included in the final analysis (one was excluded due to an MRI processing error).

Clinical assessments

Participants enrolled prospectively after 2023 underwent the Korean Mini-Mental State Examination (K-MMSE) to assess cognition (Kang et al., 1997) and the MDS-UPDRS-III to evaluate parkinsonian motor features. Participants who developed neurological symptoms during the follow-up period, such as parkinsonism, were classified as having undergone a phenoconversion.

Imaging acquisition

All patients underwent brain MRI, including NM-sensitive sequencing and 18F-FP-CIT PET. Brain 18F-FP-CIT PET images were obtained using a PET/computed tomography (CT) scanner (Discovery STE, GE Healthcare, USA), 120 min after an injection of 185 MBq 18F-FP-CIT. The PET images were acquired for 10 min in a three-dimensional mode immediately after a brain CT scan for attenuation correction and image fusion. CT scanning was performed at 140 kVp and 50 mAs with a slice thickness of 3.75 mm. The 18F-FP-CIT PET images were reconstructed with a VUE POINT algorithm using a 128 × 128 matrix. Nuclear medicine specialists visually rated each 18F-FP-CIT PET scan as normal or abnormal. Based on the 18F-FP-CIT PET results, iRBD patients were classified into two groups: those with normal DaT imaging (iRBD-CIT−) and those with abnormal DaT imaging (iRBD-CIT+).

All MRI scans were performed using a 3T Philips Ingenia CX system (Philips Healthcare). The imaging protocol included whole-brain 3D T1-weighted (T1w) and NM-sensitive imaging. T1w sagittal images were acquired using a magnetization-prepared gradient echo protocol with the following parameters: repetition time (TR), 9.9 ms; echo time (TE), 4.6 ms; acquisition matrix, 240 × 240; voxel size 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 mm3, and acquisition time, 3:30 min. NM-sensitive images were obtained using SandwichNM protocol with the following parameters: TR, 60 ms; TE, 4 ms; acquisition matrix, 320 × 320; voxel size, 0.8 × 0.8 × 1.2 mm3, and acquisition time, 5:30 min.

Template-guided semi-automated quantification of neuromelanin

To ensure objective and reproducible analysis, we implemented a template-guided semi-automated quantification pipeline for NM-MRI (Fig. 1). First, to ensure accurate spatial alignment, a stepwise registration strategy was applied. T1w images, given their higher anatomical contrast and spatial resolution, were used as the reference for spatial normalization. For each subject, NM images were first co-registered to the corresponding T1w images. The T1w images were then spatially normalized to a study-specific template, and the resulting transformation parameters were subsequently applied to the co-registered NM images to bring all NM data into a common template space. Following this registration process, standard preprocessing steps—including noise reduction, skull stripping, and bias field correction—were performed. In the template space, the periaqueductal gray (PAG) reference region was manually delineated by an experienced neuroradiologist (E.Y.K., 25 years of experience). This manual annotation was performed once on the template and subsequently applied to all subjects through spatial normalization, thereby minimizing inter-subject variability in ROI definition.

Second, the predefined template ROIs, including the PAG reference region and SNpc mask were automatically propagated to each subject. NM hyperintense regions were then extracted using a threshold-based algorithm referenced to the PAG signal. NM metrics, including volume, signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR), were automatically computed for the whole SNpc and its subregions. NM volumes were normalized to intracranial volumes derived from T1w images.

Finally, visual quality control was performed for all subjects to ensure appropriate spatial alignment and accurate ROI propagation. A detailed description of the preprocessing steps, deep learning-based structural segmentation (Base Swin UNETR) (Hatamizadeh et al., 2022), and mathematical formulations is provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Subregional definition of the SNpc

The SNpc was further subdivided into associative, limbic, and sensorimotor subregions based on the known topographical organization of the nigrostriatal pathway. In this framework, the posterolateral SNpc predominantly projects to the sensorimotor striatum, the intermediate region to the associative striatum, and the anteromedial region to limbic territories, as described in prior anatomical and imaging studies (Biondetti et al., 2021).

In the present study, the SNpc mask defined in template space was partitioned into three subregions along its principal anatomical axis to approximate these functional territories. Specifically, this axis was defined as the curved longitudinal centerline extending the anteromedial to the posterolateral pole of the SNpc. To approximate the functional territories, the SNpc mask was geometrically divided into three approximately equal-volume segments along this axis. The boundaries between subregions were determined based on their relative anatomical location (Figure S1): the anteromedial third (limbic; 190 voxels, 97.28 mm³), the intermediate third (associative; 190 voxels, 97.28 mm³), and the posterolateral third (sensorimotor; 198 voxels, 101.38 mm³), rather than voxel-wise connectivity information. The subregional masks were defined in template space and subsequently propagated to individual subjects through spatial normalization, ensuring consistent ROI definition across subjects. Visual quality control was performed to confirm appropriate alignment and anatomical plausibility in each case.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Group comparisons of demographic variables were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test or Fisher’s exact test. To compare NM metrics among groups while adjusting for potential confounders, we performed analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) and rank ANCOVA, including age and sex as covariates. Pairwise post-hoc comparisons were conducted using the Bonferroni correction to account for multiple tests. Partial eta-squared (ηp2) was calculated to measure the effect sizes for group comparisons, adjusting for age and sex. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to examine the associations between the NM-MRI metrics and clinical variables. For these analyses, the combined bilateral (both) SNpc value for each individual patient was used as the unit of analysis.

To assess the diagnostic performance of NM-MRI in identifying DaT abnormalities, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and areas under the curve (AUC) were calculated for the whole and subsegmental SNpc metrics. For the ROC analyses, NM-MRI metrics were selected based on the lateral distribution of DaT abnormalities on 18F-FP-CIT PET. Specifically, right-sided DaT abnormalities were analyzed using the NM values from the right SNpc derived from patients with either right-only or bilateral abnormalities. Similarly, left-sided DaT abnormalities were analyzed using the left SNpc values from patients with left-only or bilateral abnormalities. For patients with bilateral abnormalities, both the left and right SNpc values were included. This approach was employed to preserve the lateralized signal changes and avoid potential dilutions caused by averaging effects.

Additionally, binary logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate whether NM-MRI could discriminate DaT abnormalities, adjusting for age and sex. In these models, each NM metric was included as a continuous variable using its original measurement scale, as detailed in the descriptive tables. Age was entered as a continuous variable and sex as a categorical variable. Collinearity testing, assessed via variance inflation factors, revealed no multicollinearity across all models.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.1), with p < 0.05 considered significant.

The SNpc was segmented into the associative, limbic, and sensorimotor subregions. Subregional analyses revealed consistent patterns (Fig. 2 and Table S1). Compared to HCs, the iRBD-CIT+ group had significantly lower NM volume and CNR across all subregions, alongside reduced SNR in the limbic (p = 0.030) and sensorimotor (p = 0.002) areas. When comparing the two iRBD groups, the iRBD-CIT+ group presented more pronounced alterations; SNR was significantly lower across all subregions (p = 0.023 for associative, p = 0.021 for limbic, p = 0.037 for left sensorimotor), and NM volume was reduced in both the associative (p = 0.047) and limbic (p = 0.031) regions. Consistent with the whole SNpc findings, the iRBD-CIT− group did not differ significantly from HCs in any subregional metric.

The diagnostic performances of the NM-MRI metrics for discriminating abnormal 18F-FP-CIT PET images are shown in Fig. 3 and Table S2. Among whole SNpc metrics, SNR demonstrated the highest AUCs for distinguishing iRBD-CIT+ from iRBD-CIT− (AUC 0.70–0.75), followed by volume (AUC 0.68–0.73) and CNR (AUC 0.62–0.66). In the multivariable logistic regression analysis adjusting for age and sex, NM volume and SNR remained significant discriminators for iRBD-CIT+ status (Odds ratio [OR] = 0.24, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.05–0.91, p = 0.046 for volume; OR = 0.80, 95% CI = 0.64–0.96, p = 0.027 for SNR), whereas CNR did not reach statistical significance (Table S3).

Subregional analyses showed consistent diagnostic trends. Sensorimotor and limbic areas generally yielded the highest AUCs for SNR and volume, respectively (Fig. 3 and Table S4).

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, only volume and SNR of the limbic subregion emerged as significant discriminators for iRBD-CIT+ status (OR = 0.01, 95% CI = 0.00–0.66, p = 0.044 for volume; OR = 0.83, 95% CI = 0.68–0.98, p = 0.027 for SNR) (Table S5).

Correlation analysis with clinical parameters

Correlation analysis revealed that both volume and SNR of the limbic subregion were weakly and negatively correlated with disease duration (ρ = -0.12, p = 0.028 for volume; ρ = -0.15, p = 0.026 for SNR). However, these associations did not persist after group stratification.

No significant correlations were observed between NM-MRI metrics (whole or subregional SNpc) and other clinical features, including K-MMSE and MDS-UPDRS III scores, irrespective of 18F-FP-CIT PET findings.

During follow-up, two patients with iRBD progressed to a clinical diagnosis of PD. Notably, both patients belonged to the iRBD-CIT+ group at baseline.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that semi-automated NM-MRI metrics capture nigrostriatal degeneration in iRBD, specifically in those with dopaminergic deficits. The iRBD-CIT+ group exhibited significant reductions in NM metrics across the SNpc compared to HCs, with NM volume and SNR further differentiating them from the iRBD-CIT− group. Volume and SNR, particularly in the limbic area, emerged as independent discriminators of DaT abnormalities, highlighting NM-MRI’s potential as a screening tool prior to DaT scans.

Our findings highlight that the degree of NM-MRI alterations is dependent on the presence of underlying dopaminergic degeneration in patients with iRBD. Specifically, while we observed significant reductions in all NM metrics within the SNpc in the iRBD-CIT+ group, these metrics remained relatively preserved in the iRBD-CIT− group, showing no significant differences from HCs. Such findings provide a plausible explanation for the discrepancies observed in previous studies. While some studies have reported NM-MRI changes in iRBD similar to our findings (Fu et al., 2024; Pyatigorskaya et al., 2017; Takahashi et al., 2022), others have failed to show significant differences (Pasquini et al., 2024). This inconsistency may be attributable to a dilution effect in prior cohorts that included a relatively high proportion (62.5%) of patients with preserved dopaminergic function. This underscores the importance of stratifying patients with iRBD based on their underlying dopaminergic status to accurately evaluate the stage of neurodegeneration.

Patients with PD exhibit a gradient pattern of NM loss in the SNpc, beginning in the posterolateral sensorimotor region and progressing toward the anteromedial associative and limbic regions (Biondetti et al., 2021). Based on this established pattern, we extended our analysis by segmenting the SNpc to investigate region-specific changes. The iRBD-CIT+ group showed significant reductions in NM volume and signal intensity across all subregions, except for SNR in the associative area, compared to HCs, even after adjusting for age and sex. Furthermore, NM volume and SNR in the associative and limbic areas, but not in the sensorimotor area, were significantly reduced in the iRBD-CIT+ group, compared to the iRBD-CIT− group.

Collectively, these findings highlight subregional heterogeneity within the SNpc and support the concept of region-specific vulnerability. This regional specificity suggests an association between NM alterations in the anteromedial SNpc and detectable dopaminergic dysfunction on DaT imaging. We hypothesize that this regional vulnerability might reflect a staged pattern in iRBD, wherein the anteromedial involvement of the SNpc may reflect more extensive nigral pathology. Importantly, however, our cross-sectional design does not allow inference about temporal ordering or phenoconversion. Alternatively, the numerically longer disease duration in the iRBD-CIT+ group could contribute to broader subregional involvement, although this difference was not statistically significant. Longitudinal studies with larger cohorts, ideally with quantitative DaT metrics, are needed to determine whether limbic SNpc alterations track disease stage or predict clinical conversion.

Previous studies have established the utility of NM-MRI in differentiating patients with iRBD from HC, reporting AUCs ranging from 0.76 to 0.93 for NM volume and intensity (Fu et al., 2024; Pyatigorskaya et al., 2017; Takahashi et al., 2022). However, a gap remains, as no study to date has evaluated the diagnostic performance of NM-MRI specifically for distinguishing DaT abnormalities within the iRBD population. Our findings address this gap, demonstrating significant diagnostic performance for both NM volume and SNR in identifying iRBD-CIT+ status, while CNR showed less robust discriminative capacity with borderline significance. Subregional analysis further revealed that the limbic and sensorimotor areas offered comparable diagnostic performances.

Consistent with prior reports in both PD and iRBD, NM volume and SNR consistently outperformed CNR in our cohort. For example, previous meta-analyses have highlighted the superior sensitivity and specificity of NM volume over signal intensity (Cho et al., 2021). This trend remained consistent in the multivariable logistic regression analyses, further supporting the robustness of NM volume and SNR as potential imaging biomarkers for dopaminergic dysfunction in iRBD.

Although NM-MRI alone may not yet be sufficient to definitively predict the risk of phenoconversion, its ability to reflect underlying nigrostriatal alterations offers significant utility in clinical decision-making. Specifically, NM-MRI may serve as a practical, radiation-free triage tool to identify patients with iRBD who are more likely to harbor dopaminergic degeneration and therefore can help clinicians prioritize high-risk individuals for further evaluation with DaT imaging, such as 18F-FP-CIT PET. However, the NM-MRI sequence used in this study was based on an optimized Sandwich protocol, which is not yet widely available in routine clinical practice. This sequence is designed to enhance neuromelanin contrast through improved magnetization transfer effects and may provide higher sensitivity compared with conventional NM-MRI techniques. Therefore, the magnitude of NM alterations and the diagnostic performance observed in this study may not be directly generalizable to standard NM-MRI sequences used in other settings.

This study had several notable strengths. First, this is the first study to evaluate NM-MRI in relation to DaT imaging abnormalities in patients with iRBD. Our findings suggest that NM-MRI may serve as a potential biomarker for detecting dopaminergic dysfunction, offering a more widely available and affordable alternative to DaT imaging. Second, the segmentation of the SNpc into sensorimotor, associative, and limbic subregions enabled a more detailed analysis of region-specific vulnerability, thereby enhancing our understanding of the spatial patterns of NM changes in relation to disease progression. Third, the use of a template-based semi-automated quantification method ensured the objective and reproducible measurement of NM metrics. In line with this approach, we adopted an ROI-based strategy rather than voxel-wise analysis. Although voxel-wise methods can provide detailed spatial information, they are more susceptible to registration error and noise, particularly in small structures such as the substantia nigra. In contrast, ROI-based quantification improves robustness and enables biologically meaningful interpretation within anatomically defined subregions, while also providing summary measures that are more readily applicable in clinical settings.

However, this study had several limitations. First, only two patients in our cohort converted to PD, both of whom had abnormal DaT imaging findings. Given the small sample size, it was not feasible to evaluate NM-MRI’s predictive performance for phenoconversion. Further longitudinal studies are required to confirm these findings. Second, DaT imaging was qualitatively assessed by visual inspection. Quantitative 18F-FP-CIT PET analysis should be incorporated into future research to improve objectivity and enable more precise comparisons with NM metrics. Third, there was a significant sex imbalance between the iRBD and HC groups, which may have influenced the results. Moreover, there was only a small number of patients with right-sided DaT abnormalities (n = 3) in our cohort, limiting the statistical power to detect robust effects on the right side. Further studies should recruit a larger sex-matched population and encompass more diverse cases to confirm these findings. Finally, the subregional analysis in this study is based on an anatomically informed approximation rather than a direct connectivity-based parcellation. Therefore, the boundaries between subregions should be interpreted cautiously, and the results do not represent precise mapping of functional nigrostriatal territories. Future studies incorporating connectivity-based or high-resolution tractography approaches may further refine subregional characterization of the SNpc.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that patients with iRBD exhibit measurable reductions in NM volume and signal intensity, particularly those with abnormal DaT imaging. NM-MRI metrics, especially volume and SNR in the limbic subregion, may serve as useful imaging biomarkers for identifying individuals with dopaminergic dysfunction. Given its high accessibility and cost-effectiveness compared to DaT scans, these findings support NM-MRI as a strategic, radiation-free triage tool to identify high-risk patients for further evaluation with DaT imaging in the iRBD population.
