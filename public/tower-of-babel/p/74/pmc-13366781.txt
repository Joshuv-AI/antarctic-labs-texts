Association of pretransplant pulmonary hypertension with posttransplant renal Doppler ultrasonography findings and biopsy-proven rejection
Authors: Burak Yagdiran, Batuhan Kirisci, Ahmed Uslu, Nedim Cekmen, Mehmet Haberal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePulmonary hypertension (PH) is a complex systemic disease. There are insufficient studies in the literature regarding its effect on renal hemodynamics following transplantation.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 244 kidney transplant (KT) recipients. Patients were classified according to the severity of preoperative pulmonary hypertension based on echocardiographic systolic pulmonary artery pressure. Renal Doppler ultrasound (USG) parameters (resistive index [RI] and peak systolic velocity [PSV]) were measured at standard time points (day 3, week 1, month 1, month 3, and month 6) that have long been routinely used. Generalized estimating equations were used in longitudinal analyses to account for repeated measurements within each patient. Additionally, the relationship between PH severity and biopsy-confirmed rejection was examined.ResultsPretransplant PH severity was significantly associated with posttransplant renal Doppler USG findings, particularly RI measurements. Patients with moderate and severe PH had consistently higher RI values during follow-up compared with the other groups. Although RI values generally decreased over time after KT, they remained relatively higher in patients with increasing PH severity. No significant association was observed between PH severity and clinically indicated biopsy-proven rejection. PSV findings showed greater variability and less consistent associations during follow-up.ConclusionIncreased pretransplant PH severity was associated with higher posttransplant RI values, independent of clinically indicated biopsy-proven rejection. These findings suggest that elevated RI values in patients with PH may reflect systemic and graft-related hemodynamic changes rather than rejection alone. Therefore, Doppler USG findings should be interpreted carefully in KT recipients with preexisting PH.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Kidney transplantation, Pulmonary hypertension, Doppler ultrasonography, Resistive index, Peak systolic velocity, Graft rejection, Hemodynamics

Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a complex condition characterized by raised pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) and progressive right ventricular dysfunction, which ultimately leads to significant morbidity and mortality [1]. Recent updates to the 2022 ESC/ERS guidelines have redefined PH by decreasing the mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) threshold to > 20 mmHg and including PVR in the diagnostic criteria, thereby enabling a more refined classification of disease subtypes and earlier identification of at-risk patients [2, 3]. The pathophysiology of PH involves endothelial dysfunction, an imbalance between vasoconstrictor and vasodilator mediators, and progressive vascular remodeling; all of these lead to increased vascular resistance not only in the pulmonary circulation but also at the systemic level [4–7].

In patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage renal disease (ESRD), PH is common due to various mechanisms, including left ventricular dysfunction, high cardiac output caused by arteriovenous fistulas, and chronic hypoxia [8–10]. The common link between PH and systemic hypertension is a rise in left ventricular diastolic pressure and the subsequent development of PH over time [11]. PH and arterial systolic hypertension, independent of left ventricular diastolic injury, share similar pathogenic mechanisms that explain their relationship. These may include endothelial dysfunction, overactivation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), or increased vasoconstriction secondary to sympathetic nervous system activation, all of which increase pressure in the vascular network [12, 13]. The resulting systemic vasoconstriction may affect graft perfusion and vascular resistance.

Kidney transplantation (KT) is the best treatment for severe kidney disease and ESRD. The success of the transplant depends on many factors, including the immune system, blood flow, and blood vessels. Doppler ultrasound (USG) helps us check blood flow and resistance in the kidney after a KT. Two important measurements from this test are the renal resistive index (RI) and peak systolic velocity (PSV). These help assess kidney blood flow and resistance. High RI values (> 0.70) often indicate the kidney is not functioning well and may suggest rejection or other problems.

Preoperative cardiovascular assessment using transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is crucial for evaluating and following transplantation candidates. Systolic pulmonary artery pressure (sPAP) obtained by TTE is a widely used indicator of mPAP and enables risk stratification based on PH severity [14–16]. Additionally, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) provides a simple and reliable measure of right ventricular systolic function and has prognostic value in various cardiovascular conditions, including PH [17, 18].

Previous studies have found that having PH before a transplant can lead to problems like delayed graft function, shorter graft life, and higher death rates [19–22]. This suggests that PH might change how the graft works with blood flow and small blood vessels. Although follow-up studies have compared Doppler USG parameters within the same patient and across patients, the link between PH severity and the graft’s blood vessel resistance remains underexplored. Also, it’s still unclear whether the blood flow changes from PH are associated with rejection confirmed by biopsy.

Our primary aim was to examine changes in RI and PSV over time according to the severity of preoperative PH. The secondary aim was to evaluate the link between these parameters and biopsy-confirmed rejection in KT recipients.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was performed at a tertiary referral center in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by our University’s Ethics Committee (KA25/21 − 16/01/2025). Due to the study’s retrospective nature and the anonymization of patient data, informed consent was not required. The 305 patients who underwent KT between January 2018 and December 2023 were initially screened.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All adult patients (n = 305) who underwent KT were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria: Patients with missing preoperative TTE results and defined Doppler USG data, as well as those with missing clinical information, were excluded from the study.

After excluding patients based on these criteria, 244 patients were included in the study.

Classification of pulmonary hypertension

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) status was evaluated using sPAP measurements obtained from pretransplant TTE examinations. According to sPAP values, patients were classified as normal (< 30 mmHg), mild PH (30–45 mmHg), moderate PH (45–65 mmHg), or severe PH (> 65 mmHg). These thresholds were chosen to approximately correspond to mPAP ranges within the general framework of the 2022 ESC/ERS pulmonary hypertension guidelines.

At our institution, TTE in patients with ESRD is generally performed after dialysis sessions, whenever feasible, to minimize the potential influence of volume overload on pulmonary pressure measurements. However, because this study used retrospective archival data collected over a long period, complete standardization of echocardiographic timing could not be verified for all patients.

Tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRV) and estimated right atrial pressure measurements were not consistently available in archived echocardiographic reports and, therefore, could not be included in the analysis.

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) assessment in this study relied on TTE, which is routinely used during the pretransplant evaluation of KT candidates in daily clinical practice. Although right heart catheterization remains the gold standard for PH diagnosis, it was not routinely performed in this cohort because invasive hemodynamic evaluation was not clinically indicated in most patients.

Doppler ultrasound measurements

The hemodynamics of the kidney graft were assessed using Doppler USG data obtained at predetermined routine postoperative time points (days 3, 7, 30, 90, and 180). The renal artery RI and PSV were used.

RI was calculated using the standard formula: (peak systolic velocity − end-diastolic velocity) / peak systolic velocity.

Biopsy and pathology

Rejection status was determined by histopathological evaluation of USG-guided graft biopsy samples obtained in patients with suspected graft dysfunction. All biopsies were performed by an experienced interventional radiologist and evaluated by pathologists experienced in transplantation pathology. Since protocol biopsies were not routinely performed at our center, subclinical rejection episodes could not be evaluated within the scope of this study.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range) for continuous data, and as frequency and percentage for categorical data. To compare categorical data, such as rejection rates across PH groups, the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used. Ordinal logistic regression was used to examine the association between PH severity and rejection, with results reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Longitudinal changes in RI and PSV were analyzed using generalized estimating equations (GEE) to account for repeated postoperative measurements within individuals. A Gaussian distribution with an identity link function was used. Repeated measurements from the same patient were handled using an exchangeable working correlation structure, and robust standard errors were applied. All available follow-up measurements were included in the models. Results are presented as beta coefficients, 95% confidence intervals, and p-values. The models included PH severity, postoperative follow-up time points, and rejection status as the main variables of interest. Additional adjustment variables were not included because some clinically relevant covariates could not be consistently retrieved from the archival records, given the study’s retrospective design. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

A total of 244 KT recipients were included in the study. Patients were divided into four groups based on preoperative PH severity: normal (n = 152, 62.3%), mild (n = 63, 25.8%), moderate (n = 21, 8.6%), and severe (n = 8, 3.3%). Biopsy-confirmed rejection was observed in 58 patients (23.8%) across the entire patient group, while no rejection was detected in 186 patients (76.2%) (Table 1).

Longitudinal analysis of the renal resistance index (RI)

A longitudinal GEE study found a strong association between PH severity and graft RI values (Table 2). RI values were much higher in the moderate (β=+0.0358, p = 0.010) and severe (β=+0.1167, p < 0.001) PH groups compared to the Normal PH group. No significant difference was observed between the mild PH group and the normal PH group (β=+0.0096, p = 0.219). Increasing PH severity was associated with higher RI values. When follow-up measurements were taken after KT, RI values decreased substantially over time. Compared to the 3rd day after surgery, RI values went down by the 1st month (β = −0.0227, p < 0.001), the 3rd month (β = −0.0249, p < 0.001), and the 6th month (β = −0.0246, p < 0.001). No significant change was observed during the first postoperative week. Figure 1 shows these findings, with higher RI values in patients with moderate-to-severe PH at all time points. Importantly, no significant association was observed between rejection status and RI values (β ≈ 0, p = 0.997). These findings suggest that elevated RI values may not be explained solely by rejection-related mechanisms.

Longitudinal analysis of peak systolic velocity (PSV)

Over time, PSV was less clearly linked to PH severity than RI (Table 3). PSV values were higher in the mild PH group than in the normal group (β=+9.44, p = 0.025), but there were no significant differences between the moderate and severe groups. There was some change over time; higher PSV values were observed at 1 month compared with the 3rd day after surgery (β=+6.57, p = 0.039). No statistically significant differences were observed at the remaining follow-up time points. Rejection status was not linked to PSV values (p = 0.895). Figure 2 shows that PSV trends vary more and are less consistent among PH groups compared to the RI group.

Compared with RI, PSV measurements showed greater variability during follow-up and among PH severity groups. Unlike RI, PSV values may be affected by multiple physiological and technical factors, including vascular compliance, cardiac output, postoperative hemodynamic changes, and Doppler angle variability. These factors may partly explain the less consistent relationship observed between PSV measurements and PH severity in the present study.

Discussion

In the present study, increasing pretransplant PH severity was associated with persistently higher posttransplant RI values during follow-up. Patients with moderate and severe PH demonstrated consistently elevated RI measurements compared with the other groups, although RI values generally decreased over time after transplantation. In contrast, no significant association was observed between PH severity and clinically indicated biopsy-proven rejection. However, the relatively small number of patients in the severe PH subgroup should be considered when interpreting these findings, as the study may have been underpowered to detect weaker associations with rejection outcomes. These findings suggest that elevated RI values in patients with PH may reflect systemic and graft-related hemodynamic alterations rather than rejection alone. Previous studies have also demonstrated that RI may be influenced not only by graft pathology but also by systemic vascular factors such as arterial stiffness, pulse pressure, and vascular compliance [23, 24]. In this context, PH may represent a broader hemodynamic condition affecting graft perfusion after transplantation.

The strength of our study lies in the availability of detailed, close patient follow-up data spanning many years in the archives, as well as in the use of the GEE method. The use of the GEE approach enabled longitudinal evaluation of repeated postoperative Doppler measurements while accounting for within-patient correlations over time. We found that RI values decreased after KT, likely due to early recovery. However, patients with more severe PH had higher RI values than others. This suggests that the blood vessels in these patients continue to affect blood flow long after the transplant. These findings further support the interpretation of RI as a marker influenced not only by graft pathology but also by systemic vascular and hemodynamic factors in KT recipients.

In a study by Zlotnick et al., using a sPAP threshold of 45 mm Hg or greater, they found an increase in sPAP specificity for predicting delayed or slowed graft function. This study suggests a relationship between PH in the recipient and graft perfusion [19]. Issa et al. observed that pretransplant PH estimated by TTE in 215 KT recipients independently found an association with a raised risk of post-transplant death in patients with sPAP 50 mm Hg or greater [20]. Mehra et al. observed, in a retrospective study of 206 KT recipients, that PH was present in 19% of patients according to TTE findings performed before KT and that PH was associated with decreased graft function after KT [21]. Lai et al. found that pretransplant PH was associated with decreased 5-year graft survival in patients with sPAP > 35 mm Hg [22]. Lentine et al. reported that the presence of PH, as defined by TTE, is an essential marker of adverse outcomes after KT [9]. In our study, in parallel with these studies, we determined that RI values were closely related to PH groups determined by sPAP in the preoperative period. In addition, our findings suggest that increasing PH severity may be associated with persistently elevated renal vascular resistance independent of clinically indicated biopsy-proven rejection. This observation may help explain persistently elevated Doppler USG parameters in certain KT recipients with PH despite the absence of biopsy-proven rejection [23, 24].

These pathophysiological mechanisms may influence graft hemodynamics through multiple pathways. We did not include the possible effects of these factors in the analysis. Additionally, it was not possible to perform a detailed evaluation of specific clinical parameters, such as the etiology of PH (pre- or post-capillary), which limited the interpretation of the results. In our study, the inability to confirm PH invasively through right heart catheterization was considered a significant limitation. This situation imposes limitations on accuracy, as PH diagnosis is based on TTE parameters. Including invasive measurements in future prospective studies is vital for confirming findings and obtaining more robust results. Another significant limitation of our study is its retrospective design, which led to incomplete archival records of immunosuppressive treatment protocols. This limitation prevented a comprehensive evaluation and comparison of immunosuppressive regimens across different PH severity groups. In our prospective study, we will systematically and in a standardized manner account for the results of the power analysis and the age distribution when determining patient groups and immunosuppressive treatment data.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the retrospective, single-center design inherently carries a risk of selection and information bias. In addition, pretransplant PH assessment was based on TTE rather than right heart catheterization, which remains the reference standard for PH diagnosis.

Because the study population consisted of patients with CKD and ESRD, pulmonary pressure measurements obtained by echocardiography may also have been influenced by intravascular volume status. At our institution, TTE examinations are routinely performed after dialysis sessions whenever possible to minimize this effect. However, given the retrospective design and reliance on long-term archival data, uniform standardization of examination timing could not be verified for every patient.

Another limitation was the incomplete availability of certain echocardiographic parameters in archived reports. TRV and estimated right atrial pressure measurements were inconsistently documented and, therefore could not be incorporated into the analysis. Accordingly, some degree of misclassification in PH severity cannot be entirely excluded.

The relatively small number of patients in the severe PH subgroup may also have limited the statistical power of subgroup analyses, particularly for rejection-related outcomes. In addition, graft biopsies were performed only when clinically indicated, potentially introducing selection bias toward patients with suspected graft dysfunction. Therefore, subclinical rejection episodes may not have been captured.

Finally, PH in KT recipients is a complex and multifactorial condition. Factors such as systemic hypertension, diabetes mellitus, long-term hemodialysis exposure, arteriovenous fistula-related hemodynamic alterations, and other cardiovascular comorbidities may have contributed to the observed findings. They could not be evaluated comprehensively within the retrospective design of the present study. In addition, several potentially relevant baseline clinical variables, including detailed dialysis duration, donor characteristics, immunosuppressive treatment protocols, and certain cardiovascular parameters, could not be consistently retrieved from archival records due to the study’s retrospective design.

Conclusion

Pretransplant PH severity, as assessed by TTE, was significantly associated with higher posttransplant renal RI values in KT recipients. Patients with moderate and severe PH demonstrated persistently elevated RI measurements throughout follow-up, despite the general decline in RI values after transplantation. In contrast, no significant association was observed between PH severity and clinically indicated biopsy-proven rejection. These findings support the view that elevated RI values in patients with PH may reflect broader systemic and graft-related hemodynamic alterations rather than rejection alone. Therefore, Doppler USG findings should be interpreted cautiously, in the context of the overall clinical and hemodynamic picture, in KT recipients with preexisting PH. Further prospective multicenter studies with larger patient populations and standardized hemodynamic assessment are needed to validate and expand upon these findings.
