Packaging of Single-Stranded RNA in Viruses and Virus-Like Particles
Authors: Rees F. Garmann, William M. Gelbart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Our double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) genomes are famously compacted by proteins in the nuclei of our cells, resulting in meters of dsDNA being confined in micron-sized volumes. The most prevalent form of viral genomes, however, is single-stranded RNA (ssRNA), which is compacted at significantly higher density in protective protein shells with nanometer dimensions. In this review, we discuss the special nature of ssRNA that allows it to be spontaneously packaged in this way by co-self-assembly with viral capsid protein (CP). We focus on the few viruses whose nucleocapsids can be reconstituted from their purified CP and ssRNA genomes and whose CPs can spontaneously package heterologous RNA into virus-like particles (VLPs). These VLPs are then compared with their cell-synthesized versions, with lentivirus and adeno-associated virus vector particles, and with nucleocapsids formed by nonviral proteins whose messenger RNAs are put under directed evolutionary pressure to be packaged by them in cellulo.

INTRODUCTION

In reviewing this topic, we need to begin by explaining how we distinguish between viruses and virus-like particles (VLPs) and why we are confining our discussion to single-stranded RNA (ssRNA).

Viruses are particles of protected genomes that code for enough proteins to direct their replication in a host cell. Viruses are most commonly classified according to the Baltimore scheme, which distinguishes them by their genome type—positive- or negative-sense ssRNA, double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), ssDNA, or dsDNA—and whether or not they depend on reverse transcriptase (RT) to generate their messenger RNA (mRNA). The ways in which their genomes are associated with protective capsid proteins (CPs), and whether or not they also involve a membrane protein–containing lipid bilayer envelope, distinguish them further as enveloped or unenveloped viruses. A final layer of distinction is associated with the almost continuous spectrum of structural order present in the virus particles—virions—ranging from stoichiometrically precise, helically or icosahedrally symmetric particles to disordered aggregates of nucleic acid, protein, and lipid components that are highly polymorphic in size and shape. In this review, we focus on positive-sense ssRNA viruses and how their genomes are packaged and protected by highly structured protein and lipid components.

VLPs, on the other hand, are noninfectious particles consisting of genetic material protected by a capsid and/or lipid bilayer–embedded membrane proteins in structures that are putatively identical to those of infectious virions. We note that in the literature the term VLP is used much more loosely to include all manner of viral CP–derived particles, with empty capsids lacking ssRNA cargo at the top of the list. In this review, however, we confine our discussion of VLPs to particles that correspond to the virus with its ssRNA viral genome replaced by foreign—that is, nonviral, heterologous—ssRNA. Consequently, the VLP is taken up by host cells in the same way as the infectious virus, but no new virus particles are generated because none of the viral structural protein genes—CP of unenveloped viruses or CP and membrane protein of enveloped viruses—are present. We further focus on viruses and corresponding VLPs whose genetic cargo—genomic RNA for viruses and heterologous RNA for VLPs—is mRNA that is directly translated to give their encoded protein products.

The reason all of the viruses and VLPs discussed here involve single-stranded nucleic acid like mRNA rather than double-stranded nucleic acid like dsDNA is because of the fundamentally different three-dimensional (3D) size and shape of single- and double-stranded nucleic acid. More explicitly, long dsDNA—independent of its sequence—is a stiff linear polymer with an average persistence length of 50 nm, meaning that work needs to be done to bend it into radii of curvature smaller than this. And spherical viral capsids are typically 15–25 nm in radius, while dsDNA genomes—involving tens of thousands of base pairs—are as long as 10,000 nm. Accordingly, the genome of a dsDNA virus, equilibrated in solution, has a radius of gyration that is orders of magnitude larger than the radius of the capsid inside which it is packaged, as shown in Figure 1a. By contrast, ssRNA is more compact, as seen in Figure 1b, which shows the 2,774-nt-long gene RNA2 of cowpea chlorotic mottle virus (CCMV) that has been in vitro transcribed from the 2,774-bp-long dsDNA molecule seen looping as a linear double helix around the field of view. Note that the ssRNA is significantly self-compacted by base-pair interactions that gather in distant nucleotides along its chain and turn it into a compact and effectively branched polymer whose flexibility is maintained by the many single-stranded loops connecting two or more base-paired regions. As seen in Figure 1b, each of these RNAs is essentially the same size as the CCMV virion inside which it is spontaneously packaged when CCMV CP is added to the RNA. In contrast, the dsDNA is a stiff linear polymer that cannot be self-assembled into VLPs because of the prohibitively large free energy cost of bending it strongly and crowding it in on itself to fit into the small volume of a virus-sized capsid. This is the fundamental reason why ssRNA virus formation is spontaneous and why its genome release requires expenditure of energy, whereas work must be performed to package a dsDNA genome into a preformed capsid and its release—ejection!—is spontaneous.

In Vitro Synthesis of Viruses and VLPs: Model Systems

We are concerned with a relatively small group of viruses and VLPs and contrast their synthesis in vitro (see Section 3) and in cellulo (see Section 4). By in vitro, we mean that the only components present are purified RNA and purified structural proteins of the virus. Accordingly, we focus on the few viruses and VLPs that can be reconstituted in vitro in this way (see Sections 3.2.1-3) and on a highly structured and symmetric enveloped virus that can almost be reconstituted in vitro (see Section 3.2.4). 3D structural models of these virions are shown in Figure 2: tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) (Figure 2a), CCMV (Figure 2b), MS2 (Figure 2c), and Sindbis virus (Figure 2d). Similarly, we briefly describe efforts to reconstitute the nucleocapsid portions of less-structured and more complex RNA-genome enveloped viruses (see Section 3.2.5).

TMV’s capsid is a hollow one-molecule-thick cylinder with its proteins ordered in a perfect helix and the ssRNA genome embedded in the protein shell with the same pitch and with three nucleotides per protein (see Figure 2a), making for stoichiometrically precise particles. CCMV’s capsid is a hollow one-molecule-thick sphere consisting of 180 icosahedrally ordered proteins organized into 12 pentamers and 20 hexamers (Figure 2b), with one molecule of disordered RNA inside the hollow interior. A CCMV-sibling species in the Bromovirus genus is brome mosaic virus (BMV), whose capsid has the same quaternary structure and whose CP is similarly capable of forming infectious virus or VLPs when mixed in buffer solution with its genome or with heterologous RNA. MS2 virus, on the other hand, has one CP dimer replaced by a maturase protein (Figure 2c) that is required for attachment to and infection of its bacterial host, and this virus has not yet been reconstituted in vitro. However, noninfectious MS2 VLPs have been self-assembled from pure CP and either genomic or heterologous RNA.

Significantly, for these examples where CP can assemble around genomic or heterologous RNA in vitro, the resulting capsids are identical; more precisely, no differences in their structures have been resolved. This is shown in Figure 3, where native virions are presented along with the VLPs formed from genomic RNA and heterologous RNA.

Another one of the VLPs that we feature consists of the components of the enveloped Sindbis virus, organized into a 240-CP, icosahedrally symmetric nucleocapsid surrounded by a lipid bilayer consisting of heterodimers of membrane proteins with the same icosahedral symmetry. Exceptionally for an enveloped virus, it is a stoichiometrically precise and ordered particle in the sense that there is a specific number of CPs (240) and an equal number of membrane proteins, each organized into an icosahedrally symmetric superstructure with a single molecule of RNA inside (Figure 2d). These VLPs can be made in cells, as described in Section 4.1.4, but have not yet been reconstituted in vitro. In Section 3.2.4 we discuss how in vitro reconstitution has been achieved for the nucleocapsid portion of an enveloped virus like Sindbis but not (quite) yet for its enveloped infectious form, and in Section 3.2.5, we describe how in vitro synthesis of even just the nucleocapsid of enveloped viruses like hepatitis B virus (HBV) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) has not yet been realized.

In Cellulo Synthesis of VLPs and of De Novo Designed Protein Nucleocapsids

In Section 4, we review efforts to control the synthesis, in cellulo, of several different ssRNA- and ssDNA-specific VLPs. These efforts range from the expression in yeast and bacterial cells of CPs from BMV, MS2, and TMV (see Sections 4.1.1-3), whose VLPs can be reconstituted in vitro, to the generation in mammalian cells of Sindbis VLPs (see Section 4.1.4) and of adeno-associated virus (AAV) and lentivirus (LV) (see Section 4.2.1) noninfectious vector particles that cannot be reconstituted in vitro. We also discuss the de novo design of proteins that—under directed evolution pressure—optimize the specific packaging of the mRNA that encodes them (Section 4.2.2).

The main points covered in this review are highlighted in Figure 4. The first point is that infectious virus or noninfectious heterologous RNA–containing VLPs can be synthesized in a test tube simply by adding purified viral CP and RNA—either genomic or heterologous—to a solution with the right pH and salt conditions and waiting enough time for spontaneous formation of the particles (Figure 4a). In this way, one has complete control over the RNA content of the VLPs. In contrast, when these particles are made in cellulo, for example, by transcription of CP-encoding target RNA from a DNA plasmid in bacteria, only some of the VLPs that form contain the target RNA, the others having packaged either vector RNA or endogenous cellular mRNAs (Figure 4b).

Scope of This Review

Even after limiting our choice of viruses and VLP systems and the methods for generating them, as explained in Sections 1 and 2, it is not feasible in our allotted space to comprehensively review the literature on both in vitro packaging of RNA by CP into viruses and VLPs and in cellulo packaging of RNA by genetically engineered viruses and by directed evolution of designer proteins. Rather, our aim is to discuss just enough of these subfields so that we can critically compare and contrast what is learned from each about the factors determining the specificity of RNA packaging in VLPs in both test-tube and cellular contexts. These factors include the 3D size of the RNA as a function of nucleotide length and sequence, packaging signals in the RNA, absolute and relative concentrations of CP and the repertoire of RNA molecules, charge distribution of the capsid quaternary structure, and organization of internal membranes of the cell, among others. Accordingly, we present a broad-brush discussion of a limited number of examples of the relevant packaging contexts, citing methods papers and review articles that complete a more granular overview by discussing cases we have not considered and by providing experimental details we have not included.

In Section 2.1, we introduce the basics of capsid organization in highly structured and symmetrical viruses, in particular the key idea of minimizing the number of inequivalent protein positions in shells made up of a single CP, illustrating this point with the time-honored examples of helical and icosahedral symmetries. Section 2.2 introduces the less-explored basics relating to the fact that RNA that is replicated, and that is thousands of nucleotides long, gives rise to an ensemble of sequences due to the error rate of RNA-dependent RNA polymerases and, furthermore, that such long RNA molecules adopt a large ensemble of thermally accessible secondary structures for each of their primary sequences. Section 3.1 provides background on the spontaneous formation—self-assembly—of capsids as a nucleation phenomenon. Protein–RNA interactions are discussed in the context of RNA sequence–specific local secondary and tertiary structures and global physical properties, such as overall charge and size. Section 3.2 reviews work on the few viruses and VLPs whose in vitro reconstitution has been demonstrated—TMV (Section 3.2.1), CCMV and BMV (Section 3.2.2), and MS2 (Section 3.2.3)—and on a few other, more complicated and less structured examples—Sindbis virus (Section 3.2.4) and HIV and HBV (Section 3.2.5), whose self-assembly from purified components remains an ongoing challenge. Section 4 presents a summary of in cellulo syntheses of these same VLPs resulting from cotransformation of producer cells with the single-stranded nucleic acid of interest and with the structural proteins needed to specifically package it. The review concludes with a list of open questions and future research directions.

THE STRUCTURE OF SSRNA VIRUSES, THEIR CAPSIDS, AND THEIR GENOMES

The 3D capsid structures of many ssRNA viruses are known in atomistic detail, and these high-resolution structural models provide a valuable starting point for studying RNA packaging, revealing both the general organizational principles of virus particles and the types of molecular interactions that drive their formation. However, the dynamics of the capsid—and the structure and dynamics of the underlying RNA—remain much less well understood.

Capsid Structure: The Idea of Genetic Economy

The capsids of most ssRNA viruses come in one of two shapes: rods or spheres. In general, rod-shaped capsids display helical symmetry, while spherical capsids exhibit icosahedral symmetry. By exploiting these symmetries, ssRNA viruses can build stable capsids large enough to fully enclose and protect their fragile genomes using only a limited number of proteins—the canonical example of genetic economy (12). Rather than encoding hundreds of unique proteins that interact in specific ways to form their capsid, ssRNA viruses commonly use a single protein repeated many times in a symmetric arrangement, with each copy forming the same set of stabilizing contacts with its neighbors. The result is a cohesive and stable shell that can be encoded by as little as a single gene.

Rod-shaped capsids.

The first and best-studied example of a rod-shaped virus is TMV, whose structure was first revealed 70 years ago by Rosalind Franklin (13) using X-ray fiber diffraction. The TMV capsid consists of 2,130 copies of a single CP arranged in a right-handed helix around the 6,400-nt viral RNA (14). Each protein subunit binds 3 nt of RNA, and together they form a hollow cylindrical shell roughly 300 nm long and 18 nm in diameter.

This structure is striking in its regularity: Except for those at the very ends of the rod, each protein occupies the same position relative to the helical axis and to its neighbors, forming an identical set of stabilizing contacts. The RNA packaged into the capsid is completely stripped of its secondary and tertiary structure and passes through the protein helix in an extended conformation. The result is a stoichiometrically precise particle with a well-defined 3D structure: The number of proteins in the capsid is determined by the length of the RNA, and the structure of the RNA is imposed by the geometry of the assembled capsid.

Spherical capsids.

Whereas rod-like capsids built with helical symmetry can be arbitrarily large—involving unlimited numbers of proteins in strictly equivalent positions relative to their neighbors—spherical capsids built with icosahedral symmetry are limited to just 60 equivalent positions. However, to enclose their genomes, some ssRNA viruses require larger capsids than can be built with just 60 proteins. To achieve larger capsid sizes, these viruses adopt quasi-equivalent (15) arrangements, in which their CPs occupy a minimum number of similar—but not strictly identical—environments within the shell.

A particularly efficient way to generate quasi-equivalent arrangements is given by the triangulation (T) number (15) such that capsids are built from 60 × T proteins arranged into a lattice of 12 pentamers and 10(T−1) hexamers. Here, T specifies the number of distinct quasi-equivalent positions and takes on specific values determined by the equation T=b2+bk+k2, where b and k are nonnegative integers. This construction gives rise to values such as T=1, 3, 4, 7, and so on. Viruses from across the biosphere build their capsids according to this T number construction, with observed T numbers ranging from 1 to over 1,000 (16).

Many spherical ssRNA viruses, like CCMV and BMV, have 28-nm capsids with T=3 (17). As already mentioned, MS2 has a 27-nm capsid with T=3 but with the slight variation that a single copy of its maturation protein replaces two coat protein subunits (18). Larger spherical ssRNA viruses can adopt higher T numbers. For example, Sindbis virus has a 41-nm T=4 capsid core made from 240 copies of its nucleocapsid protein arranged as 12 pentamers and 30 hexamers (19). This core particle is surrounded by a lipid bilayer embedded with heterodimers of viral membrane proteins that mirror the same T=4 symmetry, forming an extended protein membrane superstructure.

While a remarkable fraction of viral capsids follow the T number construction, recent work has extended the framework to accommodate some outliers (20).

Long-RNA Structure: A Combination of Local and Global Features

Long RNA molecules such as viral genomes and mRNA molecules do more than just encode proteins—they also adopt 3D structures that serve biological functions. These structures arise from interactions between nucleotides at different positions along the RNA chain, such as base-pairing and stacking interactions, which draw the linear molecule into a more compact, globular shape. This property is not unique to evolved viral or cellular RNA sequences: Even mathematically random sequences of G, C, A, and U, when equilibrated under physiological conditions, adopt globular conformations stabilized by base-pairing and stacking interactions. However, the details of the resulting structure—such as which regions are base-paired into locally folded structural elements like stem-loops, how these elements group together to form domains, and how those domains are arranged in space to form the overall global structure—are strongly sequence dependent. For viruses, these structural details can have profound consequences: They mediate specific interactions with viral or host proteins, regulate translation and replication, and influence packaging through their overall size and shape.

Viral-RNA primary structure is riddled with mutations.

Because the RNA-dependent RNA polymerases that replicate ssRNA virus genomes often lack proofreading activity, they exhibit exceptionally high mutation rates—on the order of 10−4 to 10−5 substitutions per nucleotide per replication cycle—orders of magnitude higher than those of the DNA-dependent polymerases of cells or DNA viruses (21). One might therefore expect that ssRNA viruses, whose genomes have on the order of 104 nt and which are each copied thousands of times, would rapidly lose their genetic identity, diverging into unrelated sequences after only a few generations. Yet this is not the case. For example, the MS2 genome is found in essentially the same form all around the world—from prodigious locales like the New York City sewer system to more modest ones, such as the sewers of Cambridge, England (22). This remarkable sequence conservation reflects the fact that most mutations are deleterious to viral fitness and are efficiently purged by purifying selection (23). Deleterious mutations are common and yet not tolerated because they disrupt not only the protein-coding potential of the genome but also its ability to adopt functionally important RNA structures.

Long-RNA secondary structure is a statistical property.

RNA secondary structure describes the base-pairing interactions between nucleotides along the sequence (24). Unlike short RNA molecules like transfer RNAs (tRNAs) and ribozymes that fold into unique ground-state structures, long RNAs like viral genomes do not necessarily adopt a single well-defined secondary structure (25). Thermodynamic folding models show that for any viral-length sequence, there are thousands of secondary structures within a few kT units of the minimum free-energy structure (26). As a result, RNA secondary structure is best understood as a statistical ensemble of contributing conformations. This complicates structural analysis: It is only recently that experimental techniques—such as multidimensional probing (27, 28) and cross-linking (29)—have begun to address the distribution of secondary structures adopted by viral RNA free in solution and packaged in capsids.

Long-RNA tertiary structure is poorly understood.

There are very few experimental data on the 3D tertiary structure of naked RNA molecules containing a thousand nucleotides or more (30). What structural data we do have most often come from RNA molecules that are dressed up and stabilized by proteins—with the ribosome being the best-known example (31). Viruses provide other informative examples. As discussed in Section 2.1.1, early structural studies of TMV resolved the full viral RNA structure, embedded—without secondary structure—in the proteins of the rod-like capsid (14). More recently, high-resolution cryo–electron microscopy (cryo-EM) reconstructions of MS2 (32) and related RNA bacteriophages (5) have revealed the viral RNA inside spherical capsids. Unlike TMV, the RNA inside MS2 contains recognizable secondary and tertiary structure elements, providing a detailed view of the 3D structure. However, it remains uncertain whether the RNA adopts this structure before packaging, or whether the structure forms as a consequence of packaging—an important distinction for understanding the packaging process and the possible role of RNA in directing the assembly of the capsid (33). Notably, similarly performed cryo-EM reconstructions of BMV particles showed no evidence of ordered RNA structure in the capsid (34).

Our operational view of long-RNA structure.

All of these factors make it difficult to define precisely what we mean by viral RNA structure (35) or to predict how it influences packaging. Nonetheless, we find it useful to distinguish between local and global structural features. Local features include discrete elements such as stem-loops and tRNA-like motifs, typically formed by short-range base pairing. Global features refer to broader properties like the overall size, shape, and compactness of the RNA molecule. As we discuss in Section 3.1.3.1, local features can influence packaging by interacting directly with assembling CPs, often through sequence- or structure-specific contacts. By contrast, global features can affect packaging both directly and indirectly. Directly, the overall compactness of the RNA influences how easily it can fit into the limited interior volume of the capsid. Indirectly, the global fold constrains the relative positioning and accessibility of local structural motifs, and thus their ability to engage with the assembling capsid during packaging.

IN VITRO PACKAGING OF SSRNA INTO VIRUSES AND VLPS

Viruses are unique among evolving organisms in that they can be fully reconstituted in vitro from purified components. With model systems like TMV and CCMV, mixing purified CPs with genomic RNA molecules under the right buffer conditions yields infectious virions, indistinguishable in structure and function from those produced in cells. With others, like MS2, noninfectious VLPs are formed from heterologous RNA. The ability to reconstitute particles from defined components provides a robust route to packaging arbitrary RNA sequences—including synthetic or chemically modified RNAs—into protein shells. Thus, in vitro reconstitution is not only a powerful method for studying ssRNA packaging but also a versatile platform for RNA delivery and genetic medicine.

Principles of Self-Assembly from Purified Components

The spontaneous formation of infectious viruses from noninfectious purified protein and RNA components first inspired the term self-assembly (15). That well-ordered virions can form in vitro without any host-cell machinery underscores the fact that capsid formation and RNA packaging are fundamentally physicochemical processes, amenable to analysis using the tools of physical chemistry (36). Indeed, in vitro experiments and theoretical models have revealed much about the mechanisms and pathways of viral assembly and the molecular interactions that drive it. In the subsections that follow, we outline a conceptual framework for understanding how virions and VLPs self-assemble from purified components, with particular attention to the physical principles that govern RNA packaging.

Mechanisms and pathways of capsid assembly and RNA packaging.

Unlike dsDNA viruses, which require work to be performed to package their genomes into preformed empty capsids (37-40), ssRNA viruses assemble their capsids spontaneously around their genomes. Thus, ssRNA packaging and capsid formation are not separate steps but instead occur as a single, concerted process. The mechanisms and pathways by which an initially disordered mixture of protein and RNA transforms into a highly ordered capsid with the RNA inside are still not fully understood. A key step in the process is the formation of a small cluster of ordered CPs—called a nucleus—on the RNA. Initially unstable, the nucleus must become larger than some critical size before the binding of additional ordered proteins becomes favorable, leading to the growth of a complete capsid. Computer simulations show that this nucleation event can happen according to two qualitatively different pathways (41). In one, the nucleus forms on an otherwise bare RNA molecule and then grows by the stepwise addition of proteins from solution to the ordered cluster. In the other, termed the “en masse pathway” (42), the ordered nucleus forms within a protein–RNA complex containing many disordered proteins bound on the RNA; this disordered complex then undergoes collective rearrangement to yield an ordered capsid.

Molecular interactions driving assembly and packaging.

Assembly is governed by a balance of molecular interactions between the CPs and between the CPs and the RNA. Protein–protein interactions are typically weak (43), consisting of a combination of hydrophobic attractions between apolar amino acids and electrostatic repulsions between charged residues (44). Because charged amino acids can change their protonation state, protein–protein interactions are often pH-dependent and can be tuned by varying the pH of the assembly buffer (45). Protein–RNA interactions typically involve electrostatic attraction between positively charged amino acids on the protein and the negatively charged phosphate backbone of the RNA (46). Since these interactions are mediated by the RNA backbone, they are largely independent of nucleotide sequence and provide general nonspecific attractions that can be modulated by varying the ionic strength of the buffer (47). However, CPs can also form specific contacts with structured RNA motifs through directional hydrogen bonds and base-stacking interactions, giving rise to sequence- and structure-specific effects (48).

The strength of protein–protein interactions relative to protein–RNA interactions influences the assembly pathway. When nonspecific protein–RNA attractions dominate, assembly proceeds via en masse adsorption of coat proteins onto the RNA, resulting in disordered intermediates. Conversely, when protein–protein contacts are sufficiently strong, assembly is more likely to occur by nucleation from a critical-sized cluster of proteins on an otherwise bare RNA molecule, followed by the growth of an ordered capsid. Because the relative strength of these interactions can be modulated by adjusting the pH and ionic strength of the assembly buffer, it is possible to favor particular pathways and thereby control the efficiency and fidelity of RNA packaging.

Influence of RNA properties on assembly and packaging.

Viral RNA is not strictly required for capsid assembly. Indeed, as described in Section 3.2, the CPs of several ssRNA viruses can form ordered capsids around nonviral RNA (49) or synthetic polymers (50) or even in the absence of any cargo (51), yielding empty capsids. However, the kinetics and yield of assembly are influenced by the physical and chemical properties of the RNA—particularly its sequence-specific affinity for CPs as well as its overall size and shape.

General versus sequence-specific protein–RNA interactions: the role of packaging signals.

The term packaging signal is used variously in the literature to describe a range of sequence- or structure-specific RNA features involved in ssRNA virus assembly and packaging. Here, we define a packaging signal as an RNA element that exhibits unusually high affinity for viral CPs. These elements typically involve defined secondary or tertiary structures—such as stem-loops or groups of stem-loops—that are selectively recognized by CPs during assembly, thereby facilitating RNA packaging.

The importance of a packaging signal lies in its ability to outcompete nonspecific electrostatic interactions between the CPs and the RNA backbone. This competitive advantage depends on the relative binding affinities—or dissociation constants (Kd)—of the CP for the packaging signal compared to the remainder of the RNA molecule. A packaging signal is expected to be operational when the protein concentration is higher than the Kd for the signal but lower than the distribution of Kd values across other sites. Under such conditions, packaging signals can initiate assembly and influence packaging outcomes. Their effects are most pronounced under high-salt conditions, where nonspecific electrostatic interactions are screened out and sequence-specific interactions dominate (52).

Some definitions of packaging signals go beyond high-affinity binding and propose that these elements actively induce conformational changes in the CP, switching it into an assembly-competent state (53). Others suggest that multiple dispersed packaging signals distributed along the RNA molecule act together as an assembly instruction manual (54), directing the CPs to adopt specific configurations as hexamers and pentamers to form the correct T-number structure with high yield. These scenarios have been modeled by theoretical and computational work (55) and should stimulate experimental tests of their mechanisms.

Effect of RNA length and size on assembly.

In addition to local packaging signals, assembly depends on the global properties of the RNA molecule such as its total length and overall size and shape. It is generally observed that ssRNA molecules much shorter or much longer than the native genome are packaged less efficiently, as they do not provide the correct total electrostatic charge to interact favorably with the interior surface of the capsid (56). It has also been proposed that RNA sequences that fold into more compact structures are packaged more efficiently, as they fit better within the confined interior volume of the capsid (57). However, testing such models remains a significant experimental challenge: Because measuring the physical size of an RNA molecule in solution is difficult, researchers often rely on theoretical estimates obtained from RNA folding models (58). One useful metric is the maximum ladder distance (MLD), which quantifies the extendedness of an RNA molecule’s predicted secondary structures (59). Previous studies have shown that MLD values correlate well with experimental measurements of hydrodynamic radius in solution (60), making MLD a useful proxy for RNA size in the context of packaging studies.

Assembly kinetics.

The rate-limiting step of assembly is often associated with nucleation. Forming a critical nucleus involves overcoming a free-energy barrier. The height of this barrier—and thus the rate of nucleation—depends on the strength of both protein–protein and protein–RNA interactions.

When occurring on a bare RNA molecule, the nucleation rate further depends on the concentration of protein in solution (61). Because formation of a critical nucleus requires the simultaneous binding of multiple coat proteins, the nucleation rate increases steeply with protein concentration according to the law of mass action (62). This gives rise to an effective threshold concentration: Below this threshold, the nucleation rate becomes so low that assembly does not proceed on an observable timescale. The nucleation rate also depends on the sequence and structure of the RNA: Packaging signals or other high-affinity motifs can locally concentrate CPs and accelerate nucleation.

By contrast, the en masse pathway exhibits much weaker concentration dependence. Because the RNA becomes rapidly saturated with coat proteins, the limiting step is not protein binding but rather the internal rearrangement of the complex into an ordered structure (63). Accordingly, the assembly kinetics are governed less by protein availability and more by the internal dynamics of the disordered intermediate.

Assembly gone awry.

One prominent failure mode of RNA virus assembly is overnucleation, in which multiple nucleation events occur simultaneously on the same RNA molecule. This process can lead to the formation of large, irregular structures—sometimes referred to as monsters (64)—that contain several partial or incomplete capsids joined together by a shared RNA scaffold. Such overgrown assemblies occur under conditions where nucleation is fast relative to growth, such as when protein–RNA interactions are strong and protein concentrations are high. Under these conditions, multiple nuclei can form on the same RNA molecule before any one of them has time to grow into a complete shell. Alternatively, multiplet structures, which consist of two or more essentially complete capsids on the same RNA molecule, can form even under moderate assembly conditions if the RNA is too long to be fully packaged within a single capsid (8).

Model Systems for Studying In Vitro RNA Packaging

Here we present four of the most widely studied and best characterized of the few examples of viruses and VLPs that can be reconstituted from scratch—that is, from purified CP and ssRNA: TMV (Section 3.2.1), CCMV and BMV (Section 3.2.2), and MS2 (Section 3.2.3). Not coincidentally these are all unenveloped viruses. Sindbis virus (Section 3.2.4), on the other hand, is an enveloped virus that has not yet been fully reconstituted in vitro, although its nucleocapsid portion alone has. We also discuss ongoing efforts to self-assemble from scratch the more complicated and less structured nucleocapsids of the enveloped viruses HBV and HIV (Section 3.2.5), which also involve the copackaging of RT proteins.

Tobacco mosaic virus virions and VLPs.

TMV has earned a unique place of importance in the history of in vitro assemblies of viruses and VLPs for many reasons. First, in 1955, it was the first virus to be reconstituted from purified genomic RNA and CP (65). This was 57 years after it was the very first virus—of any kind—to be identified as an infectious disease agent distinct from bacteria (66) and 20 years after it was the first virus to be crystallized (67). Second, it was the first virus whose genome was found to have a packaging signal (68) that directed its specific packaging by viral CP. And third, its self-assembly—and that of VLPs containing heterologous RNA into which has been inserted the origin-of-assembly sequence (OAS) (69) that serves as packaging signal—has been studied more systematically than any other virus-derived system. These studies have been discussed critically in recent reviews (70) and have established the conditions—temperature, CP:RNA mass ratio, absolute concentrations, pH, and salt—for optimizing virus or VLP yield. While the OAS is dispensable for VLP formation, it is nevertheless important for packaging RNA molecules into single capsids (71). This is because the OAS, ensuring high-affinity binding of several CPs, is the site of capsid nucleation; without it, capsid nucleation occurs on a much longer timescale and can occur at several different sites on the RNA, resulting in branched structures of the helical rods. Alternatively, the assembly of branched structures can be engineered by including multiple copies of the OAS (72).

The most singular thing about TMV virions and TMV VLPs is that the RNA contained within them is perfectly ordered. As mentioned in Section 2.2, the large ensemble of different secondary and tertiary structures associated with gene-length RNA persists to a significant extent when the RNA is packaged in the hollow interior of spherical capsids: Even if some order can be resolved due to specific interactions of CP with stem-loops and other local bits of RNA structure, there are few constraints imposed on the overall RNA molecule because—as depicted in Figure 2—it is already the right size to fit into the spherical shell whose size (curvature) is strongly preferred by the CPs. In the TMV virion and VLPs, however, the packaged RNA needs to be stripped completely of all its secondary and tertiary structure as it becomes embedded as a single-chain helix inside the helically ordered shell formed by CP (see inset in Figure 1a).

Cowpea chlorotic mottle virus and brome mosaic virus virions and VLPs.

Along with TMV, CCMV and BMV CPs are essentially the only ones that are capable of being reconstituted in vitro into infectious virions or VLPs when added to genomic or heterologous RNA—see, however, reports of the less well-studied papaya mosaic virus virions (73) and VLPs (74). And there is an extensive literature on both these self-assembling systems, going back to the first report of CCMV and BMV reconstitutions in 1967 (75) and through subsequent systematic biophysical characterizations (76, 77).

The relative efficiencies of packaging a wide range of RNA lengths and sequences into CCMV VLPs have been measured by competing different molecules against viral RNA to be packaged by CPs (78), and it was found that ~3,000 nt is the optimum length. This is consistent with the fact that each wild-type CCMV virion contains about that amount of RNA, in the form of genomic RNA1 (~3,200 nt), RNA2 (~2,800 nt), or RNA3 + RNA4 (~2,200 nt + ~800 nt = ~3,000 nt). When RNA is much longer than this, it is shared by two or more T=3 capsids, each packaging as close to 3,000 nt as possible—for example, 6,000-nt-long RNA is packaged into doublets, 9,000-nt-RNA into triplets, and 12,000-nt-RNA into quadruplets (8). In all instances, complete packaging is ensured by providing a protein:RNA mass ratio of ~4.2 (8), corresponding to the stoichiometry of wild-type virions, and by performing the assembly reaction in two steps. In the first step, the protein and RNA are mixed at neutral pH, at which protein–protein interactions are weak, forming disordered protein–RNA complexes; in the second step, the pH is reduced toward the isoelectric point of the CP, strengthening protein–protein attractions and driving the formation of well-ordered VLPs by the en masse pathway (47). These findings, and the physical biochemistry principles underlying them, are reviewed extensively elsewhere (79, 80).

MS2 VLPs.

In 1967, 27-nm spherical VLPs identical in size and curvature to native MS2 virions were reconstituted in vitro by mixing purified MS2 coat protein with RNA, marking the first demonstration that a spherical VLP could be assembled entirely from purified components (10). While these particles closely resembled natural virions in structure, they were not infectious, as infectivity requires the presence of a single copy of the viral maturation protein to initiate infection (81). Incorporation of the maturation protein into VLPs in vitro has proved challenging, as it must precisely substitute for one coat protein dimer during assembly (82). Nevertheless, there is a long and productive history of in vitro studies producing and characterizing noninfectious T=3 MS2 VLPs made of pure coat protein assembled around both genomic and heterologous RNAs (11).

These studies played a central role in shaping current understanding of how specific RNA sequences influence capsid assembly. Early biochemical experiments established that the translational repressor (TR) stem-loop—a short RNA hairpin located near the start of the replicase gene—binds the MS2 coat protein with high affinity (83). Structural resolution of the coat protein–TR complex (84), together with biochemical characterization of the residues required for binding (85), clarified the molecular mechanisms of RNA recognition. These findings led to the proposal that the TR loop could serve as a packaging signal by nucleating capsid assembly. However, in vitro assembly experiments later showed that efficient assembly also requires a longer RNA backbone, which provides nonspecific interactions and steric scaffolding (86). Building on these observations, more recent models have proposed that the MS2 genome contains multiple dispersed stem-loops—structurally similar to TR—that collectively serve as a packaging signal during assembly (87).

Recent single-particle kinetic experiments have provided direct evidence that MS2 VLPs assemble via a nucleation-and-growth mechanism (61). By tracking the number of coat proteins bound to individual surface-tethered RNA molecules over time, these experiments revealed that assembly begins with a stochastic nucleation event, in which an initially unstable cluster of coat proteins must reach a critical size—estimated to be a dozen or fewer subunits—before additional proteins can bind favorably. Once nucleation occurs, growth proceeds rapidly, yielding complete, well-formed capsids under moderate protein concentrations. At higher concentrations, however, the time required to form a critical nucleus becomes shorter than the time for the nucleus to grow into a complete capsid. Under such conditions overnucleation becomes common, leading to the formation of malformed, oversized monsters containing multiple incomplete or misconnected capsid fragments, akin to those found with overnucleated TMV VLPs.

Complementary advances in mapping protein–RNA binding in solution (88) and within intact capsids (89) have revealed the nature of these interactions in increasing detail. As single-particle methods continue to evolve, they provide a promising route for testing how specific features of RNA structure influence both the nucleation kinetics and the fidelity of capsid formation and RNA packaging. In Section 4.1.2, we discuss experiments in which MS2 CP is expressed in bacterial cells from a variety of different plasmids and the resulting VLPs are analyzed for their RNA content.

Sindbis virus.

Sindbis virus is included in this short compendium of in vitro reconstitutable viruses on a still more qualified basis than MS2. Whereas for MS2 the qualification is that only noninfectious VLPs can be synthesized from purified components—specifically, maturation protein–deficient nucleocapsid formed from CP and genomic or heterologous RNA—the in vitro synthesis of Sindbis virus or VLPs has not yet proved to be successful. This is because Sindbis is an enveloped virus, and therefore, its reconstitution requires working with lipid components and with transmembrane glycoproteins whose myriad functions are difficult to maintain in a test tube. More explicitly, one must first reconstitute the nucleocapsid portion of the virus or VLP and then get it wrapped by lipid bilayer membrane in which functional viral glycoproteins are incorporated in stoichiometric amounts, e.g., 240 copies of the E1 and E2 heterodimers. Functional here refers to the intracellular ends of these heterodimers binding in one-to-one interactions with the CPs (to stabilize the nucleocapsid) and their extracellular ends having strong affinity for host cell receptors (to ensure infectivity).

To date, despite some valiant attempts (90; A. Helenius, personal communication), satisfying these stringent criteria has not proved possible. But if reconstitution of an infectious enveloped virus is ever to succeed, it will most likely happen with Sindbis virus, for several compelling reasons. First, the Sindbis nucleocapsid—unlike those of the large majority of enveloped viruses—is a stoichiometrically precise highly ordered structure with T=4 icosahedral symmetry involving 240 CPs, which can be reconstituted in vitro from purified genomic RNA and CP (91, 92). In a related development, Sindbis E1 and E2 glycoproteins were reconstituted in liposomes that spontaneously shed lipid and reduce their size to that of the viral envelope with close packing of the membrane proteins (93). And earlier work with Sindbis’s alphavirus sibling, Semliki Forest virus, showed that judicious detergent treatment could remove its lipid plus glycoprotein envelope without solubilizing the membrane proteins (94). These observations/facts were the basis for attempts to reconstitute Sindbis in vitro by mixing (purified or in vitro reconstituted) nucleocapsids with glycoprotein-containing lipid bilayer that was removed from Sindbis virus and subjected to detergent dialysis (90). But the resulting enveloped virus particles were not monodisperse or infectious. In a related attempt, purified nucleocapsids were microinjected into BHK cells that had been transformed to express the Sindbis glycoproteins, and the secreted virus particles were shown to be infectious (95). Here one cannot quite declare victory for in vitro synthesis of the virus, because the most delicate step in the synthesis—the incorporation of functional glycoproteins in the wrapped lipid bilayer—was achieved by recruiting the highly evolved cellular pathway for synthesizing glycoproteins and secreting them to the plasma membrane so that the nucleocapsids can be enveloped by them as part of the budding exocytosis process. These experiments do suggest, however, that full in vitro reconstitution of the virus might indeed be carried out in the near future. More certainly, they connect directly with the in cellulo synthesis of Sindbis VLPs discussed in Section 4.1.4.

Hepatitis B virus and human immunodeficiency virus.

We mention these two particular viruses not only because they are among the most important human viral pathogens but because—among human pathogens—they have been studied most extensively from a physical point of view in vitro, particularly with regard to self-assembly of their nucleocapsids. In both cases, the form of the genome that is packaged is ssRNA, and in both cases, its selective packaging includes an RT protein. During the cytoplasmic packaging of the pregenomic RNA form of the HBV genome, it is bound specifically at a particular site by one copy of RT, but this process has not yet been recapitulated in cell-free systems (96). In the case of HIV, two copies of the RNA genome make up approximately half of the RNA content of the capsid, along with one copy each of the RT protein and the integrase protein, many copies of the nucleoprotein, and a roughly equal mass of cellular tRNA that facilitates the priming of reverse transcription. To date, the assembly of all these components has proven too complicated a process to be reconstituted from purified components, but there are many in vitro studies of capsid formation involving just the polyprotein Gag (from which the capsid, nucleocapsid, and matrix subunits arise by self-proteolysis) in the presence and absence of RNA (see 97-99).

IN CELLULO PACKAGING OF SSRNA INTO VIRUSES AND VLPS

Given that viral CPs spontaneously assemble in vitro to form ordered capsids around RNA, it is not surprising that VLPs can also be produced in cellulo by expressing the relevant proteins from a plasmid. In principle, the production of VLPs in cellulo has the advantage that the protocols are simpler and more readily scalable. However, the cellular environment is complicated, and unknown or hard-to-control factors can lead to variation in the fidelity of assembly and packaging.

Making VLPs In Cellulo That Can Be Compared with VLPs Made In Vitro

In the following subsections we address the open question of whether—or to what extent—VLPs produced in cells adopt the correct structures and package the correct RNA.

Brome mosaic virus VLPs made in yeast.

The first in cellulo study of VLP formation providing direct connections to in vitro synthesis on the one hand and to wild-type infection on the other was designed and carried out for BMV in 1999 (100), a little over 30 years after CCMV was the first spherical virus to be reconstituted from purified components (75). Like the later in cellulo studies of MS2, TMV, and Sindbis VLPs that we discuss immediately below—and the in cellulo studies of AAV, LV, and designer-protein systems that are not reconstitutable in vitro, which are discussed in the following section (Section 4.2)—the idea is to transform cells to express packaging proteins and an RNA that is specifically packaged by them. In the BMV study, the only relevant packaging protein is the CP, encoded by RNA4, which is less than 1,000 nt long. Accordingly, when it is expressed in yeast the nuclease-resistant VLPs that are generated are found to contain the CP-encoding RNA and—significantly—to consist of 60 CP dimers with T=2 organization rather than the 90 dimers that make up wild-type T=3 particles. When RNA2 of BMV, with a length of almost 3,000 nt, is generated in the same cells, the resulting VLPs contain predominantly RNA2 (rather than CP-encoding RNA4 or cellular mRNAs) and have T=3 structure, establishing the ability of the CP to prefer different curvatures according to the RNA with which it is interacting. Subsequent studies have shown in cellulo synthesis of T=3 structures by wild-type CP and various other structures with mutant CP (101).

MS2 VLPs made in Escherichia coli.

MS2 VLPs can be produced in very high yield—tens of milligrams per liter of liquid culture—using standard laboratory strains of Escherichia coli by expressing the MS2 coat protein from an inducible plasmid (102). The resulting VLPs have capsid structures that are identical to those produced in vitro, but their RNA content is heterogeneous, with a mixture of transcripts being packaged, including the mRNA encoding the coat protein, transcripts derived from the plasmid backbone, and various host-cell RNAs. Early work showed that nonviral RNAs could be targeted for encapsidation by inserting a high-affinity TR-loop motif into their sequence (103), an approach that has since been adapted to produce armored RNA (104)—synthetic, capsid-protected RNA molecules that are resistant to degradation and have found use as stable controls in molecular diagnostics (105).

However, simply appending a TR loop to an RNA molecule is not sufficient to recapitulate the high-fidelity packaging observed in natural MS2 infection. High-throughput sequencing has shown that native MS2 particles package their own genome with ~99% fidelity, with host-derived RNA making up less than 1% of the encapsidated material (106). In contrast, the fidelity of RNA encapsidation in VLPs produced from plasmid-based expression depends strongly on both the length of the RNA transcript and the presence of multiple packaging-signal stem-loops (106). Despite efforts to optimize these factors, VLPs produced in E. coli have not yet achieved the selectivity observed in native infection. This suggests that additional viral factors—particularly the RNA replicase, which amplifies the viral RNA relative to the background concentration of cellular RNA, or the maturase, which binds to the ends of the viral RNA and could facilitate its packaging—may play an important role in in cellulo packaging fidelity.

In parallel with these studies, extensive work has been done to characterize how mutations in the MS2 coat protein affect RNA binding (107). Recently, a comprehensive high-throughput screening approach measured the effects of every possible single-point mutation in the coat protein on capsid assembly and RNA packaging (108). This approach identified a particular mutation that results in the formation of smaller, T=1, capsids, rather than the canonical T=3 structure (109). In addition, advances in cryo-EM imaging—performed without imposing icosahedral symmetry (110)—have revealed that even the wild-type MS2 coat protein can form a mixture of capsid morphologies under certain conditions (111), while specific mutants can assemble into an even broader range of noncanonical geometries (112).

Tobacco mosaic virus VLPs made in bacteria and yeast.

TMV VLPs have been generated in bacteria and yeast by simple transformation with a plasmid encoding the CP gene and by cotransformation with a plasmid that is transcribed in the cell to give RNA containing the TMV packaging signal—the OAS—that has high affinity for TMV CP and serves as the nucleation site for initiation of capsid growth. In both cases, VLPs are formed that contain a wide variety of cellular RNA molecules, and even in the second case, there is only a slight preference (8%) for the OAS-containing molecule (113). In contrast, the main reasons why TMV virions produced in plant cells predominantly contain the viral genome (71) are because it is the only RNA molecule in the cell that is replicated, and therefore present in much larger numbers, and the only molecule that contains the OAS. It follows that if we want to generate TMV VLPs in cellulo that predominantly contain a particular RNA of interest we need that molecule to be replicated strongly.

Sindbis virus enveloped VLPs made in mammalian cells.

As mentioned in Section 1 and elaborated on a bit in Section 3.2.5, Sindbis virus is a good choice for making VLPs in cellulo because of how exceptionally ordered this particular enveloped virus is and because its virus-derived vectors have been investigated extensively. Its major glycoproteins, the transmembrane E1 and E2, form heterodimers that interact one-to-one with the CPs of the genome-containing nucleocapsid, correspondingly showing the same T=4 icosahedral symmetry. The viral one-molecule genome is bicistronic, with the first open reading frame (ORF) encoding the replication-related nonstructural proteins and the second encoding the capsid and membrane proteins. By splitting the one-molecule genome into two molecules, one including the first ORF and the other including the second ORF, it has been shown (114) that—by cotransfecting these molecules into host cells—one can make noninfectious particles consisting of the same capsid shell and the same enveloping lipid bilayer, but with the first ORF molecule, instead of the original genome, packaged in the capsid. This molecule is preferentially packaged over the second ORF molecule—the helper molecule whose only job is to provide the structural proteins for making vector particles—because it is closer to the genome length and uniquely contains the packaging signal. Further, because this molecule encodes the replicase-related proteins that bind it and replicate it strongly, it is a self-replicating mRNA—or replicon—that can be fused to a gene of interest that one wants to amplify. In this sense, while the vector particles produced by cotransfection can bud out of cells and enter new ones, resulting in replication of their RNA content, the particles are not infectious, because there are no structural proteins in the new cells to package the amplified RNA into nucleocapsids and wrap them in envelope for budding. But a large number of copies of the protein encoded by the fused gene of interest are synthesized, which can be used as reporters or for therapeutic purposes.

Making VLPs In Cellulo That Cannot Be Synthesized In Vitro

Below, we turn to in cellulo systems for which no corresponding in vitro reconstitution has yet been achieved. We first discuss natural virus-evolved systems such as AAV and LV vectors, which have been adapted for gene delivery, and then we consider de novo designed protein cages that have been evolved under selection pressure to package their own mRNAs. These examples highlight both the power and the limitations of in cellulo–only routes to RNA and DNA packaging.

Natural (virus-evolved) systems.

What we mean by natural or virus-evolved in cellulo–generated VLPs is best illustrated by the examples of LV and AAV particles. Like the BMV, MS2, TMV, and Sindbis virus vectors discussed in Section 4.1, these particles also do not contain complete viral genomes but rather just selected viral genes plus a gene of interest—in the form of positive-sense ssRNA for LV VLPs and ssDNA for AAV VLPs. And, instead of the gene being directly translated into protein in the cytoplasm, it is turned into dsDNA and replicated and transcribed in the nucleus. Correspondingly, instead of cotransfection of gene-of-interest and helper mRNAs that initiate cytoplasmic synthesis of gene-specific VLPs, the LV and AAV VLPs are synthesized by providing producer cells with DNA plasmids that separately generate the proteins that replicate and package the gene of interest.

For example, AAV particles are made from three separate plasmids (115, 116): one that encodes the gene of interest, flanked by the inverted terminal repeats necessary for replication and chromosomal integration; another that encodes the AAV capsid and replication proteins; and a third that encodes accessory helper virus proteins that act as cofactors to facilitate efficient replication. Similarly, LV particles are made by transformation of producer cells with several plasmids (117), for example, one encoding a gene of interest, flanked by long terminal repeats; one encoding the Gag and Pol viral genes; and one encoding a desired envelope membrane protein, such as that of vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV-G).

AAV and LV systems are currently the predominant choice of vector particle for gene delivery, even though they are not stoichiometrically precise and are, indeed, quite polymorphic in their protein and nucleic acid content. For example, in the case of AAV vectors, many of the particles are empty or are associated with an ensemble of different protein compositions (118) and contain endogenous RNA and ssDNA other than the gene of interest (119). And LV vector particles are polymorphic in shape and size, contain endogenous RNAs (predominantly tRNAs), and integrate dsDNA into the target cell, with a risk of oncogenicity due to mis-integration (120).

Synthetic (directed-evolution) systems.

Here, an elegantly simple and fundamental question is posed: Is it possible to design an mRNA molecule that is packaged into a spherical nucleocapsid by the protein that it encodes? Equivalently, can one design a protein that—when synthesized in the cell cytoplasm—forms a single-molecule-thick shell containing the mRNA from which it was translated? The answer is yes but a rather limited yes.

One approach involves generating a combinatorial library of de novo designed proteins in E. coli and then evolving them under selective pressure to form robust nucleocapsids that contain their encoding mRNA and to have long in vivo circulation times (121). RNA packaging is enhanced by optimizing the distribution of positively charged residues on the capsid inner surface. But the degree of packaging selectivity is low, no more than 10%; that is, roughly one out of every ten particles contains the CP-encoding mRNA.

Another approach starts with a particular cage-forming enzyme, Aquifex aeolicus lumazine synthase (AaLS), which naturally self-assembles into a 60-subunit T=1 shell that encapsulates riboflavin synthase via a small recognition peptide, thereby forming a protected active complex that catalyzes the final steps of riboflavin synthesis (122). A circularly permuted mutant of AaLS is used that repositions the N and C termini from the outside to the inside of the capsid, thereby enabling genetic fusion of nucleic acid–binding domain peptides. Error-prone RT-PCR is then employed to generate a combinatoric library that is subjected to successive rounds of RNA-packaging pressure, each new round starting with the RNA extracted from capsids isolated from bacterial expression and used in turn to generate a new combinatoric library for transforming bacteria. Here, again, a success rate of approximately 10% is achieved (122). Subsequent approaches have given rise to higher RNA specificity, reaching nearly 70% (123) but still shy of the 99% success rates of some natural viruses (124-126). No matter how well designed and focused the protocol for laboratory-directed evolution, it is hard to compete with a billion years of natural evolution.
