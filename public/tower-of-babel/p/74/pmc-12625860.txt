Stimuli-responsive STING nanovaccine for systemic therapy of HPV-induced cancers
Authors: Shuang Chen, Shuyue Ye, Gang Huang, Zhichen Sun, Qiang Feng, Maggie Wang, Raymundo Pantoja, Baran D. Sumer, Jinming Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceNanoparticle vaccine (or nanovaccine) holds considerable promise for the treatment of cancer. Herein, we defined biochemical mechanisms for a cooperative strategy combining stimuli-responsive stimulator of interferon genes (STING) activation with cell-tropic delivery of tumor antigen. The STING nanovaccine enhances antigen and adjuvant codelivery to antigen-presenting cells, thereby improving tumor immunogenicity. This vaccine design masks systemic toxicity while effectively treating both localized and metastatic tumors in mouse models, potentially paving the way for clinical evaluation in patients with advanced human papillomavirus-induced cancers.

Vaccines play a pivotal role in public health, particularly in their prophylactic use against infectious diseases. Recently, therapeutic vaccines against cancer have been actively pursued to stimulate T cell immunity against various tumors (1–3). Despite the progress, the effectiveness of these vaccines remains modest across many oncologic indications. Although depot-forming water-in-oil adjuvant and antigen systems can improve immunogenicity (4, 5), booster immunizations can cause T cell sequestration at the vaccine site, leading to T cell exhaustion and deletion (6). Inefficient codelivery of adjuvants and antigens to secondary lymphoid organs (SLOs) and tumor microenvironment, and subsequent low accumulation in antigen-presenting cells, may lead to weak immune stimulation or even immune tolerance (7, 8). To optimize T cell priming and vaccine efficacy, simultaneous innate immune activation and antigen processing and presentation are essential for creating the immunological synapse (9–13).

To bolster the vaccine efficacy, many agonists of pathogen recognition receptors have been investigated as adjuvants (14–17). Among these, stimulator of interferon genes (STING) has emerged as a potent immune target for vaccine development and cancer immunotherapy (18–20). Activation of the STING pathway initiates a cascade of host defense responses, including the production of type I interferons and proinflammatory cytokines, leading to enhanced antigen presentation, T cell priming, and ultimately, antitumor immunity (18, 21). For treatment of advanced diseases, systemic administration is desirable but it has been limited due to off-target toxicity. Balancing immune activation with circumvention of immune-related toxicity is important to achieve safe and efficacious immunotherapy (22, 23).

In our previous studies, we reported that ultra-pH-sensitive polymers, including PC7A and PSC7A, enhance the immune response through a STING-dependent pathway (24, 25). These polymers bind to different sites on the STING protein compared to small-molecule agonists such as cGAMP, leading to a prolonged activation (26). Combination of polymer and small-molecule agonists is able to potentiate the immune response and enhance the efficacy of the treatment (27). In addition, polymeric nanoparticles are found to deliver antigen with high efficiency (24, 25). Our current research is dedicated to developing a more potent systemic tumor vaccine that not only targets tumors but also activates SLOs, thus maximizing therapeutic outcomes by ensuring a virtuous cancer-immunity cycle (Scheme 1).

STING nanovaccine is designed to deliver tumor antigens (Ag) and STING agonists (diABZI, PSC7A) to the tumor and secondary lymphoid organs (SLOs) to activate antitumor immunity. The nanovaccine stays in the assembled “OFF” state to minimize systemic toxicity, and becomes activated in the antigen-presenting cells in tumors and SLOs to boost T cell immunity.

Here, we report the development of a stimuli-responsive nanovaccine composed of a STING-activating polymer and HPV16 E7 protein for the treatment of late-stage HPV-positive tumors. To boost STING activation, we conjugated diABZI to the biodegradable, STING-activating polymer (PSC7A) using an azobenzene linker. Under normal physiological conditions (e.g., blood), the E7 protein is encapsulated in the polymeric micelles with masked immune activity. Upon reaching tumors and SLOs, the nanovaccine is selectively taken up by antigen-presenting cells [dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages], where acidic pH and azoreductase enzymes (e.g., NQO1), which is elevated under tumor hypoxic conditions (28, 29) and breaks azo bonds (30, 31) selectively dissociate micelles and cleave diABZI from the polymers for cooperative antigen release and STING activation. Systemic administration of the nanovaccine incurred mild toxicity but dramatically boosted tumor-specific T cell immunity against late-stage HPV-positive tumors with distal and metastatic foci, where local administration (e.g., subcutaneous or intratumoral injection) is less effective. The integration of stimuli-responsive nanotechnology with antigen delivery and innate activation may prove important in harnessing the STING pathway for cancer immunotherapy.

Results

Engineering Nanovaccine for Antigen and STING Agonist Delivery.

We chemically conjugated a small-molecule STING agonist, diABZI [EC50 ~20 nM, (32)] to the biodegradable STING-activating polymer, poly(ethylene oxide)-b-PSC7A [PSC7A, Kd ~30 nM, (25)], using an azobenzene linker (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The average repeating unit of PSC7A polymer is 125 and each polymer contains approximately 7 molecules of diABZI based on the UV-Vis and 1H NMR analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). pH titration experiments show the PEG-b-P(SC7A-azo-diABZI) polymer (or STING polymer) maintained the ultrasensitive pH response inherent to the parent polymer PSC7A (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). At pH above the apparent pKa (6.9), the polymers self-assemble into micelle nanoparticles (STING NP) with a hydrodynamic diameter of 24 ± 3 nm by dynamic light scattering analysis (Fig. 1B). HPV16 E7 protein [98 amino acid, isoelectric point 5.4, (33)] was physically mixed with STING NP at room temperature for 1 h with a 10% w/w loading ratio. This process resulted in the quantitative encapsulation of E7 proteins within the STING NP as indicated by the disappearance of E7 peak by FPLC analysis (Fig. 1C). Encapsulation of E7 protein slightly increased the hydrodynamic diameter of the nanoparticles (28 ± 3 nm) with a polydispersity index of 0.33 (Fig. 1B).

We investigated the stimuli-responsive release of diABZI from the STING NPs (Fig. 1D). We used acidic pH (6.5) and NQO1/NADH (200 µg/mL and 1 mM, respectively) as stimulants. DiABZI was efficiently released (85% in the first 6 h, 98% after 24 h incubation) when both acidic pH and NQO1/NADH signals were present (Fig. 1 D and E). In contrast, less than 5% of diABZI was cleaved even after 24 h when only one of the stimulus conditions was met (i.e., pH 6.5 solution without NQO1/NADH, or pH 7.4 solution with NQO1/NADH). When neither condition was present, negligible release of diABZI was observed. We evaluated STING activation in THP-1-ISG cells after STING NPs were pretreated under different conditions. Specifically, STING NPs were incubated at pH 6.5 or 7.4 in the presence or absence of NQO1/NADH, after which the medium was added to THP-1-ISG cell culture to measure interferon-β (IFN-β) signals. PSC7A NP physically encapsulated with diABZI (PSC7A/diABZI, without covalent conjugation) was used as a control. Data show STING NP resulted in ~70-fold IFN-β elevation when both pH 6.5 and NQO1/NADH dual stimuli exist, which is markedly higher than the other conditions (Fig. 1F). In contrast, PSC7A/diABZI NP showed strong STING activation when incubated at pH 6.5 or 7.4 with comparable IFN-β response to free diABZI, suggesting the premature release of diABZI from PSC7A NP even at neutral pH (Fig. 1G). This dual-stimuli responsive design of STING NP is intended to mask the immune-related toxicity under normal physiological conditions, while under acidic and hypoxic conditions in tumors [where NQO1 level is high, (28, 29)], STING signaling is selectively activated for immune stimulation.

Pharmacokinetic, Biodistribution, and Cell Tropism Studies.

For pharmacokinetic and biodistribution (PKBD) studies, we fluorescently labeled E7 protein and PSC7A polymer with tetramethyl rhodamine (TMR) and Cy5 dyes, respectively. We employed HPV16 E6/E7-induced TC-1 tumors (~120 mm3) in C57BL/6 mice in these studies (Fig. 2A). E7-TMR/PSC7A-Cy5 STING nanovaccine was intravenously administered in mice and blood was collected at different time points to evaluate the plasma concentrations. Data show almost identical blood clearance kinetics for E7-TMR and Cy5-labeled polymer (Fig. 2B), suggesting the E7 antigen was stably encapsulated in the polymer nanoparticle during blood circulation. The blood half-lives for α- and β-elimination phases are 0.06 and 3.1 h for E7-TMR, and 0.06 and 3.3 h for STING polymer, respectively. The organ distribution of STING nanovaccine at 24 h after intravenous (i.v.) injection shows the highest uptake in the spleen (40% ± 3% ID/g), followed by the liver (21% ± 3% ID/g), tumor (14% ± 5% ID/g), and tumor-draining lymph nodes (tdLN, 13% ± 2% ID/g) (Fig. 2C).

We further analyzed the cellular distribution of the STING nanovaccine in the TC-1 tumors, tdLNs, and spleen (Fig. 2D). Results show that the STING nanovaccine was preferentially taken up by immune cells (CD45+) over tumor cells (CD45−) in the TC-1 tumors. Within the immune cell populations, NK cells, macrophages, and DCs are the predominant cell types that took up the nanoparticles with negligible uptake by the T cells. In the spleen and tdLN, STING nanovaccine is mostly internalized by DCs and macrophages (Fig. 2D). These results highlight the STING nanovaccine’s selective accumulation in antigen-presenting cells in the tumor and SLOs for T cell activation.

PSC7A Polymer Increases NQO1 Expression in APCs for Elevated STING Activation.

NQO1 is a homodimeric, two-electron oxidoreductase with the ability to reduce a broad range of quinone and organic substrates (34, 35). In the hypoxic environment often found in solid tumors, it contributes to cellular adaptation by regulating redox homeostasis and protecting cells from oxidative damage (28, 36). While the expression and functional implications of NQO1 are well documented in tumors, its expression and regulatory mechanisms in secondary lymphoid tissues are less understood. Based on the cell tropism of STING NP uptake in myeloid cells in the spleen and tdLNs, we investigated the expression levels of NQO1 in a representative macrophage (RAW264.7) and DC lines (DC2.4). We treated these cells with STING NP and several controls. PSDBA NP (pKa = 5.2) serves as a nanoparticle control with buffering ability for endosomes but no endosomolytic or STING activating properties (24). Free diABZI and PSC7A NP (without diABZI conjugation) are also included.

After 24 h incubation, the cells were collected, lysed, and analyzed by Western blot for protein expression (37) and Methyl Red assay for NQO1 reducing functions (35). Data show treatment with PSDBA NP and diABZI did not lead to elevated NQO1 expressions in RAW264.7 and DC2.4 cells compared to the PBS control (Fig. 2E). In contrast, PSC7A NP and STING NP treatments increased the NQO1 expressions in these cell lines with a more pronounced effect from PSC7A NP. Protein expression by Western blot analysis is further corroborated by the Methyl Red assay where PSC7A NP and STING NP induced high NQO1 expressions compared to PBS, PSDBA, and diABZI (Fig. 2F). These data indicate PSC7A NP and STING NP stimulate NQO1 expressions and their reducing capacities in these cell lines. We further investigated the downstream IFN-β levels after treating these cells with STING NP. Data show IFN-β levels increased 40 folds for DC2.4 and 8 folds RAW264.7 after STING NP treatment over PBS control, and the effect can be abrogated by the cotreatment with dicoumarol (38), an inhibitor of NQO1 (Fig. 2G).

STING Nanovaccine Elicits Potent Antitumor Efficacy in TC-1 Tumors.

To assess the effect of the STING nanovaccine, we first investigated the HPV16 E6/E7 transfected TC-1 tumor model in C57BL/6J mice. When tumors reached volumes of 100 to 150 mm3 (10 to 12 d after inoculation), the mice were intravenously injected with different doses of STING nanovaccine (1.5, 5, and 15 mg/kg, 10% E7 loading density) in the phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) on days 10 and 15 post tumor cell inoculation (Fig. 3A). The highest dose of 15 mg/kg displays the best tumor growth inhibition and complete tumor regressions (Fig. 3B). Body weight measurements show weight loss for the PBS and 1.5 mg/kg group, likely due to the tumor burden, whereas no significant weight changes were observed for the 5 and 15 mg/kg dose groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S4).

Several control groups were included to evaluate the antitumor efficacy of STING nanovaccine over its components (Fig. 3 C–F and SI Appendix, Figs. S5 and S6). Without E7 antigen, diABZI or PSC7A NP alone resulted in a small degree of tumor growth inhibition compared to PBS control, whereas STING NP displayed marked improvement over either agonist alone (Fig. 3C). E7 antigen alone (same antigen dose as in STING nanovaccine) had negligible effect on tumor growth over PBS control (Fig. 3D). Mixing of E7 with diABZI and PSC7A NP showed improved antitumor response over diABZI and PSC7A NP alone, with stronger effect from the E7-PSC7A NP group, likely due to the improved E7 antigen delivery by the nanoparticle. E7-encapsulated STING NP, or STING nanovaccine, illustrates the best tumor regression and long-term survival over STING NP and the other E7-adjuvant controls (Fig. 3 D–F). These results highlight the cooperative design of the STING nanovaccine, in which each of its component, PSC7A NP, diABZI, and E7 antigen, plays an irreplaceable role in eliciting robust antitumor effects against the aggressive TC-1 tumor.

We investigated different administration routes (subcutaneous = s.c., intratumoral = i.t., and intravenous = i.v.) on the antitumor effect in the primary and distal TC-1 tumor models. Primary and distal tumors were inoculated on the right and left flanks of mice on day 0 and day 2, respectively (Fig. 4). Nanovaccines were administered subcutaneously (s.c.) near the tail base, in the right primary tumor (i.t.), or through the tail vein (i.v.) at the same vaccine dose (15 mg/kg) on days 10 and 15. All the administration methods inhibited tumor growth in both primary and distal sites compared to the PBS control. The s.c. and i.t. treatments are effective in treating primary tumors but less effective in distal tumors (Fig. 4C), whereas i.v. administration achieved the greatest efficacy in reducing the growth of both primary and distal tumors with significantly improved survival outcomes (Fig. 4 B–D and SI Appendix, Fig. S7).

STING Nanovaccine Activates Antigen-Presenting Cells for T Cell Priming.

We investigated immune cell activations in tumor and SLOs after treatment with STING nanovaccine over its molecular components. We systematically evaluated the potentiation of myeloid cell and lymphocyte compartments in these tissues by flow cytometry (gating strategies are shown in SI Appendix, Figs. S8 and S9, respectively). In the first cohort of experiments (Fig. 5), we examined the immune cell profiles from STING nanovaccine over E7 antigen and STING NP (without E7); in the second cohort, we compared the STING nanovaccine over E7-encapsulated PSC7A NP or E7 mixture with diABZI to evaluate the combined adjuvant effect of P(SC7A-azo-diABZI) polymer over either component alone (SI Appendix, Fig. S10).

In the first cohort of the study, we observed dramatic elevation of CD86 expressions in the DC and macrophage populations after treatment with either STING nanovaccine or STING NP (Fig. 5 A–E). Over 50% of DC and macrophage populations are CD86 positive in the spleen after either treatment, compared to less than 10% for E7 or PBS control. A slightly less percentage (40-50%) of DCs and macrophages are CD86 positive in the tdLN than spleen after STING nanovaccine and STING NP treatment. Furthermore, we observed the repolarization of M2-like macrophages (CD206hiCD86low) to a more M1-like state (CD206lowCD86hi) in mice treated with STING nanovaccine in the spleen and the tdLN (Fig. 5F). Despite similar myeloid cell activation by the STING NP and STING nanovaccine groups, STING nanovaccine drove a markedly higher level of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells over STING NP and other groups (Fig. 5G). In TC-1 tumors, over 80% of CD8+ T cells are E7-specific, compared to less than 20% for STING NP, E7, and PBS control. This difference persisted in the spleen and tdLN. In the tumor microenvironment, the CD8+ T, CD4+ T, NK, and NKT cells were all significantly increased in the STING nanovaccine group over control groups (Fig. 5H), which may cooperatively contribute to the antitumor immunity. These data suggest E7-specific CD8+ T cells are the driving factor for elevated antitumor immunity, which is corroborated by tumor regression and long-term survival outcomes (Figs. 3 and 4).

In the second cohort of the study, we delineated the effect of immune cell activation from combined diABZI-PSC7A activation to the individual component alone. At 24 h after the first vaccination, we observed a higher level of CD86 costimulation on both DC and macrophage populations by diABZI+E7 over the STING nanovaccine group (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A and B). The PSC7A NP group displayed a mild level of CD86 costimulation mostly in the spleen. Despite the highest level of DC and macrophage potentiation, diABZI+E7 treatment did not significantly increase the formation of E7-specific CD8+ T cells in TC-1 tumors, spleen, or tdLN compared to the PBS group (SI Appendix, Fig. S10C). In contrast, STING nanovaccine treatment yielded the highest level of E7-specific CD8+ T cells. Despite the mild effect in myeloid cell activation, the PSC7A-E7 NP group produced higher level of E7-specific CD8+ T cells in TC-1 tumors and tdLN than the diABZI+E7 group. Interestingly, the PSC7A-E7 NP group also led to significantly increased CD4+ T cells and NKT cells over PBS control (whereas the diABZI+E7 group did not) (SI Appendix, Fig. S10D), which correlates with slightly improved tumor growth inhibition by PSC7A-E7 NP over the diABZI+E7 group (Fig. 3D). Overall, these data highlight the importance of cooperative antigen delivery with robust STING activation to achieve efficient antigen-specific T cells to drive antitumor immunity.

Finally, we evaluated the PD-1 and Tim-3 expressions on the CD8+ T cells after STING nanovaccine treatment and compared them with E7 or STING NP group. Data show significantly higher percentage of PD-1+CD8+ T cells in the tumor, spleen, and tumor-draining lymph nodes over the other treatment groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S11A). Interestingly, the STING nanovaccine group also showed the highest percentage (14%) of terminally exhausted PD-1+Tim-3+CD8+ T cells in the TC-1 tumors over control groups, but not in the spleen or tumor-draining lymph nodes (SI Appendix, Fig. S11B). These data suggest a potential synergy in combining STING nanovaccine with checkpoint blockade therapy to avoid T cell exhaustion in the tumor microenvironment.

STING Nanovaccine Clears MLM3 Metastases in the Lung.

We also evaluated the STING nanovaccine in a metastatic MLM3 tumor model in the lung. In this model, male C57BL/6J mice were inoculated i.v. with the mEERL lung metastasis cell line clone 3 (MLM3), which was transfected by HPV16 E6/E7. According to the treatment regimen outlined in Fig. 6A, mice treated with PBS showed notably large metastatic nodules on day 35. Anti-PD1 treatment (i.p. injection, 200 μg for three times) did not significantly reduced the number of lung nodules, however, it showed improved survival over the PBS control. STING nanovaccine showed the dramatic reduction of metastatic burden over both the PBS and anti-PD1 groups (Fig. 6B), which is further supported by histological analysis (Fig. 6C). The survival outcome is further improved when STING nanovaccine is combined with anti-PD-1 (aPD-1) over STING nanovaccine alone, where all and 71% of the mice are still alive at 60 d post treatment, respectively (Fig. 6D).

Safety Evaluations of STING Nanovaccine.

To assess the systemic toxicity of STING NPs, we measured the plasma levels of several cytokines at 6 and 24 h and liver and kidney functions at 24 h after i.v. administration (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). The cytokine panel included interleukin-6 (IL-6), monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). As expected, free diABZI (1.5 mg/kg) triggered significant elevations of IFN-γ, IL-6, MCP-1, and TNF-α at 6 h but went down at 24 h after injection. In contrast, STING NP (15 mg/kg) and PSC7A NP (15 mg/kg) induced minimal or undetectable levels of inflammatory cytokines comparable to PBS control (SI Appendix, Fig. S12B). We also measured liver and kidney functions, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CREA). Data indicate comparable levels of AST and ALT in the group that received STING NP and PBS. Significant increase of AST and ALT levels was observed with free diABZI group over the PBS control (SI Appendix, Fig. S12C). The levels of CREA and BUN for all groups were within the expected span of variations (SI Appendix, Fig S12D). IFN-β and CXCL10 expressions in tumors and different organs were analyzed to evaluate STING activations in mice bearing TC-1 tumors treated with STING nanovaccine. We included free diABZI mixed with E7 antigen (diABZI and E7 doses match those in STING nanovaccine) as a control. Free diABZI+E7 group showed about two times higher IFN-β and CXCL10 levels in the kidney and heart, with comparable inductions in tumors, spleen, and liver compared to STING nanovaccine (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). These results illustrate a reduced systemic toxicity for stimuli-responsive design of STING NP, which is further supported by the lack of weight loss in the mice treated with STING nanovaccine (SI Appendix, Fig. S4).

Discussion

Nanoparticles are of great interest as carriers of subunit vaccines owing to their ability to codeliver antigen and immune adjuvant, promote cross-presentation of antigens in antigen-presenting cells, and augment cellular and humoral immunity (3). In conventional vaccines, inadequate codelivery of antigens and adjuvants to the SLOs and tumors may lead to suboptimal antitumor response while producing strong immune-related toxicity. In this study, we report a stimuli-responsive nanoparticle-based vaccine design (Fig. 1A). This construct leverages the STING signaling pathway, known for its role in innate immune responses through the production of type I interferons and proinflammatory cytokines (18, 19). Our prior STING nanoparticle design consists of physical encapsulation of cGAMP inside the PSC7A polymeric micelles (27). Although this formulation is sufficiently stable for intratumoral administration, it is not optimal for intravenous usage because of premature release of cGAMP during blood circulation. In this study, we chose diABZI for its high potency (EC50 = 20 nM) (32) and ease of chemical modification for covalent conjugation to the PSC7A polymer. The introduction of stimuli-responsive linkers further improved the stability and safety of the nanovaccine design. STING activation through PSC7A-azo-diABZI polymer in coordination with antigen delivery addresses the need for a balanced immune activation. Through the stimuli-responsive design, we overcome the inherent limitations of always ON STING agonists which trigger cytokine release syndrome but is limited by insufficient T cell priming (e.g., as indicated by the diABZI+E7 control). This systemic approach not only treats primary tumor sites but is also effective at eradicating distant metastases (Figs. 4 and 6), showcasing a broadened therapeutic window.

Our approach introduces an activation mechanism involving NQO1—an enzyme that is elevated in hypoxic tumor microenvironment (28, 29) and plays important roles in redox biology and detoxification (37, 39). We serendipitously found that PSC7A polymer is able to elevate NQO1 expressions in the antigen-presenting cells, which resulted in diABZI release and STING activation which can be blocked by dicoumarol, an NQO1 inhibitor (Fig. 2 E–G). Our prior studies have shown that besides STING activation, PC7A/PSC7A polymers are disruptive to endosomal membranes allowing efficient cytosolic delivery of tumor antigens upon pH activation in the early endosomes (24, 25). To investigate whether STING activation or endosomolytic properties of PSC7A is driving the NQO1 expression, we investigated free diABZI that activates STING but does not disrupt endosomes, and PSDBA polymer that buffers endosomal pH but does not damage endosomal membranes. Data show neither free diABZI nor PSDBA induced high level of NQO1 expressions in the DC2.4 or RAW264.7 cells, suggesting endosomolytic property of PSC7A is responsible for the transcriptional activation of NQO1. Future studies are warranted to elucidate the biochemical mechanism of PSC7A-induced NQO1 expression, and whether intracellular reactive oxygen species play a role in the process. Nevertheless, the unexpected finding illustrates the synergy of azobenzene linker with PSC7A polymer in NQO1-mediated release of diABZI, which may be extendable to other polymer–drug conjugates to improve the therapeutic window.

A key element of our nanovaccine design is the engagement of SLOs, such as the spleen (40, 41) or tumor-draining lymph nodes (10, 11, 42), where nanovaccines are readily taken up by the APCs (Fig. 2 C and D). Since the biodistribution studies were performed at 24 h, which is longer than the circulation half-lives of STING nanovaccine (i.e., ~3 h for the β-phase), it is conceivable that STING activation at earlier times may affect metabolism and clearance mechanism to boost their uptake in the immune organs. Through combined antigen delivery and STING activation, significant control and elimination of distal or metastatic tumors are achieved from systemic vaccination but not by local administration (Figs. 4 and 6). These data suggest the benefit of targeting the STING pathway not only in tumors but also in SLOs for the generation of antigen-specific cytotoxic T cells. Our data revealed spleen contained higher percentage (~30%) of E7-specific CD8+ T cells over that (~5%) in the tumor-draining lymph nodes (Fig. 5G). However, it is not clear whether the lower percentage in the tumor-draining lymph node is a result of T cell migration from the node to the TC-1 tumors. Future work is necessary to evaluate the role of spleen versus lymph nodes in nanovaccine-mediated tumor rejection. By applying a stimuli-responsive strategy to mitigate systemic toxicity, the current design offers a viable strategy against late-stage metastatic tumors. This approach, when combined with immune checkpoint blockade therapy, can further improve the antitumor response, as demonstrated in our preclinical MLM3 tumor model (Fig. 6). The current E7 nanovaccine is designed to treat HPV-induced cancers, the incidence of which especially head and neck oropharyngeal cancer is growing in the United States and worldwide. The related nanoparticle design is expected to be applicable to other cancer indications by the choice of different tumor-specific antigens.

In conclusion, systemically administered, stimuli-responsive STING nanovaccine is effectively boosting the tumor-specific T cell immunity against late-stage cancers. By targeting and activating the immune system’s natural processes in a controlled manner, the nanovaccine paves the way for developing safer, more effective therapeutic options for a range of cancers, potentially leading to better patient outcomes and broader applicability of immunotherapeutic strategies.

Methods

Cell Culture.

THP1-ISG cells were provided by Dr. Z. J. Chen (UT Southwestern). TC-1 cells were provided by Dr. T. C. Wu (John Hopkins University). MLM3 cells were purchased in Applied Biological Materials Inc. (Cat. No. T8311). All cells were cultured at 37° C in an atmosphere of 5% (v/v) CO2. THP1-ISG cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin antibiotics. TC-1 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin antibiotics. MLM3 cells were cultured in a mixture of DMEM and Ham’s Nutrient Mixture F12 Media (Hams F12) with 3:1 (v:v) ratio with 10% FBS, 0.5 µg/mL hydrocortisone, 5 µg/mL transferrin, 5 µg/mL insulin, 1.36 ng/mL Tri-Iodo-Thyronine, 5 ng/mL mouse EGF and 1% penicillin–streptomycin antibiotics.

Formulation and Characterization of STING Nanovaccine.

PEG-b-P(SC7A-azo-diABZI) NP (STING NP) was used as an example. In a typical procedure, 1 mg PEG-b-P(SC7A-azo-diABZI) polymer was dissolved in PBS at pH 5.0. NaOH solution was added to adjust the final pH to ~7.4 under stirring. The solution was further stirred for 30 min followed by addition of 100 µg E7 protein (GenScript USA, Inc.) and kept for additional 30 min to form PEG-b-P(SC7A-azo-diABZI)/E7 nanovaccine (STING nanovaccine). The obtained STING NP or STING nanovaccine was analyzed using dynamic light scattering (Zeta Sizer, Malvern, He-Ne laser, λ = 633 nm) to measure the hydrodynamic diameter and size distribution. The E7 loading efficiency was analyzed by fast protein liquid chromatography (ÄKTA pure™ protein purification system). The release efficiency and kinetics of diABZI from the STING NP were quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (1260 Infinity II, Agilent).

Western Blot Analysis.

All solutions were purchased from Bio-Rad and antibodies anti-NQO1 C-terminal (N5288, rabbit polyclonal, 1:1,000 to 1:2,000) were obtained from Sigma/Aldrich. RAW264.7 and DC2.4 cells were treated with PBS, PSDBA NP, PSC7A NP, diABZI, and STING NP for 24 h. The concentration of all NPs was at 50 µg/mL, and equivalent diABZI concentration (7.85 µg/mL) was used in these studies. Growth medium was replaced with complete medium containing 10 μM β-lapachone for 2 h after which the medium was removed, and the plates were washed twice with PBS. Cells were collected and lysed in SDS sample buffer (with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail) and heated for denaturation. The supernatant was loaded onto a 4 to 15% Mini-PROTEAN gel (Bio-Rad) and run at 50 V for 20 min followed by 100 V for 60 min. Electrotransfer was performed using 100 V for 60 min on ice. After transfer, the membrane was blocked either in 5% nonfat milk or BSA (phosphorylated protein) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Goat anti-rabbit IgG HRP-linked secondary antibody (1:3,000, BioLegend) was used for 1 h at room temperature before detection on gel imaging system (GelDoc Go Gel Imaging System, Bio-Rad). The membrane was stripped in stripping buffer for 30 min and reused for β-actin (A2228, Sigma/Aldrich, mouse mAb, 1:5,000) detection.

Methyl Red Assay.

RAW264.7 and DC2.4 cells were cultured in a 6-well plate and treated with PBS, PSDBA NP, PSC7A NP, diABZI, and STING NP for 24 h. The concentration of all NPs was 50 µg/mL, and equivalent diABZI concentration (7.85 µg/mL) was used in these studies. Cells were collected and lysed with 60 µL RIPA buffer on ice for 30 min, and the supernatants were collected for assay. In a Methyl Red assay, Methyl Red (50 µM) and NADH (800 µM) were dissolved into a mixed solvent of DMSO and H2O with 1:10 ratio. 5 µL supernatants were added into the Methyl Red assay and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The decrease in absorbance (λmax at 430 nm) was used to quantify NQO1 using a calibration curve. Total proteins were determined by BCA assay (Thermo Scientific, 23227).

IFN-β Assay.

RAW264.7 and DC2.4 cells were treated with PBS, STING NP (50 µg/mL), and STING NP with dicoumarol (10 µM) for 24 h. The supernatants were collected. IFN-β levels were measured by Mouse IFN-beta DuoSet ELISA (DY8234-05, R&D).

Pharmacokinetic Analysis.

C57BL/6 mice bearing TC-1 tumor were intravenously administered with dye-labeled nanovaccine, in which a hybrid nanoparticle consisting of PEG-b-P(SC7A-azo-diABZI) and PEG-b-PSC7A-Cy5 (each PSC7A polymer is conjugated with ~3 Cy5 dye) with a molar ratio of 7:3 and TMR-labeled E7 (10%) was formulated. The injected dose was 15 mg/kg for the nanoparticles. At predefined time points following injection, mice were bled to collect 50 μL of blood (n = 5 for each group). The plasma fraction was collected after centrifugation, diluted fivefold with PBS buffer containing 5 mM EDTA at pH 6.0, and the fluorescence was quantified using a plate reader. Fluorescent nanoparticles were quantified using a 640 nm (for Cy5) and 540 nm (for TMR) excitation and 670 (for Cy5) and 570 nm (for TMR) emission wavelength by a plate reader. Data are presented as percent injected dose (% ID) and plasma collected at 5 min was taken to represent the maximum injected dose (100% ID). Data were fitted using nonlinear regression and a two-phase decay model using GraphPad Prism software.

Organ and Cell Distribution.

C57BL/6 mice bearing TC-1 tumors (~120 mm3) were intravenously injected with dye-labeled STING NP as described in the previous section. Mice were euthanized 24 h after i.v. injections according to the animal guidelines in UT Southwestern Medical Center, and different tissues (tumor, tdLN, liver, lung, heart, kidney, and spleen) were collected and weighed. Tissues were mechanically dissociated and homogenized with lysis buffer (2 wt% Triton X-100 + 100 mM HEPES + 5 mM EDTA, pH 7.1) using tissue grinder tubes (Precellys Lysing Kits). Next, tissues were centrifuged at 500×g for 3 min. The supernatants were transferred to a black 96-well plate for quantification using a 640 nm excitation and 670 emission wavelengths by a plate reader. The concentration of nanoparticle was determined using a tissue-specific standard curve prepared with tissue digests from untreated mice. Organ uptake was reported as the percentage of injected dose per gram of tissue.

For cell distribution studies, mice were euthanized 24 h after injections. Tissues including tumor, tdLN, and spleen were collected and dissociated into single-cell suspensions, and then stained, washed, and resuspended in a fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer. The following antibodies were used: CD45 PerCP (clone 30-F11, BioLegend), MHC-II AF700 (clone M5/114.15.2, Invitrogen), CD11c BV605 (clone N418, BioLegend), CD11b BV605 (clone M1/70, BioLegend), CD4 FITC (clone RM4-5, BioLegend), CD8a PE (clone 53-6.7, BioLegend), CD3e BV786 (clone 145-2C11, BD BioSciences), F4/80 PE (clone BM8, Miltenyi Biotec), NK1.1 PE-Cy7 (clone PK136, BD Biosciences), B220 APC-Cy7 (clone RA3-6B2, BioLegend). The LIVE/DEAD Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain Kit (Invitrogen, L34966) was used. Data were collected on BD LSRFortessa or Beckman CytoFLEX flow cytometer and analyzed by FlowJo (Tree Star Inc., Ashland, OR) software.

Tumor Treatment by STING Nanovaccine.

In the TC-1 tumor model, C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously inoculated with TC-1 (2 × 105 cells) that were suspended in 100 μL sterile PBS on the right shaved flank on day 0. For distal tumor experiments, mice were inoculated with TC-1 (1 × 105 cells) on the left flank opposite to the initial tumor inoculation. Mice were intravenously treated on days 10 and 15 after tumor inoculation. For the administration method study, subcutaneous treatment was near the tail base, intratumoral treatment was injected in the right primary tumor, and intravenous treatment was through the tail vein. For all studies, mice were randomized into groups before treatment initiation. Tumor size was measured using calipers, and the tumor volume was calculated with the formula V = (length × width × width)/2, where length is the longest dimension and width is the perpendicular dimension. Mice were killed when the tumor volume exceeded 1,500 mm3 or tumor ulceration became severe.

In the MLM3 tumor model, C57BL/6 mice were intravenously inoculated with MLM3 (1 × 106 cells) that were suspended in 100 μL sterile PBS on day 0. Mice were intravenously treated with STING nanovaccine or other control groups on days 10 and 16 after tumor inoculation. Anti–PD-1 (200 μg) was intraperitoneally injected on days 10, 13, and 16. Mice were euthanized, and their lungs were collected and fixed in Bouin’s solution on day 35 and further analyzed by histology analysis. Additional mice were also included for long-term survival studies.

Safety Evaluations.

Tumor-free C57BL/6 mice were intravenously injected with the STING NP, PSC7A NP, free diABZI, and PBS. Mice (n = 4 for each group) were bled to collect 500 μL of blood 6 or 24 h after injections. The plasma fraction was collected after centrifugation. The systemic concentrations of interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-10 (IL-10), monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-12p70 (IL-12p70) were measured using a mouse inflammation kit (BD™ Cytometric Bead Array, 552364). The activities of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (SCEA) were measured in the Metabolic Phenotyping Core at UTSW. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 7.

Flow Cytometry.

H&E Analysis.

Fresh tissues were first fixed in 10% formalin solution and then were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and H&E stained by the Molecular Pathology Core at UTSW. The slides were scanned using Hamamatsu Nanozoomer 2.0HT and the images were analyzed using NDP.view 2.7.25 software.

Statistical Analysis.

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism Version 9.0. Statistical tests used are indicated in the figure legends. Data were considered statistically significant if P < 0.05. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.
