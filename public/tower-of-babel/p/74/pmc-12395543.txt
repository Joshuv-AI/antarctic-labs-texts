Pharmacological antiemetic prophylaxis and treatment for opioid‐induced nausea and vomiting (OINV) in patients treated for cancer pain and cancer‐related breathlessness
Authors: Jan Gaertner, Christian Appenzeller-Herzog, Vanessa Piechotta, Guido Schwarzer, Constanze Rémi, Katja Suter, Christopher Boehlke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis is a protocol for a Cochrane Review (intervention). The objectives are as follows:To evaluate the benefits and harms of pharmacological treatments given prophylactically or on‐demand after onset of opioid‐induced nausea and vomiting (OINV) in people being treated for cancer pain or cancer dyspnoea, when compared to placebo or other pharmacological interventions.

Background

Cancer‐related pain and dyspnoea (breathlessness) are the most common symptoms in people with incurable diseases, with high prevalence rates [1, 2, 3]. When opioids are used to treat these symptoms, about 40% of opioid‐naive patients suffer from opioid‐induced nausea and vomiting (OINV) [4, 5]. This review will aim to help inform healthcare professionals, including policymakers, to recommend the best treatment for OINV.

Description of the condition

OINV refers to the experience of nausea or vomiting, or both, that arises as a result of taking opioids [4, 5]. Not only can OINV be a very distressing side effect, but it is also a frequent reason for patients to discontinue opioid treatment [6]. Additionally, OINV can hinder the resorption of other (often crucial) medications, decrease caloric intake, increase cachexia, and ultimately reduce quality of life (QoL) [6].

Tolerance to OINV generally appears within days to weeks after the start of opioid therapy. However, it may not always be maintained, especially when opioid dosages are increased [6]. This may lead to more OINV in participants with higher pain intensity.

OINV should not be confused with chemotherapy‐induced nausea/vomiting (CINV), radiation‐induced emesis (RIE), or postoperative nausea/vomiting (PONV) [6], which are caused by specific interventions [7] or relate to the perioperative process including anaesthetic factors [8].

Description of the intervention and how it might work

To mitigate these adverse opioid effects and improve compliance with opioid therapy, many clinicians (36 to 80 per cent, depending on the country) prescribe prophylactic or on‐demand antiemetics to reduce the incidence of OINV [9, 10].

Two key areas in the body are thought to facilitate the vomiting reflex: the vomiting centre, located within the medulla including the nucleus tractus solitarius, and the chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ), located on the floor of the fourth ventricle [11].

There are three known distinct mechanisms by which opioids can induce OINV [6, 12, 13]:

binding receptors at the CTZ;enhancing vestibular sensitivity;delaying gastric emptying.

Opioids act centrally and may trigger the release of neurotransmitters (e.g. serotonin, dopamine), which stimulate the CTZ. Antiemetics can counteract these mechanisms by targeting the neurotransmitter receptors [14]. Furthermore, opioids act peripherally by reducing oesophageal, gastric, and intestinal motility [6]. Why it is important to do this review

Clinical guidelines

Whether antiemetics should be given as prophylaxis to prevent OINV and/or on demand when OINV occurs, is still debated. This uncertainty is reflected by current guidelines. For example, the American Society of Clinical Oncology suggests administration “around the clock for the first few days”, while other guidelines fail to mention how to use antiemetics in this circumstance (European Society of Medical Oncology, European Association of Palliative Care) [16, 17, 18]. These three guidelines also suggest using antidopaminergic medication, although this practice is not supported by randomised controlled trials. Given the unknown efficacy of antidopaminergic medications, their associated adverse effects on the central nervous system—such as headache, drowsiness, and extrapyramidal symptoms—should be even more carefully considered. As a result of guidelines not giving clear guidance, a recent survey found substantial heterogeneity in clinical practice and expressed that evidence‐based guidance is needed [9]. Our review aims to inform healthcare professionals, including policymakers, to recommend the right (or best available) treatment.

Other Cochrane reviews

One Cochrane review covers OINV in an acute care setting (emergency room), finding no reduction of nausea and vomiting when metoclopramide is given prophylactically [14]. Two other Cochrane reviews investigate PONV and CINV [7, 8]. However, prophylaxis and symptomatic treatment of OINV for cancer pain or cancer‐related dyspnoea have not been investigated.

Objectives

To evaluate the benefits and harms of pharmacological treatments given prophylactically or on‐demand after onset of opioid‐induced nausea and vomiting (OINV) in people being treated for cancer pain or cancer dyspnoea, when compared to placebo or other pharmacological interventions.

Methods

We will follow the methodological expectations for Cochrane intervention reviews when conducting the review [19], and PRISMA 2020 for reporting [20]. Some of the following methods have been adapted from other recently published Cochrane reviews of our group [21, 22].

Criteria for considering studies for this review

Types of studies

We will include randomised controlled trials (RCTs), including cluster‐RCTs. We will include quasi‐randomised trials due to the limited availability of high‐quality evidence. Quasi‐randomised trials are studies that use non‐random methods of allocation (e.g. date of birth or hospital record number); we will assess them for risk of bias using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool (Risk of bias assessment in included studies). We will exclude cross‐over RCTs because OINV diminishes within a few days to weeks of initiating opioid treatment.

We will include studies reported in peer‐reviewed journal articles, clinical trial registry entries, conference proceedings, summaries of otherwise unpublished clinical trials, preprints, and abstracts. If eligible studies are published in multiple formats, such as a conference abstract followed by a journal article, we will include all citations. We will gather together all references relevant to each primary included study. If there is insufficient data to assess the eligibility of a study, or extract or assess study data, we will attempt to procure more information by contacting the study authors.

We will limit our inclusion to RCTs because, if performed appropriately, this design provides the most reliable evidence on the effects of experimental interventions in a highly controlled therapeutic setting. We will exclude other study designs, such as non‐randomised studies, case reports, and clinical observations.

We will search for studies in any language to limit language biases.

We will not restrict eligibility by the publication date.

Data collection and analysis

Selection of studies

Two review authors will independently determine the eligibility of each study identified by the search using Covidence [32]. We will use the inbuilt Cochrane RCT classifier to automatically remove non‐RCTs [ 35]. We will code the remaining abstracts as either 'include' or 'exclude'. The review authors will eliminate studies that do not meet inclusion criteria and obtain full texts of the remaining studies. Two review authors will read these full texts independently to select relevant studies, and in the event of disagreement about inclusion, a third author will adjudicate. We will not anonymise the studies in any way before the assessment. We will include a PRISMA‐flowchart in this review to depict the study selection process [20].

If we identify records in a language that our review team cannot translate, we will ask for assistance via Cochrane Engage.

We will conduct data extraction and assessment according to the guidelines proposed by Cochrane [36]. We will pilot the data extraction form prior to its being used for the full data extraction process.

To facilitate the import, we will use Covidence [32]. We will use a structured approach to address conflicts of interest in trials [38]. Measures of treatment effect

For dichotomous outcomes, we will describe the percentage of participants experiencing a certain event. We will analyse other dichotomous outcomes by calculating risk ratios (RR) for each trial, with the uncertainty in each result expressed using 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In the case of very rare events (e.g. serious adverse events), we will use the risk difference to incorporate double‐zero studies. We will calculate the number needed to treat for an additional beneficial (NNTB) or harmful (NNTH) outcome. We will assume a range of values for the comparator risk, for example, values of x, y, and z for the outcome complete control of emesis.

For continuous outcomes using the same scale, we will perform analyses using the mean difference (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We will use change scores when pre‐ and post‐values are available. If no information regarding correlation between pre‐ and post‐values is reported, we will calculate the standard error for the MD by assuming no correlation between pre‐ and post‐values, which will lead to wider confidence intervals.

For continuous outcomes measured on different scales, we will perform analyses using the standardised mean difference (SMD). We will re‐express the SMD in the original units of a particular scale with the most clinical relevance and impact. We will enter data presented as a scale with a consistent direction of effect.

We will apply the minimally important difference for nausea on the NRS (scale 1 to 10) of ≥ 1 to judge the effect size [43].

Unit of analysis issues

Events that may re‐occur

If events are observed multiple times, we will consider the number of participants experiencing any event and not the number of experienced events for analysis.

Multiple treatment attempts

We will consider the number of participants and not the number of assigned treatments for analysis.

Studies with multiple treatment groups

For studies with multiple treatment groups, we will combine arms when they can be regarded as subtypes of the same intervention. If they cannot, we will compare each arm with the common comparator separately. For pairwise meta‐analyses, we will split the 'shared' group into two or more groups with smaller sample sizes, and include two or more independent comparisons. For this purpose, for dichotomous outcomes, both the number of events and the total number of participants will be divided up by the common comparator. For continuous outcomes, the total number of participants will be divided up by unchanged means and standard deviations (SDs) for the common comparator.

Cluster‐randomised trials

For cluster‐randomised trials, we will extract effect estimates from analyses that appropriately account for the clustering, such as using multilevel models or generalised estimating equations. If such analyses are not reported, we will apply approximate methods to adjust for clustering as outlined in Chapter 23 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [39].

Dealing with missing data

Several potential sources of missing data are suggested in Chapter 6 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, which we have taken into account: at study level, outcome level, and at summary data level [39]. In the first instance, we will differentiate between data 'missing at random' and 'not missing at random'. We will identify additional and unpublished data by contacting investigators or study sponsors to verify key study characteristics or obtain missing outcome data. We will assess the risk of bias due to missing results by thoroughly assessing selective non‐reporting or under‐reporting results in the studies identified.

When possible, we will calculate missing standard deviations (SDs) or other necessary data using additional data from the trial, such as CIs, based on methods outlined in Chapter 6 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [39]. When the reported results do not allow calculation of the standard error (SE) of the mean difference in a paired analysis, we will try to approximate the SE by estimating the correlation within participants. When there are insufficient data to calculate the correlation coefficient, we will assume a correlation of zero, which will result in a conservative scenario, that is, the SE will be slightly overestimated.

Reporting bias assessment

We will not formally assess reporting bias (e.g. using funnel plots [44]), because we only expect a small number of RCTs to be included in the review. We will use a funnel plot to assess reporting bias in future updates of the review if there are 10 or more included studies.

As we are only including RCTs, these should all be registered. As part of matching trial registry records with journal/abstract publications, we will map studies that are still ongoing versus those that should have been completed but lack published results. This will help us to estimate the extent of publication bias.

Synthesis methods

Meta‐analysis of numerical data

If the clinical and methodological characteristics of individual studies are sufficiently homogeneous, we will pool data in meta‐analysis. We will perform analyses according to the recommendations in Chapter 10 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [23].

We will use RevMan for analyses [37]. One review author will enter the data into the software, and a second review author will check the data for accuracy.

We will use the random‐effects model for all analyses, as we anticipate that true effects will be related but not the same for the included studies. We will base the estimation of the between‐study variance on the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method [45]. We will use the inverse‐variance method in all random‐effects meta‐analyses. We will explore heterogeneity with subgroup analyses (Investigation of heterogeneity and subgroup analysis).

The Hartung‐Knapp‐Sidik‐Jonkman method will be used to calculate a confidence interval for the meta‐analysis effect estimate when there are at least three studies and the estimate of heterogeneity is greater than zero. In other scenarios (i.e. in pooled analyses of two studies, or where the estimate of heterogeneity is equal to zero), we will use the Wald‐type method.

Synthesis using other methods

If meta‐analysis is not possible, we will follow Chapter 12 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [46]. For example, we will use structured tabulation of results across studies for important outcomes and adverse events.

Statistical heterogeneity

We will assess the statistical heterogeneity of treatment effects between studies as recommended in Chapter 10 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, using a Chi2 test with a significance level at P < 0.1 and through visual examination of forest plots to consider the direction and magnitude of effects and the degree of overlap between CIs [23]. We will use I2 statistics to quantify possible heterogeneity, with an I2 over 30% signifying moderate heterogeneity and an I2 over 75% signifying considerable heterogeneity [23]. We will explore potential causes of heterogeneity through subgroup analyses (Investigation of heterogeneity and subgroup analysis).

Clinical heterogeneity

We will not pool the results in cases of significant clinical diversity.

Assessment of the certainty of the evidence

We will use the GRADE approach to assess the certainty of the evidence for the outcomes listed above [49]. Two review authors will independently rate the certainty of the body of evidence for the outcomes, with disagreements resolved by discussion or by involving a third review author. We will justify, document, and incorporate judgements into the reporting of results for each outcome.

The GRADE approach uses five domains (risk of bias, consistency of effect, imprecision, indirectness, and publication bias) on which to assess the certainty of the body of evidence for each outcome. According to GRADE guidance 18 [50], the initial rating for randomised trials is high certainty.

Factors that may decrease the certainty level of a body of evidence are:

serious or very serious study limitations (risk of bias as defined in Reporting bias assessment);important or serious inconsistency of results;some or major indirectness of evidence;serious or very serious imprecision;probability of publication bias.

The overall judgement of study limitations in the GRADE assessment will be based on the risk of bias of studies contributing most to the pooled effect, and further informed by sensitivity analyses exploring the impact of excluding high‐risk studies.
