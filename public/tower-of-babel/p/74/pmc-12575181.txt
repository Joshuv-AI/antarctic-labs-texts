Risk of preterm delivery in women with a history of preterm labor successfully arrested by tocolytic treatment in their previous pregnancy
Authors: Thibaud Quibel, Anne Rousseau, Claire Thuillier, Mireille Ruiz, Patrick Rozenberg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe aimed to determine if women with a history of preterm labor successfully arrested by tocolytic treatment who gave birth at term in their previous pregnancy are at an increased risk of preterm delivery in their next pregnancy.Material and MethodsThis case‐control study included women with two consecutive singleton pregnancies who gave birth in the 15‐year period of 2000–2014 at the tertiary hospital of Poissy‐Saint‐Germain. Cases (preterm labor [PTL] group) included all women admitted with intact membranes for preterm labor that was successfully arrested by tocolytic treatment between 24 + 0 and 34 + 6 weeks' gestation and who gave birth at term in the first of these two pregnancies. Two control groups were selected: (i) a spontaneous preterm delivery (sPTD) group including all women with a preterm delivery in the first pregnancy, and (ii) a term delivery (TD) group that included women selected among those who gave birth at term in the previous pregnancy. The primary outcome was the spontaneous preterm birth in the next pregnancy.ResultsThe PTL, sPTD, and TD groups included 114, 50, and 114 women, respectively. There were no significant differences for maternal age, body mass index, or relationship situation for both pregnancies. The mean (± SD) gestational age at the first study delivery was 39.1 (±1.2) weeks in the PTL group, 33.2 (±3.7) weeks in the PTD group, and 39.6 (±1.2) weeks in the TD group (p < 0.001 for each comparison). The delivery rate before 37 weeks of gestation in the subsequent pregnancy was 18.4% in the arrested PTL group, 34.0% in the PTB group, and 4.4% in the TD groups (p = 0.047 between arrested PTL and sPTD; p < 0,001 between arrested PTL and TD) and before 34 weeks, 6.1%, 16.0%, and 0%, respectively (p = 0.044 between arrested PTL and sPTD; p < 0,001 between arrested PTL and TD).ConclusionsWomen with a history of preterm labor that was successfully arrested by tocolytic treatment, resulting in a TD, are at a higher risk of preterm delivery in their next pregnancy than women with no such history, but a lower risk than those with a preterm delivery in their last pregnancy.Keywords: cervical length, preterm delivery, preterm labor, tocolysis

Key message

A history of preterm labor that was successfully managed and resulted in a term delivery should be carefully documented, as these women continue to have an elevated risk of preterm birth in subsequent pregnancies.

INTRODUCTION

Reducing preterm birth remains one of the greatest challenges in obstetrics due to the high morbidity and mortality of preterm newborns. 1 It is the leading cause of infant death worldwide and increases the risk of neurodevelopmental impairment and respiratory and gastrointestinal complications. 2 , 3 , 4 In the United States, the preterm birth rate was 10.0% in 2022, 5 and 7.0% in 2021 in France. 6 Spontaneous preterm birth is considered to be a syndrome resulting from a multifactorial process, in which the specific cause of spontaneous preterm labor remains unidentified in nearly half of all cases. 7

An ultrasonographic short cervix in women reporting symptoms of preterm labor (defined as regular painful uterine contractions) before 37 weeks of gestation is a major risk factor for preterm birth. 8 Because it is often unclear whether these symptoms will progress to clear preterm labor and delivery, transvaginal ultrasound measurement of cervical length is sometimes used to try to distinguish true from false preterm labor. 9 A meta‐analysis applying a 25‐mm cervical length cutoff showed a sensitivity of 78% with a specificity of 70% for preterm birth for the current pregnancy in a symptomatic population, with a pooled positive likelihood ratio of 2.8 and a negative likelihood ratio of 0.36. 10

A history of preterm birth is also a major risk factor for recurrent preterm delivery. 11 The recent NICHD Consecutive Pregnancies Study reported a recurrent spontaneous preterm delivery (sPTD) rate of 31.6%. 12 Moreover, this risk increases with the number of preterm births and is inversely proportional to the gestational age of the previous such birth. 13

It remains unknown, however, whether women with a history of suspected preterm labor, defined by regular painful uterine contractions and a cervical length <25 mm, but who gave birth at term after successful tocolysis are at higher risk of preterm birth in their next pregnancy. The objective of this study was to assess the risk of preterm birth in this particular population.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

This case‐control study included women with two consecutive singleton pregnancies, delivered between 2000 and 2014 at Poissy‐Saint Germain Hospital, a tertiary referral university hospital specializing in over 4000 deliveries annually during the study period. Pregnancies are referred to hereafter as each woman's first and second pregnancy, a characterization that describes their order in time and their consecutive nature (parity increase of 1 between the two), but not the actual parity, which may differ.

The study included as the preterm labor (PTL) case group all women who were admitted during their first pregnancy for diagnosed preterm labor with intact membranes (occurring after 23+0 weeks of gestation) that was successfully arrested by tocolytic treatments, and who gave birth at term (≥37+0 weeks of gestation). Diagnosed preterm labor was defined by symptoms of preterm labor, that is, regular and painful uterine contractions (at least 2 per 10 min documented by external tocography during a 1‐h period) and a cervical length <25 mm at admission, measured in the sagittal plane by transvaginal ultrasound according to clinical practice guidelines. 14 , 15 Women admitted for preterm labor were managed by tocolysis with nifedipine and a course of betamethasone 12 mg, given intramuscularly and repeated after 24 h. When delivery was delayed by 48 h, tocolysis was stopped and the women were discharged. Only women whose subsequent pregnancy was delivered at Poissy hospital were included. These women were identified from our electronic health system record.

Two control groups were selected. The sPTD group included all women who were admitted for preterm labor and gave birth before 37+0 weeks of gestation in their first pregnancy. Like the PTL group, all these women received tocolysis and antenatal corticosteroids during that admission. The second control group included women with term deliveries (the TD group), specifically the first woman who gave birth at term immediately after each woman in the PTL group and who had no history of preterm labor in this pregnancy and no history of either preterm labor or preterm delivery during the first pregnancy. We identified, from the birth registries, the woman belonging to each control group who had delivered after each case of the study group. Due to a smaller population in the spontaneous PTD group, we had less than one woman in this group for every two women in the PTL group.

During the study period, subsequent pregnancies in the PTD group were managed according to our department guidelines for the prevention of recurrent preterm birth with weekly administration of 17‐alpha hydroxyprogesterone caproate, and cerclage was only considered for repeated preterm deliveries. Women in the PTL group did not receive any preventive measures during the subsequent pregnancy.

Women with multiple gestations, preterm rupture of membranes, chorioamnionitis, placental abruption, major fetal anomalies, stillbirth, history of cervical cerclage, or indicated preterm birth for any other maternal or fetal pathology were excluded from this study.

Research midwives took electronic files, verified against paper records, and then stored or entered paper records into this dataset. The maternal data set included the women's demographic characteristics, medical history, reproductive history, cervical length at admission for preterm labor, and delivery outcomes in the first and second pregnancies. Determination of the duration of gestation for all patients included in the study was based on the results of the first trimester ultrasound scan, routinely performed in France.

The primary outcome was spontaneous preterm birth <37+0 weeks of gestation in the second pregnancy. Secondary outcomes included mean gestational age at delivery, preterm birth <34 weeks, and admission for preterm labor in the second pregnancy.

The characteristics of the three groups are described with means and standard deviations for quantitative variables and frequencies and percentages for qualitative variables. Continuous variables that were normally distributed were compared between the PTL group and each control group with Student's t‐test, and categorical variables were compared with the Chi‐square or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Last, we performed both univariate and multivariate regression analyses to investigate among women of the preterm labor group risk factors for preterm birth. Statistical significance was defined by p < 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed with R statistical software, version 2.10.0.

RESULTS

During the study period, 278 patients met the inclusion criteria: 114 were included in the PTL group, 50 in the sPTD group, and another 114 in the TD group. Body mass index, prenatal tobacco smoke exposure, country of birth, and marital status did not differ between the PTL group and the two control groups. Table 2 reports the primary and secondary outcomes. Preterm birth occurred in the second pregnancy in 18.4% (21/114) of women in the PTL group; compared with 34.0% (17/50) of those in the sPTD group (p = 0.044) and 4.4% (5/114) in the TD group (p < 0.001). The delivery rate before 34 weeks of gestation in the second pregnancy was significantly higher in the PTL (6.1%) than in the TD group (0%, p < 0.001), but also significantly lower than the sPTD group (16.0%, p < 0.047). The mean gestational age at delivery in the second pregnancy was 38.2 ± 3.4 weeks in the PTL group, 36.6 ± 4.3 in the sPTD group, and 39.3 ± 1.5 in the TD group (p < 0.001). Overall, 28.1% of the women in the PTL group were admitted for preterm labor before 34 weeks of gestation in the second pregnancy, compared with 50% in the sPTD group and 1.8% in the TD group (p < 0.001).

DISCUSSION

Our case‐control study showed that women who gave birth at term after an episode of preterm labor (defined by regular painful uterine contractions and a cervical length < 25 mm) that was successfully arrested by tocolysis were at a higher risk of preterm birth in the subsequent pregnancy than women who gave birth at term without any episode of PTL.

Screening for women at risk of preterm birth is important in the management of their pregnancies, and the identification of risk factors could enable the use of specific interventions that might prove helpful. Women with a previous preterm birth are a population at high risk of recurrence in their next pregnancy. This risk of recurrence ranges from 15% to more than 50%, depending on the number and gestational age of previous deliveries. 16 , 17 , 18 In a recent large consecutive pregnancy cohort, Laughon et al. found a recurrence rate of 31.6% for previous spontaneous preterm deliveries, close to the 34% rate in our study. 12 Furthermore, cervical length appears to be of limited value in predicting preterm delivery among women reporting symptoms of preterm labor who previously had such a delivery and are thus at high risk of recurrence. 19

Until now, however, the risk of preterm birth in the subsequent pregnancy of a woman who gave birth at term after an episode of regular painful uterine contractions with an ultrasonographic short cervix successfully arrested by tocolysis has been unclear. A PubMed search using the key words “preterm delivery” or “premature labor/birth” or “threatened preterm birth” and “cervical length” without date or language restrictions failed to identify any studies that evaluated this risk. Obstetricians could therefore draw two opposite conclusions: (i) that the previous preterm labor was false labor, in view of the term delivery (TD), despite the short cervix, that is, that the woman is at low risk of preterm delivery, or (ii) that this history was true preterm labor arrested by tocolytic drugs and that the woman may thus be at potentially high risk. Our study showed that although this woman is at significantly lower risk of preterm delivery than a woman with such a history, she is also at significantly higher risk than a woman with no history of preterm labor.

In our opinion, the finding of a short cervix in the first pregnancy associated with an intermediate risk of preterm birth in the second indicates that the preterm labor arrested by tocolysis in the first pregnancy was likely to be the clinical expression of an incomplete multifactorial disease that led to the episode of contractions, but was insufficient to result in preterm birth. If this multifactorial disease recurs and becomes complete in the second pregnancy, it will be clinically expressed as preterm birth. The incomplete recurrent disease might, for example, be a just barely competent cervix, and bacterial vaginosis or an intercurrent inflammatory disease in the second pregnancy might be the additional factor that completes it, leading to the preterm birth.

Research is now focusing on the different pathways that lead to preterm birth; its clinical presentations should be considered in evaluating its risk of recurrence. In a retrospective cohort study, Drassinower et al. showed that the risk of recurrence differs according to the clinical presentation of the previous spontaneous preterm birth; recurrence occurred in 55.2% of the women with a history of advanced cervical dilatation but only 27.2% of those with preterm premature rupture of membranes and 32.2% with preterm labor (p = 0.001). Furthermore, the mean cervical length before 24 weeks was significantly shorter in women with the history of advanced cervical dilation than those with the other clinical presentations. 20 In a retrospective cohort study that included asymptomatic women at high risk of preterm delivery because of a history of uterine anomaly, excisional treatment for cervical dysplasia, or previous spontaneous preterm birth, Crane and Hutchens showed that women with a short cervical length (<30 mm) in their previous pregnancy who gave birth at term (short‐cervix term‐delivery group) were more likely to have a short cervix during their next pregnancy than those with a history of a normal cervical length in their previous pregnancy who gave birth at term (long‐cervix term‐delivery group) (43.5% vs. 15.4%, respectively; p = 0.003). However, women in the first (short‐cervix) group were not at a higher risk of spontaneous preterm birth at <37 weeks in the next pregnancy than those in the second. 21 This result, apparently contrary to ours, may be explained by the difference in the study populations: they studied asymptomatic women at high risk, while we focused on high‐risk women with symptoms. Nonetheless, the increased risk of recurrence of short cervical length that Crane and Hutchens observed reinforces our hypothesis of recurrent incomplete multifactorial disease.

One strength of our study is that all births took place at a single institution, so that the women received uniform care (e.g., cervical length measurements and tocolytic drugs) for the management of preterm labor during the study period. Furthermore, all cervical measurements were taken by practitioners who had received specific training in the acquisition and interpretation of cervical images during pregnancy.

The main limitation of this study is linked to our methodology, due to the potential uncontrolled confounding biases of all case‐control studies. Furthermore, we could not obtain an adequate number of women in the preterm birth group. Nonetheless, the rate of recurrence was similar to that of the largest consecutive pregnancy cohort reported, that of Laughon et al. 12 In addition, we could not document the cervical length of women who had an episode of preterm labor and a TD in the previous pregnancy but who were asymptomatic during the subsequent pregnancy, because guidelines in our department did not call for the routine measurement of cervical length in this particular population. Finally, healthcare providers were not blinded to the cervical length results, as cervical length assessment is part of standard care for women with a history of preterm birth at our center.

CONCLUSION

Despite the limitations of our study, we found that women delivering at term after an episode of preterm labor with a short cervix are at a higher risk of preterm delivery in the subsequent pregnancy than women without such a history. These women and the healthcare providers should be aware of this increased risk. Future research is recommended, focusing on the prevention of this risk of preterm birth, notably by investigating the potential efficacy of vaginal progesterone in the subsequent pregnancy.
