Association between body composition and disease progression in adults with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hamid Abbasi, Mehrnoosh Shafaatdoost, Arefeh Mohajerani, Milad Asadollahi, Maryam Rashidi, Mahsa Malekahmadi, Payam Sarraf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by motor neuron degeneration, muscle wasting, and respiratory failure, with a median survival of 30 months. Due to the strong link between dysphagia, weight loss, and disease progression, this study investigates the relationship between body composition and clinical outcomes in ALS adults. This cross-sectional study involved 93 ALS adults (29 females, 64 males) from Imam Khomeini Hospital in Tehran, selected based on EI Escorial criteria. Researchers assessed body composition, functional abilities, and disease progression using ALSFRS-R, MRC scores, and DPR, analyzing associations through linear regression models with RStudio in conjunction with R software. In this study, significant differences were found between the third and first tertiles for various measures. Significant associations were observed between body composition and ALSFRS-R for MAC (β: 3.0; P = 0.006), with underweight and moderately active adults exhibiting notable differences. The MRC score was positively associated with FFM (β: 5.8; P = 0.002), SLM (β: 5.6; P = 0.002), SMM (β: 3.8; P = 0.001), MAC (β: 3.2; P = 0.002), ICW (β: 2.7; P = 0.002), and ECW (β: 1.5; P = 0.003), while underweight and low-to-moderate physical activity adults indicated inverse associations. For DPR, significant relationships were noted for weight (β: 4.5; 95% CI: 0.02, 9.3; P = 0.002) and FFM (β: 11; P < 0.001), influenced by gender and physical activity. The findings highlight the role of gender, weight, and activity in ALS management, suggesting that maintaining a healthy weight along and muscle mass along with regular activity is associated with better outcomes. Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), also referred to as Lou Gehrig’s disease, is an incurable and widespread neurodegenerative disorder.it is marked by degeneration of both superior and inferior motor neurons, resulting in a broad array of motor and non-motor symptoms [1]. The prevalence of ALS is estimated to be between 2.6 and 3.0 cases per 100,000 individuals, with a median survival time of around 30 months [2, 3]. ALS is typically classified into two subtypes; sporadic ALS, which accounts for approximately 90–95% of cases and is influenced by environmental and genetic factors, and familial ALS, which makes up about 5–10% of cases and is primarily inherited in an autosomal dominant manner [4, 5]. The clinical manifestations of ALS encompass muscle wasting, dysphagia, breathing difficulties, dysarthria, limb paralysis, and ultimately, pulmonary failure leading to death [6]. There are multiple contributing factors to this condition. Dysphagia in ALS is well-documented to correlate with reduced dietary intake and subsequent weight loss [7]. Recent research has also indicated that loss of appetite [8] and alterations in taste [9] may contribute to dietary changes. Furthermore, clinical research has identified hypermetabolism in ALS individuals [10]. Despite ongoing debate, ample clinical studies have reported a connection between hypermetabolism and both an accelerated disease progression and reduced survival time [11, 12].

Given the challenge of developing an effective treatment for ALS, there has been a growing interest in exploring metabolism and managing nutrition in ALS individuals. So far, the focus has primarily been on assessing the nutritional state of ALS individuals using parameters such as weight, body mass index (BMI), waist-hip ratio (WHR), and certain serum nutritional biomarkers [13]. Studies on body composition changes among people living with ALS, with particular attention to fat-free mass (FFM) and fat mass (FM) [8, 14, 15]. A study executed by Li et al. exhibited that individuals with ALS had a lower BMI in compared with controls. Furthermore, ALS individuals experienced a median weight loss of 4.11% [16]. Moreover, some researchers have suggested that muscle wasting is linked to poor prognosis, whereas gaining fat might be linked to improved survival in individuals with ALS [15, 17, 18]. Consequently, addressing nutritional status and providing nutrient supplementation could potentially be a promising therapeutic approach.

Given the significant impact of dysphagia on weight loss, which accelerates disease progression in affected individuals, we aimed to explore the association between body composition and ALS-relate clinical scores among adults with ALS.

Methods and materials

Study design

This cross-sectional study included a total of 93 adults diagnosed with ALS, consisting of 29 females and 64 males. These participants were referred for routine health assessments at the neuromuscular disorder clinic located within the Imam Khomeini Hospital Complex, which is associated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences in Tehran, Iran. Selection of participants was based on neurological evaluations and diagnoses made in accordance with the EI Escorial criteria [19, 20], performed by a neurologist who specializes in neuromuscular disorders. The study included adults aged 18 years and older, while adults with specific exclusionary factors such as recent pregnancy, breastfeeding, or pediatric conditions were not considered for participation. A qualified nutritionist oversaw all aspects of participant recruitment and data collection through personal interviews. Prior to their involvement in the study, all participants provided written informed consent. The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences, with the registration number of IR. TUMS.NI.REC.1401.059. All procedures in this study were carried out in compliance with relevant guidelines/regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants provided informed consent prior to their involvement in the study and were afforded the option to withdraw at any time. The study ensured that patient anonymity and confidentiality were strictly maintained.

General characteristics and anthropometric assessments

Demographic data were gathered through a structured questionnaire encompassing variables such as age, gender, levels of physical activity, and medical history related to ALS adults. Physical activity level was assessed using the international physical activity questionnaire [21]. A trained nutrition researcher conducted all anthropometric assessments in a single session for each participants. Body composition analysis was performed utilizing bioelectrical impedance analysis via the InBody S10 device. Height and weight were measured with a wall-mounted stadiometer and a wheelchair scale (Seca Co., Hamburg, Germany), with measurements rounded to the nearest 0.5 cm and 0.1 kg, respectively. in instances where it was impractical to measure standing height, ulna length was employed as an alternative method for height estimation. Furthermore, mid-arm circumference (MAC) was recorded in adults to the nearest 0.1 cm using a flexible, non-elastic tape measure, positioned at the midpoint processes on the shoulder and ulna, respectively.

Clinical and functional measurements

To evaluate functional capabilities, we employed the Amyotrophic lateral Sclerosis Functional Rating Scale-Revised (ALSFRS-R). This instrument comprises 12 items organized into four distinct domains: bulbar function, fine motor skills, gross motor abilities, and respiratory function. Each item is rated on a scale from 0 to 4, culminating in a total score that ranges from 0 to 48; higher scores reflect a lower degree of disability [22]. Furthermore, the Medical Research Council (MRC) score is obtained by assessing six bilateral movements across both superior and inferior limbs. Muscle strength is classified on a scale from zero (representing complete paralysis) to five (indicating normal strength), with the highest attainable score being 60 points, as outlined by Kleyweg et al. [23]. An MRC score of less than 48 signifies the presence of muscular weakness, as noted by Connolly et al. [24].

Statistical analyses

Initially, tertile cutoff values for the ALSDRS-R, MRC score, and disease progression rate (DPR) were established. Participants in this study were categorized according to these cutoff points, with ALSFRS-R tertiles defined as follows: T1 < 39, T2 ranging from 39 to 44, T3 > 44. The MRC scores were similarly classified into tertiles: T1 < 55, T2 between 55 and 62, and T3 > 62. The DPR was computed using the formula (). Based on calculated DPR values, participants were classified into tertiles of slow (< 0.5), intermediate (0.5–1.0), and fast (> 1.0) disease progression [25]. The difference in continuous variables were analyzed using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), while chi-square tests were employed to examine the distribution of categorical variables. To investigate the association between body composition and ALSFRS-R, MRC score, and DPR among adult participants with ALS, multiple linear regression analyses were executed. Four distinct regression models were utilized, as detailed in the tables, with first tertile of ALSFRS-R, MRC score, and DPR serving as the reference category. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons between tertiles were performed using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference test for continuous variables and Bonferroni-adjusted proportion tests for categorical variables. Statistical analyses were performed using RStudio version 2026.01.0 in conjunction with R version 4.5.2, utilizing relevant R packages such as “lm,” “chisq.test,” and “aov,” for the computations [26–28]. A two-sided P-value of ≤ 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of adults with ALS

In this cross-sectional study, a total of 93 adult subjects diagnosed with ALS was stratified into three distinct groups in congruence with their scores on the ALSFRS-R, MRC score, and DPR. The average age of the participants was recorded at 56.95 years. Comprehensive details regarding the demographic, anthropometric, body composition, and ALS-associated characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. According to the DPR, a significant difference was noted between the third tertile and the first tertile, characterized by an extended duration of diagnosis (P = 0.006), higher body weight (P = 0.008), elevated scores on the ALSFRS-R (P < 0.001), and greater MRC scores (P < 0.001). When evaluating based on ALSFRS-R, the third tertile demonstrated significant differences compared to the first tertile, with a greater proportion of participants classified as moderate to active (P = 0.021), longer diagnosis duration (P = 0.027), greater body weight (P = 0.004), lower weight changes (P = 0.007), higher MRC scores (P < 0.001), lower DPR values (P = 0.002), greater intracellular water levels (ICW) (P = 0.044), higher fat-free mass (FFM) (P = 0.048), higher skeletal muscle mass (SMM) (P = 0.035), and larger MAC measurements (P < 0.001). In alignment MRC scores, adults in the third tertile compared to those in the first tertile exhibited shorter diagnosis durations (P = 0.006), greater body weight (P = 0.008), higher ALSFRS-R scores (P < 0.001), and lower DPR values (P < 0.001).

Association between body composition and ALSFRS-R

As presented in Table 2, after adjusting for confounders like age, gender, BMI, physical activity, ALS history, and disease duration, a higher ALSFRS-R score was significantly associated with MAC (β: 3.0; 95CI%: 0.89, 5.1; P = 0.006). Other body composition components did not show meaningful relationships with ALSFRS-R. Supplementary figure 1 illustrates that, in subgroup analyses of the fourth model for the third tertile of ALSFRS-R, male adults had a significant positive association between extracellular water (ECW) and ALSFRS-R compared to females (β: 3.1; P < 0.001). Underweight and obese adults exhibited significant inverse and positive relationships between ECW and ALSFRS-R compared to normal weight adults (β: −1.10; P = 0.038; β: 3.3; P < 0.001). Low and moderate physical activity levels showed positive associations between ECW and ALSFRS-R compared to sedentary adults (β: −1.5; P = 0.009; β: −1.5; P < 0.001). Underweight adults had an inverse link between intracellular water (ICW) and ALSFRS-R compared to normal weight adults (β: −1.9; P = 0.045), and moderate physical activity also exhibited a negative association (β: −1.5; P = 0.044). Males demonstrated a direct relationship between fat-free mass (FFM) and ALSFRS-R (β: 11; P < 0.001), while underweight adults showed an inverse association (β: −4.0; P = 0.043). Low and moderate physical activity levels also demonstrated negative associations with FFM (β: −4.3; P = 0.041; β: −5.2; P = 0.037). Males had a lower association between fat mass (FM) and ALSFRS-R compared to females (β: −7.6; P < 0.001), and underweight adults exhibited a negative association with FM (β: −11; P < 0.001). A negative link between MAC and the third tertile of ALSFRS-R was observed in males (β: −2.0; P = 0.033) and underweight adults (β: −2.2; P = 0.037). Ultimately, underweight adults displayed an inverse association between soft lean mass (SLM) and the third tertile of ALSFRS-R, as did those with low and moderate physical activity compared to normal and sedentary adults (β: −3.7; P = 0.048; β: −4.1; P = 0.042; β: −4.9; P = 0.040).

Association between body composition and MRC score

As depicted in Table 3, after adjusting for confounders such as age, gender, BMI, physical activity, ALS history, and disease duration, meaningful positive links were found between FFM (β: 5.8; 95CI%: 2.2, 9.4; P = 0.002), SLM (β: 5.6; 95CI%: 2.1, 9.0; P = 0.002), SMM (β: 3.8; 95CI%: 1.6, 6.1; P = 0.001), MAC (β: 3.2; 95CI%: 1.2, 5.3; P = 0.002), ICW (β: 2.7; 95CI%: 1.0, 4.4; P = 0.002), and ECW (β: 1.5; 95CI%: 0.51, 2.4; P = 0.003) with the MRC score. Supplementary figure 2 exhibits subgroup analyses revealing that ALS participants with underweight status and low to moderate physical activity demonstrated meaningful inverse associations between ECW and MRC score in compared to normal weight and sedentary adults (β: −1.0; P = 0.037; β: −2.0; P < 0.001; β: −2.0; P = 0.003). Moreover, low and moderate activity levels and underweight status inversely affected the link between FFM and MRC score compared to those with a sedentary lifestyle and normal weight (β: −6.1; P = 0.005; β: −7.1; P = 0.005; β: −3.9; P = 0.038). Male adults like underweight participants exhibited a meaningful negative association between FM and MRC scores in compared to females and normal-weight adults (β: −8.1; P < 0.001; β: −11; P < 0.001). Additionally, underweight ALS participants, those with low physical activity, and those with moderate physical activity exhibit an inverse ICW-MRC association compared to normal-weight and sedentary adults (β: −1.8; P = 0.039; β: −2.7; P = 0.010; β: −3.2; P = 0.006). Underweight status also negatively impacted the MAC-MRC link (β: −2.3; P = 0.033). Negative associations were detected between SLM and MRC scores in underweight as well as in those with low and moderate activity levels (β: −3.6; P = 0.043; β: −5.9; P = 0.005; β: −6.7; P = 0.005). Lastly, in participants with low and moderate levels of physical activity, an inverse relationship was found between SMM and MRC scores compared to sedentary adults (β: −3.6; P = 0.009; β: −4.1; P = 0.008).

As displayed in Table 4, after adjusting for confounders such as age, gender, BMI, physical activity, and ALS history, a positive meaningful link between weight and DPR was found (β: 4.5; 95CI%: 0.02, 9.3; P = 0.002). Male adults and those with high and moderate physical activity illustrated a positive FFM-DPR relationship (β: 11; P < 0.001; β: 13; P < 0.001; β: 6.0; P < 0.001). ALS males had an inverse FM-DPR association (β: −1.4; P = 0.013), similar to underweight participants compared to normal-weights (β: −8.4; P < 0.001). Discussion

The analysis highlighted gender, weight, and activity-related differences in ALS outcomes. Males and obese adults showed positive associations between ECW, FFM, and functional scores, while underweight and low-activity adults exhibited inverse relationships. Positive links were observed for FFM, SLM, and SMM with MRC and DPR scores, but underweight and male participants demonstrated negative associations with FM and MAC. The analysis of gender, weight, and activity-related differences in ALS outcomes reveals complex interactions that influence disease progression and patient prognosis.

Notably, the observed β coefficient of 3.0 for MAC in relation to ALSFRS-R suggests a clinically meaningful association. Specifically, for each unit increase in MAC, the ALSFRS-R score increases by approximately 3 points, indicating better functional status. Clinically, this finding implies that greater peripheral muscle mass, as reflected by MAC, may be associated with slower functional decline and better overall disease status. Therefore, MAC could serve as a simple, non-invasive surrogate marker for nutritional and muscular status with potential prognostic value in ALS management.

Research indicates that gender plays a significant role in ALS outcomes. Males generally exhibit worse disease progression compared to females, as evidenced by shorter survival times and greater weight loss [29]. This gender disparity may be linked to differences in body composition and metabolic responses, which are crucial in ALS progression [30]. Weight variations significantly impact ALS outcomes. Obese adults tend to have better functional scores, possibly due to higher energy reserves that can be beneficial in the disease's progression [31, 32]. Conversely, underweight adults often experience worse outcomes, as low body mass index (BMI) is associated with rapid disease progression and shorter survival [30, 32]. The genetic link between weight loss and ALS further underscores the importance of maintaining a healthy weight in managing the disease [33]. Activity levels also influence ALS outcomes. Adults with low activity levels often show inverse relationships with functional scores, suggesting that maintaining physical activity could be beneficial in managing ALS symptoms [31]. The positive associations of FFM, SLM, and SMM with functional scores like the MRC and DPR scores highlight the importance of muscle mass in preserving function [30].

The study highlights positive associations of FFM, SLM, and SMM with MRC and DPR scores, indicating that higher muscle mass is associated with better functional outcomes. However, underweight and male participants show negative associations with FM and MAC, suggesting that these factors may contribute to poorer functional outcomes in these groups [30].

The pathophysiology of body composition changes in ALS involves several interconnected factors, including motor neuron degeneration, hypermetabolism, and nutritional imbalances, which collectively contribute to disease progression and survival outcomes. ALS leads to motor neuron degeneration, which, along with malnutrition, results in the loss of weight, FM, and FFM. This loss is associated with shorter survival times in ALS adults [34]. Hypermetabolism: A significant proportion of ALS adults experience hypermetabolism, characterized by increased resting energy expenditure. This condition is linked to greater functional decline, lower fat mass, and shorter survival [34]. Making it an adverse prognostic factor [35, 36]. Hypermetabolism may be driven by factors such as mitochondrial dysfunction and increased pro-inflammatory cytokine production [36]. Fat Mass and Disease Progression: Loss of fat mass correlates with faster disease progression. Low baseline fat mass and its subsequent loss are significant predictors of reduced survival in ALS adults [37]. Energy Balance: Despite losses in lean mass, some ALS adults maintain caloric balance by increasing fat mass, which can partially compensate for energy deficits due to disease progression [14]. Metabolic and Inflammatory Pathways: Alterations in metabolic and inflammatory pathways, including changes in cholesterol metabolism, are implicated in ALS. These changes may affect body composition and are potential targets for therapeutic intervention [33, 38]. Nutritional Management: Maintaining a higher body mass index (BMI) and nutritional state is associated with improved outcomes in ALS. Nutritional interventions focusing on energy balance and body mass maintenance are crucial for managing ALS [39].

Strengths and weaknesses

This research explore the connection between body composition and the progression of ALS. Several strengths bolster the study, including the utilization of RStudio and R software for data analysis and visualizations, as well as conducting subgroup analyses based on variables such as age, gender, BMI, physical activity, ALS history, and disease duration, offering insights into group-specific patterns that might otherwise remain unnoticed. However, the study faced certain limitations. The small sample size, attributed to the rarity of ALS and its typically poor prognosis, posed a challenge. Additionally, not all participants were recently diagnosed, leading to varying disease severities and contributing to data heterogeneity. Moreover, given the multiple subgroup comparisons and the limited sample size in certain strata, these analyses should be interpreted as exploratory in nature, with an acknowledged increased risk of Type I error and limited statistical power to draw definitive subgroup-specific conclusions. Ultimately, in this study, ALS subtype and onset were not categorized, and we evaluated the association between the DII and body composition and disease progression in people living with ALS.

Conclusion

Overall, the findings emphasize the importance of considering gender, weight, and activity levels in managing ALS. Maintaining a healthy weight and muscle mass, along with regular physical activity, is associated with better outcomes and survival in ALS adults. These insights could guide personalized treatment strategies to enhance patient care.
