Risk of psychiatric hospitalization in low-income youth: longitudinal findings from the 100 Million Brazilian Cohort
Authors: Lidiane Toledo, Rodrigo Rodrigues, Flávia Alves, Fillipe Guedes, Jacyra Azevedo Paiva de Araújo, John A Naslund, Maurício L Barreto, Vikram Patel, Daiane Borges Machado
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYouth psychiatric hospitalizations have been associated with negative outcomes, including premature death and post-discharge self-harm. Identifying risk factors for youth psychiatric hospitalization is crucial for informing prevention strategies. We aimed to evaluate the risk factors for psychiatric hospitalizations among low-income youth in Brazil.MethodsThis cohort study used interpersonal violence and psychiatric hospitalization data linked to the 100 Million Brazilian Cohort baseline. We considered 9 985 917 youths aged 5–24 years who enrolled at the baseline, between 2011 and 2018. We estimated the incidence rate (IR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) for psychiatric hospitalization by calculating the number of hospitalizations per person-year in 100 000 individuals at risk. The multilevel, multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression estimated the hazard risks (HR) with 95% CI for psychiatric hospitalization.ResultsThe IR of psychiatric hospitalization was 12.28 per 100 000 person-years (95% CI, 11.96–12.6). Interpersonal violence victimization was the main risk factor for youth psychiatric hospitalization (HR, 5.24; 95% CI, 4.61–5.96). Other risk factors for psychiatric hospitalization included living with the oldest family member who had low education (HR, 2.51; 95% CI, 2.16–2.91) or was unemployed (HR, 1.49; 95% CI, 1.36–1.62), living with seven or more family members (HR, 1.84; 95% CI, 1.49–2.26) and being male (HR, 1.28; 95% CI, 1.21–1.36).ConclusionsUrgent action is needed to prevent youth from suffering violence. Addressing this may alleviate the mental health burden in developmental ages, benefiting youth, families and the government through reduced costs in preventable psychiatric hospitalizations.Keywords: Child, adolescent, young adult, hospitalization, mental health, low- and middle-income countries, incidence, multilevel analysis, cohort study

Introduction

The incidence of mental disorders among young people has increased by 6.8% worldwide over recent decades.1 Different levels of care are needed to treat youth mental disorders.2 Depending on the severity of the case and risk of self-harm, youth psychiatric hospitalization may be required. However, psychiatric hospitalization has been linked to several negative outcomes, such as a high mortality rate, premature death, post-discharge self-harm, and readmissions during their lifespans.3 Conversely, severe psychiatric disorders may have a confounding role, contributing not only to hospitalization but also to the occurrence of adverse outcomes (i.e. peer rejection, suicidal behaviour).4 Youth psychiatric hospitalization also contributes to school dropouts, jeopardizing future workforce participation and increasing the likelihood of economic and social disadvantages into adulthood.5 Youth psychiatric hospitalization is also costly for health care systems,6 which is particularly important in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where mental health investments are minimal, and require rationalization. Characterizing the risk factors for youth psychiatric hospitalization is critical, in order to inform prevention efforts. Interpersonal violence has been described as one of the important risk factors for youth psychiatric hospitalization in LMICs and high-income countries (HICs).7–9 In addition, individual (e.g. sex, age, adolescent or young adult) and low socioeconomic status (e.g. low household income and having an illiterate father) have also been identified as risk factors in LMICs and HICs.7,10 Nonetheless, high-quality and longitudinal evidence on risk factors for youth psychiatric hospitalization is lacking, particularly from low-resource countries but also from HICs. The majority of previous studies have been descriptive, had cross-sectional designs, were time-limited, lacked appropriate comparison groups, and included small, and sometimes non-representative, only clinical samples.5–10 To the best of our knowledge, there have been no investigations using a population-based cohort to identify risk factors for psychiatric hospitalization among low-income youth. Understanding the risk factors that lead to psychiatric hospitalization in this population is important in orientating early intervention, informing policy makers on allocating resources to more effective prevention programmes and identifying the groups at greatest risk. We hypothesized that individual factors, such as sex and interpersonal violence, would also emerge as risk factors for psychiatric hospitalization among low-income youth, in addition to previously unreported factors related to low household socioeconomic status. Using nationwide data from the 100 Million Brazilian Cohort, this study investigated the risk factors associated with psychiatric hospitalizations among low-income youth in Brazil from 2011 to 2018.

Methods

Study design and dataset source

We conducted a population-based, nationwide, retrospective cohort study with data from the 100 Million Brazilian Cohort.11 Data from the National Hospital Information System (SIH) and from the Information System for Notifiable Diseases—Violence subset (SINAN) were linked to the cohort baseline. The baseline contains the sociodemographic and household data of low-income individuals who registered at Cadastro Único (CadÚnico) at any time in the study period to receive social welfare benefits from the Brazilian government. SIH records include all hospital admissions within the Unified Health System of Brazil (SUS), covering 70% of the Brazilian population.12 SINAN incorporates the National Violence and Accident Surveillance System, and records notified violence cases across national territory.13 The three datasets were anonymized and stored at the Centre for Data and Knowledge Integration for Health (CIDACS), following ethical and legal regulations and according to Brazilian data protection law.14 Deterministic linkage was performed using CIDACS-RL, which is a record-linkage tool developed to link Brazilian large-scale administrative datasets (Supplementary Material, available as Supplementary data at IJE online).15 Details of the study protocol on the evaluation of social determinants of youth mental health-related hospitalizations have been published elsewhere.16 We followed the RECORD statement (Supplementary Material, available as Supplementary data at IJE online) guidelines for observational study reporting.

Study population

The study population included individuals aged between 5 and 24 enrolled on the 100 Million Brazilian Cohort baseline at any date between 1 January 2011 and 31 December 2018. Supplementary Figure S1 (available as Supplementary data at IJE online) describes the selection process to obtain our final cohort and the exclusions criteria adopted to ensure the longitudinal structure of our dataset (i.e. covariates always occurred before the outcome).

Variables

The primary outcome was psychiatric hospitalization (yes or no). Hospitalization may include both voluntary and involuntary psychiatric admissions; however, we are not able to distinguish this in our dataset. The psychiatric reform law in Brazil states that hospitalization is only indicated when extra-hospital resources are insufficient to manage acute crises and life-threatening situations.17 We considered the first hospitalization in the period and psychiatric conditions were recorded as a primary or secondary cause according to International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) codes F00-F99.

The baseline covariates were: sex (female and male), race/skin colour/ethnicity measured according to the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE in Portuguese) (Black, Brown, White, Asian descendants, and Indigenous people); 18 household size (from two to six people, and seven people or more); oldest household member's education level (<4 years of education, 4 and <12 years of education and ≥12 years of education); oldest household member’s unemployment (yes or no). The oldest member in the family was used as a proxy for the head of household’s socioeconomic status, as described elsewhere (Supplementary Material, available as Supplementary data at IJE online).19 In addition, we also created a composite variable to represent household living conditions, which included information on an appropriate water supply, sanitation, electricity, housing materials, garbage disposal. This household composite is a continuous variable that has scores ranging from 0 to 5, where 0 indicates none of these essential services and 5 indicates it has all of them (Supplementary Material, available as Supplementary data at IJE online). We also included the location of residence (urban and rural) and interpersonal violence notification covariate (yes or no). The interpersonal violence covariate was obtained from SINAN-Violence, which takes part in the Brazilian Violence Surveillance System. Since 2011, Brazil has had mandatory notification of cases of violence attended by the SUS or private health units.13 In SINAN-Violence, interpersonal violence is defined as the intentional use of physical force or power against another person, resulting in embarrassment, physical pain or psychological distress.13 We considered all causes of interpersonal violence and subtypes of causes of violence including non-sexual physical violence, sexual violence and psychological violence (yes or no). Interpersonal violence is recorded in SINAN-Violences by health professionals who register the cases according to ICD-10, code Y09.13 Only the first observed episode of violence during the study period, before the hospitalization, was considered.

Mental health care units’ covariates included coverage of psychosocial care centres (CAPS, its acronym in Portuguese), reflected by municipal coverage (Supplementary Material, available as Supplementary data at IJE online), using the number of CAPSs per municipality and its respective population by year,20 and the number of psychiatric beds per municipality, normalized by the respective municipal population. Both were measured as continuous variables.

Follow-up

Follow-up began on the cohort entry date if the individual was between 5 and 24 years old, or on their fifth birthday if they turned 5 years of age during the follow-up. Follow-up ended at the earliest occurrence of one of the following censoring criteria: the individual's 25th birthday, death, psychiatric hospitalization or the end of the cohort on 31 December 2018.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses considered the absolute and relative frequency of the categorical covariates and the mean and standard deviation of the continuous covariates. We estimated the incidence rate (IR) of psychiatric hospitalization as the number of hospitalization events per 100 000 person-years at risk. The IR with a 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated overall by covariates and by specific ICD-10 causes of hospitalization stratified by sex and age group: children (5–11 years old), adolescents (12–17 years old) and young adults (18–24 years old). To identify the risk factors for youth psychiatric hospitalization, we conducted a multilevel, multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis allowing for municipality and region of residence random effects. We used a random intercept model to measure the random effect or clustering of participants at the municipality and regional levels. The multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis multilevel model was run to estimate the hazard risks (HR) with a 95% CI for all-cause psychiatric hospitalization across all ages and specific age groups, by type of psychiatric hospitalization across all ages and by type of interpersonal violence.

Results

We investigated 9 985 917 low-income youths, 9 980 075 of whom were not hospitalized (followed up on average for 5.29 years) and 5842 were hospitalized (followed up on average for 2.97 years). For descriptive characteristics of hospitalized and non-hospitalized youths, see Table 1. For descriptive results by ICD-10 psychiatric diagnoses subgroups, see Supplementary Table S1 (available as Supplementary data at IJE online). The overall IR was 12.28 per 100 000 person-years (95% CI, 11.9–12.6) (Table 2). The IR of psychiatric hospitalization was higher in young adults (IR, 43.04; 95% CI, 40.63–45.55) and adolescents (IR, 38.45; 95% CI, 37.03–39.91) compared with children (IR, 4.99; 95% CI, 4.77–5.22). The IR among young people who were victims of interpersonal violence was 80.1 per 100 000 person-years (95% CI, 71.78–89.13), whereas it was 11.67 per 100 000 person-years (95% CI, 11.37–11.98) among non-victims (Table 2). Figure 1 displays the IR of hospitalization by ICD-10 diagnosis subgroup, disaggregated by sex. Male children, adolescents and young adults had a higher IR of substance use disorders (1.5, 20.4 and 27.8 per 100 000 person-years, respectively) compared with their female peers (1.0, 6.7, and 8.1 per 100 000 person-years, respectively). Female adolescents and young adults had a higher IR of mood disorders (10.7 and 12.1 per 100 000 person-years, respectively) compared with their peers (5.6 and 9.1 per 100 000 person-years, respectively).

Table 3 presents the hazard ratios for youth psychiatric hospitalization, considering all and specific age groups. The young people who were victims of interpersonal violence had a >5-fold higher risk of psychiatric hospitalization (HR, 5.24; 95% CI, 4.61–5.96) than non-victims. Across all age groups, interpersonal violence was associated with psychiatric hospitalization. This association was particularly important for children, who had a more than 7-fold greater risk (HR, 7.72; 95% CI, 6.03–9.89) of psychiatric hospitalization compared with their non-victim counterparts. Living with the oldest family member who had low education or was unemployed was associated with an increased risk of psychiatric hospitalization (HR, 2.51; 95% CI, 2.16–2.91 and HR, 1.49; 95% CI, 1.36–1.62, respectively). Moreover, youths living in a crowded household with seven or more members had their risk of psychiatric hospitalization increased by 84% (HR, 1.84; 95% CI, 1.49–2.26) compared with those in less crowded households. Finally, being a male youth was associated with an increased risk of psychiatric hospitalization (HR, 1.28; 95% CI, 1.21–1.36). Discussion

This population-based, nationwide cohort study investigated the risk factors for psychiatric hospitalization among more than 9 million low-income youths over an 8-year period in Brazil. Factors such as: experiencing previous interpersonal violence; indicators of the poorest socioeconomic situation, including living in households where the oldest member had low educational attainment, was unemployed, or the household was crowded; and male sex, were the main risks identified. These findings underscore the interconnections between violent victimization, unfavourable socioeconomic conditions and severe psychiatric disorders in a critical stage of life, emphasizing the importance of early interventions to mitigate these risks.

The age gradient observed, whereby the older the age group, the higher the psychiatric hospitalization rate, aligns with prior studies conducted in HICs.6,10 Consistent with previous studies, we found that the IR of youth psychiatric hospitalization was uneven by sex according to the diagnosis.5,21 As we found, substance use disorders have been described as one of the most important causes of psychiatric hospitalization for young males.22,23 Across all age groups worldwide, males are more affected by substance use disorders than females.24 This can be partly attributed to biological factors (e.g. differences in pharmacokinetics, sex hormones, the metabolism of certain substances, and genetic differences), as well as social norms affecting male and female youths in different ways.25 Typically, substance use disorders among women are less acceptable in certain social contexts than among men.25 Often, women are more stigmatized and face more prejudice than men who use substances, which may create barriers to seeking treatment. On the other hand, in both HICs and in LMICs, females are more likely to suffer from mood disorders than males.5,26

Our findings highlight the role of interpersonal violence as an important risk factor of all and specific causes of psychiatric hospitalization, corroborating previous evidence from LMICs and HICs.8,9,27,28 The estimates we observed among interpersonal violence victims were higher than those observed by Stewart and colleagues (2022) in Ontario, Canada (OR, 1.36; 95% CI, 1.22–1.53; 2012–20).9 Violent victimization is a major stressor, particularly in the early stages of life, and is associated with acute trauma and long-term negative repercussions such as poor mental health.27,28 Furthermore, the relationship between interpersonal violence and psychiatric hospitalization is probably furthered by the context of socioeconomic inequality. Young people from low-income families may be more vulnerable to violence-related stress than those with better socioeconomic conditions.29,30 These stress factors may encompass economic pressure that raises family stress due to the uncertainty of covering basic needs, which can lead to parental conflicts and affect the ways of parenting.29,30 Furthermore, low-income youth may be more vulnerable to bullying and peer aggression,31 neighbourhood violence and substandard health care, all of which may negatively impact on their mental health.

Our study demonstrated the association between factors related to the poorest socioeconomic condition—such as lower education and unemployment of the oldest family member, as well as living in crowded households with the risk of youth psychiatric hospitalization. Numerous researches have demonstrated that poverty increases the likelihood of developing or worsening psychiatric disorders.32–34 Ziebold et al. (2023)35 published a recent prospective community cohort study of 1796 young people in Brazil, to examine the association between childhood poverty and mental health disorders in adolescents (13 years old) and early adulthood (18 years old) as compared with their non-poor peers. The authors found a correlation between baseline poverty and an increased risk of having an externalizing disorder in either adolescence or early adulthood (standardized estimate, 0.27; P = 0.016).35 Multiple factors can explain the link between poverty and poor mental health outcomes, including but not limited to exposure to stressful life events such as financial hardships, social exclusion, inequities in access to health care and education, and environmental stresses such as living in inadequate and crowded housing.33,34 Also, consistent with previous research, we observed sex differences in the risk of psychiatric hospitalization.6,9 In our study, young males had their risk of being hospitalized increased by 28%. Similarly, a cross-sectional study in Ontario, Canada, has shown that being male was associated with a greater likelihood of adolescent psychiatric inpatient admissions (OR, 1.19; 95% CI, 1.08–1.31) compared with being female.9

Various implications emerge from our findings. First, most mental disorders emerge between the ages of 14 and 18,36 which makes youth a crucial phase of life for primary and secondary prevention to avoid the onset and aggravation of mental illness during the life course.37 Thus, school-based interventions, such as social and emotional skills development, should be strengthened.37 Preventing youth psychiatric hospitalization could also be achieved by training primary health care providers in the early detection of and intervention in mental illness and expanding the coverage of community-based services, such as psychosocial care centres. Each psychiatric hospitalization should be considered a critical opportunity for tertiary preventive measures to reduce illness severity and hospital readmissions, by integrating primary and secondary health team visits after discharge, to monitor at-risk youth's mental health needs. Second, breaking the poverty cycle, which may be exacerbated by mental disorders, is crucial. Enrolling low-income families with young people in conditional cash transfer programmes should be prioritized, as these programmes have been proven to lessen the burden of violence and mental disorders among youth.38,39 Preventive measures beyond cash-based incentives such as positive parenting skills training, may prevent youth from suffering violence and consequently contribute to lessening the burden of psychiatric morbidity.40 Timely trauma-informed intervention to support youth victims of violence is urgently required.

Strengths and limitations

Our findings enhance those from previous studies on the risk factors of youth psychiatric hospitalization in HICs, and add new, robust results from LMICs. To date, evidence on the risk factors of youth psychiatric hospitalization from LMICs has been rare in the mental health field, particularly considering cohort studies. Brazil has a wide morbidity and violence surveillance information system and is possibly one of the few LMICs in the world with an installed capacity for the linkage of administrative records from these systems. The linkage of hospitalization and violence records with sociodemographic information has enabled us to assess individual and household factors for youth psychiatric hospitalization in an unseen way. Contrary to previous studies, we analysed one of the largest nationwide, population-based datasets, linked to a non-hospitalized comparison group. Additionally, we ran data beyond descriptive analysis, providing estimates and confidence intervals that may be useful for future metaregressions.

This study has limitations. First, the 100 Million Brazilian Cohort only comprises low-income individuals; therefore, our results could not be extrapolated for the entire Brazilian population. Second, as an inherent characteristic and limitation of administrative data, we lack information on patients who met the case severity criteria for hospitalization but did not have access to psychiatric beds due to structural barriers, such as stigma, ethnoracial inequalities, a shortage of services and providers and living in remote areas, which may lead to worse mental health outcomes. Third, routine diagnostic data may present the misclassification of mental disorders in health records using ICD codes. However, the Brazilian Ministry of Health has provided protocols and training to medical professionals to follow the International Classification of Diseases diagnostic standard recommendations, in order to avoid this. Fourth, by design, individuals who entered the cohort at younger ages were followed up for longer periods than individuals who entered at older ages, and thus they have a differential risk to present the outcome. Fifth, by design, we lack some covariates such as parents' psychiatric disorders, family dysfunction and parental practices. Sixth, the sociodemographic variables were measured once when the individual was enrolled in the cohort baseline and were not updated thereafter. Finally, due to the structural barriers to health care access and the heterogeneity of health care unit availability, which tends to be scarce in the poorest Brazilian Regions such as North and Northeast for example, low-income individuals may be more likely to have their violence cases underreported.41,42

Conclusion

In conclusion, there is an urgent need for health authorities to take effective action to protect youths from suffering violence. Addressing the prevention of violence among youth may reduce the burden of mental disorders in the development stages, which may benefit youths, families and the society in general, by reducing the costs of preventable psychiatric hospitalizations. Children and teenagers currently have increased mental health issues due to the lasting impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic, which is likely to persist in the coming years. However, youth mental health has been a neglected agenda in Brazil and other LMICs, particularly with regards to access to specific public, community-based, mental health services, and research focused on overcoming barriers, in order to make these services more widely available. Last, we hope our findings will help to inform more equitable access to mental health treatment for neglected youth, not only in Brazil but also in other middle-income countries and globally.
