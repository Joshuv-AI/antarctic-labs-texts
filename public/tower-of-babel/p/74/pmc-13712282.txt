Ultra-sensitive detection of receptor binding domain of SARS-CoV-2 based on artificial intelligence assisted antibody selection and antibody‒nucleic acid conjugation signal amplification
Authors: Jiatong Liu, Wenfan Chen, Xiufeng Li, Yuanyuan Luo, Qiaomin Li, Wenbin Zhao, Qiaojun He, Su Zeng, Zhan Zhou, Sheng Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The emergence of SARS-CoV-2 has led to a global pandemic, causing unprecedented disruption to health systems and economies worldwide, calling for an urgent imperative for precise and sensitive diagnostic method. To address this, a novel detection method, termed antibody‒nucleic acid conjugate linked immunosorbent assay (ANCLISA), was designed for the ultra-sensitive detection of the receptor binding domain (RBD) of SARS-CoV-2, which play a pivotal role in the diagnosis of COVID-19. Leveraging the Alphafold 2 model for simulating antibody‒antigen complexes and the ΔΔG predictor model for forecasting the binding free energy of antigen‒antibody complex, we successfully identified the high-affinity antibody TB1. Through the utilization of succinimidyl 4-[N-maleimidomethyl] cyclohexane-1-carboxylate (SMCC) for antibody‒nucleic acid conjugation and the integration of the hybridization chain reaction (HCR) for signal amplification within the system, we have established an ultra-sensitive approach for the SARS-CoV-2 Delta variant detection. The proposed immunoassay provided exhibited a linear detection range spanning 11 fg/mL to 11 ng/mL, achieving a limit of detection (LOD) of 1.48 fg/mL. Our innovative approach represents a significant stride in enabling precise and ultra-sensitive detection of SARS-CoV-2, thereby holding promise for enhancing diagnostic capabilities in the ongoing global battle against the pandemic.

Introduction

The emergence of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) has led to a global pandemic1,2, causing unprecedented disruption to health systems3, 4, 5 and economies6, 7, 8 worldwide. The urgent imperative for precise and sensitive diagnostic methodologies to detect SARS-CoV-2 infections has been underscored by the virus's rapid dissemination and the identification of new variants9, such as the highly transmissible Delta variant10,11. Commonly used viral detection methods contain nucleic acid-based techniques and antigen-based techniques. As the gold standard for nucleic acid detection, the reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) technique exhibits exceptional sensitivity and specificity12, 13, 14. However, inevitable limitations, such as the requirement for sophisticated and costly instrumentation, well-trained personnel, have influenced rapid high-throughput on-site viral screening in public settings or swift diagnostics in remote areas. Antigen-based testing, renowned for its swift outcomes, has emerged as a widely adopted strategy for rapid point-of-care testing (POCT)15, 16, 17. As an emerging optical label, colloidal gold is renowned for its high quantum yield and extensive application in colorimetric and sensing processes. Therefore, the Dot-immunogold filtration assay (DIGFA) is distinguished by its rapid detection time and portability, rendering it the most commonly utilized method for antigen-based testing18, 19, 20. Nevertheless, its indisputable limitations, including low sensitivity and dependence on antibody recognition specificity, present certain constraints. In light of these concerns, an accurate, specific, sensitive, equipment-free, personnel-friendly, diagnostic method is highly needed.

A high-quality antibody is extremely important for antigen detection. Up to now, the traditional antibody discovery methods, such as hybridoma technology, phage display technology, and single B cell antibody screening technology21, had made many achievements, but the efficiency still needs to be improved. Firstly, an antibody binding to some antigens or epitopes of multiple transmembrane proteins and with low immunogenicity is hard to acquire. Secondly, limited by developability, only a few antibodies can continue to be developed and become therapeutics22. Moreover, to obtaining high-affinity antibodies by simple modification when the antigen is mutated at a rapid rate, high-precision complex structure is needed, but requires X-ray crystallography or NMR, which is often time-consuming and low-throughput23. Now the application of artificial intelligence (AI) models based on big data and deep learning has permeated various aspects of the biotechnological landscape24. Integrating AI into antibody screening not only expedites the development process but also furnishes more precise and personalized therapeutic strategies, holding significant significance. Recently, Shanker et al.25 developed a novel protein design method to achieve efficient antibody evolution by integrating structural information into language models, which significantly improved antibody 25-fold neutralizing activity against SARS-CoV-2 variants. This study not only provides new ideas for the development of antibody drugs, but also opens new avenues for directed evolution of other proteins. He et al.26 proposed a pre-trained large language model for antibody generation (PALM-H3) for de novo generation of antibody CDRH3 with strong specificity, thereby reducing the dependence on natural antibodies. The authors also constructed an antigen–antibody binding predictor (A2binder), which can pair the antigen epitope sequence with the antibody sequence to predict the binding specificity and affinity. PALM-H3 generated antibodies showed the ability to bind to SARS-CoV-2 antigens, including the emerging XBB variant, as validated by computer simulation analysis and in vitro experiments. Beyond traditional methodologies, the incorporation of these AI methods into the screening of antibodies targeting the SARS-CoV-2 variants paves the way for enhanced efficiency and precision in identification27.

Being the most prevalent antigen‒antibody detection method, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is an extensively used immunological assay in basic scientific research, clinical investigations, and diagnostics28,29. The execution of ELISA relies on the interaction between the antigen and the primary antibody, while enzyme-linked secondary antibodies are employed to confirm the presence of the target. However, when used to detect the SARS-CoV-2, although this method has demonstrated effectiveness, it still faces the dual challenge of high cost and the need to enhance sensitivity to meet the urgent clinical demands30,31. Presently, there has been rapid development in nucleic acid signal amplification techniques. Methods such as rolling circle amplification (RCA)32, 33, 34, 35, catalytic hairpin assembly (CHA)36, 37, 38, and HCR39,40 have been extensively investigated for their capacity to produce substantial amounts of amplified products from minimal initial inputs. Among these techniques, the enzyme-free method HCR holds particular promise due to its simplicity and high specificity. Based on antibody‒nucleic acid conjugation, the HCR technique can be applied to antigen detection, presenting a novel approach for highly sensitive detection in rapid POCT.

Recently, antibody‒nucleic acid conjugate has discreetly captivated public attention41. This methodology astutely capitalizes on the function of linkers, whose distinctive functional groups can effectively undergo distinct covalent binding reactions with nucleic acids and antibodies, thereby establishing a proximate association between the two. This method has furnished the field of target detection with a more diversified arsenal, simultaneously holding immense potential in the exploration of varied and efficient detection strategies42, 43, 44. In this study, we developed an integrated ultra-sensitive detection strategy, termed antibody‒nucleic acid conjugate linked immunosorbent assay (ANCLISA), designed for the ultra-sensitive detection of the receptor binding domain (RBD) of the SARS-CoV-2, which was responsible for facilitating viral attachment to the host cell receptor ACE2 and representing a prime target for neutralizing antibodies, plays a pivotal role in the diagnosis of COVID-19. Our approach integrates a variety of cutting-edge AI techniques for the screening of SARS-CoV-2 RBD antibodies. By simulating the secondary structure of antigen-antibody complexes and conducting in-depth assessments of their binding free energy, we comprehensively analyze and predict the interaction characteristics between antibodies and antigens, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of the screening results. By leveraging the established antibody‒nucleic acid conjugation chemistry as a bridge, we successfully incorporated HCR signal amplification into the immunoassay system to enhance sensitivity. Through this approach, we aim to significantly contribute to the arsenal of diagnostic tools available for combating the ongoing challenges posed by SARS-CoV-2 variants.

Construction of delta RBD-hFc vector

The Delta RBD-hFc vector was constructed as follows. Initially, the RBD fragment and human IgG1-modified Fc fragment (hereafter referred to as hFc) were separately amplified via PCR. Subsequently, overlapping extension PCR was employed to splice RBD with hFc. In the third step, extension PCR was utilized to introduce the EcoRI restriction site (GAATTC) and the Kozak sequence (ACCACC) upstream, along with the 293 cell signal peptide (Supporting Information Fig. S1). Additionally, a stop codon (TGA) and XhoI restriction site (GCGGCCGC) were introduced downstream. Finally, the double-digested PCR product was ligated into the eukaryotic expression vector pCDNA3.1. The ligated product was then transformed into DH5α Escherichia coli, and single clones with correct sequencing were selected and preserved.

Expression of recombinant RBD protein

In a laminar flow hood, 10 mL of serum-free and antibiotic-free SMM 293TI medium was added to each flask. In one flask, 2 mL of PEI was added, while in another flask, 120 μg of plasmid was added. Both flasks were gently inverted to mix, and then the plasmid-containing SMM 293TI medium was added to the PEI-containing SMM 293TI medium. After thorough mixing, the mixture was left to stand at room temperature for 10 to 15 min. The medium containing the plasmid‒PEI complex was then added to a larger bottle, supplemented with an additional 25 mL of SMM 293TI medium. Finally, antibiotics and serum were added to achieve final concentrations of 1% and 0.5% respectively. The transfected cells were returned to the cell culture incubator and cultured for 4 to 5 days before purification (Supporting Information Fig. S2).

Mouse immunization and RNA extraction

According to the instructions provided by Suzhou Boltorn Immunotechnology Co., Ltd. for QuickAntibody-Mouse5 (Catalog No.: WKX0210041), the antigen RBD Delta-Fc was diluted to a final concentration of 2-fold using physiological saline and mixed at a 1:1 ratio with QuickAntibody-Mouse5W. The mice were immunized by injecting 100 μL of the mixture into the muscles of the hind legs, and a booster immunization was administered in the same manner on the 21st day. On the 35th day, a small amount of blood was collected from the tail for ELISA testing. The spleen was harvested, lymphocytes were isolated, and total RNA was extracted.

Amplification and splicing of antibody variable heavy (VH) and light (VL) chains

Using mouse total RNA as a template, cDNA was reverse transcribed according to the kit instructions. The VH and VL genes of the antibody were separately amplified using the cDNA as a template (Supporting Information Figs. S3 and S4). The purified VH and VL fragments were mixed in a 1:1 ratio and spliced into the ScFv gene via PCR (Supporting Information Fig. S5). The PCR program included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 70 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The spliced ScFv gene was then diluted 1:100 and used as a template for amplifying the target ScFv gene. The PCR program for this step included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The PCR products were identified and the target fragments were purified using 10 g/L agarose gel electrophoresis.

ScFv library construction and screen

Mice is immunized by RBD-hFc according to QuickAntibody-Mouse5W protocol (Biodragon, KX0210041), the tail vein blood was collected for ELISA. The spleen was chosen to construct phage libraries (Supporting Information Fig. S6). A liquid phase protocol was performed for panning of phagedisplayed antibody library according to Streptavidin Agarose (beyotim, P2159). After the third round of panning, randomly selected clones were cultured and infected with M13K07 helper phage. The rescued phages were applied to phage ELISA with 0.5 μg/mL RBD (Supporting Information Fig. S7). The positive clones were selected for DNA sequencing. Each scFv gene was sequenced using primers: the forward primer F1 (5′-CCATGATTACGCCAAGCTTTGGAGCC-3′) and the backward primer R1 (5′-CGATCTAAAGTTTTGTCGTCTTTCC-3′). Sequences were analyzed using Snapgene software and fused to human IgG1 for binding test.

Antibody expression

The positively screened phage antibody sequences were aligned, and after constructing the complete antibody sequence, codon optimization was performed. The synthesized genes were then constructed into the eukaryotic expression vector PTT5. The purified recombinant heavy and light chain plasmids were co-transfected into HEK293 cells using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent at 3 μg of plasmid per well (1.5 μg each for heavy and light chains) following the manufacturer's instructions. The transfected cells were then cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 48 h.

SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis

Protein samples were denatured with 6 × Loading Buffer at 100 °C for 5 min and separated via SDS-PAGE using a precast polyacrylamide gel system (Supporting Information Fig. S8). Electrophoresis was performed at 90 V for 15 min followed by 170 V until the bromophenol blue tracking dye migrated appropriately. Gels were stained with Coomassie Bright Blue, destained in deionized water, and imaged using a gel documentation system.

Binding test of antibody

Antibody was expressed in HEK293F and purified. The 96-well microtiter plates were coated with 100 μL of 0.5 μL/mL antigen at 4 °C for 16 h. After three washings with 0.05% PBST, blocked with 200 μL of 5% (w/v) skimmed milk at 37 °C for 2 h. After three washings with 0.05% PBST, a series of concentration of antibody was added to each well. The plates were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C and washed three times with 0.05% PBST. Then the plate wells were incubated with 100 μL of 1:5000 diluted HRP-labeled goat anti-human IgG antibody for 1 h at 37 °C. After washing, 100 μL of TMB solution was added for 15 min, and 50 μL/well of the stop solution is added to stop reaction and measure the absorbance at 450 nm.

AI simulation of antibody‒antigen complex modeling

We begin by using AlphaFold2 to model the 3D structure of SARS-CoV-2 RBD and antibody candidates, which provides the structural basis for evaluating potential antibody binding sites45. Due to the lack of available complex crystal structures in the existing PDB database, the Alphafold2 software has demonstrated the most accurate model precision in the field of protein structure prediction. It also provides models for predicting protein‒protein complexes. Therefore, Alphafold2 was used to predict the complex structures of the RBD region of the self-expressed antibodies TB1 and TB2 with different variants of the SARS-CoV-2 virus, including Delta (GISAID: EPI_ISL_1534938), Omicron JN.1 (GISAID: EPI_ISL_18518369), and the Wild Type (GenBank: BCN86353.1).

GeoPPI analyzes the interaction hotspots between RBD and antibodies, guiding the selection of promising antibodies based on predicted residue-level interactions46. The tool GeoPPI was used to calculate the changes in affinity resulting from single-point mutations between TB1 and TB2, specifically the VH chain mutations D56G, N57H, T58S, N63K, I70L, A72T, S114T, and L115V, as well as the VL chain mutations V3L, L4M, and L106I.

The ΔΔG predictor is used to estimate the binding affinity change of the TB1/TB2-RBD complexes47. Antibodies are ranked based on their predicted ΔΔG values, with lower values indicating stronger predicted binding affinities. The binding free energy of the complexes between the TB1 and TB2 antibodies with the RBD of the wild type, Delta, and Omicron JN.1 was comparatively predicted using ΔΔG predictor model. The TB2 antibody complex was used as the wild-type template, and the TB1 antibody complex was used as the mutant template to calculate the changes in free energy.

Validation of antibody affinity

The RBD protein antigen was coated on a 96-well plate under conditions of 37 °C for 2 h. Next, each well was washed three times with 200 μL of washing solution, and then blocked with blocking solution for 1 h. The TB1 and TB2 were diluted to a concentration of 50 nmol/L using a hybridization solution. After blocking, each well was washed three times, and 100 μL of the mutated antibody solution was added to allow complete binding with the antigen for 1 h. Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-human IgG, diluted 250 times in hybridization solution, was added to each well. After binding, the wells were washed three times with washing solution, and then 100 μL of the diluted enzyme-conjugated second antibody solution was added to each well. Following this, each well was washed three times with 200 μL of washing solution. Subsequently, 50 μL of the chemiluminescent substrate luminol was added to the 96-well plate, and the plate was placed in a microplate reader. Finally, 50 μL of the chemiluminescent substrate hydrogen peroxide was added for chemiluminescence detection.

Synthesis of NH2-DNA-mAb

The NH2-trigger powder was dissolved in ddH2O to a concentration of 1 mmol/L and divided equally into two tubes. The 1 mmol/L NH2-trigger was then diluted to 100 μmol/L using a hybridization solution. Succinimidyl 4-[N-maleimidomethyl] cyclohexane-1-carboxylate (SMCC) was diluted to 5 mmol/L using dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and 100 μmol/L NH2-trigger was mixed with 5 mmol/L SMCC in a 1:1 volume ratio. The mixture was oscillated at 25 °C, 666 rpm for 2 h. After the reaction, an amount of anhydrous ethanol twice the total reaction volume was added to the centrifuge tube and left at −20 °C for 20 min. The centrifuge tube was then placed in a centrifuge and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm, 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and an amount of 75% ethanol equal to the total reaction volume was added to the centrifuge tube. It was then centrifuged at high speed in a cold centrifuge at 12,000 rpm, 4 °C for 5 min, repeating this step three times. After discarding the supernatant, the precipitate in the centrifuge tube was dried to a white, dry state, resulting in solid NH2-DNA-SMCC. 100 μL of ddH2O was added, vortexed, and its absorbance at 260 nm was measured using Nanodrop 2000. TCEP was diluted to 10 mM with ddH2O, and 10 μL of mutated antibody was added to 6.1 μL of the diluted TCEP. This was placed in an oscillator at 666 rpm, 20 °C for 3 h. After oscillation, the pre-synthesized NH2-DNA-SMCC was added, thoroughly mixed, and left to react for another 6 h.

Synthesis of HS-DNA-mAb

The HS-trigger powder was dissolved in ddH2O to a concentration of 1 mmol/L and then mixed with 500 mmol/L TCEP. This mixture was placed in a dry heat constant temperature oscillator at 20 °C for 2 h to undergo reduction, with a DNA:TCEP ratio of 1:50. The reduced solution was then added to PBS to prepare a 100 μmol/L reaction solution. SMCC was diluted to 4 mmol/L using DMSO and mixed with the HS-trigger reaction solution at a 1:1 ratio by volume. The mixture was oscillated in a dry heat constant temperature oscillator at 20 °C and 666 rpm for 3 h. Next, 800 μL of anhydrous ethanol was added, and the solution was left at −20 °C for 20 min. The centrifuge tube was then placed in a high-speed refrigerated centrifuge at 12,000 rpm, 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and 400 μL of 75% ethanol was added. The tube was centrifuged at high speed in a cold centrifuge at 12,000 rpm, 4 °C for 5 min, repeating this step three times to remove excess SMCC. The resulting white precipitate was the synthesized HS-DNA-SMCC, which was dried to a white, dry solid in a fume hood. After adding 100 μL of ddH2O and vortexing, its absorbance at 260 nm was measured using a nanodrop 2000 to calculate the concentration of the HS-DNA-SMCC. Subsequently, 10 μL of the antibody to be coupled with nucleic acid was mixed with the previously synthesized HS-DNA-SMCC and left to react in a dry heat constant temperature oscillator at 666 rpm, 20 °C for 6 h, yielding the product HS-DNA-mAb.

ANCLISA detection system

In a 96-well plate, 100 μL of the capture antibody solution was added and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Next, the wells were washed three times with 200 μL of washing solution, and then 150 μL of blocking solution was added at 37 °C with agitation at 100 rpm for 1 h. Following blocking, the wells were washed three times. The RBD protein was diluted to a series of concentration gradients using the hybridization solution, and 100 μL was added to each well for 1 h incubation. After that, the wells were washed three times with washing solution, and then 100 μL of the reporter antibody solution, containing the conjugated nucleic acid, was added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by another washing process. The HCR probe stock solution H1-B and H2 were mixed, then appropriately diluted with dilution solution. After washing the wells three times, the HCR probe solution was added and allowed to react for 1 h. After three washes, SA-HRP was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Following another three washes with washing solution, 50 μL of the luminol solution was added to each well, and chemiluminescence was measured by adding 50 μL of hydrogen peroxide in a microplate reader.

Results and discussion

Working principle of the ANCLISA assay

To achieve efficient detection of target RBD-hFc, the development of this assay was divided into two steps: 1) AI-based antibody screening. 2) A sandwich-type immunoreaction based on antibody‒nucleic acid conjugation and HCR reaction. In the application of several AI methods for antibody screening, Alphafold245 was utilized to predict the binding structure of the antigen‒antibody complex, the GeoPPI 46 was employed to forecast the impact of different sites on antibody binding capabilities, and the ΔΔG predictor47 was used to assess the binding free energy of the antigen‒antibody complex. The ANCLISA detection system was shown in Scheme 1. The capture antibodies were initially immobilized on microplates through physical adsorption, the selected reporter antibodies could undergo a covalent reaction with linker SMCC, enabling the conjugation of functionalized DNA with the antibodies. In the presence of RBD-hFc, a sandwich immune complex forms between the capture antibody-coated microplate and DNA-mAb. Accompanying the immune complex, the functionalized DNA on the DNA-mAb can serve as a trigger sequence, initiating the HCR reaction upon the addition of HCR hairpin probes H1-B and H2. The formation of elongated HCR products was further confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis, where trigger-dependent generation of multiple higher-molecular-weight bands was observed (Supporting Information Fig. S9). As a result of biotin modifications on the H1-B, the ultimate product of the HCR will incorporate multiple biotins within the long-chain ssDNA. Library construction and screen

To identify high-affinity antibodies targeting the SARS-CoV-2 Delta variant RBD, BALB/c mice were immunized with recombinant Delta RBD-hFc protein. Serum titers from immunized mice were monitored, and spleen-derived lymphocytes from the highest-titer mouse (BALB/c 2) were used to construct a phage display antibody library (Supporting Information Fig. S10A). Three rounds of biopanning yielded two candidate antibodies, TB1 and TB2, which exhibited binding to both wild-type (WT) and Delta RBD in ELISA (Fig. S10B and S10C). To expand variant recognition coverage, we incorporated S309, a previously reported broad-spectrum antibody developed by GSK48 that neutralizes SARS-CoV-2 variants including Delta and Omicron sublineages (Fig. S10D).

Antibody selection based on AI and practical experiments

Alphafold245 developed by the DeepMind team, not only showcases the utmost precision in predicting protein structures but also furnishes a predictive model for protein‒protein complexes, thereby addressing the deficiency of available complex crystal structures in the PDB database. To validate the binding capability of TB1 and TB2 to Delta RBD, AlphaFold2 was utilized to simulate their complex structures with RBD from Delta (GISAID: EPI_ISL_1534938), Omicron JN.1 (GISAID: EPI_ISL_18518369), and Wild Type (GenBank: BCN86353.1). As depicted in Fig. 1A, TB1 and TB2 have been demonstrated to possess the capability to bind to the RBD protein of the Delta virus. The RBD of SARS-CoV-2 that mediates viral entry by binding with ACE2 also serves as the primary target for the majority of neutralizing antibodies. Antibody-mediated neutralization could be impaired by the RBD variants of SARS-CoV-249,50. To validate the recognition capability of TB1 and TB2 towards other variants, we selected two prevalent American variant strains, Omicron JN.1 (GISAID: EPI_ISL_185183 69) and Wild Type (GenBank: BCN86353). The GeoPPI46 has demonstrated outstanding performance in predicting the changes in binding affinity for both single-point and multi-point mutations across six benchmark datasets. Especially, it has been shown to accurately estimate the differences in binding affinities between SARS-CoV-2 antibodies and the receptor-binding domain of the S protein. As Fig. 1B shown, among the 11 single-point mutations from TB2 to TB1, the majority of single amino acid mutations enhanced the binding capability to the RBD protein of different variants.

To further screen the affinity between antibodies and Delta RBD, a deep learning model ΔΔG predictor47 was calculated to be used to predict the binding free energy of the complexes. In comparison to TB2, the ΔΔG predictor forecasted a lower binding free energy for TB1 and the Delta RBD. The ΔΔG was −1.97 kcal/mol, indicating a stronger binding capability between TB1 and Delta RBD. For the RBD of Wild-Type and Omicron JN.1, the ΔΔG values were −3.21 and −1.53 kcal/mol, respectively. Above the results of AI, TB1 exhibits a stronger binding capacity to the antigen compared to TB2. The actual affinity in practical experiments had been tested by the following experiments. Firstly, by using HRP-labeled Goat Anti-Human IgG (H + L), the binding capacity of TB1 and TB2 was validated in a non-specific approach. As shown in Fig. 1C, TB1 was stronger binder for Omicron JN.1, which was consistent with the calculated results. From this perspective, TB1 could be a broad-spectral antibody. This demonstrated the power of AI models in predictive binding ability and screening antibody.

Next, the affinity in practical experiments of the antibodies to antigen and its pairing capability with S309 was validated. S309 was conjugated to nucleic acids and utilized as the reporter antibody, while blank control (antigen adsorbed on the plate directly), TB1, and TB2 were used as capture antibodies respectively. As depicted in Fig. 1D, TB1 outperformed TB2 and the blank group noticeably, which further corroborated the AI simulation results of GeoPPI. In summary, TB1 demonstrated superior performance, and the combination of TB1 and S309 was utilized for subsequent detection experiments.

While the AI-assisted antibody selection strategy presented in this study shows promising results, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of the computational models used. First, the models are trained on available experimental data, which may introduce bias if the data does not adequately represent all SARS-CoV-2 variants, particularly those with rare mutations. This could affect the generalizability of the predictions to new variants. Additionally, although the models have been applied to a range of variants, they may not perform as accurately for future variants that differ significantly from those in the training data. As the virus continues to evolve, it is crucial to continually update the models with new variants and experimental data. Furthermore, while AlphaFold2 and ΔΔG provide powerful tools for structure prediction and affinity estimation, their accuracy is limited by the quality of the input data and may be less reliable for regions with insufficient experimental data. To address these limitations, future work will focus on incorporating more diverse data, updating the models with emerging variants, and improving the robustness of the predictions through experimental validation.

Antibody‒nucleic acid conjugation method selection

Monoclonal antibodies contain a significant number of amino groups and disulfide bonds that can be reduced to thiol groups. SMCC possesses an NHS ester at one end and a maleimide group at the other, enabling the conjugation of compounds containing thiol and amino groups. By modifying one end of the nucleic acid with amino or thiol groups, nucleic acid can be conjugated to the monoclonal antibody via SMCC, which are named NH2-DNA-mAb and HS-DNA-mAb. The efficiency of the two conjugation methods was validated by 10% SDS-PAGE. Lane 1 represented mAb, lane 2 represented NH2-DNA-mAb, and lane 3 represented HS-DNA-mAb. As shown in Fig. 2A, lane 2 exhibited two new bands above the heavy chain band comparison to lane 1, indicating successful conjugation with the nucleic acid. In lane 3, new bands were observed above both the heavy and light chain bands. Optimization of experimental parameters for ANCLISA

Several experimental parameters, such as conjugation of functionalized DNA, the concentration of HS-DNA-TB1, the concentration of HCR probes, the concentration of S309 and the concentration of SA-HRP were known to influence the efficiency of detection. The nucleic acids and antibodies obtain conjugated through the thiol groups, which have an impact on antibody's activity. Thus, conjugation of functionalized DNA with the antibodies, HS-DNA-TB1 and HS-DNA-S309, were synthesized as reporting antibodies. As Fig. 3A depicted, employing TB1 as the reporting antibody resulted in a significantly higher ΔCL compared to the other groups, suggesting that the nucleic acid conjugation process has a minimal impact on the activity of TB1. S309, as the capture antibody, represents the initial pivotal component of the entire reaction system. A low concentration of the capture antibody results in a reduced capture of antigens, affecting the detection sensitivity. Conversely, an excessive concentration of the capture antibody leads to surface saturation in the wells, causing unnecessary waste. As depicted in Fig. 3B, with an increase in the concentration of the capture antibody, the CL intensity gradually rose. When the concentration of S309 reached 50 nmol/L, the capture antibody on the 96-well plate became saturated. A concentration of 50 nmol/L S309 was selected for following experiments. HS-DNA-TB1 serves both as report antibody linked to the antigen and as the trigger for the HCR reaction. As shown in Fig. 3C, the peak value of SNR was acquired at 30 nmol/L, therefore, 30 nmol/L was chosen as the concentration of HS-DNA-TB1.

As shown in Fig. 3D, we estimated the effect of the concentration of HCR probes on the detection efficiency using 5 different concentrations (30, 50, 100, 200 and 400 nmol/L). 200 nmol/L was selected as an optimal concentration. As depicted in Fig. 3E the CL intensity showed an increasement as the concentration of SA-HRP increased. The dilution ratio for SA-HRP was chosen as 5000 times with the maximum SNR.

Analytical performance of ANCLISA

Under the optimal experimental conditions, the RBD protein were diluted into a series of concentration gradients and measured with CL intensity (the peak value derived from the chemiluminescence kinetic curve). As shown in Fig. 4B, the CL signal increased with the increasing protein concentration. The net chemiluminescence intensity (ΔCL) was obtained by subtracting the maximum CL intensity of different protein concentrations from the maximum intensity of the blank group. A linear relationship was observed between ΔCL and the logarithm of RBD protein concentration (LogC) (Fig. 4A). Within the range of 11 fg/mL‒11 ng/mL, the linear regression fit equation is ΔCL = 23,358 × LgC – 23,187 with a correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.9829. The limit of detection (LOD) was calculated to be 1.48 fg/mL (n = 3), demonstrating the high sensitivity of the assay (defined as the slope of the calibration curve). The superior performance of ANCLISA originates from the synergistic integration of AI-assisted high-affinity antibody selection and enzyme-free in situ signal amplification achieved by HCR. This distinguishes it from ELISA, which relies on chemical luminescence with limited amplification potential, and RCA that requires nuclease involvement—introducing operational complexity and stricter reaction condition constraints. The selectivity of ANCLISA was investigated with various interfering proteins, including programmed cell death 1 ligand 1 (PD-L1), streptavidin (SA), and cardiac troponin I (cTnI). As Fig. 4C illustrated, we can find an obvious relative detection signal in the presence of RBD protein solution (50 ng/mL), besides negligible signal changes obtained in the solution containing PD-L1, SA or cTnI (the concentration of the interfering protein was 500 ng/mL). This demonstrated the excellent selectivity of ANCLISA for RBD protein.

The reproducibility of ANCLISA was demonstrated by testing 3 different concentrations (100 pmol/L, 1 nmol/L and 10 nmol/L) of RBD protein independently 3 times. The relative standard deviations (RSD) were 0.83%, 1.64%, and 1.38% (Fig. 4D), respectively, proving an excellent reproducibility of ANCLISA.

It should be acknowledged that the current study has a limitation regarding sample validation. The analytical performance of the ANCLISA platform was validated using RBD antigen spiked into healthy human serum rather than clinical patient specimens. This was due to restricted access to clinical samples under the required biosafety containment conditions. Nevertheless, the assay demonstrated excellent selectivity against a panel of interfering proteins and showed robust performance in a complex serum matrix (Fig. 4C), suggesting a strong resilience to potential matrix effects. These results provide a solid proof-of-concept for the proposed method. Future studies will be essential to validate the performance of our ANCLISA platform with a statistically significant cohort of clinical samples to fully establish its practical diagnostic utility.

To assess the short-term storage stability of the HS-DNA-mAb conjugate, the conjugate was stored at 4 °C and used to detect 1 nmol/L Delta RBD on Day 0, Day 3, and Day 6 (Supporting Information Table S3). The SNR values remained within the same order of magnitude over 6 days, with only a slight decrease observed on Day 6, indicating that the HS-DNA-mAb maintained acceptable activity under these storage conditions. These results demonstrate that the antibody–DNA conjugate can be prepared in advance and stored at 4 °C for at least several days without significant loss of performance.

Conclusions

In this work, we established an integrated analytical platform for detecting RBD of SARS-CoV-2 Delta variant by combining AI-assisted antibody selection with nucleic acid signal amplification. The target antigen was immunized with mice, constructed a phage display antibody library, and screened for high-affinity antibodies. AI techniques were employed for the specific antibody selection, such as simulating the antigen–antibody binding structure and predicting the binding free energy of the antigen-antibody complex. The optimal antibodies were functionalized with HCR triggers via standard SMCC-mediated conjugation, thereby enabling the HCR signal amplification within the system. The ANCLISA we established demonstrated excellent analytical performance, with a LOD of 1.48 fg/mL This method, characterized by simple and convenient operation, low environmental requirements, and high sensitivity, holds broad utility value in the diagnosis of COVID-19.
