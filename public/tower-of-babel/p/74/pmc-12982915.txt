LDL Cholesterol Modulates Astrocyte Metabolism, Lipid Handling, and Morphology: Evidence From In Vitro and In Vivo Models
Authors: Gabriela Joras Baumart, Matheus Scarpatto Rodrigues, Juliete Nathali Sholl, Arieli Cruz de Sousa, Augusto Ferreira Weber, Lílian Corrêa Costa‐Beber, Hémelin Resende Farias, Mariana Viana Costa, Ariadni Mesquita Peres, Pedro Rocha de Camargo, Fernanda Telles Fróes, Rachel Krolow Bast, Andreza Fabro de Bem, Fabrício Figueiró, Fátima T C R Guma, Jade de Oliveira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAstrocytes are the primary antioxidant defense cells of the brain, protecting the central nervous system (CNS) through a controlled inflammatory response and acting as metabolic suppliers to neurons. These cells exhibit morphological, functional, and molecular changes in pathological conditions, such as neurodegenerative diseases. Previous studies have demonstrated a link between hypercholesterolemia, especially elevated levels of low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, and brain disorders, including hippocampal astrogliosis. In this context, this study aimed to investigate how LDL cholesterol modulates astrocyte biology. In vitro, high‐passage rat C6 astroglial cells were exposed to human LDL cholesterol (50 or 300 μg/mL) for 24 or 48 h. We evaluated lipid accumulation, cholesterol metabolism‐related gene expression, astrocyte‐related gene expression, reactive species production, antioxidant activity, redox‐related gene expression, fatty acid and glucose uptake, cell proliferation, and metabolic activity ‐ 3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) reduction. LDL exposure increased intracellular lipid content and downregulated LDL receptor (LDLR), 3‐hydroxy‐3‐methylglutaryl‐coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR), and sterol regulatory element‐binding transcription factor 1 (SREBF1) gene expression. LDL exposure altered astrocytic marker expression, as evidenced by increased glial fibrillary acid protein (GFAP) messenger RNA (mRNA) levels at 24 h with 300 μg/mL LDL and at 48 h with 50 μg/mL LDL. LDL cholesterol decreased long‐chain fatty acids (LCFA) uptake and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity at 24 h and increased cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36), also known as fatty acid translocase levels, at 48 h. Nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2 (NRF2) expression was significantly increased after 48 h of incubation with 300 μg/mL LDL. The MTT reduction assay did not indicate decreased cell viability; instead, it revealed increased metabolic activity after 24 h of incubation with 300 μg/mL LDL, with no changes observed in glucose uptake. In vivo, hippocampal astrocytes from young (3‐month‐old) and middle‐aged (14‐month‐old) LDL receptor knockout (LDLr−/−) and wild‐type C57BL/6 mice were analyzed by immunofluorescence and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR). In the hippocampal Cornu Ammonis 3 (CA3) region, 14‐month‐old LDLr−/− mice showed an increase in the number of processes compared to 3‐month‐old wild‐type C57BL/6 mice. Aging and genotype influenced astrocyte morphology and expression of genes such as S100 calcium‐binding protein B (S100B) and aquaporin‐4 (AQP4). Our findings demonstrate that LDL cholesterol induces morphological, metabolic, and molecular changes in astrocytes, both in vitro and in vivo, suggesting that astroglial cells are sensitive to lipid imbalance and may play a role in the brain consequences of hypercholesterolemia. Keywords: astrocytes, hypercholesterolemia, LDL cholesterol, LDLr−/− mice, lipid droplets, metabolism

Introduction

Hypercholesterolemia, defined by elevated plasma low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, is a well‐established risk factor not only for cardiovascular disease (Borén et al. 2022; Steinberg 2013; Tabas et al. 2015; Boffa and Koschinsky 2019), but also for cognitive decline and dementia in later life (Kivipelto et al. 2001; Kivipelto and Solomon 2006; Livingston et al. 2024; Sparks et al. 2000, 2003; Zambón et al. 2010). In fact, in 2024, high levels of blood LDL cholesterol were officially recognized as a strong midlife risk factor for dementia (Livingston et al. 2024). This link is supported by experimental models of hypercholesterolemia, which reveal that early pathological events such as neuroinflammation and blood–brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction precede brain impairment (de Oliveira et al. 2014; de Oliveira, Engel, de Paula, Dos Santos, et al. 2020; de Oliveira, Engel, de Paula, Melo, et al. 2020; Moreira et al. 2012; Paul and Borah 2017; Rodrigues et al. 2021, 2024; Streit and Sparks 1997; Ullrich et al. 2010). A compromised BBB is particularly critical, as it may permit the entry of peripheral molecules like LDL into the central nervous system (CNS), a compartment from which they are normally excluded. Indeed, apolipoprotein B (ApoB), the primary protein component of LDL, has been detected in the brain parenchyma of hypercholesterolemic animals, suggesting that LDL influx is a key pathological consequence of BBB breakdown (Chen et al. 2010; Löffler et al. 2013).

Astrocytes are a highly heterogeneous and widely distributed glial cell population that plays a critical role in maintaining brain homeostasis and responding to pathological insults in the CNS. As integral components of the neurovascular unit, astrocytes regulate BBB integrity, modulate cerebral blood flow, and provide essential metabolic and trophic support to neurons (Bélanger et al. 2011; Bonvento and Bolaños 2021; Herculano‐Houzel and Dos Santos 2018; Patani et al. 2023; Pellerin and Magistretti 1994; Zimmer et al. 2017). Owing to their intimate association with cerebral vasculature, astrocytes are among the first cells to encounter circulating factors that traverse a compromised BBB (Patabendige and Janigro 2023). Consistent with this role, astrogliosis, classically characterized by increased glial fibrillary acid protein (GFAP) immunoreactivity, is a prominent feature of hypercholesterolemic animal models and frequently co‐localizes with regions of BBB disruption (Ramírez et al. 2011; de Oliveira et al. 2014; de Oliveira, Engel, de Paula, Dos Santos, et al. 2020).

In this context, in vitro studies have demonstrated that exposure of cultured astrocytes to lipid‐derived species induces multiple cellular alterations. For example, treatment with lipolysis products has been shown to increase lipid droplet (LD) accumulation, upregulate inflammation‐related gene expression, elevate cellular stress responses, and enhance cytokine release (Liu et al. 2015; Lee et al. 2017). Emerging evidence further suggests that LDs may act as indirect markers of oxidative stress, as oxidized lipids are transferred from neurons to astrocytes through neuron–astrocyte metabolic coupling mechanisms (Liu and Zhang 2014; Smolič et al. 2021). In parallel, studies examining the effects of native (non‐oxidized) and oxidized LDL (ox‐LDL) on brain cells have reported that LDL exposure in primary cortical neurons and neuronal cell lines disrupts endolysosomal function, increases amyloid‐β production, reduces synaptophysin levels, and induces mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress (Engel et al. 2016; Hui et al. 2012; Yamchuen et al. 2017; Farias et al. 2025). Moreover, incubation with LDL has been shown to alter astrocyte morphology, although the specific consequences and mechanisms by which LDL cholesterol affects astrocytes remain incompletely understood (Dehouck et al. 1994; Engel et al. 2019; Pitas et al. 1987).

Therefore, BBB dysfunction may permit the entry of LDL cholesterol into the CNS under peripheral inflammatory conditions, such as hypercholesterolemia and metabolic disease. Once within the CNS, LDL cholesterol can exert neurotoxic effects on neurons and disrupt glial function. In this study, we investigate the impact of LDL cholesterol on astrocytes using complementary in vitro and in vivo approaches, focusing on a cell type that is critical for maintaining brain homeostasis and whose dysfunction may contribute to the initiation and progression of neuropathological processes. We hypothesize that astrocytes are highly sensitive to LDL, and that their response involves a cascade of metabolic and structural changes that contribute to the pathological brain environment associated with hypercholesterolemia and contributes to dementia development.

Materials and Methods

Chemical Reagents

Bovine serum albumin (BSA) (INLAB), culture media Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM)—low glucose (SIGMA), 2′‐7′‐dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH‐DA) (SIGMA), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (DINÂMICA), disodium hydrogen phosphate dihydrate (Na2HPO4) (NEON), ethylene diamine tetra acetic acid (EDTA) (MERCK), fetal bovine serum (FBS) (CRIPION BIOTECNOLOGIA LTDA), Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (SIGMA), glucose (NEON), 4‐(2‐hydroxyethyl)‐1‐piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) (NEON), potassium chloride (KCl) (NEON), potassium bromide (KBr) (NEON), sodium chloride (NaCl) (NEON), sodium hydrogen carbonate (NaHCO3), potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KH2PO4) (NEON), magnesium chloride (MgCl2) (VETEC), calcium chloride (CaCl2) (NUCLEAR), 3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) (SIGMA), paraformaldehyde (PFA) (NEON), saccharose (NEON), sulforhodamine (SRB) (SIGMA), penicillin–streptomycin (SIGMA), gentamicin, and amphotericin B solution (BioReagent).

Animals and Experimental Design

C57BL/6 wild‐type and low‐density lipoprotein receptor (LDLr) knockout (LDLr−/−) mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME; RRID:IMSR_JAX:000664, RRID:IMSR_JAX:002207, respectively), and bred at the Federal University of Rio Grande do Sul (UFRGS, Porto Alegre, Brazil). The animals were maintained under a controlled temperature (23°C ± 1°C) and a 12 h light cycle (lights on at 7:00 a.m.), with free access to food and water.

In this study, we used male C57BL/6 wild‐type and LDLr−/− mice, aged 3 months and 14 months old, divided into four experimental groups: 3‐month‐old C57BL/6 wild‐type mice, 3‐month‐old LDLr−/− mice, 14‐month‐old C57BL/6 wild‐type mice, and 14‐month‐old LDLr−/− mice (3–5 animals per group). The sample size was determined based on previous studies from our research group in which a two factor animal Genotype × Age design was applied (Rodrigues et al. 2024; de Oliveira, Engel, de Paula, Melo, et al. 2020). Therefore, the study was initially designed to detect medium‐to‐large main effects (Cohen's f = 0.35‐0.40) with α = 0.05 and 1−β = 0.80, which indicated a target of approximately six animals per Genotype × Age condition. Due to practical constraints, the final sample size consisted of 3–5 animals per group. Post hoc evaluation of the achieved sensitivity indicated that pairwise comparisons with expected large effect sizes (Cohen's d = 0.8) corresponded to an average detectable power of approximately 0.16. Young animals typically weigh approximately 30–32 g, whereas middle‐aged animals weigh around 38–43 g. Between 3 and 5 animals are housed per cage (individually ventilated cages; floor area: 501 cm2/77.66 in2; height: 15 cm; floor dimensions: 30 cm × 16.7 cm). All animal procedures were performed following the local ethics committee and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health (Bethesda, MD, USA). Process numbers: 39781/2020 and 38693/2021. The predefined inclusion criteria required that all animals be in visibly good health, with uniform fur, no signs of fighting, and appropriate body weight. No exclusion criteria were applied beyond these general health parameters. No animals were excluded, as all met the established criteria and remained in good condition throughout the study. Accordingly, no replacements were necessary. No randomization was performed to allocate animals in the study.

Two experimental cohorts were used: the first (n = 3–4 animals/group) was submitted to intracardiac perfusion with 4% PFA, under previous deep anesthesia with a mixture of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) via intraperitoneally (i.p), and the brain was collected for immunofluorescence analysis to evaluate hippocampal astrocytes morphology. A second cohort of animals was utilized for the evaluation of the gene expression of astrocytic proteins via quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) (n = 5 animals per group). The animals were euthanized with an overdose of anesthetics (ketamine 150 mg/kg and xylazine 100 mg/kg, administered i.p), followed by exsanguination. Residual blood components may still be present in the brain tissue and influence the qPCR analyses. No formal calculations were conducted to determine the sample size for any of the animal procedures. The sample size for all animal procedures was determined based on prior studies from our research group investigating similar variables (de Oliveira et al. 2014; de Oliveira, Engel, de Paula, Dos Santos, et al. 2020).

Cell Culture and LDL Cholesterol Exposure

The high‐passage C6 rat cell line (Rattus norvegicus; RRID:CVCL_0194) was cultured in DMEM low glucose media containing 26.2 mM NaHCO3, 40 μ/mL penicillin‐streptomycin, 40 μg/mL gentamicin, 0.25 μg/mL fungizone (amphotericin B), 8.39 mM HEPES buffer, sodium salt, and supplemented with 5% FBS at 37°C and 5% CO2 using standard cell culture methods. The C6 cell line is not listed in the International Cell Line Authentication Committee (ICLAC) Register of Misidentified Cell Lines. It is important to note that the use of high‐passage C6 cells (> 100 passages), while common in resource‐limited research settings, presents inherent limitations. A lack of strict passage‐number control can lead to phenotypic drift and may reduce the extent to which findings can be extrapolated to primary astrocytes. To minimize this risk, in our study, the cells were used for a maximum of five passages after thawing. Authentication of the C6 line is carried out by the major international cell banks that distribute it, including ATCC, DSMZ, ECACC, BCRJ, KCB, CLS, and NCBI. According to the available records, the entry was created on April 4, 2012, with its most recent update on April 10, 2025. After reaching 90% confluence and being cultured for 2 to 3 days, adherent cells were seeded in 12‐, 24‐, and 96‐well plates at a density of 0.5 × 104 cells per well, and in 6‐well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. After plating, the cultures were allowed to reach semi‐confluence (24 h later), and then the cells were treated with two different concentrations of LDL cholesterol (50 and 300 μg/mL) or phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) ‐ control (Ctrl) for 24 or 48 h.

Isolation of LDL Cholesterol From Human Plasma

Human plasma was collected from donors at Nossa Senhora da Conceição Hospital. This project was approved by the Ethics Committee at UFRGS (Ethics Committee Approved Project Number 43754). The inclusion criteria consisted of healthy adult individuals without metabolic disorders, following an overnight fasting period. Exclusion criteria included individuals with preexisting medical conditions, pregnant women, and smokers. All criteria were assessed using a standardized questionnaire administered prior to sample collection. As previously reported, LDL cholesterol was isolated from the plasma using discontinuous density gradient ultracentrifugation (de Bem et al. 2008; Farias et al. 2025). To 35 mL of human plasma, 0.5% saccharose was added to prevent LDL aggregation, 1 mg/mL EDTA to prevent LDL oxidation, and 0.326 g/mL KBr to adjust the density. Then, 5 mL of the mixture was transferred to a centrifuge tube, and 5 mL of 0.85% NaCl solution was added to the plasma. Ultracentrifugation was performed at 191 000×g relative centrifugal force (RCF) for 2 h at 4°C in a Hitachi Himac CP80WX ultracentrifuge, RPS40T rotor. After ultracentrifugation, LDL cholesterol was collected from the yellow band in the center of the tube and dialyzed in 5 L of PBS (8 mM Na2HPO4, 1.4 mM KH2PO4, 2.6 mM KCl, 136 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) at RT for approximately 12 h. The concentration of LDL protein was measured by the Lowry method, and the concentration was calculated assuming a molecular weight of LDL ApoB‐100 of 500 kDa (Lowry et al. 1951). Following isolation, LDL cholesterol was stored at −20°C, protected from light, and used promptly, without exceeding 30 days of storage.

Measurement of Intracellular Lipid Accumulation in the C6 Cell Line

The level of neutral lipid accumulation in the C6 cell line was measured after cholesterol exposure using the AdipoRed reagent and flow cytometry assay (AdipoRed Lonza Walkersville; Cat# PT‐7009) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The cells were plated in 12‐well (3n) and exposed to LDL for 24 or 48 h. After LDL cholesterol exposure, these cells were trypsinized and centrifuged at 750 RCF for 10 min. The cells were then incubated with the AdipoRed reagent (195 μL AdipoRed to 7605 μL calcium‐ and magnesium‐free saline solution [CMF]; 1:40) for 15 min, centrifuged, and resuspended in PBS. All samples were analyzed in the FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and the data acquired (10.000 events) were examined using FlowJo v10 software.

AdipoRed and 4′,6‐Diamidino‐2‐Phenylindole (DAPI) Staining for Qualitative Images

C6 astroglial cells were stained for confocal microscopy with AdipoRed (40× dilution; AdipoRed Lonza Walkersville; Cat# PT‐7009), where green indicates staining of neutral lipid‐containing LDs, and the nuclear marker DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#D1306, RRID:AB_2629482, 1:1000). The cells were incubated with PBS (Ctrl) and 300 μg/mL LDL cholesterol. After 24 and 48 h of exposure, the culture media were discarded, and the cells were washed four times with PBS at RT. Briefly, astrocytes were fixed with 4% PFA in PBS pH 7.4 for 15 min, and the cells were washed four times with PBS and incubated with AdipoRed diluted in PBS for 15 min. The nuclei were stained with DAPI, and the cells were observed using an OlympusFV1000 confocal microscope. Qualitative images were acquired with an OlympusFV1000 laser‐scanning confocal microscope using a plan apochromatic oil immersion objective ×40 (Olympus, U plan‐apochromatic, UPLSAPO40X) and the laser excitation at 488 nm and diode laser excitation at 405 nm. Channels were merged using Fiji‐ImageJ software for Mac OS X.

LysoTracker Red DND‐99 Assay

LysoTracker Red DND‐99 (LYRS) (Invitrogen; Cat# L7528), a fluorescent acidotropic probe, was used to detect and label lysosomes in viable cells. LYRS is a marker often employed for labeling and tracking acidic organelles in live cells. After LDL cholesterol exposure, 24‐well‐plated cells (3n) were trypsinized and centrifuged at 1000 RCF for 10 min at RT. The pellet was resuspended with LysoTracker for incubation (30 min) (Ilha et al. 2019). Finally, the cells were centrifuged and resuspended in PBS. All samples were analyzed using the FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and the data obtained (10.000 events) were examined using FlowJo v10 software.

Cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36) Measurement

To measure CD36 protein levels, the cells were plated in 6‐well (3n) and exposed to LDL for 24 and 48 h. After LDL cholesterol incubation, cells were stained with anti‐CD36 (1:20, Invitrogen Anti‐CD36 Monoclonal, eBioscience, Cat# 25‐0369‐42) for 30 min on ice and then washed twice with PBS. Data were acquired on a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA) and analyzed using FlowJo v10 software (BD Biosciences, USA).

Fatty Acid Uptake Assay

The 4,4‐difluoro‐5,7‐dimethyl‐4‐bora‐3a,4a‐diaza‐s‐indacene‐3‐hexadecanoic acid (BODIPYFLC16) is a palmitate analog. C6 cells were plated in 6‐well (3n) and exposed to LDL for 24 and 48 h. After LDL cholesterol incubation, the cultures were preincubated in a fasting buffer (PBS 10% FBS) for 10 min to measure long‐chain fatty acids (LCFA) uptake. The cells were then incubated in PBS containing 2 μM BODIPY FL C16 (BODIPYFLC16; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# D3821) for 30 min at 37°C in a water bath. Data were acquired on a FACSCalibur (BD Biosciences, USA) flow cytometer, and data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (BD biosciences, USA). Fatty acid uptake was quantified by measuring the median fluorescence intensity (MFI).

2‐[N‐(7‐Nitrobenz‐2‐Oxa‐1,3‐Diazol‐4‐Yl)amino]‐2‐Deoxy‐D‐Glucose (2‐NBDG) Glucose Uptake Assay

2‐NBDG, a fluorescent d‐glucose analog, is commonly used to measure glucose uptake by flow cytometry (Zou et al. 2005). Cells were plated in 6‐well (3n) and exposed to LDL cholesterol. After LDL cholesterol incubation, the cells were preincubated in a fasting buffer (PBS 10% FBS) for 10 min to measure glucose uptake. The cells were then incubated in PBS containing 40 μM 2‐NBDG (Sigma‐Aldrich; Cat# 72987) for 20 min each, at 37°C in a water bath. Data were acquired on a FACSCalibur (BD Biosciences, USA) flow cytometer, and data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (BD biosciences, USA). Glucose uptake was quantified by measuring the MFI.

RT‐qPCR

Cells were plated in 6‐well (3n) and exposed to LDL cholesterol. After LDL cholesterol incubation, the C6 astroglial cells ( Rattus norvegicus ) and hippocampal tissue ( Mus musculus ) from young and middle‐aged C57BL/6 wild‐type and LDLr−/− mice were lysed and total ribonucleic acid (RNA) isolated with TRIzol (Invitrogen), and quantified using a BioDrop DUO+ spectrophotometer (Biochrom Ltd, Cambridge, UK). Total RNA (0.6 and 1 μg, respectively) was reverse transcribed using a High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (Applied Biosystems, an affiliate of Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Primers were synthesized by Exxtend (São Paulo, SP, Brazil). The specific forward and reverse oligonucleotides were described in Table S1. RT‐qPCR was performed using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystem); the cycling conditions were 50°C for 2 min, 95°C for 2 min, and 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 15 s, and 72°C for 1 min. The relative expression levels of the genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT.

Metabolic Activity Assay by Analysis of Mitochondrial Dehydrogenase Activity

Cellular metabolic activity was analyzed using a MTT reduction assay. After 24 and 48 h of LDL cholesterol exposure, cells were incubated with 0.5 mg/mL MTT (Sigma‐Aldrich) dye for 3 h at 37°C, 5% CO2, and experiments were performed in 96‐well plates (3n). This assay evaluates the labeling activity of mitochondrial dehydrogenases such as succinate dehydrogenase by reducing MTT (yellow) to a purple formazan salt (Ghasemi et al. 2021). The intensity of formazan was measured using a SpectraMax M5 spectrophotometer from Molecular Devices. This assay was performed according to the protocol described by Mosmann (1983).

Measurement of Intracellular Reactive Species Levels

Reactive species (oxygen and nitrogen species) were measured by the non‐fluorescent cell‐permeating compound DCFH‐DA oxidation. After exposure of cells to LDL cholesterol (96‐well plates, 3n), the cells were incubated in the dark with 10 μM DCFH‐DA (Sigma‐Aldrich) for 20 min at 37°C and 5% CO2. Fluorescence was measured using a SpectraMax M5 UV/Vis spectrophotometer from Molecular Devices with excitation at 488 nm and emission at 525 nm (Colle et al. 2016; Hempel et al. 1999). In addition, 2′,7′‐dichlorofluorescein (DCF) fluorescence kinetics (10 μM) was measured over 60 min. Time 0 represents basal DCF fluorescence.

Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) Activity

After incubation, cells were lysed and homogenized with a 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer containing 140 mM KCl on ice and at pH 7.4. SOD activity was measured spectrophotometrically using the RANSOD kit (RANDOX; Cat# SD125) at 37°C. SOD activity is measured by the degree of inhibition of this reaction (Delmas‐Beauvieux et al. 1995). Kinetics were measured using a SpectraMax M5 UV/Vis spectrophotometer from Molecular Devices at 𝜆 = 492 nm, time = 4 min (0:26 s), reads = 10. The results are expressed as U/mg protein.

SRB Colorimetric Assay

SRB is a purple dye that binds to basic amino acids in cellular proteins under acidic conditions, and its binding is used to measure cell density, survival, and proliferation by correlating dye extraction with total protein mass (Skehan et al. 1990; Vichai and Kirtikara 2006). In 96‐well plates (3n), cells were covered with 75 μL SRB (Sigma‐Aldrich) (0.025 g SRB in 25 mL acetic acid) and incubated for 1 h. After incubation, the SRB was removed, and the wells were washed with 200 μL water to remove any excess. Then, 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) was added, and the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 560 nm using the SpectraMax M5 UV/Vis spectrophotometer from Molecular Devices.

Ki‐67 Cell Proliferation Assay

The expression of Ki‐67 nuclear protein is closely associated with cell proliferation, which explains its use as a proliferation marker. Ki‐67 staining is expressed during all active phases of the cell cycle G(1), S, G(2), and mitosis, suggesting that Ki‐67 protein expression is an absolute requirement for progression through the cell division cycle but is absent in resting or quiescent cells G(0) (Scholzen and Gerdes 2000; Sun and Kaufman 2018). C6 astroglial cells were plated in 6‐well plates (3n), and after 24 h of exposure to LDL cholesterol, the cells were trypsinized. Briefly, the cells were suspended in BD Cytofix/Cytoperm solution and incubated on ice for 20 min. The cells were then washed with BD Perm/WashTM buffer (1×) and incubated with anti‐Ki‐67 (clone SolA15, 1:200; eBioscience, Cat# 11‐5698‐82) for 30 min on ice and acquired on a flow cytometry (FACSCalibur, BD Biosciences, USA). Data were collected (10.000 events) and analyzed using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences, USA).

Annexin V‐FITC (Fluorescein Isothiocyanate)/Propidium Iodide (PI) Apoptosis Assay

The medium of cells after the incubation periods was transferred to microcentrifuge tubes together with cells that were first trypsinized. The tubes were centrifuged for 10 min at 1200 revolutions per min (RPM). The cells were then incubated for 15 min in the dark with 1× binding buffer, annexin V‐FITC (QuatroG Biotecnologia, Code. 100034; 200 μg/mL), and PI (QuatroG Biotecnologia, Code. 100034; 40 μg/mL). The samples were analyzed on a flow cytometry (FACSCalibur, BD Biosciences, USA) (10.000 events), and the results were analyzed using FlowJo v10 software (BD Biosciences, USA). Cells were classified as apoptotic cells (annexin V–FITC+/PI−) and late apoptotic (annexin V–FITC+/PI+)/necrotic cells (annexin V–FITC−/PI+), as previously described (Rieger et al. 2011; Vermes et al. 1995).

Protein Determination

The Lowry method was used to measure protein concentration BSA was used as the standard (Lowry et al. 1951).

Immunofluorescence Staining and Morphological Analysis

Coronal sections from the mouse hippocampus were stained for GFAP protein by immunofluorescence. The immunofluorescence protocol was performed as previously described, with minor modifications (de Oliveira et al. 2014; de Oliveira, Engel, de Paula, Dos Santos, et al. 2020). The mice were anesthetized with ketamine‐xylazine (K: 80 mg/kg; X: 10 mg/kg) administered i.p. The animals were then perfused through the left cardiac ventricle with 0.9% saline solution followed by 4% PFA prepared in 0.1 M PBS, pH 7.4. Brains were removed and immersed in 4% PFA solution at 4°C for 24 h, followed by cryopreservation in 30% sucrose solution in PBS at 4°C. Afterward, brains were frozen in isopentane at −80°C. Coronal 30 μm sections of the hippocampus were cut using a Microm HM 525 microtome cryostat (Thermo Scientific‐Germany) at −25°C and stored in 0.1 M PBS with 0.5% sodium azide at 4°C. The sections were first washed three times with 0.1 M PBS and blocked with 5% horse serum (HS) diluted in 0.1 M PBS containing 2% Triton ×100 (PBS‐Tx) for 2 h at RT. After this procedure, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibody mouse anti‐GFAP (Sigma‐Aldrich, G3893, RRID: P14136, 1:400) in 1% HS diluted in 0.5% PBS‐Tx. The sample sections were rinsed (3×) with 0.1 M PBS and incubated with fluorescent secondary antibody, anti‐mouse Alexa 488 (Invitrogen, A11001, RRID: AB_2534069, 1:400) in 1% HS diluted in 0.5% PBS‐Tx for 2 h at RT. Finally, the samples were washed three times with PBS and incubated with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#D1306, RRID: AB_2629482, 1:1000) for 5 min. After three more washes, the sections were mounted with CC/Mount (Tissue Mounting Medium)‐Sigma‐Aldrich Cat. n° C9368 and coverslipped. Briefly, the images were captured using an OlympusFV1000 laser‐scanning confocal microscope. Approximately, 12–15 confocal sections of 1 μm thickness parallel to the coverslip (xy sections) were taken with an ×40 objective (Olympus, U plan‐apochromatic, UPLSAPO40X) and 3× zoom on the ×40 objective (resolution of 1024 × 1024 px, field size of 317.3 × 317.3 μm, scanning speed of 4us/px, and pinhole aperture of 110 μm). Astrocyte morphology was then assessed using the Simple Neurite Tracer (SNT) plugin for Fiji‐ImageJ (RRID:SCR_002285; http://rsb.info.nih.gov/ij/). As a selection criterion, each GFAP analyzed included a single DAPI‐stained nucleus. Parameters such as total process length and total number of processes were used to assess changes in cell morphology between experimental groups. To evaluate astrocyte morphology complexity, we have also performed the Sholl Analysis. In this analysis, skeletonized astrocytes were used as outcomes to create concentric circles around the cell nucleus and measuring the number of times each astrocyte branch intersects each circle. In this study, the interval between concentric circles was set at 2 μm for all cells. The results were then presented in an XY graph, where the average number of intersections per group was plotted as a function of the distance from the cell nucleus (Sholl 1953; Ferreira et al. 2010). Approximately 15‐20 astrocytes were analyzed per animal.

Statistical Analysis

All data were verified using the Normal (Gaussian) distribution test, followed by the Shapiro–Wilk normality test to test the distribution. In the in vitro study, the dataset (n's) that passed the normality test received a one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's post hoc multiple comparisons, and n's that did not pass the normality test received Kruskal–Wallis test (nonparametric test) followed by Dunn's post hoc multiple comparisons. The in vivo data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were analyzed by two‐way ANOVA followed by using Duncan's post hoc test, without multiple comparison adjustments. For the in vitro experiments, the reported n (typically n = 3) corresponds to the number of independent biological experiments, where each replicate was performed on a different day or using a different cell passage. Each data point shown in the graphs represents one independent biological sample. Data are presented according to the graphical representation used. Dot plots show individual samples with horizontal lines or bars indicating the mean, and error bars representing the SD when applicable. Box plots depict the median, interquartile range (IQR), and minimum–maximum values. For the in vivo experiments, the small sample size (n = 3–5) is a limitation inherent to exploratory studies with genetically modified animal models, constrained by ethical and financial resources. Results were considered significant when the p‐value was less than 0.05, and were demonstrated by “*.” In addition, we considered a statistical tendency when p‐value ≤ 0.1. The statistical tendencies were demonstrated by “#”. When normalization was applied, the control group was used as the reference. In these cases, all values from the experimental groups were divided by the mean value of the control group, resulting in the control being expressed as 100%. Sholl analysis data were analyzed using the R statistical software (version 4.3.2, http://www.r‐project.org/). Outliers were defined, and then, excluded after analysis. All other statistical analyses and graphs were performed in GraphPad Prism 8 for MacOS (version 8.02) statistical software, and Statistic software package (Stat‐Soft Inc., Tulsa, OK, USA).

Lipid accumulation in the astrocyte cell line was assessed, as previous studies have indicated that exposure to ox‐LDL can induce the formation and accumulation of LDs (Ioannou et al. 2019). We used AdipoRed dye to evaluate the MFI using flow cytometry and image acquisition using a confocal fluorescence microscope (Figure 1a). After 24 or 48 h of LDL cholesterol incubation, lipid accumulation was observed in cells exposed to 300 μg/mL LDL (Figure 1b,c; Figure S1a,b). Incubation With LDL Cholesterol Does Not Alter Lysosomal Content in C6 Cell Line

Since lysosomes are cholesteryl ester‐degrading organelles and act as a process center for LDL‐derived cholesterol, we investigated whether LDL cholesterol affects lysosome content by evaluating LYRS‐stained cells and analyzing MFI by flow cytometry. There was no significant difference in LYRS in cells exposed to LDL cholesterol compared to the Ctrl at 24 h (F(2, 6) = 0.1155, p = 0.8929, Dunnett's post hoc test: Ctrl vs. 50 μg/mL: p = 0.9784; Ctrl vs. 300 μg/mL: p = 0.8538) and 48 h (F(2, 6) = 0.01915, p = 0.9811, Dunnett's post hoc test: Ctrl vs. 50 μg/mL: p = 0.9897; Ctrl vs. 300 μg/mL: p = 0.9958) (Figure 1d,e; Figure S1c), indicating that there was no increase in acidification in cells exposed to LDL cholesterol.

Discussion

Alterations in peripheral cholesterol homeostasis with damage to the BBB and alterations in CNS cholesterol metabolism appear to result in brain dysfunction. Sparks et al. (1995) showed that amyloid plaques were formed when rabbits were fed a hypercholesterolemic diet. After this research, several hypotheses about the relationship between high cholesterol levels and the onset of a pathology similar to Alzheimer's disease (AD) have been experimentally tested, ranging from vascular alterations, such as loss of BBB integrity, and inflammatory processes triggered by glial cells (de la Torre 2004; de Oliveira et al. 2014; de Oliveira, Engel, de Paula, Dos Santos, et al. 2020; Sparks et al. 2000; Streit and Sparks 1997; Ullrich et al. 2010) to altered lipid metabolism in the brain (Chew et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2021; Liu and Zhang 2014; Sun et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2023). In addition, in vitro studies have investigated the impact of excess cholesterol on CNS cells (Engel et al. 2016, 2019; Hui et al. 2012; Ioannou et al. 2019; Pitas et al. 1987; Shie et al. 2004). In this sense, we believe that the impairment of the BBB that can occur due to hypercholesterolemia may trigger the extravasation of LDL cholesterol into the brain parenchyma, which could harm cell homeostasis. In this work, we investigated the effects of LDL cholesterol on astrocytes.

Considering previous works, we chose the LDL cholesterol concentrations and exposure times (Engel et al. 2016, 2019; Hui et al. 2012; Smolič et al. 2021; Farias et al. 2025). In addition to cholesterol and cholesteryl esters, LDL particles also carry other lipids, including triglycerides and phospholipids. Therefore, we aimed to investigate whether the neutral lipid content of LDL, specifically triglycerides, can contribute to the formation of intracellular LDs. Importantly, astrocytes presented intracellular accumulation of lipid species after being challenged with LDL cholesterol. Several conditions predispose to the formation of LDs, such as inflammatory processes, mitochondrial defects, aging, and extracellular lipids, which coincidentally are conditions previously described in AD (Hu et al. 2017). Of particular importance, LDs have already been observed in neurons of the hippocampus and cortex of patients with AD (Gómez‐Ramos and Asunción Morán 2007). Next, we analyzed the positive LYRS+ by Lysotracker Red staining, which labels acidified compartments. We performed this labeling because Marschallinger et al. (2020) demonstrated that a novel, age‐associated, LD‐rich microglial cell, “LDAM,” exhibits an accumulation of dysfunctional lysosomal deposits, impaired phagocytosis, and release of pro‐inflammatory mediators. Furthermore, in cortical neurons incubated with apoB‐cholesterol, endolysosomal dysfunction was associated with increased deposition of amyloid‐beta (Aβ) and hyperphosphorylated tau (Hui et al. 2012). However, our findings revealed no significant differences between experimental conditions, suggesting that LDL exposure does not alter the number of acidified compartments in astrocytes. Further investigations are needed to evaluate additional lysosomal parameters, including organelle pH, size, subcellular localization, enzymatic activity, and the expression of hydrolases and classical lysosomal and endosomal markers. These analyses will help to better elucidate the impact of LDL on astrocyte function.

Moreover, as the concentration of LDL‐cholesterol increased, we observed a significant reduction in the gene expression of LDLR, HMGCR, and SREBF1. However, the functional implications of these changes, and whether they represent adaptive or pathological responses, remain to be fully elucidated. On one hand, the coordinated downregulation of LDLR and HMGCR is consistent with classical feedback inhibition, an expected adaptive response to increased intracellular cholesterol availability. This interpretation is supported by our findings showing substantial LD accumulation, which would naturally suppress further cholesterol uptake and endogenous synthesis. On the other hand, the simultaneous decrease in SREBF1, a master regulator of lipid metabolism, suggests a deeper disruption in lipid regulatory pathways. Sustained LDL exposure may impair the astrocytic regulatory machinery itself, potentially compromising critical functions such as cholesterol delivery to neurons, membrane remodeling, and metabolic adaptability (Shimano and Sato 2017). Importantly, an initially protective response may become maladaptive under chronic stress. For example, reduced LDLR expression may limit cholesterol overload but could also impair processes beyond lipid handling. Because LDLR contributes to Aβ clearance in the brain (Basak et al. 2012), its persistent downregulation could favor Aβ accumulation, a hallmark of neurodegenerative pathology. In this context, our observation that APOE mRNA expression also tended to decrease is noteworthy, although it contrasts with in vivo studies in which hypercholesterolemia increased ApoE protein levels in rabbit cortex (Sparks et al. 1995), underscoring key differences between simplified in vitro systems and complex in vivo environments. Recognizing these limitations, future studies employing pharmacological or genetic manipulation of these pathways will be essential to establish causality and to distinguish truly adaptive responses from emerging pathological consequences.

Given that, our next question was whether astrocytes once exposed to LDL cholesterol exhibit changes in CD36. CD36, a class B scavenger receptor also known as fatty acid translocase, has been studied in inflammatory conditions such as atherosclerosis, ischemic brain injury, and AD (Kim et al. 2008). The scavenger receptor family recognizes danger‐associated molecular patterns and expression of CD36 mediates cell‐specific responses such as inflammatory responses, phagocytosis of apoptotic cells, fatty acid transport across the BBB, and lipid uptake, leading to activation of PPARγ transcriptional pathways (Canton et al. 2013; Silverstein and Febbraio 2009; Ioghen et al. 2021). In astrocytes, the inflammatory mediator CD36 has been implicated as a novel mediator of injury‐induced astrogliosis and scar formation in stroke, with the hypothesis that CD36 is required for GFAP expression (Bao et al. 2012). In addition, LCFA and lipoproteins such as LDL and ox‐LDL are ligands for CD36 (Park 2014). In the atherogenic process, the CD36 receptor mediates the recognition and internalization of ox‐LDL in immune system cells (e.g., macrophages) and triggers signaling cascades for pro‐inflammatory responses (Bao et al. 2012; Park 2014). Exposure to 300 μg/mL LDL for 48 h promoted an increase in CD36 levels. This phenomenon can be explained by the fact that isolated native LDL cholesterol may contain fractions of ox‐LDL, and in the more extended incubation period, this fraction may be internalized.

Astrocytes are essential CNS cells for glucose uptake (Pellerin and Magistretti 1994; Pellerin 2018). Therefore, we also measured parameters related to glucose and energy metabolism. Our results showed that when cells were incubated with LDL cholesterol, the glucose analog (2‐NBDG) uptake was not significantly altered. However, there is a tendency for absorption to decrease after 48 h of exposure to 300 μg/mL LDL. This potential alteration in glucose uptake should be further investigated, as decreased glucose uptake may be related to the metabolic state of the cells, because this decrease should also reduce the production of lactate, an essential nutrient for neurons. We can speculate that higher concentrations of LDL cholesterol may interfere with glucose transport via glucose transporter type 1 (GLUT‐1) present in the membrane. After 24 h of exposure to 300 μg/mL LDL, astrocytes showed a tendency to reduce the expression levels of the SLC1A3 gene, and after 24 h of incubation with the 300 μg/mL of LDL, the expression of the SLC1A2 gene tended to increase. In this regard, recently, it was reported that a mouse model of brain oxysterol imbalance, especially the elevated levels of 27‐hydroxycholesterol, displays a downregulation of glutamate transporters in the hippocampus (Spanos et al. 2024). This deregulation tendency of SLC1A3 and alterations in SLC1A2 expression may indicate an impairment in glutamate reuptake, which could contribute to synaptic dysregulation in hypercholesterolemia.

Our findings indicate that LDL cholesterol exposure induces functional alterations in astrocytes that extend beyond disruptions in cholesterol homeostasis. Changes in glucose uptake tendencies and glutamate transporter expression suggest that LDL affects core astrocytic functions critical for neuronal support. These functional alterations are accompanied by molecular evidence of astrocytic activation, as reflected by increased GFAP mRNA expression in LDL‐treated C6 cells. Specifically, exposure to 300 μg/mL LDL increased GFAP gene expression at 24 h, whereas treatment with 50 μg/mL LDL led to a significant upregulation at 48 h, revealing a concentration‐ and time‐dependent response. This pattern is consistent with previous reports showing that deleterious stimuli induce GFAP expression in C6 cells (Shu et al. 2011; Chao et al. 2015), supporting the interpretation that LDL triggers a classical astrocytic stress response.

The MTT reduction assay was used to study cell metabolic activity. The MTT reagent can cross cell and mitochondrial membranes, and its intracellular reduction is mainly mediated by oxidoreductase, dehydrogenase, and electron donor enzymes (Ghasemi et al. 2021). At both exposure times, 300 μg/mL LDL cholesterol increased reagent reduction, although this effect reached statistical significance only after 24 h of exposure. The finding observed with 300 μg/mL LDL suggests that elevated LDL cholesterol levels may enhance metabolic activity in astrocytes. However, it is essential to highlight that the formazan formation reaction has many limitations because other species can reduce MTT, and it is a low‐specificity reagent that translocates to different cellular compartments (Ghasemi et al. 2021).

LDL cholesterol appears to elicit a transient oxidative stress response in astrocytes that is followed by activation of protective mechanisms. Cells incubated with LDL accumulate intracellular lipids that may favor β‐oxidation, a metabolic shift potentially linked to pro‐inflammatory states. Compared with neurons, astrocytes are relatively resistant to oxidative stress (Bélanger et al. 2011; Wilson 1997). LDL exposure promoted an immediate oxidative challenge, as evidenced by increased levels of ROS within the first 30 min. In vitro studies previously published by our group using endothelial cells (Hort et al. 2014) and macrophages (Straliotto et al. 2013) also demonstrated increased reactive species generation after 1 h of exposure to oxidized LDL. As ROS are generated rapidly and have a very short half‐life in biological systems, the early increase in ROS represents a transient kinetic peak rather than sustained accumulation. In the present study, after 24 h of exposure to LDL cholesterol, ROS production remained unchanged, whereas a significant decrease in SOD activity was observed. After 48 h, both SOD activity and ROS levels remained unchanged. SOD was selected as a representative antioxidant enzyme indicative of a complex and coordinated cellular antioxidant response, as cellular antioxidant defense relies on the integrated action of multiple enzymatic and nonenzymatic systems. Accordingly, we hypothesize that the reduction in SOD activity observed after 24 h of exposure may be related to an early oxidative stress event. We further propose that, at this time point, astrocyte cultures do not exhibit detectable increases in reactive species due to the action of other antioxidant mechanisms independent of SOD activity. After 48 h, the recovery of SOD activity suggests the reestablishment of its contribution to the cellular antioxidant response. As our experimental design compares groups within each time point, we cannot definitively distinguish between a true restoration of SOD activity in LDL‐treated astrocytes as part of a compensatory antioxidant response and/or time‐dependent changes in baseline SOD activity in the control group. Supporting this interpretation, we also observed an increase in NRF2 mRNA expression following LDL exposure, indicating activation of endogenous cellular stress‐response pathways. We propose that this temporal pattern reflects activation of the nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2‐antioxidant response element (Nrf2‐ARE) axis, which promotes the transcription of antioxidant enzymes and restores redox balance (Sigfridsson et al. 2018; He and Hewett 2025). We evaluated NRF2 gene expression at 24 h as a general marker of pathway engagement rather than as a precise temporal readout of early activation. We fully agree that detailed temporal analyses of NRF2 activation ‐ particularly at very early time points (≤ 1 h) ‐ require dedicated experimental protocols, including assessment of nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity, and remain an important direction for future studies. Increased SOD activity, in particular, would be effective in dismutating the superoxide radicals detected in our assays. The apparent discrepancy with our previous in vivo findings (de Oliveira et al. 2014), where LDLr−/− mice exhibited impaired glutathione peroxidase activity and altered glutathione (GSH) levels, actually supports this interpretation. In the complex in vivo environment, multiple cell types experience chronic hypercholesterolemia‐induced stress, creating sustained demand for antioxidant defenses that eventually become overwhelmed. In contrast, our controlled in vitro system allows astrocytes to mount an effective compensatory response through their intrinsic antioxidant machinery. This demonstrates that astrocytes possess robust protective capabilities that can respond to acute LDL exposure, but these same mechanisms become insufficient under chronic pathological conditions. These findings highlight the importance of considering both the temporal dynamics and the complexity of the cellular environment when interpreting oxidative stress responses in hypercholesterolemia. This interpretation is supported by reports that SH‐SH5Y incubated with 300 μg/mL LDL cholesterol for 1 and 24 h exhibited oxidative stress (Engel et al. 2016). In summary, astrocytes exhibit some resistance to oxidative stress. After a short incubation period with LDL cholesterol, there is the generation of ROS, which does not persist with longer incubation times, likely due to the action of antioxidant mechanisms. Our oxidative stress findings reveal a complex temporal pattern that provides important insights into astrocyte adaptive responses to LDL cholesterol exposure.

Exposure to LDL cholesterol alone does not appear to trigger proliferation in astrocyte cultures. In some situations, such as traumatic brain injury, where BBB dysfunction and plasma extravasation into the brain occurs, some astrocytes acquire a proliferative reactive phenotype (Alluri et al. 2015; Liddelow and Barres 2017). First, cell density was measured using the SRB staining method. After 24 h of LDL exposure, both exposures appeared to reduce the binding of the dye to basic amino acid residues; however, the differences were not statistically significant. Ki‐67 levels were then measured, demonstrating that LDL cholesterol did not alter the proliferative profile under these conditions. In our experimental model, exposure to LDL cholesterol did not alter the proliferation levels of the astrocyte culture, confirming other studies showing that the reactive astrocytic phenotype does not always promote proliferation (Liddelow and Barres 2017).

Our findings demonstrated that, following the longest incubation period with LDL, the astrocyte lineage did not exhibit significant changes in apoptosis or late apoptosis/necrosis. Therefore, LDL‐cholesterol at this concentration does not appear to affect cell death‐related parameters. A previous study evaluated cell death parameters in C6 cells after incubation with 27‐OHC and demonstrated that starting at a concentration of 10 μM, 27‐OHC induced early and late apoptosis in C6 cells (An et al. 2017). In LDLr−/− mice, an increase in neuronal apoptosis was observed in the CA3 region of the hippocampus, visualized by the co‐localization of caspase 3 (mediator of neuronal death) and NeuN (neuronal marker). However, caspase 3 and GFAP colocalization was not verified in these animals (de Oliveira, Engel, de Paula, Melo, et al. 2020).

Ideally, the concentration of LDL used in vitro should reflect the physiological levels present in the brains of humans or mice with hypercholesterolemia, which would substantially enhance experimental rigor. However, such values remain unknown, as LDL is not normally found in the CNS and its concentration under pathological conditions of BBB disruption has not yet been quantified. For this reason, the LDL concentration adopted in our study followed methodological precedent established by previous investigations examining LDL effects on CNS cells (Hui et al. 2012; Moreira et al. 2012; Engel et al. 2016, 2019; Farias et al. 2025). These studies provide the most biologically relevant reference range currently available for in vitro exposure. Nonetheless, the absence of direct measurements of LDL penetration into the hypercholesterolemic brain represents an important limitation. Future studies employing fluorescently or radioactively labeled LDL particles in LDLr−/− mice will be crucial to quantify the extent of LDL entry into the CNS during BBB breakdown. Such experiments will help establish physiologically relevant LDL concentrations for in vitro studies and improve translational accuracy in modeling hypercholesterolemia‐induced neurobiological changes.

The primary limitation of our in vitro study is the use of the high‐passage C6 glioma cell line, a transformed model that may not fully recapitulate the complex behavior of primary astrocytes. Nevertheless, the selection of this model was deliberate, driven by two key factors: First, its use aligns with the 3Rs principle (Reduction), enabling a comprehensive mechanistic analysis with a significant decrease in the number of animals required for the in vivo component. Second, higher passage C6 cells tend to display more astrocyte‐like characteristics, including functional responses that closely parallel those of primary astrocytes (Galland et al. 2019; Lee et al. 1992), thereby supporting their validity for studying cellular responses to LDL cholesterol. To mitigate the risk of phenotypic drift, we strictly limited the use of the cells to a maximum of five passages after thawing and confirmed that the line is not listed in the ICLAC Register of Misidentified Cell Lines. Beyond the model choice, the acute exposure paradigm used in our experiments may also contribute to the lack of statistical significance observed in some effects. Given that astrocytic responses to chronic lipid challenges may require longer periods to fully manifest, future studies will include extended incubation times to better characterize the long‐term effects of LDL on redox balance and metabolic function.

Our in vivo data demonstrate pronounced astrocytic morphological alterations in middle‐aged LDLr−/− mice, particularly within the hippocampal CA3 region. These changes are consistent with astrocytic hypertrophy and suggest a reactive phenotype associated with disrupted lipid metabolism (Owasil et al. 2020; Dai et al. 2023). Previous studies have reported increased astrocyte density in the hippocampus of LDLr−/− mice (de Oliveira et al. 2014; de Oliveira, Engel, de Paula, Dos Santos, et al. 2020) and have identified age‐dependent heterogeneity in astrocytic reactivity profiles (Clarke et al. 2018; Lee et al. 2022). Previous studies from our research group have demonstrated elevated GFAP immunoreactivity in young LDLr−/− mice compared with wild‐type controls (de Oliveira et al. 2014; de Oliveira, Engel, de Paula, Dos Santos, et al. 2020), indicating early biochemical signatures of astrocyte activation. In the present study, we extended these observations by moving beyond GFAP immunoreactivity to quantify astrocytic morphological complexity, using Sholl analysis, and parameters of astrocytic morphology. Importantly, we show that astrocytic morphology is altered by age and is region specific: While biochemical markers of activation emerge early, more pronounced structural alterations, such as increased process number, appear later and are preferentially localized to the CA3 hippocampal region. Together, these findings support a model in which astrocytic reactivity in FH represents a progressive event, evolving from early biochemical changes to more complex morphological remodeling with aging. However, these in vivo findings should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size. Furthermore, while these morphological changes are significant, their direct functional consequences for neuronal health and cognition remain to be established and require further investigation.

Also, studies involving LDLr−/− mice have shown that these animals exhibit impairments in the BBB (de Oliveira, Engel, de Paula, Dos Santos, et al. 2020; de Oliveira, Engel, de Paula, Melo, et al. 2020), and microgliosis that worsens with age (Rodrigues et al. 2024). In this investigation, two astrocyte markers, the glial water channel AQP4 and the Ca2+‐binding protein S100B, demonstrated alterations mainly due to aging. AQP4 is abundant in the endfeet of astrocytes that are in contact with cerebral vasculature and pia; this isoform of aquaporin, an integral transmembrane protein, is involved with water‐conduction and our levels of expression are associated with reactive gliosis (Owasil et al. 2020; Dai et al. 2023). Studies have indicated that the levels of expression of AQP4 increased in mouse models of AD and in humans with the disease, a situation that may favor the reported disturbances in water homeostasis and increased BBB permeability (Wilcock et al. 2009). We previously observed an increased immunoreactivity of this protein in the hippocampus of LDLr−/− mice (de Oliveira et al. 2014). In contrast, herein, we found a decreased gene expression of AQP4 in 3‐month‐old LDLr−/− mice. In addition, we also observed that both aged wild‐type and LDLr−/− mice present a reduction in the mRNA levels of AQP4 in the hippocampus. Then, these changes can generate functional consequences, such as causing an imbalance in the regulation of water flow and altering the permeability of the BBB. Expressed mainly in astrocytes, S100B is an essential cytoskeleton component (Barateiro et al. 2016). We observed increased S100B mRNA levels in the hippocampus of aged wild‐type mice. In aging, the S100B expression showed regional specificity; however, the functional consequences of these specificity alterations remained unknown (Rodríguez et al. 2014).

The selection of 3‐ and 14‐month‐old mice was strategically based on clinical relevance (Zambón et al. 2010) and our previous characterization of the LDLr−/− model (Moreira et al. 2012; de Oliveira, Engel, de Paula, Melo, et al. 2020; Rodrigues et al. 2024). Fourteen months represents middle age in mice (Dutta and Sengupta 2016), corresponding to a period of increased cognitive vulnerability in patients with FH, whereas 3 months reflects an early stage of pathology. Notably, our prior studies have shown that LDLr−/− mice already exhibit BBB dysfunction, neuroinflammation, astrogliosis, oxidative stress, and memory deficits at this young adult stage (de Oliveira et al. 2014; de Oliveira, Engel, de Paula, Dos Santos, et al. 2020; de Oliveira, Engel, de Paula, Melo, et al. 2020). By combining these two time points, the present study allows assessment of the temporal evolution of astrocytic responses to hypercholesterolemia, distinguishing early alterations from age‐dependent changes that may contribute to cognitive decline in middle‐aged FH.

Although previous studies have examined the effects of LDL on glial cells (Chen et al. 2024; Niu et al. 2025), the present work substantially advances the field by focusing specifically on astrocyte responses in the context of FH, a mechanism that remains insufficiently explored. By combining complementary in vitro and in vivo approaches, we provide one of the first systematic characterizations of astrocyte‐specific metabolic, oxidative, and functional adaptations to LDL overload. Our data demonstrate that elevated LDL cholesterol induces a coordinated astrocytic reprogramming, characterized by LD accumulation, downregulation of genes involved in cholesterol synthesis and uptake, upregulation of CD36 at higher concentrations and longer exposures, and the activation of a transient oxidative stress response marked by early ROS generation followed by compensatory antioxidant mechanisms. These metabolic alterations are accompanied by increased GFAP gene expression and changes in glutamate transporter expression, suggesting that LDL exposure impacts astrocytic reactivity and neurotransmitter homeostasis. Extending these findings in vivo, we show that hypercholesterolemia alters astrocytic morphology in an age‐ and region‐dependent manner, challenging the concept of uniform astrogliosis and highlighting the progressive nature of astrocytic dysfunction in aging hypercholesterolemic brains. Taken together, our findings identify astrocytes as key cellular mediators of cholesterol‐induced brain dysfunction and provide new mechanistic insight into how peripheral hypercholesterolemia can disrupt central nervous system homeostasis, with potential implications for neurodegenerative disease risk and progression.
