When skin color/race and education matter: disparities in obesity among Brazilians (2013–2019)
Authors: Luciana Alves de Medeiros, Letícia Martins Okada, Luciana Saraiva da Silva, Renata Bertazzi Levy, Catarina Machado Azeredo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the growing burden of obesity in middle-income settings, evidence integrating socioeconomic and racial dimensions remains insufficient. This study examined educational inequalities in obesity prevalence among Brazilian adults in 2013 and 2019, assessing intersections with race/skin color and sex.MethodsCross-sectional study with a representative sample of Brazilian adults aged ≥ 23 years, using data from the National Health Survey (PNS), 2013 and 2019 editions. Obesity was assessed using BMI, calculated from self-identified weight and height. Descriptive data and complex measures of inequality (Slope Index of Inequality – SII and Relative Index of Inequality – RII) were used. Analyses were stratified by sex, race/skin color, and educational level, with an intersectional approach when analytically possible.ResultsA difference in the magnitude of relative educational inequality was observed only for Brown women (RII = 0.5 in 2013 and 0.7 in 2019), largely reflecting greater increases in obesity prevalence among women with secondary and tertiary education. Among Black men, both absolute and relative inequality were higher in 2019 survey than in 2013, with a shift in higher obesity prevalence from the less educated to the more educated. In 2019, White and Brown women showed negative SII values (-15.0 and − 9.7, respectively) and RII values less than one (0.5 and 0.7, respectively), indicating a lower obesity prevalence among those with higher educational attainment, which was not significant for Black women. Among men, educational inequality was greater for Black and Brown individuals, with higher obesity prevalence among the more educated (SII = 9.9 and 8.5, respectively); among White men, there was no significant inequality.ConclusionEducational inequality in obesity prevalence in Brazil shows patterns consistent with the nutritional transition observed in high-income countries with higher obesity prevalences among less educated subgroups. However, racial intersections revealed significant differences, placing Black and Brown individuals at a disadvantage in both sexes. Among women, education appears to have a less protective effect against obesity among Black women, whereas among men—where a positive educational gradient in obesity is commonly observed—this pattern is more pronounced among Black and Brown men.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-026-02930-y.Keywords: Obesity, Inequality, Intersectionality, Education, Race/skin color

Background

Obesity is a complex health problem that, in recent decades, has reached pandemic proportions, with significant impacts on health, the economy, and society [1]. Recent estimates indicate that by 2035, more than 1.5 billion people worldwide will be obese [2]. Far from being merely a matter of individual responsibility, obesity results from a combination of biological, psychological, environmental, and social factors [1]. Once considered a disease typical of high-income countries, obesity is now increasing more rapidly in low- and middle-income countries [3].

Amid growing urbanization and profound changes in the global food system that promote excessive energy intake [4], studies show that obesity is not equally distributed among social groups [5, 6], and that its patterns differ according to the development level of each country [6].

In general, in high-income countries and in many middle-income countries, individuals from socioeconomically disadvantaged groups experience a higher obesity prevalence. This relationship varies by sex, being more evident among women [6–8], and by race/skin color [9–11]. In contrast, in low-income countries, there is a direct association between obesity prevalence and socioeconomic status (SES), meaning a higher prevalence among groups with higher SES [7]. Thus, understanding the impact of obesity across different social groups is not straightforward.

Brazil, a middle-income, multiracial, and highly unequal country, is experiencing a rapid increase in obesity prevalence, which more than doubled between 2002 and 2019—from 9.6% to 22.8% in men and from 14.5% to 30.2% in women [12]. Income inequalities and sex intersections have been well stablished. As in other developing countries, evidence shows that obesity initially increased across all social strata, especially among privileged groups. With socioeconomic development, it shifted to primarily affect poorer groups, with stability or decline observed only among the most privileged women [13–15].

On the other hand, studies exploring the interaction between SES and race/skin color in Brazil remain scarce, outdated, or limited in representativeness [11, 16]. Available evidence suggests that the relationship between these factors and obesity varies according to sex and socioeconomic position, with indications of greater vulnerability among Black and Brown individuals, particularly women, at higher socioeconomic levels [9, 11]. However, the intersection between race/skin color, gender, and SES in shaping obesity patterns has been insufficiently investigated from an intersectional perspective [17].

Therefore, this study aimed to examine educational inequalities in obesity prevalence among the Brazilian adult population in 2013 and 2019 and to assess how these inequalities intersect with race/skin color and sex, using summary measures of inequality.

Methods

This was a repeated cross-sectional study based on data from the 2013 and 2019 editions of the Brazilian National Health Survey (PNS), a nationwide population-based and nationally representative household survey conducted by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE) in partnership with the Ministry of Health. The PNS provides extensive information on the health status of the Brazilian population, alongside socioeconomic and demographic characteristics [18, 19].

A complex, multi-stage probabilistic sampling design was employed. In the first stage, census tracts were selected with probability proportional to size (number of private households). In the second and third stages, private households within these tracts and one individual from each selected household were chosen with equal probability. The target population comprised individuals aged 18 years and older in 2013 and 15 years and older in 2019, all residing in permanent private households. Regarding geographic coverage, the PNS is nationally representative, as well as representative for macro-regions, urban and rural areas, and state capitals. Detailed methods have been described in prior publications [18, 19].

Ethical approval for both PNS editions was granted by the Brazilian National Research Ethics Committee (CONEP), under protocol numbers 328.159 in 2013 and 3.529.376 in 2019.

For this study, only adults aged 23 years and older were included. This age threshold was adopted as an operational criterion, assuming that most individuals in Brazil have completed or are no longer enrolled in higher education by this age. According to OECD data, higher education in Brazil is typically attended between the ages of 18 and 22 [20]. Although variations exist — such as delayed entry, part-time attendance, or extended study duration — this cutoff minimizes the inclusion of individuals still engaged in higher education. Pregnant women (n = 600 in 2013; n = 591 in 2019) and individuals with missing data on self-identified race/skin color (n = 3 in 2013; n = 9 in 2019) or on self-identified weight and height (n = 16,820 in 2013; n = 14,888 in 2019) were excluded. Individuals who self-identified as Asian (n = 479 in 2013; n = 621 in 2019) or Indigenous (n = 363 in 2013; n = 611 in 2019) were excluded due to insufficient sample size for conducting intersectional analyses, as a minimum of 20 obesity cases within each analytical subgroup was used as the threshold for inclusion (see Supplementary Table S1). Because these groups were not part of the defined analytical population, exclusions were applied before the analyses. Other demographic variables used in the study had no missing data. The final analytical sample consisted of 36,443 individuals in 2013 and 66,303 in 2019 (see Supplementary Figure S1).

To assess inequalities among population subgroups, obesity prevalence estimates were calculated separately for men and women and stratified by self-identified race/skin color categories (White, Black, and Brown) and educational level. Education was selected as the socioeconomic position indicator due to its feasibility, practicality for classification in developing countries, and its sensitivity to social living conditions known or suspected to influence health outcomes [21]. Education was originally collected in seven categories in the PNS questionnaire (VDD004A). Using the original response options reported verbatim, these categories were grouped into four educational levels: “sem instrução” and “fundamental incompleto” (pre-primary); “fundamental completo” and “médio incompleto” (primary); “médio completo” and “superior incompleto” (secondary); and “superior completo” (tertiary).

The outcome variable, obesity, was assessed using Body Mass Index (BMI), calculated from self-identified weight and height (weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared). A dichotomous variable (Yes/No) was created using the World Health Organization (WHO) reference cut-off: BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² [22].

Considering that weight and height were self-reported and that analyses were stratified by race/skin color and educational level, the prevalence of missing data for the BMI variable in these groups—resulting from nonresponse for weight and/or height — was assessed. A sensitivity analysis was then conducted using weighted obesity prevalence estimates, giving greater weight to respondents from groups with lower response rates. Accordingly, the survey weighting variable was multiplied by the inverse of the response rate (i.e., the frequency of individuals with sufficient data to calculate BMI, among all eligible respondents within each race/skin color and educational level stratum). All analyses incorporated the complex sampling design of the survey, and standard errors were estimated accordingly.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Stata® software, version 17 (StataCorp. 2021. Stata Statistical Software: Release 17. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC), incorporating the survey sampling design and applying the survey command for prevalence estimates and their respective 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).

Because the study is based on two independent cross-sectional surveys, all analyses were conducted separately for the 2013 and 2019 datasets. Inequality estimates were calculated for each survey year, and differences between years were assessed by comparing estimates obtained from these independent samples.

Absolute and relative educational inequalities in obesity were assessed using the Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and the Relative Index of Inequality (RII). These indices were estimated using the sii command in Stata based on regression models including a ranked educational variable and obesity as the binary outcome. SII represents the absolute difference in predicted obesity prevalence between the lowest and highest positions in the educational hierarchy, expressed in percentage points (p.p). The RII represents the relative ratio of predicted prevalence across the same hierarchy. All models were estimated separately by sex, race/skin color, and survey year.

For SII, negative values indicate higher obesity prevalence among the less educated, positive values indicate higher prevalence among the more educated, and zero represents the absence of inequality [23, 24]. For the RII, values above 1 indicate higher obesity prevalence among the more educated, while values below 1 indicate higher prevalence among the less educated. Both measures account for all categories of the stratification variable, not just the extreme groups, and are easily interpretable even for non-specialist audiences [23, 24].

Inequality estimates were calculated separately for each survey year to characterize educational inequalities within each period, and differences between 2013 and 2019 were assessed by comparing independent estimates.

Differences in SII and RII between the 2013 and 2019 surveys were assessed using a z-test for the comparison of independent estimates. The test statistic was calculated as the difference between the indices divided by the square root of the sum of their squared standard errors, and two-sided p-values were derived assuming a normal distribution.

Results for obesity prevalence were presented graphically using equiplots [25], generated with the Equiplot Creator tool [25], whereas SII and RII estimates were reported in tables. In 2013, sample size limitations were more pronounced for smaller groups; therefore, for Black men and Black women, the 2019 results were emphasized in analyses involving triple stratification (sex, race/skin color, and educational level), as the expanded sample provided greater analytical power.

Results

The prevalence of obesity was 18.8% in 2013 and 21.9% in 2019 and was consistently higher among women than men (20.2% vs. 17.2% in 2013 and 23.3% vs. 20.4% in 2019). The obesity prevalence stratified by educational level for the total sample in 2013 and 2019, and by sex for each year, is presented in Fig. 1. Among women, the pattern was similar in both years, with higher obesity prevalence among those with lower educational attainment (26.5% and 27.7% in 2013 and 2019, respectively). Among men, educational inequality was less pronounced than among women; however, those with higher education levels consistently showed the highest obesity prevalence in both years (19.0% in 2013 and 22.4% in 2019).

Figure 2 displays the equiplots stratified by race/skin color. Among White men, obesity prevalence was higher in the 2019 survey than in 2013 in the pre-primary and primary education groups (2.5 and 3.5 p.p., respectively), with the primary education group emerging as the one with the highest prevalence in 2019. Among Brown men, obesity prevalence was higher in the 2019 survey across all educational levels (ranging from 3 to 6 p.p.), with a more pronounced rise among those with secondary and tertiary education. For Black men, considering only 2019 data, obesity prevalence was highest among those with tertiary education, approximately 5 p.p. higher than that observed for White and Brown men with the same educational level.

Obesity prevalence by education, sex and race/skin color. Brazil, National Health Survey, 2013–2019

For women, Fig. 2 shows that among White women, obesity prevalence was higher in the 2019 survey than in 2013 in the primary education group (over 5 p.p.). Together with the pre-primary group, these groups had the highest obesity prevalence levels. Among Brown women, the increase in prevalence was more pronounced in the two highest education levels (6.9 and 8.4 p.p. for secondary and tertiary education, respectively). In both years, a clear linear pattern was observed among White and Brown women, with decreasing obesity prevalence as educational level increased —a pattern not observed among Black women.

In 2019, Black women with the lowest education level stood out as the group with the highest obesity prevalence (32.7%) across all categories of sex, race/skin color, and education. Among Black women with higher education levels, obesity prevalence was 8 to 10 p.p. lower than among those with pre-primary education. However, no educational gradient was observed, as prevalence values were similar across the other education groups. Compared with White women at the same educational level, Black women with tertiary education had an obesity prevalence nearly 9 p.p. higher (Fig. 2). For numeric values of Figs. 1 and 2, see Supplementary Table S2.

To quantify the patterns of educational inequality observed above, Table 1 presents the complex inequality measures for each survey year. When comparing estimates from the two independent surveys, no statistically significant differences were observed between 2013 and 2019 for either sex (p > 0.1). In both years, absolute and relative inequalities were more pronounced among women, indicating higher obesity prevalence among less educated women (SII = -10.2 and − 12.9; RII = 0.5 and 0.6 in 2013 and 2019, respectively). For men, higher obesity prevalence was observed among more educated individuals (SII = 4.6 and 6.6; RII = 1.4 a nd 1.3 in 2013 and 2019, respectively).

According to the complex inequality measures by race/skin color (Table 1), in 2019, absolute and relative educational inequalities showed a greater concentration of obesity among more educated men across all racial groups. Absolute inequality was higher and statistically significant for Black (SII = 9.9) and Brown men (SII = 8.5), but not for White men (SII = 3.8; 95% CI: -0.7; 8.3). Relative inequality was also greater among Brown men (RII = 1.5). When comparing the two survey years, a significant difference in inequality estimates was observed only for Black men, suggesting a reversal in both absolute and relative inequality.

In 2019, obesity prevalence was higher among more educated Black men (SII = 9.9). Regarding women, educational inequality among Black women was small and not statistically significant, likely reflecting the absence of a clear linear relationship between education and obesity. For White and Brown women, inequality was greater and statistically significant in both years, with higher obesity prevalence among less educated women. A reduction in inequality was observed only among Brown women when comparing the two surveys (SII = − 11.6 in 2013 vs. −9.7 in 2019; RII = 0.5 in 2013 vs. 0.7 in 2019) (Table 1), primarily reflecting a greater increase in obesity prevalence among women with secondary and tertiary education in the 2019 survey (Fig. 2).

To further explore differences across groups, Fig. 3 presents the equiplot stratified by race/skin color alone (see Table S2 for numeric values). Obesity prevalence was higher in the 2019 survey compared with 2013 across all racial groups. Among Brown women, the increase in prevalence was 4.7 p.p., widening the gap with White women (who experienced a smaller increase of 1.5 p.p. during the same period) and approaching the levels observed among Black women, who had the highest prevalence in both years. Among men, White men had the highest obesity prevalence in the 2013 survey; however, in 2019 racial differences had nearly disappeared, as obesity prevalence increased more markedly among Black and Brown men (5.9 and 4.2 p.p., respectively).

Discussion

Using nationally representative data from Brazil for 2013 and 2019, this study identified that, despite an overall increase in obesity prevalence across nearly all groups, the magnitude of educational inequality remained stable during this period, except among Brown women, who showed a reduction in inequality primarily due to increased obesity prevalence among the two most educated groups. For Black men, a reversal was observed in both absolute and relative inequality, with higher obesity prevalence shifting from individuals with lower education in 2013 to those with higher education in 2019. Although this result could reflect a late nutritional transition among Black men, we cannot rule out the possibility of the limited sample size for this group in 2013 explain these observed differences, as the larger sample in 2019 allowed for greater analytical power.

Women consistently exhibited greater absolute and relative inequalities, characterized by an inverse relationship between education and obesity, while among men, inequality was smaller and followed a positive gradient.

In 2019, stratified analysis by race/skin color revealed that White and Brown women presented greater absolute and relative inequalities, with decreasing obesity prevalence as educational level increased. For Black women, although the highest prevalence was observed among those with the lowest educational level, similar prevalence rates across other education groups resulted in small and non-significant inequality measures. Among men, educational inequality was more pronounced for Black and Brown individuals, with higher obesity prevalence among the more educated. For White men, those with higher education no longer represented the group with the highest obesity prevalence (as in 2013), and inequality was smaller and not significant in both complex measures.

The relationship between education or socioeconomic position and obesity is well documented [6]. In middle-income countries, this association is predominantly negative for women but inconsistent for men [8], aligning with the pattern found in our study: negative for women and still positive for men, a pattern also observed in Peru, Mexico, and Colombia [26], and in several Latin American cities [5]. However, this scenario may shift as Brazil undergoes further economic development, following patterns observed in most European countries [27], where obesity is more concentrated among lower socioeconomic groups, especially among women.

This nutritional transition reflects the capitalist economic system’s capacity to produce both undernutrition and an obesogenic environment [28], with obesity growth rates becoming higher among the poorest, particularly women, as industrialization and urbanization progress [6]. Major lifestyle and food system changes, especially the widespread availability of ultra-processed foods (UPF), play key roles [29, 30]. UPF consumption, strongly associated with obesity [31, 32] and chronic diseases [33], has increased among the poorest and least educated in Brazil [34], who often reside in neighborhoods with a higher density of outlets selling predominantly unhealthy foods [35].

Women appear more responsive to the protective effects of education in obesogenic environments and may undergo this transition earlier [8], likely due to greater sensitivity to adopting healthy eating practices [36, 37] and body dissatisfaction [38] as their knowledge increases. Moreover, more educated women have greater resources to manage weight gain associated with motherhood, compared to less educated women, who tend to have more children and become mothers at younger ages [39]. Additionally, they have more time for leisure-time physical activity [40]. Although this is also true for more educated men [40], vigorous occupational physical activity remains more common among men and those with lower education levels in developing countries [41, 42], which may act as a protective factor against obesity in less educated men.

Our findings also demonstrate that educational inequality in obesity behaves differently across racial groups and sexes. In 2019, Black and Brown men exhibited more evident absolute and relative inequalities, with higher obesity prevalence among the more educated, a pattern less pronounced and non-significant among White men. This suggests that the transition already observed in other countries, where education is no longer a risk factor for obesity among men [6], may be occurring in Brazil, but initially among White men. Similarly, a 2008 study showed that obesity odds increased with socioeconomic status among Brazilian men, more markedly for Black and Brown individuals (OR = 1.26 and 1.37, respectively) compared to Whites (OR = 1.12) [11].

For women, the negative relationship between education and obesity was more consolidated among White women, with stronger inequality measures compared to Brown women. Among Black women, neither absolute nor relative inequalities were significant, suggesting that education does not confer the same protection against obesity as observed in White women. In 2008–2009, data from the Brazilian Household Budget Survey showed that Black women with higher socioeconomic status had three times the odds of obesity compared to White women, but 30% lower odds at the lowest socioeconomic level [11], consistent with our 2013 findings. However, in 2019, this scenario reversed, with less educated Black women exhibiting the highest obesity prevalence, though no clear educational gradient was observed across the other groups.

Education appears to provide protection against obesity among Black women; however, unlike the clear educational gradient observed among White and Brown women, obesity prevalence remains relatively similar across most educational levels among Black women, except for the lowest level, which shows higher prevalence. Brown women, though resembling White women in inequality patterns, showed a more pronounced increase in obesity among the two highest education levels between 2013 and 2019, resulting in reduced relative inequality. These findings suggest that the protective effect of education on obesity may be less consistent among Black women and may have weakened among Brown women.

It is well known that the living conditions of Black individuals in Brazil, even with higher education and socioeconomic status, are not equivalent to those of White individuals. Despite some reduction in occupational segregation for highly educated individuals in recent years, Black individuals continue to face wage discrimination, earning lower incomes even when occupying the same positions as Whites [43]. Moreover, socially disadvantaged students often attend less prestigious higher education institutions and enter lower-paying fields [44].

This aligns with the “Blacks’ diminished gain” theory, which posits that the positive effects of socioeconomic and psychological resources on health are systematically smaller for Black individuals, largely due to structural racism and systemic inequalities that exacerbate racial health disparities [45]. Assari et al. (2024) showed that over 24 years, education played a more substantial role in maintaining healthy BMI among non-Hispanic Whites than non-Hispanic Blacks in the USA [46].

Building on this perspective, some studies suggest that experiences of racial discrimination may represent one pathway linking education and obesity disparities. Higher educational level may increase exposure to or awareness of discriminatory experiences [47], potentially generating psychosocial stress and related behavioral responses such as stress-related eating, as well as neuroendocrine processes linked to fat accumulation [48, 49]. Although discrimination was not measured in the present study, previous research has reported associations between perceived racial discrimination and obesity or weight gain in different populations [50–53].

Differences in food environments have also been discussed in the literature as a potential pathway linking education and obesity disparities across racial groups. Previous studies in Brazil suggest that even among individuals with higher educational attainment, Black and Brown populations may have lower consumption of fresh and minimally processed foods and different dietary patterns compared with White individuals [54, 55]. Other research indicates that ultra-processed food consumption has increased in Brazil and, although it remains more prevalent among individuals with higher educational level, it has grown more rapidly among those with lower socioeconomic status and among Black populations [30].

As demonstrated, the relationship between obesity, education, and race/skin color is complex and requires an intersectional approach. The differences observed are not explained solely by education or sex but also by racial identity and its associated social processes. Merely increasing education is unlikely to eliminate racial disparities in obesity and may even widen gaps. Health interventions may have smaller-than-expected effects among Black individuals. More intersectional research is needed to address these inequities.

Behavior-focused prevention policies alone seem ineffective in halting obesity trends. Structural changes targeting the current economic model — the main driver of social inequities and obesogenic environments — should be prioritized [28].

This study has some limitations. Despite national representativeness, small subgroup sizes and insufficient sample domain for some stratification categories limited statistical power. Future surveys should ensure adequate representation and sample domain across education and race/skin color strata. Obesity was defined based on self-reported weight and height, which are commonly used in population-based studies and have been shown to be valid measures for nutritional status assessment [56]. However, self-reported measures may be subject to reporting bias that varies across socioeconomic and race/skin color groups, potentially leading to underestimation of obesity prevalence and attenuation of inequality estimates. In the 2013 survey, where both self-reported and measured anthropometric data were available for the full analytical sample, obesity prevalence based on self-reported data was consistently lower across all groups (see Supplementary Table S6), but the overall pattern and educational gradient remained similar, suggesting that the use of self-reported BMI may have affected the magnitude but not the overall interpretation of inequality patterns. Furthermore, the use of self-reported measures allowed a larger sample for analyzing prevalence across social dimensions. Black and Brown individuals, as well as those with lower educational levels, had significantly higher rates of missing data for weight and height in both years (Supplementary Table S3). Nevertheless, the sensitivity analyses revealed no meaningful differences in the prevalence estimates when inverse response-rate weighting was applied (Supplementary Table S4). Thus, missing data did not materially affect obesity prevalence estimates but underscores the barriers that these populations face in accessing health services [57]. Additionally, the analysis was restricted to individuals aged ≥ 23 years to ensure a more stable classification of completed educational level. Alternative age thresholds or approaches to account for ongoing education among younger individuals could yield slightly different estimates and should be explored in future studies. Strengths include a nationally representative sample, publicly available data from two waves corresponding to the most recent nationally available data for Brazil, consistent methodology, and an intersectional analytical approach that remains scarce in middle-income countries. The use of WHO-recommended complex inequality measures adds analytical rigor [24].

Conclusions

This study highlights persistent educational and racial inequalities in obesity among Brazilian adults, with distinct patterns observed between men and women and among different race/skin color groups. The findings reinforce the need for an intersectional approach to address obesity, recognizing the cumulative effects of social disadvantages related to education, race/skin color, and gender.

The results suggest that policies focused solely on individual behavioral change may be insufficient to reduce obesity and its associated disparities. Structural interventions targeting the broader social determinants of health, including economic and food system changes, should be prioritized. Furthermore, strategies should be tailored to the specific needs of the most vulnerable groups, particularly Black and Brown populations, to avoid widening existing inequalities.

Future research with an intersectional lens is essential to better understand the complex mechanisms underlying these disparities and to guide more equitable and effective public health actions.
