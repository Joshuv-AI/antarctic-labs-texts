Research on Dust Migration and Dust Deposition Rules of Breathing Zone in Fully Mechanized Mining Face
Authors: Tianxuan Hao, Lei Wang, Zehua Wang, Fan Li, Wan Jiang, Shan Jiang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe dust in the breathing zone of the fully mechanized mining face poses a serious threat to the lives and health of the workers, and clarifying the distribution and settlement pattern of the dust in the breathing zone is an important foundation for reducing dust concentrations in the breathing zone and improving the workers’ working environment. The dust concentration distribution at the mining face and the settlement results of different dust particle sizes along the breathing zone were analyzed in detail using a combination of computational fluid dynamics simulation and downhole measurements by establishing a highly simulated 3D solid model of the 2302 fully mechanized mining face at the Xinqiao coal mine. The study’s findings reveal that dust from advanced support and coal-cutting processes converge within 20–60 m of the coal mining region, generating a high-concentration pollution zone at the height of the sidewalk breathing zone. The particle size of dust in the breathing zone is primarily concentrated between 1 and 40 μm, and the fully mechanized mining face is split into a rapid settling area, a medium settling area, and a slow settling area based on particle size settling speed. Eddy currents in the return airway lead to an increase in dust particles of 20–40 μm in the breathing zone area. The results of the study can provide a theoretical basis for dust management at the fully mechanized mining face for the breathing zone area.

Introduction

When the drum and advancing support work together without any dust control measures, the amount of floating dust generated from the fully mechanized mining face accounts for approximately 40–80%1 of the total floating dust in the mine, and the concentration of full dust in the tunnel is as high as 4000 mg/m3.2 Even with dust management measures such as spraying, a considerable number of microscopic coal dust particles remain suspended on the coal mining face. This coal dust does not settle quickly and remains in the breathing zone for a long period. Operators inhale dust suspended in the tunnel for an extended period, increasing the risk of pneumoconiosis among workers. According to the National Health Commission’s 2019–2021 National Occupational Disease Report, occupational pneumoconiosis accounts for up to 77%. Pneumoconiosis patients in the coal industry account for more than half of them,3−5 and are the hardest afflicted by the disease. Coal mine dust pollution not only ruins underground production equipment and influences enterprise safety production, but it also offers a significant hazard to the occupational health of underground workers.6

So far, greater progress has been made in the investigation of the dust migration rule at the coal mining face. For example, Zhou et al.7,8,9 investigated the law of dust migration along the range in the case of multiprocess and multisource dust in the large mining height fully mechanized mining face and discovered that the dust produced by the advancing support mainly diffuses and migrates near the roof, and the dust settling in the height of the walkway’s breathing zone is not obvious, and the mass concentration of dust in the height of the breathing zone reaches. By numerical simulation, Zuo et al.10,11 explored the difference between the air flow-dust migration rule of a big mining height fully automated mining face and a general fully mechanized mining face. Xu et al.12 conducted numerical simulations of two processes, advancing support and coal-cutting drum, and analyzed the dust dispersion law under single and multiple dust sources, concluding that the dust generated by advancing support primarily pollutes the sidewalk area, while the dust generated by cutting process peaks at the front and rear drums of the shearer. Wang13 developed physical models for downwind and upwind coal cutting and investigated the distribution of dust mass concentration in personnel breathing zones near the shearer under single and multiprocess conditions. Under both downwind and upwind coal-cutting conditions at the coal mining face, the highest respiratory dust mass concentrations in the crew working area near the shearer were detected in the middle of the shearer. Wu and Wang14 investigated the dust concentration variation law of dust particles in the fully mechanized mining face air flow field at various dust source heights and dust source concentrations, concluding that dust concentration decreases with increasing wind speed or dust source height, while dust concentration in the turbulent flow area is less affected by wind speed and dust source height. Tan et al.15 investigated the relationship between dust mass concentration at the fully mechanized mining face and airflow velocity, drum speed during drum coal-cutting, and scraper conveyor speed using numerical modeling. Lei16 used numerical simulation to investigate the dust concentration distribution and migration trajectory of the fully mechanized mining face at different time scales of 20, 40, and 80 s of dust creation from coal-cutting. The position of the dust from the front drum coal-cutting into the sidewalk is reduced from about 10.3m downwind from the center of the front drum to 6.2 m after the shearer produces dust in the 20 to 80 s. The dust from the rear drum coal-cutting enters the sidewalk space approximately 11.3 m downwind from the rear drum’s center. Based on a discrete particle model, Feng et al.17 investigated the dust fugitive law of shearers in various sections of the fully mechanized mining face. It was determined that significant concentrations of dust clumps occur often around the front drum of the shearer and its downwind side in various areas, with the most serious pollution of the sidewalk occurring when the shearer is at the head of the tunnel. Pang et al.18 investigated the dust distribution characteristics of a fully mechanized mining face using on-site actual measurements of dust mass concentration and dust dispersity. The results show that the proportion of respiratory dust generated by the advancing support is higher, that dust larger than 10 μm on the fully mechanized mining face has the greatest dispersity and accounts for the majority of the dust, and that dust with particle size less than 10 μm dominates the dust exposure of the staff.

According to the preceding studies, the majority of academics are researching the dust migration and concentration distribution law of the fully mechanized mining face under various working conditions or diverse dust source conditions. There has been little research on dust migration in the coal mining face’s breathing zone, as well as the distribution and settling pattern of different particle sizes along the coal mining face, even though dust in the breathing zone has a direct impact on miners’ physical and mental health.

As a result, the research of the dust migration and deposition law in the fully mechanized mining face’s breathing zone may be targeted to design dust prevention and control methods, which is critical to the safe production of coal mines and the health of operators. Using the 2302 coal mining face of the Xinqiao coal mine as an example, the author investigated the dust migration law in the breathing zone area of the fully mechanized mining face using a combination of numerical simulation and field measurements and analyzed the dust settling law of different particle sizes in the breathing zone from the standpoint of particle size, providing theoretical support for dust management in the fully mechanized mining face.

Airflow-Dust Two-Phase Mathematical Model

Airflow and dust migration are examples of flow-solid two-phase flow motion in the production process of a fully mechanized mining face. The Euler–Lagrange model is commonly used to describe airflow and dust movement. The airflow is considered continuous,19−21 and the Navier–Stokes equation and the k-turbulence equation are solved in Fluent using the SIMPLE algorithm. The airflow in the fully automated mining face is an incompressible fluid, and the fluid viscous forces and frictional heat between the fluid and the solid surface are negligible; therefore, heat transmission is ignored. To capture the trajectory of dust particles in the tunnel, the force equation of coal dust particles is solved in Lagrangian coordinates, and the interaction between the airflow and coal dust particles and between coal dust particles and coal dust particles is considered.22−24 This is used to examine the fully mechanized mining face’s wind flow field and dust dispersion law.

Physical Model and Boundary Conditions

Engineering Background

The net width of the Xinqiao coal mine’s 2302 fully mechanized mining face is 4.5 m, the net height is 3 m, the tendency length is 170 m, and the coal mining face has a total of 120 ZY4000/17.5/38 type cover hydraulic support, with a minimum roof control distance of 3460 mm and a maximum roof control distance of 4060 mm. When cutting coal, the advance support is 4–5 hydraulic supports behind the shearer. The fully mechanized mining face uses “U″ style ventilation, with inlet and returns air lanes that are respectively 3.7 × 4.3 and 3 × 3 5 m in net height and breadth. On the coal mining face, the MG300/700-QWD double-drum shearer is utilized, which uses the technique of mining the entire height of coal at once.

Physical Model and Mesh Generation

Solidworks was used to create a physical model based on the actual site working conditions of the 2302 fully mechanized mining face of the Xinqiao coal mine, with the direction of coal cutting as the positive X-axis, away from the coal wall as the positive Y-axis, and the bottom of the roadway to the top plate as the positive Z-axis, as shown in Figure 1. The entire physical model is divided into a 15 m intake airway, a 170 m coal mining face, and a 50 m return airway, with the coal mining face further divided into a 30 m advancing support area and a 140 m nonadvancing support area, with the nonadvancing support area including the mining area (15 m) and the unmined area (125 m).

Physical model.

The hydraulic support width is 1.5 m, the base plate height is 0.4 m, the support column is simplified to a cylinder with a diameter of 270 mm, and the hydraulic support advance distance is 0.8m; the shearer is simplified to a rectangular body with length width height: 5 × 1.8 × 1.3 m, the rocker arm length is 2 m, the cutoff depth is 0.7 m, the shearer’s front and rear drums are simplified to a 1.5 m diameter cylinder, and the scraper conveyor is simplified to a rectangular body with length width height: 170 × 1 × 0.3 m, as illustrated in Figure 2. The ICEM software is used to divide the physical model’s nonstructural grid, and the grid is manually encrypted in locations where turbulence is easily caused, such as the shearer, and then the grid is mended. Because the quality and quantity of the model mesh have a direct impact on simulation accuracy, mesh independence must be validated. To begin, the global cell parameters are adjusted, and the minimum cell size is changed to 0.15, 0.20, and 0.25, while the maximum cell remains intact. As illustrated in Figure 3, three different types of mesh, A, B, and C, were obtained with 5,972,536, 7,316,515, and 9,215,214 cells, respectively. There are 5,947,065, 7,307,149, and 9,208,512 cells with mesh quality greater than 0.4, accounting for 99.57, 99.87, and 99.92% of the total cells, respectively. This suggests that the mesh quality is quite excellent and that numerical simulations can be performed using any of the three mesh types. To test the effect of cell size on numerical simulation results, one measurement point was placed every 20 m at a height of 1.7 m on the sidewalk of the 2302 coal mining face. The airflow simulation results for these three sets of mesh are compared while keeping the flow field simulation parameters constant. As shown in Figure 3, all three sets of data are different, but the overall airflow pattern is similar, suggesting that the three sets of mesh are independent of one another and that the structure of the mesh division is the same, maintaining the pattern’s consistency. Taking into account the link between simulation time, quantity of cells, and mesh quality, the B-type mesh is finally utilized for numerical simulation, and the mesh division results are given in Figure 4.

Import the model mesh into Fluent and create three main dust source sites in Fluent: the inlet air, the advancing support, and the shearer’s front and rear drums. Dust produced by the drum, dust produced by the advancing support, and dust carried by the air inlet of the coal mining face had mass flow rates of 0.10, 0.05, and 0.0002 kg/s, respectively, and particle dispersion coefficients of 1.44, 1.38, and 1.15, respectively. Field Measurements Arrangements

Field measurements were taken at the Xinqiao coal mine’s 2302 fully mechanized mining face to test the model’s reliability. Figure 5 shows how eight sections were set up for measurement. A–E and F–H set up measurement sections every 20 and 15 m, respectively, with A on the downwind side of the drum in front of the shearer and F 15 m from the end of the coal mining face in the return airway. At each measurement section sidewalk with a (z = 1.7 m), and b (z = 1.5 m) two different height measurement points. When the fully mechanized mining face ceased production, the average airflow velocity of the section was measured with a low-speed mechanical wind meter, and the measured data were compared with the simulated section airflow velocity to verify the model flow field’s accuracy. The dust concentration at measurement point b was measured using a CCF-7000 direct reading dust concentration measuring instrument after 0.5 h of normal production at the coal mining face (the tool used for the experiment is shown in Figure 6), with each measurement point measured three times and the final concentration averaged over three times. Figure 7 depicts the measured airflow velocity, dust concentration, and simulated data. The measured data have a little wider variation range than the simulated data, as shown in the figure, due to the complexity of the roadway section and sometimes minor sections of coal-wall collapse, resulting in a slightly larger variation range of the observed data. In general, the measured and simulated data have the same growth pattern, and the error is within 10%, indicating that the numerical simulation data are in line with the actual situation, and the simulated data may be used in the field.

Analysis of Airflow Motion Laws in the Mining Face

As shown in Figure 8, the airflow velocity distribution cloud map intercepted from the bottom plate Z = 0.5 1.5, 1.7, 2.5 m; from the figure can be seen, in the corner of the intake airway to the coal mining face and the corner of the coal mining face to the return airway, there are two sections of high-speed airflow, airflow velocity reached 3 m/s. Because the airflow channel is altered, a greater pressure gradient is formed at the tunnel’s corners, resulting in increased airflow velocity near the coal-wall side. From the intake airway to the return airway, the airflow velocity has the characteristic of “small up and down and big in the middle,″ with low airflow velocity near the bottom plate (z = 0.5 m) and top plate (z = 2.5 m), while airflow is more concentrated in the middle of the tunnel, z = 1.5, and z = 1.7 m breathing zone height, and the highest airflow velocity reaches 3 m/s near the shearer.

Figure 9 shows a cross-sectional airflow velocity distribution cloud intercepted every 15 m from the coal mining face to the return airway. Figure 9 depicts the findings of four airflow velocity measurement locations of varying heights (z = 0.5, 1.5, 1.7, and 2.5 m) placed on the sidewalk of each section.

Cloud map of the airflow velocity distribution in different sections of the fully mechanized mining face.

Figures 9 and 10 indicate that the overall airflow velocity in the coal mining face changes in a “first decrease—then increase—then decrease” pattern. When the airflow enters the coal mining area from the advancing support area, the unadvanced hydraulic support increases the space area of the tunnel, resulting in a sudden decrease of the airflow velocity in the tunnel, and the left side of the hydraulic pillar decreases most noticeably, and the airflow velocity drops to 0.6 m/s. When the airflow passed through the shearer, the shearer and drum blocked the airflow and reduced the tunnel space, creating a high-speed turbulent flow area near the shearer, and the airflow spread to the sidewalk area under the influence of the shearer, forming an 8 m-long high-speed airflow belt with airflow velocity up to 2.7 m/s in the sidewalk breathing zone area. The tunnel area is relatively fixed as the airflow enters the unmined coal area, and the airflow velocity gradually lowers and stabilizes at 2.2–2.3 m/s. The high-speed airflow generates a vortex about 5 m from the coal mining face’s corner, forming a group of airflow from the outside of the coal wall up to the inside of the coal wall below and from the inside of the coal wall down to the outside of the coal wall above.

The Migration Laws of Multisource Dust in the Mining Face

As shown in Figure 11, the same interception of the dust concentration distribution cloud map near the bottom plate (z = 0.5 m), the top plate (z = 2.5 m), and the operator’s breathing zone (z = 1.5, z = 1.7 m) areas were used to better combine the airflow field to analyze the distribution law of dust concentration in the tunnel. Figure 12 depicts the distribution of the dust concentrations in various areas. The dust formed by the coal-cutting drum primarily spreads along the coal wall, as shown by these two figures, and the dust concentration on the side near the coal wall surpasses 2000 mg/m3. The dust created by moving the hydraulic support spreads to the middle of the tunnel and in the direction of the sidewalk, and dust concentrations on both sides near the hydraulic pillar can reach 2000 mg/m3. The following laws exist for the migration—distribution of this dust in the fully mechanized mining face:

Cloud map of dust concentration distribution at different heights.

Distribution of the dust concentration at different cross sections.

(1) As shown in Figure 11, dust pollution is worst around the height of the roof plate (z = 2.5 m). The dust generated by the shearer’s front drum (cutting the top coal) diffuses to the roof area, resulting in a high-concentration dust pollution zone near the coal wall. The dust generated by the advancing support disperses from the roof toward the middle of the tunnel and the sidewalk, generating two 60 m-long high-concentration dust pollution zones in the middle of the tunnel and the sidewalk on the downwind side of the advancing support. Due to the change in airflow velocity and the action of the shearer, the former has a process of first decreasing and then rising dust concentration within 15 m of the downwind side of the advancing support. After settling, the latter form a high-concentration dust pollution zone at the height of the breathing zone (z = 1.7, z = 1.5 m), with dust mostly concentrated on both sides of the hydraulic pillar on the coal mining area’s downwind side.

(2) Figure 13 depicts the simulated distribution of dust concentration along the 1.5 and 1.7 m breathing zone heights at the coal mining face sidewalk, demonstrating that dust concentration in the coal mining area follows a law that increases first and then decreases. The dust formed by moving the hydraulic support progressively travels to the sidewalk with airflow, increasing the concentration of dust in the breathing zone. When the airflow passes the shearer, the rapid diffusion of dust from the sidewalk to the left side of the hydraulic pillar reduces the dust concentration. Combined with Figure 12, it can be seen that within 20 m (45–65 m) on the downwind side of the coal mining area, the dust produced by moving the hydraulic support is concentrated and diffused on both sides of the hydraulic pillar from the roof area, and at this time the dust pollution in the breathing zone height of the sidewalk is primarily caused by it. The concentration is as high as 1500 mg/m3. The dust concentration in the breathing zone of the sidewalk area decreases as it gradually moves to the center of the tunnel under the influence of airflow. At this point, the dust produced by the coal-cutting drum likewise slowly moves from the coal-wall side to the center of the tunnel.

Distribution of dust concentrations along different breathing zones on the sidewalk.

The dust created by the coal-cutting drum and advancing support converge within 20–60 m of the downwind side of the shearer as the airflow migrates, and the dust concentration in the middle of the tunnel eventually achieves a maximum in this location, with an average concentration above 1000 mg/m3. At this point, the dust from the coal-cutting drum has moved to the sidewalk area, causing the dust concentration in the sidewalk’s breathing zone to steadily rise to 2000 mg/m3. As a result, the coal mining area’s 60 m downwind side is considered a high-concentration dust pollution area.

(3) When the airflow from the coal mining face enters the return airway’s corner, the airflow velocity rapidly increases, and the resulting vortex creates secondary dust in the return airway. As seen in Figure 12, the dust concentration in the vortex area falls and subsequently increases from (a) to (c). When high-speed airflow travels through the area, the vortex generates branch airflow from the outside of the coal wall up to the interior of the coal wall down, accelerating the settling of the high-concentration dust at the tunnel’s bottom. When it reaches the b area, the dust concentration decreases, and the dust settled at the bottom is subjected to the branch airflow from the inside of the coal wall down to the outside of the coal wall up, causing the dust concentration in the c area to increase. It caused the dust concentration in the return airway to begin to grow dramatically at 10 m from the corner of the coal mining face.

(4) Within 10–80 m of the downwind side of the coal mining area, there is a high dust concentration near the bottom plate (z = 0.5 m), indicating that a large amount of dust has settled in this area, and the settlement area is concentrated near the coal-wall, on both sides of the hydraulic pillar and at the confluence of dust from the coal-cutting drum and advancing support.

Particle Size Distribution Law of Coal Dust in the Fully Mechanized Mining Face

The dust was collected at the coal mining face with a CC-20 dust sampler, sorted, and preserved, and the dust at each measurement point was evaluated for particle size in the laboratory with an MD-1 dust particle size analyzer. Figure 14 depicts the dust particle size distribution curves along the 1.5 and 1.7 m breathing zone heights for the coal mining face and return airway sidewalk region. Figure 15 depicts the varied particle size distributions and dust-settling durations at the coal mining face. It can be shown that the particle size at the height of the breathing zone is primarily concentrated between 1 and 40 μm, with a mass fraction of 67–82%. Dust larger than 60 μm settles more quickly within 20 m on the downwind side of the coal mining area, showing that dust larger than 60 μm settles readily and stays in the air for a shorter period. The mass percentage of dust with particle sizes larger than 20 μm was reduced by 20% along the process from A to B, and by around 10% from B to D with a moderate transition. The mass fraction reduced insignificantly from D to E. When combined with the dust settling time in Figure 15, it is clear that the dust stays in the A–B region for less than 20, 20–50 s in the B–D region, and more than 50 s in the tunnel after the D location. As a result, dust settling is classified into three sections based on the change of dust particle size mass fraction and residence time in the tunnel: quick settling area (A–B), medium settling area (B–D), and slow settling area (D–H). Figure 15 shows that after the airflow from the coal mining face enters the return airway, the vortex pulls the dust particles settling in the b-region with particle sizes below 40 μm back up to the c-region. Simultaneously, Figure 14 shows that the mass fraction of dust larger than 40 and 20 μm increases dramatically in the breathing zone from E to F. It indicates that the return-airway vortex pulls the dust particles between 20 and 40 μm, which have settled at the bottom of the breathing zone, back up into the breathing zone area.

Different particle size distributions and dust settling time.

Figure 14b depicts the mass fraction of dust with a particle size less than 7 μm increases in the respiratory zone, dust concentration begins to decrease after passing through the confluence area, and the mass fraction of dust with a particle size less than 7 μm climbs from 29% in position B to 31% in position E (Figure 14b). The cumulative proportion of dust particles below 7μm in two heights in Figure 14b was obtained by sampling from the production site of the working face and analyzing it in the laboratory. The proportion of dust particles below 7μm in the 1.7m breathing zone is higher than that in the 1.5m breathing zone because the smaller the dust particles, the longer they stay suspended in the air, and the higher the position along the tunnel, the larger the proportion of fine dust particles.It shows that the mass fraction of dust smaller than 7 μm is gradually increasing in the area of decreasing dust concentration. From coal mining face E to return airway F, the mass fraction of dust smaller than 7 μm reaches more than 34%, and continues to climb along the return airway, reaching up to 40% when it reaches position H. At this point, the mass fraction of dust smaller than 7 μm is 1.3 times that of position E and 1.8 times that of location A. Overall, the mass fraction of dust smaller than 7 μm is greater at 1.7 m than at 1.5 m. This means that the smaller the particles, the longer the dust will be suspended in the air, and the less likely it will settle.

Conclusions

(1) Numerical simulation of the airflow field at the fully mechanized mining face reveals that the airflow exhibits a “decrease first—then increase—then decrease” change process. The airflow produces an 8 m-long high-speed airflow belt at the height of the sidewalk’s breathing zone and then forms a vortex approximately 5 m away from the corner of the coal mining face after entering the return airway under the effect of the shearer.

(2) A numerical simulation of the dust field at the fully mechanized mining face reveals that the high-concentration dust pollution zone is within 60 m of the coal mining area’s downwind side, which is where dust deposition is more concentrated. The dust from the moving support spreads to the tunnel’s center and the sidewalk area, generating two high-concentration dust pollution zones about 60 m long near the ceiling. The dust from the advancing support and coal-cutting drum converge within 20–60 m downwind of the coal mining region, resulting in a maximum dust concentration of 2000 mg/m3 in the sidewalk’s breathing zone.

(3) The particle size distribution of dust in different breathing zones along the fully mechanized mining face is primarily concentrated between 1 and 40 μm, and the downwind side of the coal mining area is divided into 40, 40–80 m, and after 80 m as dust fast settling zone, medium speed settling zone, and slow settling zone. The eddy current in the return airway pulls 20–40 μm dust particles back up to the breathing zone area, resulting in secondary dust emission.

(4) The fully mechanized mining face should focus on the following three areas to arrange dust control measures: near the top plate on the downwind side of the advancing support should focus on preventing dust from spreading to the sidewalk area and can be installed at the top beam of the hydraulic support spray dust removal device; in the coal mining area, downwind side 20–60 m focus on preventing dust from the shearer to spread to the sidewalk area; chemical dust suppressants should be utilized in the return airway 15 m from the end of the coal mining face area to catch dust and avoid secondary dust.
