Chlorophyll a/b-binding protein gene AcLhcb1.1 promotes chlorophyll accumulation and improves cold stress resistance in kiwifruit
Authors: Xinling Liu, Yingying Zhang, Daolin Ye, Xiaoyan Luo, Meijing Wu, Xiaoli Zhang, Yue Xie, Dong Liang, Hui Xia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChlorophyll degradation coupled with the accumulation of other pigments during fruit ripening result in different colors in kiwifruit flesh. Light- harvesting chlorophyll a/b binding proteins (Lhc) play crucial roles in photosynthesis and chlorophyll metabolism. However their specific functions in kiwifruit fruit coloration remain poorly understood. Here, we identified 31 Lhcs family members from the kiwifruit genome, most of which possess cis-acting elements in response to light, low temperature and abscisic acid in their promoters. Through transcriptome profile analysis, AcLhcb1.1 was screened positively associated with flesh coloration. AcLhcb1.1 displayed lower expression levels in the yellow-fleshed variety ‘Jinshi 1’ but higher expression levels in the green-fleshed variety ‘White’. Moreover, overexpression of AcLhcb1.1 in tobacco and kiwifruit resulted in a significant increase in leaf chlorophyll content compared with wild type (WT), accompanied by down-regulated expression of chlorophyll degradation genes AcPPH and AcPAO. In addition, transgenic kiwifruit plants exhibited reduced malondialdehyde content and relative electrolyte leakage compared to WT when exposed to low temperature, and higher antioxidant enzyme activities of SOD, CAT, and POD. Introduction

Photosynthesis, which converts solar energy into chemical energy, plays a crucial role in the formation and maintenance of the Earth’s biosphere [1]. Higher plants mainly rely on a series of ordered pigment protein complexes on the thylakoid membrane to absorb, transfer and transform light energy [2]. Chlorophyll a/b photocapture binding protein (Lhc) is a thylakoid membrane protein distributed in photosystem I (PSI) and photosystem II (PSII), which binds to various pigment molecules to form pigment protein complex [3]. Monomer, dimer or other state embedded on the thylakoid membrane [4].

The Lhc family is classified into two subfamilies, Lhca and Lhcb, based on their structural and evolutionary relationships, Lhca, consisting of Lhca1-4, consist the main members of light harvesting complex I (Lhc I) in PSI. Lhcb, composed of Lhcb1-3, Lhcb4, Lhcb5 and Lhcb6, together constitute light harvesting complex II (Lhc II) in PSII [5]. Typically, Lhcb1/Lhcb2/Lhcb3 form homologous or heterotrimers while Lhcb4-6 predominantly exist functionally as monomers [4]. Recently, two additional Lhca proteins - namely, Lhca5 and Lhca6 has identified in Arabidopsis thaliana, rice (Oryza sativa), and tomato (Lycopersicon esculentum) [6–8]. And Lhcb7 and Lhcb8 (Lhcb4.3), derived from Lhcb4, were identified in poplars (Populus) and other dicotyledonous plants [9]. Therefore, there are a total of 6 Lhca members and 8 Lhcb members, respectively.

With the increasing identification of Lhc family genes in plants [10, 11], their roles in various processes of plant growth and development have been discovered, including leaf aging, leaf color change, flowering and fruiting, as well as diverse responses to biotic and abiotic stresses [4, 12]. The expression levels of Lhc genes displayed an increase with leaf maturation but significantly decrease during leaf senescence. In Arabidopsis thaliana, deletion or downregulation of Lhcb1 and Lhcb2 expression resulted in reduced photosynthetic rate and chlorophyll content, leading to a light green or white leaf phenotype [13, 14]. Transient overexpression of AcLhcb3.1/3.2 in tobacco leaves markedly enhanced chlorophyll a content [15], while VIGS-mediated GhLhcb2.3 silencing significantly reduced chlorophyll a content in cotton leaves [16]. Further investigations have revealed that Lhc can also interact with other proteins involved in regulating leaf coloration and aging. Interactions between Lhcs with SGR (STAY-GREEN), a key gene responsible for chlorophyll degradation, as well as chlorophyll catabolic enzymes (CCEs), form a SGR-CCE-Lhc-II protein complex which plays a critical role during the process of senescence-induced degreening [17].

Lhc proteins have been confirmed to be involved in response to various abiotic stresses [18–21]. Under conditions of iron deficiency, the abundance of Lhca14 protein was significantly reduced in rice seedlings, leading to a decrease in chlorophyll content and photosynthetic efficiency [22]. The expression level of Lhcb1 in celery increased when exposed to heat, cold, salt, and drought stresses [22]. Moreover, overexpression of LeLhcb2 in tobacco plants enhanced their tolerance to low temperature stress and reduced photooxidation of PSII [23]. Overexpression of SaLhcb2 in tobacco plants increased their capacity for cadmium absorption [24].

Although there has been some research on Lhc proteins over the years, there is a lack of systematic understanding in kiwifruit. In this study, 31 Lhc gene families were identified from the kiwifruit genome, and AcLhcb1.1, which were related to kiwifruit pulp color, were screened out, and its functions and mechanisms in chlorophyll accumulation and response to low temperature stress were verified through genetic transformation. The results will enrich our understanding of the role of Lhc in kiwifruit coloring and response to adversity.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Samples were collected from 10-year-old kiwifruit trees in the Experimental Base of Sichuan Agricultural University in Chongzhou (103°07’E, 30°53’N). Fruit of green-fleshed ‘White’ (Actinidia eriantha cv. ‘White’) and yellow-fleshed ‘Jinshi 1’ (Actinidia chinensis cv. ‘Jinyan’) were picked at 30, 55, 70, 95, 130 and 145 d after full blossom, designated as T1-T6, respectively. A detailed fruit images and morphological characteristics of both cultivars at all six stages were shown in supplemental Fig. S1 and supplemental Table S1. Leaves, roots, and flowers were sampled from ‘Jinshi 1’. All samples were collected in three biological replicates, immediately brought back to the laboratory, and stored at −80 ℃ in an ultra-low refrigerator.

Tissue culture seedlings of ‘Hongyang’ (Actinidia chinensis cv. ‘Hongyang’) kiwifruit were used for stable genetic transformation, and tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) was used for subcellular localization and transient genetic transformation. The obtained positive overexpressing kiwifruit lines were subjected to low temperature treatment at 4 ℃. Leaves were collected at 48 h after treatment.

Genomic-wide identification of Lhc gene family in kiwifruit

Genome and cDNA, CDS, protein sequence and GFF3 annotation files of ‘Red5’ kiwifruit were downloaded from Kiwifruit Genome Database (http://kiwifruitgenome.org/). The Lhc conserved domain (PF00504) was retrieved from the Pfam database (https://pfam.xfam.org) and used as a seed sequence to search for candidate Lhcs members in kiwifruit database using the HMMER with default parameter and an E-value cutoff of 10−5. The identified proteins were compared with Arabidopsis Lhcs using blastp to further confirm the candidate gene family members. To validate the conservative domain structure, the SMART website (http://smart.embl.de/smart) and CDD (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) were employed to filter out proteins lacking the conserved domain or containing redundant sequences. The remaining proteins were designated as AcLhc genes. ExPASy (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) was used to predict the amino acids number, molecular weight, and isoelectric point. Wolf PSort (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/) was employed for predicting the subcellular localization. The basic information of Lhc family members in Actinidia chinensis was shown in supplement Table S2. TBtools (https://github.com/CJ-Chen/TBtools) was used to construct physical maps of chromosome locations based on the annotated information in gene annotation files.

Phylogenetic tree construction of Lhc gene family

Phylogenetic analysis of the Lhc gene family was performed using 52 protein sequences, including 31 Actinidia chinensis and 21 Arabidopsis thaliana. Sequences were aligned with MEGA11 (www.megasoftware.net) (Tamura et al., 2021) using MUSCLE, followed by tree reconstruction via Neighbor-Joining method (Saitou and Nei, 1987). Branch support was assessed with 1,000 bootstrap replicates, and the final tree was visualized using ITOL (https://itol.embl.de/) (Letunic and Bork, 2024). The The protein sequences of AcLhc and AtLhc were shown in Supplemental Table S3.

Analysis of gene structure, motif, cis-element and gene duplication of Lhc

The motifs and gene structures of AcLhc family members were obtained and visualize using TBtools. The 2000 bp promoter sequence were extracted, and the cis-elements were predicted using PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/). The evolutionary relationships of Lhc in kiwifruit, Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa and Malus domestica were analyzed using MCScanX, and Ka and Ks values for duplicate gene pairs were calculated to determine the selection mode of AcLhcs. Finally, DupGen-finder (https://github.com/qiao-xin/DupGen_finder) was applied to classify gene duplication events in more detail [11].

Cloning and sequences analysis of AcLhcb1.1

The coding sequence (CDS) of AcLhcb1.1 was cloned from kiwifruit using specific primers (Supplementary Table S4). Multiple sequence alignment was performed using DNAMAN with default parameters. A phylogenetic tree was constructed by MEGA 7 using the neighbor-joining (NJ) method with 1000 bootstrap replications to evaluate the reliability of the nodes.

Gene expression analysis by qRT-PCR

The total RNA was extracted from root, stem, leaf, flower and fruit at different developmental stages using the Wolact Plant RNA Isolation Kit (Wolact, Hong Kong, China). First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The gene expression levels were detected by qRT-PCR using a CFX96 instrument (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). All primers were designed using Primer 5.0 listed in Supplemental Table S4, Actin (EF063572) was used as the internal reference gene [25]. Each sample included three independent biological replicates and three technical repetitions. 2−ΔΔCt method was used to calculate the relative expression levels of genes.

Subcellular localization

The full-length CDSs of AcLhcb1.1 was inserted into pCAMBIA2300 vector (containing a GFP tag) using a One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The 35 S:: AcLhcb1.1 construct was infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves through Agrobacterium-mediated transformation as described previously described [26]. After 2 d of cultivation, fluorescence signals were detected using confocal microscopy (FV1000; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Transient transformation in tobacco leaves

To generate overexpression (OE) vectors, the full-length CDS of AcLhcb1.1 was inserted to pBI121 vector. Recombinant were then transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101, respectively. All A. tumefaciens were resuspended in the MES buffer and injected into tobacco leaves. Empty pBI121 vector was used as negative control. After 5 d, the samples surrounding the injection site were collected for subsequent experiments.

Stable transformation assay of kiwifruit

The transformation of ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit was performed according to our previous method [27]. The precultured leaf pieces (1*1 cm) were immersed in Agrobacterium suspension (OD600 = 0.6) contained recombined plastic for 10 min, and then co-cultivated in the dark for 2 d. Subsequently, the explants were then transferred onto MS medium supplemented with 1.0 mg·L−1 6-BA, 0.1 mg·L−1 NAA, 20 mg·L−1 Kan, and 400 mg·L−1 carbenicillin to induce callus information and bud differentiation. a culture period of 50 d, new resistant plants were obtained. Leaves of resistant plants were used for identification at DNA and RNA level.

Determination of pigments

The fresh leaf sample (0.5 g) was sliced and incubated in 50 mL of pigment extraction solution containing acetone and anhydrous ethanol (1:1, v/v) in darkness for 12 h at 25 ℃. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected and the absorbance of at 663 nm, 645 nm and 450 nm was measured using UV-1200 spectrophotometer (MAPADAR, Shanghai), and the chlorophyll and carotenoid content in the sample were calculated.

Determination of other physiological index

The relative leakage (REL), malondialdehyde (MDA) content and activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD) and catalase (CAT) were measured by referring to the method of Liang et al. (2019) [28].

Data analysis

All values were expressed as mean ± SD with three replicates. SPSS Statistics software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA) was employed to analyze data with methods of one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s tests. Significant differences were evaluated by (P < 0.05) and Student’s t-test (**P < 0.01).

Results

Genome-wide identification of Lhc gene family in kiwifruit

A total of 31 AcLhc members were identified from ‘Red5’ kiwifruit genome database (http://kiwifruitgenome.org/) through Hmmer and blastp with A. thaliana (Suppmental Table S2). Phylogenetic trees were constructed using Lhc proteins from Arabidopsis and kiwifruit, revealing that AcLhcs were divided into 6 subfamilies: I (Lhcb), II and III and IV (Lhca), V (CP24), VI (CP29), comprising 16, 2, 2, 7, 2 and 2 members, respectively (Fig. 1). All Lhca (II, III, IV) members contained similar conserved domains (Motif7), suggesting their relative conservation in evolution and potential functional similarities. Notably, V (CP24), VI (CP29) lacked typical conserved domain (Motif2), placing it within the ELIP (early light-induced protein) Psb subfamily of the broader Lhc superfamily. Furthermore, significant structural diversity existed among I (Lhcb), potentially leading to functional divergence. The number of introns in Lhc genes ranges from 0 to 5, however, eight genes lacked any introns (Fig. S2).

Evolutionary relationship of Lchs. Phylogenetic tree of Lhcs constructed using ML method with 1000 bootstrap

Chromosome location distribution and collinearity analysis

Members of the AcLhc family were distributed on 15 chromosomes. There were at most five members on the LG6 chromosome, and at least one member on the LG3, LG7, LG16, LG17 and LG24 chromosomes, with few gene clusters (Fig. S3A). Collinearity analysis showed that a total of 16 segmental duplications were detected, which might be the main force driving the expansion of the AcLhc gene family (Fig. S3B). Interspecies co-linearity between kiwifruit and rice (Oryza sativa), Arobidopsis thaliana, and apple (Malus domestica) were further analyzed. The findings revealed that the kiwifruit genome exhibited collinear Lhc genes with rice, Arabidopsis, and apple in 6, 24, and 48 pairs respectively (Fig. S3C). Moreover, the Lhcs in kiwifruit showed greater similarity to woody apple Lhcs, indicating a continuous expansion of Lhc genes during the evolutionary transition from herbaceous to vine and subsequently to woody plants. This expansion may be closely associated with plant responses to photosynthetic metabolic pathways.

We further calculated the Ka/Ks ratio of these synteny genes and found that the Ka/Ks ratio of majority gene pairs was much less than 1.0 (Table S5), indicating strong evidence for purifying selection acting upon most of these AcLhcs. Only two gene pairs (Acc23436.1 and Acc13590.1; Acc02751.1 and Acc16705.1) displayed a Ka/Ks ratio above 1.0, suggesting positive selection events.

Analysis of cis-acting elements of AcLhcs promoter

To predict the expression characteristics of AcLhcs, we conducted an analysis of cis-acting elements within the promoter region (−2000 bp). A total of 33 cis-acting elements were identified, with a majority promoters containing responsive cis-elements associated with light, low temperature, drought, auxin, gibberellin, abscisic acid and other factors. These findings suggested that AcLhcs may play a role in light response as well as stress and hormone signaling pathways (Fig. S4).

Expression of AcLhcs during kiwifruit development

The chlorophyll and carotenoid contents in ‘White’ fruits remained relatively stable throughout development, with only a slight decrease at T4 and a minor increase at T6. In contrast, the chlorophyll content of ‘Jinshi 1’ fruits declined sharply during the post-ripening period, while carotenoid content increased at the T6 stage (Fig. 2A-B). Consequently, the chlorophyll-to-carotenoid ratio was significantly lower in ‘Jinshi 1’ fruits than in ‘White’ fruits (Fig. 2C).

To assess the correlation between AcLhcs expression level and green color in kiwifruit flesh, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of AcLhcs expression during fruit development in both ‘Cuilv’ (green-fleshed) and ‘Jinshi 1’ (yellow-fleshed) varieties using transcriptome data. A Heat map depicting gene expression revealed significantly higher levels of most AcLhcs transcripts in green-fleshed kiwifruit compared to yellow-fleshed kiwifruit at later stages of fruit development (Fig. 3A). Notably, the expression levels of AcLhb3.1, AcLhb1.1, AcLhb1.3, and AcLhb1.8 were lower at T4 than at T1 but increased again at T6. This trend was consistent with the changes in chlorophyll and carotenoid contents observed in green-fleshed fruits. Furthermore, correlation analysis revealed that AcLhcb1.1 expression exhibited the strongest correlation with color differences between yellow- and green-fleshed fruits at the T4 and T6 stages (Fig. 3B, PAcLhcb1.1=0.88/0.96). These results suggest AcLhcb1.1 may participate in fruit color development.

Cloning, sequence analysis and subcellular localization of AcLhcb1.1

We separately cloned AcLhcb1.1 from green-fleshed ‘White’ and yellow-fleshed ‘Jinyan’. The open reading frames (ORFs) of AcLhc1.1 was 798 bp in length, encoding 265 amino acid, respectively. Notably, there were no differences in the amino acid sequences between ‘White’ and ‘Jinyan’ varieties. Alignment of amino acid sequences revealed a high degree of conservation among AcLhc proteins, including the conserved sequence of the functional domain for light-trapping chlorophyll a/b binding protein (Fig. S5).

Phylogenetic tree analysis based on NJ method showed that AcLhcb1.1 was closely related to that from Camellia oleifera, followed by grape (Vitis vinifera) and Gossypium hirsutum (Fig. 4A). The expression pattern of AcLhcb1.1 was analyzed using qRT-PCR. The highest transcript levels were detected in leaves, followed by fruits, flowers, stems, and roots (Fig. 4B). During fruit development, AcLhcb1.1 exhibited high expression in the early stages (T1-T3), which decreased in the later stages (T4-T6). Furthermore, AcLhcb1.1 expression was significantly higher in ‘White’ fruit compared to ‘Jinshi 1’, and was nearly undetectable during the late ripening stage in ‘Jinshi 1’ (Fig. 4C). Subcellular localization analysis showed that AcLhcb1.1 was located in the chloroplasts (Fig. 4D).

Overexpression of AcLhcb1.1 increases the chlorophyll content

To investigate the role of AcLhcb1.1 in chlorophyll accumulation, we overexpressed it in tobacco leaves. Compared with the control (infiltrated with EV), the leaves of AcLhcb1.1-overexpression plants exhibited a darker color (Fig. 5A), with an up-regulation of 1-fold expression compared to EV (Fig. 5B). Overexpression leaves had significantly higher contents of chlorophyll a and total chlorophyll than EV, with an increase by 41.67% and 22.22%, respectively (Fig. 5C). qRT-PCR analyses indicated that overexpression of AcLhcb1.1 significantly down-regulated relative expression of chlorophyll degradation related gene AcPPH by 23.4% compared to EV plants, while up-regulating relative expression of degradation related genes AcSGR and AcNYC1 by 9.46- and 35.70-fold, respectively (Fig. 5D).

Stable overexpression of AcLhcb1.1 in kiwifruit

The recombinant vector pBI121-AcLhcb1.1 was transformed into ‘Hongyang’ kiwifruit using Agrobacterium-mediated method. After 7 months of culture, 10 resistant plants were obtained, and DNA confirmed two positive lines (Fig. S6), OE1.1-9 and OE1.1-10 (Fig. 6A). qRT-PCR analysis revealed that the expression level of AcLhcb1.1 in these two lines was more than 8-fold higher compared to WT (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, overexpression of AcLhcb1.1 significantly increased chlorophyll a content without affecting chlorophyll b content, resulting in a significant upregulation of total chlorophyll content by 37.71% compared to WT (Fig. 6C-E). Additionally, qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that transgenic kiwifruit lines exhibited significant regulation of genes involved in the chlorophyll pathway; specifically, downregulation of degradation related gene AcPPH expression by approximately 24.3% compared to WT and upregulation of degradation related genes AcSGR, AcNYC1, and AcHCAR gene expressions by approximately 10.65-, 32.70-, and 3.-fold respectively relative to WT (Fig. 6F-J).

AcLhcb1.1 transgenic plants improves the tolerance of kiwifruit to low temperature

Lhc has been reported to play an important role in plant response to abiotic stress. Here, we subjected kiwifruit seedlings to 4℃ for 48 h and found that the leaves of WT exhibited curling, browning, and desiccation, while the AcLhcb1.1 transgenic plants only displayed slight browning at the leaf edges (Fig. 7A), suggesting that transgenic plants experienced less damage under low temperature stress conditions. Upon exposure, the transcription levels of AcLhcb1.1 gradually up-regulated (Fig. 7B), and exhibited a peak expression at 24 h, with an approximately 4.32-fold increase, indicating that the expressions of AcLhcb1.1 responded to low temperature stress. After exposure to 4℃, both MDA and REL levels in WT significantly increased, while these indictors remained significantly lower in AcLhcb1.1 overexpression lines than those in WT plants (Fig. 7C-D). After undergoing low temperature treatment, the activities of SOD, CAT, and POD in all plants exhibited an increase; however, when compared to WT, the enhancement was more pronounced in transgenic kiwifruit (Fig. 7E-G). These results indicate that overexpression of AcLhcb1.1 alleviated cell membrane damage caused by low temperature stress and improved cold resistance.

Discussion

Lhc proteins, binding with pigments in the thylakoid, serve as antenna proteins to receive, transmit and transform light during plant photosynthesis [29]. Therefore, it inevitably influence chlorophyll metabolism and in responding to various stresses. To explore the potential role of Lhc in kiwifruit, we performed genomic wide identification of AcLhcs from the kiwifruit genome and identified 31 members. Subsequently, through comprehensive analysis, we identified AcLhcb1.1 as putative members involved in pulp coloration. Furthermore, we validated their roles in chlorophyll metabolism during kiwifruit ripening process along with their response to low temperature stress.

Lhc is the most abundant thylakoid membrane protein in nature and compose the peripheral antenna proteins of PSI and PSII photosystems [29]. A total of 21 Lhc members have been identified in Arabidopsis. Recently, castor bean (Ricinus communis), cassava [30], papaya [10], and grape [31] were found to have 28, 35, 28, and 20 Lhc members respectively. In previous study, 42 and 45 Lhc genes were identified in Actinidia chinensis (Ac) and A. eriantha (Ae), which were grouped into four subfamilies (Lhc, Lil, PsbS, and FCII) [15]. In this study, we identified 31 Lhc genes from A. chinensis, including two newly discovered ones: AcLhcb1.9 (Acc25200.1) and AcLhcb1.5 (Acc21442.1), due to using different E-value thresholds for hmmer and blast searches. This number is higher than those reported in peach, apple and pear, indicating their important role in kiwifruit [32, 33]. Besides, motif analysis showed that 31 identified AcLhcs contain conserved domains, and Motif 1, Motif 2, and Motif 5 or Motif 7 are possessed by most of the AcLhc family members. In addition, members of different subfamilies contain distinct conserved motifs, and members of the same branch of the evolutionary tree have similar ones. These results indicate that the AcLhcs family is highly conserved.

Lhcbs play a crucial role in the regulation of chlorophyll levels in plants. Downregulation of Lhcs, such as CsLHCB1 and CsLHCB5, has been closely associated with abnormal chloroplast development in yellow-leaf and albino tea plants [34, 35]. The involvement of AcLhcb3.1/3.2 in controlling chlorophyll a content has been confirmed in kiwifruit [15]. In this study, transcriptomic analysis showed that AcLhcb1.1 were significantly differentially expressed in green-fleshed and yellow-fleshed cultivars of kiwifruit during fruit development, thus speculating that they are involved in chlorophyll degradation during fruit development. By transient overexpression in tobacoo leaves and stable overexpression in kiwifruit, it was confirmed that AcLhcb1.1 influences chlorophyll content. Similar results were observed when tea CsLhc overexpression in Arabidopsis Lhcb mutants, promoted chlorophyll accumulation by increasing the expression level of genes related to chlorophyll biosynthesis and kept the leaves green [36]. Overexpression of MdLhcb4.3 from apple also enhanced chlorophyll content in Arabidopsis [33]. Knockout mutations of AtLhcb6, AtLhcb5, and AtLhcb4 showed significantly lower chlorophyll content in Arabidopsis [37].

Furthermore, the dynamic balance of chlorophyll is closely related to the synthesis, degradation and maintenance of chlorophyll as well as the disintegration and assembly of PSI and PSII complexes [38]. Lhcbs modulate chlorophyll content by regulating the expression of chlorophyll metabolism genes. qRT-PCR results demonstrated that overexpression of AcLhcb1.1 in transgenic kiwifruit significantly upregulated the gene expression of upstream chlorophyll degradation pathway genes, AcSGR, AcNYC1 and AcHCAR, while downregulating the expression of downstream genes such as AcPPH and AcPAO. AcNYC1 encodes a chlorophyll b reductase responsible for catalyzing the degradation of chlorophyll b and maintaining the stability of the photosystem [39]. AcSGR forms a CCE multi-protein complex with LhcII on the thylakoid membrane to activate chlorophyll degradation mainly during leaf senescence [16, 40, 41]. Recent increasing evidence have confirmed a positive correlation between Lhcb expression level and chlorophyll content [42], which is consist with the observed downregulated AcPPH and AcPAO in AcLhcb1.1 overexpression kiwifruit lines. This suggest that PPH and PAO play a crucial role in maintaining chlorophyll balance during developmental stages, while SGR primarily contributes to chlorophyll degradation during leaf senescence [16, 40]. Notably, during kiwifruit ripening process, there is a decrease in Lhcb expression accompanied by an increase in SGR expression leading to reduced chlorophyll content [43].

Chlorophyll a/b binding proteins in thylakoid membranes can affect multiple biological pathways and metabolic processes through photosynthesis [44–46]. We have identified a variety of hormone and stress-related cis-elements in the promoter region of the AcLhcs (Fig. 3), including those associated with light, low temperature, drought, auxin, gibberellin, abscisic acid and other factors. Previous studies have reported that hormonal and abiotic stresses induce the expression of Lhcs. For example, salt, drought and low temperature downregulated the expression of most Lhcbs [34, 42]. ABA was required for expression of different Lhcb members [14, 47, 48]. Overexpression of alfredii SaLhcb2 in tobacco led to higher bud biomass accumulation and higher Cd2+ uptake [24]. A recent study demonstrated that the silencing of TaLhc86 by BSMV-VIGS severely impaired the photosynthetic rate and electron transport, and reduced wheat tolerance to salt [36]. Wheat TaLhc2 negatively impacts host immunity and functions as a susceptible gene, meanwhile it was down-regulated by drought and salt stresses [42]. Meanwhile, many studies have shown that the Lhc genes of pomegranate, ryegrass and other species could respond to the cold stress of plants [49, 50]. In this study, transgenic kiwifruit plants expressing AcLhcb1.1 exhibited significantly reduced leaf damage after low temperature treatment compared to WT, accompanied by lower levels of MDA and REL and enhanced activity of SOD, CAT, and POD, suggesting stronger stress resistance in transgenic kiwifruit. Similar results were observed when overexpression of MdLhcb4.3 conferred enhanced resistance to drought and osmotic stress on apple callus and transgenic Arabidopsis plants [33]. Additionally, LHCB proteins play positive roles during these developmental processes through ABA signaling pathways. The WRKY-domain transcription factor WRKY40 was identified as an ABA-responsive regulator that represses LHCB expression to balance the positive function exerted by LHCBs during ABA signaling [51]. Perhaps, Lhcb protein is also regulated by some signaling pathway or some transcription factor to respond to low temperature and function.

Conclusion

AcLhcb1.1 was identified from transcriptome data of yellow- and green-fleshed kiwifruit cultivars. Their expression levels showed a significant positive correlation with chlorophyll content during fruit development. Overexpression of AcLhcb1.1 in tobacco and kiwifruit seedlings resulted in enhanced chlorophyll content by modulating the expression of chlorophyll degrading genes, AcPPH and AcPAO. Moreover, transgenic kiwifruit plants carrying AcLhcb1.1 exhibited reduced accumulation of malondialdehyde and relative electrolyte leakage under low temperature stress conditions, along with elevated activities of SOD, CAT, and POD compared to WT counterparts, indicating that they possess improved stress resistance capabilities. These findings provide valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying coloration in kiwifruit as well as potential applications for breeding resistant varieties.
