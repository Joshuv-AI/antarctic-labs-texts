Integrated Phytochemical and Pharmacological Assessment of Uraria rufescens Leaves: Insights Into Its Antioxidant, Thrombolytic and CNS Activities
Authors: Ayesha Siddika Farha, Md Abdus Samadd, Nahida Akter, Md Sifat Foysal, Md Riaz Hossain, Md Jahid Hossain, Tanvir Hasan, Choudhury Mahmood Hasan, Syed Mohammed Tareq, Mohammed Aktar Sayeed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Uraria rufescens is traditionally used in Bangladesh for fever and inflammation, yet its phyto‐pharmacological profile was not explored adequately. This investigation exerted the phytochemical composition and pharmacological activities of its leaves methanolic extractives (URME), focusing on in vitro antioxidant and thrombolytic, along with in vivo anxiolytic, antidepressant and analgesic effects. Phytochemical screening was conducted via various chemical tests, whereas secondary molecules were isolated using chromatographic techniques and identified by 1H NMR spectroscopy. Antioxidant activity was evaluated by DPPH (2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl) radical scavenging, total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC). Thrombolytic activity was determined by clot lysis. In vivo studies assessed antidepressant effects through forced swim test (FST) and tail suspension test (TST), anxiolytic activity by the elevated plus maze (EPM) and hole‐board test (HBT), and analgesic effects by acetic acid‐induced writhing and formalin‐induced paw licking models. Screening confirmed the presence of alkaloids, flavonoids, glycosides, phenols, steroids and terpenoids. Seven compounds were isolated and identified for the first time from U. rufescens leaves: trans‐sinapic acid 1, fisetin 2, oleanolic acid 3, aesculin 4, taraxerone 5, sitogluside 6 and syringaresinol 7. The ethyl acetate fraction showed strong antioxidant activity (IC50 = 32.78 µg/mL), whereas the chloroform fraction had the highest TPC (742.24 mg GAE/g) and TFC (703.15 mg QE/g). The aqueous fraction demonstrated notable thrombolytic activity (52.03%). URME significantly reduced immobility in FST and TST, indicating strong antidepressant effects. In EPM and HBT, URME moderately enhanced open‐arm time (41.8 s) and head dips (36.8 s). URME also produced dose‐dependent analgesic activity in both central and peripheral models. The plant contains various phenolic compounds and exhibits strong antioxidant and thrombolytic activities, as well as dose‐dependent antidepressant and analgesic effects with moderate anxiolytic potential. Further studies are needed to isolate bioactive molecules and clarify mechanisms.Keywords: Chromatographic separation, In vitro, In vivo, Phytochemical screening, Uraria rufescens

Introduction

The global prevalence of anxiety, depression, pain, oxidative stress‐related disorders and thrombotic diseases underscores the urgent need for safer and more effective therapeutics. Conventional treatments for mental disorders and pain, such as antidepressants, benzodiazepines, opioids and NSAIDs, are often limited by side effects, dependency and poor long‐term efficacy [1, 2]. Oxidative stress plays a critical role in the progression of chronic diseases, yet synthetic antioxidants frequently pose toxicity risks [3]. Likewise, thrombolytic agents like streptokinase and t‐PA, though effective, carry serious bleeding risks and contraindications [4]. These limitations ultimately urge the scientist to explore of natural compounds, particularly from medicinal plants, which offer multi‐target effects with improved safety profiles [5, 6].

Phyto‐medicines have several pharmacologically active molecules, such as tannins, steroids, carbohydrates, alkaloids, terpenoids and flavonoids [7], which have widely been used from the ancient times [7, 8]. Among the various medicinal plants, the Uraria genus is well known for its significant phytochemicals and bioactivity. For example, U. lagopodies and U. lobata are traditionally well known for antidiabetic, antioxidant, antibacterial, immunomodulatory and hepatoprotective properties in Bangladesh. Similarly, other species of this genus, such as Uraria rufescens, are also significant because of the presence of bioactive phyto‐molecules such as flavonoids, polyphenols and sterols in the roots and stems [9], while one previous study revealed the antioxidant efficacy [10].

Now, the target of ongoing investigations is to find out the types of phytochemical presence and isolate the secondary metabolites in the leaves of U. rufescens. Furthermore, this study will extend to assess the in vivo analgesic, anxiolytic and antidepressant activity, as well as the in vitro antioxidant and thrombolytic activity of this plant. Although a few published works have focused on the roots and stems of this plant, as per our knowledge, it is the first study of U. rufescens leaves.

Methods and Materials

Reagents and Chemicals

All chemicals and reagents used in this study were analytical grade level. Methanol, n‐hexane, chloroform, ethyl acetate, distilled water, aluminium chloride (AlCl3, anhydrous), sodium carbonate (Na2CO3), sulphuric acid (H2SO4), formalin and acetic acid were obtained from Taj Scientific & Chemicals Ltd. (Chittagong, Bangladesh). DPPH (2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl), Tween‐80 and ascorbic acid were purchased from Merck (Germany). Standard drugs, including diclofenac sodium, morphine, fluoxetine, and diazepam, were generously provided by Square Pharmaceuticals Ltd. (Dhaka, Bangladesh). All chemicals and reagents were ≥99% pure and used without further purification.

Plant Collection and Extract Preparation

The U. rufescens leaves were verified by a taxonomist from the Department of Botany, University of Chittagong (Accession number: SB‐05062022‐19) (Figure S1). Plant materials were collected in July 2022 from Hazarikhil Wildlife Sanctuary in Chittagong, Bangladesh. The investigation exclusively utilized freshly collected leaves of U. rufescens and shade‐dried at room temperature (∼25–30°C) for 7 days to preserve thermolabile constituents after cleaning the unwanted substances. Subsequently, they were fragmented into smaller segments through grinding (NOWAKE, Japan). The powder (350 g) was dissolved in methanol (2 L, 99%, 25°C) for 15 days with frequent shaking. The sample was then passed through a cotton plug and filtered using Whatman filter paper. The methanolic extract of U. rufescens leaves (URME) was evaporated at 50°C under reduced pressure (Buchi Rota‐evaporator). The resultant extract was weighed 14.39 g and stored at 4°C until further used.

Fractionation

The URME was subjected to the fractioning through modified Kupchan partitioning technique [11, 12] to obtain four fractions of varying polarity. Initially, 5 g of URME were dissolved in 10% aqueous methanol and sequentially partitioned with n‐hexane (URHF), chloroform (URCF), ethyl acetate (UREA) and water (URAQ). The partitioning process was repeated twice to obtain a sufficient quantity of each fraction. The yields of the fractions were as follows: URHF—3.31 g, URCF—3.68 g, UREA—1.57 g and URAQ—1.28 g.

Phytochemical Screening

URME and it's extractives were underwent a phytochemical screening via several qualitative tests to identify the class of existence phyto‐molecules, including alkaloids, steroids, flavonoids, saponins, glycosides, terpenoids, lignins, carbohydrates, phenols, tannins and resin, as per prior study [13].

Chromatographic Approaches

The URHE and URCF were chosen for the size‐exclusion chromatography (SEC) to generate extensive sub‐fractions that ultimately would be subjected to purify and isolate secondary metabolites. This was carried out using lipophilic Sephadex LH‐20 as the stationary phase and a gradient mixture of solvents as the mobile phase. The mobile phase consisted of the following solvent ratios—n‐hexane:chlorofom:methanol (2:5:1), followed by increasing concentrations of methanol in chloroform: 10%, 20%, 50%, 80% and finally, 100% methanol. At that time, to isolate and purify compounds from the sub‐fractions of URHE and URCF, thin‐layer chromatography (TLC, silica gel‐coated aluminium sheets, 20 × 20 cm2, Kieselgel 60 F254) was extensively employed with various solvent systems (mobile phase). The developed plates were initially examined under UV light at wavelengths of 254 nm (short wave) and 366 nm (long wave) to observe fluorescence quenching. Subsequently, the plates were sprayed with 1% vanillin‐sulphuric acid reagent and heated at 105°C for 2–3 min to reveal color compounds. Finally, repeated preparative TLC was performed to isolate and purify compounds.

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Procedures

The 1H NMR spectra were generated using a Bruker AMX‐400 NMR spectrometer operating at a frequency of 400 MHz. All measurements were conducted in deuterated methanol (CD3OD), and deuterated chloroform (CDCl3), whereas the chemical shift (δ) values were calibrated against tetramethylsilane (TMS) and the residual solvent peaks.

Experimental Animal

Male and female Swiss albino mice (4–5 weeks old, 25–30 g body weight [b.w.]) in healthy condition were procured from ICDDR, B in Dhaka, Bangladesh. Before the experiments, the mice were allowed to the lab conditions (25 ± 2°C, 12‐h light/dark cycle) for 1 week for adjustment. Throughout the research period, subjects received a normal lab diet and were allowed unlimited consumption of clean water. All experimental protocols and animal handling adhered to the ethical standards developed by the Swiss Academy of Medical Sciences (SAMS) and the Swiss Academy of Sciences (SAS). Additionally, the study followed the most recent ARRIVE guidelines [66]. Furthermore, ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Animal Ethical Committee of the International Islamic University Chittagong, Bangladesh (approval number: P&D‐147/13‐18).

Dosing

For the negative control (Group I), a 1% Tween 80 solution had been prepared in normal saline was administered. The test samples (Groups III–IV) were suspended in 1% Tween 80 using a vortex mixer and delivered orally with a specialized feeding needle. In earlier, in vivo studies of plant extracts, dose ranges of 200–400 mg/kg b.w. have been frequently utilized [14, 15]. Moreover, these specific doses were chosen on the basis of preliminary acute toxicity assessments conducted according to OECD guidelines, which revealed no mortality or noticeable toxic effects in mice even at the 400 mg/kg level [16]. Therefore, two doses of URME, 200 and 400 mg/kg, were selected for the present in vivo experiments.

Antioxidant Activity

The antioxidant activity of URME and its derived fractions (URHF, URCF, UREA and URAQ) was evaluated following Brand‐Williams et al.’s (1995) procedure by employing the DPPH free radical scavenging method [67]. Initially, each sample, and the standard (ascorbic acid), was serially diluted from 500 to 0.977 µg/mL. Next, 2 mL of the test samples and standard were mixed with 3 mL of methanolic DPPH solution (20 µg/mL), whereas a methanolic DPPH solution alone served as the negative control. After incubating the mixtures in the dark at 25°C for 30 min, absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a UV spectrophotometer. Total Phenolic Component Analysis

Following the method of Škerget et al. (2005), the total phenolic content (TPC) of URME and its fractions (URHF, URCF, UREA, URAQ) were analysed [6, 68]. In this assay, the Folin–Ciocalteu reagent was used as the oxidant, whereas gallic acid functioned as the standard. For the analysis, test samples (2 mg/mL, 0.5 mL) and varying concentrations of gallic acid were added with 2.5 mL of 10‐fold‐diluted Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, subsequently mixed with 2 mL of 7.5% sodium carbonate. The reaction mixtures were then incubated at 25°C for 20 min. The absorbance at 760 nm was recorded using a UV–visible spectrophotometer, with TPC determined against a gallic acid standard curve. The phenolic content was calculated and expressed as gram gallic acid equivalents per gram of sample (g GAE/g extract).

Determination of Total Flavonoid Content (TFC)

The flavonoid concentration in the plant extract was quantified through an aluminium chloride‐based colorimetric assay, as outlined by Chang et al. [69]. Briefly, 1.0 mL (100 µg/mL) aliquots of URME or its fractions were combined with 1.0 mL of 2% AlCl3 solution (w/v) and 1.0 mL of 120 mM potassium acetate. The mixture was subsequently brought to volume with 2.8 mL of distilled water and permitted to react in darkness for 30 min. Spectrophotometric analysis was performed at 415 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. A quercetin standard curve was generated using serial dilution between 10 and 100 µg/mL. Data were reported as milligram quercetin equivalents per gram dry weight (mg QE/g DW).

Thrombolytic Activity Assay

The clot‐dissolving potential of URME and its derived fractions was examined using an established protocol [5]. Following this methodology, venous blood (15 mL) was obtained from healthy participants who was abstained from blood‐thinning medications or hormonal contraceptives for at least 7 days. Aliquots of 0.5 mL blood were dispensed into sterile, pre‐measured microcentrifuge tubes (1 mL capacity) and allowed to coagulate at 37°C for 45 min. After clot formation, the liquid serum component was carefully decanted, and the remaining clot mass was determined by differential weighing (clot weight = total tube weight—empty tube weight). To evaluate thrombolytic effects, three treatments were applied: 100 µL of sterile water (negative control), 100 µL streptokinase (30,000 IU, positive control) and 1 mg/mL test extractives. Anti‐Anxiety Property

Elevated Plus Maze (EPM) Test

The EPM test was conducted to assess the anxiolytic‐like activity of URME, following the method described by Montakim et al. [17]. The maze was constructed with two opposing open arms (35 × 5 cm2) and two enclosed arms (35 × 20 cm2), all extending perpendicularly from a central square platform (5 × 5 cm2). The maze was elevated 25 cm above the floor to induce a mildly aversive environment, promoting natural avoidance behaviour in mice. For comparison, the standard control group was treated with diazepam (1 mg/kg b.w. dose, administered intraperitoneally (i.p.)). All treatments were applied 60 min prior to the behavioural assessment. Each mouse was individually positioned on the central platform oriented towards one of the open arms, and its behaviour was recorded over a 5‐min observation period. The primary parameter measured was the per cent time spent in the open arms, which serves as an index of anxiolytic activity [17]:

Hole‐Board Test (HBT)

This test was conducted to evaluate the exploratory behaviour and potential anxiolytic activity of URME [18]. The apparatus consisted of a wooden board measuring 20 × 40 cm2, fitted with 16 evenly spaced holes. The board was elevated 15 cm above the ground using four wooden corner posts, as described by Babar et al. [18]. Group II received an intraperitoneal administration of diazepam (1 mg/kg b.w.). About 30 min after administering the respective treatments, mice from each group were placed individually on the hole‐board apparatus. Exploratory behaviour was recorded for 5 min, during which the number of head dips (nose pokes into the holes at eye level) was manually counted using a tally counter. Each group underwent a single trial per day, and the test was repeated once daily for three consecutive days to evaluate behavioural consistency and treatment effects.

Tail Suspension Test (TST)

The TST was performed to determine the antidepressant‐like effects of URME [19]. Group II (positive control) received fluoxetine 10 mg/kg b.w. [19]. One‐hour post‐treatment, individual mice were suspended by the tail using adhesive tape placed approximately 1 cm from the tail tip and suspended from a horizontal bar so that the animal hung freely without touching any surfaces. The total duration of immobility time (DIT) and percentage of immobility time (PIT) were calculated until mice ceased struggling and remained motionless, hanging passively. Following the completion of the behavioural assessment, all animals were euthanized under diethyl ether anaesthesia as per guidelines.

Analgesic Properties

Acetic Acid–Induced Writhing Test

The URME was assessed for analgesic properties using the acetic acid–induced writhing test in mice, following the established protocol of Adnan et al. [20]. Group II received diclofenac Na (50 mg/kg b.w, i.p.). Rest, all treatments were administered orally. URME was administered 30 min prior to test, whereas the standard was given 15 min before the acetic acid injection. To induce nociception, mice were administered 0.1 mL acetic acid (1% w/v, i.p.). Formalin‐Induced Paw‐Licking Test

To induce pain, mice were subcutaneously injected (in their right hind paws [20 µL]) with a 2.5% v/v formalin solution [20]. Group II received the standard drug morphine 5 mg/kg b.w. All treatments were administered orally. URME was administered 30 min prior to test, whereas morphine was given 15 min before the formalin solution injection. To induce nociception, mice were administered 0.1 mL acetic acid (1% w/v, i.p.). Five minutes post‐injection, animals were examined for 25 min, and the inhibition of paw licking time recorded.

Statistical Analysis

All experimental data are denoted as mean values ± SEM (standard error of the mean). Statistical analyses were conducted using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett's post‐test for multiple comparisons, implemented through SPSS statistical software (version 25.0). A probability value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Graphical representations of the results were generated utilizing Microsoft Excel (2016 version).

Result

Phytochemical Screening

The qualitative phytochemical analysis of the extractives of URME, URHE, URCF and UREA revealed the presence of various classes of bioactive constituents (Table 1). Alkaloids and glycosides were abundantly present in all extracts, with intense reactions (+++) observed in URME and URHE. Steroids were strongly detected in URME and URHE (+++), whereas their presence was less marked in URCF and UREA (+). Tannins and resins were absent in all tested fractions. Flavonoids were prominently present in URCF (+++), moderately in URME and UREA (++), and weakly in URHE (+). Carbohydrates showed a strong presence in URME (+++), followed by moderate to low presence in the other fractions. Saponins and terpenoids exhibited moderate presence in URME and URCF, whereas their presence varied in the remaining fractions. Phenols were strongly expressed in all fractions except UREA. Lignins were moderately present in URME (++), weakly in URHE (+) and absent in URCF and UREA (Table 1).

Compound 1: The 1H NMR spectrum (CDCl3, 400 MHz) exhibited characteristic signals of cinnamic acid derivative. A singlet at δ 3.71, which was integrated for six protons, indicated the presence of two equivalent methoxy groups. The aromatic region showed a singlet at δ 6.90 integrating for two protons, corresponding to H‐2 and H‐6 of the 1,3,4,5‐tetrasubstituted benzene ring. The trans‐olefinic protons were clearly resolved: H‐1′ appeared as a doublet at δ 7.41 (J = 16.4 Hz) and H‐2′ as a doublet at δ 6.47 (J = 15.8 Hz). The large coupling constants confirm the trans‐configuration of the α,β‐unsaturated system, typical of the cinnamic acid moiety (Table 2). Moreover, after carefully matching with previous study, the Compound 1 was detected as sinapic acid, which is a cinnamic acid derivatives [21].

Compound 2: The 1H NMR spectrum (CDCl3, 400 MHz) exhibited multiplet signals between δ 6.79 and 6.83, integrating for three protons (H‐5, H‐6, H‐7), characteristic of a trisubstituted A‐ring in the flavonol core. In the B‐ring region, H‐2′ appeared as a doublet at δ 7.61 (J = 2.2 Hz), indicating meta coupling, whereas H‐3′ appeared as a doublet of doublets at δ 7.53 (J = 2.5, 8.2 Hz), reflecting both meta and ortho couplings. H‐6′ was observed as a doublet at δ 7.84 (J = 9.1 Hz), consistent with ortho coupling, confirming the 1,3,4‐trisubstitution pattern of the B‐ring (Table 2). After matching and comparison with previous literature, Compound 2 was detected as a flavonol molecule name fisetin [22].

Compound 3: The 1H NMR spectrum (400 MHz, CDCl3) displayed six singlet which may correspond to six tertiary methyl groups (a total of 18 protons) between δ 0.71 and 1.07, a characteristic of a pentacyclic triterpenoid skeleton. The proton H‐18 appeared as a doublet of doublets at δ 2.84 (J = 4.4, 13.1 Hz), whereas the H‐3 methine proton was observed as a double doublet at δ 3.22 (J = 10.5, 4.3 Hz), indicating a hydroxyl group's existence. Moreover, an olefinic proton signal at δ 5.26 (1H, t) was attributed to H‐12, confirming the presence of a double bond (Table 2). Roughly, a similar NMR value was also reported in prior literature, which ultimately denoted Compound 3 as oleanolic acid, a pentacyclic triterpenoid [23].

Compound 4: The 1H NMR spectrum (CD3OD, 400 MHz) revealed two typical α,β‐trans‐unsaturation protons as doublets: H‐3 at δ 6.21 (J = 9.6 Hz) and H‐4 at δ 7.67 (J = 9.6 Hz), indicating a coumarin ring. Two additional aromatic protons were observed as singlet at δ 6.87 (H‐5) and δ 6.79 (H‐8), consistent with di‐substitution at C‐6 and C‐7. Contrarily, an anomeric proton signal at δ 5.00 (1H, d, J = 7.6 Hz, H‐1′) might indicate a β‐glycosidic linkage, whereas a multiplet between δ 3.20 and 3.90 integrating for six protons is attributed to the sugar protons H‐2′–H‐6′ (presence of a β‐glucoside moiety) (Table 2). According to the NMR spectra value and comparison with the previous data [70], Compound 4 was detected as a coumarin glycoside, name aesculetin‐6‐O‐glucoside (aesculin).

Compound 5: The 1H NMR spectrum (400 MHz, CDCl3) displayed a characteristic olefinic proton at δ 5.52 (1H, dd, J = 8.2, 3.2 Hz, H‐15), confirming the presence of an exocyclic double bond typical of taraxerone‐type triterpenes. Two methylene protons at C‐3 were observed at δ 2.49 (1H, m, H‐2b) and δ 2.28 (1H, ddd, J = 15.9, 6.4, 3.3 Hz, H‐2a), showing a splitting due to geminal and vicinal couplings. Moreover, the eight tertiary methyl groups (24 protons) resonated as singlets between δ 1.08 and 0.76, integrating for 24 protons, consistent with a highly substituted pentacyclic triterpenoid framework (Table 2). Furthermore, existence literature of 1H‐NMR spectra indicated that Compound 5 is a pentacyclic terpenoid name taraxerone [24].

Compound 6: The 1H NMR spectrum (400 MHz, CD3OD) showed a signal at δ 3.80 (1H, m) indicating a hydroxylation at C‐3 position, which might corresponding to H‐3 of the aglycone. The olefinic proton H‐6 appeared as a multiplet at δ 5.6 (1H, m), one of the key distinctive of triterpenoid skeleton. Additionally, the spectrum also exhibited a substituted steroidal nucleus through the presence of six methyl singlet between δ 1.02 and 0.71 (18H total). Conversely, the glucose moiety with anomeric proton was confirmed by a multiplet between δ 4.37 and 3.15, suggesting the presence of a β‐linked glucoside (Table 2). In comparison with the previously recorded 1H NMR spectrum, the phyto‐molecules was detected as beta‐Sitosterol d‐glucoside which is also known by sitogluside [25].

Compound 7: The 1H NMR spectrum (400 MHz, CDCl3) exhibited two aromatic singlets at δ 6.55 (4H, s) corresponding to protons H‐2/H‐6 and H‐2′/H‐6′, indicating symmetrically 1,3,4,5‐tetrasubstituted aromatic rings. The oxygenated methine protons H‐7 and H‐7′ appeared as doublets at δ 4.68 (J = 3.9 Hz), indicating coupling with H‐8 and H‐8′, which were observed as multiplets at δ 3.09–3.02. The benzylic methylene protons H‐9 and H‐9′ appeared as multiplets at δ 4.24–4.17 and δ 3.90–3.79. A singlet at δ 3.81 integrating for 12 protons corresponded to four methoxy groups, confirming the syringyl substitution pattern. These spectral features are consistent with the structure of syringaresinol, a symmetrical dibenzyl tetrahydrofuran–type lignin (Table 2). Furthermore, comparison with reported 1H NMR data in the literature confirmed the identity of Compound 7 as syringaresinol [26].

The DPPH‐free radical scavenging activity of the tested samples was evaluated, where the ascorbic acid (standard) exhibited the strongest antioxidant activity (IC50 = 16.41 µg/mL) (Figure 1). Among the tested samples, URAQ demonstrated the weakest antioxidant activity (IC50 = 172.90 µg/mL). Discussion

In the past few years, constituents from botanical origins have drawn global interest due to their extensive range of therapeutic benefits, including thrombolytic, anticancer, antioxidant, and so on. As a result, plant‐based molecules are increasingly recognized as a rich reservoir of potential pharmaceutical agents, aiming to discover alternative treatments with enhanced potency and fewer adverse consequences [8, 14, 15].

The present study explored the phytochemical composition and pharmacological properties of URME and its respective fractions. Qualitative and chromatographic analysis confirmed the presence of diverse phytochemicals, including alkaloids, glycosides, steroids, phenols, and flavonoids, which are known for a broad range of pharmacological effects. Moreover, column chromatography with 1H‐NMR spectrum purified and detected seven different phyto‐molecules, including trans‐sinapic acid 1, fisetin 2, oleanolic acid 3, aesculin 4, taraxerone 5, sitogluside 6, and syringaresinol 7. After the extensive literature searching and as per our conscience, these molecules are reported for the first time from U. rufescens leaves.

Oxidative stress is the key pathogen of numerous diseases and metabolic disorder [7, 8, 14]. The presence of phenolic groups and fewer toxicity, plant‐based antioxidant attract the attention through the worldwide [14]. To find out the antioxidant potential of U. rufescens, the current study performed three different antioxidant experiment such as DPPH free radical scavenging, TPC and TFC. In the DPPH assay, URME and UREA demonstrated a strong free radical scavenging activity with an IC50 of 38.42 µg/mL, and 32.78 µg/mL, respectively. Contrary, the high content of TPC, and TFC, particularly in the URCF (TPC = 742.24 mg GAE/g and TFC = 703.15 mg QE/g) suggests the extensive antioxidant property of U. rufescens. The antioxidant activity of UREA may not be solely explained by its relatively similar levels of TPC (211.22 mg GAE/g) and TFC (226.78 mg QE/g). The results of the DPPH radical scavenging assay (IC50 = 32.78 µg/mL) suggest the probable presence of other antioxidant constituents. Moreover, according to the previous experiment, the isolated every phyto constituents 1–7 has strong antioxidant profile, such as sitogluside 6 (IC50 = 27.3 ± 0.015 µg/mL) [28], sinapic acid 1 (IC50 = 0.02–0.5 mM) [29], fisetin 2 (IC50 = 6.08 ± 0.12 µg/mL) [30], syringaresinol 7 (EC50 = 10.77 mg/mL) [31], oleanolic acid 3 (IC50 = 61.5 µg/mL) [32]. Additionally, the detected phytoconstituents class, such as flavonoids and phenolic acids, possesses well‐documented redox properties [33]. Further investigation is needed to find out more anti‐oxidant molecules or any new compounds that may be responsible for such strong antioxidant activity.

Thromboembolic disorders are among the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, often resulting in severe clinical events such as stroke, myocardial infarction and pulmonary embolism [34, 35]. Although synthetic thrombolytic agents are widely used in clinical practice, their utility is often limited by complications such as haemorrhage, allergic reactions and high cost [36, 37, 38]. Consequently, safer and more accessible plant‐based thrombolytics are gaining attention. In the present study, U. rufescens all extractives demonstrated promising thrombolytic activity. The isolated compounds such as oleanolic acid 3, a pentacyclic triterpenoid with known antiplatelet and vasodilatory effects [39, 40] and sinapic acid 1, a phenolic acid with antioxidant and endothelial‐stabilizing properties [41, 42] likely contribute to the thrombolytic potential. Fisetin 2 and syringaresinol 7 both reported to inhibit platelet aggregation and oxidative stress [43, 44]. Sitogluside 6, a plant glycoside, is also suggested to have anticoagulant effects [45]. However, phyto‐molecules isolation was performed for only URHF, and URCF fractions, the most active fractions URAQ and UREA (52.03% ± 5.02% and 43.38% ± 3.27%, respectively) were not subjected to compounds’ isolation and purification purpose. Further phytochemical study of the active fractions is needed for mechanistic studies, and in vivo validation investigations.

Anxiety and depressive disorders are among the most prevalent neuropsychiatric conditions globally, often managed with pharmacological agents that can lead to tolerance, dependence or adverse side effects [46]. In this context, natural products with CNS activity and improved safety profiles have become a focus of neuropharmacological research [47]. In the present study, URME exhibited significant anxiolytic‐like effects, particularly at a dose of 400 mg/kg, as evidenced by increased time spent in the open arms of the EPM and enhanced head‐dipping behaviour in the HBT. These behavioural changes are indicative of reduced anxiety‐like responses. Moreover, URME showed notable antidepressant‐like effects in both the FST and TST, further supports its CNS‐modulating potential. The observed anxiolytic and antidepressant activities may be attributed to the presence of bioactive constituents, including flavonoids, alkaloids and other phytochemicals capable of modulating central neurotransmission. Specifically, flavonoids and certain alkaloids are known to interact with GABAA receptors and serotonergic (5‐HT) pathways, which play pivotal roles in anxiety and depression regulation [48, 49, 50, 51]. Previous reports have confirmed that specific flavonoids can cross the blood–brain barrier and exert benzodiazepine‐like effects by binding to the GABAA receptor complex, often without inducing dependence [71]. Compounds identified in U. rufescens, such as fisetin 2, syringaresinol 7, aesculin 4 and sinapic acid 1, have been independently associated with neuroprotective and anxiolytic or antidepressant activities through various direct or indirect pathways [52, 53, 54, 55]. Additionally, the presence of taraxerone, a triterpenoid and oleanolic acid, known to exhibit neuroprotective and anti‐stress effects, may further contribute to the extract's CNS activity. Collectively, these results position U. rufescens as a promising candidate for the development of plant‐based anxiolytic and antidepressant agents. Future studies focusing on receptor binding assays, neurotransmitter level analysis and long‐term behavioural assessments are essential to validate the therapeutic potential and clarify the precise mechanisms underlying its CNS effects.

Depression and chronic pain often coexist, sharing overlapping neurochemical pathways such as dysregulation of serotonergic and noradrenergic systems. Both conditions are also linked to chronic inflammation and oxidative stress [56, 57, 58]. In the present study, URME showed prominent antidepressant‐like effects. Parallelly, URME exhibited significant analgesic activity in both the acetic acid‐induced writhing and formalin‐induced paw‐licking tests. In the acetic acid model, a reduction in the number of writhes suggests peripheral analgesic action, likely due to inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis. In the formalin test, URME effectively suppressed nociceptive behaviour in both the early neurogenic phase and the late inflammatory phase, indicating involvement of both central and peripheral mechanisms. These effects are consistent with the phytochemical composition of the extract. Identified compounds, such as oleanolic acid 3, taraxerone 5 and sitogluside 6, possess anti‐inflammatory and analgesic properties through modulation of cyclooxygenase (COX) pathways and neuronal pain signalling [59, 60, 61, 62]. Similarly, the presence of alkaloids, terpenoids and steroids in the extract are well‐known to act on opioid and adrenergic receptors, potentially enhancing pain threshold and reducing inflammatory responses [63, 64, 65]. The concurrent antidepressant and analgesic activities of URME underscore its possible role in managing comorbid depression and chronic pain. Future research should focus on bioassay‐guided fractionation, mechanistic exploration through receptor binding and neurotransmitter assays, and comprehensive in vivo modelling to establish its clinical relevance and safety profile.

Study Limitations and Future Directions

Despite promising results, this study has some limitations. The use of mouse models limits direct translatability to humans, and specific routes of administration were tested, which ultimately restricted the insight of dose–response and pharmacokinetics. The investigation was restricted to the two extract concentrations, which ultimately constrains the accurate characterization of a dose–response relationship. Another limitation was the lack of in vivo toxicity and safety assessments, as the selected dosages were considered on the basis of the prior literature of plant‐based extracts. Moreover, the behavioural assessments were short‐term and covered a narrow range of tests, whereas active fractions were not fully isolated or mechanistically characterized. Future work should therefore include dose–response and long‐term studies, broader neuropharmacological evaluations, mechanistic analyses and clinical validation of U. rufescens extract. However, this study provides the first in vivo evidence of the neuropharmacological and antioxidant activities of U. rufescens’s leaves. The purpose of our study was to generate baseline pharmacological data and to highlight the therapeutic potential of the extract, which can guide future research into specific bioactive compounds, mechanisms of action and translational studies.

Conclusion

The study demonstrated that the different fractions of U. rufescens’s methanolic leaves extract possess a diverse array of phytochemical constituents, with alkaloids, glycosides, flavonoids, phenols and terpenoids. A total of seven bioactive phytomolecules were isolated and structurally identified, including trans‐sinapic acid 1, fisetin 2, oleanolic acid 3, aesculin 4, taraxerone 5, sitogluside 6 and syringaresinol 7, through the extensive chromatographic techniques and NMR spectra which are known for potent pharmacological effects. In vitro antioxidant assays revealed that UREA and URME showed the most significant DPPH radical scavenging activity. URCF also demonstrated the highest flavonoid and phenolic content. URAQ displayed superior thrombolytic activity. In vivo pharmacological evaluations showed that Groups III and IV animals (treated with extract samples) exhibited substantial antidepressant‐like behaviour, as evidenced by significantly reduced immobility time in both tail suspension and FSTs. Anxiolytic effects were also noted, with increased open‐arm exploration time in the EPM and moderate increases in head‐dipping behaviour in the HBT, particularly at 400 mg/kg doses. Furthermore, both doses (200 and 400 mg/kg) demonstrated significant analgesic effects in the acetic acid‐induced writhing and formalin‐induced paw licking models, whereas 400 mg/kg showing more pronounced activity, indicating dose‐dependent efficacy. These findings collectively suggest that fractions and extracts of U. rufescens have the potential to be developed or considered the source of new therapeutic agents.
