The injectable cationic double-crosslinked hydrogel facilitates bone regeneration and repair through sustained antibacterial activity
Authors: Yimeng Xie, Yuedi Yu, Zhengwei Cai, Jian Sun, Wei Bi, Fei Yang, Qianrong Zhou, Xingwen Wu, Ruixue Li, Keshi Zhu, Youcheng Yu, Wenguo Cui, Wenjuan Liu, Yang Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Due to the barrier effect of dental plaque and the growing issue of antibiotic resistance, the efficacy of conventional antimicrobial agents against oral pathogens has progressively declined. Chronic periodontal infections are recalcitrant to eradication and exert inhibitory effects on bone repair and regeneration, representing one of the primary challenges in the management of periodontitis-induced bone defects. In this study, we developed an injectable cationic photopolymerizable dual-crosslinked O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL hydrogel. The aldehyde groups on oxidized HAMA undergo a Schiff base reaction with the amino groups of ε-polylysine (ε-PLL), forming dynamic crosslinks. Subsequently, blue light triggers a radical polymerization of the double bonds present in GelMA and O-HAMA, creating a secondary layer of covalent crosslinks. This dual-crosslinked structure enables the sustained release of the cationic antimicrobial peptide ε-PLL for up to 14 days, demonstrating superior controlled-release properties. Furthermore, the hydrogel exhibits excellent biocompatibility and enhanced mechanical strength, thus fulfilling the requirements for clinical applications. The in vivo bone repair efficacy was assessed using rat cranial defect and periodontitis models. The results indicated that the O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL hydrogel improves the inflammatory microenvironment through sustained antibacterial activity and promotes bone tissue regeneration. This strategy offers strong antibacterial performance and shows promising potential for the repair of alveolar bone defects caused by periodontitis.

Introduction

Clinically, periodontitis and peri-implantitis, primarily caused by dental plaque and dysregulated host inflammatory responses, often result in alveolar bone resorption and destruction. The presence of bacterial microorganisms further exacerbates alveolar bone defects through surface-associated virulence factors and metabolic byproducts [1]. Due to the complex interplay of biological, mechanical, and chemical factors within the oral microbiome, the treatment and regeneration of alveolar bone defects remain challenging. On one hand, bacteria adhere to bio-scaffolds or bone surfaces, forming biofilms. These biofilms protect the bacteria from antibiotics, antibodies, and immune cells, resulting in persistent local chronic infections that are difficult to resolve. On the other hand, bacteria compete with osteoblasts for adhesion sites, diverting the host's response from bone healing processes to inflammatory and antibacterial mechanisms, thereby inhibiting bone repair and regeneration [2]. Currently, systemic or local administration of antibiotics is commonly employed to mitigate bacterial infections. However, the barrier effect of biofilms, such as dental plaque, can impede antibiotic penetration, resulting in inadequate local drug concentrations. For instance, after Porphyromonas gingivalis colonizes dental plaque, its susceptibility to antibiotics, including chlorhexidine, minocycline, and metronidazole, is markedly reduced [3]. Bacterial susceptibility to antibiotics can be reduced by up to 500-fold within biofilms [4]. Furthermore, the single-target mechanism of conventional antibiotics can promote bacterial mutations, and the use of high-dose antibiotics often leads to the development of multidrug resistance.

Recent studies have increasingly focused on utilizing nanomedicine carriers to deliver antibiotics for the elimination of infections. While carrier materials such as liposomes and polycaprolactone nanospheres can enhance the antibacterial efficacy of antibiotics to some extent, these materials exhibit limited biofilm penetration and are ineffective in eradicating biofilm-associated infections. We have observed that the incorporation of cationic antimicrobial peptides into biomaterials imparts direct and potent antibacterial activity against a wide range of microorganisms through multiple mechanisms, including contact killing. Cationic antimicrobial peptides target diverse molecular pathways, resulting in enhanced anti-biofilm efficacy, broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, and bactericidal effects against multidrug-resistant bacteria [5]. In translational medicine, cationic antimicrobial peptides have emerged as promising therapeutic candidates, demonstrating increasing significance [6].

ε-Polylysine (ε-PLL), a natural broad-spectrum antimicrobial cationic peptide, is a water-soluble homopolymer formed by the polymerization of L-lysine monomers. It exhibits cationic properties in physiological environments due to the protonation of free amines on its side chains [7]. Notably, ε-PLL has the potential to rapidly accumulate, penetrate, and persist within biofilms, effectively disrupting their structure and inhibiting further biofilm formation [8,9]. Liu et al. incorporated ε-PLL and funme peptide as active antimicrobial agents in toothpaste. Oral microbial analysis revealed a reduction in the abundance of specific pathogenic bacteria in patients with gingivitis. Clinical trials demonstrated its significant efficacy in reducing dental plaque biofilms, alleviating halitosis, and mitigating gingival bleeding [10]. ε-PLL adsorbs onto bacterial surfaces via electrostatic interactions, disrupting cell membrane integrity and interfering with cellular metabolism. Shen et al. demonstrated, through a randomized controlled trial, that mouthwash containing ε-PLL exhibited effective inhibitory and bactericidal effects against oral pathogens, including Porphyromonas gingivalis, Prevotella bivia, Streptococcus mutans, and Actinobacillus oralis, without compromising the viability of human gingival fibroblasts [11]. ε-PLL demonstrates rapid bactericidal activity against both antibiotic-resistant Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, with a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for bacterial growth lower than 100 μg/mL [12]. Additionally, this cationic polymer is classified by the FDA as “Generally Recognized As Safe” (GRAS) and exhibits excellent biocompatibility.

The extracellular matrix (ECM) is a tissue-specific nanofibrous platform that serves as a prototype for materials designed to promote bone development and facilitate tissue regeneration. Methacrylate-based gelatin (GelMA) hydrogels, which structurally resemble the ECM, are commonly used as scaffold materials to mimic the bone microenvironment. As a dual-bond-modified hydrogel, GelMA undergoes rapid photopolymerization due to the methacrylation of amino and carboxyl groups on its side chains [13]. However, pure GelMA hydrogels exhibit limited osteogenic potential, poor mechanical properties, and uncontrollable degradation rates [14]. Hyaluronic acid methacrylate (HAMA) has also been widely investigated as a photopolymerizable hydrogel. However, HAMA hydrogels exhibit limited cell adhesion [15]. Previous studies have shown that the incorporation of HAMA can modify the compressive strength of hydrogels. The GelMA-HAMA composite hydrogels prepared by Lu et al. exhibited enhanced rheological properties, compressive strength, and compressive modulus [16]. Additionally, our findings suggest that HAMA can improve cell adhesion through aldolization. The chemical structure of HAMA contains cis-diol groups, which can be oxidized to form reactive aldehyde groups. Liu et al. demonstrated that aldehyde-modified HAMA hydrogel microspheres can form covalent bonds with amino groups on damaged cartilage surfaces via Schiff base reactions, thereby enhancing targeted adhesion to the damaged tissue [17]. Chen et al. prepared aldehyde-modified HAMA hydrogels, which demonstrated strong adhesion in vitro and effectively facilitated integration between the hydrogel scaffold and host tissue [18].

In an oral inflammatory environment, alterations in the composition and quantity of dental plaque microorganisms can trigger pro-inflammatory immune responses in the connective tissue and send paracrine signals to adjacent osteocytes, disrupting the coupling between osteoclasts and osteoblasts, ultimately resulting in alveolar bone damage [19]. Therefore, the development of bio-scaffold materials with long-term antimicrobial properties is crucial for clinical applications. By constructing a favorable bone microenvironment, bio-scaffolds help maintain bone homeostasis, integrate signaling pathways, and promote bone regeneration and repair [20]. ε-PLL, as a cationic polymer, exhibits excellent antimicrobial properties and can also function as a crosslinking agent to form biodegradable hydrogel networks [21]. We synthesized aldehyde-functionalized HAMA (O-HAMA) via an oxidation reaction, resulting in abundant aldehyde groups on the surface that can form a dynamic crosslinked network with the lysine groups on ε-PLL. Additionally, O-HAMA can undergo covalent crosslinking with the methacrylate groups (-MA) on the GelMA molecular chains through photoinitiated radical polymerization. Compared to single-crosslinked hydrogels, double-crosslinked hydrogel scaffolds offer the potential to enhance mechanical properties while more closely mimicking the osteogenic microenvironment, presenting a promising but challenging strategy.

Given the above background, we aim to design an injectable cationic double-crosslinked hydrogel system that facilitates the controlled release of ε-PLL through its porous structure, thereby exerting sustained antibacterial effects and promoting bone regeneration and repair (Fig. 1). This study systematically characterized the hydrogel scaffold to assess its mechanical properties, antibacterial efficacy, and in vitro osteogenic capacity. Materials and methods

Preparation of injectable cationic dual-crosslinked hydrogels

GelMA, HAMA, ε-PLL, lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphonate (LAP), sodium periodate (NaIO4) and ethylene glycol were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). At room temperature and under dark conditions, sodium periodate (NaIO4) solution was added dropwise to the HAMA solution and stirred for 6 h to achieve complete oxidation. Ethylene glycol was then added to terminate the reaction, followed by dialysis, purification, and freeze-drying to obtain O-HAMA powder. GelMA/PLL hydrogel, O-HAMA/GelMA hydrogel, and O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL hydrogel were prepared using 8 % GelMA, 2 % O-HAMA, 1 % ε-poly-L-lysine (ε-PLL), and 0.1 % (LAP) as the base materials. Different proportions of these raw materials were dissolved in double-distilled water, heated in a 60 °C water bath, and then crosslinked under UV light at a wavelength of 405 nm.

Characterization of the hydrogels

The morphology of the hydrogels was characterized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, ZEISS, Germany). The hydrogels were freeze-dried under vacuum at 80 °C, then fractured at the center and coated with platinum (Pt) for 60 s to examine the porous structure of the hydrogel scaffold's fractured surface. The spectra of pure GelMA and O-HAMA were recorded at room temperature using a 1H nuclear magnetic resonance system (1H NMR, Bruker Avance 400, Germany). The chemical properties of the photopolymerized hydrogels were characterized using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Bruker Vertex 70, Germany) within the range of 500–4000 cm−1. The spectral resolution was 4 cm−1, with 32 scans per minute.

Swelling Behavior Testing: The hydrogel was freeze-dried and weighed to determine the initial dry weight (W0) of each sample prior to immersion. The samples were then incubated in PBS buffer (pH 7.4) at 37 °C. At specified time points (3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h), the hydrogels were removed from the solution, and excess water was blotted with filter paper. The weight after reaching swelling equilibrium (We) was recorded. The swelling rate was calculated as (We-W0)/W0 × 100 %. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

In Vitro Degradation Rate: The hydrogels from each group were immersed in PBS at pH 7.4 and pH 5.5, respectively. At the designated time points, the hydrogels were removed, gently rinsed with deionized water, and excess surface water was blotted with filter paper. The weight change of the hydrogel was recorded.The degradation rate (%) was calculated using the formula: (W0-Wt)/W0 × 100 %, where Wt represents the weight of the hydrogel at a given time, and W0 represents the initial weight of the hydrogel.

Rheological Properties: The rheological properties of the hydrogel were evaluated using a rheometer with a 25 mm diameter parallel plate and a gap distance of 1 mm for all tests. Real-time changes in the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were measured through oscillation frequency scanning. At a constant frequency of 10 rad/s (0.1–100 % strain) and a constant strain of 1 % (0.01–100 rad/s), the relationship between the hydrogel's deformation behavior and its composition ratio was investigated.

ε-PLL release experiment

Samples were immersed in 2 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) and incubated at 37 °C with an oscillation rate of 50–100 rpm, simulating the in vivo fluid environment. At predetermined time intervals, 0.2 mL of supernatant was collected and mixed with 0.2 mL of a 1 mmol/L methyl orange solution. The mixture was incubated for 30 min, then centrifuged. A 0.1 mL aliquot of the supernatant was diluted with PBS to a final volume of 1 mL for further analysis. Remove the remaining immersion solution and replace it with an additional 2 ml of fresh PBS. Measure the absorbance of the solution at 465 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer to quantify the amount of released ε-PLL. All measurements were performed in triplicate. The cumulative release rate was calculated using the following formula: Cumulative release (%) = Areleased/Aadded × 100, where Areleased represents the amount of ε-PLL released into PBS, and Aadded represents the theoretical loading amount of ε-PLL added to the hydrogel sample.

Live/dead cell staining and CCK8 assay

Each group of samples was co-cultured with MC3T3-E1 cells seeded in a 24-well plate. After washing once with PBS, the cells were stained with the Calcium AM/PI solution (Beyotime, China) and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Cell viability was then observed and photographed using a fluorescence inverted microscope (Olympus, SpinSR10, Japan). The excitation/emission wavelengths for Calcium AM were 494/517 nm, and for PI, they were 535/617 nm, respectively.

MC3T3-E1 cells were co-cultured with each group of hydrogels in a 96-well plate. Cell proliferation was assessed on days 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 using the CCK-8 assay kit (Dojindo, CK04, Japan). At each time point, the cells were washed three times with deionized water and incubated with CCK-8 reagent for 1 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, Varioskan ALF, USA).

Cell adhesion experiment

MC3T3-E1 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded onto each group of hydrogels and co-cultured for 1 and 3 days. After fixation and permeabilization, the cells were incubated at room temperature in the dark with trypan blue (1:800, 1 % BSA/PBS) for 1 h. DAPI (3 nmol/mL) was used to stain the cell nuclei. The distribution of cell pseudopodia was then observed under a laser confocal microscope (Olympus, FV3000RS, Japan).

Antimicrobial assay

Gram-negative Escherichia coli (E. coli ATCC 25922) was selected to evaluate the antimicrobial activity of the composite hydrogel. All hydrogel samples were sterilized using Go60 prior to bacterial culture. Four groups of samples were placed in a 12-well plate (n = 3), and 1 mL of 106 CFU/mL bacterial suspension was added to each well. To observe the morphology of E. coli, the bacteria were fixed with 2.5 % glutaraldehyde, dehydrated through an alcohol gradient, dried, gold-sprayed, and then examined using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, ZEISS, Germany).

Alkaline phosphatase(ALP) and alizarin red (ARS) staining

Hydrogel extracts were prepared for ALP and ARS staining by incubating the samples in medium at 37 °C. After 7 days of incubation in the conditioned medium, the samples were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min. ALP staining was performed using an ALP kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After 14 days of incubation, the samples were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde and stained with alizarin red dye (Beyotime, China), following the manufacturer's protocol.

Immunofluorescence staining for osteogenic-related proteins

To assess representative proteins indicating osteogenic differentiation, immunofluorescence staining for Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2) was performed after culturing cells in conditioned medium for 7 days. And after 14 days of culture, immunofluorescence staining for Type I collagen (Col-I) and Osteopontin (OPN) was performed. Samples were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 for 30 min, and blocked with 5 % BSA for 1 h. The samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Runx2 (1:500; Abcam), Col-I (1:200; Abcam), and OPN (1:200; Abcam). Afterward, the samples were incubated at room temperature for 2 h with Alexa Fluor 594 secondary antibody (1:200; Abcam) to detect Runx2, or Alexa Fluor 488 secondary antibody (1:500; Abcam) to detect OPN and Col-I. Following washing, the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI for 5 min. Representative images were acquired using a laser confocal microscope (Olympus, FV3000RS, Japan). The fluorescence intensity of Col-I, Runx2, and OPN was quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH).

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Animal Experiment Center, Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, and conducted in strict accordance with the “Guidelines for the Ethical Review of Laboratory Animal Welfare” (Ethics No.2023-296). The functional evaluation of the dual-crosslinked hydrogel was performed using rat cranial defect and periodontitis (maxillary defect) models.

Rat cranial defect model

Rats were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane, and the cranial region was disinfected with povidone-iodine, shaved, and covered with sterile dressings. A circular drill was used to create 5 mm diameter cranial defects on both sides, ensuring no damage to the brain tissue. The hydrogel scaffold was implanted into the defect area, after which the skin was sutured, and the wound was disinfected. Rats were euthanized at 4 and 8 weeks post-surgery, and the entire skull was harvested. All specimens were examined using a micro-CT scanning system (Micro-CT, Bruker, USA). Scanning parameters: Specimens were scanned using a 10 μm spot size, 250 μA current, and 40 kV maximum voltage. Three-dimensional images were reconstructed after scanning. Auxiliary software was used to calculate bone mineral density (BMD), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), bone volume fraction (BV/TV), and Trabecular separation (Tb.Sp).

After micro-CT examination, the cranial bone specimens from each group were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, decalcified in 10 % ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), and embedded in paraffin. The specimens were sectioned to a thickness of 5 μm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson's trichrome, and Van Gieson's (VG) picrofuchsin for histological evaluation.

To investigate the dynamic changes in bone formation, each group of rats was labeled with dual fluorescence using alizarin red and calcein. Subcutaneous injections were administered at 3 and 5 weeks post-surgery. Tissue samples were prepared by resin embedding, and fluorescence observations were made using a laser confocal microscope (Olympus, FV3000RS, Japan) with excitation wavelengths of 543 nm for alizarin red labeling and 488 nm for calcein labeling.

Immunohistochemistry

To assess the expression of bone remodeling proteins in the cranial defect region, immunohistochemical analysis was conducted using osteopontin (OPN) and osteocalcin (OCN). Tissue sections were incubated in Tris-buffered saline (TBS), followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies: OPN (rabbit, 1:100 dilution; Novus Biologicals, USA) and OCN (rat, 1:100 dilution; Abcam, USA). After washing three times with PBS, the sections were incubated with biotin-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit or anti-rat IgG, Boster Bio Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China) for 30 min, followed by incubation with a preformed streptavidin-biotin complex for an additional 30 min. Staining was performed using 3-3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate (Dako, Cambridge, UK), and counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin.

Periodontal inflammation model

After anesthesia, a 0.2 mm orthodontic ligature wire was tied around the cervical region of the first molars on both sides of the upper jaw. During this period, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (1 mg mL−1, 0.02 mL) was injected subgingivally into the first molars on both sides of the upper jaw every day. After 4 days, the orthodontic ligature wire was removed, and 20 μL of hydrogel was injected into the periodontal pocket. The hydrogel was then polymerized by applying UV light for 15 s. After 4 weeks, all rats were anesthetized, and perfusion was performed with 4 % paraformaldehyde. Three-dimensional (3D) images of the maxilla were reconstructed using micro-CT scanning. Using auxiliary software, the following parameters were measured: cementoenamel junction to alveolar bone crest (CEJ-ABC) distance, Tb.Th, trabecular number (Tb.N), bone volume (BV), BV/TV, and BMD. Immunohistochemical analysis was performed to detect the expression of IL-1β in the maxilla. Paraffin sections were prepared for H&E staining, while hard tissue sections were subjected to VG Picrofuchsin staining. The percentage of new bone formation areas was quantified using ImageJ software following VG staining.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Results were analyzed using independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA. Results with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Dental plaque biofilm serves as the primary initiating factor in the development of periodontitis. In clinical practice, local administration of antibiotics, such as minocycline ointment and doxycycline gel, is commonly employed as adjunctive therapy to reduce infection and inflammation. However, due to the narrow and deep three-dimensional structure of periodontal pockets, current treatment methods often face challenges in achieving complete restoration of the structure and function of periodontal tissues. Additionally, high doses of antibiotics are often required to achieve optimal antimicrobial effects at the lesion site, which can contribute to the development of antibiotic resistance. In this study, the aldehyde groups on O-HAMA reacted with the primary amine (-NH2) groups on the ε-PLL molecular chains through an aldehyde–amine condensation reaction, forming imine (C=N) bonds. These bonds exhibit reversible and dynamic exchange behavior under physiological conditions, resulting in the formation of a dynamic covalent cross-linked network within the hydrogel. This network imparts the hydrogel with rapid in situ gelation capability and excellent injectability [22]. The injectable cationic hydrogel developed in this study demonstrates excellent flowability, allowing it to be easily injected into periodontal pockets as a liquid. Upon injection, the hydrogel rapidly undergoes in situ crosslinking to form a gel, enabling it to reach deep regions of periodontal pockets and irregular bone defects that traditional hydrogels cannot reach. This property facilitates the effective, non-invasive delivery of the antimicrobial peptide ε-PLL, ensuring targeted treatment of infected areas.

Cationic polymers, on the other hand, eliminate bacteria through a non-specific membrane disruption mechanism, presenting an innovative solution to the growing global antibiotic resistance crisis.By modulating the charge density and the balance between hydrophilicity and hydrophobicity of the polymer chains, cationic polymers can interact effectively with the negatively charged bacterial cell membranes through electrostatic forces [23,24]. This interaction selectively disrupts the membrane integrity, leading to efficient broad-spectrum bactericidal activity [25]. This strategy, which targets the fundamental chemical structure of bacteria cells, presents a minimal risk of inducing antibiotic resistance. Yu et al. demonstrated that incorporating the cationic polymer ε-PLL into a hydrogel system effectively inhibits the growth of antibiotic-resistant bacteria [26]. ε-PLL is an antimicrobial peptide composed of 25–30 lysine residues, classified as a natural cationic antimicrobial agent. It disrupts the balance of membrane potential, damaging the cell membrane and ultimately causing bacterial death [27]. Quorum sensing (QS) refers to a chemical communication signal between bacterial cells. Hyperbranched polylysine can effectively disrupt biofilms by interfering with the QS system, offering a promising approach for treating infections [28]. This positively charged macromolecule can adhere to the bacterial surface, forming a dense membrane that obstructs the exchange of nutrients and metabolic waste, thereby inhibiting bacterial growth and biofilm development [29].

Developing biomaterials that mimic the ECM of bone tissue is a promising strategy for bone regeneration. However, many hydrogel materials face significant challenges due to their uneven network structures and weak mechanical properties, which limit their clinical application in hard tissue regeneration. The artificial simulation of the bone microenvironment and the development of hard tissue repair materials with mechanical properties comparable to natural bone tissue remain significant research challenges.

Due to their hydrophilic nature, all hydrogel groups exhibited continuous swelling within 24 h. The swelling ratio of the O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL hydrogel (∼526 %) was lower compared to that of the GelMA/PLL (∼693 %) and O-HAMA/GelMA (∼765 %) hydrogels (Fig. 2D). We speculate that the higher degree of crosslinking in this group of hydrogels may have restricted the penetration of water molecules. Excessive swelling may impose mechanical stress on the surrounding tissues, and prolonged compression can result in irreversible tissue damage [30]. Moderate swelling facilitates favorable cell migration, water/nutrient diffusion, and controlled release of bioactive factors, while preserving the hydrogel's shape and mechanical integrity. For instance, Chen et al. employed a heterogeneous hydrogel with an overall swelling ratio of approximately 540 % to mimic the ECM, which demonstrated excellent bone regeneration potential [31]. The O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL hydrogels developed in this study exhibited a swelling ratio of approximately 526 %, providing adequate mechanical support while enabling the controlled release of ε-PLL. The rheological properties of hydrogels were measured to determine the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) (Fig. 2E). The G′ values of all hydrogel groups were greater than those of G″, indicating that the materials exhibit elastic properties and typical gel characteristics. This suggests that the hydrogels function effectively as bone repair scaffolds, providing structural support primarily through their storage modulus response rather than viscous dissipation [32]. The O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL hydrogel demonstrated the highest G′ value, suggesting stronger crosslinking within the gel network and increased resistance to deformation.

During bone remodeling, cells can perceive external physical stimuli from tissue engineering scaffolds. Highly functionalized biomaterials can alter the epigenetic state of cells and ultimately promote their osteogenic differentiation [33]. Mechanical strength influences cellular biological properties through various cell/matrix and cell/cell interactions. In vitro 3D cell culture studies have demonstrated that hydrogel materials with enhanced mechanical properties, such as stiffness and stress relaxation, facilitate the differentiation of hard tissue lineages [34]. It has been reported that mechanical stress can inhibit cadherin, causing β-catenin to translocate to the cell nucleus and initiate the transcription of osteogenic-related genes [35]. The O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL cationic hydrogel developed in this study forms a more stable three-dimensional structure through a dual-crosslinked network, effectively transmitting and dispersing stress, thereby significantly enhancing the mechanical strength of the hydrogel.

The degradation curves of the hydrogels in buffers with pH values of 5.5 and 7.4 are shown in Fig. 2F. A pH 5.5 buffer was used to simulate the mildly acidic inflammatory environment typically found in the early stages of bone defects. All hydrogel groups exhibited faster degradation in acidic conditions compared to normal physiological conditions (pH 7.4).This phenomenon may be attributed to the acid sensitivity of the Schiff base bonds, as acidic conditions promote protonation. For small to medium-sized bone defects (such as alveolar or mandibular defects), the ideal hydrogel degradation process should occur gradually over approximately 4–12 weeks to support early osteoblast proliferation, osteogenic differentiation, and initial callus formation. In this study, the bone defects in the animal model were relatively small, and the O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL hydrogels exhibited substantial degradation within 5–6 weeks, which was well-matched with the rate of early bone regeneration. In the in vitro release study of ε-PLL, the release from the GelMA/PLL hydrogel was completed within 3 days. In contrast, the release curve for ε-PLL in the O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL hydrogel displayed a gradual increase, with the release period extending up to 14 days (Fig. 2G).

FE-SEM was employed to observe the morphology of E. coli on the surface of each hydrogel group (Fig. 3). In the control group and the O-HAMA/GelMA group, E. coli displayed a short rod-shaped morphology with intact cell membranes and clearly visible pili, indicating good bacterial survival. In the GelMA/PLL group, pili were absent, the cell membranes appeared wrinkled, and some bacterial cells were ruptured. On the surface of the O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL hydrogel, the normal morphology of E. coli was nearly unrecognizable, with the majority of bacterial cell membranes showing signs of damage, including wrinkling, rupture, and leakage of cellular contents. FE-SEM results indicated that the O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL group exhibited the most severe cell membrane damage, demonstrating the most potent antibacterial effect against E. coli.Fig. 3SEM images of E. coli treated with hydrogels.Fig. 3

E. coli was selected in this study to assess the broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity of the cationic hydrogel. E.coli is a representative strain of Gram-negative bacteria, characterized by its outer membrane, which contains LPS, as well as a peptidoglycan layer [36]. The O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL hydrogel releases ε-PLL, which, due to its positive charge, interacts with multiple sites on the E. coli cell membrane. This interaction facilitates the removal of the LPS layer, leading to the induction of negative membrane bending. As a result, multiple irregular ruptures form on the bacterial cell membrane surface, compromising its structural integrity.The rapid outflow of intracellular fluid disrupts the bacterial internal environment, ultimately leading to bacterial cell death [37]. Additionally, some researchers have suggested that ε-PLL may penetrate bacterial cells and interfere with metabolic processes, specifically inhibiting the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. This inhibition could result in elevated concentrations of glucose and citrate within the cell, further impairing bacterial function and viability [38].

Cell viability was assessed at different time points using Live/Dead staining and the CCK-8 assay kit, with results indicating that the O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL hydrogel demonstrates excellent biocompatibility (Fig. 4A and B). Additionally, we prepared dual-crosslinked hydrogels containing 0.5 %, 1 %, and 3 % ε-PLL. Live/dead staining and CCK-8 assay results indicated that the hydrogel containing 3 % ε-PLL exhibited increased cytotoxicity, resulting in poorer biosafety and cell compatibility (Fig. S1). Phalloidin staining was used to observe the cellular cytoskeleton and cell adhesion morphology. The properties of hydrogels significantly influence cell adhesion, proliferation, and extracellular matrix production. Hydrogels with higher mechanical strength offer enhanced support for cell adhesion [39]. The O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL group exhibited enhanced cell adhesion-promoting capabilities. The underlying mechanism may be explained as follows. Firstly, the aldehyde functional groups on O-HAMA serve as reactive sites that facilitate adhesion to natural proteins and collagen on cell or tissue surfaces (-NH2, -SH groups and non-covalent interaction) [40]. Secondly, GelMA contains collagen-active sequences, such as the arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD) peptide, which facilitate cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation [41]. Last but not least, ε-PLL introduces additional cell adhesion binding sites and directly interacts with the cell lipid bilayer, preventing cells from aggregating into spheres through electrostatic attraction [42,43]. Therefore, the O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL hydrogel facilitates cell adhesion to the material and spreading into a monolayer, significantly enhancing cell adhesion efficiency. Yuan et al. also reported that porous poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) microspheres modified with PLL are more conducive to MG63 cell adhesion [44]. Currently, most in situ cross-linking strategies achieve only physical interpenetration with tissue defects, resulting in insufficient interfacial bonding strength. We hypothesize that the O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL hydrogel can form chemical bonds with tissue components, thereby enhancing its retention at the bone defect site and preventing detachment, which may ultimately promote more effective tissue repair. Furthermore, we observed that cells in the O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL group exhibited better pseudopod extension. Highly branched cell morphology and elongated pseudopods are conducive to osteogenesis [45].

To evaluate the osteogenic potential of the O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL polycationic hydrogel, the composite hydrogel was co-cultured with MC3T3 cells. Initially, the activity of the early osteogenic marker, ALP, was assessed. After 7 days of culture, the O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL group showed the strongest ALP staining, followed by the O-HAMA/GelMA group (Fig. 4D). Additionally, on day 14, calcium salt deposition during osteogenic differentiation was assessed using ARS. The O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL hydrogel demonstrated a greater number of stained areas, indicating enhanced calcium deposition compared to the other groups (Fig. 4E).

To further confirm the osteogenic differentiation-inducing effect at the protein level, immunofluorescence staining was performed for Col-I, OPN, and Runx-2 (Fig. 4F). Semi-quantitative analysis of the fluorescence intensity was conducted using ImageJ software (Fig. 4G). Runx-2 is a key transcription factor that initiates early osteogenic processes and activates the expression of osteogenic-related ALP and OPN genes. On day 7, Runx-2 expression was highest in the O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL hydrogel. Col-I, an indicator of osteoblast functional status, is a bone structural protein secreted by osteoblasts. It provides a scaffold for calcium salt deposition and facilitates cell adhesion. The OPN molecule contains regions rich in aspartic acid, which enables it to bind to hydroxyapatite, facilitating bone matrix resorption and mineralization. On day 14, the expression of Col-I and OPN in the O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL group was significantly higher compared to the other groups, indicating enhanced osteogenic differentiation.

In our study, the GelMA/PLL group exhibited the highest cytotoxicity and the lowest osteogenic efficacy. This can be attributed to the fact that in this hydrogel system, ε-PLL and GelMA primarily interact through electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonds, and other physical interactions, lacking stable covalent bonds. Consequently, ε-PLL remains relatively unstable within the hydrogel matrix and is easily released into the surrounding medium by diffusion. The cytotoxicity of cationic polymers tends to increase with their concentration, molecular weight, and charge density [46]. In the GelMA/PLL group, the rapid release of ε-PLL caused a swift increase in its local concentration. Due to its cationic antibacterial properties, the high density of positive charges on ε-PLL can disrupt the phospholipid bilayer of the cell membrane, thereby exerting significant cytotoxic effects on cells [47]. To achieve controlled and sustained release of ε-PLL, we modified HAMA within the O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL cationic hydrogel. The oxidized aldehyde groups in HAMA react with the amino groups of ε-PLL molecules through a Schiff base reaction, forming covalent bonds that anchor ε-PLL to the HAMA molecular chains. This covalent bond stabilizes ε-PLL within the hydrogel matrix, preventing its free diffusion and ensuring a controlled, sustained release over a period of 14 days. By adjusting parameters such as the oxidation degree of HAMA, the reaction ratio between ε-PLL and HAMA, and the crosslinking density of the hydrogel, the release rate of ε-PLL can be precisely regulated. The cationic double-crosslinked hydrogel developed in this study successfully mitigates cytotoxicity by ensuring a controlled, sustained release of ε-PLL. This controlled release not only maintains long-term antibacterial effects but also promotes bone regeneration and repair.

The deep regions of periodontal pockets serve as the primary colonization sites for Gram-negative bacteria, whose metabolic byproducts, LPS, induce strong pro-inflammatory responses in gingival epithelial cells and periodontal ligament cells [50]. LPS can activate the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting osteoblast differentiation [51]. This study established a rat periodontitis model, where hydrogel was injected into the subgingival pocket and maintained at the lesion site. This approach minimized bacterial infection and inflammation, thereby promoting osteogenesis. Dima et al.'s research demonstrated that ε-PLL exhibits satisfactory antibacterial effects against oral pathogens, such as Streptococcus mutans and Porphyromonas gingivalis [52]. All tested concentrations of ε-PLL in their study completely inhibited Streptococcus mutans after 24 h and significantly reduced the viable cell count of Porphyromonas gingivalis. Inhibiting inflammation promotes wound healing and the repair of surrounding bone and connective tissue. Tian et al.'s study demonstrated that polycaprolactone (PCL) scaffolds containing ε-PLL can suppress the levels of pro-inflammatory type 1 helper T cells (Th1), type 17 helper T cells (Th17), and type 1 macrophages (M1), while promoting the infiltration of anti-inflammatory type 2 helper T cells (Th2) into the regenerating tissue area [53]. The inflammatory factor IL-1β is a key mediator in periodontitis and bone destruction. Cationic hydrogels exhibit distinctive antibacterial activity and functional advantages under laboratory conditions; however, their clinical translation remains challenging due to issues such as establishing safe in vivo dosage ranges and ensuring long-term implant safety, including potential immunogenicity. Additional obstacles include achieving sterile and scale‐up manufacturing processes and meeting complex regulatory requirements as well as clinical operability. Successful clinical translation will require an integrated approach that combines systematic toxicological and immunological assessments with optimized manufacturing and regulatory strategies.

Conclusion

In summary, the O-HAMA/GelMA/PLL injectable cationic dual-crosslinked hydrogel developed in this study exhibits excellent mechanical properties and antimicrobial performance, making it a promising scaffold for applications in bone defects and periodontitis. In subsequent studies, we will utilize machine learning techniques to optimize the structural design of cationic antimicrobial polymers. Additionally, we will further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the interactions with membranes, as well as conduct pharmacokinetic analyses and long-term toxicity evaluations.
