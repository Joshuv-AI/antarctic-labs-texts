Atomic-Level Valence-State Engineering Redirects CO2 Electroreduction on Cu Nanoclusters
Authors: Qilin Li, Mandira Ghosh, Mohd Rashid, Rupa Sarma, Pradip Kumar Mondal, Tokuhisa Kawawaki, Sourav Biswas, Biswarup Pathak, Yuichi Negishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Atomically precise thiolate-protected Cu nanoclusters (NCs) typically suffer from an intrinsic bias toward the two-electron formate pathway in CO2 electroreduction, limiting access to more deeply reduced products. Breaking this selectivity within a structurally well-defined system remains a significant challenge due to the difficulty of precisely tuning the Cu valence states. Here, we address this limitation through atomic-level valence-state engineering by introducing [S@Cu50S12(S t Bu)20(CF3COO)12] (S@Cu50) NC, featuring a controlled Cu­(I)/Cu­(II) ratio within a conserved structural framework. Single-crystal analysis reveals a core–shell S@Cu14S12@Cu36 architecture, while XPS confirms an increased Cu­(II) population compared to the reference [Cu50S12(S t Bu)20(CF3COO)12] (Cu50) analogue. Despite similar overall catalytic activity, S@Cu50 exhibits a striking shift in product selectivity during CO2 electroreduction, suppressing formate formation (Faradaic efficiency of <11% vs 38% in Cu50) and enabling CH3OH production with a Faradaic efficiency of ∼19% at –1.0 V vs RHEabsent in the Cu50 system. Density functional theory calculations attribute this mechanistic switching to valence-induced electronic modulation, which stabilizes CO-derived intermediates and promotes sequential hydrogenation toward CH3OH, in contrast to HCOO stabilization in the reference NC. This work establishes that subtle modulation of the Cu­(I)/Cu­(II) balance can fundamentally redirect reaction pathways, providing a molecular-level strategy to overcome intrinsic selectivity limitations in Cu NC catalysis.

Introduction

Atomically precise metal nanoclusters (NCs), featuring well-defined compositions and atomic arrangements, have emerged as powerful platforms at the interface of molecular chemistry and nanoscience. Their quantized electronic structures position them in the unique transition regime between discrete molecules and bulk nanoparticles, enabling fundamental insights into structure–property relationships at the atomic level. , While pioneering studies on gold (Au) and silver (Ag) NCs established superatomic electronic concepts and advanced applications in catalysis and sensing, copper (Cu) NCs have recently attracted increasing attention owing to the earth abundance, low cost, and rich redox chemistry of Cu. − The accessible Cu(0), Cu­(I), and Cu­(II) states offer exceptional opportunities for electronic-structure modulation, rendering Cu NCs particularly attractive as tunable catalytic model systems. −

Among catalytic transformations, the electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR) represents a critical pathway toward carbon neutrality and renewable energy storage. However, CO2RR involves complex proton-coupled electron transfer processes and competing reaction pathways, often leading to broad product distributions. − In atomically precise thiolate-protected Cu NCs, formate formation is frequently observed as the dominant pathway, particularly in thiolate-protected Cu­(I)-based systems (Table S1). − Even in cases where metallic Cu(0) species coexist with Cu­(I), the overall selectivity often remains biased toward formate, suggesting that simple incorporation of reduced Cu species is insufficient to fundamentally redirect the reaction mechanism. , Despite extensive mechanistic proposalsranging from hydride-mediated pathways to surface-bound *OCHO intermediatesstrategies capable of breaking the intrinsic formate preference in molecularly defined Cu NCs remain elusive.

Valence-state engineering at the atomic level offers a promising yet underexplored route to overcome this limitation. In particular, it is assumed that the controlled incorporation of Cu­(II) within an atomically precise Cu framework may significantly perturb the electronic structure, adsorption energetics, and intermediate stabilization during CO2RR. However, structurally well-defined Cu­(I)/Cu­(II) NCs with tunable ratios are rare, and their application in CO2 electroreduction has scarcely been investigated. Achieving stable coexistence of Cu­(I) and Cu­(II) within a single, atomically precise architecture remains synthetically challenging, yet it is essential for establishing direct correlations between valence-state distribution and catalytic pathway selection.

Herein, we report a [S@Cu50S12(S t Bu)20(CF3COO)12] (S@Cu50) NC featuring a tailored Cu­(I)/Cu­(II) balance that enables valence-state–driven mechanistic switching in CO2 electroreduction. Unlike reported [Cu50S12(S t Bu)20(CF3COO)12] (Cu50) NC analogues that preferentially yield formate, the Cu­(II)-enriched system redirects the reaction pathway toward CH3OH formation. Notably, the incorporation of a sulfide template modulates the Cu­(I)/Cu­(II) ratio while preserving an almost identical structural and geometric framework, thereby allowing a direct and meaningful comparison between the two systems. These results demonstrate that precise modulation of the Cu­(I)/Cu­(II) ratio can break the intrinsic formate pathway in thiolate-protected Cu NCs, providing direct evidence that atomic-level valence-state engineering governs product divergence in CO2RR. These findings establish Cu­(I)/Cu­(II)–balanced NCs as molecularly defined platforms for decoding and controlling reaction mechanisms in Cu-based CO2 reduction catalysis.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Structural Architecture

A one-pot solvothermal strategy was employed for the synthesis of the S@Cu50 NC. Although several synthetic approaches have been reported for the preparation of Cu NCs, the majority of them rely on the chemical reduction of precursor salts using strong reducing agents such as borohydride [BH4]−. − In contrast, the present system was obtained through a mild synthetic route that does not involve any external reducing agent. In a typical synthesis, equimolar amounts of the Cu­(I) precursors [CuS t Bu]n and Cu­(CH3CN)4BF4 were dissolved in 3 mL of CH3OH to form a homogeneous reaction mixture. Subsequently, CF3COOH was introduced into the solution, and the mixture was heated at 60 °C for 6 h under solvothermal conditions as depicted in Scheme . During this process, the controlled assembly of the Cu–thiolate framework facilitated the formation of the NC without the need for an external reducing agent. After completion of the reaction, the solution was allowed to slowly cool and stand at room temperature to promote crystallization. This procedure resulted in the formation of well-defined red block-shaped single crystals, which were isolated and structurally characterized using single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD). The characteristic color and morphology of the obtained crystals were further confirmed by optical microscopy, revealing uniform block-like crystals consistent with the bulk crystalline phase (Scheme ).

SCXRD analysis reveals that the obtained crystals crystallize in a cubic crystal system with the space group F–43c (No. 219). The detailed crystallographic parameters are summarized in Table S2. Structural analysis discloses a well-defined core–shell architecture S@Cu50 NC, in which the inner core is composed of Cu14S12, while the shell can be formulated as Cu36(S t Bu)20 (Figure ). The shell is further coordinated with 12 CF3COO– ligands toward the outside of the surface. Notably, the inner metal framework is not hollow; instead, it encapsulates a central S2– ion, giving rise to an S@Cu14S12 core (Figure b and Figure S1). Interestingly, no external S2– source was introduced during the reaction. The formation of S2– ions is therefore likely associated with the disproportionation process of the Cu–thiolate precursor, where partial oxidation of Cu­(I) to Cu­(II) occurs concurrently with C–S bond cleavage, ultimately generating S2– species that become incorporated into the cluster core. Similar in situ S2– generation processes have been reported in related Cu–thiolate NC systems. −

The Cu14 core framework adopts a rhombic dodecahedral geometry, consisting of 12 congruent [Cu4] rhombic faces (Figure S1). The Cu–Cu bond distances are differentiated into two sets, 2.699 and 2.683 Å, indicating a slightly distorted metallic framework. Surrounding this Cu14 framework, 12 S2– ions are positioned at the faces of the rhombic dodecahedron, forming an icosahedral-like arrangement around the Cu14 architecture (Figure S1). Each S2– ion coordinates with three Cu atoms of the Cu14 unit; however, the coordination is slightly asymmetric. One Cu–S bond is shorter (2.167 Å), while the other two measure 2.301 and 2.313 Å, indicating a slight displacement of the S2– ion toward one of the Cu centers (Figure S2). These S2– ions also play a crucial structural role by connecting the inner core to the outer shell (Figure S3). Collectively, they form a middle anionic layer that bridges the core and shell components, a structural feature commonly observed in several Ag NC systems. , In the present structure, each S2– ion exhibits a μ6-coordination mode, simultaneously binding to three Cu atoms from the inner core and three Cu atoms from the outer shell (Figure S4). On the shell side, the S2– ions coordinate with three Cu atoms located on the rhombic facets of the outer framework, but in an inward-oriented geometry. The Cu–S bond distances in this region average 2.3263 Å. The shell structural motif is composed of six equivalent double-star units. These motifs are arranged with each other via edge sharing into a symmetric outer framework surrounding the S2–-bridged core (Figure c and Figure S5). In addition to the μ6-S2– bridges, the shell is further connected directly to the inner Cu framework through six μ2 and eight μ3 Cu–Cu interactions (Figure S6). Due to the distortion in the core metallic architecture corresponding Cu–Cu distances are slightly asymmetric, measuring ∼2.631 Å for μ2 connections and ∼2.876 Å for μ3 connections. Within the outer shell itself, direct Cu–Cu interactions are relatively limited, with six Cu–Cu contacts observed at an average distance of ∼2.413 Å (Figure S5). The NC surface is stabilized by 20 S t Bu– ligands, eight coordinate to the Cu atoms through μ3 bridging, while the remaining 12 bind via μ4 coordination modes to the Cu atoms, thereby reinforcing the outer NC framework (Figure S7). In addition, CF3COO– ligands are uniformly coordinated to the shell. Each CF3COO– ligand adopts an overall μ2 coordination mode, in which the two oxygen atoms each bind to individual Cu centers through μ1 interactions (Figure a and Figure S8). The crystal packing is stabilized through C–H···F intermolecular interactions, occurring via a face-to-face contact arrangement between neighboring NCs (Figure S9).

Comparison with the Existing Literature

Although the structural architecture of the S@Cu50 NC is intriguing, a structurally related Cu50 NC has been previously reported. The most significant distinction between these two systems lies in the presence of a templating S2– anion in the present structure. In the earlier reported Cu50 NC, no such template anion was identified within the metal framework. The difference likely originates from the variation in reaction conditions, including the choice of metal precursors and reaction temperature in acidic condition which in the present case promotes the in situ generation of S2– ions through C–S bond cleavage of the thiolate ligand. , The incorporation of this S2– anion at the center of the spherical Cu14S12 core results in a noticeably more compact inner framework compared to the reported Cu50 NC. As a consequence, the diameter of the inner core contracts to ∼0.6 nm, compared to 0.64 nm in the previously reported Cu50 NC (Figure S10). This structural compression can be attributed to the direct coordination of the central S2– anion with the surrounding Cu atoms, which leads to shortened Cu–Cu and Cu–S bond distances within the slightly distorted S@Cu50 NC relative to the reported analogue. The size and geometry of this core are comparable to those of a similar core structure reported in Cu56 and Cu54 NCs. , The contraction of the core further propagates to the shell, leading to a slightly reduced overall NC size. Specifically, the outer spherical framework decreases from ∼1.2 nm in the reported Cu50 NC to ∼1.1 nm in the present S@Cu50 NC. These structural variations are consistent with the modified Cu–Cu and Cu–S bonding parameters observed in the synthesized NC. Although the magnitude of this structural contraction appears modest, the incorporation of the central template anion is expected to significantly influence the electronic configuration of the Cu framework.

Characterizations

The bulk phase purity of the synthesized crystals was first verified by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) analysis. The experimental PXRD pattern closely matches the simulated diffraction profile generated from the SCXRD structure, confirming the phase purity of the isolated NC in the bulk state (Figure a). Further compositional analysis was carried out using scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM–EDS), which reveals the uniform distribution of all constituent elements, including Cu, S, F, O, and C, throughout the crystal matrix (Figure b). This observation confirms the homogeneous elemental composition of the synthesized NC. To gain deeper insight into the chemical states and surface composition, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed. The survey spectrum confirms the presence of Cu, S, F, O, and C elements, consistent with the elemental composition determined from structural analysis (Figure S11). Importantly, the high-resolution Cu 2p spectrum clearly indicates the coexistence of both Cu­(I) and Cu­(II) oxidation states in the synthesized S@Cu50 NC (Figure c). In particular, the appearance of a pronounced satellite shakeup peak centered at approximately 944 eV is a characteristic signature of Cu­(II) species. , In contrast, the previously reported Cu50 NC exhibits only a very weak satellite feature, suggesting a lower Cu­(II) content (Figure S12). This comparison clearly indicates that the synthesized S@Cu50 NC possesses a higher proportion of Cu­(II) relative to Cu­(I), reflecting a distinct modulation in the Cu­(I)/Cu­(II) ratio compared with the reported Cu50 NC analogue. Deconvolution of the Cu 2p high-resolution spectrum further resolves two sets of spin–orbit doublets corresponding to different Cu oxidation states. The peaks located at ∼933.8 eV (Cu 2p3/2) and ∼952.4 eV (Cu 2p1/2) are assigned to Cu­(I) species, whereas the peaks at ∼934.7 eV (Cu 2p3/2) and ∼954.6 eV (Cu 2p1/2) are attributed to Cu­(II) centers. These results unambiguously confirm the mixed-valence nature of the synthesized NC. The Cu LMM Auger spectra of both NCs confirm the presence of Cu­(I) and Cu­(II) species with altered Cu­(I)/Cu­(II) ratios (Figure S13). However, the exact contributions could not be determined due to strong charge transfer between closely positioned mixed-valence metal centers and the inherent limitations of XPS analysis for core–shell architectures. In addition to that, neither the SCXRD analysis nor the XPS survey spectrum reveals the presence of any counteranion species. The absence of additional elemental signals associated with counterions, similar to the previously reported Cu50 NC, suggests that the present S@Cu50 NC is also electrically neutral. Based on this analysis, the cluster composition can be rationalized as containing forty-two Cu­(I) atoms and eight Cu­(II) atoms, whereas the reported Cu50 NC consists of forty-four Cu­(I) atoms and six Cu­(II) atoms, indicating a clear variation in the Cu­(I)/Cu­(II) ratio between the two systems. The theoretical Mulliken charge was estimated for each Cu atom in the cluster, and it was identified that eight Cu atoms possess charges close to +2 (Figure S14 and Table S3). The surface ligand environment of the synthesized NC was further examined using Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, which confirms the presence of the characteristic vibrational modes corresponding to the organic ligands coordinated on the NC surface (Figure S15). The optical property of the NC was investigated using UV–vis absorption spectroscopy. The synthesized S@Cu50 NC exhibits a relatively featureless absorption profile, similar to that observed for the previously reported Cu50 NC, with a weak shoulder appearing around ∼390 nm (Figure S16). However, notable differences are observed in the photoluminescence behavior. While the reported Cu50 NC displays dual emission bands upon excitation at 370 nm, the synthesized S@Cu50 NC does not exhibit such dual emission features. This difference in emission behavior may likely associated with the modified valence-state distribution within the Cu framework.

CO2 Reduction Capabilities and Comparison

Electrocatalytic performance of the synthesized S@Cu50 NC was systematically evaluated for the CO2RR in CO2-saturated 0.1 M KHCO3 aqueous electrolyte and directly compared with that of the previously reported Cu50 NC analogue. Detailed procedures for catalyst ink preparation and immobilization on carbon black (CB) are provided in the Supporting Information. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurements under CO2-saturated conditions reveal nearly identical current density profiles for both NCs over the investigated potential range (Figure a). This similarity indicates comparable overall catalytic activity consistent with their closely related structural architectures. However, chronoamperometric measurements conducted at a series of constant applied potentials uncover pronounced differences in product distribution between the two systems. Despite exhibiting similar total current densities in LSV, the S@Cu50 NC displays a markedly altered selectivity profile relative to the reference Cu50 NC (Figure b–c). Gas and liquid product analyses confirm the formation of H2, CO, HCOOH, and CH3OH during electrolysis. These results demonstrate that while the overall catalytic rate remains largely unchanged, the reaction flux is redistributed among competing pathways. The decoupling of total activity from product selectivity strongly suggests that the observed divergence originates from valence-state–induced electronic modulation rather than from variations in intrinsic activity or surface area. To verify that the detected carbon-containing products arise exclusively from CO2 reduction, control experiments were performed under Ar-saturated conditions (Figure S17). Under these conditions, no carbon-based products were detected, confirming that CO, HCOOH, and CH3OH originate from CO2 conversion.

The Faradaic efficiencies (FE) of the individual products exhibit a pronounced potential dependence and reveal a clear divergence in selectivity between the two NCs. For the previously reported Cu50 NC, only H2, CO, and HCOOH were detected as reaction products over the examined potential range (Figure b and Figure S18 and Table S4). Notably, a strong preference for formate formation is observed between –0.8 and –1.4 V vs.

RHE (Figure b). The FEHCOOH increases steadily with increasing overpotential, rising from approximately 17% at –0.8 V to a maximum of ∼61% at –1.4 V vs RHE. At this potential, the total FE approaches ∼100%, indicating negligible formation of undetected side products and high catalytic selectivity. In contrast, CO production remains minimal throughout the investigated potential window, with FECO consistently limited to ∼1–3%. This negligible CO formation, combined with the monotonic increase in FEHCOOH, clearly demonstrates that the reported Cu50 NC intrinsically favors the two-electron formate pathway. Hydrogen evolution (HER) competes with CO2 reduction at lower overpotentials, where FEH2 is relatively high. However, as the applied potential becomes more negative, CO2RR progressively dominates the catalytic process, leading to suppression of HER. At –1.2 and –1.4 V vs RHE, FEH2 stabilizes at ∼30%, while formate formation remains the dominant carbon-containing product. These results collectively confirm that the previously reported Cu50 NC exhibits a strong and potential-dependent intrinsic selectivity toward formate production, with only minor contributions from CO formation and competitive hydrogen evolution. Notably, several other thiolate-protected Cu­(I) and mixed-valence Cu­(I)/Cu(0) NCs reported in the literature similarly demonstrate a pronounced preference for formate generation across their investigated potential ranges.

In contrast to the previously reported NCs, the synthesized S@Cu50 NC displays a markedly different product distribution, producing H2, CO, HCOOH, and CH3OH, with the relative amounts strongly dependent on the applied potential (Figure c and Figure S19). Most notably, CH3OH formationabsent in the reference Cu50 NC systememerges as a significant product in the S@Cu50 NC catalyst. The Faradaic efficiency for FECH3OH reaches a maximum of ∼19% at –1.0 V vs RHE, increasing from ∼10% at –0.8 V and subsequently decreasing gradually to ∼8% at –1.4 V vs RHE (Figure c). Formate production, in contrast, remains consistently suppressed across the examined potential window, with FEHCOOH maintained below ∼11% from –0.8 to –1.4 V vs RHE. This behavior stands in sharp contrast to the strong formate selectivity observed for the reference Cu50 NC and clearly indicates a shift in the reduction pathway toward the formation of more deeply reduced products (Figure d). Meanwhile, CO formation becomes increasingly pronounced at more negative potentials, rising from ∼2% at –1.0 V to ∼29% at –1.4 V vs RHE (Figure c). The competitive HER remains more prominent in S@Cu50 NC than in the reference system throughout the entire potential range, indicating a different balance of intermediate adsorption and proton-coupled electron transfer kinetics. Taken together, these observations reveal a clear mechanistic shift in CO2 reduction when comparing the two NCs, despite their nearly identical structural frameworks. The exclusive formation of CH3OH in the S@Cu50 NC catalyst, accompanied by the substantial suppression of HCOOH production, suggests a fundamental alteration in intermediate stabilization. At moderate overpotentials (−1.0 V vs RHE), we assumed that the electronic structure of S@Cu50 NC likely favors stabilization of *CO-derived hydrogenation intermediates, thereby promoting the multielectron reduction pathway toward CH3OH rather than the conventional HCOO*-mediated formate pathway. However, upon further increasing the overpotential, enhanced desorption of the *CO intermediate becomes kinetically favorable, leading to a rapid increase in CO production and a concomitant decline in CH3OH formation. These potential-dependent trends provide compelling evidence that subtle valence-state modulation in S@Cu50 NC redistributes the reaction flux among competing intermediates, resulting in a distinct mechanistic pathway compared to the formate-dominant reference Cu50 NC.

To gain further insight into the catalytic behavior of the two NCs, the partial current densities of the individual products were calculated based on their respective FEs. As shown S@Cu50 NC in Figure d, the previously reported Cu50 NC exhibits a dominant partial current density toward formate formation across the investigated potential range. The formate partial current density j HCOOH increases steadily with increasing overpotential, consistent with the rising FE for HCOOH production. The partial current densities for CO and H2 remain relatively low, confirming the intrinsic preference of this NC for the two-electron formate pathway. In contrast, the S@Cu50 NC displays a distinctly different distribution of partial current densities (Figure e). Notably, a significant partial current density for CH3OH formation j CH3OH is observed at moderate potentials, reaching its maximum around –1.0 V vs RHE. Meanwhile, the partial current density for CO increases progressively at more negative potentials, eventually becoming the dominant carbon-containing product at –1.4 V vs RHE. The partial current density for formate remains comparatively low throughout the potential range, further confirming the suppression of the conventional formate pathway in the S@Cu50 NC.

Mechanistic Insights into the Diverse Product Selectivity

To understand the origin of the distinct catalytic behavior of these two NCs, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out to evaluate the reaction free-energy profiles (Figure a–d) of the CO2 reduction reaction on both DFT optimized structures depicted in Figures S20 and S21. , The calculated free-energy diagrams reveal notable differences in the stabilization of key intermediates, which ultimately govern the product selectivity (Figure a,b). On Cu50 NC (Figure a), the reaction can then proceed through the formation of either the *COOH or HCOO* intermediates (Figure c and Figure S21). The calculated free-energy values indicate that the HCOO* forms at a lower energy 1.15 eV compared to 1.38 eV for *COOH, suggesting a reference for the formate pathway. After overcoming this barrier, the formation of *HCOOH becomes highly exergonic, with a free energy of –1.09 eV, indicating strong stabilization of the formate-related intermediate on the Cu50 NC surface. Alternatively, the reduction pathway may proceed through the *CO intermediate, which forms at – 0.99 eV and can subsequently lead to the generation of CO as a minor product. In contrast, the S@Cu50 NC (Figure b) exhibits a markedly different free-energy landscape through a different active catalytic center (Figure S21). The formation of the HCOO* intermediate becomes the highest energy point with a free energy of 1.31 eV (1.15 eV for *COOH). Importantly, once this step is overcome, the *CO intermediate is significantly stabilized with a free energy of –0.89 eV, which is considerably more favorable than HCOOH* (Figure d and Figure S21). Furthermore, CO desorption is highly endergonic, thereby promoting the retention of the *CO intermediate on the catalyst surface and facilitating its further hydrogenation. In this pathway, *CO is converted through the *CHO intermediate (−0.13 eV), followed by the formation of *OCH2 (−0.89 eV) and *OCH3 (0.44 eV). Ultimately, the generation of *CH3OH is highly exergonic with a free energy of – 1.06 eV, suggesting that the S@Cu50 NC catalyst thermodynamically favors deeper CO2 reduction toward CH3OH formation. Therefore, while Cu50 NC stabilizes the HCOO*/HCOOH* intermediates and drives the reaction toward the formate pathway, S2– incorporation significantly alters the overall electronic environment of the synthesized NC and stabilizes the *CO intermediate (−0.78 eV), thereby promoting sequential hydrogenation steps toward CH3OH formation. Concurrently, slightly higher reaction free energy for hydrogen adsorption (Figure S22) on S@Cu50 alters HER, affecting the CO2RR selectivity.

To further understand the origin of the distinct catalytic behavior between the Cu50 NC and S@Cu50 NC catalysts, the calculated electronic structure analysis (Figure ) shows that S incorporation induces a downshift of the Cu d-band center and therefore optimizes the binding with the important *CO and *COOH intermediates, which in turn favors the CH3OH formation. We have further investigated to understand the subtle electronic modulation, which drives a mechanistic shift from formate to methanol production, highlighting how atomic-level Cu­(I)/Cu­(II) balance governs CO2RR selectivity in atomically precise nanoclusters. The electronic structures were analyzed using total density of states (DOS) and projected density of states (p-DOS) calculations. As shown in Figure S23, the total DOS profiles of both NCs reveal that the electronic states near the Fermi level are primarily dominated by the Cu 3d orbitals, indicating that the Cu centers play a decisive role in governing the catalytic activity toward CO2 electroreduction. The p-DOS analysis of individual atomic contributions shows that the Cu d states strongly overlap with the ligand-derived S p orbitals, forming hybridized electronic states around the Fermi level (Figures S24–26). In the Cu50 NC, the Cu d-band is positioned slightly closer to the Fermi level, resulting in a calculated d-band center of – 1.79 eV (Figure c and Figure S27). This relatively higher d-band position suggests stronger adsorption of oxygenated intermediates such as HCOO*, which stabilizes the formate pathway and is consistent with the experimentally observed high FEHCOOH formation. − In contrast, S@Cu50 NC induces subtle electronic redistribution within the Cu framework, leading to a downward shift of the Cu d states and a calculated d-band center of –1.83 eV (Figure d and Figure S28). The p-DOS plots further reveal enhanced hybridization between Cu d orbitals and S p orbitals, which slightly broadens the electronic states (d-width of Cu50 NC: 2.778 eV; d-width of S@Cu50 NC:2.809 eV) and consequently reduces the density of Cu d states near the Fermi level (Figure c,d). This downward shift weakens the binding strength of the HCOO* intermediate while stabilizing *CO-derived intermediates such as *CHO and *OCH3, thereby facilitating deeper protonation steps that lead to CH3OH formation. Therefore, the theoretical analysis indicates that Cu NCs containing a higher proportion of Cu­(II) exhibit slightly shifted d-states. This heterogeneous electronic distribution creates multiple adsorption environments for CO2 reduction intermediates, thereby contributing to the observed divergence in product selectivity. So, these results demonstrate that even minute valence-state–induced electronic perturbations in atomically precise Cu50 NC are sufficient to induce mechanistic switching, rather than merely tuning product ratios, underscoring the critical role of atomic-level Cu­(I)/Cu­(II) balance in governing CO2RR pathways.

Catalytic Sustainability over Cycle of Experiments

Although the modulation of the Cu­(I)/Cu­(II) ratio in atomically precise thiolated-Cu NCs provides an effective strategy to break the intrinsic formate-production pathway and enables the formation of more deeply reduced products, the structural stability of such mixed-valence systems remains an important consideration. In general, Cu NCs are intrinsically less stable than their Au and Ag counterparts due to the relatively higher chemical reactivity of Cu. The coexistence of multiple oxidation states within the NC framework can further increase their susceptibility to structural or electronic changes under catalytic conditions. To evaluate its catalytic durability, long-term chronoamperometry measurements were performed at –1.0 V vs RHE, where the catalyst initially exhibited consistent reduction product distribution. The FE of the reduction products were monitored at hourly intervals, revealing that the catalyst maintained a nearly constant product distribution for up to 5 h of continuous electrolysis (Figure a and Figure S29). However, beyond this period, a gradual shift in product selectivity was observed, characterized by a decrease in CH3OH formation accompanied by an increase in CO production. This evolution in product distribution suggests the possibility of catalyst restructuring or gradual degradation during prolonged electrolysis. To probe the origin of this change, postcatalysis spectroscopic analyses were conducted. The Cu 2p XPS spectrum of the catalyst recovered after 6 h of electrolysis shows a slightly enhanced satellite shakeup feature compared with the pristine sample, indicating a subtle modification in the Cu oxidation state (Figure S30). In addition, extra peaks were observed in the S 2p spectrum, suggesting the presence of free thiol ligands based on the binding energy analysis (Figure S31). Complementary X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) analysis at the Cu K-edge reveals a relatively smooth absorption edge ∼8994 eV, further suggesting a modification in the electronic environment of the Cu centers during catalysis (Figure b). More importantly, Fourier-transformed extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) analysis displays a noticeable shift of the dominant coordination peak from approximately 1.6 Å in the synthesized NC to ∼1.9 Å after catalysis (Figure c). This shift toward a longer radial distance suggests an increase in Cu–Cu coordination and/or the loss of thiolate coordination environment thereby enhancement in oxygenated ligand coordination. The spectra fitting also indicate such losses of thiolate ligands while the enhancement in Cu–O coordination (Figure S32 and Table S5). However, the in situ FT-EXAFS data did not show any noticeable shift in the spectral features, suggesting that the active catalytic center remains associated with the mixed-valence Cu state (Figure S33). Thus, the observed changes indicate partial aggregation or structural reorganization of the nanocluster framework under prolonged electrochemical conditions. However, these changes are not directly associated with metal leaching, as confirmed by the ICP-MS experiments (Table S6). Therefore, such reorganization may reduce the number of well-defined active sites responsible for stabilizing the key intermediates involved in CH3OH formation, thereby redirecting the reaction pathway toward the release of CO intermediates. Therefore, although the S@Cu50 NC demonstrates promising catalytic performance and stable product selectivity during the initial stage of electrolysis, the observed structural evolution under prolonged catalytic operation highlights the inherent stability challenges associated with mixed-valence Cu NCs. These results highlight that preserving the structural integrity of atomically precise Cu NCs is essential for maintaining their valence-state-dependent catalytic selectivity during CO2 electroreduction, and this strategy may also be applicable to other core–shell nanocluster systems.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we demonstrate that atomic-level valence-state engineering provides a powerful strategy to modulate catalytic pathways in atomically precise Cu NCs. The sulfide-templated S@Cu50 NC, featuring an increased Cu­(I)/Cu­(II) ratio, preserves the structural framework of its parent analogue while inducing pronounced electronic perturbations. This subtle valence redistribution effectively breaks the intrinsic formate selectivity and redirects CO2 electroreduction toward deeper reduction products, enabling CH3OH formation. Combined experimental and theoretical analyses reveal that this mechanistic shift originates from the preferential stabilization of CO-derived intermediates over formate. Although partial structural evolution occurs under prolonged operation, the catalyst maintains stable selectivity during the initial stages, highlighting both its potential and the challenges associated with mixed-valence Cu systems. Overall, this work establishes valence-state control as a key design principle for tuning reaction selectivity in molecularly defined catalysts and advancing CO2 conversion toward value-added fuels.
