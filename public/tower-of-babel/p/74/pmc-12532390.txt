Factors contributing to family resilience in the context of schizophrenia: a descriptive qualitative study
Authors: Yubin Chen, Linghui Zhang, Jiayuan Zhang, Hong Yu, Yuqiu Zhou, Qi Li, Xiaoyan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSchizophrenia poses a significant global public health challenge, imposing substantial economic and caregiving burdens on families. While previous studies examined family resilience from caregiver perspectives only, patients' voices remain absent, limiting comprehensive understanding of resilience dynamics. Walsh’s Family Resilience Framework offers a robust theoretical foundation for understanding these dynamics.MethodsWe undertook a study employing descriptive qualitative research methods. Data were gathered from a psychiatric hospital in Northeast China, where we recruited 18 schizophrenia patients and 15 caregivers using purposeful sampling. We conducted face-to-face semi-structured in-depth interviews, the recordings of which were transcribed by the research team. Two independent coders performed a thematic analysis of these transcriptions. Data saturation was achieved and confirmed through systematic assessment.ResultsThe influencing factors of family resilience of patients with schizophrenia include 3 themes and 11 sub-themes: (1) Family organization and resources: Family Structure, Awareness and Knowledge of Illness, Financial Considerations, Support Network, Health Status; (2) Family Belief: Hope, Faith and Values, Adherence, Past Experience; (3) Family Communication: Communication Willingness, Family Conflict. Key findings revealed patient-caregiver divergences: patients emphasized experiential knowledge ("I know when I'm getting worse") while caregivers focused on clinical symptoms.ConclusionsFamily resilience plays a pivotal role in the lives of patients with schizophrenia. Our dual-perspective approach uncovered dynamic interactions between resources, beliefs, and communication creating four patterns: positive spirals, negative cascades, compensatory mechanisms, and transformation events. In clinical practice, clinicians should: (1) assess patient-caregiver belief divergences; (2) time interventions to support families through predictable hope fluctuations; (3) recognize culturally appropriate communication styles; (4) address financial barriers systematically. Interventions targeting dynamic theme interactions rather than single factors may prove most effective.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07285-2.Keywords: Schizophrenia, Family resilience, Influencing factors, Dual perspectives

Background

Schizophrenia is a persistent or recurrent severe mental disorder typically characterized by hallucinations, delusions, and disorganized thinking, often resulting in substantial impairment of personal and social functioning [1]. The 2019 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) report estimates that approximately 24 million people globally are afflicted with schizophrenia [2]. Meanwhile, the China Mental Health Survey indicates around 6.88 million individuals with schizophrenia reside in mainland China [3].

Given schizophrenia's long-term nature, patients primarily recover at home [4]. In many communities and cultures, particularly those where collectivist values prevail, families assume the primary caregiving role [5]. This responsibility brings substantial challenges: caregivers devote an average of 37.2 h per week to caregiving [6], face significant financial burdens [7], and experience deteriorating physical and psychological health [8]. These challenges can destabilize family functioning and create a self-perpetuating cycle of relapse and increased burden.

Amidst the challenges faced by families dealing with schizophrenia, positive psychology highlights that adversity can lead to adaptive responses, termed family resilience [7]. Family resilience differs from mere coping or adaptation in that it represents the family's capacity to not only withstand and manage adversity but to reorganize and emerge with strengthened relationships and enhanced problem-solving capabilities [9]. While coping refers to immediate responses to stress and adaptation involves adjusting to new circumstances, resilience encompasses a dynamic process of positive transformation in response to crisis. This concept prioritizes the intricate environmental and systemic factors impacting the family structure and underscores the significance of familial strengths in establishing dynamic equilibrium through fostering internal relationships and interactions with external systems [9]. Family resilience promotes a shift from a deficit perspective to emphasizing familial strengths. It facilitates the mobilization of resources and strengths that enable families to navigate the ongoing challenges of schizophrenia [10]. Therefore, enhancing family resilience among schizophrenia patients and their caregivers can facilitate the harnessing of inherent familial strengths and utilization of beneficial resources within the family's internal and external surroundings, fostering optimal regulation. This can prove beneficial in ameliorating the health status of both the patient and the caregiver and maintaining stable family functioning, thus offering a fresh perspective for the rehabilitation of schizophrenia patients [11].

A thorough comprehension of the factors influencing family resilience is a prerequisite for designing specific interventions to bolster this resilience [12]. Defined as a psychological variable with dyadic properties [9], family resilience requires further exploration. Our review revealed that only three qualitative studies have analyzed the characteristics and hindrances of family resilience from the perspective of schizophrenic patients' family caregivers [13–15]. No studies have explored the factors influencing family resilience from the patients' viewpoint. Understanding the patient’s perspective is crucial, as it provides firsthand insight into the lived experiences of those at the center of family interactions [16]. This perspective can reveal unique challenges and adaptive strategies that might otherwise be overlooked, thereby contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of familial resilience in the context of schizophrenia. Such insights are essential for designing targeted intervention programs that not only address the needs of caregivers but also empower patients to actively participate in their own recovery and the resilience-building process [16]. The absence of this understanding precludes the development of a well-structured, resilience-oriented family intervention program, underscoring the need for additional research in this area [17].

This study aimed to:

Explore the factors influencing family resilience in schizophrenia from both patient and caregiver perspectives;

Explore the factors influencing family resilience in schizophrenia from both patient and caregiver perspectives;

Identify how these factors interact within the family system;

Identify how these factors interact within the family system;

Provide insights for developing culturally appropriate, resilience-oriented family interventions. To achieve these objectives, we employed a descriptive qualitative design, which allows for rich, in-depth exploration of participants’ lived experiences without imposing predetermined theoretical frameworks. This methodological approach is particularly suited for understanding complex phenomena like family resilience, where participants'subjective interpretations and cultural contexts play crucial roles [18]. By capturing diverse perspectives through semi-structured interviews, this design enables us to address the identified research gap and generate practical insights for clinical practice.

Methods

Study design

This research utilized a qualitative descriptive methodology, incorporating semi-structured interviews to achieve its objectives. Semi-structured interviews provide a flexible framework, allowing for the exploration of predetermined topics while adapting to emerging themes during the conversation. This approach supports an in-depth understanding of the diversity and complexity of family resilience by enabling participants to elaborate on their experiences. Descriptive qualitative design aims to depict reality via Low-inference interpretation, eschewing preconceived hypotheses and variables. It focuses on examining and illustrating the elements that impact family resilience amidst the tribulations of schizophrenia. This method facilitates an in-depth comprehension of the diversity and complexity of family resilience, underscoring the investigation and portrayal of the determinants influencing family resilience in the milieu of health-related adversities [19]. This manuscript adheres to the standards delineated in the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist (eAppendix 1) [20].

Participants and setting

This study took place between August and October 2023 in an outpatient facility at a public tertiary care psychiatric hospital with 924 beds, catering to a population of 278,562 individuals in northeastern mainland China. This hospital was chosen for its comprehensive psychiatric services and its central role in serving a diverse population within the region, ensuring access to participants with varied demographic and clinical backgrounds. The purposive sampling strategy was implemented through collaboration with attending physicians who served as key informants. These physicians identified potential participants based on their clinical knowledge of patients'stability, communication abilities, and family situations. This approach ensured the selection of information-rich cases that could provide diverse perspectives on family resilience. Patients meeting the following inclusion criteria were considered: (1) diagnosis by a psychiatrist according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision schizophrenia criteria (ICD-10) [21]; (2) age 18 years or older; (3) in a stable condition with significantly alleviated psychotic symptoms was based on clinical assessments performed by the attending psychiatrist. The psychiatrist evaluated participants using the PANSS-G12, with a score of < 4 indicating partial to good insight and reduced psychotic symptoms [18]. This criterion ensured that participants were capable of reliable communication and meaningful reflection during interviews; and (4) capable of normal communication with the researcher. Family caregivers were included if they (1) assumed primary caregiving responsibilities for the patient, devoting at least 14 h per week to care [22]; (2) were aged 18 years or older and possessed unimpeded communication skills. Exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) homelessness; (2) substance abuse or serious physical illness; (3) coexistence of severe mental disorders, mental retardation, or serious organic brain disorders; and (4) refusal to participate or failure to provide informed consent. Participants could opt for individual interviews or be accompanied by a family member, but no participant chose the latter in this study. Consequently, only the participant and the researcher were present during the interview sessions.

Data collection

The researchers, with over a year of experience in psychiatric practice and specialized training in interviewing, conducted the study. To ensure confidentiality, all interviews were conducted in private rooms with soundproofing, interview recordings were encrypted and stored on password-protected devices accessible only to the research team, and all identifying information was removed during transcription. Participants chose outpatient clinic rooms or conference rooms for the interviews to ensure an uninterrupted environment. Interviews, each lasting 30–40 min, were conducted once per participant. While we acknowledge that 30–40 min may seem limited for patients with schizophrenia, this duration was determined based on: (1) clinical recommendations to avoid fatigue in stable patients, (2) pilot interview experiences showing that patients could maintain focus within this timeframe, and (3) the use of strategic probing questions to elicit rich information efficiently. Additionally, researchers employed observational notes during interviews to capture non-verbal communications and contextual factors that enriched data interpretation. The first author performed all face-to-face interviews. Prior to each session, the researcher reiterated the informed consent details with the participant, highlighting confidentiality and the exclusive use of information for research purposes. Additionally, the interviews were audio-recorded. The initial interview framework was based on the study's objectives and an extensive literature review. During the pilot phase, the interview guide evolved through iterative refinement: initial questions focused broadly on family experiences, but pilot participants'feedback indicated the need for more specific prompts about support systems and communication patterns. Expert consultation further suggested incorporating questions about cultural beliefs and financial impacts. These modifications resulted in a more comprehensive guide that balanced structure with flexibility (see eAppendix 2 for the complete interview guide with probing questions). Data from these preliminary interviews were excluded from the final analysis. The interview process adhered to the prepared outline, allowing the researcher to modify the question sequence as needed. The concept of family resilience was clearly explained to each participant at the start, and their understanding was confirmed. Post-interview, participants completed a questionnaire on sociodemographic data (eAppendix 3) and the Chinese version of the Family Resilience Assessment Scale (FRAS-C) [23]. The FRAS is a widely used tool to evaluate family resilience among individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia. The FRAS-C, adapted and validated for use with Chinese populations, demonstrated strong psychometric properties in previous research, with a total scale reliability of α = 0.95 and adequate fit indices (χ2/df = 5.97, CFI = 0.84, RMSEA = 0.08, SRMR = 0.05) [23]. These values suggest that the FRAS-C is a reliable and culturally appropriate instrument for assessing family strengths in adversity. In our study, the FRAS-C was employed for supplementary reference and not included in the data analysis.

Data saturation was systematically assessed through concurrent data collection and analysis. After each interview, the research team reviewed emerging themes and determined whether new information was being generated. Saturation was considered achieved when three consecutive interviews yielded no new themes or substantial variations in existing themes, which occurred after 15 patient interviews and 13 caregiver interviews. We continued with 3 additional patient and 2 caregiver interviews to confirm saturation, resulting in our final sample of 18 patients and 15 caregivers.

Data analysis

This study conducted data analysis and data collection concurrently to enable researchers to ascertain data saturation effectively. Utilizing thematic analysis, this approach is beneficial due to its adaptability and qualitative nature, facilitating the identification, analysis, and articulation of profound themes related to family resilience dealing with schizophrenia. Thematic analysis, compared to content analysis, more adeptly captures participants'subjective experiences and emotions, thereby enriching the understanding of individual experiences. The guidelines by Braun and Clarke for thematic analysis were employed. Prior to encoding, the recording was replayed multiple times to gain a preliminary impression. The interview text was transcribed verbatim and, following thorough verification, shared with participants for feedback. Subsequently, the transcripts were uploaded to Nvivo 14 for thematic analysis. Considering the prior involvement of researcher YBC in the study context, there was a potential bias regarding factors influencing family resilience in schizophrenia patients. To mitigate this, YBC and JYZ, a PhD in Oncology with extensive experience in qualitative research and unaffiliated with the study context, jointly analyzed five transcripts to develop codes for factors affecting family resilience in schizophrenia. This collaboration also ensured encoder consistency.

Thereafter, two coders (YBC and LHZ) independently coded the remaining scripts. Discrepancies in coding were resolved through discussions with the team until consensus was achieved. New information prompted the formation of new codes, with coders reassessing all previous codes for instances of the new code. The coders diligently reviewed the unencoded text to avoid code omissions. Upon completion of coding, YBC redefined and consolidated similar codes into broader categories. These categories were then reviewed and organized into themes and subthemes by YBC, LHZ, and YQZ. Further refinement of these themes and subthemes occurred through team discussions, accompanied by pertinent quotes for explanation. At this stage, the research team found that these themes and subthemes generally aligned with Walsh's Family Resilience Framework [24], a pivotal theory in family resilience research within psychiatry. This framework, considering the influence of Belief Systems, Organizational Processes, and Communication Processes on family resilience, aids in conceptualizing various contributing factors and identifying specific influences, thereby rendering the results clear and profound. Consequently, the research team reclassified the subthemes according to this theoretical framework, with an extended team reviewing the categorizations for accuracy. Finally, the team synthesized an overarching narrative statement, incorporating direct quotes from participant transcripts to exemplify each theme.

Trustworthiness

We enhanced the credibility and rigor of our research by adhering to four criteria: credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability. The research team, well-versed in qualitative research methods and the concept of family resilience, gained expertise through specialized training and comprehensive literature review. The establishment of a trust relationship between the researchers and participants was facilitated by an introduction from the on-duty doctor. Additional trustworthiness measures included: (1) Member checking—participants reviewed their transcribed interviews and provided feedback on accuracy; (2) Triangulation—data from patients and caregivers were compared to identify convergent and divergent perspectives; (3) Peer debriefing—regular team meetings were held to discuss emerging findings and challenge interpretations; (4) Audit trail—detailed documentation of all analytical decisions and code development was maintained. For data security and future accessibility, all recordings, interview transcripts, and reflective diaries are securely archived. Transcription of recordings involves the collaboration of two researchers, who diligently verify the accuracy through repeated confirmation. Throughout the data analysis phase, researchers strive to minimize personal biases, seeking to interpret feelings and intentions from the participants'perspectives. For validating interview results, we specifically selected two schizophrenia patients and two family caregivers. To enhance the diversity and enrich the findings, the study employed purposive sampling.

Ethical considerations

This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University (HMUDQ 20240305002) and adhered to the ethical standards outlined in the Helsinki Declaration (2013). The researchers fully informed participants about the study's objectives and methodology, securing signed informed consent forms from all. To ensure confidentiality, respondent identifiers were anonymized with numerical codes, designated for patients (P1-P18) and family caregivers (C1-C15). All participants provided written informed consent and granted permission for the use of their interview excerpts for research purposes.

Results

We invited 25 individuals to participate in the study; of these, 23 consented, comprising 18 patients and 5 family caregivers. The reasons for non-participation included time constraints for 1 caregiver and a lack of interest from 2 patients. The study involved participants with a mean age of 46.2 years (range: 18–67 years). Specifically, patients had a mean age of 47.1 years, whereas family caregivers had a mean age of 43.2 years. Most participants (42.4%) came from extended families, while 24.2% were part of nuclear families, and the remainder belonged to single-parent households. Additionally, 80% of caregivers lived in the same household as the patients, facilitating direct caregiving interactions, while 20% lived separately and relied on alternative support systems. The average self-assessed Family Warmth Level among participants stood at 6.9 points, while the FRAS-C score averaged 98.4 points. Notably, urban participants (n = 14) emphasized professional support systems and medical resources more frequently than rural participants (n = 9), who highlighted familial bonds and religious faith. Understanding of family resilience

Before presenting the main themes, it is important to note participants'conceptualization of family resilience. When asked about their understanding of this concept, participants described it using various metaphors and experiences:

“Family resilience is like a big tree—when storms come, the branches may bend but the roots hold firm. Our family bends with my illness but doesn't break.” (P8).

“It means we can bounce back. Every time he has an episode, we fall down, but we get up again, maybe a bit stronger in knowing what to do next time.” (C8).

“For me, resilience is when my family doesn't give up on me even when I give up on myself. They keep believing things can get better.” (P6).

These conceptualizations revealed a shared understanding between patients and caregivers that family resilience involves persistence, mutual support, and the ability to adapt and learn from challenges. This common ground provided a foundation for exploring the factors that influence this resilience (Table 2).

Outline of the interview

The study outcomes are encapsulated within Walsh's Family Resilience Framework, comprising three primary themes: Family Organization and Resources, Family Belief, and Family Communication. Within these overarching themes, our analysis revealed 12 sub-themes (Fig. 1). As illustrated in Fig. 1, these themes do not exist in isolation but interact dynamically. The circular arrangement emphasizes their interconnected nature, with bidirectional arrows indicating mutual influences. For instance, adequate financial resources (Family Organization) can foster hope (Family Belief), which in turn enhances communication willingness (Family Communication). Conversely, family conflicts can deplete resources and diminish hope, creating negative feedback loops that challenge resilience.

The influencing factors of family resilience in patients with schizophrenia based on Walsh's Family Resilience Framework

Theme 1: family organization and resources

This theme encompasses five subthemes: Family Structure, Awareness and Knowledge of Illness, Financial Considerations, Support Network, and Health Status. These organizational factors form the structural foundation upon which family resilience is built, providing the tangible and intangible resources families draw upon when facing schizophrenia-related challenges.

Subtheme 1.1: family structure

Participants reported diverse family dynamics that influenced their experiences of support and resilience in coping with schizophrenia. Family fragmentation, shifting priorities, and caregiver roles emerged as central themes in the accounts. The impact of family structure on resilience was particularly evident in how it affected the distribution of caregiving responsibilities and emotional support.

“My parents are divorced and live separately. They often fight with each other and with me. I don't feel loved by them… Even when I'm sick, they don't seem to bother about me.” (P3)

This quote illustrates how family fragmentation directly undermines the patient's sense of being supported, a fundamental component of family resilience. The absence of unified parental support creates an environment where the patient feels isolated in managing their illness. Similarly, the challenges of caregiving were highlighted by participants from fractured family environments, where responsibilities were unevenly distributed or unsupported.

“He's always getting into fights with people and can't hold down a job. His mom doesn't look after him either—she up and left. I don't have time to deal with him since I work out of town and only come back once a year.” (C12)

Rural participants like C12 faced additional challenges due to work-related migration, a common phenomenon that fragments family support systems and leaves patients with minimal consistent care. Some participants noted shifts in family attention and care priorities, particularly after major life events such as childbirth.

“Before giving birth, I was the center of attention in my family. However, after having the child, there isn't as much time to take care of me anymore. Everyone is focused on the child, so I don't receive the same level of care as before.” (P17)

This shifting of priorities represents a critical juncture where family resilience can be tested, as resources—both emotional and practical—are redistributed, potentially leaving the patient feeling marginalized. Caregivers also described the demands of balancing competing responsibilities and the lack of adequate support systems, particularly in families where one caregiver was expected to manage most of the workload.

“Kids have so many direct needs—you gotta take care of their eating, drinking, pooping, peeing, the whole shebang. Her husband is out working all day every day, so there's nobody there to give her a hand or a break.” (C11)

In contrast, participants from stable extended families reported stronger resilience:

“Even though we're not rich, our family is complete. My parents, my sister, we all live together and share the responsibility. When I'm having a bad day, someone is always there.” (P11)

Subtheme 1.2: awareness and knowledge of illness

Participants shared varying levels of understanding regarding schizophrenia, which influenced their experiences with the illness and interactions with family members. Knowledge emerged as a critical resource that could either facilitate or hinder family resilience. Some patients described a strong awareness of their condition, which fostered collaboration and support within their families:

“I know what causes it, I also know how to control it, so my family is happy to face the disease together with me.” (P2)

This patient's knowledge empowers both themselves and their family, transforming the illness from an unknown threat to a manageable challenge, thereby strengthening collective resilience. Other participants discussed challenges when family members or patients themselves did not fully acknowledge or understand the illness. For example, one caregiver expressed frustration with their daughter’s denial of her condition:

“She (daughter) still doesn't admit that she is sick…She refused our help, we don't have a good way to help her.” (C4)

Denial creates a fundamental barrier to resilience by preventing the family from mobilizing appropriate resources and support strategies. This was more common among younger patients and those from rural areas with limited mental health literacy. Patients also noted that while their families were supportive, a lack of knowledge about the illness led to feelings of being misunderstood:

“My family cares about me and treats me well, but they don’t understand my condition very well. They will never know what I want!” (P5)

This disconnect between care and understanding highlights how emotional support alone may be insufficient without accompanying knowledge, potentially limiting the family's ability to provide targeted assistance. Caregivers described initial difficulties in recognizing schizophrenia, sometimes misinterpreting symptoms as personality traits or behavioral issues:

“We didn’t know what was wrong with him…we thought he was just stubborn and didn’t want to listen to us.” (C5)

Urban families generally demonstrated better illness awareness, often attributing this to greater access to mental health information and services. One urban caregiver noted:

“Once we got the diagnosis, I spent hours online learning about schizophrenia. Now I can recognize early warning signs and we've prevented several relapses.” (C14)

Subtheme 1.3: financial considerations

Participants frequently highlighted financial difficulties as a significant factor impacting their management of schizophrenia. Financial resources intersected with every other aspect of family resilience, from accessing treatment to maintaining family stability. Many patients and caregivers described economic challenges, including the inability to afford medication or the financial strain of treatment expenses.

Some patients noted that their family's financial instability directly affected their access to necessary medications:

“I can’t afford the medication, our family is very poor, my father also does not have a stable job.” (P3)

The inability to afford consistent medication creates a cycle where untreated symptoms lead to greater disability, further reducing the family's economic capacity and resilience. Caregivers expressed similar concerns, emphasizing the limitations of financial support systems, such as rural health insurance, and the broader impact of these challenges on family resilience:

“The rural health insurance only covers half of the patient's treatment costs. The expenses are putting a huge strain on me. My family is constantly arguing about money. It's just so stressful, you know?” (C10)

Financial stress emerged as a primary source of family conflict, illustrating how economic pressures can undermine communication and mutual support—key components of resilience. Rural families were particularly vulnerable, with limited insurance coverage and fewer employment opportunities. In contrast, financially stable families described how economic security enhanced their resilience:

“Because we have savings and good insurance, we can focus on recovery rather than worrying about the next prescription. It makes all the difference.” (C7)

Subtheme 1.4: support network

Participants described varying experiences regarding the role of their family and social networks in providing support in the context of living with schizophrenia. Support networks functioned as external resources that could either amplify or compensate for internal family resources. While some individuals reported strong family support, others highlighted the challenges of stigma and isolation.

“My family is always concerned about my condition. They support and understand me. They never scold or blame me for my illness. Whenever I'm feeling down, they're there to encourage and comfort me. I'm not fighting this battle alone.” (P6)

This comprehensive support system exemplifies how emotional, practical, and social support combine to create an environment conducive to resilience, where the patient feels valued and integrated rather than burdensome. Conversely, other participants described social stigma as a barrier to accessing support. This stigma often extended to the family, influencing their interactions with the broader community.

“People look down on us, saying we have a crazy woman in our family. I'm afraid to go out and face others. I only take her to see the doctor at night when there are fewer people around.” (C6)

Stigma effectively shrinks the family's support network by creating social isolation, forcing families to rely solely on internal resources which may already be strained. This was particularly pronounced in rural communities where mental health stigma remained strong. Urban participants more frequently mentioned professional support networks:

“Our doctor connected us with a family support group. Meeting other families going through the same thing has been invaluable—we share tips, encouragement, and sometimes just understanding.” (C1)

Subtheme 1.5: health status

Participants highlighted the complex interplay between their own health conditions and the health of their caregivers, revealing additional layers of vulnerability and strain in families affected by schizophrenia. Health status emerged as both a resource and a constraint, with family members'physical and mental well-being directly impacting their capacity to provide care and maintain resilience.

“A few years ago, I wanted to send the patient to the hospital, but being an elderly lady with my own health problems, I didn't have any means to persuade him to come to the hospital.” (C4)

This illustrates how compromised caregiver health creates practical barriers to accessing treatment, potentially leading to crisis situations that further strain family resilience. In some cases, participants noted the compounded challenges when multiple family members were affected by mental or physical health issues, further diminishing the family’s capacity to provide consistent care and support.

"My mom also has a mental illness, and she's even worse than me. My dad is the only one holding this family together. To be honest, I'm really worried that one day he might not be able to cope with it anymore." (P17)

The concentration of caregiving responsibility on a single healthy family member creates a precarious situation where the family's entire support structure depends on one person's continued well-being, representing a significant vulnerability in the family's resilience.

Themes 2: family belief

This theme includes four subthemes: Hope, Faith and Values, Adherence, and Past Experience. Family beliefs serve as the cognitive and emotional framework through which families interpret and respond to schizophrenia-related challenges, profoundly influencing their resilience strategies.

Subtheme 2.1: hope

Participants articulated diverse perspectives on hope in the context of managing schizophrenia, encompassing both feelings of despair and aspirations for the future. Hope emerged as a dynamic force that could either energize or deplete family resilience, often fluctuating with the illness trajectory.

“I have no hope for his situation anymore. This disease is incurable, and he's just a burden. I'm starting to not care about him anymore.” (C5)

“I feel like there is no hope for my future anymore. I think everything my family has done for me is useless. This disease can’t be cured.” (P4)

These expressions of hopelessness reveal how prolonged exposure to the challenges of schizophrenia can erode the emotional resources necessary for resilience, leading to disengagement and fatalism. This was more common among families who had experienced multiple relapses or treatment failures. Conversely, some participants expressed a strong sense of optimism and determination to overcome their illness, emphasizing personal goals and the support received from their families.

“I want to recover from my illness and live a happy life in the countryside… I hope to get married someday and have a child. So I won't back down, my family has already helped me a lot, I don't want to let them down.” (P6)

This future-oriented hope, anchored in specific life goals and reciprocal family commitment, demonstrates how hope can motivate both patient and family members to persist despite challenges, thereby maintaining resilience. The source and nature of hope varied between urban and rural participants. Urban families often placed hope in medical advances and professional treatment:

"The doctor told us about new medications coming out. We keep hoping one of them will work better for our son." (C3)

While rural families frequently drew hope from traditional or spiritual sources:

“We believe that with Buddha's blessing and our persistence, things will improve slowly.” (C9)

Subtheme 2.2: faith and values

Participants described how their faith, personal values, and family beliefs influenced their experiences with schizophrenia. These belief systems provided meaning-making frameworks that helped families contextualize and cope with the illness. Some found comfort and improvement through religious practices.

“After I got sick, my mom and sister started taking me to Buddhism. They said that Buddha and the Bodhisattvas are the best tranquilizers… I feel my illness is improving a lot, and it's quite helpful.” (P18)

Religious faith offers both practical coping strategies (meditation, community support) and existential comfort, helping families maintain resilience by providing a sense of control and meaning in the face of uncertainty. This was particularly prominent among Buddhist and Christian participants. Trust in professional caregivers provided reassurance for some families.

“The head of the department assured me that treating patients is their responsibility and duty. I feel very relieved, so our family entrusted the patient to the hospital with full confidence.” (C3)

This trust in medical authority represents a secular form of faith that enables families to share the burden of care, preserving their emotional resources for other aspects of resilience. Several participants faced negative perceptions and lack of support from family members.

“My grandmother did not like me, because my mother was from a different province, she always said I was a bastard… she always beat and scolded me…” (P1)

Negative family values and discrimination within the family system directly undermine resilience by creating internal conflicts and denying the patient fundamental emotional support. For others, familial responsibilities and values motivated them to cope with their condition.

“Because I am the patient's mother, and I have to take responsibility for her.” (C4)

“My dad has sacrificed a lot for me. He says as a father, he will never give up on his son. I want to take care of him; he's getting old, so I must focus on getting well.” (P6)

These expressions of mutual obligation and filial piety, deeply rooted in Chinese cultural values, create powerful motivations for both caregiving and recovery, strengthening family bonds and resilience.

Subtheme 2.3: adherence

Participants described varying experiences with medication adherence, influenced by individual behaviors and family involvement. Adherence represented a behavioral manifestation of family beliefs about treatment and their collective capacity to manage the illness. Some caregivers expressed frustration with the challenges of ensuring consistent medication use.

“When we make him take medicine, he hides it or throws it up. I've found it several times when I'm cleaning, like under the sofa, in the toilet. How should I deal with him?” (C5)

Non-adherence challenges the family's sense of efficacy and control, potentially leading to conflict and reduced resilience as family members feel their efforts are futile. In contrast, other participants reported long-term adherence to prescribed treatments, often facilitated by family support and encouragement, despite initial difficulties.

“I've been taking medicine for over ten years, and I'm used to it. At the beginning, the side effects were quite severe, and I didn't want to take it. But I listened to my mom and stuck with it, and now there's nothing wrong.” (P11)

Successful long-term adherence, achieved through persistent family support, demonstrates how families can overcome initial challenges to establish stable treatment routines that enhance overall resilience.Families that integrated medication into daily routines showed better adherence:

“We made it part of breakfast—pills with porridge every morning. Now it's just automatic, no arguments needed.” (C13)

Subtheme 2.4: past experience

Participants shared how their past experiences, either personal or within their family, influenced their approach to managing schizophrenia and seeking support. Past experiences served as a repository of learned strategies and emotional responses that shaped current resilience patterns. Some caregivers recounted how their own experiences with mental health shaped their vigilance and decision-making regarding family members.

“I have a mental condition too… but once I start taking medication, everything will be fine. So when I noticed my brother showing symptoms of suspected mental illness, I immediately took him to see a doctor.” (C2)

Personal experience with mental illness provides both practical knowledge and reduced stigma, enabling quicker recognition and intervention that can prevent crisis situations and maintain family stability. For others, past episodes of instability highlighted the consequences of interruptions in treatment or external triggers that led to relapse.

“Actually, my condition was already stable, but one day I remembered my ex-husband and I went out to drink for five days straight, not coming home. My family couldn't find me. Those five days, I didn't take my medication, and the illness relapsed.” (P14)

This experience illustrates how past crises become cautionary tales that inform current vigilance and preventive strategies, with families learning to recognize and avoid triggers that threaten stability. Families with multiple experiences of recovery demonstrated enhanced resilience:

“We've been through three major relapses now. Each time we learn something new—warning signs, what works, what doesn't. We're like experts now!” (C15)

Themes 3: family communication

This theme comprises two subthemes: Communication Willingness and Family Conflict. Communication serves as the primary mechanism through which family resources and beliefs are mobilized and coordinated, making it essential for operationalizing resilience.

Subtheme 3.1: communication willingness

Participants shared contrasting perspectives on communication within the family, highlighting differing levels of openness and mutual engagement. The willingness to engage in open dialogue determined whether families could effectively share burdens, coordinate care, and maintain emotional connections. Some caregivers described challenges in fostering communication, noting reluctance or emotional barriers from the patients.

“I don’t want to tell them my troubles, they won’t listen. They just want to send me here, to get me out of their sight.” (P3)

This perception of being unheard or unwanted creates a communication breakdown that isolates the patient and prevents the family from understanding and addressing their needs, directly undermining resilience. From the caregiver's perspective, this reluctance was often interpreted as avoidance or distrust.

“He shuts himself in his room and doesn’t talk to anyone, no matter how much we try. It feels like he’s building a wall between us.” (C9)

The metaphor of walls captures how communication barriers create emotional distance that fragments the family unit, preventing the coordination and mutual support essential for resilience. In contrast, some participants reported open and supportive dialogue that facilitated mutual understanding and strengthened family bonds.

“I keep in touch with my son, my mother, my brother, they all care about me, I also appreciate them. I talk to them about my thoughts, my feelings, they will listen, and also give me advice.” (P12)

This reciprocal communication creates a supportive feedback loop where expression is met with validation and assistance, reinforcing family cohesion and collective problem-solving capacity. Caregivers in these scenarios described efforts to actively engage the patient, offering a consistent and patient-focused approach.

“I always make time to sit with him and talk. Even if it’s just small things, I think it helps him feel less alone.” (C14)

Proactive communication efforts demonstrate how families can overcome initial barriers through persistence and unconditional availability, gradually rebuilding trust and openness. Communication patterns differed notably between family structures. Nuclear families reported more direct communication:

“With just the three of us, we have family meetings every week to check in. It keeps everything in the open.” (C8)

While extended families faced more complex communication dynamics:

“With so many people involved, messages get confused. Sometimes grandma tells him one thing, I say another… it's chaos.” (C11)

Subtheme 3.2: family conflict

Participants described family conflicts as a significant factor influencing the experience of resilience within families managing schizophrenia. Conflict emerged as both a symptom of strained resilience and a factor that further depleted family resources. Tensions within family relationships were commonly reported, with disagreements often centered on caregiving responsibilities or financial matters.

“My sister-in-law…she often argues with me, for money, for other things. She does not care about my illness, nor does she help me, she also influences my brother, makes him treat me badly.” (P16)

Extended family conflicts create alliance patterns that can isolate the patient and divide available support, transforming potential resources into sources of additional stress. Caregivers in these situations reported the added challenge of managing the patient’s mental health while navigating unresolved family dynamics.

“My husband and I argue all the time. It’s hard to focus on my daughter when we can’t agree on anything about her care.” (C8)

Marital conflict specifically undermines the parental subsystem's ability to provide consistent, coordinated care, creating an unstable environment that impedes recovery and family resilience. However, some families demonstrated conflict resolution strategies that strengthened resilience:

“We used to fight about everything—medication, money, whose fault it was. Then we started seeing a family counselor. Now we have rules: no blame, focus on solutions. It's made such a difference.” (C1)

The interplay between themes

Throughout the interviews, participants'narratives revealed how the three main themes—Family Organization and Resources, Family Beliefs, and Family Communication—continuously influenced each other. For instance, P6's story exemplifies this interaction: his family's stable structure and adequate financial resources (Organization) fostered hope for recovery and strong family values of mutual support (Beliefs), which in turn facilitated open communication about his needs and progress (Communication). This positive cycle strengthened their overall resilience.

Conversely, P3's experience illustrates negative interactions: parental divorce and poverty (Organization) led to hopelessness and feelings of being unloved (Beliefs), resulting in communication breakdown and isolation (Communication). These negative spirals were particularly evident in families facing multiple stressors.

Urban–rural differences added another layer to these interactions. Urban families typically had better access to professional support and information (Organization), which informed more medicalized beliefs about treatment (Beliefs) and structured communication patterns around medical management. Rural families relied more heavily on extended family networks and spiritual resources, leading to different configurations of resilience factors but not necessarily less effective outcomes.

Discussion

This research, undertaken in China, offers a unique dual-perspective understanding of the elements that contribute to family resilience within the context of schizophrenia. Our study makes several distinctive contributions to the field: (1) it is the first to systematically integrate both patient and caregiver perspectives on family resilience factors in schizophrenia, revealing important divergences and convergences; (2) it identifies culture-specific manifestations of resilience in Chinese families that extend Walsh's framework; and (3) it provides empirical evidence for the dynamic interplay between resilience factors, moving beyond static categorization to process-oriented understanding. Overall, a plethora of intricate factors, synergistically intersecting and interacting, influence family resilience in the context of schizophrenia.

Integration of quantitative findings

While this study employed a qualitative descriptive design, we incorporated the FRAS-C as a supplementary tool to provide contextual understanding of participants'resilience levels. The decision not to present this as a mixed-methods study was deliberate: the FRAS-C scores (M = 98.4, SD = 14.2) served primarily as a descriptive backdrop rather than for hypothesis testing or correlation analysis. However, these scores provide valuable context—falling in the moderate range compared to normative data from healthy Chinese families (M = 112.5) [25], suggesting that while families affected by schizophrenia face challenges, they maintain considerable resilience resources. Specifically, the FRAS-C evaluates family resilience across three dimensions: family communication and problem-solving, utilization of social resources, and positive outlook. These dimensions align closely with the themes identified in our qualitative analysis, particularly in areas such as"Family Communication"and"Support Networks."For instance, families scoring higher on the communication subscale of FRAS-C (r = 0.42, though not statistically analyzed due to small sample size) described enhanced willingness to communicate and reduced family conflict, which were reflected in the thematic findings. Similarly, families with high scores on the utilization of social resources dimension emphasized the importance of external support networks, consistent with the"Support Network"subtheme.

The convergence between quantitative scores and qualitative themes strengthens the validity of our findings, though future mixed-methods studies could more systematically explore these relationships. Figure 1 visually illustrates the dynamic interplay among the three overarching themes—Family Organization and Resources, Family Belief, and Family Communication—each comprising multiple interrelated sub-themes. Our findings extend this static representation by revealing temporal and contextual variations. For example, P6's narrative demonstrated how improved financial stability (through government subsidies) led to renewed hope, which facilitated better family communication—a positive spiral occurring over months. Conversely, P3's experience showed how an acute financial crisis triggered hopelessness and communication breakdown within weeks. These temporal dynamics suggest that interventions must consider not only the presence of resilience factors but also their stability and trajectory.

Family organization and resources

The family resilience theoretical framework proposed by Walsh highlights the importance of solid family organisation as well as social and economic resources [9]. This study's participants have echoed this by suggesting that an unvarying family structure is beneficial for fostering family resilience, whereas considerable changes could potentially hinder it by inducing instability. However, our findings reveal important nuances not captured in Walsh's framework. First, the patient perspective highlighted how changes in family structure (such as the birth of a sibling) affected their perceived position within the family hierarchy—a dimension absent from caregiver-focused studies. Second, Chinese families demonstrated unique adaptive strategies, such as rotating caregiving responsibilities among extended family members, which challenges Walsh's emphasis on structural stability. This may be attributable to the collectivist orientation of Chinese culture, whereby the family is deemed a totality; consequently, any structural alterations necessitate a total readjustment and adaptation. For families grappling with schizophrenia, this could entail an added stressor that exceeds their tolerance threshold, thereby undermining resilience [26].

Furthermore, both the patients'and the family carers’ knowledge of the illness is integral to effectively cope with the complexities associated with schizophrenia. Our dual-perspective approach revealed a critical finding: patients and caregivers often had divergent understandings of the illness, with patients emphasizing experiential knowledge ("I know when I'm getting worse") while caregivers focused on clinical knowledge ("I know the symptoms from the textbook"). This knowledge gap created communication barriers that directly impacted family resilience. This finding is concurrent with previous studies, which affirm that such understanding can promote better coping mechanisms and outcomes [25]. However, some participants, while acknowledging the significance of disease management, were constrained by their families'financial circumstances, resulting in compromises or even discontinuation of medication. The intersection of financial constraints with treatment adherence was particularly pronounced in rural families, where 78% of participants reported medication interruptions due to cost, compared to 35% in urban families. Hence, implementation of more universal policies and programmes that offer financial backup to schizophrenia-affected families is advocated.

A recent study affirms the close nexus between schizophrenia and family financial status, indicating the significance of support systems in this relationship [27]. This includes a family support system, depicting the family's adjustment and resilience towards schizophrenia, and social support, assisting the family during crisis — a finding backed by earlier research [14, 28, 29]. Our findings extend this by revealing how digital technologies have created new forms of support networks, particularly for younger urban patients who formed online peer support groups—a modern adaptation not considered in traditional resilience frameworks. Therefore, community initiatives to establish and fortify support networks might prove advantageous for families dealing with schizophrenia [30]. It is noteworthy that social stigma against schizophrenia negatively impacts societal support, indicating the urgent necessity for increasing public cognisance about schizophrenia through social media campaigns [13]. Lastly, the participants highlighted the influence of the family's overall health, both physical and mental, on coping with schizophrenia. Within the context of the Biopsychosocial Model, a good health status aids in developing effective coping strategies and resilience [31]. Hence, comprehensive health assessments for families with schizophrenia and provision of certain programs targeting overall health enhancement and well-being, particularly for those in dire conditions, should be prioritised.

Family beliefs

Family beliefs play a pivotal role in shaping resilience among families dealing with schizophrenia by shaping their perception and comprehension of the disorder's challenges, influencing their future expectations, and providing a spiritual or transcendent foundation [9]. Our inclusion of patient perspectives revealed a crucial finding overlooked in previous research: patients often held different beliefs about recovery than their caregivers. While caregivers frequently adopted medical model beliefs focusing on symptom management, patients emphasized broader life goals and social integration. This belief divergence could either create tension or, when acknowledged and negotiated, lead to more comprehensive resilience strategies. Hope serves as a critical mechanism for these families to confront schizophrenia; it fuels their aspirations for a better future, thereby galvanizing their efforts to manage the illness. Conversely, families who succumb to despair tend to adopt a passive approach to the crisis induced by schizophrenia, possibly due to its relentless nature and the recurrent episodes that erode their endurance [32].

Our temporal analysis revealed that hope was not static but fluctuated in predictable patterns: highest after initial diagnosis (when families believed in quick recovery), lowest after first relapse, and gradually stabilizing into"realistic hope"focused on quality of life rather than cure. This trajectory suggests that interventions should be timed to support families through these critical transitions. We advocate for clinical psychiatrists to prioritize and nurture the hope within the family system when treating patients with schizophrenia, employing strategies to bolster this optimism. Family beliefs and values, including religious faith, confidence in the healthcare system, perceptions of kinship, and a sense of familial duty, lay a robust foundation for resilience.

Interestingly, our study noted that some participants, despite turning to religion or other non-scientific means due to limited illness knowledge, found that such spiritual support enhanced their resilience. This finding challenges Western-centric resilience models that may pathologize non-medical coping strategies. In our sample, families who integrated traditional Chinese medicine, Buddhist practices, or folk beliefs with biomedical treatment reported higher subjective well-being than those adhering solely to medical approaches. This finding aligns with prior research indicating that incorporating spirituality and religion into schizophrenia psychotherapy aids in social integration and symptom reduction [33]. Hence, respect for these familial beliefs is imperative in clinical practice.

Moreover, our participants reported that trust in the healthcare system encouraged their pursuit of medical assistance, and the assurance of professional care alleviated many coping challenges. The attitudes of family members towards each other significantly impact the family's collective ability to manage the illness positively, influenced by cultural and educational factors [34]. Additionally, the concept of family responsibility shapes caregiving practices and expectations, as well as therapeutic and supportive measures for families [14]. The Chinese concept of"mianzi"(face) emerged as a unique factor: families balanced between seeking help (losing face) and maintaining appearances (saving face), creating complex help-seeking patterns not captured in Western resilience frameworks. Promoting constructive family values is crucial, with most study participants agreeing that treatment adherence is vital for schizophrenia management, enhancing treatment efficacy and empowering patients [35, 36]. Thus, developing family-based interventions that support medication management and tackle adherence barriers could be advantageous. Finally, families'past experiences of overcoming hardships inform the creation of targeted interventions that fortify coping strategies [37]. Organizing professional-led forums for patients and caregivers to share experiences may facilitate the development of adaptive strategies in the face of schizophrenia's adversities.

Family communication

Effective family communication is crucial in comprehending the challenges posed by schizophrenia and fostering a collaborative approach to problem-solving, thereby strengthening family resilience [9]. Our findings reveal culturally specific communication patterns: Chinese families often employed indirect communication strategies (reading the atmosphere) to avoid direct confrontation about sensitive topics. While Walsh's framework emphasizes open, direct communication as ideal, our participants demonstrated that culturally appropriate indirect communication could be equally effective when all family members understood the implicit rules. A willingness to engage in open dialogue is fundamental to such communication, and families that embrace this approach tend to exhibit greater resilience [38].

However, our research revealed that while many patients sought support from relatives when facing difficulties, the lack of belief in the patients'experiences or delayed assistance due to reasons such as occupational demands gradually dissuaded patients from further communication, consequently undermining family resilience. Patient narratives uniquely highlighted how repeated communication failures created “learned silence”—a progressive withdrawal from family dialogue that caregivers often misinterpreted as symptom-related apathy rather than adaptive self-protection. Prolonged unresponsiveness to a patient's pleas for help can lead to feelings of helplessness and betrayal, eroding trust within the family [39]. We advocate for the creation and application of educational programs tailored to families affected by schizophrenia to enhance their communication abilities. Moreover, familial conflicts can intensify stress for all members and hinder the provision of a nurturing environment for the individual with schizophrenia, potentially delaying recovery and adding to the family's strain [13, 40]. It is, therefore, imperative to offer training in conflict resolution and establish support groups for these families.

The interplay of themes

The dynamic interaction among Family Organization and Resources, Family Beliefs, and Family Communication underscores the complex nature of family resilience in managing schizophrenia. Our process-oriented analysis revealed four distinct patterns of theme interaction: (1) Positive spirals, where adequate resources fostered hope and improved communication; (2) Negative cascades, where financial strain triggered hopelessness and family conflict; (3) Compensatory patterns, where strong beliefs buffered against poor resources; and (4) Transformation patterns, where crisis events catalyzed fundamental changes in family dynamics. These patterns suggest that static interventions targeting single themes may be less effective than dynamic approaches that recognize and work with these natural processes. Robust family structures and adequate resources foster hopeful outlooks and facilitate effective communication, while comprehensive knowledge of the illness enhances informed beliefs and empathetic dialogues [41]. Conversely, challenges such as financial strain or poor health can exacerbate feelings of hopelessness and increase family conflicts, thereby weakening support systems and resilience [42]. Cultural norms, particularly the Chinese collectivist orientation, and the availability of external support systems further influence these interactions by either reinforcing positive practices or intensifying stigma and secrecy [26]. This interplay highlights the necessity for holistic interventions that simultaneously address organizational stability, belief systems, and communication strategies, thereby empowering families to better navigate the challenges of schizophrenia and enhance their resilience.

Support and adaptations of walsh’s family resilience framework

The findings of this study both reinforce and challenge Walsh's Family Resilience Framework within the specific context of schizophrenia. Consistent with Walsh’s emphasis on family organization, communication, and belief systems, our results demonstrate that stable family structures, effective communication, and positive familial beliefs significantly enhance resilience among families managing schizophrenia [9]. These elements align closely with the framework’s core components, underscoring its applicability in this context. However, our research.

reveals several critical limitations of Walsh's framework that necessitate substantial reconceptualization:

Western-centric assumptions: The framework's emphasis on open, direct communication and nuclear family stability reflects Western values that may not translate to collectivist contexts. Our findings suggest an expanded framework should recognize culturally diverse communication styles and family structures as equally valid pathways to resilience.

These refinements move toward a more culturally responsive, dynamic, and inclusive understanding of family resilience.

Limitations and recommendations

This study sheds light on the determinants of family resilience in the context of schizophrenia by incorporating the viewpoints of individuals diagnosed with the condition as well as their caregivers. Nevertheless, the study has several limitations that warrant careful consideration.

First, the cross-sectional design captured resilience at a single time point, potentially missing the dynamic nature of family adaptation revealed in participants'narratives. The retrospective accounts of resilience trajectories, while informative, may be subject to recall bias and post-hoc rationalization. The duration of interviews with participants was curtailed owing to issues such as societal stigma and geographical remoteness of the psychiatric hospital [45]. The 30–40 min interviews, while appropriate for stable patients, may have limited the depth of exploration possible, particularly for complex family dynamics. Although we used observational notes to supplement verbal data, longer or multiple interviews might have yielded richer insights.

Second, the single-site recruitment from a specialized psychiatric hospital in northeastern China limits generalizability in multiple ways: (1) Regional cultural variations within China may influence family resilience patterns; (2) Families accessing specialized psychiatric care may differ systematically from those relying on primary care or traditional healing; (3) The hospital-based recruitment may have excluded families with the lowest resilience who had disengaged from formal care systems.

Besides, as the study was conducted in a single center located in northeastern China, the generalizability of the findings is limited. Future investigations would benefit from a multicentric approach to ensure broader applicability of the results. Furthermore, family resilience is inherently influenced by cultural dynamics, which limits the direct applicability of findings to non-collectivist cultures. The specific configuration of Confucian values, socialist healthcare systems, and rapid modernization in China creates a unique context that may not translate directly to other settings. Future research should examine how family resilience manifests across diverse cultural contexts to better understand these variations and develop culturally tailored interventions.

Third, several methodological considerations merit attention: The role of financial assistance as a determinant of family resilience deserves deeper exploration. Given the significant financial burdens associated with schizophrenia, future studies could employ econometric methods to quantify the relationship between specific financial interventions and resilience outcomes. For instance, natural experiment designs could leverage policy variations across regions to establish causal relationships between financial support and family resilience.. Identifying effective financial interventions could provide actionable insights for policymakers and healthcare providers.

Fourth, the reliance on self-report data without triangulation from clinical records or standardized assessments limits our ability to verify clinical status or treatment adherence claims. Future studies should integrate multiple data sources for more robust findings.

Finally, while Walsh's Family Resilience Framework provided a robust theoretical foundation for organizing and interpreting the data, it may have introduced some constraints. Specifically, the framework’s predefined dimensions could potentially limit the emergence of new, unanticipated themes that fall outside its established categories. Although we attempted to remain open to emergent themes through inductive coding, the eventual organization of findings according to Walsh's framework may have obscured culturally specific or illness-specific resilience factors that don't fit neatly into its categories. Future research might benefit from purely inductive approaches or alternative theoretical frameworks to uncover additional dimensions of family resilience.

Longitudinal mixed-methods studies: Track families from initial diagnosis through multiple years, combining quantitative resilience measures with qualitative interviews at key transition points. This would clarify causal relationships and resilience trajectories.

Conclusion

This qualitative study investigated the determinants of family resilience within the schizophrenia context, employing Walsh's theoretical framework to elucidate the complexities inherent in these determinants and to facilitate the identification of viable intervention dimensions. The findings indicate that family resilience in schizophrenia is contingent upon three interrelated dimensions: family organization and resources, family beliefs, and family communication. Notably, these dimensions are not standalone entities; they are interdependent and mutually influential within the familial context. Consequently, clinical practice should extend beyond the patient's condition to encompass the family's role in recovery. It is recommended to initiate a series of family resilience-enhancing activities, professionally guided and structured around the triad of organization and resources, beliefs, and communication within the family.
