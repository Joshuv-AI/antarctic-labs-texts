Evaluation of Pediatric Cardiomyopathy Patients in Pediatric Intensive Care Unit
Authors: Şeyda Kesen, Merve Havan, Mehmet Ramoğlu, Tayfun Uçar, Ercan Tutar, Tanıl Kendirli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:Cardiomyopathies (CMP) are an important cause of end-stage heart failure in childhood. The study aims to evaluate the clinical features, treatments, and outcomes of the patients diagnosed with CMP followed in the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU).Materials and Methods:This retrospective study was conducted using the data of the patients followed in the PICU between January 1, 2008, and December 31, 2019. Demographic information of the patients, CMP types, informations of echocardiography and angiography, findings upon admission to the PICU, medical treatments applied, respiratory and circulatory support, cardiac transplantation status, and results were examined.Results:A total of76 patients hospitalized with the diagnosis of CMP were included in the study. Among them, 39 (51%) patients were female. The majority (68.4%) were diagnosed with dilated CMP. The remaining patients were diagnosed with noncompaction left ventricular (LV) dilated CMP (14.5%), restrictive CMP (10.5%), hypertrophic CMP (5.3%), and noncompaction LV restrictive CMP (1.3%). Diuretics, inotropes, and invasive mechanical ventilation were used in 90.8%, 93.4%, and 75% of patients, respectively. While 43.4% (n = 33) of the patients needed extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support during their PICU hospitalization, 15.8% (n = 12) received LV assist device (LVAD) and 2.6% (n = 2) biventricular assist device (BiVAD) support. Heart transplantation was performed in 10 patients (13.2%). Finally, 40 (52.6%) of the patients died.Conclusion:Pediatric patients with CMP with severe heart failure require inotropes, mechanical ventilation, ECMO, LVAD, BiVAD, and heart transplantation, which are crucial for lifesaving measures, along with intensive care follow-up.

Introduction

Cardiomyopathies (CMP) are a group of diseases that directly change the structure of the heart and lead to deterioration in myocardial function. Cardiomyopathy is rare in children but carries a significant risk of morbidity and mortality when diagnosed.1 It is most common in children under 1 year of age. Cardiomyopathy is the main indication for heart transplantation in childhood, especially in children older than 1 year. While some individuals may be asymptomatic, approximately 40% of children with a symptomatic CMP either undergo a heart transplant or die within 5 years.2

This group of diseases, which is characterized by heart muscle involvement, can mostly result in heart failure. In 1995, the World Health Organization (WHO) divided it into 5 groups: dilated CMP (DCMP), hypertrophic CMP (HCMP), restrictive CMP (RCMP), arrhythmogenic right ventricular dysplasia/CMP, and unclassifiable CMPs.3 The causes of pediatric CMP are variable, ranging from genetic variations that affect the myocardium to systemic diseases that cause diffuse myocardial damage.4 Pediatric CMP are generally progressive and have a high mortality rate. Identifying risk factors and high-risk populations can change the course of the disease and be useful in early diagnosis and treatment.2

The aim of this study was to evaluate the clinical and laboratory characteristics, admission findings, disease course, treatment modalities (including extracorporeal therapies and cardiac support devices), and mortality outcomes of pediatric patients diagnosed with CMP and followed in the PICU. The study also aimed to identify possible factors associated with mortality.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

This retrospective, observational, single-center study included 76 patients who were diagnosed with CMP and treated in the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) between January 1, 2008, and December 31, 2019. All patients who were treated in the PICU with a diagnosis of CMP during the specified period were included in the study without any exclusion criteria. The study was approved by the local ethics committee of Ankara University Faculty of Medicine (Approval No: İ5-218-19, Date: November 14, 2019). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design of the study.

Data Collection

Patient data were collected retrospectively from hospital records and electronic databases. A standardized data form was used to record demographic information, admission findings, comorbidities, family history, physical examination findings, laboratory results, and results of metabolic and genetic screenings. Echocardiographic findings at admission, including z-scores for left ventricular internal diameter at end-diastole (LVIDd) and left ventricular internal dimension (LVIDs) diameters adjusted for body surface area, were documented⁵. Information on treatments—including diuretics, inotropic support, and other cardiac medications—was collected. The need for respiratory support, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), cardiac assist devices (LV assist device, biventricular assist device (BiVAD)), dialysis, and heart transplantation was also recorded. Complications during hospitalization and transplant list status were noted. Patient outcomes were classified as survival or death. Mortality rates and possible contributing factors were evaluated.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 22.0 (IBM SPSS Corp.; Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation, median (min-max), or number and percentage. Appropriate statistical tests—including independent samples t-test, Mann–Whitney U, Kruskal–Wallis, Chi-square, and Fisher’s exact tests—were used for group comparisons based on the distribution and type of variables.

The distribution of continuous variables was assessed prior to statistical testing. The specific statistical test used for each comparison is indicated in the footnotes of the relevant tables. A P-value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

The mean age at diagnosis for the 74 patients with accessible data was 69.49 ± 73.82 months, with a median age of 28.5 (1-221) months. The demographic characteristics of the patients, age distribution, complaints at presentation, accompanying diseases detected by genetic and metabolic screening, physical examination, and laboratory findings at the time of hospitalization are given in Table 1.

Distribution and Etiology of Cardiomyopathy

Of 76 patients, 68.4% (n = 52) had DCMP, 5.3% (n = 4) had HCMP, 10.5% (n = 8) had RCMP, 14.5% (n = 11) had noncompaction LV DCMP, and 1.3% (n = 1) had noncompaction LV-RCMP. Etiologies according to CMP types are presented in Table 2.

A documented family history was available for 72 of 76 patients. Parental consanguinity was present in 47.3% (n = 36) of patients. A known family history of cardiac disease was noted in 22.3% (n = 17).

Echocardiographic Findings

Echocardiographic findings at admission are presented in Table 3. Pulmonary hypertension was detected in 17.1% (n = 13) of patients, and pericardial effusion was observed in 7.9% (n = 6), with a mean thickness of 8.66 ± 5.5 mm and a median of 7.5 (4-18) mm.

Medical Treatments

Medical treatments administered in the PICU are summarized in Figure 1. Diuretic therapy was given to 90.8% (n = 69) of patients, and inotropic therapy was initiated in 93.4% (n = 71), with milrinone being the first inotrope in 69.7% (n = 53).

During hospitalization, various cardiac medications and vasoactive agents were used, including dopamine, adrenaline, noradrenaline, levosimendan, and others.

Respiratory Support

High-flow nasal cannula was administered to 11.8% of patients (n = 9), continuous positive airway pressure to 10.5% (n = 8), and bilevel positive airway pressure to 14.5% (n = 11). A total of 75% (n = 57) of patients required invasive mechanical ventilation, with a mean duration of 21.52 ± 20.47 days. Tracheostomy was performed in 5.3% (n = 4) of patients.

Advanced Mechanical Support and Transplantation

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support was provided to 43.4% of patients (n = 33). Of these, 5 patients were transferred with ECMO from external centers, and 36.8% (n = 28) received ECMO during their stay in the PICU. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation was required in 45.8% (n = 22) of 48 newly diagnosed patients. Among those who received ECMO, 21.2% (n = 7) underwent heart transplantation. A left LVAD was implanted in 15.8% (n = 12), a total artificial heart (TAH) in 1.3% (n = 1), and a BiVAD in 2.6% (n = 2) of patients. Overall, 46.1% (n = 35) of patients were listed for heart transplantation, and among them, 13.2% (n = 10) underwent the procedure. Among transplant recipients, 60% (n = 6) had DCMP, 30% (n = 3) had RCMP, and 10% (n = 1) had noncompaction LV DCMP.

Of the 10 heart transplant recipients, 4 died: 2 under LVAD, 1 under TAH, and 1 not receiving any mechanical support.

Complications

Complications occurred in 75% (n = 57) of patients during hospitalization, including bleeding, ischemia, infection, thromboembolism, neurologic complications, arrhythmia, and pneumothorax. The distribution of complications is shown in Table 4.

Mortality and Associated Factors

Among the 76 patients with CMP, 47.4% (n = 36) died. Mortality rates based on demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 5. A statistically significant difference in mortality was observed between sexes (chi-square test, P: .012), with male patients showing a higher mortality rate (62.2%) compared to female patients (33.3%). There was also a significant difference in age at diagnosis between survivors and deceased patients (Mann–Whitney U Test, P: .048). The mean age at diagnosis was 45.8 ± 56.2 months (median: 14.3 months) in survivors and 94.5 ± 82.3 months (median: 82 months) in deceased patients. Specifically in the DCMP group, older age at diagnosis was significantly associated with higher mortality (Mann–Whitney U Test, P: .007). The mean age at diagnosis in this group was 80.4 ± 77.6 months, with a median of 50 months (1-221). When patients were analyzed in age-based subgroups, a significant trend was observed indicating that increasing age correlated with increased mortality. Although mortality rates were compared across different CMP subtypes, the small number of patients in the non-DCMP groups limited the statistical power to detect significant differences among these subgroups. No significant differences were found in brain natriuretic peptide (BNP), Troponin I, or echocardiographic parameters (EF, FS, LVIDd Z, LVIDs Z) at PICU admission between deceased and surviving patients (Mann–Whitney U test, P > .05). Among ECMO patients, 72.7% (n = 24) died, with significantly higher mortality than non-ECMO patients (Chi-square test, P < .01). Among the 12 patients who underwent LVAD implantation, 58.3% (n = 7) died. One of the 2 BiVAD patients died. Among the 10 heart transplant patients, 4 died. Of the 57 patients who developed complications, 34 died. Mortality was significantly higher in patients with bleeding, ischemia, and renal failure requiring CRRT (Chi-square test, P < .05).

Discussion

Cardiomyopathies are a group of diseases that affect the heart’s structure, impairing myocardial function and often leading to heart failure with a high mortality risk.1,3 It is estimated that over 100 000 children worldwide are affected by CMPs at any given time. In the United States, the incidence of CMP in children is about 1.13 per 100 000 children.6 This study represents one of the earliest and most comprehensive evaluations of critically ill CMP patients in the PICU.

Cardiomyopathy incidence is higher in children under 1 year. A multicenter study by Wilkinson et al6, 7 found a 43% diagnostic rate in children under 1, and another study reported a higher incidence in boys than girls. In this study, 32.4% of patients were diagnosed before 1 year. Age at diagnosis was linked to mortality, with a delayed diagnosis leading to higher mortality. Alvarez et al’s8 2011 study showed that children diagnosed with idiopathic DCMP after 6 years had higher mortality rates than those diagnosed earlier. The study also found a significant association between older age at diagnosis and increased mortality in DCMP patients. Among the patients, 39 (51%) were female, and 37 (49%) were male, with a male-to-female ratio of 0.94. This differs from the literature, likely due to the small sample size in the PICU. A significant difference in mortality rates was observed between genders, with males having higher mortality.

Published reports indicate that DCMP and HCMP are the most common types.7 In the study of Jin et al, the frequency of DCMP was found to be 66.4% and the frequency of HCMP to be 23.4%.9 Wilkinson et al,6 using PCMR (Pediatric Cardiomyopathy Registry) data, reported DCMP in 54% and HCMP in 32% of cases. This study aligns with these findings, showing DCMP as the most common type, but differs in the prevalence of other types. This may be due to the study focusing solely on PICU patients, as those with HCMP generally require less intensive care.

The mortality rate of the patients with a diagnosis of CMP followed in the PICU was 47.4%. Studies have reported that approximately 40% of children with a symptomatic CMP underwent a heart transplant or died within 5 years.2 The type of CMP was not associated with mortality in the study for patients followed in the PICU.

As a result of studies conducted so far, it is known that high BNP values are associated with poor prognosis in patients with CMP.10-12 Sato et al13 reported that serum Troponin T concentrations are increased, especially in patients with idiopathic DCMP and poor prognosis. However, although Troponin I and T are highly sensitive and specific indicators of cardiac myocyte damage, further studies are required to determine the association between Troponin values and disease progression in patients with CMP.14,15 In the study, no association with mortality was found for BNP and Troponin I values. This discrepancy with the literature may be due to the limited sample size in the study. More comprehensive and detailed studies are needed, especially regarding the prognostic value of Troponin values and their relationship with mortality.

In this study, no significant difference was found between the deceased patients and surviving patients groups in terms of Biplane EF, FS, LVIDd Z score, and LVIDs Z score at PICU admission. In order to obtain more reliable information about the prognostic values of echocardiographic findings, studies that comparatively evaluate echocardiographic findings in a larger patient group and at different times of the disease are considered necessary.

Thirty-three (43.4%) of the patients were followed with ECMO support. Among the newly diagnosed patients, 45.8% required ECMO. Andrews et al16 reported that ECMO was used in 12.5% of CMP patients presenting with heart failure for the first time. This discrepancy may be due to the patient group consisting primarily of critically ill patients. Twenty-four (72.7%) of the patients who underwent ECMO died, with the mortality rate significantly higher in this group (P < .01). This finding may be attributed to the poor prognosis of the patient population, as ECMO is typically used in cases with a high mortality risk. In line with the findings, a recent multicenter study reported an in-hospital mortality rate of 44% among 540 pediatric patients who received ECMO support, although the patient group consisted of more critically ill individuals, which may explain the higher mortality rate observed in the cohort.17

Ventricular assist device support should be considered in any child with end-stage acute or chronic heart failure unresponsive to conventional medical therapy. However, experience with VADs in children is limited, and publications are based on a small number of patients.18 The LVAD was applied to 12 (15.7%) of the patients in the study, and 7 (%58.3) of them died. One of the 2 patients supported with BiVAD also died. The mortality rate for VAD-supported CMP patients in the PICU was 57.1%. In the study of Miera et al,19 which included 149 cardiac patients followed up with VAD, 65.2% of whom were children with a diagnosis of CMP, the mortality rate was found to be 32.2%. In another study published by Blume et al,20 78% of which consisted of children with CMP, it was reported that 17% of 99 patients followed up with VAD died. The differences in mortality rates between these studies and ours may be due to variations in patient groups and the limited number of VAD-supported patients in the study.

Approximately 20% of CMP patients on the heart transplant list die while waiting for a donor.21-23 Alsoufi et al24 reported a waiting list mortality of 10.9% in children with end-stage CMP in 2015. In this study, 35 patients (46%) were on the heart transplant list, and 18 (23.6%) died while awaiting a donor. The waiting list mortality in the cohort was 51.4%, which can be attributed to the critical illness of the patients and the low organ donation rate in Türkiye. In addition, the inability to offer Berlin Heart Excor, a mechanical support device for patients under 20 kg, as a treatment option is one of the main reasons for patients who die while waiting on the heart transplant list. Pietra et al25 reported a 19.1% post-transplant mortality rate in 261 DCMP patients, while the study found a 33.3% post-transplant mortality rate for DCMP patients. Although literature reports a 70% survival rate post-transplant,26 the study showed a 60% survival rate. According to a single-center study conducted by Kantor PF et al27 in Toronto with 189 DCMP patients, a significant improvement in survival was found in pediatric DCMP patients who received a heart transplant, compared to the overall survival of DCMP patients who did not. Despite heart transplantation being a definitive treatment for end-stage CMP, no statistically significant survival difference was found between transplant and non-transplant patients in the study, likely due to the small sample size.

Complications such as renal failure, infection, and neurological issues were observed in 75% of the patients in the study. The mortality rate was significantly higher in patients with complications compared to those without. Furthermore, patients with bleeding, ischemia, and those requiring CRRT had a notably higher mortality rate. While the literature lacks sufficient data on complications during the follow-up of CMP patients, it is evident that a multisystem approach to prevent complications during follow-up plays a crucial role in improving prognosis.

Limitations

This study is a retrospective, single-center study, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Some patient data could not be accessed due to incomplete file records. In addition, limited access to advanced genetic and metabolic testing, especially in the earlier years of the study period, may have led to underdiagnosis or misclassification of etiologies.

Conclusion

Pediatric CMP remains a life-threatening condition with high morbidity and mortality, particularly among patients requiring intensive care. The findings highlight that late diagnosis and the need for advanced mechanical support are strongly associated with poor outcomes. The notably high mortality among patients who received ECMO or were listed for heart transplantation underscores the need for earlier intervention, better donor availability, and improved access to pediatric-specific mechanical circulatory support devices. Early recognition of CMP and timely referral to centers with advanced care capabilities are essential for improving outcomes. National health policies should prioritize increasing awareness, organ donation rates, and the pediatric mechanical support infrastructure to reduce mortality in this vulnerable population. Further multicenter, prospective studies are needed to better understand risk factors and improve clinical outcomes.
