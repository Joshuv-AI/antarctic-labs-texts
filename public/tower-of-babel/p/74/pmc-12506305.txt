Physiological and ecological response of Cinnamomum camphora under drought stress
Authors: Taoyuan Wang, Demei Xia, Ting Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study focused on one-year-old Cinnamomum camphora seedlings and set up three stress environments: drought, waterlogging, and drought rehydration. The physiological and ecological responses of Cinnamomum camphora under different stress conditions were comprehensively analyzed using methods such as biomass measurement, gas exchange parameters (including net photosynthetic rate, stomatal conductance, transpiration rate), stable carbon isotope ratios, and analysis of non structural carbohydrate content. The results showed that drought stress significantly reduced the total biomass of Cinnamomum camphora (23.57 ± 0.56 g, compared to the control group of 40.84 ± 0.61 g), especially the leaf biomass (4.30 ± 0.45 g), which was 59.28% lower than the control group. The root biomass of the waterlogging stress group was 8.86 ± 1.15 g, which was 38.51% lower than the control group. Stable carbon isotope analysis showed that under drought stress, the δ 13 C value of Cinnamomum camphora significantly increased (-26.7‰), indicating that plants improve water use efficiency by closing stomata; The waterlogging stress reduced the δ 13 C value (-29.1‰), indicating limited photosynthetic efficiency. The rehydration experiment showed that the leaf water potential quickly recovered after rehydration, and the photosynthetic parameters (such as photosynthetic rate and stomatal conductance) gradually increased, demonstrating good drought tolerance. Compared with previous studies on single stress or a single organ, this study analyzed the effects of drought, waterlogging, and rehydration stress on the biomass of different organs in Cinnamomum camphora, revealing the resource allocation strategies and physiological adaptation mechanisms of Cinnamomum camphora under different stresses. This study provides a scientific basis for the adaptive management of Cinnamomum camphora in ecological restoration and urban greening.Keywords: Cinnamomum camphora, Waterlogging, Drought, Biomass, Water potential

Introduction

Extreme weather events have become more frequent and more intense as a result of global climate change. These events include, but are not limited to, droughts, floods, heat waves, and cold waves, which have far-reaching impacts on global ecosystems [3, 30]. The growth and distribution of plants, as the basic producers of ecosystems, are directly affected by climate change. In urban environments, plants face even more complex survival challenges due to microclimate changes caused by urbanization, such as the urban heat island effect [25]. With the improvement of ecological protection awareness and the popularization of the concept of sustainable development, more and more cities begin to pay attention to ecological protection and environmental restoration [2, 7]. Tree species with high ecological value, such as Cinnamomum camphora, have become one of the choices for urban greening and ecological restoration because of their functions in purifying air, regulating climate, and maintaining soil and water [30]. Several scholars have studied the growth and physiological status of Cinnamomum camphora. Mao et al. [17] conducted an experiment to analyze the effect of combined application of nitrogen and phosphorus during the growth of Cinnamomum camphora. A variety of fertilization concentration gradients were set up in the experiment, and the changes in height and diameter of different Cinnamomum camphora seedlings after fertilization were determined. The outcomes of the experiment demonstrated that phosphorus encouraged the formation of the Cinnamomum camphora root system. The role of bacteria that promote plant growth in the growth of Cinnamomum camphora seedlings was studied by Shi et al. [18] Organic fertilizer was used as substrate in the experiment and experimental and control groups were set up to compare the growth results. The experimental results showed that plant growth promoting bacteria could effectively accelerate plant growth. Manghwar et al. [16] stated that waterlogging stress poses a greater threat to major crops and plants, and to reveal the gene regulatory network and functional characteristics of important genes related to waterlogging tolerance, they discussed the current understanding of the molecular mechanisms of plant response to waterlogging stress, as well as its impact on plant growth, development, and productivity. Wang et al. [23] investigated the effects of drought stress on the growth, photosynthetic capacity, and physiological responses of Cinnamomum camphora seedlings in Guangxi, China to assist in the development of plant protection and management strategies. The results showed that with the increase of drought stress, the basal diameter growth and seedling height growth significantly decreased. The basal diameter growth of mild drought, moderate drought, and severe drought treatments decreased by 23.79%, 114.85%, and 175.50%, respectively.

Some scholars have also studied the effects of stress and adversity on plant growth. Cui et al. [6] studied the growth changes of trees in polluted environments with respect to the environmental pollution by copper and cadmium. In the experiment, wind frequency data were collected and the effects of atmospheric cadmium and mercury on the annual rings of Cinnamomum camphora were analyzed. The experimental results showed that the annual rings of Cinnamomum camphora were able to show cadmium deposition. The effects of flood stress (FS) and drought stress (DS) on the growth of Cinnamomum camphora were studied by Zhao et al. [28] During the process, a 40 day differentiated water potential treatment was carried out to monitor the growth status of corresponding roots, shoots, and leaves. The experimental results showed that DS caused an increase in carbon consumption in Cinnamomum camphora and that alternating drought and flooding may lead to stress responses in the trees. Kong et al. [13] conducted a study on the growth of Cinnamomum camphora in different iron levels. The effects of desferrioxamine and divalent iron content in plants were analyzed using a multidisciplinary approach. The results of the experiment showed that deferoxamine could improve the photosynthesis effect of Cinnamomum camphora and could effectively alleviate the chlorosis of clostridium camphorum. Luo et al. [15] carried an experiment on the growth and role of plants in environments with large amounts of apoplastic material. The chemical defense substances of Cinnamomum camphora were extracted and the selective response of microorganisms was tested in the medium. The outcomes revealed that the chemical substances of Cinnamomum camphora would substantially increase the environmental decomposition rate of apoplastic material and enhance the activity of acid phosphatase in soil. Tai et al. [20] conducted a study on the role of Cinnamomum camphora on the environment in global warming. In the experiment, the parameters of Cinnamomum camphora forest were collected using laser point cloud, and the carbon stock of Cinnamomum camphora forest was analyzed after single-wood partitioning to predict the future growth trend of Cinnamomum camphora. The experimental results showed that Cinnamomum camphora could contribute to carbon neutrality.

With the development of molecular biology technology, research has gradually penetrated into the molecular level to explore the molecular mechanisms of plant adversity response [5, 19]. Plant adversity response has been better understood in recent years thanks to the use of genomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics, and other histological techniques [9, 24]. These techniques also allow one to examine plant adversity adaptation strategies from a systems biology perspective [8]. Although modern biotechnology provides powerful tools for plant adversity physiology research, there are still some technical and methodological limitations [10, 26]. In addition, the existing methods for measuring physiological indexes may not be able to comprehensively reflect the state of the plant’s response to adversity, especially the dynamic changes at the molecular level. In this context, research attempts to optimize the growth experimental environment of plants under stress, and appropriately combine the analysis of non structural carbohydrates to conduct a more comprehensive study on the physiological and ecological response of Cinnamomum camphora under stress, in order to provide some reference for forestry development.

The difference between this study and other previous literature is that previous studies mainly focused on certain single physiological indicators or specific environmental variables. Although these studies provided data on the physiological responses of Cinnamomum camphora under specific conditions, they lacked research on the comprehensive physiological and ecological responses under multiple stress conditions. Previous studies have mostly focused on the specific response analysis of a certain organ or growth stage of Cinnamomum camphora, while this article will expand the research to the synergistic changes between different organs and the impact of environmental stress on the entire plant. This comprehensive perspective helps to understand the adaptive regulation mechanism of Cinnamomum camphora in the face of multiple pressures such as drought, floods, and rehydration. The aim of this study is to explore the physiological and ecological response mechanisms of Cinnamomum camphora seedlings under drought, waterlogging, and post drought rehydration stress. By analyzing changes in biomass allocation, leaf water potential regulation, gas exchange characteristics, and non structural carbohydrate metabolism, the physiological strategies of Cinnamomum camphora to adapt to adversity stress are revealed, providing scientific basis for the ecological protection and urban greening applications of Cinnamomum camphora.

Main experimental materials

The Cinnamomum camphora seedlings used in the experiment were purchased from an ecological nursery base in Wuhu City, Anhui Province. The seedlings used are all one-year-old and have good growth. In order to ensure comparability of the experiment and reduce the interference of individual differences on the experimental results, all seedlings were selected from the same batch, with seedling height within the range of 20 ± 2 cm and stem thickness within the range of 0.5 ± 0.05 cm, and had not experienced significant environmental stress or pest infestation. Before the experiment began, all seedlings underwent an adaptation period of cultivation (30 day) to ensure their normal growth under experimental conditions. In order to minimize the impact of the seedlings’ own conditions, the selected batch of Cinnamomum camphora seedlings should have uniform growth. The soil used in the study was collected from the surface soil of the Cinnamomum camphora cultivation area. The soil was sieved to remove stones and large particle impurities, ensuring uniformity [21, 29]. The soil is loamy soil, with a saturated hydraulic conductivity of 1.2 × 10−5m/s determined by the constant head method, and has good air permeability and drainage. Its main physical properties include a bulk density of 1.25 ± 0.05 g/cm3 and a field water holding capacity of about 25%. In terms of chemical properties, the soil pH value is 6.5 ± 0.2, which is slightly acidic and suitable for the growth of Cinnamomum camphora; The organic matter content is 25 ± 2 g/kg, the alkaline nitrogen content is 80 ± 5 mg/kg, the available phosphorus content is 20 ± 2 mg/kg, and the available potassium content is 150 ± 10 mg/kg. The test site is shown in Fig. 1.

Adversity stress experimental site

In Fig. 1, the adversity stress experimental site constructed for the study uses a steel frame to build the outer frame, which is a rectangle with a length of 10 m and a width of 5 m in the top view. The site was surrounded by no walls and transparent polyvinyl chloride panels with about 30% light shading were installed on the top as a rain shelter. After punching holes in the bottom of the planter, the surface soil of the plantation forest was air-dried and evenly mixed. Only one Cinnamomum camphora seedling was planted in each pot, and after planting, it was placed in an empty bucket of water 10 cm deeper than the pot [27]. All treatment groups of seedlings were randomly arranged in the same steel frame experimental shed and shared uniformly controlled lighting (1000 ± 50 µmol·m−2·s−1), CO2 concentration (400 ± 10 ppm), and humidity (60 ± 5%). Using full spectrum LED lights as artificial light sources, installed at the top of the steel frame experimental shed, under the opaque transparent PVC board, the light shines downwards to simulate natural light conditions and ensure that Cinnamomum camphora seedlings can carry out normal photosynthesis. The concentration of carbon dioxide in the air is maintained at 400 ± 10 ppm, which is close to the common concentration in the atmosphere, to avoid the impact of excessive or insufficient CO2 concentration on photosynthesis and respiration. During the experiment, the environmental humidity was controlled at 60 ± 5% to simulate a humid environment suitable for the growth of Cinnamomum camphora. The study setup adversity stress scenario is shown in Fig. 2.

Adversity stress scenarios

In Fig. 2, the study set up three adversity stress scenarios of DS, FS, and rehydration after drought. The drought stress of Cinnamomum camphora refers to the cessation of watering the pots of the drought stress group after the completion of Cinnamomum camphora seedling planting, aiming to simulate the drought conditions in the natural environment and explore the physiological and ecological response of Cinnamomum camphora seedlings under extreme water scarcity. The experiment was conducted from May to June 2023 in Wuhu City, Anhui Province, with a longitude of 118° 21′ E and a latitude of 31° 20′ N. The daily average temperature was 25.3 ± 3.2 °C, and the total duration was 40 days. The key time nodes include: potting of seedlings (30 days before the start of stress), stress initiation (Day 0), first biomass/water potential measurement (Day 4), mid-term harvesting (Day 18), and final measurement and harvesting (Day 40). This study set up four treatment groups, including control group, DS group, FS group, and post drought rehydration group, with a total sample size of 48 seedlings. Each group has 3 biological replicates, which means conducting independent multiple experimental operations and measurements on the seedlings to ensure the reliability of the experimental results. Each replicate contains 4 seedlings, and each group treats a total of 12 seedlings. The control group maintained a relatively stable humidity in the flowerpot during the experiment, simulating conditions close to the field water holding capacity. The DS group was not watered after completing the planting of Cinnamomum camphora seedlings. In the FS group, after completing the planting of Cinnamomum camphora seedlings, water was added to the bucket so that the water in the bucket completely submerged the pots and the height of the water surface exceeded the soil surface by more than 5 cm. Xylem noon water potential (WP) is an important indicator for measuring the water status in plant xylem, reflecting the tension and availability of water in the plant body. For the 12 seedlings in the post drought rehydration group, when the stomatal conductance of the seedlings under drought stress drops to 0 mol·m−2·s−1 or the WP of the xylem drops below − 50 MPa, the seedlings are subjected to rehydration operation. The rehydration method is to restore soil moisture to the field capacity (about 25%) and maintain it until the end of the experiment. This study used a TDR soil moisture sensor (HH2 type, Delta-T company, UK) to monitor the soil moisture content in the surface layer of 0–20 cm daily. When the measured value deviates from the target value by more than 3%, water was replenished.

Measurement indicators and methods

In the determination of the biomass of Cinnamomum camphora seedlings, three time nodes were set up for the full harvest of Cinnamomum camphora seedlings before the start of the experiment, up to the 18th day of the experiment, and up to the 40th day of the experiment. Four pots of seedlings were harvested at each time point for data collection. The seedlings harvested before starting the experiment were used as background values. After harvesting the Cinnamomum camphora seedlings, the leaves, roots and stems were taken and cleaned. They were then dried to a consistent weight in an oven that was preheated to 70 °C. Three indexes of wood water potential, predawn water potential and noon WP were collected in the determination of leaf water potential of Cinnamomum camphora seedlings. Woody WP was collected by wrapping Cinnamomum camphora leaves with plastic wrap and then wrapping a layer of tin foil on the outermost part, and after one hour, collect the leaves in a self sealing bag. The method of collecting pre-dawn WP is shown in Fig. 3.

Method of collecting water potential before dawn

In Fig. 3, when pre-dawn WP collection was conducted, Cinnamomum camphora leaves were wrapped with plastic wrap during the night of the day prior to collection and then wrapped with a layer of tin foil on the outermost part. The leaves were collected into self-sealing bags and kept in a holding tank approximately 1 h before sunrise. The midday WP is measured after the completion of leaf gas exchange measurement, and the xylem WP is measured before gas exchange, using the same method as the pre-dawn WP measurement. This study used PMS-Model1505D digital WP pressure chamber to measure the WP of collected blades. The time points for leaf WP measurements were set at the beginning of the experiment, and on days 4, 8, 12, 16, and 20 of the experiment. In determining the gas exchange parameters of Cinnamomum camphora leaves, the daily time around 10:00 a.m. on the first 20 days of the experiment was selected as the measurement time. Select mature leaves of Cinnamomum camphora and use a gas exchange system to measure stomatal conductance (gs), transpiration rates (TRs) and light-saturated photosynthesis rate (Pn). Measure mature leaves using a Li-6400 portable photosynthesis analyzer, with a clamped leaf area of 6 cm2. The saturated light intensity is 1000 µmol·m−2·s−1, provided by the built-in LED light source. Maintain 400 ppm through CO2 injection into the system, with an airflow velocity of 500 µmol·m−2·s−1. The blade temperature is 25 ± 0.5 °C, regulated by the Peltier temperature control system. After the fluctuation of photosynthetic rate and stomatal conductance readings is below 1%, record the mean data continuously for 3 min. The duration of a single measurement is 10 min per leaf, including 3 min of pre equilibration time. The process of measuring the water utilization capacity and stable carbon isotope ratio (Rr) of the leaves is shown in Fig. 4.

The process of determining the Rr of leaves

In Fig. 4, when measuring the Rr of leaves, seedlings harvested on the 40th day of stress were collected and dried continuously in a 70 ℃ oven for 48 h until constant weight (mass change < 0.1%) was reached. After cooling to room temperature in the dryer, they were immediately ground through a 100 mesh sieve. After sieving using a 100 mesh sieve, 5 mg of the sample obtained from sieving was weighed using an electronic scale. A catalyst was added and the temperature was set to 1020 °C to start combustion to produce carbon dioxide. The Rr of the leaves were measured using an elemental analyzer and mass spectrometer. When measuring the Non-structural carbohydrates (NSC) in camphor tree seedlings, the specific sampling time was on the 40th day of stress treatment. Mature leaves, intact roots, and stem segments on the sunny side were collected at 10:00 am daily, frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 ℃. NSC extraction was performed using the alcohol perchloric acid method. The samples of Cinnamomum camphora seedlings were first dried and then ground into powder form. 50 mg of powder from the corresponding site was weighed into a centrifuge tube with a volume of 10 ml and vortexed by adding 4 ml of alcohol at a concentration of 80%. Centrifuge tubes containing samples are placed in a water bath (WB) and heated for 30 min at a temperature of 80 °C. After heating and cooling, the tubes were placed in a centrifuge and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant from the centrifuge tube was collected and transferred to a centrifuge tube with a volume of 15 ml. The remaining lower residue was used for soluble starch determination, and the upper supernatant was used for soluble sugar determination. To carry out soluble starch determination, 10 ml of perchloric acid at 30% v/v was added to the lower residue and placed at ambient temperature. On the second day it was placed at 80 °C for 10 min in a WB for heating. Following the cooling process, it was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min. After adding ultrapure water to a volumetric flask (VF) containing the centrifuge, the sample was fixed at 50 milliliters. After transferring 1 milliliter of the filtrate to a fresh test tube, 5 milliliters of anthrone solution was added, thoroughly mixed, and heated to boiling for ten minutes. A UV spectrophotometer was used to measure the optical density values of the samples after they had cooled. For the determination of soluble sugars, activated charcoal was added to the transferred supernatant and vortexing was carried out for 1 min. The temperature was set at 80 °C and after 30 min of decolorization, the sample was filtered and transferred to a VF, where ultrapure water was added and the volume was fixed to 50 ml. After transferring 1 milliliter of the filtrate to a fresh test tube and thoroughly stirring in 5 milliliter of anthrone solution, the sample was heated to a boil for ten minutes. Using a UV spectrophotometer, the optical density values of the samples were ascertained after they had cooled.

Statistical methods

For statistical analysis, data were analyzed using SPSS 19.0 statistical software. One-way repeated measures analysis was done for nitrogen and phosphorus content and WP data. Where data collection at different time points was involved, one-way ANOVA was performed on the collected data. All experimental results data were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. In the analysis of stable carbon isotope ratios, the δ 13 C values under DS were significantly higher than those in the control group, at −26.7‰ and − 28.4‰, respectively (P < 0.05); The δ 13 C value of the waterlogging stress group was − 29.1‰, lower than that of the control group. Discussion

In the study, Cinnamomum camphora seedlings grown up to one year were selected and their physiological and ecological responses were thoroughly analyzed by setting flooding, drought and rehydration after drought as stress environments. In the experiments conducted in the study, Cinnamomum camphora seedlings in the control group were significantly larger than those in DS and FS in terms of stem, leaf and root biomass. It indicated that FS and DS had significant negative effects on the growth of Cinnamomum camphora. In contrast, DS resulted in a significant decrease in leaf biomass and a reduction in root biomass, but the stem biomass was relatively high. It might indicate that under drought conditions, Cinnamomum camphora prioritized the maintenance of stem growth at the expense of leaf and root development, which was in general agreement with the conclusions obtained by other studies [4, 11, 14]. DS resulted in a continuous decrease in pre-dawn WP, midday WP and xylem WP of leaves, reflecting the gradual loss of water in plants under drought conditions. FS and DS significantly reduced Pn, which might be related to stomatal closure to reduce water transpiration losses. This indicates that long-term drought stress has had a serious impact on the overall water status of seedlings. At the same time, the fluctuation of leaf water potential under waterlogging conditions is relatively small, indicating that waterlogging has a relatively limited impact on the water potential of camphor tree seedlings. This may be because camphor tree seedlings have a certain degree of water resistance, which can regulate water absorption and utilization to a certain extent, reducing the negative effects of flooding. DS decreased NSC and starch concentrations in stems, whereas FS increased these indices. It indicated that plants under flooded conditions increased energy reserves to cope with hypoxic stress, in agreement with other studies [12]. When water was restored after drought, the leaf WP of Cinnamomum camphora seedlings was able to rapidly recover to near normal levels, showing strong drought tolerance. After rehydration, the Pn, gs and TR of drought-stressed treated seedlings displayed signs of recovery. After drought stress, the recovery rate of seedlings is inversely proportional to the intensity of drought they experience, meaning that the heavier the drought, the slower the recovery rate. This may be related to the degree of physiological damage caused by drought in plants, with the more severe the damage, the longer the recovery time required. Moreover, the rate of recovery was inversely proportional to the intensity of drought, which was basically consistent with the conclusions obtained from other studies [22]; Wang et al., [22].

Research has found that Cinnamomum camphora seedlings improve water use efficiency by closing stomata under DS, prioritizing stem growth to ensure water transport [1]. Under waterlogging stress, due to root hypoxia, seedlings increase NSC content in leaves and stems as energy reserves, while reducing photosynthesis and water loss. The impact of these strategies on growth is twofold: on the one hand, by prioritizing resource allocation to maintain the functional and structural stability of key organs and enhance survival capabilities; On the other hand, due to limited photosynthesis and adjustments in energy allocation, the growth of leaves and roots is inhibited. Overall, Cinnamomum camphora seedlings exhibit flexible physiological regulation abilities under drought and waterlogging stress, by coordinating water balance, photosynthesis, and carbon metabolism to minimize the negative impact of stress on their growth and achieve adaptation to adversity. This indicates that Cinnamomum camphora adapt to different stress conditions by regulating carbon metabolism and water strategies. This study provides important information for understanding the physiological-ecological response of Cinnamomum camphora under adversity stress. Through in-depth analysis of biomass changes, leaf WP regulation, adjustment of gas exchange characteristics, and non-structural carbohydrate metabolism, the physiological mechanisms of Cinnamomum camphora adapting to adversity are revealed. However, the study only considered water stress scenarios, which is not sufficient for the study of complex ecological environment, and more special stress factors will be added in the future to analyze the growth characteristics of Cinnamomum camphora from a multifactorial perspective.

Conclusion

The study comprehensively analyzed the physiological and ecological responses of Cinnamomum camphora seedlings under different stress conditions, including drought, waterlogging, and drought rehydration, combined with multidimensional measurements of water balance, photosynthesis parameters, and non structural carbohydrate metabolism. The results showed that DS significantly reduced the total biomass of seedlings (from 40.84 ± 0.61 g in the control group to 23.57 ± 0.56 g), the leaf biomass significantly decreased to 4.30 ± 0.45 g, and the δ 13 C value increased to −26.7‰, indicating that stomatal closure improved water use efficiency. Waterlogging stress led to a decrease in root biomass to 8.86 ± 1.15 g, while non structural carbohydrates significantly accumulated in stems and leaves to meet the metabolic demands of low oxygen environments. After rehydration, the leaf water potential and photosynthetic parameters quickly recovered, demonstrating strong drought tolerance. The innovation of the research lies in the comprehensive study of the response mechanisms of Cinnamomum camphora seedlings under various water stresses from the perspective of overall physiological processes, breaking through the previous research mode of single stress or limited analysis of a certain organ. By revealing the resource priority allocation strategy (stem growth priority) under drought conditions and the energy reserve mechanism under waterlogging conditions, this study proposes adaptive regulation strategies for Cinnamomum camphora seedlings under complex stress conditions. This provides a scientific basis for the application of Cinnamomum camphora in ecological restoration and urban greening, and offers new methods and perspectives for the comprehensive physiological regulation of plants under multiple stresses.
