Prevalence and socio-behavioural determinants of periodontal disease among adolescents in the northern West bank: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sura Ibrahiem Al-Hassan, Mazen Mohammed Kazlak, Elham Kateeb
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeriodontal disease during adolescence is increasingly recognised as a public health concern due to its high global prevalence and long-term health implications. Despite this, no large-scale studies have assessed its burden among adolescents in Palestine. This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of periodontal diseases among 9th-grade students in the northern West Bank and examine its association with key behavioural and socioeconomic factors.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 3,352 15-year-old students, selected through stratified proportional random sampling from governmental and private schools. Periodontal health was assessed using the WHO Community Periodontal Index for Treatment Needs, and oral hygiene status was measured with the Simplified Oral Hygiene Index. Participants also completed a structured questionnaire that assessed their socioeconomic status, oral hygiene practices, dietary habits, and smoking habits. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics, bivariate analysis, and multivariate logistic regression.ResultsThe prevalence of periodontal diseases was 86.8% among the participating students. Gingival bleeding affected 44.6% of the sample, and calculus was present in 42.2%. Brushing frequency and oral hygiene status were identified as the most significant predictors that greatly increased the risk of periodontal disease, with Adjusted Odds Ratios of 2.05 and 1.6, respectively. Using medium-bristled toothbrushes was a protective factor for the likelihood of having healthy gums (AOR = 1.79). Frequent fish consumption also had a protective association (AOR = 2.04). Conversely, smoking water pipes was negatively associated with periodontal health (AOR = 0.63), indicating a higher risk of disease among users.ConclusionThis study revealed a high prevalence of early periodontal disease among 9th-grade students in the northern governorates of the West Bank, with only 13.2% exhibiting healthy gingiva. Several modifiable determinants—such as oral hygiene practices, toothbrush type, dietary habits, and water-pipe smoking—were significantly associated with periodontal health. Addressing these determinants is critical to preventing disease development and reducing its progression in this age group. Therefore, implementing structured, school-based educational programs tailored to these determinants is strongly recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07083-y.

Introduction

Periodontal diseases (PD) are among the most prevalent oral health conditions, affecting individuals of all ages worldwide [1, 2]. The early onset of PD often begins in childhood [3]. The condition is primarily caused by bacterial accumulation in dental plaque [4], which triggers a chronic inflammatory process [2].

The classification of PD is based on several parameters, including its extent (generalised vs. localised), severity (mild, moderate, or severe), rate of progression (chronic vs. aggressive), and affected tissues. However, gingivitis, the earliest and most common form of PD, is characterised by redness, swelling, and bleeding confined to the gums [5]. When inflammation extends into the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone, it leads to the apical migration of the junctional epithelium, resulting in the formation of a periodontal pocket. This stage of PD is called periodontitis, during which resorption of the alveolar bone occurs, leading to tooth mobility and premature tooth loss [6].

Globally, PD affects approximately 80% of the population, with its prevalence continuing to rise [7, 8]. Among older children and adolescents (12–15 years), prevalence rates range from 50% to 100% [9]; calculus and gingival bleeding are the most commonly observed periodontal conditions in this age group [10, 11]. Epidemiological studies highlight significant variations across populations: in Brazil, around 30% of adolescents exhibit periodontal changes, with gingival bleeding affecting 14.1% and dental calculus 14% [12]. In India, the prevalence of gingivitis was reported at 22% among adolescents [13]. In Jordan, only 29.8% of school students have healthy gingiva, while 38.5% present with mild gingivitis, 31.4% with moderate gingivitis, and 0.3% with severe gingivitis [3]. Despite the high global burden of PD, data on its prevalence and severity remain limited in certain regions. In Palestine, epidemiological studies on PD are scarce. However, a study conducted in North Gaza and Gaza City reported that among school-aged children, 28.5% had mild gingivitis, 44.5% had moderate gingivitis, and 27% had severe gingivitis [14]. These findings underscore the need for further research and targeted public health interventions to address periodontal health in underserved populations.

Epidemiological data indicate that gingivitis is most frequently observed in children around the age of 10, while periodontitis becomes more prevalent in individuals aged 11 and older [15]. This progression suggests that reversible forms of (PD) can persist over extended periods and, if left unmanaged, may advance from mild subclinical inflammation to more destructive stages, ultimately leading to tooth loss, a defining characteristic of periodontitis [12, 16]. Given this trajectory, greater attention must be directed toward PD in adolescents rather than focusing solely on older adults [17]. Oral health during adolescence serves as a crucial predictor of long-term periodontal status and offers valuable insights for targeted health promotion strategies [18]. Addressing periodontal health in this age group is not only essential for preventing disease progression but also for mitigating the broad economic and social burdens associated with PD. These burdens include treatment costs, school absenteeism, and intangible consequences such as pain, difficulties with chewing, tasting, and speaking, as well as psychosocial effects like impaired smiling and reduced self-confidence [19].

The development and progression of PD are influenced by a range of structural and intermediary determinants that vary across populations. Structural determinants include the broad healthcare system and access to dental care, while intermediary determinants refer to factors that mediate the relationship between structural determinants and periodontal health outcomes. These include demographic and social characteristics such as age, sex and family income in addition to behavioural and lifestyle-related factors like oral hygiene practices, dietary habits, and smoking [20, 21].

Indeed, among adolescents, lifestyle factors like oral hygiene habits, smoking, and dietary choices are crucial in the development of PD. By adopting healthier lifestyle behaviours, individuals can enhance their immune response, decrease their susceptibility to periodontal infections, and ultimately slow the progression of the disease [22].

This study aims to address two primary research questions: (1) What is the prevalence of PD among 9th-grade students in the northern governorates of the West Bank? Moreover, (2) Is there an association between PD prevalence and key determinants such as socioeconomic status (SES), oral hygiene practices, dietary habits, and smoking behaviours?

Given the scarcity of data on periodontal conditions among adolescents in the West Bank, this study seeks to bridge this gap by providing an updated assessment of PD prevalence using the Community Periodontal Index for Treatment Needs (CPITN) in this population. Furthermore, it examines potential associations between PD and various determinants, including SES, oral hygiene status, assessed by Simplified Oral Hygiene Index (S-OHI), oral hygiene practices, dietary habits, and smoking behaviours. By identifying these modifiable and non-modifiable determinants, the study would inform targeted intervention programs and public health strategies to improve adolescent oral health outcomes.

Methodology

Study design, setting, and population

This cross-sectional study was conducted in schools across the northern governorates of the West Bank, which include Jenin, Tulkarm, Nablus, Qalqilya, and Salfit. These governorates collectively have a population of approximately 1.1 million people and encompass a total of 933 schools. Of these, 813 are government-run, 38 are operated by the United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees (UNRWA), and 82 are privately managed [23].

This study targeted adolescents aged 15 (9th-grade students), who are at a critical developmental stage characterised by significant physical, educational, social, and emotional transitions [24]. The World Health Organisation (WHO) has recognised the importance of periodontal health in this age group. It established a global target in 2010, which stipulates that all 15-year-olds should have at least five healthy sextants in their mouths [25]. Furthermore, age 15 serves as a key reference point for epidemiological studies on oral health, facilitating international comparisons and enabling global monitoring of PD trends [26, 27].

Sampling process

A multistage sampling technique was employed to obtain a representative sample of schools and students. Three governorates—Nablus, Jenin, and Tulkarm—were selected in the first stage based on their high population density and significant concentration of schools and students, ensuring a robust sample size for the study. Additionally, including these governorates allowed for the capture of variations in cultural, economic, and educational factors among participants. Practical considerations, such as accessibility and logistical feasibility, also influenced the selection process.

In the second stage, schools within each governorate were selected using a proportional stratified random sampling method. Schools were stratified by type (governmental or private) and gender (male or female) to ensure balanced representation. The final sample comprised 15 schools from Nablus (10 governmental and 5 private), 14 schools from Jenin (11 governmental and 3 private), and 15 schools from Tulkarm (11 governmental and 4 private) (Fig. 1).

To be eligible for inclusion in the study, schools were required to have 9th-grade classes, permit data collection, and obtain informed consent from both students and their parents.

Pilot study

Following established guidelines for conducting a complete study [28], a pilot survey was conducted with 10% of the predetermined sample (100 students). The pilot’s study primarily focused on assessing the feasibility of the research process, the students’ comprehension of the questionnaire items and performing a descriptive analysis that did not involve hypothesis testing of the clinical outcomes [29].

Based on feedback from the pilot study, the final version of the questionnaire was refined. Additionally, piloting the questionnaire with participants helped to establish several forms of validity. Face validity, which was confirmed by ensuring the questionnaire appeared relevant and understandable to participants [30]. Content validity was achieved by verifying that the questionnaire comprehensively covered all aspects of the study variables [31]. An Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was performed using Principal Component Analysis to evaluate the instrument’s Construct validity. The suitability of the data for factor analysis was assessed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity. The KMO value was 0.70, indicating satisfactory sampling adequacy, and Bartlett’s test was significant (p < 0.001), which supports the factorability of the correlation matrix. Factors were extracted based on eigenvalues greater than 1 and by inspecting the scree plot. The analysis identified a 4-factor solution that explained 71.30% of the total variance. Items were retained if they had factor loadings of 0.50 or higher and had theoretical relevance.

The data collected during the pilot study were not included in the final analysis.

Clinical screening

This study employed the Community Periodontal Index of Treatment Needs (CPITN) to assess the prevalence of PD among 9th-grade students. The CPITN is widely recognised as a reliable and practical tool for measuring PD prevalence and for guiding the development of preventive oral health policies [27, 32–34]. It offers a straightforward approach compared to other methods that require detailed measurements of clinical attachment loss, such as the Basic Periodontal Examination (BPE). The CPITN focuses on essential indicators, including the presence or absence of gingival bleeding upon probing, as well as the detection of supra- or subgingival calculus and periodontal pockets. This index employs a 0.5 mm ball-tip probe, developed explicitly by WHO, making it particularly suitable for evaluating adolescents aged 15 and under [35].

The operational characteristics of CPITN have been extensively evaluated in the literature, including comparisons with standard periodontal examinations and diagnostic criteria. Research has reported its sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value as 58%, 80%, 87%, and 46.3%, respectively [36]. Moreover, CPITN has demonstrated validity in identifying both gingivitis and periodontitis [37].

Since this study’s participants were adolescents, severe periodontal conditions (CPITN scores 3 and 4) were generally uncommon and rare [27, 38]. Therefore, in this study, only scores 0, 1, and 2 of CPITN were considered, with the following classifications:

This study examined each sextant of the mouth using index teeth: 16, 11, 26, 36, 31, and 46 [26].

To evaluate oral hygiene status, this study employed the Simplified Oral Hygiene Index (S-OHI), developed by Greene and Vermillion [39]. This index has been widely applied in oral health research [40, 41]. The S-OHI scores range from 0 to 3, assessing the presence of calculus and debris. Calculus and debris accumulation were examined on the facial surfaces of teeth 11, 16, and 26 and the lingual surfaces of teeth 31, 36, and 46.

During the pilot phase, inter-examiner reliability was assessed using a two-way random-effects model of the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), following the methodology outlined by Koo and Li [42]. Five data collectors independently evaluated 20 students five times, yielding ICC values of 0.83 for CPITN and 0.98 for S-OHI, indicating good inter-examiner reliability. The choice of the ICC model assumed that the raters were randomly selected from a larger population of dentists in the West Bank and consistently participated throughout the study [43, 44].

Questionnaire

The questionnaire used in this study was developed based on established literature [27, 45, 46] with some modifications to suit the study objectives. Initially, the questionnaire was designed in English and subsequently translated into Arabic to ensure accessibility for participants. To validate the translation, a back-translation process was carried out, wherein the Arabic version was translated back into English to verify its accuracy before it was administered as a pre-test exercise.

The questionnaire consisted of standardised, pretested, closed-ended questions that underwent face and content validity assessments by three experts: a clinical periodontology specialist, a professor and researcher in public health, and a practitioner and academic with experience in clinical research.

The questionnaire was structured into five sections covering key determinants of PD. The first section gathered information on students’ background, including school type and school system, while the second section focused on family background, including family size, parental education and occupation, as well as the family economic status. The third section assessed oral hygiene practices, such as the frequency, duration and technique of tooth brushing, while the fourth examined dietary habits, including the frequency of weekly intake of nutritious versus non-nutritious foods. The final section explored smoking behaviours, assessing the frequency of cigarette and waterpipe smoking and the number of cigarettes smoked.

The questionnaire’s internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. The reliability analysis, conducted in 100 cases, yielded an alpha value of 0.967 across 47 items, indicating an excellent internal consistency.

Data collection process

Following ethical approval from the Al-Quds committee (REF. 13/24), consent forms were sent to the students’ parents, and data collection commenced upon receiving parental approval. To ensure standardisation and calibration of the data collection process, the researcher trained five dentists to conduct both interviews and clinical dental examinations. Training sessions were conducted every 15 days throughout the study period to maintain consistency among data collectors.

The questionnaire was administered through direct interviews with individual students, allowing for accurate and comprehensive data collection. All participants completed both the interview and the clinical examination, resulting in a 100% response rate. The study was conducted over a period of five months, from January 2024 to May 2024. Data was processed in accordance with Al-Quds University’s confidentiality standards, and all records were de-identified before analysis and stored on encrypted institutional servers, with access limited to authorised team members.

Data management and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 25.0 IBM.

To determine the (CPITN), the highest score recorded among the students’ mouth sextants was considered the representative score for each student, and the frequency of each score was subsequently calculated [47]. The mean of the (S-OHI) score was also computed [48]. Descriptive statistics, including frequency and percentage, were calculated for all variables in the study. The distribution of CPITN categories among the examined sextants was analysed, and the distribution of CPITN categories among participants was also calculated. The 95% confidence interval (CI) and standard errors were calculated to provide an overall summary of each category’s status, assess the precision of the estimates, and quantify uncertainty in the estimated percentages.

To identify the determinants that influence periodontal status among the participant students, logistic regression statistical analysis was employed due to the non-normal distribution of the original CPITN scores (the dependent variable) [49, 50]. Bivariate logistic regression for CPITN data is a commonly used and accepted approach in the international peer-reviewed literature [51].

Consequently, the CPITN scores were reclassified into a binary system: healthy gingiva (0) and diseased gingiva (1). This approach adhered to the WHO guidelines for epidemiological studies [27], and this binary system simplified the analysis and interpretation of the findings.

Before analysis, missing values were handled via imputation. The K-nearest neighbour (K-NN) imputation method was used to achieve accurate data completion for each case with minimal error [52]. Outliers were analysed using boxplots, as well as skewness and kurtosis, and were managed through trimming, excluding extreme cases.

Regarding multicollinearity, no independent variables had a Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) value below 5, and no tolerance value was less than 2.5, indicating no multicollinearity issues among the variables.

To prepare variables for regression analysis, dummy coding was applied as a commonly used method of categorical variable representation [53]. Each variable was recoded into a set of binary indicators (0/1), with the category coded as zero serving as the reference level. Prior to model fitting, prespecified checks were performed to ensure sufficient cell sizes and an appropriate number of events per variable (EPV ≈ 5–10 per coefficient) [54, 55]. Contingency tables were examined to detect any quasi-complete separation; variables that failed to meet these criteria were excluded from further analysis (see supplementary Table A for the complete contingency table used to screen variables for regression analysis).

To ensure the validity of the binary logistic regression model, the Box-Tidwell test was applied to verify linearity in the continuous predictors. This involved adding a product term between each predictor and its natural log transformation and examining the significance of this term. The results revealed a non-significant interaction term (B = −0.001, p = 0.789), confirming that the assumption of linearity in the logit was met.

Socio-behavioural factors, such as SES, oral hygiene practices, dietary habits, and smoking behaviours, were considered potential predictors for CPITN [56–58]. Based on theses previous literature, only those variables that presented with a significance level of (p ≤ 0.25) in bivariate logistic regression were included in the multivariate analysis. In contrast, variables that showed no significant association in bivariate analyses (p ≤ 0.25) were excluded from the multivariate analysis [12, 59]. The study employed a stepwise forward inclusion procedure for model development, beginning with an empty model. Predictors were added one by one based on their statistical significance, continuing until no further variables met the inclusion criteria. The final model retained only statistically significant predictors (p-value < 0.05). Adjusted odds ratios were calculated with their respective 95% confidence intervals.

The Hosmer-Lemeshow test was used to assess the goodness of fit of the logistic model.

The ratio of PD cases to the number of independent variables was calculated to assess the risk of overfitting in the logistic regression model. With 2900 PD cases and 30 independent variables, the ratio was 96.67. Since the ratio exceeds 10, the model is considered appropriate for including 30 independent variables without the risk of overfitting, supporting both model stability and the generalizability of the results.

Result

The sample comprised slightly more males (52.1%) than females (47.9%), predominantly from governmental schools (58.3%) and the Nablus governorate (39.0%), with most (72.3%) reporting average or low family economic status (Table 2). The majority of students exhibited a medium level of oral hygiene (90.6%) (Table 4). Dietary habits indicated limited consumption of healthy foods, with fewer than 5% consuming fruits (1.6%), vegetables (3.9%), red meat (3.3%), fish (4.5%), or nuts (1.6%) five or more times per week. Meanwhile, 41.1% frequently consumed milk and dairy products (Table 5). Smoking behaviours were prevalent: 46.2% reported cigarette smoking and 85.0% reported water-pipe smoking; 72.0% reported frequent water-pipe sessions overall (~ 84.8% among water-pipe users) (Table 6).

The (CPITN) distribution among the participant students revealed that only 13.2% of the students had healthy gingiva. Most students exhibited PD signs, with 44.6% experiencing gingival bleeding and 42.2% presenting with calculus (Table 2).

Bivariate analysis revealed that geographic residence and SES were the strongest predictors of PD. Students living in Jenin had 68% higher odds of developing PD compared to those in Tulkarm (Crude Odds Ratio = OR: 1.68), while those in Nablus showed a similar but statistically nonsignificant trend (OR: 1.35). Paternal education (at the university level) and family economic status were also among the sociodemographic determinants of PD (OR: 1.30 and OR: 1.42, respectively) (Table 2).

Several oral hygiene practices act as determinants of students’ periodontal status in bivariate analysis — namely, brushing status, brushing frequency, brushing duration, use of a medium-bristle toothbrush, interdental-brush use, and dental visits — but the strongest determinant was the (S-OHI), which conferred a roughly 27-fold higher odds of PD as scores increased (OR: 26.58). Brushing for only one minute, compared to brushing for two minutes or more, increased the odds by about 60% (OR:1.62) (Table 4).

The bivariate analysis also indicated potential associations between students’ periodontal status and some dietary habits, such as the frequency of fish consumption (OR:1.647), frequency of red meat consumption (OR:0.668), and frequency of vegetable consumption (OR:0.704) (Table 5). Among the smoking-related behaviours, Smoking status (water pipe) stood out as the dominant risk factor: students who reported water-pipe use had 49% higher odds of PD than non-users (OR: 1.49). Three additional variables crossed the bivariate screening cutoff (p ≤ 0.25). They were therefore eligible for multivariable modelling—namely “Smoking status (tobacco) (OR:0.86), Frequency of tobacco smoking (OR:1.24), and Frequency of water pipe smoking (OR:1.22) (Table 6).

The initial multivariate logistic regression model included a wide range of sociodemographic, behavioural, and lifestyle determinants: gender, governorate, father’s education level, family economic status, brushing status, brushing frequency, brushing duration, use of a medium-bristled toothbrush, interdental brush use, dental visits, (S-OHI), frequency of fish, red meat, and vegetable consumption, smoking status (tobacco and water pipe), and frequency of tobacco and water pipe smoking.

The final model retained several significant determinants associated with PD among students. Brushing twice daily or more was found to increase the likelihood of having healthy gingiva, with an Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) of 2.05. Other determinants that were associated with higher odds of maintaining healthy gingiva included using a medium-bristled toothbrush (AOR: 1.79), having a good (S-OHI) score (AOR:1.60), residing in Nablus (AOR:1.66), and eating fish five or more times a week (AOR:2.04). Conversely, students from average or low economic backgrounds had lower odds of having healthy gingiva compared to those from good or excellent economic status (AOR:0.61). Additionally, students who smoked water pipes had lower odds of healthy gingiva than non-smokers (AOR:0.63) (see Table 7).

The logistic regression model’s goodness-of-fit was assessed using a likelihood ratio test, yielding a significant chi-square statistic of χ2 = 849.068, p = 0.000. Hosmer-Lemeshow test yielded a non-significant result (p = 0.077), suggesting that the model adequately fits the examined variables.

Additionally, the model’s pseudo-R² value of 0.524 suggests that the model accounts for approximately 52% of the variability in the periodontal outcome, representing a substantial improvement over the null model.

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the prevalence of PD and explore its association with a range of potential determinants, namely SES, oral hygiene status and practices, dietary habits, and smoking behaviours, among 9th-grade students attending both governmental and private schools in the northern governorates of the West Bank. Adolescents, particularly at the age of 15, are known to be highly susceptible to gingivitis and calculus accumulation due to hormonal changes, evolving oral hygiene practices, and other lifestyle habits [60, 61]. As such, they represent a critical age group for the early detection of oral diseases.

The study’s strengths stem from its large, representative sample of 3,352 individuals selected through stratified random sampling, ensuring broad generalizability. The study demonstrated high inter-examiner reliability, supporting methodological rigour, and the school-based setting that facilitated cost-effective data collection in line with global recommendations for monitoring adolescent oral health [62]. In addition to validated instruments, including the structured, closed-ended questionnaire, S-OHI and CPITN. CPITN serves as a reliable tool for conducting epidemiological surveys [27], as it aligns with international standards and has findings included in over 500 publications [63]. Only scores of 0, 1, and 2 of the CPITN were considered in this study. This is because periodontal pockets are uncommon in adolescents, because the short duration of exposure to bacterial plaque limits the accumulation of matrix-degrading enzymes necessary for the formation of pockets that are 3 mm or deeper [64]. Additionally, children’s gingival tissues are highly vascularized and rich in active fibroblasts, which promote connective tissue repair and resist breakdown [65]. Indeed, calculus (CPITN Score 2) is classified under diseased gingiva because its presence indicates established gingival pathology and promotes further inflammation [66]. Specifically, calculus hosts and shields subgingival bacterial biofilm, making plaque removal difficult, and this maintains inflammatory stimuli in the gingival sulcus. In other words, the presence of calculus shows that the balance between the host and biofilm has shifted towards disease [67].

The findings of this study reveal a concerning oral health profile among adolescents in this sample. Only 13.2% of students had healthy gingiva, while 44.6% presented with gingival bleeding, and 42.2% exhibited calculus accumulation. These results are generally consistent with studies conducted in other middle-income settings, such as those by Kazemnejad et al. [68] in Tehran and Silveira et al. [12] in Brazil. However, they differ from the conclusions of Nazir [10] and Chen et al. [11], who reported calculus as the most common condition among adolescents. Such discrepancies may be attributed to population-specific factors, including differences in dietary texture, cultural attitudes toward oral hygiene, and access to preventive dental care [69, 70].

According to the standard WHO guidelines for epidemiological surveys using CPITN [27], a thorough examination of periodontal health by mouth sextants was conducted, allowing for the targeted identification of specific sites that need attention. It was found that healthy gingiva was most frequently observed in the posterior lower left sextant and least in the posterior upper right, possibly reflecting asymmetrical cleaning related to hand dominance. In contrast, gingival bleeding was distributed uniformly across all sextants, suggesting widespread gingival inflammation, which may represent an early stage of periodontitis [71]. Calculus was particularly prevalent in the lower anterior sextants, which aligns with existing evidence regarding plaque retention challenges in this area [72, 73].

This study confirmed that both oral hygiene status and frequency of tooth brushing are key determinants of periodontal health, as they emerged together in the final multivariate logistic regression model, emphasising their combined relevance as predictors among students. Poor oral hygiene was significantly associated with an increased risk of PD, confirming the findings of previous research that identify biofilm accumulation as a central etiological factor in gingival and periodontal inflammation [74–76]. The pathogenic mechanisms are well established, with subgingival plaque harbouring Gram-negative anaerobic bacteria that trigger host inflammatory responses and tissue damage [77]. Some studies have indicated that the link between brushing frequency and periodontal health may be less pronounced in socioeconomically disadvantaged populations. For instance, a systematic review and meta-analysis found that while infrequent tooth brushing is typically associated with an increased risk of severe periodontitis with an AOR of (1.41–1.44), there is moderate variability across studies (I-squared ≈ 48%), suggesting differences in results based on specific contexts and populations [78]. Furthermore, research shows that individuals with lower SES tend to brush their teeth less frequently and have poorer periodontal health, regardless of their brushing habits. This implies that socioeconomic factors may influence the direct relationship between brushing frequency and periodontal conditions [79, 80], and this supports the findings of this study, as socioeconomic status emerged as a significant determinant in both bivariate and multivariate analyses.

The type of toothbrush used was also significantly associated with gingival health. Students using medium-bristle toothbrushes were 79% more likely to have healthy gums than those using soft or hard bristles. This finding aligns with recent studies [81, 82] that support the efficacy of medium-bristled toothbrushes in balancing plaque removal with gum protection. Medium-bristle toothbrushes may offer an optimal combination of stiffness and flexibility, effectively removing biofilm without causing undue trauma to gingival tissues, unlike hard bristles, which may increase recession risk, or overly soft bristles, which could reduce plaque removal efficiency [83]. However, some studies argue that toothbrush bristle stiffness alone may be less influential than brushing technique, duration, and frequency. The combined impact of these factors suggests that further investigation is needed [84, 85].

Dietary habits, particularly fish consumption, were positively associated with periodontal health. Frequent fish intake conferred a protective effect (AOR:2.04), consistent with findings by Martinon et al. [86] and Ottosson et al. [87], who attributed the benefits primarily to anti-inflammatory nutrients such as omega-3 fatty acids and vitamin D. Omega-3 fatty acids modulate the host inflammatory response by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby limiting periodontal tissue destruction and enhancing tissue repair mechanisms [88]. However, some studies have shown weaker or non-significant associations between fish intake and periodontal health, suggesting variability due to methodological differences, population characteristics, or dietary assessment methods [89]. Additionally, fish consumption might serve as a proxy for higher SES, which is commonly linked to improved oral hygiene practices and further enhancing periodontal outcomes [90].

Water pipe smoking was found to have a negative effect on periodontal health, increasing the probability of diseased gums. Despite the perception that water pipe smoking is less harmful than cigarettes [91], this study corroborates earlier findings that highlight its deleterious effects on gingival tissues. Interestingly, cigarette smoking, although reported by nearly half of the students, was not significantly associated with periodontal health in this sample. This may be due to lower frequency or intensity of cigarette use in this age group or possibly underreporting.

This study found that residents of Nablus were 66% more likely to have healthy gums compared to those in Jenin and Tulkarm. This regional advantage may reflect better access to dental services and oral health promotion programs. Moreover, the observed regional disparities may also reflect underlying socioeconomic inequalities, given that lower socioeconomic conditions are consistently linked with limited access to preventive care, as well as lower utilisation rates and poorer oral health outcomes [92, 93]. This finding aligns with the current study, which found that students from average or low socioeconomic backgrounds had lower odds of having healthy gingiva compared to those from good or excellent socioeconomic status (AOR:0.61).

The limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, due to the inherent nature of cross-sectional studies [94], it is not possible to establish temporal relationships between the potential determinants examined in this study and PD. Second, this study focused exclusively on adolescents from the northern West Bank, which limits its generalizability to other age groups and other Palestinian regions, particularly rural or underprivileged areas that may have distinct risk characteristics. Third, this study utilised the CPITN, a screening (not diagnostic) tool. Additionally, self-reported behavioural data in this study (oral hygiene, diet, and smoking) may introduce social desirability biases, particularly among adolescents. Participants might inaccurately recall their behaviours or report socially acceptable responses, which could potentially affect the validity of the findings.

Conclusion

This study is the first to investigate the prevalence of early (PD) among Palestinian adolescents. The findings reveal a high burden of early PD among 9th-grade students in the northern governorates of the West Bank, with only 13.2% of students showing healthy gums. Several determinants were found to be significantly associated with periodontal health in this population. Poor oral hygiene was identified as a critical issue. Additional associations included SES, frequency of brushing, use of medium-bristled toothbrushes, frequency of fish consumption, and the rising prevalence of water pipe smoking among adolescents. These results suggest that both behavioural and lifestyle factors play a substantial role in shaping periodontal health outcomes in this age group.

Recommendations

Periodic dental screenings should be institutionalised in schools to enable early detection and management of periodontal problems, particularly in underserved regions where students demonstrate poorer periodontal health outcomes.

Structured oral health educational programs should be integrated into the school curriculum, with a focus on proper toothbrushing techniques (e.g., frequency, duration, and appropriate toothbrush type). Teachers should be trained to reinforce these messages consistently within the classroom setting.

Targeted awareness campaigns are needed to address the risks of water pipe smoking among adolescents. These should be complemented by strict enforcement of age restrictions on tobacco sales and the implementation of school-based anti-smoking interventions.

Promoting healthy dietary habits should be prioritised, primarily by increasing the intake of fish and other nutrient-rich foods that support periodontal health. Nutritional counselling should be incorporated into broader oral health promotion strategies, particularly in low socioeconomic settings.

Looking forward, several avenues for future research are essential to deepen understanding and inform targeted interventions. Longitudinal studies are needed to track the progression of PD from adolescence into adulthood and to assess the long-term impact of oral hygiene behaviours, dietary habits, and tobacco use. Additionally, comparative studies across different cultural and socioeconomic contexts could provide valuable insights into the influence of regional dietary patterns and lifestyle behaviours on adolescent periodontal health.
